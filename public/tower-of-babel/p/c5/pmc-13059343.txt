Single-center analysis of relapse risk factors and prognosis in 256 cases of ETV6::RUNX1-positive pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Binglian Hu, Fangzhou Hu, Hailong He, Lingjun Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common pediatric malignancy, and although ETV6::RUNX1–positive B-cell ALL generally carries a favorable prognosis, it has a notable risk of late relapse. This study aimed to identify key relapse-associated risk factors and evaluate their impact on survival outcomes in children with ETV6::RUNX1–positive ALL.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study including 256 pediatric patients diagnosed with ETV6::RUNX1–positive ALL at the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University between May 2008 and August 2020. Clinical characteristics, treatment responses, and relapse patterns were assessed. Independent predictors of relapse were identified using multivariate logistic regression, and survival outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method.ResultsElevated initial white blood cell (WBC) count (≥ 50 × 10⁹/L), poor steroid response at day 7, and persistence of the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion transcript at the first post-therapy minimal residual disease assessment were significant independent predictors of relapse (P < 0.05). Among relapsed patients, early relapse (within 36 months of diagnosis) and lack of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) were associated with markedly inferior survival (5-year overall survival (OS): 17.6% vs. 55.6%, P = 0.006).ConclusionsThis study identifies three critical risk factors for relapse in ETV6::RUNX1–positive pediatric ALL and highlights the adverse prognostic impact of early relapse. Importantly, the persistent detection of ETV6::RUNX1 fusion transcript at initial minimal residual disease (MRD) evaluation emerged as a novel and independent biomarker for early risk stratification. These findings may guide refinement of individualized therapeutic strategies to improve long-term outcomes in this pediatric subgroup.

Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most common pediatric malignancy, constituting approximately 20–30% of all childhood cancers and a leading cause of cancer-related death in children [1, 2]. Significant progress in risk-adapted multi-agent chemotherapy protocols and genetic profiling have dramatically improved the OS rate of pediatric ALL, from less than 10% in the 1960s to over 90% in recent decades [3, 4]. Among various genetic subtypes, the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion, present in around 25% of pediatric B-cell ALL, is associated with favorable clinical outcomes and high chemo-sensitivity [5–7]. However, a characteristic clinical challenge of ETV6::RUNX1-positive ALL is its propensity for late relapse, with a substantial proportion of relapse events occurring beyond five years from initial diagnosis, which complicates long-term disease management [8, 9].

Recently, the contemporary clinical management of pediatric ALL has been reshaped by precise diagnostic methodologies. Techniques such as advanced cytogenetics, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and PCR-based approaches have facilitated accurate genetic stratification, enabling the delivery of tailored, risk-adapted therapies [10–12]. Furthermore, highly sensitive minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring techniques, such as flow cytometry and high-throughput sequencing, have emerged as critical tools for early detection of residual leukemia and effective treatment adjustments [13, 14]. Despite these substantial advances, significant clinical challenges persist. Crucially, robust early predictive markers for relapse, particularly late relapse in ETV6::RUNX1-positive patients, remain elusive. Genomic studies reveal that relapses often arise from therapy-resistant leukemic subclones present at diagnosis, harboring secondary mutations such as deletions in glucocorticoid signaling pathways (e.g., NR3C1, BMF) which confer chemoresistance and survival advantages [15, 16]. However, the precise biological mechanisms driving late relapse and effective clinical predictors of late relapse remain incompletely understood, highlighting critical gaps that hinder improved therapeutic stratification and outcomes in pediatric ALL.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed clinical data from 256 pediatric patients with ETV6::RUNX1-positive ALL treated at our center. The primary objectives were to identify clinical and molecular predictors of relapse, and to evaluate factors associated with post-relapse survival outcomes. We aimed to provide evidence that may contribute to improved risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making in this specific pediatric ALL subgroup.

Patients and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (Approval No. 2023CS098). Written informed consent was obtained from the guardians of all enrolled pediatric patients prior to the initiation of any therapeutic procedures.

We retrospectively analyzed clinical data from pediatric patients diagnosed with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) harboring the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion gene, who received treatment at the Children’s Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University between May 2008 and August 2020. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged > 1 year and < 18 years at diagnosis; (2) diagnosis of B-ALL confirmed according to MICM classification [17] ; (3) positive ETV6::RUNX1 fusion gene detected by reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and/or FISH, with specific application based on clinical indications [18] ; and (4) treatment administered according to the Chinese Children’s Leukemia Group (CCLG)-ALL-2008 or Chinese Children’s Cancer Group (CCCG)-ALL-2015 protocols [19, 20]. All patients meeting the above inclusion criteria were enrolled in this study, and no additional exclusion criteria were applied. None of the ETV6::RUNX1-positive ALL patients identified during the study period were excluded from the analysis.

Diagnostic assessments included: (1) Morphological examination: Fresh bone marrow samples collected at diagnosis via bone marrow aspiration under general anesthesia were immediately smeared, stained, and classified according to standard French-American-British (FAB) criteria [21]. (2) Immunophenotyping: Fresh heparin-anticoagulated bone marrow samples were collected at diagnosis. Nucleated cells were isolated routinely. Immunophenotyping was performed according to previously described methods [22]. Briefly, monoclonal antibodies specific for cytoplasmic antigens (cCD79a, CD34, CD45, CD58, CD123, and HLA-DR) and membrane antigens were used to define leukemia cell populations. Positivity thresholds were set at ≥ 10% expression for cytoplasmic antigens and ≥ 20% for membrane-bound antigens. (3) Cytogenetic analysis: Standard chromosomal karyotyping was performed on freshly collected bone marrow samples from newly diagnosed patients using direct bone marrow culture or a 24-hour culture followed by routine R-banding techniques [23]. A minimum of 20 metaphases were analyzed per sample. Normal karyotype was defined as 46, XX or 46, XY without clonal abnormalities; abnormal karyotype included any numerical or structural chromosomal aberration. Recurrent secondary abnormalities reported in ETV6::RUNX1-positive ALL, including deletion of the non-rearranged ETV6 allele, del(6q), del(9p), and extra copies of chromosome 21, were documented when detected [24, 25]. FISH was used to confirm the t(12;21) (p13;q22) translocation. (4) Molecular biology: Fusion gene detection was conducted using nested RT-PCR and FISH methods to confirm the presence of the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion gene. (5) MRD assessment: MRD was evaluated at two standardized time points—the first at the end of induction therapy (day 33) and the second at the end of consolidation therapy (week 12). MRD was measured using multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC) with a sensitivity of 10⁻⁴ (0.01%), and MRD negativity was defined as < 10⁻⁴. Additionally, the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion transcript was monitored by quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) at the same time points. The qRT-PCR assay used ABL1 as the reference gene, with a sensitivity of 10⁻⁵. ETV6::RUNX1 positivity was defined as a detectable fusion transcript with ETV6::RUNX1/ABL1 ratio ≥ 10⁻⁵, while negativity was defined as an undetectable transcript or ratio < 10⁻⁵. Importantly, the MRD detection methods, including MFC panels, sensitivity thresholds, and positivity criteria, remained consistent throughout the entire study period (2008–2020), ensuring comparability of MRD-related analyses across different treatment eras.

Risk stratification criteria

Risk stratification criteria differed between the two protocols. In the CCLG-ALL-2008 protocol, patients were stratified based on clinical features and early treatment response [26]. Standard-risk criteria included age 1–10 years, WBC < 50 × 10⁹/L, good prednisone response (day 8 blasts < 1 × 10⁹/L), and MRD < 10⁻⁴ on day 33. High-risk criteria included poor prednisone response, failure to achieve complete remission, MRD ≥ 10⁻² on day 33, or unfavorable genetics (BCR-ABL1, MLL rearrangement, hypodiploidy). In the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol, risk stratification was primarily MRD-guided [27]. Low-risk criteria included age 1–10 years, WBC < 50 × 10⁹/L, favorable genetics (ETV6-RUNX1 or hyperdiploidy), and MRD < 10⁻⁴ on both day 19 and day 46. High-risk criteria included unfavorable genetics, MRD ≥ 10⁻² on day 46, or induction failure. Patients meeting neither low- nor high-risk criteria were classified as intermediate-risk in both protocols.

Patients diagnosed from January 2015 to August 2020 were treated following the Chinese Children’s Cancer Group-ALL2015 protocol (Table 2). The CCCG-ALL2015 protocol was developed based on the Total-XV protocol from St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital, with modifications optimized according to Chinese clinical practice, including adjustments in drug dosages and optimized treatment phases. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and comparisons between groups were performed using independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median with interquartile range [M (P25, P75)] and analyzed using nonparametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages [n (%)] and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Specifically, Pearson’s chi-square test was applied when the total sample size (N) was greater than 40 and theoretical frequency (T) was greater than 5; continuity correction chi-square test was used when N > 40 and 1 < T < 5; Fisher’s exact test was utilized when N ≤ 40 or T < 1. Multivariate analysis was conducted using binary logistic regression. Collinearity among variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF values below 5 considered acceptable. To address potential overfitting due to a limited events-per-variable ratio, forward stepwise logistic regression (entry criterion P < 0.05, removal criterion P > 0.10) was performed as a sensitivity analysis. Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier curves, and comparisons between groups were assessed with the Log-rank test. Factors with statistical significance (P < 0.05) in univariate survival analysis were included in the Cox proportional hazards regression model for multivariate analysis. Statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

We analyzed the clinical characteristics and treatment outcomes of 256 pediatric patients newly diagnosed with ETV6::RUNX1-positive B-ALL. Among these, 142 (55.47%) were male, and 114 (44.53%) were female, with a median age of 4.5 years (interquartile range (IQR): 3.5–5.8 years). Treatment protocols included the 2008 protocol for 87 patients (39.45%) and the 2015 protocol for 169 patients (60.56%). At initial diagnosis, the median white blood cell (WBC) count was 8.54 × 10⁹/L (IQR: 4.79–23.41 × 10⁹/L). Patients were classified as low-risk (73.6%), intermediate-risk (18.2%), or high-risk (8.2%). With a median follow-up of 79.0 months (range: 6–188 months), the 5-year event-free survival (EFS) and OS for the entire cohort were 84.8 ± 2.3% and 89.8 ± 1.9%, respectively. A total of 36 patients (14.1%) experienced relapse during the follow-up period.

We further analyzed characteristics associated with recurrence in a subset of 36 patients who experienced relapse compared to 220 who did not. No significant differences in age or sex were found between these two groups. Notably, patients treated with the 2008 protocol had a significantly higher recurrence rate than those treated with the 2015 protocol (61.1% vs. 38.9%, P < 0.01). Patients in the recurrence group exhibited significantly higher median initial WBC counts compared to the non-recurrence group (14.32 × 10⁹/L [IQR: 4.95–42.19 × 10⁹/L] vs. 8.26 × 10⁹/L [IQR: 4.72–20.85 × 10⁹/L], P < 0.05). Furthermore, a significantly higher proportion of high-risk cases (66.6% vs. 30.0%, P < 0.001) and chromosomal abnormalities (25.8% vs. 11.1%, P < 0.05) were observed in the recurrence group (Table 3).

Analysis of treatment responses revealed significantly lower glucocorticoid sensitivity in the recurrence group (55.6%) compared with the non-recurrence group (73.2%; P = 0.031). MRD negativity (< 1 × 10⁻⁴) at the first evaluation was significantly lower in the recurrence group compared with the non-recurrence group (2.9% vs. 37.7%; P < 0.001), a pattern also observed at the second evaluation (40.0% vs. 78.3%; P < 0.001). Similarly, clearance of the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion gene was significantly less frequent in the recurrence group at both the first (3.3% vs. 54.5%; P < 0.001) and second evaluations (35.5% vs. 81.3%; P < 0.001) (Table 4). Finally, analysis of B-cell antigen expression revealed significantly elevated CD19 (P < 0.01) and cCD79a (P < 0.05) in the recurrence group compared with the non-recurrence group, while no significant differences were observed in CD10, CD20, CD38, or HLA-DR expression levels (Table 5). Additionally, no significant differences were found in non-specific B-cell antigen expression between the two groups (Table 6).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis, including variables significantly associated with recurrence (treatment protocol, initial WBC count, CD19, cCD79a, glucocorticoid response, risk stratification, chromosomal abnormalities, MRD levels at first and second evaluations, and ETV6::RUNX1 status), was performed. Collinearity diagnostics revealed that all VIF values were below 5 (range: 1.15–2.15), indicating no significant multicollinearity among the variables. The analysis showed that only initial WBC count (OR = 1.02 per 1 × 10⁹/L increase, 95% CI: 1–1.04; P = 0.05), glucocorticoid insensitivity (OR: 3.73; 95% CI: 1.04–13.41; P = 0.04), and positive ETV6::RUNX1 status at first evaluation (OR: 13.89; 95% CI: 1.68–40.90; P = 0.01) were independent predictors of recurrence (Fig. 1). Given the relatively low events-per-variable ratio (36 events with 11 variables) in the full model, forward stepwise logistic regression was performed as a sensitivity analysis. This parsimonious model retained only the three significant predictors: positive ETV6::RUNX1 status at first evaluation (OR = 13.80; 95% CI: 2.58–42.15; P = 0.01), glucocorticoid insensitivity (OR = 3.60; 95% CI: 1.18–10.85; P = 0.03), and initial WBC count (OR = 1.02 per 1 × 10⁹/L increase; 95% CI: 1.001–1.048; P = 0.04) (Fig. 2). The stepwise model yielded an improved events-per-variable ratio of 12:1, and the consistent identification of these three predictors across both analytical approaches supports the robustness of our findings.

Among the 36 relapsed patients, 14 (38.9%) underwent allogeneic HSCT following reinduction chemotherapy. According to international guidelines for relapsed childhood ALL, indications for HSCT in second complete remission (CR2) are stratified based on relapse timing, site, and MRD response: (1) early bone marrow relapse (< 36 months from diagnosis), (2) late bone marrow relapse with inadequate MRD response (MRD ≥ 10⁻³ after reinduction), and (3) combined bone marrow and extramedullary relapse [28, 29]. In our cohort, 13 of 14 (92.9%) transplanted patients had bone marrow involvement (9 isolated bone marrow relapse, 4 combined relapse). Among these, 5 patients had early relapse, which itself constitutes an HSCT indication, while the remaining 9 patients with late relapse underwent HSCT primarily due to bone marrow involvement and/or inadequate MRD clearance after reinduction therapy.

Subgroup analysis revealed that both relapse timing and post-relapse HSCT significantly impacted survival outcomes. Early relapse (< 36 months post-diagnosis) was associated with significantly reduced 5-year OS (17.6 ± 10.2% vs. 55.6 ± 13.2%, P = 0.006) and EFS (19.4 ± 11.3% vs. 35.8 ± 14.0%, P = 0.017). Patients receiving HSCT after relapse had significantly better 5-year OS (56.6 ± 16.1% vs. 23.3 ± 11.0%, P = 0.023) and EFS (44.6 ± 7.0% vs. 15.2 ± 9.3%, P = 0.011) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Our study identified several critical risk factors associated with relapse and post-relapse survival in pediatric patients with ETV6::RUNX1-positive ALL. Specifically, a high initial WBC count (≥ 50 × 10⁹/L), poor steroid response at day 7, and persistence of the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion transcript at the first post-therapy assessment were independent predictors of relapse. Moreover, patients who experienced early relapse (< 36 months from initial diagnosis) or who did not undergo HSCT post-relapse had significantly inferior survival outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of early risk stratification based on initial clinical and molecular profiles, as well as post-relapse treatment intensification, to improve outcomes in this genetic subgroup.

Our findings are consistent with previous large-scale international studies that have established prognostic significance of initial leukocyte counts and glucocorticoid responsiveness. Elevated initial WBC counts often indicate a high leukemia cell burden and rapid proliferation, contributing to increased relapse risk and poorer clinical outcomes [18, 30]. In our multivariable analysis, WBC count was analyzed as a continuous variable to maximize statistical power. For clinical interpretation, we applied the widely accepted threshold of ≥ 50 × 10⁹/L, which is based on the National Cancer Institute (NCI) risk classification criteria and has been validated in numerous international collaborative studies. Similarly, early steroid response is recognized as a crucial prognostic indicator, reflecting intrinsic chemoresistance in leukemic cells [31]. Recent genomic analyses, including studies by Kuster et al. [16], demonstrated that relapse clones in ETV6::RUNX1-positive ALL frequently harbor deletions or mutations in glucocorticoid signaling pathway genes such as NR3C1 and BMF, which confer steroid resistance and survival advantages [32], thus explaining the clinical correlation between poor steroid response and higher relapse risk.

Additionally, our results confirmed the prognostic value of MRD monitoring in pediatric ALL. Persistent positivity of the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion transcript at early post-treatment assessments is a strong predictor of relapse, aligning with MRD’s established role as one of the most powerful relapse predictors [33, 34]. Furthermore, relapse timing critically impacted survival outcomes; Gandemer et al. [35] similarly observed significantly better survival rates in patients experiencing late relapse compared to early relapse cases. Our data further highlight the therapeutic importance of HSCT for improving survival in early relapse patients, consistent with international guidelines advocating for timely HSCT to enhance long-term remission rates in high-risk relapsed pediatric ALL cases [36].

Notable differences were observed between our findings and some prior international studies. For instance, our cohort exhibited higher incidence of early relapse compared to studies conducted in European and North American populations, despite receiving comparable treatment protocols [37]. This discrepancy may be attributed to genetic and ethnic differences affecting drug metabolism and treatment response. Variations in supportive care infrastructure and access to novel therapeutic agents may also contribute to these disparities. Moreover, the relatively constrained application of novel immunotherapies and targeted agents in our cohort compared to certain Western centers may partially explain differences in relapse patterns and long-term survival [38]. These findings underline the need to tailor treatment strategies not only based on disease biology but also on population-specific factors.

Our study supports the hypothesis that MRD persistence reflects therapy-resistant leukemic clones. The persistence of ETV6::RUNX1 fusion transcripts likely indicates subclonal populations with acquired resistance mechanisms, including alterations in apoptotic pathways and chemoresistance-associated gene networks [39–41]. For example, glucocorticoid resistance observed in early relapse patients aligns with genomic data showing NR3C1 mutations and decreased expression of pro-apoptotic genes, resulting in impaired therapy-induced cell death. These mechanistic insights not only explain the observed prognostic differences but also reveal potential targets for novel therapies, such as combining epigenetic modulators with standard induction regimens to enhance initial treatment responses.

This study provides valuable insights. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. As a retrospective, single-center analysis, our findings are subject to selection biases and incomplete data retrieval. In addition, the modest sample size may constrain the generalizability of our conclusions. The relatively limited number of relapse events (n = 36) in relation to the variables examined in our multivariable model warrants cautious interpretation of the regression estimates. However, several analytical approaches strengthen confidence in our findings: collinearity diagnostics confirmed the independence of predictor variables (all VIF values < 5), and sensitivity analysis using forward stepwise regression yielded a parsimonious model that retained only three robust predictors with an adequate events-per-variable ratio. Importantly, these identified risk factors align with established biological rationales and prior clinical evidence, reinforcing the validity of our conclusions. Furthermore, the absence of paired diagnostic and relapse specimens with comprehensive genomic profiling precluded analysis of clonal evolution patterns that may underlie treatment resistance and disease recurrence. Future research should focus on multicenter collaborations with larger patient cohorts and greater ethnic and regional diversity. Prospective studies incorporating systematic genomic profiling at diagnosis and relapse would help validate our findings and elucidate the biological mechanisms of relapse in ETV6::RUNX1-positive ALL.

Conclusion

High initial WBC count, poor steroid response, and persistence of the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion transcript at first MRD assessment are pivotal predictors of relapse in pediatric patients with ETV6::RUNX1-positive ALL. Early relapse is associated with significantly inferior survival outcomes, while timely HSCT markedly enhances long-term survival rates. These findings offer practical guidance for early risk stratification and tailored therapeutic interventions.
