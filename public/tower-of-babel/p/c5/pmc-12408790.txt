Efficient secretory expression of type III recombinant human collagen with triple-helical structure in Komagataella phaffii
Authors: Yaqian Ma, Yang Li, Nan Wang, Chenxiao Han, Qisheng Liu, Liqin Sun, Zhuqing Ma, Hailing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Recombinant human collagen (rhCol) holds broad potential in biomedical and industrial applications due to its high purity and low immunogenicity. However, large-scale production of structurally stable and functionally active rhCol remains challenging. A novel strategy integrating collagen sequence optimization and microbial prolyl-4-hydroxylase (P4H) screening was developed to enable efficient production of triple-helical rhCol in Komagataella phaffii. Five Type III collagen variants (ColP1 ~ ColP5) were rationally designed based on interchain salt-bridge engineering to improve structural stability and biological activity, with ColP2 showing superior expression and functionality. A systematic evaluation of four microbial P4Hs identified Bacillus megaterium P4H (BmP4H) as the most effective catalyst for proline hydroxylation, enabling stable triple-helix formation. Combined with strain optimization, promoter and signal peptide screening, and 5-L scale fermentation, this approach achieved a high rhCol yield of 2.54 g/L with confirmed triple-helical structure. These results demonstrate an integrated and scalable platform for high-level production of functional recombinant collagen, providing a promising foundation for its industrial and clinical applications. Key Points• Co-expression of BmP4H enables stable triple-helical collagen in yeast.• Strain X-33, promoter PAOX1, and a-factor leader optimize collagen secretion.• Scale-up in 5L bioreactor achieves 2.54 g/L rhCol production. Introduction

Collagen is a major structural component of the extracellular matrix and the most abundant protein in the human body, accounting for nearly one-third of total protein. In recent years, recombinant human collagen (rhCol) has attracted growing attention due to its controllable composition, low immunogenicity, and excellent biocompatibility. Compared to animal-derived collagen, rhCol offers superior batch-to-batch consistency, enhanced solubility, and eliminates the risk of pathogen transmission (Guo et al. 2024). It is typically produced by genetically engineering key functional domains of human collagen and has been widely applied in various biomedical fields. Most of the studies cited here focus on rhCol in general, without specifying collagen subtypes (Samantha Lo and Fauzi 2021; Kong et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024, 2025; Revert-Ros et al. 2024). However, a subset of recent studies has specifically examined recombinant type III collagen (rhCol III) for its therapeutic and regenerative potential (Ye et al. 2024; Wang 2021; Yang et al. 2021; Cao et al. 2024). Recent studies have shown that rhCol can promote skin regeneration, inhibit melanin production, and enhance endogenous collagen synthesis. Additionally, it can function as a vehicle (e.g., hydrogels and scaffolds) to deliver drugs or bioactive agents to specific tissues (Huo et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2023). In the fields of periodontal and corneal stromal regeneration, rhCol has also demonstrated promising therapeutic potential (Kong et al. 2022; Yoshino et al. 2023; Qu et al. 2024).

The structural and functional integrity of rhCol depends on its ability to form a stable triple-helical conformation, a hallmark of native collagen. This structure consists of three α-chains twisted into a right-handed triple helix, based on a repeating Gly–X–Y motif in which proline (Pro) and hydroxyproline (Hyp) are frequently found at the X and Y positions, respectively (Ramachandran and Kartha 1955; Hua et al. 2019). Among them, Hyp plays a pivotal role in stabilizing the triple-helical conformation by forming a robust hydrogen bonding network between the chains. Its absence significantly compromises structural stability at physiological temperatures. A key breakthrough came from Vuorela et al., who first demonstrated that co-expression of human collagen III with human prolyl 4-hydroxylase in Komagataella phaffii (K. phaffii) enabled the formation of stable triple-helical collagen (Vuorela et al. 1997). This foundational study established the feasibility of using yeast as a host for producing structurally authentic recombinant human collagen. Type III collagen expressed in E. coli by Yan et al. formed triple helices and gelled only at low temperatures, likely due to the lack of Hyp (Yan et al. 2024). Similarly, in another study using K. phaffii to express the COL3α1 fragment, structural features were detected by CD spectroscopy but failed to detect the characteristic triple-helix signature (Yan et al. 2025). To overcome this limitation, Xu et al. co-expressed human P4H with full-length COL3A1 in K. phaffii under both inducible and constitutive promoters, demonstrating the importance of appropriate expression strategies in achieving hydroxylation and triple-helix formation. Notably, the constitutive system yielded higher Hyp levels (up to 9.08%), although protein yield was not quantified (Xu et al. 2015). In a more recent study, Fang et al. employed viral P4H (A085R from PBCV-1) in a similar yeast system, achieving stable expression and a secretion yield of up to 0.7 mg/mL of hydroxylated rhCOL3A1 after 120 h of induction (Fang et al. 2023). However, neither of these studies incorporated rational protein design strategies at the sequence level; instead, both relied on co-expression of P4H systems and conventional optimization techniques. Recent studies have explored in silico design of collagen sequences using machine learning or structural modeling (Khare et al. 2022; Perumal et al. 2008) but did not proceed to recombinant expression or secretion validation. Although human P4H has shown functionality, it is a heterotetrameric enzyme (α2β2) that is difficult to express and fold properly in yeast. Microbial-derived P4Hs, in contrast, are typically single-subunit and more soluble, making them more suitable for yeast-based systems. In this study, four microbial P4Hs from different species—Alteromonas mediterranea (AlP4H), Bacillus anthracis (BaP4H), Bacillus megaterium (BmP4H), and Dactylonectria alcacerensis (DaP4H)—were selected based on prior reports of functionality in heterologous systems and co-expressed with ColP2 to assess their ability to promote triple-helix formation (Wang et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2023; Gong et al. 2023). These findings highlight a major challenge in rhCol production: ensuring sufficient Hyp incorporation to support triple-helix assembly, which is essential for its biomedical functionality.

Rational design of recombinant collagen sequences has become a critical strategy to enhance structural stability, guide chain specificity, and optimize biosynthetic efficiency. One major approach involves engineering electrostatic interactions, such as introducing interchain salt bridges at defined X and Y positions within Gly-X–Y repeats to stabilize the triple-helix conformation. For instance, Zheng et al. reported that rationally designed complementary electrostatic networks can significantly improve heterotrimeric collagen assembly and identified the energetic hierarchy of interchain salt bridges in atomic-resolution structures (Zheng et al. 2018). Building on this, Sun et al. demonstrated that complex salt bridges, especially those involving three charged residues, exhibit composition-dependent stability contributions and can be quantitatively predicted using empirical models integrating molecular dynamics and CD spectroscopy data (Sun et al. 2021). These studies underscore the potential of sequence-level rational design to improve both the thermodynamic stability and assembly fidelity of synthetic collagen constructs, offering valuable frameworks for functional rhCol engineering.

K. phaffii is a widely used host for heterologous protein expression, offering both high-yield fermentation capabilities and eukaryotic-like post-translational modifications (Karbalaei et al. 2020; Barone et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2023). These features make it a promising platform for the production of recombinant human collagen, as it allows scalable fermentation and supports certain post-translational processes necessary for functional protein folding. The expression efficiency of exogenous proteins in K. phaffii can vary substantially across different genotypes. Among them, strains X-33 and GS115 are widely used for recombinant protein production due to their robust growth and high expression capacity (James M. Cregg et al. 2000). In contrast, SMD1168 and SMD1163 have been engineered with mutations in vacuolar protease genes, which enhance their genetic manipulability and improve the stability of heterologous proteins (M. A. Gleeson et al. 1998). KM71, characterized by a modified methanol metabolism pathway, exhibits limited capacity for direct methanol utilization. However, its distinctive metabolic profile renders it valuable for specific bioprocess applications that require controlled induction conditions (James M. Cregg et al. 2000). For example, Tian et al. achieved a titer of 424.3 mg/L of human lactoferrin in strain GS115 by optimizing promoters, signal peptides, co-expression of chaperones, and protease deletions in a 5-L bioreactor (Tian et al. 2025). Similarly, Lv et al. expressed recombinant bovine lactoferrin fused with antioxidant peptides in strain X-33, reaching 193.9 mg/L in a 3-L bioreactor (Lv et al. 2024). Additionally, Zhao et al. and Huo et al. reported successful expression of antimicrobial peptides LIG and I20, respectively, with notable titers and demonstrated bioactivity and biocompatibility (Huo et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2024). Promoter and signal peptide optimization—two key factors for secretory expression—is a proven strategy to boost recombinant protein production in K. phaffii (Fubao Fuelbiol Sun et al. 2016; Bohan Zhang et al. 2021). Signal peptides, short N-terminal sequences guiding proteins into the secretory pathway, have been extensively studied due to their significant impact on secretion efficiency (G von Heijne 1983). Beyond widely used heterologous signal peptides such as α-factor, PHO1, and SUC2 (Li et al. 2002; Mouillon and Persson 2006; Barrero et al. 2018), endogenous signal peptides like PDI1, UTR2, DSE4, and EXG1 have also shown promise (Liang et al. 2013; Massahi and Çalık 2016). Furthermore, hybrid signal peptide designs that fuse native and heterologous elements—such as PDI1α, UTR2α, DSE4α, EXG1α, SCW11α, SP24α, and PHO5α—have emerged as a flexible strategy to enhance secretory expression (Liang et al. 2013; Massahi and Çalık 2016).

However, a key limitation of K. phaffii is the absence of endogenous prolyl 4-hydroxylase (P4H), an enzyme essential for the hydroxylation of proline residues into hydroxyproline (Hyp). Hyp is critical for stabilizing the collagen triple helix through interchain hydrogen bonding, and its absence often results in incomplete or unstable helix formation. Therefore, co-expression of functional P4H is necessary when expressing collagen in this system. Notably, Myllyharju et al. successfully expressed functional rhCol types I–III in K. phaffii with proper triple-helical conformation by co-expressing prolyl 4-hydroxylase (Myllyharju et al. 2000). Our study builds upon this strategy and aims to optimize the system specifically for rhCol III, with improved expression levels and structural integrity.

To address the challenges associated with low yield and improper folding of garnered increasing attention, this study aimed to establish a robust expression system in K. phaffii for the production of structurally stable and biologically functional rhCol. By integrating rational protein design, host strain selection, promoter and signal peptide optimization, and co-expression with prolyl-4-hydroxylase (P4H), a scalable strategy was developed to achieve efficient synthesis of Type III rhCol variants. Among the engineered constructs, ColP2 demonstrated the highest expression level and was selected for further optimization and scale-up. The resulting recombinant collagen exhibited a stable triple-helical structure and high production yield, highlighting the potential of this platform for industrial and biomedical applications.

Materials and methods

Bacterial and yeast strains, plasmids, media, and growth conditions

The cloning process was conducted using E. coli DH5α, while the expression was performed utilizing K. phaffii X-33. The strains and plasmids used are listed in Tables 1 and 2, and the primers used are listed in Table S1. E. coli was cultured in Luria–Bertani medium (10 g/L tryptone,5 g/L yeast extract, and 5 g/L NaCl) at 37 °C, and K. phaffii was cultured in yeast extract peptone dextrose medium (20 g/L tryptone,10 g/L yeast extract, and 20 g/L Glucose) at 30 °C. During the shake flask fermentation of K. phaffii, Buffered Glycerol-complex Medium (tryptone 20.0 g/L, yeast extract 10.0 g/L, biotin. 4.0 × 10−4 g/L, YNB 13.4 g/L, glycerol 10.0 g/L, KH2PO4 11.8 g/L, K2HPO4 3.0 g/L) and buffered methanol-complex medium (tryptone 20.0 g/L, yeast extract 10.0 g/L, biotin. 4.0 × 10−4 g/L, YNB 13.4 g/L, methanol 2% (v/v), KH2PO4 11.8 g 2%, and K2HPO4 3.0 g/L) are employed. Bleomycin (50 μg/mL) and kanamycin (50 μg/mL) were used for selection in E. coli; bleomycin (100 μg/mL) and geneticin (100–3200 μg/mL) were used for selection in K. phaffii. Protein expression and purification

Hexahistidine (6 × His) was added to the C-terminal of the recombinant collagen ColP2 gene and the four hydroxylases AlP4H, BaP4H, BmP4H, and DaP4H genes, respectively. The constructed recombinant plasmid was amplified and expressed in E. coli DH5α and was electrotransformed into K. phaffii after being verified correctly. After the colonies were verified correctly by PCR and sequencing, flask fermentation was carried out.

Single colony was selected from the successfully transformed K. phaffii strains and inoculated into a 250-mL triangular bottle containing 50 mL YPD medium at 30 °C and 250 rpm overnight to obtain primary seed liquid. The primary seed liquid was transferred to a 250-mL flask containing 50 mL BMGY medium at 2% (v/v) and cultured at 30 °C and 250 rpm to OD6₀₀ up to 30 (about 16 ~ 18 h). The yeast were centrifuged at room temperature at 4000 rpm for 5 min, washed with sterile water three times to remove the remaining glycerol, and then the cells were suspended with 50 mL BMMY and cultured at 250 rpm in a 250-mL triangle bottle at 30 °C. The pure methanol was increased to the final concentration of 1% (v/v), and the growth continued according to the above conditions, and the methanol was added every 24 h to make the induction continue. One milliliter fermentation liquor solution was taken every 24 h to analyze the expression level and determine the optimal induction time, and the results were tested using SDS-PAGE.

After methanol-induced expression, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, resuspended in buffer A (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 6.0 and 500 mM NaCl), and lysed at a low temperature using a high-pressure homogenizer. After centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 45 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was loaded onto an ÄKTA nickel column at a flow rate of 5 mL/min, and the protein peaks were collected by washing and elution with different ratios of buffers A and B (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 6.0, 500 M NaCl, and 500 mM imidazole).

Detection of protein production

After purification of the target protein, the content of the protein was determined by BCA protein concentration detection kit of Biyuntian. The 20 mg/mL Bovine Serum Albumin standard was diluted to gradient concentration (0, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 mg/mL) using PBS, and the working solution was prepared according to reagent A:B = 50:1 (ready for use). After centrifugation, the samples to be tested were discarded and precipitated. Twenty microliter samples (high concentration samples should be diluted to the linear range) and standard products were added to the microplate, and 200 μL working liquid was added to each well. After mixing, the samples were incubated at 60 °C for 30 min in the dark. The OD562 value was read by the enzyme marker, and the concentration was calculated by linear equation after drawing a standard curve (R2 ≥ 0.99). The working liquid of the kit is stable for 2 h at room temperature and stored unopened at 4 °C away from light.

Protein characterization

Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China), based on the reduction of Cu2⁺ ions and colorimetric detection. A BSA standard (20.00 mg/mL) was diluted in PBS to generate a concentration gradient ranging from 0.00 to 0.50 mg/mL. The working reagent was freshly prepared at a 50:1 ratio of reagents A to B. After centrifugation, 20 μL of each sample or standard was mixed with 200 μL of working solution in a 96-well plate and incubated at 60 °C in the dark for 30 min. OD562 was measured using a microplate reader, and protein concentration was calculated using a linear standard curve (R2 ≥ 0.99).

To analyze protein expression, culture supernatants were harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 × g, 4 °C for 10 min. For intracellular proteins, cells were washed with deionized water and lysed using a high-pressure homogenizer (100 ~ 250 MPa, 3 ~ 5 cycles). After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, mixed with loading buffer, and boiled for 10 min at 100 °C. Proteins were separated using 12% SDS-PAGE at 120 V for 60 min and visualized by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining.

For Western blotting, proteins resolved by SDS-PAGE were transferred to PVDF membranes using semi-dry transfer. Membranes were blocked at room temperature, incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody on ice for 1 h. Signals were developed with chemiluminescent substrates and visualized using a gel documentation system.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was used to assess secondary structure. Purified recombinant collagen was analyzed in the far-UV range (190 ~ 260 nm) using a J-1500 CD spectropolarimeter (JASCO, Japan) at a scan rate of 50 nm/min. Characteristic peaks at ~ 220 nm (positive) and ~ 198 nm (negative) were used as indicators of triple-helical structure.

Fed-batch fermentation in bioreactor

K. phaffii X-33 harboring the type III recombinant collagen ColP2 gene under the control of the PAOXS1 promoter and bleomycin resistance marker was used as the host for fed-batch fermentation in a 5-L DIBELL BIOTECH precision fermenter. Key parameters were as follows: temperature 30 ± 0.5 °C (growth and secretion inhibited above 32 °C); DO ≥ 20% with kLa ≥ 200 h−1; pH maintained at 5.0 to inhibit protease activity during methanol metabolism. Stirring ranged from 500 to 1500 rpm to balance oxygen demand and methanol volatilization; silicone oil-based defoamer < 0.1% (v/v) stabilized the gas–liquid interface. A two-stage feedback control (stirring priority, oxygen < 30%) regulated pH and DO. For seed culture, a single colony from YPD agar (100 μg/mL bleomycin) was expanded. In the glycerol phase, 1 L BMGY (2% v/v) was inoculated; glycerol depletion (DO ≥ 100% for ≥ 10 min) occurred after ~ 24 h. Feeding used 50% glycerol + 12 mL/L PTM1; target biomass: 180–220 g/L (wet cell weight). Methanol induction included a 0–4 h adaptation (3.6 mL/h/L) and 4–35 h expression (10.9 mL/h/L). Key parameters were recorded online; fermentation ceased at 35 h or under abnormal conditions (DO < 10% > 30 min, pH loss, or autolysis).

Results

Sequence design and heterologous expression of rhCol

To establish an efficient production pipeline for rhCol, a systematic strategy was implemented (Fig. 1a), including functional sequence design, host strain screening, and expression optimization, post-translational modification enhancement, fermentation process refinement, and final verification and scale-up. These steps were designed to ensure both the biosynthetic efficiency and the functional integrity of the target protein. To ensure the structural stability and biological activity of recombinant collagen, the design strategy was based on established principles including sequence solubility, triple-helix stability, codon optimization, and removal of degradation-prone sites. Further refinements were introduced by considering salt bridge interactions, overall protein size, triple-helix formation, and the retention of integrin-binding motifs such as RGD and GFOGER. Salt bridges between charged residues were analyzed to improve thermal stability and resistance to enzymatic degradation, while integrin-binding sites were preserved to maintain cell-interactive functions. Based on these criteria, five Type III collagen variants (ColP1 ~ ColP5) were rationally designed using selected (GXY)6 repeat units. The recombinant human collagen III sequences designed in this study are shown in Table S2-a, and a 6 × His tag was added at the C-terminus to facilitate purification.

Based on comprehensive design principles encompassing structural stability, bioactivity, solubility, and resistance to degradation, five human type III collagen variants (ColP1–ColP5) were rationally engineered. These variants were constructed by incorporating (GXY)6 repeat units to enhance triple helix formation, retaining the integrin-binding GFOGER motif to preserve biological function, and introducing charged residues to strengthen salt bridge interactions and thereby improve molecular stability. To verify whether the five rhCols could be expressed, codon optimization was first performed on the protein sequences. The optimized DNA sequences were then ligated into the integrative plasmid pPICZαA-PpHIS4, which was constructed by integrating the HIS locus in pPICZαA. Five recombinant expression plasmids, pPICZαA-COLP1 ~ 5, were obtained, fully retaining key functional elements, including the strong constitutive promoter PGAP, α-factor signal peptide, and Zeocin resistance selection marker (Fig. 1b). After linearization, the plasmids were transformed into K. phaffii X-33, resulting in genomic integration and the generation of recombinant strains X-33/ColP1 ~ P5. Shake flask fermentation was performed using both the recombinant strains and the control strain. The SDS-PAGE analysis of the fermentation supernatant revealed distinct protein bands in X-33/ColP2 and X-33/ColP5, which were absent in the control strain (Fig. 1c). The observed molecular weights of 65 kDa and 80 kDa were consistent with the predicted values. Specifically, the observed molecular weight of ColP2 (~ 65 kDa) is lower than that of the full-length human type III collagen monomer (~ 95 kDa), which is expected because ColP2 was designed as a functional fragment containing only a portion of the triple-helical domain, rather than the entire collagen sequence. Subsequent Western blot analysis further confirmed the successful expression of recombinant proteins ColP2 and ColP5 (Fig. 1d).

Screening of K. phaffii hosts for extracellularly expressing ColP2

To identify an efficient host for ColP2 production, the pPICzαA-ColP2 plasmid was successfully transformed into X-33, GS115, SMD1163, SMD1168, and KM71, resulting in the construction of recombinant strains X-33/ColP2, GS115/ColP2, SMD1163/ColP2, SMD1168/ColP2, and KM71/ColP2. SDS-PAGE analysis of fermentation supernatants was performed to detect the presence of ColP2, and distinct target protein bands were observed in all strains, indicating successful gene integration and extracellular expression (Fig. 2a). After purification and quantification, significant variation in ColP2 titers was observed among the strains. The measured titers were: X-33 (0.20 g/L), GS115 (0.15 g/L), SMD1168 (0.18 g/L), SMD1163 (0.11 g/L), and KM71 (0.10 g/L) (Fig. 2b). X-33 exhibited the highest expression level, considerably exceeding that of the other genotypes. While X-33 and SDM1168 showed similar expression levels (0.20 g/L vs. 0.18 g/L), the small difference lies within the margin of error associated with the BCA assay and is not statistically significant. X-33 was selected as the production host for further optimization due to its more consistent growth performance and fermentation reproducibility.

Optimization of promoters and secretory signal peptides for ColP2 in K. phaffii

To enhance ColP2 yield, four endogenous promoters (PAOX1, PFLD1, PGCW14, and PTEF1) were systematically evaluated (Fig. 3a). Among them, PAOX1 and PFLD1 are inducible (methanol- and methylamine/formate-responsive, respectively), whereas PGCW14 and PTEF1 are strong constitutive promoters suitable for continuous expression. SDS-PAGE and protein quantitative analysis revealed that all tested endogenous promoters were able to express ColP2 (Fig. 3b). PAOX1 reached 0.45 g/L, significantly higher than that of the other tested promoters. The PFLD1 promoter exhibited only 49.2% of the PAOX1 expression level (0.22 g/L), while constitutive promoters PGAP and PTEF1 showed lower performance, reaching only 14.9% and 8.0% of the PAOX1 titer, respectively.

Signal peptides, short N-terminal sequences that direct proteins into the secretory pathway, play a critical role in determining extracellular protein yield in K. phaffii. To identify the most effective signal peptide, the commonly used α-factor was systematically compared with a panel of alternatives, including heterologous, endogenous, and hybrid signal peptides. These comprised α-factor, PHO1, and SUC2 (heterologous); PDI1, UTR2, DSE4, and EXG1 (endogenous); and PDI1α, UTR2α, DSE4α, EXG1α, SCW11α, SP24α, and PHO5α (hybrids) (Fig. 3c). SDS-PAGE analysis revealed differential secretion efficiencies among signal peptides (Fig. 3d). Subsequent quantification demonstrated that α-factor achieved the highest expression titer (Fig. 3e). Relative expression levels further confirmed the superior performance of α-factor over other candidates (Fig. 3f). The experimental results showed that the titers of different signal peptides were as follows: α-factor (0.34 g/L), PHO1 (0.13 g/L), SUC2 (0.10 g/L), PDI1 (0.14 g/L), UTR2 (0.13 g/L), DSE4 (0.12 g/L), EXG1 (0.07 g/L), PDI1α (0.09 g/L), UTR2α (0.17 g/L), DSE4α (0.09 g/L), EXG1α (0.09 g/L), SCW11α (0.24 g/L), SP24α (0.09 g/L), and PHO5α (0.09 g/L).

Co-expression with prolyl-4-hydroxylases facilitates triple-helix formation of ColP2

Based on heterologous activity reports, four microbial P4Hs (from Alteromonas mediterranea, Bacillus anthracis, Bacillus megaterium, and Dactylonectria alcacerensis) were selected and their expression cassettes were integrated into the pPICZα-PAOX1-ColP2 vector (Fig. 4a). Western blotting of intracellular fractions confirmed that BaP4H, BmP4H, and DaP4H were stably expressed (Fig. 4b), with distinct bands at their respective predicted molecular weights (25.4, 25.1, and 29.7 kDa). In contrast, no detectable signal was observed in the AlP4H group. In addition, in the BmP4H group, a secondary band appeared around 60 kDa. In the strain expressing ColP2 alone, no signal was detected in the cell lysate, consistent with complete secretion of the protein into the culture supernatant.

SDS-PAGE analysis of extracellular proteins (Fig. 4c) revealed a major band near 60 kDa across all strains, corresponding to the ColP2 monomer. Notably, in the BmP4H and DaP4H co-expression strains, additional bands appeared near 140 kDa. These high-molecular-weight bands were absent in the control and AlP4H groups. The 140 kDa bands likely represent oligomeric forms of ColP2 (e.g., dimers or trimers), which may result from enhanced interchain interactions following hydroxylation. To verify structural folding, circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was performed using purified ColP2 proteins. The BmP4H co-expression group exhibited a characteristic collagen CD spectrum, with a distinct positive peak near 220 nm and a negative peak around 198 nm (Fig. 4d, e), indicating the presence of a stable triple-helix conformation (Bella 2016; Chen et al. 2025). This spectral signature was not observed in samples lacking P4H or co-expressed with AlP4H or BaP4H.

Discussion

To mimic the functional domains of native type III collagen (Col III) while reducing sequence length and increasing modularity, we selected seven 18-residue collagen-like fragments (Fragments 1–7) containing known integrin-binding motifs such as GFOGER and GASGER. Each fragment included six Gly-X–Y (GXY) repeats and was analyzed for intra- and interchain salt bridge potential based on charged residue statistics. The active fragments selected in this study are shown in Table S2-b. Fragments 1, 3, and 7 were selected as the most promising units based on high salt bridge counts and/or proline content. Using these core motifs, five synthetic peptides (COLP1-COLP5) were designed. All constructs preserve the Gly-X–Y repeat structure typical of collagen helices. The nucleotide sequence of COLP2 is shown in Table S3.

Among the evaluated K. phaffii strains, X-33 demonstrated the highest ColP2 production, substantially surpassing other genotypes, while SMD1168 exhibited relatively high secretion efficiency. The superior performance of X-33 is likely attributable to its enhanced methanol metabolism, more efficient transcriptional regulation mediated by PGAP, and elevated activity within the secretory pathway. Although both GS115 and X-33 are widely used, X-33 showed notably higher ColP2 expression in this study, likely due to better methanol metabolism and secretion capacity. This underscores the importance of host selection in optimizing collagen production. It should be noted that gene copy number was not quantified in this study, and therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that the observed differences in expression levels among strains were influenced by variable gene integration events rather than intrinsic host properties. Future studies should incorporate gene copy number analysis to more accurately interpret strain performance.

Promoter and secretion signal optimization further contributed to the enhancement of ColP2 expression. PAOX1 induced by methanol emerged as the most effective one among the screened promoters, exhibiting high transcriptional activity under controlled induction conditions. In parallel, signal peptide screening revealed that the α-factor signal peptide significantly outperformed other candidates, with a secretion titer of 0.34 g/L, followed by SCW11α (0.24 g/L) and UTR2α (0.17 g/L). The efficacy of α-factor is largely attributed to its bipartite architecture—comprising a pre-region responsible for endoplasmic reticulum targeting and a pro-region that facilitates efficient trafficking through COPII-mediated vesicular transport (Otte and Barlowe 2004; Dancourt and Barlowe 2010). Although certain studies have reported potential limitations in ER translocation efficiency due to the pre-region (Barrero et al. 2018), a fivefold increase in rhCol secretion was achieved with this combination. The primary goal was to establish an effective and generalizable screening strategy for signal peptides, rather than identifying a single optimal peptide.

Post-translational modification, especially proline hydroxylation, is crucial for the correct folding of collagen and the assembly of its triple-helix structure. Among the P4H candidates evaluated, BmP4H demonstrated superior expression and catalytic efficiency in K. phaffii. The superior performance of B. megaterium P4H is likely due to its monomeric structure, high solubility, and better compatibility with the intracellular environment of K. phaffii. Previous studies have demonstrated that stable triple-helical rhCol III can be achieved in K. phaffii through the co-expression of human or viral P4Hs under both inducible and constitutive promoters (Xu et al. 2015; Fang et al. 2023), underscoring the host’s suitability for recombinant collagen production due to its competent secretory machinery and post-translational capabilities. Building on these foundations, our study further explores the use of bacterial-derived P4H, specifically the homodimeric BmP4H from Bacillus megaterium, which avoids the complexity of heterotetrameric human P4H and its reliance on auxiliary subunits. This not only simplified the co-expression system but also significantly enhanced hydroxylation efficiency. Through systematic evaluation of P4H sources, host strains, promoter elements, and secretion signals, we established an efficient expression strategy that enabled high-level rhCol production at 2.54 g/L in 5-L scale fermentation. This highlights the feasibility and scalability of bacterial P4H-based systems as a robust alternative for collagen biosynthesis. Compared to the shake-flask level yield (~ 15 mg/L) reported by Vuorela et al., our result represents a nearly 170-fold improvement and confirms the industrial scalability of bacterial P4H-based collagen production. Co-expression of ColP2 with BmP4H enabled the accumulation of a high-molecular-weight species (~ 140 kDa) corresponding to trimeric, triple-helical collagen. Consistent with the increased folding burden imposed by triple-helix formation, intracellular accumulation of ColP2 was only detected in strains co-expressing functional P4Hs, whereas no significant intracellular retention was observed in strains lacking P4H. In contrast, AlP4H showed limited functionality, likely due to structural instability or degradation under cellular stress. Western blot analysis further confirmed the presence of active hydroxylase in BmP4H- and DaP4H-coexpressing strains, accompanied by partial degradation products, whereas intracellular accumulation of misfolded or unhydroxylated collagen was observed in the absence of functional P4H. These results underscore the indispensable role of proline hydroxylation in stabilizing collagen structure and facilitating efficient secretion.

Structural analysis by far-UV circular dichroism spectroscopy supported these findings, revealing that the BmP4H co-expression strain exhibited the characteristic of well-folded triple-helical collagen. This signal was absent in monomeric forms and in strains lacking sufficient post-translational modification. Interestingly, even in the absence of exogenous prolyl-4-hydroxylase (P4H), ColP2 exhibited a weak but detectable triple-helical signal in 5-L fermentation, suggesting that rational protein design and optimized bioprocess conditions may partially compensate for insufficient hydroxylation. Furthermore, scale-up fermentation in a 5-L bioreactor significantly enhanced folding quality, indicating that both hydroxylase activity and optimized cultivation conditions contribute synergistically to the stabilization of the collagen tertiary structure. Notably, molecular weight subpopulation analysis confirmed that the triple-helix signature was most prominent in the 140 kDa species, which likely represents an oligomeric form (e.g., dimer or trimer) of ColP2. Such oligomerization may enhance interchain stability and promote more complete triple-helix formation. It should be noted that collagen molecules themselves are intrinsically capable of forming triple-helical structures. Rationally designed recombinant collagen fragments, such as ColP2, can adopt stable triple-helix conformations even in the absence of P4H. Although we did not quantify protein abundance, the consistently stronger bands observed for the 140-kDa species in SDS-PAGE and Western blot suggest it may be more prevalent than the 60-kDa monomer, potentially contributing to its stronger circular dichroism signal. In contrast, the lower molecular weight species likely represent misfolded or proteolyzed intermediates with reduced structural integrity.

A systematic strategy was developed to achieve high-level production of functional type III rhCol in K. phaffii. Among five rationally engineered rhCol variants based on interchain salt-bridge stabilization, ColP2 was selected for its superior structural and expression attributes. Through host screening, strain X-33 was identified as the most suitable chassis. Subsequent optimization of expression elements—specifically the PAOX1 in conjunction with the α-factor signal peptide—facilitated high secretion levels in shake-flask cultures (0.45 g/L). Further enhancement through co-expression with BmP4H enabled proper triple-helix formation and elevated the final yield to 2.54 g/L in 5-L fed-batch fermentation. Notably, intracellular accumulation of collagen was only observed in strains co-expressing functional P4Hs, whereas strains lacking P4H showed negligible intracellular retention, suggesting that triple-helix formation delays secretion by increasing folding complexity. Unlike previous studies focusing on limited parameters, this work presents a systematic and modular optimization pipeline covering strain selection, expression element screening, P4H source evaluation, and scale-up validation. The framework was specifically tailored to support correct folding and post-translational modification of triple-helical rhCol III, providing both mechanistic insight and industrial relevance.
