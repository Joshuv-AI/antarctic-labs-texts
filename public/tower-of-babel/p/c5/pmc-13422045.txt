Environmental footprint of a soybean-supplemented low-fat vegan diet in postmenopausal women: a secondary analysis of a randomised clinical trial
Authors: Ilana Fischer, Arathi Jayaraman, Brighid McKay, Laura Chiavaroli, Songhee Back, Reagan Smith, Richard Holubkov, Neal D Barnard, Hana Kahleova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDietary patterns that limit or avoid animal products contribute fewer greenhouse gases and require less energy than diets containing more animal products. Introduction

Changes in dietary intake can be an effective strategy to reduce one’s environmental footprint.1 Dietary patterns that limit or avoid animal products contribute fewer greenhouse gases and require less energy than diets containing more animal products.2 However, a quantification of these outcomes on a vegan diet in real-life conditions still needs to be explored.

As previously reported, a low-fat vegan diet supplemented with soybeans led to a significant reduction in hot flashes in postmenopausal women.3 This secondary analysis hypothesised that this intervention diet would have a reduced environmental footprint, shown by reductions in greenhouse gas emissions (GHGEs) and cumulative energy demand (CED).

Methods

The parallel-design study3 was conducted in postmenopausal women aged 40–65 years reporting at least two moderate-to-severe hot flashes per day. Participants were assigned at an equal ratio to the intervention or control group using a random-number table.3 For 12 weeks, the intervention group was asked to follow a low-fat vegan diet that included a half cup of cooked soybeans per day and control group participants maintained their usual diets.3 3-day diet records were analysed at baseline and week 12, and dietary adherence was assessed weekly. The outcomes reported in the present manuscript are consistent with those prespecified in the registered study protocol (see online supplemental file 1).

Three independent reviewers (AJ, BM and SB) linked dietary intake with the US Department of Agriculture Food Commodity Intake Database and the database of food impacts on the environment for linking to diets.4 The reviewers, the senior researcher (LC) who verified the linking accuracy and the statistician were all blinded to dietary group assignment. A repeated measure analysis of variance was performed, and results are presented as means with 95% CIs. Spearman correlations were used to test the relationship between changes in GHGEs and changes in severe hot flashes.

Results

Of 1662 volunteers, 361 were screened by telephone, and 84 participants were randomly assigned to the two study groups, with 71 participants completing the whole study (online supplemental figure 1). The groups were comparable in the number and severity of hot flashes at baseline.

GHGEs decreased (p<0.001) in the vegan group by 35% (−662.7 g CO2-eq/person-day), compared with no change in the control group (effect size −806.8 g CO2-eq/person-day (95% CI −1378.5 to −235.2); p=0.006; figure 1; online supplemental table 1). This decrease in the vegan group was mainly attributable to reduced meat consumption (effect size −534.3 g CO2-eq/person-day (95% CI −833.1 to −235.5); p<0.001), followed by reduced dairy consumption (effect size −251.1 g CO2-eq/person-day (95% CI −447.5 to −54.6); p=0.01).

Changes in total greenhouse gas emissions (A) and cumulative energy demand (B) in the control (C) and vegan (V) group from baseline to week 16. The data are shown as means with 95% CIs.

CED decreased (p=0.002) in the vegan group by 34% (−4956 kJ/person-day), compared with no change in the control group (effect size −5987.6 kJ/person-day (95% CI −10 991 to −984.1); p=0.02; figure 1; online supplemental table 1). Again, most of the reduction was attributable to reduced meat intake (effect size −3811.9 kJ/person-day (95% CI −5964.9 to −1658.9); p<0.001), followed by reduced dairy consumption (effect size −1341.4 kJ/person-day (95% CI −2389.2 to −293.7); p=0.01). Changes in GHGEs correlated with changes in severe hot flashes (r=+0.27; p=0.03), and the correlation remained significant after adjustment for changes in energy intake (r=+0.32; p=0.01).

Discussion

This randomised controlled trial found that the intervention diet—a low-fat vegan diet containing a half cup of soybeans per day—had 35% lower GHGEs and 34% lower CED than the control diet.

Our findings, showing that adoption of a low-fat vegan diet reduces environmental impact, are consistent with other studies that performed similar comparisons to omnivorous diets.5–7 Our results were specifically aligned with a review of the environmental impact of food groups reported in NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey), which also found meat and dairy products to be the top contributors to GHGEs, and legumes to have the smallest contribution.1

The observed reductions in GHGEs and CED were meaningful not only in relative but also in absolute terms, underscoring the practical relevance of the dietary modification. The observed reduction of 662.7 g CO₂-eq per person per day—a 35% decrease—is environmentally meaningful, equivalent to eliminating approximately 600 miles of driving or the annual carbon sequestration of 11 mature trees per individual. From a public health perspective, even modest absolute reductions in GHGEs, land use and water demand at the individual level may translate into substantial environmental benefits when scaled across populations. These findings therefore highlight the potential of plant-based dietary patterns as a feasible strategy for mitigating diet-related environmental burdens.

The strengths of the current study include a randomised, parallel design. The study also has limitations. Food consumption was based on self-reported diet records after a short duration of 12 weeks. The participants were research participants and may not represent the general population. However, they may be representative of the population ready and willing to make dietary changes. This secondary analysis is exploratory and was not powered for CED/GHGEs.

In conclusion, adoption of a soybean-supplemented low-fat vegan diet by postmenopausal women not only reduced hot flashes, but also significantly reduced GHGEs and CED, which are important drivers of climate change.
