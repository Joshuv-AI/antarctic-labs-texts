What is the clinical question being addressed?

What is the association between PM2.5 exposure and PE mortality?

What is the main finding?

Exposure to PM2.5 was associated with higher PE mortality, affirming PM2.5's independent link to increased PE mortality.

Pulmonary embolism (PE) ranks among the leading causes of cardiovascular mortality.1 Emerging evidence suggests that environmental factors, including air pollution, may contribute to the burden of PE.2 Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is implicated in cardiovascular and thromboembolic events, yet its specific link to PE mortality remains underexplored.3 Additionally, socioeconomic and demographic factors, summarized by the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI), may modulate these relationships. This study investigates the association between PM2.5 exposure and PE mortality across U.S. counties from 1999 to 2019, while assessing the influence of social vulnerability.

We extracted county-level PE mortality data from the CDC WONDER database for 1999 to 2019, identified via International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision code I26. PM2.5 exposure estimates were derived from the Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group, integrating satellite data, chemical transport models, and ground measurements. The 2010 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)/Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry Social Vulnerability Index SVI served as a socioeconomic indicator, reflecting the study's midpoint. We selected the 2010 SVI because it was the most temporally appropriate version available during the 1999 to 2019 study period, as county-level SVI data were only published for selected years (2000, 2010, 2014, 2016, 2018, and 2020). Data were mapped to county boundaries using QGIS v3.16. Counties with complete PE mortality, PM2.5, and SVI data were analyzed, with stratification by sex, age (<65, ≥65 years), and U.S. Census region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West). Due to CDC privacy rules, counties with fewer than 10 PE deaths were excluded, affecting sample sizes across subgroups. Multivariable linear regression assessed the PM2.5–PE mortality link, adjusting for SVI. The final multivariable model included PM2.5 and SVI. These covariates were selected a priori based on prior literature and biological plausibility. We verified the linearity assumption through visual inspection of scatterplots and residual plots, confirming that the relationship between PM2.5 and PE mortality was appropriately linear. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and results are reported as regression coefficients (β).

The analysis included 1,997 counties. Mean PE mortality was 4.2 per 100,000, and mean PM2.5 concentration was 7.8 μg/m3. PM2.5 exposure significantly correlated with higher PE mortality (β = 0.116, P < 0.01) (Figure 1). This association held for both sexes: males (r = 0.07, P = 0.032, N = 917) and females (r = 0.08, P = 0.018, N = 1,080). Age-stratified data covered 784 counties for those <65 years and 1,219 for those ≥65 years. A stronger correlation emerged in the younger group (r = 0.12, P = 0.001) vs a weaker, nonsignificant link in the older group (r = 0.03, P = 0.285). Regionally, PM2.5 positively correlated with PE mortality in the Northeast (r = 0.18, P = 0.012, N = 192) and Midwest (r = 0.09, P = 0.026, N = 475), but inverse associations appeared in the South (r = −0.09, P = 0.01, N = 844) and West (r = −0.27, P < 0.001, N = 187). An interaction model confirmed these regional differences: compared to the Midwest (reference), the PM2.5–PE mortality association was significantly more negative in the South (interaction β = −0.387, P = 0.001), with no significant interaction in the Northeast or West. SVI showed no significant association with PE mortality (β = 0.001, P = 0.620), and the model explained just 1.3% of mortality variation (R2 = 0.013). This may be partially explained by the use of a single-year SVI value that does not reflect temporal changes. Additionally, residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as access to health care, diagnostic practices, rurality and urbanicity, and comorbidities may contribute to the null finding. Stratification by SVI quartiles confirmed PM2.5's consistent association with PE mortality across vulnerability levels. However, in the adjusted model that included PM2.5, SVI, percent female, and Census region, PM2.5 was no longer significantly associated with PE mortality (β = −0.077, P = 0.079).

These findings affirm PM2.5's independent link to increased PE mortality, aligning with prior studies.4 The stronger association in females and those under 65 may reflect differences in exposure, susceptibility, or risk profiles. Regional disparities—positive correlations in the Northeast and Midwest vs inverse trends in the South and West—may stem from variations in environmental conditions, health care access, or reporting practices.

Data suppression for small counties introduces potential bias, possibly underrepresenting rural areas. The study's strength lies in leveraging the comprehensive CDC WONDER database. However, it omits other pollutants (eg, ozone, nitrogen oxides) and individual-level factors like income, education, or comorbidities, which could refine the analysis. Furthermore, we were limited to the use of a single-year SVI value that does not reflect temporal changes. All of these factors likely explain why the multivariable model explained just 1.3% of mortality variation.

Our findings highlight air pollution's public health impact, supporting stricter air quality regulations to curb PE mortality. Future studies should investigate individual-level socioeconomic and clinical variables to investigate the complex interplay between environmental exposures and PE outcomes. Despite limitations, this large-scale analysis underscores PM2.5's significant association with PE mortality from 1999 to 2019, independent of social vulnerability, reinforcing the need for pollution control to reduce cardiovascular and thromboembolic risks.
