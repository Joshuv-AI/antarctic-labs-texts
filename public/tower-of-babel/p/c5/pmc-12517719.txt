Patient-reported outcomes as drivers of postoperative delirium in the postanaesthesia care unit
Authors: Joana Berger-Estilita, Isabel Marcolino, Mia Gisselbaek, Christian Seidl, Thomas W. Buehrer, Ines Fortuna, Basak C. Meco, Finn M. Radtke, Sarah Saxena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDValue-based healthcare emphasises patient-centred outcomes. However, Patient-Reported Outcomes Measures (PROMs) and Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) remain underused in peri-operative care. Postoperative delirium (POD) is a common and serious complication associated with increased morbidity and healthcare costs. Understanding the prognostic value of PROMs and PREMs may support identification and prevention of POD in the postoperative anaesthesia care unit (PACU).OBJECTIVE(S)We assessed the association between peri-operative patient-reported symptoms and PACU POD using systematically collected PROMs and PREMs (including pain, anxiety, thirst, stress, and satisfaction), following the implementation of a brain protection care bundle.DESIGNProspective cohort study.SETTINGA single-centre study in a secondary-care private hospital in Switzerland from January 2023 to January 2024.PATIENTS1419 adults undergoing elective or urgent surgery. Exclusion criteria included age <18 years, inability to provide consent, language barriers, need for postoperative mechanical ventilation, or pre-operative Nursing Delirium Screening Scale (Nu-DESC > or equal to 2).INTERVENTION(S)Implementation of the Safe Brain Initiative (SBI) care bundle, incorporating 18 multidisciplinary, nonpharmacologic strategies to optimise peri-operative brain health.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURESThe primary outcome was PACU POD incidence, assessed using the Nu-DESC at emergence and PACU discharge. Secondary outcomes included associations between PACU POD and pre-operative PROMs (pain, anxiety, stress, nausea) and PREMs (satisfaction, well being).RESULTSPACU POD occurred in 19.6% of patients. Pre-operative anxiety (NRS > 7) was an independent predictor of POD (P = 0.012). Pre-operative PROMs showed increasing trends, while postoperative symptoms (pain, anxiety, nausea) significantly decreased. Patients with POD reported lower well being scores despite high satisfaction in other PREM domains.CONCLUSIONSPre-operative anxiety and stress strongly predict PACU POD, supporting early risk stratification and targeted interventions. Integrating PROMs and PREMs into peri-operative workflows enhances patient-centred care.

Introduction

Value-based healthcare (VBHC) has emerged as a framework prioritising quality care that maximises patient value and focuses on quality of life, patient-centred outcomes, and satisfaction.1,2 The shift towards patient-centred care has led to the adoption of broader health-related quality measures beyond traditional clinical outcomes. At the core of this evolution are Patient-Reported Outcomes (PROs), which offer a direct and unfiltered perspective of a patient's health status and care experience, as reported by patients.3 Two key tools used to capture PROs are Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) and Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs), which are essential for evaluating the quality and effectiveness of healthcare interventions. 4,5 These tools enable healthcare systems to assess interventions from the patient's perspective, improving alignment between clinical care and patient priorities.

Despite their potential, PROs remain underused in peri-operative care,6,7 particularly concerning their potential prognostic value for outcomes such as postoperative delirium (POD), pain, anxiety, and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV).8 The systematic and time-sensitive collection of PROMs and PREMs offers an opportunity to identify early indicators of complications like POD in the PACU, allowing for timely intervention and improved patient care. However, there is a gap in understanding which specific PROMs and PREMs are most predictive of these outcomes and how they evolve.

Hospitals and healthcare providers are increasingly adopting peri-operative brain care bundles like the ‘Safe Brain Initiative (SBI)’ to address the above challenges.6,9,10 The SBI is a multidisciplinary, evidence-based approach to optimise peri-operative brain health and improve patient-centred outcomes through standardised, nonpharmacologic strategies.9,11,12 A key feature of the SBI is the structured integration of both objective intra-operative practices (e.g. EEG-guided anaesthesia, capnography) and subjective real-time patient feedback through PROMs and PREMs, which together aim to support early identification of risk and continuous quality improvement.

While previous studies have primarily focused on clinical and demographic predictors of POD,13–15 a significant gap remains in understanding how subjective, patient-reported factors influence this outcome. PROMs and PREMs provide a unique lens into patient vulnerability and peri-operative stress responses, but are rarely used as early prognostic tools.6,7

This study aims to address two key research questions: What are the longitudinal trends of PROMs and PREMs after the implementation of a brain healthcare bundle? Which PROMs and PREMs are prognostically important for PACU POD? By systematically integrating these measures across the peri-operative trajectory, our study aims to bridge this knowledge gap and support the development of personalised, patient-centred interventions to improve brain health and reduce early POD risk.

In this study, PROMs included patient-reported pain, anxiety, stress, thirst, and nausea, while PREMs captured satisfaction with care and perceived well being.

Methods

Ethics

The Cantonal Ethics Committee of Bern (BASEC-number: Req-2022-01502) waived ethics approval for the study on 8 December 2022, as it involved a quality improvement project. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki,16 and researchers followed the Data Protection Acts of their respective academic institutions. It also followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.17

Study design and setting

This observational prospective cohort study included patients screened between 6 January 2023 and 6 January 2024, at Salem-Spital Bern (Switzerland). The Salem-Spital is a private hospital part of the Hirslanden Hospital Group, with a public mandate. It offers 147 ward beds and various medical services, focusing on musculoskeletal medicine, general surgery, spinal surgery, urology, gynaecology, and obstetrics. The hospital performed 6918 surgical procedures during the study period.

Intervention

The SBI care bundle incorporates 18 multidisciplinary, nonpharmacologic strategies (Supplemental Digital Content File 1).9 Rooted in VBHC principles, it standardises peri-operative care by systematically monitoring critical patient-centred outcomes, like PACU POD, pain, stress, and nausea, aiming to personalise treatment plans and decision-making. This study specifically focused on the PROMs and PREMs components of the SBI bundle, rather than evaluating all 18 elements of the bundle individually. Specifically, it examined patient-reported symptoms and experiences – including pain, anxiety, stress, thirst, nausea, and satisfaction – as potential predictors of very early postoperative delirium, systematically collected at multiple peri-operative time points.

The SBI care bundle, implemented as part of routine peri-operative care, served as the broader intervention. SBI is a structured, nonpharmacologic, multidisciplinary approach to optimise peri-operative brain health, particularly preventing POD.9 In our implementation of the SBI bundle, PROMs and PREMs were collected systematically at predefined peri-operative time points. These data were used to identify patients with high levels of pre-operative anxiety or pain, and informed targeted discussions with the care team. Implemented at Salem-Spital Bern in Switzerland, the initiative involved comprehensive staff training for nurses, anaesthesiologists, and surgeons using guides, videos, workshops and simulation exercises. A senior coordinator (JBE) ensured protocol adherence, monitored outcomes via a digital dashboard, and fostered interdisciplinary collaboration through regular meetings and open communication.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcome of this study includes patient-reported pain, anxiety, stress, thirst, nausea, and satisfaction, which were used to evaluate the impact of a peri-operative brain healthcare bundle on peri-operative well being and the occurrence of very early, PACU postoperative delirium. Specifically, we aim to:

Patients

This study included adult patients who underwent elective and emergency surgeries at the Hirslanden Salem-Spital Bern, with a postoperative postanaesthesia care unit (PACU) stay. Patients under 18 years of age, those with language barriers, patients needing postoperative mechanical ventilation and those unable to provide consent were excluded from the study. For this analysis, we excluded all patients who scored a Nu-DESC >2 points (considered already delirious) before induction.

Data collection

Data collection was performed by the peri-operative team as part of routine clinical care, across all days of the week and during standard working shifts. These measures were gathered using a structured questionnaire designed explicitly for this purpose (Supplemental Digital Content File 2). Patients were asked to rate their satisfaction levels and overall well being based on their experiences, ensuring their perspectives were systematically captured and quantified. Outcome measures were collected only when patients were clinically alert and able to respond reliably. For patients who developed PACU delirium, PROMs and PREMs were recorded only during periods when the Nu-DESC was <2 and the patient was re-evaluated as cognitively capable of providing self-reported data. All data were recorded on paper case report forms (p-CRFs), which were later manually transferred to a digital database. To ensure high-quality and reliable data, the team included only patients with >85% of data points completed at four different time points, disregarding incomplete datasets.

The collected data complied with European data protection regulations and were stored using the EasyTrial clinical trial management system (EasyTrial ApS, Aalborg, Denmark). Access to the data was restricted to authorised personnel, and once digitised, the data were anonymised and visualised through an online web dashboard for easy access by designated users (Supplemental Digital Content File 3). Although data collection followed a standardised SBI framework, the absence of formal inter-rater reliability testing over time may have allowed for team- or examiner-dependent variability in documentation, especially for subjective symptoms.

Patient-Reported Outcomes Measures and Patient-Reported Experience Measures assessment

Data collection was conducted at four specific time points (Fig. 1). The first time point (T1) was during the pre-operative period when the patient was being prepared for surgery. The second time point (T2) occurred upon the patient's handover to the PACU. At the third time point (T3), data were collected at the time of the patient's discharge from the PACU. Finally, T4 captured the worst value recorded during the patient's entire stay in the PACU. T4 was retrospectively selected as the worst recorded value from T2 to T3 rather than an independent assessment point (Fig. 1). Patients were asked to classify their pain level, quality of recovery, anxiety & stress level, thirst, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and shivering. Pain on mobilisation was defined as the first patient-initiated movement (e.g., sitting upright or transferring from bed to chair). Additionally, we asked patients to classify their satisfaction with the pre-operative assessment, anaesthesia management and PACU stay, and overall well being during their peri-operative stay. PROMs and PREMs were all measured using a self-reported Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) ranging from 0 to 10, where 0 indicated no symptom (e.g., no pain, stress, or thirst) and 10 represented the most severe experience of the respective symptom.

Peri-operative patient journey in the Safe Brain Initiative Value-Based Healthcare Framework.

In the Safe Brain Initiative (SBI) Value-Based Healthcare (VBHC) framework, the peri-operative patient journey is structured into four key time points – T1, T2, T3 and T4 – which represent critical phases where Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) and Patient-Reported Experience Measures (PREMs) are systematically collected and analysed. These time points facilitate the early identification of high-risk patients, enable timely interventions, and support continuous quality improvement in peri-operative brain health.

We used the Nursing Delirium Screening Scale (Nu-DESC)18,19 to evaluate PACU POD at the same time points. The Nu-DESC assesses five categories: disorientation, inappropriate behaviour, inappropriate communication, illusions/hallucinations, and psychomotor retardation. Each category is scored from 0 (absent) to 2 (severe), and POD is considered present if the total score equals or exceeds two at any assessment point. Delirium was assessed only in the PACU, and our findings therefore pertain to POD in the PACU, not delayed or ward-based POD.

Information on patient characteristics included age, sex, and physical status as classified by the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status system. Additionally, procedural details were recorded, such as the duration of the surgery in hours and whether the patient underwent general or regional anaesthesia.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient characteristics and key study variables. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. In contrast, continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations (SDs) if normally distributed or medians with interquartile ranges [IQR] for skewed distributions, such as length of stay (LOS). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots.

We applied appropriate statistical tests based on variable types and data distribution to compare patients who developed PACU POD with those who did not. Categorical variables, such as sex, ASA classification, and type of surgery, were compared using χ2 tests, with Fisher's exact tests applied when expected cell counts were <5. Continuous variables, including age, PROMs (e.g., pain, anxiety, stress), and length of stay, were analysed using independent t-tests for normally distributed data, whereas Mann–Whitney U tests were employed for skewed distributions. The incidence of PACU POD was calculated as the proportion of new cases among the total at-risk population.

Logistic regression models were developed to investigate the prognostic value of PROMs and PREMs for PACU POD. Univariate analyses were first performed to screen potential predictors, followed by multivariable models adjusting for key confounders, including demographic variables (age, sex, ASA classification), surgical factors (type of surgery, anaesthesia duration, intra-operative hypotension), and baseline cognitive status (pre-operative Nu-DESC score, history of cognitive impairment). Variable selection for the multivariate logistic regression models followed a combined clinical and statistical approach. Specifically, variables were included if they demonstrated statistical significance in univariate analyses (P < 0.05), and/or were supported by prior evidence and clinical plausibility as potential predictors of postoperative delirium, such as age, ASA classification, duration of anaesthesia, and pre-operative anxiety. This dual strategy was employed to ensure that the final model maintained both statistical rigor and clinical relevance while minimising overfitting and preserving parsimony. Significant predictors from the univariate analysis were then included in a multivariate logistic regression model using stepwise selection to determine the strongest independent associations. The variables included in the multivariate model were checked for multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor (VIF), ensuring no significant multicollinearity between predictors.20 Model evaluation metrics included the Akaike information criterion (AIC), the percentage of variance explained by the model and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) to assess model performance and goodness of fit.

To handle missing data, k-nearest neighbours (KNN) imputation (with K = 5) was applied, allowing for multivariate analysis without excluding patients with incomplete data.21 A two-step regression approach was employed to evaluate the prognostic significance of PROMs and PREMs for POD. For univariate logistic regression, each individual PROM (e.g., pain, anxiety, nausea, stress) and PREM (e.g., satisfaction, well being) was first assessed separately to identify potential predictors of PACU POD.

A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.1) 22 and SPSS (version 29.0). Data analysis and visualisations were performed using R statistical software, leveraging several R packages for specific analyses (R Packages used: Stat for logistic regression and statistical tests, VIM for KNN imputation, car for multicollinearity checks (VIF)).

Descriptive analysis

After exclusions, the studied cohort included 1412 adult individuals (Fig. 2), with a nearly equal distribution of men and women (Table 1). Surgical duration emerged as a significant POD predictor variable in the study. Among patients with documented surgical times, 46% underwent procedures lasting between 1 and 2 h. Delirium was observed in 273 patients during the evaluation period, representing 19.6% of the cohort.

Patient-Reported Outcomes Measures

Pre-operative pain, anxiety, thirst and stress followed similar increasing trends throughout the year (Fig. 3). The trends for postoperative pain, anxiety and PONV were characterised by a postoperative decrease with time (Fig. 4). Shivering occurred immediately postsurgery in 4.3% of the cohort. The high shivering incidence decreased over time. Patients experiencing POD reported significantly higher levels of pain, anxiety and stress compared to those without (Table 2). Specifically, high pain levels after surgery, particularly during mobilisation and at PACU discharge, were strongly associated with PACU POD. High anxiety before induction and after surgery was also associated with POD. In contrast, symptoms such as thirst and shivering showed no significant differences between patients with and without PACU POD. Potential prognostic Value of Patient-Reported Outcomes Measures and Patient-Reported Experience Measures for postoperative delirium

When performing univariate regression analyses, gender, age group, anaesthesia type, surgical time category, pre-operative high anxiety (NRS > 7), length of PACU stay, pre-operative pain on mobilisation, and ASA category emerged as significant. The multivariable model was obtained by the stepwise method, using statistically significant variables in univariate models. The model with the best AIC value combined the following variables: age group, sex, length of stay in the PACU, ASA, surgical time (category variable), type of anaesthesia, high anxiety and high pre-operative pain mobilisation. The length of stay in the PACU, surgical time (over 3 h), anaesthesia and high pre-operative pain mobilisation were statistically significant (P < 0.05). Due to convergence issues, some multivariate models failed to stabilise, limiting the ability to assess the combined predictive value of all PROMs and PREMs in a single model. The multivariate model with the set of variables that most accurately explained the model's variability concerning the POD outcome combined the following variables: age group, sex, PACU time, ASA, surgical time, type of anaesthesia, high pre-operative anxiety and pain on mobilisation (Supplemental Digital Content File 4). Other multivariate models revealed statistical significance for high pre-operative anxiety, but these models were less accurate. High pre-operative anxiety (NRS > 7) emerged as the only statistically significant independent predictor of PACU POD in the final model (P = 0.012) (Supplemental Digital Content Files 4 and Supplemental Digital Content Files 5). This suggests potential intercorrelation among predictors, such as anxiety and stress, and highlights anxiety as the most robust independent risk factor. An analysis excluding the imputed surgical time variable showed consistent results, with high pre-operative anxiety remaining a significant independent predictor of POD. PREMs were analysed similarly, but no individual PREM retained statistical significance in the adjusted multivariate model.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to document PROs related explicitly to anaesthesia care, providing unique insights into peri-operative patient experiences. Beyond routine peri-operative documentation of pain and PONV, thirst, stress, anxiety and overall well being scores were systematically documented, revealing distinct time patterns.

Our findings highlight the critical role of pre-operative pain, anxiety and stress on postoperative delirium. In our cohort, patients who developed POD reported higher levels of pain, anxiety, and stress before surgery. These findings highlight the potential role of pre-operative management in mitigating these factors to reduce PACU POD risk. High anxiety (NRS > 7) before induction was also a significant predictor of POD in the univariate analysis. Aligned with our results, Aminpour et al.8 recently showed that pre-operative anxiety, depression, and pain significantly predict the worsening of postoperative psychological outcomes. Elevated pre-operative anxiety and pain likely indicate underlying psychological vulnerability or a heightened stress response, which exacerbates postoperative distress and increases the risk of PACU POD. This connection underscores the importance of pre-operative psychological assessments and interventions to identify and support at-risk patients. Given the strong association between pre-operative anxiety and PACU POD, nonpharmacological interventions such as structured pre-operative education, patient engagement, and improved communication may play a key role in anxiety reduction. Recent evidence, including ESAIC guidelines,12 do not support the routine use of benzodiazepines for anxiety management due to their potential to increase delirium risk. Instead, interventions that enhance patient understanding, trust, and preparation for surgery may offer a safer and more effective strategy. No association was observed between the type of surgery and pre-operative anxiety, suggesting that other factors—such as individual psychological predisposition—may play a more prominent role. Recently, Meco et al.11 demonstrated that the SBI care bundle significantly reduced PACU POD incidence. By addressing modifiable risk factors, such as peri-operative pain, anxiety and stress, this care bundle probably lowered the likelihood of PACU POD, enhanced patient recovery, and potentially reduced associated healthcare costs. In addition, the observed trends in symptom recognition and management underscore the impact of a peri-operative brain healthcare bundle on both patient-centred care and staff practices, making it a valuable model for broader implementation.

The increasing trends in reported pre-operative pain, anxiety, thirst, and stress likely reflect improved symptom detection and protocol adherence over time, rather than a true clinical deterioration. This ‘learning effect’ is characteristic of quality improvement cycles, where documentation quality and assessment consistency improve as staff gain experience. Similarly to other quality improvement studies,23–25 as the initiative progressed, healthcare providers likely became more proficient at systematically assessing and documenting PROs, leading to a spurious increase in incidence. The enhanced awareness and adherence to brain health protocols could have led to more consistent identification and reporting of pre-operative symptoms. This compliance reflects an evolving focus on thorough patient assessment and care optimisation, which may have heightened the recognition of these issues over time. Consequently, the observed trend likely represents an improvement in capturing patient experiences rather than an actual increase in the severity of symptoms. Nonetheless, we acknowledge this as a limitation of interpreting symptom trends in observational implementation research.

On the other hand, the postoperative decrease in pain, anxiety, thirst, and stress can be attributed to effective peri-operative care interventions and the natural progression of recovery. Implementing the SBI bundle of care likely played a significant role, mainly through evidence-based strategies for pain management, anxiety reduction, and hydration.9 pre-operative anticipation and fear often elevate stress and anxiety levels, which naturally subside postsurgery as patients gain reassurance about their condition.26 Adequate pain control measures, such as multimodal analgesia, likely alleviated pain and reduced related anxiety and stress. Proper fluid management likely helped mitigate thirst, especially by reintroducing oral intake.27 Close monitoring and support in the PACU may have provided patients with a sense of security, further contributing to psychological relief. This overall decline underscores the effectiveness of structured peri-operative care in improving recovery and patient outcomes.

Our study has several strengths. By systematically reporting pain, anxiety, stress, thirst, PONV, and overall well being at multiple peri-operative time points, the study provides valuable insights into the patient experience and recovery process. A key strength is the study's large and diverse cohort, comprising 1419 adult patients with a wide age range undergoing various surgical procedures. This diversity enhances the generalisability of the findings and provides robust data for evaluating the impact of the SBI bundles. Another significant advantage is the focus on both pre-operative and postoperative factors. We demonstrated how elevated pre-operative pain, anxiety, and stress were linked to POD, emphasising the importance of targeted pre-operative interventions. While prior studies have reported associations between PROMs and POD,12,28 our findings contribute additional insights through a prospective, real-world implementation study. Specifically, our approach incorporated routine and structured assessment of patient-reported symptoms – pain, anxiety, stress, thirst and nausea – at multiple peri-operative time points, using standardised instruments. Unlike prior studies that often relied on single-time-point assessments or narrowly focused populations, our analysis adjusted for key clinical confounders in a diverse surgical cohort. This strengthens the understanding of which self-reported symptoms are independently associated with PACU delirium and highlights the feasibility and prognostic value of systematically integrating PROMs and PREMs into peri-operative care. Finally, the study's relevance to clinical practice and healthcare policy is significant. By demonstrating the benefits of tailored interventions for at-risk patients, the study provides actionable recommendations for optimising peri-operative care. Its alignment with VBHC principles, prioritising patient-centred outcomes and cost-effectiveness, makes a compelling case for broader adoption of similar care bundles in other healthcare settings.

While the study offers valuable insights into the effectiveness of the SBI in monitoring PROMs, several limitations should be taken into consideration. First, the study's single-centre design may limit the generalisability of its results. A key limitation of this study is the high exclusion rate due to incomplete data collection (4174 of 6918 patients screened). This reflects real-world challenges of integrating PROMs and PREMs into routine peri-operative workflows, particularly in busy clinical environments where response burden, staff availability, and timing may affect completeness. The data were collected in a specific healthcare setting, which may not reflect the diversity of clinical practices, patient populations, and resource availability. For instance, the availability of specialised personnel or adherence to standardised protocols might differ significantly across settings. Second, the study solely relied on the Nu-DESC assessment tool for identifying PACU POD. While Nu-DESC is practical and easy to administer, it may not capture all cases of delirium, especially in patients with fluctuating or hypo-active symptoms. This underdetection could lead to underestimating POD prevalence and potentially skew the findings. Another significant limitation is the timing of delirium assessment in the PACU. Delirium identified immediately upon arrival may be confounded by residual effects of anaesthetic drugs during emergence, rather than representing true PACU postoperative delirium. This introduces the possibility of misclassification and overestimation of the delirium rate. Future studies should consider delayed or repeated assessments beyond emergence to better differentiate between emergence-related phenomena and PACU POD. Incorporating additional or more sensitive tools, such as a battery of testing, including the Confusion Assessment Method,29 could enhance diagnostic accuracy and provide a more comprehensive picture of PACU POD incidence and severity. Third, data gaps posed a challenge, particularly with the variable “surgical time”, where 46% of the data were missing and required imputation. This high proportion of missing data for surgical time introduces a potential source of bias. Although this method preserved the dataset structure, the assumptions of KNN may not fully capture the underlying clinical reasons for omission. Our sensitivity analysis, excluding surgical time from multivariable models, showed consistent findings, supporting the robustness of our conclusions regarding high pre-operative anxiety. Nonetheless, interpretations involving surgical time should be made with caution.

While the univariate analysis that assessed the statistical significance was applied after the KNN imputation, we acknowledge that missing data can dilute the analysis's accuracy and the conclusions’ reliability. only high pre-operative anxiety remained statistically significant in the final multivariate model – despite several PROMs being significant in univariate analysis – highlights the importance of confounder adjustment. It also suggests that anxiety may act as a proxy for broader psychological vulnerability, possibly overlapping with stress and pain through shared variance. Convergence issues and limited statistical power may have further contributed to the attenuation of independent effects in multivariate modelling. Future studies using larger datasets or external validation cohorts may benefit from applying penalised regression approaches, such as LASSO or elastic net, to address collinearity and sparse data. These methods could improve model robustness and further clarify the independent contribution of PROMs and PREMs to early POD risk.

To ensure validity, PROM and PREM data were only collected from patients who were not actively delirious at the time of assessment. This approach reduces the risk of bias due to impaired cognition but may limit our ability to capture symptom experiences from patients with persistent or fluctuating delirium. Monthly variations in reported symptoms such as pain, anxiety, and nausea may reflect fluctuations in staff engagement, subjective interpretation, or variation in documentation rather than actual changes in patient condition. While all staff were trained in symptom assessment using SBI protocols, inter-observer variability cannot be excluded and may have affected consistency. This represents a limitation of the real-world setting and should be considered when interpreting temporal trends. We also acknowledge that our study did not include formal psychological assessments (e.g., STAI-Y,30 Beck Anxiety Inventory31) to differentiate between trait and state anxiety. Therefore, it is possible that some patients had pre-existing anxious-depressive or catastrophising personality traits, which may have influenced both their reported anxiety levels and their vulnerability to PACU POD. Future research should explore this relationship using validated psychometric instruments to better characterise the psychological profiles of high-risk patients.

A key limitation of this study is the lack of systematic documentation on how the individual elements of the SBI bundle were implemented in clinical practice. Although PROMs and PREMs were collected routinely, we did not formally track whether these data were consistently used to modify care. This limits our ability to directly attribute observed outcomes to specific SBI-related interventions.

Finally, the study primarily focused on immediate postoperative outcomes measured during PACU stay, such as pain, anxiety and PACU POD, without assessing the long-term implications of these findings. POD is known to have lasting effects on cognitive and functional outcomes, including prolonged recovery times and higher morbidity rates.32,33 Without extended follow-up, the study cannot capture the full impact of a peri-operative brain healthcare bundle on patients’ long-term recovery and quality of life.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that high pre-operative anxiety and stress are strong predictors of PACU POD, underscoring the importance of systematic screening and PACU risk stratification. While only pre-operative anxiety remained a statistically significant independent predictor in the final multivariate model, the broader use of PROMs and PREMs provides clinically valuable information beyond pure prognostication.

The integration of PROMs and PREMs into routine peri-operative workflows supports patient-centred care by enabling early symptom detection, enhancing team communication, and guiding tailored management strategies. Moreover, observed trends in PROMs over time suggest potential process-level improvements resulting from structured peri-operative assessment.

Although our observational data indicate a positive trajectory in patient-reported outcomes following the implementation of the SBI, limitations such as model convergence issues and incomplete data restrict causal inferences regarding the care bundle's effect on POD incidence. The instability of multivariate models also constrained our ability to assess the combined predictive value of PROMs and PREMs.

Future studies with larger, more complete datasets and the application of robust statistical methods – such as penalised regression or external validation cohorts – are needed to confirm these findings and further evaluate the prognostic utility of PROMs and PREMs in peri-operative brain health strategies.
