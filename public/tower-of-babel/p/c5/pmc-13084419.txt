Development of an Anticancer Fungal Paclitaxel-Loaded Hydrogel System via 3D Bioprinting: A Next-Generation Biomedical Platform
Authors: Swathe Sriee Angalaparameshwari Elayarasan, Vijayalakshmi Shankar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Paclitaxel (Taxol) is a widely used anticancer drug, but its limited natural availability necessitates alternative production strategies. In this study, Aspergillus flavus isolated from wastewater was identified as a source of paclitaxel, which was confirmed by chromatographic and spectroscopic analyses. To improve solubility and delivery, paclitaxel was incorporated into a three-dimensional bioprinted hydrogel composed of sodium alginate and hyaluronic acid. A 2:1 sodium alginate-to-hyaluronic acid ratio provided the most suitable formulation, as it balanced viscosity, stability, and printability. The resulting scaffolds exhibited uniform porosity and elastic recovery, supporting controlled drug release. Paclitaxel-loaded hydrogels showed significant cytotoxicity against breast cancer cells (IC50 = 11.63 μg/mL) and inhibited cell migration. These findings demonstrate a sustainable microbial route for paclitaxel production and highlight the potential of bioprinted hydrogels for targeted, controlled anticancer therapy.

Introduction

Paclitaxel (Taxol) is a diterpenoid compound of the taxane family that has become one of the most effective chemotherapeutic agents. Its unique mechanism of action involves stabilizing microtubules and inhibiting mitotic division, making it valuable in the treatment of breast, ovarian, and nonsmall-cell lung cancers, as well as AIDS-related Kaposi’s sarcoma. , Despite its clinical significance, the large-scale production of paclitaxel remains a major challenge. Extraction from the bark of Taxus species is inefficient, yielding only about 0.01% of the compound on a dry weight basis, and is ecologically unsustainable due to the destruction of mature trees. Semisynthetic approaches utilizing 10-deacetylbaccatin III from Taxus leaves and plant cell culture techniques have been developed; however, these methods are hindered by low yields and high costs. , Microbial biosynthesis has emerged as a promising alternative to paclitaxel production. Several endophytic fungi associated with Taxus plants and other ecological niches have demonstrated the ability to synthesize paclitaxel and its derivatives. Among these, Aspergillus flavus is a particularly attractive candidate because of its rapid growth, genetic tractability, and well-characterized metabolic pathways. Although A. flavus is known for its aflatoxin production, not all isolates are toxic, and nonaflatoxigenic strains have been successfully applied in agriculture as biological control agents. , In this study, a nonaflatoxigenic strain of A. flavus was isolated from wastewater, an environment that may enhance secondary metabolite production under stress conditions. While microbial synthesis provides a sustainable source of paclitaxel, clinical application of the drug is hindered by its poor aqueous solubility, rapid clearance, and dose-limiting toxicity. These limitations highlight the need for advanced delivery systems capable of ensuring controlled, localized, and biocompatible drug release. Hydrogels are hydrophilic polymer networks with tunable porosity, biocompatibility, and responsiveness to environmental conditions, making them ideal carriers for hydrophobic drugs like paclitaxel. Recent advances in three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting have further enhanced hydrogel-based delivery systems by enabling precise control over scaffold architecture, porosity, and mechanical strength. The formation of sodium alginatehyaluronic acid hydrogels occurs through a combination of ionic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and physical chain entanglements. Both polymers are rich in hydroxyl and carboxyl groups that can interact with each other to form a 3D stable three-dimensional network. Hydrogen bonding between hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of alginate and hyaluronic acid provides cohesion within the matrix, while ionic interactions between negatively charged carboxyl groups and sodium ions contribute to network stabilization, especially when divalent cations such as Ca2+ are introduced during cross-linking. − In addition, the long polysaccharide chains become physically entangled in solution, further enhancing the viscosity and structural integrity. These combined interactions trap large amounts of water within the network and give rise to the hydrogel properties. In summary, this study was designed to achieve two main objectives: (1) to produce paclitaxel sustainably using a nonaflatoxigenic strain of A. flavus isolated from wastewater, and (2) to develop a 3D bioprinted sodium alginatehyaluronic acid hydrogel for the controlled delivery of the fungal-derived drug. The workflow integrated microbial fermentation, paclitaxel extraction and characterization, bioink formulation, and the 3D printing of drug-loaded scaffolds. The resulting bioprinted hydrogel demonstrated high structural stability, sustained drug release, and significant cytotoxic activity against breast cancer cells, highlighting its potential as an ecofriendly and effective therapeutic platform.

Materials and Methodology

Materials

Hyaluronic acid (CASNo. 9067-32-7, purity = ≥95%), sodium alginate (CAS No.9005-38-3, purity = 99%), sabouraud dextrose agar (MH063), methanol (AS061), acetonitrile (CAS No. 75-05-8. purity = ≥ 99.9%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (GRM5856, purity = ≥99.5%), dichloromethane (AS045), DDPH reagent (1898-66-4), Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (RM10822), ascorbic acid (50-81-7, purity = ≥99%), and gallic acid (5995-86-8, purity = ≥98%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Himedia, Bangalore.

Extraction and Quantification of Taxol

The Aspergillus flavus obtained from wastewater samples was cultivated in Sabouraud Dextrose Broth (SDB), as previously reported by swathe et al., 2024. A 6 day-old culture of each isolate grown on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) was transferred into the SDB medium, and the cultures were incubated for 15 to 20 days at 30 °C. Following incubation, the cultures were filtered through layers of cheesecloth, and 0.02% (W/V) sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) was added to the filtrate to prevent contamination from fatty acids. After centrifugation of the culture filtrate at 5000 rpm to discard the cell debris, the supernatant was extracted with dichloromethane (DCM) in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio. The extraction was replicated twice to recover maximum Taxol. The resulting mixture of organic layers was recovered, and the solvent was removed by reduced pressure at 35–50 °C under a vacuum in a rotary vacuum evaporator. The dried compound was dissolved in methanol and passed through a syringe filter of 0.22 μm to clear the particulate contaminants. The crude methanolic extract was then passed through further processes of purification by use of chromatography techniques such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to ascertain the purity and presence of Taxol. Each procedure was performed in a light-protected area to avoid photodegradation of Taxol. ,

High-Performance Liquid Chromatography Characterization of Fungal Extract

To verify the presence of Taxol, the fungal extract underwent analysis using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Agilent, Model: Infinity 2000). The Taxol was examined with a reverse-phase C18 column equipped with a UV detector. A volume of 20 μL of the sample was injected for each analysis and detected at a wavelength of 227 nm. The mobile phase consisted of methanol, acetonitrile, and water at a ratio of 25:35:40 by volume, flowing at a rate of 1.0 mL/min. Prior to injection into the column, the samples were filtered through a 0.2 μm PVDF filter. The presence of fungal Taxol was confirmed by comparing the peak areas of the samples to those of authentic Taxol.

Spectroscopic Characterization of Fungal Extract

The absorbance of taxol extracted from the effective fungal isolate was analyzed using UV–visible spectrophotometry (Specord, Model: 210 Plus) over a wavelength range of 200–400 nm. The concentration and purity of the extract were assessed by comparison with authentic Taxol, using methanol as the spectrophotometric blank. Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was conducted using (Agilent Cary 630, Model: FTIR Benchtop Systems), and the spectra were recorded over the range of 4000 to 400 cm

Free Radical Scavenging Activity of the Fungal Extract

2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) is a stable free radical that appears as a deep purple color, which transitions to yellow when it is reduced by an electron donor. Due to this characteristic, DPPH is commonly utilized to assess the Total Antioxidant Capacity (TAC) of compounds, including metabolites and antioxidants capable of donating electrons. The extent of color alteration in the presence of an antioxidant is measured spectrophotometrically at a specific wavelength, with the intensity of the shift reflecting the TAC. The TAC of fungal extracts can be quantified by generating a standard curve using known antioxidants, such as ascorbic acid. Furthermore, the percentage of scavenging activity and IC50 values of antioxidant metabolites can be determined by comparing the absorbance values of the sample with those of the standard. , %Scavenging=(1−Absorbanceofsample/Absorbanceofcontrol)×100

Determination of Total Phenolic Content of the Fungal Extract

Preparation of Standard Gallic Acid for Calibration Curve

Using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric test, ,, the total phenolic content of fungal extracts was ascertained. Ten milligrams of gallic acid were dissolved in 10 mL of methanol to create a standard stock solution of gallic acid (1 mg/mL). This solution was diluted with methanol in different amounts (0.5, 0.75, and 1 mL) and then mixed with 4 mL of 7% sodium carbonate and 5 mL of 10% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent to get a final volume of 10 mL. Following a vigorous vortex, the mixtures were incubated for 30 min at 40 °C in a water bath. Using a UV–visible spectrophotometer, absorbance was measured at 760 nm against a blank. The Folin-Ciocalteu reagent reacts with phenolic substances to form a blue complex whose intensity is proportional to the phenolic concentration. All experiments were run in triplicate, and the standard calibration curve was generated using the mean absorbance outcomes.

Preparation of Samples for Total Phenolic Content

The method used for the gallic acid standard was used to prepare fungal extracts at different concentrations (0.5, 0.75, and 1 mL). Absorbance was measured at each concentration in triplicate. The mean absorbance values were utilized to create a calibration curve, which was then applied to determine the extracts’ phenolic content. The results were reported in milligrams of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of dry matter. The total phenolic content was determined using the following formula C=c.V/m where C is the total phenolic content (mg GAE/g dry extract), c is the gallic acid concentration obtained from the calibration curve (mg/mL), V is the extract volume (mL), and m is the mass of the extract (g).

Preparation of Bioink

A bioink composed of sodium alginate and hyaluronic acid was developed to create a hydrogel suitable for extrusion-based bioprinting. To optimize the formulation for this method, various ratios of sodium alginate and hyaluronic acid were tested. These ratios included 1:1, 1:2,1:3, 2:1, 2:2, and 2:3% (w/v), maintaining a total polysaccharide concentration of 2%. The goal was to evaluate properties such as gel stability, mechanical strength, and printability (Figure S1). The components were dissolved under magnetic stirring at 60–70 °C.

Fabrication of Sodium Alginate: Hyaluronic Acid Scaffold

Scaffold fabrication was carried out using microextrusion-based three-dimensional printing equipment (Anga Pro 3D Printer, Alfatek Systems, Kolkata). The dispensing machine was linked to the printer to extrude the bioink through a micronozzle, with print patterns determined by G-code created with AutoCAD software. The setup comprised a unique heating and cooling module to keep the printing temperature at 37 °C. The resulting bioink was placed into the printer cartridge and dispensed to a Petri dish using a conical taper nozzle (25G). Printing was carried out with an extrusion pressure of 0.8 bar and a speed of 10 mm/s. Polymer cross-linking was achieved by using calcium chloride (CaCl2) prepared at a concentration of 0.5 M in deionized water. After every three printed layers, the construct was immersed in the cross-linking solution for 5–10 min, followed by removal of excess CaCl2 and subsequently transferred onto a fresh plate (Figure ).

Characterization of Sodium Alginate: Hyaluronic Acid Scaffold

Swelling Degree Analysis

To assess the behavior of swelling, 3-layered hydrogel constructs were bioprinted at different compositions of sodium alginate and hyaluronic acid, in formulations of 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, 2:1, 2:2, 2:3 (% w/v). Carefully, the printed hydrogels were loaded into sterile containers filled up with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7) to mimic physiological conditions. All hydrogel constructs were filled with PBS and incubated at room temperature for a period of five days. During the period of incubation, swelling analysis was carried out daily. Edging out the hydrogels at every time point (i.e., 24 h) was done using forceps to prevent mechanical damage. The excess amount of PBS in the surface of hydrogel was removed by gently blotting with lint-free absorbent paper without squeezing the hydrogel to get an accurate weight. Swollen weights of each hydrogel were immediately recorded by using a precision analytical balance. All the measurements were performed three times in order to reproducibility and because of the possibility of variability between the samples. Using this procedure, a comparative evaluation of the swollen dynamics of all hydrogel compositions as a function in time was possible, presenting an account of how the polymer concentration and ratio affect the hydrogel’s capacity to take up and retain fluid. , Swelling(%)=(WS−W0)/W0×100

Viscosity Determination of Polymer Mixture

The viscosity of mixtures of sodium alginate and hyaluronic acid at ratios of 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, 2:1, 2:2, and 2:3 (% w/v) was determined. For this study, a Brookfield digital viscometer was used and operated at a constant temperature of 50 ± 5 °C to maintain uniformity among samples. Before measurement, maximum homogeneity was achieved by subjecting each of the polymer mixtures to uniform stirring to remove any air bubbles. The viscosity readings were determined in spindle number 02 or 03 (depending on the density of the solution) at a constant rotational speed of 100 rpm. Each sample was taken for 15 s to get stable values of viscosity, and the readings were recorded in mPa·s. All of the measurements were performed three times to guarantee reproducibility and statistical credibility for the findings.

In Vitro Biodegradation Study of SA: HA Hydrogel

Biodegradation experiments were carried out at pH 1.2 (gastric fluid) and 6.8 (intestinal fluid) in a glass beaker. The beakers containing the sample (2:1 ratio of sodium alginate: Hyaluronic acid) were placed on a hot-plate magnetic stirrer at 100 rpm (stirring speed) and 37 °C. At a specific time interval, samples were collected with a fixed time interval, washed with distilled water, and frozen by lyophilization to determine the dry weight. WeightRemained(%)=(Wd/Wo)×100 where W d is the weight of the dried hydrogel after degradation and W o is the initial weight of the hydrogel

Thermogravimetric Analysis of SA: HA Hydrogel

The thermal stability and mode of decomposition of the hydrogel blend were determined by conducting thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the sodium alginate-hyaluronic acid using a NETZSCH STA 2500 thermal analyzer (model STA2500A-0028 N). The sample mass of around 7.1 mg was properly weighed and introduced into a large alumina (Al2O3) crucible, which was specifically designed to fit the instrument. The analysis was performed in the flow of pure nitrogen gas in an inert atmosphere with the purpose of eliminating oxidation. The programmed temperature ranged between 30 and 600 °C at a constant equal heating rate of 10 °C/min. Thermal analysis was performed by DTA-TG, where the thermal behavior of the sample was monitored as a function of thermal response to the heating process, which was recorded simultaneously with any changes in weight. The data acquisition was controlled with the help of NETZSCH Proteus software, and the mass loss was taken as a function of the temperature to determine the moisture content, degradation phases, and remaining composition of the SA-HA hydrogel system.

Optical Coherence Tomography Analysis of Hydrogel

Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) was used as a nondestructive method of imaging that allows studying the internal microarchitecture of the bioprinted hydrogel scaffolds. Full-depth scans of 40 individual cross-sectional slices of the OCT images through the scaffold were made to assess structural homogeneity. Images were analyzed with ImageJ (Fiji) software to obtain quantitative information, including the number of pores, the sum of pores, average pore size, and percentage area occupied by pores. Based on this, pore density (pores/mm2) was determined to be able to evaluate the porosity of the scaffold distribution. Standard slice-wise analysis, as well as large field OCT imaging with a focus on the whole structure assembly of the distribution of pores in a larger scaffold area, were carried out. Static OCT analysis was performed under nonloaded conditions to evaluate the baseline pore morphology and wall integrity, which gave an idea of how well the scaffold maintained its structural integrity when no external forces were present on it. Moreover, mechanical responses of the scaffold subjected to mechanical load, or some external perturbation, were recorded in real time by dynamic OCT imaging, and features were assessed such as pore deformation, structural elasticity, and mechanical recovery property.

Incorporation of Taxol into Hydrogel

Paclitaxel (taxol) was incorporated into the hydrogel by physical entrapment during gel formation. A stock solution of Taxol at a concentration of approximately 1 mg/mL (solvent system, e.g., DMSO/methanol) was mixed thoroughly with the polymer solution to ensure a uniform distribution of the drug, followed by gelation to obtain a stable Taxol-loaded hydrogel. The bulk hydrogel was designed to contain approximately 1 mg of Taxol per mL, ensuring homogeneous dispersion while maintaining the hydrogel’s integrity. For downstream experiments, the hydrogel was applied at different effective concentrations, depending on the specific assay requirements. This approach allowed the hydrogel to serve as an effective delivery vehicle while accommodating varying experimental conditions, ensuring that the observed biological or material effects could be attributed to Taxol incorporation without compromising hydrogel performance.

Characterization of Hydrogel with Taxol

FTIR and XRD Analysis of Taxol-Loaded Hydrogel

The chemical structure of the hydrogel scaffold with and without fungal extracts (Taxol) and the incorporation of polymers such as sodium alginate and hyaluronic acid were determined by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The FTIR spectra were detected in an Agilent Cary 630 FTIR Benchtop System using the attenuated total reflectance (ATR) sampling technique between the wavenumber ranges 400–4000 cm–1. XRD analysis (Bruker AXS GmbH, Model: Bruker D8 QUEST) was carried out to determine the crystalline or amorphous nature of the hydrogel and to evaluate the possible interactions between Taxol and the polymeric matrix.

Drug Release Study

A controlled drug release assay was conducted in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.0) to evaluate the release behavior of paclitaxel (Taxol) from the 3D-printed hydrogel scaffolds. Accurately weighed amounts of the Taxol-loaded scaffolds were placed in individual sterile vials containing PBS to maintain the sink conditions. At predetermined time intervals (initially every 30 min during the first 6 h and then at 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 120, 168, 240, and 336 h), aliquots of 1 mL were removed from the medium and promptly replaced with an equivalent volume of fresh, prewarmed phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The withdrawn samples were filtered through 0.22 μm syringe filters to remove any suspended particles, and the filtrates were analyzed using a UV–visible spectrophotometer at 227 nm, the wavelength corresponding to the maximum absorbance (λmax) of paclitaxel. The concentration of the drug released at each time interval was quantified by using a standard calibration curve. The calibration curve was generated by measuring the absorbance of Taxol solutions at known concentrations in the same release medium. The release experiment was carried out in triplicate for each sample, and the data were utilized to calculate the cumulative release profile of paclitaxel from the scaffold.

RBC Membrane Stabilization Assay

To make the red blood cell suspension for the membrane stabilization assay, fresh goat blood samples were used and purchased from the local slaughterhouse, Vellore. The blood was initially centrifuged at a rate of 3000 rpm for the duration of 10 min to isolate the red blood cells (RBCs) from the plasma. The RBC pellet was washed three times with freshly prepared 0.9% NaCl isotonic saline solution prepared by dissolving 9 g of NaCl in 1 L of distilled water. Supernatant was discarded following each wash, and the cells were suspended again in saline to eliminate plasma protein and other contaminants. The RBCs were resuspended in isotonic saline after the last wash, and by diluting the packed RBCs with the saline, a 10% (v/v) suspension of RBCs was obtained by adding one part of packed RBCs to nine parts of saline. After 30 min of incubation at 37 °C, the mixtures were centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 rpm. The absorbance of the supernatant was analyzed at 660 nm, and the amount of hemolysis was evaluated. Cytotoxicity Assay

The cytotoxicity of the polymer, drug, and polymer-drug combination was evaluated using the MTT assay on MCF-7 breast cancer cells. , The ATCC provided the MCF-7 cells, which were then cultivated in DMEM media enhanced with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. The cells were kept at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 until they achieved 80–90% confluence. The monolayer cells were then trypsinized using 0.2% trypsin–EDTA, centrifuged, and resuspended in a fresh culture medium. To reach final concentrations between 0 and 100 μg/mL, serial 2-fold dilutions of the polymer, drug, and polymer-drug combination were made in culture medium. In order to facilitate cell adhesion, 50,000 cells per well were planted in a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h. The medium was taken out after the incubation period, and 100 μL of test solutions with different concentrations (0–100 μg/mL) were added into each well. Control wells received only fresh medium. The plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h to facilitate the interaction between the test compounds and the cells. Following incubation, the medium was carefully removed, and 100 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well. To enable the conversion of MTT into purple formazan crystals by mitochondrial dehydrogenases in living cells, the plate was incubated for an additional 4 h. After the supernatant was carefully removed, 100 μL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. The plate was placed in a microplate reader, and the absorbance was measured at 590 nm. The percentage growth inhibition was calculated using the formula %Inhibition=100−(ODofsampleODofControl)×100

Using GraphPad Prism 6 software and nonlinear regression analysis, we calculated the IC50 values. The results were analyzed based on dose–response curves. This methodology ensures reproducibility and accuracy in evaluating the cytotoxic effects of the test compounds on MCF-7 cells.

In Vitro Scratch Assay

The in vitro scratch assay was performed to evaluate the antimigratory effect of a polymer-drug formulation (polymer with Taxol) on MCF-7 breast cancer cells. As the hydrogel alone had no significant effect on MCF-7 cells in the cytotoxicity assay, it was anticipated that the hydrogel system would have no effect on migration; therefore, the scratch assay was aimed at testing the drug-loaded hydrogel system. MCF-7 cells were cultivated at 37 °C in an environment with 5% CO2 in a full DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. After the cells were confluent, a sterile pipet tip was used to make a homogeneous scratch, and PBS was used to remove the detached cells. The polymer-drug formulation exhibited an IC50 of ≥11.63 ± 0.05 μg/mL, indicating the concentration required to inhibit 50% of cell viability. Based on this result, an in vitro scratch assay was conducted using this concentration. The treatment group received the polymer-drug formulation, while migration was monitored at 0, 12, 24, and 48 h under an inverted microscope. To assess cell viability and migration, fluorescence staining was performed, with live cells visualized using a green fluorescent marker. ,

All experiments were carried out in triplicate within the same trial, and the average values are provided in the present work. Results and Discussion

Characterization of Fungal Extract (Taxol)

The presence of paclitaxel in the A. flavus extract was confirmed through UV–Visible spectroscopy, HPLC, and FTIR analyses. The UV–visible absorption spectrum exhibited a pronounced peak at ∼230 nm with a maximum absorbance of 2.4, along with a less intense shoulder between 240 and 260 nm (Figure c). Beyond 300 nm, the absorbance declined sharply, with no major peaks, suggesting the absence of colored impurities or degradation products. The strong absorbance near 230 nm corresponds to electronic transitions of aromatic rings within the paclitaxel structure, while the shoulder at 240–260 nm is attributed to transitions of oxygen-containing functional groups such as esters and ketones. This spectral profile is consistent with that of standard paclitaxel and closely resembles previous reports for Aspergillus fumigatus derived from Taxus species, which showed a λmax of 228 nm and a secondary shoulder at 250 nm. The similarity to earlier fungal paclitaxel profiles strengthens the identification of the compound as paclitaxel. Further confirmation was obtained from HPLC analysis. The chromatogram of the standard paclitaxel displayed a major peak at ∼17.5 min, corresponding to the known retention time under the described chromatographic conditions (Figure a,b). The fungal extract also exhibited a prominent peak at the same retention time, although with lower intensity due to the relatively small amount of paclitaxel present in the crude extract. Several minor peaks appeared at earlier retention times, likely representing additional secondary metabolites. The alignment of retention times between the standard and the fungal extract provides strong evidence that the compound synthesized by A. flavus is paclitaxel, consistent with previous findings of fungal-derived paclitaxel production.

Characterization of fungal extract. (a,b) HPLC analysis of standard paclitaxel and Taxol extracted from A.flavus, respectively, (c) UV–Visible spectroscopy analysis of Taxol, and (d) FTIR analysis of standard paclitaxel and extracted Taxol from A.flavus.

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to compare the functional groups of fungal-derived paclitaxel with those of commercial paclitaxel (Figure d). The FTIR spectrum of the fungal extract revealed characteristic absorption bands at 3332.25 cm–1 (O–H/N–H stretching), 2945.69 cm–1, and 2830.40 cm–1 (C–H stretching of aliphatic chains) and a strong band at 1656.07 cm–1 (CO stretching of ester/amide groups). Additional peaks were observed at 1451.94 cm–1 (CH2 bending) and 1022.01 cm–1 (symmetric C–O stretching). The spectrum of commercial paclitaxel showed comparable features, including an O–H/N–H stretching at 3365.50 cm–1, multiple C–H stretching bands at 2960.48, 2938.82, and 2838.27 cm–1, and ester-related C–O–C stretching at 1181.08, 1131.52, and 1076.03 cm–1. Both spectra shared a distinct fingerprint region with the most intense band appearing at ∼577.78 cm–1, further supporting their structural similarity. These findings indicate that the fungal extract possesses the same functional groups as standard paclitaxel, suggesting that the compound synthesized is chemically equivalent to the authentic drug. Similar spectral characteristics have been reported in previous studies of fungal-derived paclitaxel. For example, Kumar et al. (2019) described Aspergillus fumigatus paclitaxel with OH/NH stretching at 3336–3436 cm–1, aliphatic C–H stretching at 2920–2939 cm–1, ester/amide CO stretching between 1590 and 1735 cm–1, and ester-related C–O stretching between 1045 and 1068 cm–1. The close agreement of these data confirms the biosynthesis of paclitaxel by A. flavus and establishes its chemical resemblance to commercially available drug.

Antioxidant Activity of the Fungal Extract

Fungal extract antioxidant potential was performed using a DPPH radical scavenging assay and compared with ascorbic acid (standard antioxidant). A higher amount of antioxidant activity was exhibited by the ascorbic acid (standard) when compared with all concentrations. A maximum % DPPH inhibition was 50.795% at 100 μg/mL. The moderate antioxidant activity of the fungal extract was shown to reach 43.847% DPPH inhibition at 100 μg/mL. The IC50 value for the DPPH radical inhibition was 4.649 and 6.441 μg/mL for ascorbic acid and the fungal extract, indicating that the extract was less potent than ascorbic acid but still had some antioxidant properties.

Total Phenolic Content of the Fungal Extract

The total phenolic content (TPC) of the fungal extract was calculated using the Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric technique with gallic acid as the standard. A calibration curve with gallic acid concentrations ranging from 200 to 1000 μg/mL revealed a significant linear relationship between absorbance and concentration (R 2 = 0.9857). The regression equation obtained from the calibration was applied to calculate the gallic acid equivalent (GAE) of the fungal extract. The absorbance values of the extract at 500, 750, and 1000 μg/mL were 1.461, 1.665, and 1.989, corresponding to 535.9, 695.6, and 947.3 μg/mL GAE, respectively. These results demonstrated a concentration-dependent increase in the phenolic content, confirming the presence of substantial amounts of phenolic compounds in the extract. Although paclitaxel itself is a diterpenoid and is not directly quantified by phenolic assays, measurement of TPC provides complementary evidence of the bioactive potential of the crude extract. The observed phenolic compounds, along with other secondary metabolites, likely contribute to the antioxidant activity and may exert synergistic effects. While UV–Vis, HPLC, and FTIR analyses specifically confirmed the presence of paclitaxel, the TPC and antioxidant assays offer additional insights into the broader functional profile of A. flavus metabolites.

Characterization of Hydrogel

Swelling Degree Analysis of Alginate: Hyaluronic Acid Scaffold

The swelling behavior of the alginate–hyaluronic acid hydrogels was evaluated over 6 days (Table ). Among the formulations, the 2:3 ratio exhibited the highest swelling capacity, exceeding 1600% by day 5, followed by the 2:2 formulation, which also showed substantial water uptake. In contrast, formulations with lower alginate contents, such as 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3, demonstrated markedly lower swelling ratios, with the 1:1 formulation displaying the least swelling throughout the study. Most formulations reached equilibrium after 3 days, with swelling increasing rapidly from day 1 to day 3, followed by a plateau phase. The variation in swelling behavior is attributed to differences in polymer concentration, cross-linking density, and network porosity. Formulations with higher polymer content (2:2 and 2:3) exhibited reduced cross-linking density and greater porosity, allowing for higher water absorption. Conversely, the lower swelling ratios of the 1:1 and 1:2 formulations reflect denser cross-linking and limited pore volume, which restrict water uptake but improve mechanical stability. A swelling ratio below 100% in these formulations indicates absorption of less than the hydrogel’s dry weight in water, confirming a tightly cross-linked structure. These observations are consistent with those of previous studies. Kowalski et al. (2024) reported that sodium alginate–acrylic hydrogels with higher cross-linking densities showed reduced swelling potential, whereas lower cross-linking promoted extensive water absorption. The trends observed in the present formulations align with this pattern, underscoring the role of network density in determining the swelling behavior. From an application perspective, the 2:2 and 2:3 hydrogels, with their high swelling capacity, may be advantageous for moisture-retentive uses such as wound dressings and absorbent biomaterials. In contrast, the lower-swelling 1:1 and 1:2 hydrogels are better suited for load-bearing applications, where dimensional stability is required. The 2:1 formulation displayed an intermediate swelling ratio, balancing water uptake with structural integrity, making it a versatile option for drug delivery systems or controlled-release applications.

Viscosity of Polymers

Viscosity measurements of hydrogels with varying sodium alginate (SA) and hyaluronic acid (HA) ratios revealed distinct trends in rheological behavior (Figure a). Increasing the alginate concentration significantly elevated viscosity, thereby enhancing structural integrity. The lowest viscosity was observed in the 1:2 formulation (30 mPa·s), followed by 1:1 (40.3 mPa·s) and 1:3 (62.4 mPa·s). These relatively low values indicate poor mechanical stability and reduced printability due to the dominance of HA. By contrast, formulations with a higher alginate content displayed markedly greater viscosities: 2:1 (111.6 mPa·s), 2:2 (140.4 mPa·s), and 2:3 (256.8 mPa·s). Although the 2:3 formulation exhibited the highest viscosity, extrusion during bioprinting was challenging, leading to difficulties in achieving uniform scaffold structures. The 2:1 formulation, with its intermediate viscosity, provided the best balance between smooth extrusion and postprinting stability, making it the most suitable candidate for bioprinting applications. These findings are consistent with previous reports. Serafin et al. (2023) demonstrated that increasing the alginate concentration in alginate–gelatin–HA hydrogels enhanced viscoelastic properties, including storage and loss moduli, thereby improving both structural stability and printability. The present results confirm that polymer composition directly influences viscosity and, consequently, the rheological behavior that is essential for extrusion-based bioprinting.

Degradation Study of Sodium Alginate: Hyaluronic Acid Scaffold

The degradation behavior of the 3D-bioprinted sodium alginate–hyaluronic acid hydrogel (2:1 ratio) was evaluated under simulated physiological conditions using gastric fluid (pH 1.2) and intestinal fluid (pH 6.8) at 37 °C with continuous stirring (100 rpm). Weight loss was determined at different time intervals (Figure b). The hydrogel exhibited a biphasic degradation profile. An initial rapid degradation was observed within the first 30 min, with 21.4% weight loss. This was followed by a slower phase between 30 and 90 min, during which weight loss increased gradually from ∼22% to ∼30%. After 90 min, the degradation rate accelerated again, reaching ∼45% at 120 min, ∼55% at 150 min, and ∼77% after 180 min. The biphasic degradation suggests a two-step mechanism: an initial surface erosion phase followed by bulk erosion characterized by internal matrix swelling, polymer chain relaxation, and water penetration. The ability of the hydrogel to maintain structural integrity during the first 90 min, followed by progressive breakdown, indicates its suitability for controlled release or short-term biomedical applications. pH played an important role in the degradation behavior. At acidic pH (1.2), protonation of alginate chains led to transient stabilization through gel-like structure formation, delaying degradation. At an intestinal pH (6.8), both alginate and hyaluronic acid ionize more readily, promoting water absorption, swelling, and hydrolytic cleavage of polymer chains. The lyophilization step ensured accurate weight loss measurement by eliminating the water content and quantifying only polymer degradation. Comparable trends were reported for other hydrogel systems. Kocak et al. (2024) observed a similar biphasic pattern in injectable chitosan-based hydrogels, with initial rapid degradation followed by progressive breakdown reaching 60–70% over several weeks. Likewise, Serafin et al. (2023) reported rapid degradation of alginate–gelatin–hyaluronic acid hydrogels in the first week (∼55%), followed by slower degradation reaching ∼90% in the second week. The sodium alginate-hyaluronic acid scaffold degrades quickly, losing around 77% of its weight in 180 min. This makes it suitable for short-term applications, such as oral drug delivery or temporary scaffolds.

Thermogravimetric Analysis of SA: HA Scaffold

The thermal stability of sodium alginate–hyaluronic acid (SA: HA) scaffold was assessed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) (Figure c). Two primary steps comprise the degrading process. An initial decrease in the weight below 150 °C was associated with the elimination of residual moisture and physically adsorbed water. The first major decomposition step occurred between 150 and 310 °C, with a mass loss of 48.27%, attributable to cleavage of the polysaccharide backbone and decomposition of hydroxyl, carboxyl, and acetyl functional groups of alginate and hyaluronic acid. A second degradation phase between 310 and 400 °C resulted in an additional 8.75% weight loss, likely associated with the breakdown of more thermally resistant polymer fragments and partial carbonization of the scaffold. At temperatures above 400 °C, degradation slowed, and a final residual mass of 42.93% remained at 599.6 °C. The high char content suggests the presence of sodium-derived inorganic salts and thermally stable cross-linked structures. Comparison with a supramolecular hydrogel based on carboxymethylcellulose (SHG4) reported by Kurdtabar et al. (2024) showed a three-step degradation profile: 6% mass loss at 100–180 °C (peak ∼120 °C) due to water removal, ∼27% loss at 190–320 °C (peak ∼290 °C) from intramolecular reactions releasing CO, CO2, NH3, and H2O, and ∼35% loss at 340–470 °C (peak ∼400 °C) due to backbone scission, leaving ∼32% residue at 600 °C. The SA/HA scaffold displayed a similar initial dehydration step and overlapping main-chain degradation range, indicating a comparable polysaccharide thermal chemistry. However, the higher residual mass of SA/HA (42.93% vs 32%) reflects the contribution of sodium alginate-derived inorganic content and more thermally resistant cross-links, resulting in greater thermal resilience than SHG.

OCT Analysis of Hydrogel

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) was used to assess the pore distribution and structural integrity of the bioprinted sodium alginate–hyaluronic acid scaffolds. Quantitative analysis of 40 OCT slices revealed uniform pore distribution throughout the scaffold depth, with a mean pore size of 32.77 μm2, a pore density of 30,222.03 pores/mm2, and an average pore area proportion of 13.43%. Minimal variation in pore count and pore area between slices indicated the high reproducibility of the fabrication process. Large-field OCT imaging confirmed homogeneous pore arrangement without erosion, collapsed regions, or fabrication defects (Figure d). Static OCT analysis demonstrated well-defined pore boundaries with a consistent wall thickness and no irregularities. Dynamic OCT further revealed uniform pore contraction under mechanical loading, followed by complete recovery upon removal of stress (Figure e). This reversible deformation highlights the scaffold’s viscoelastic properties, mechanical resilience, and structural stability under dynamic conditions. The integration of static, dynamic, and large-field OCT imaging provided comprehensive insights into the scaffold architecture. The layer-by-layer data confirmed high structural repeatability across the 40 cross sections, a critical parameter for consistent cell seeding and nutrient diffusion. The measured pore dimensions fall within the optimal range for tissue engineering, supporting both the mechanical strength and biological permeability. Large-field imaging additionally demonstrated negligible regional variability, underscoring the reproducibility and fidelity of the bioprinting process (Figure f). Collectively, the OCT analysis confirmed that the bioprinted scaffold possesses a uniform and stable microarchitecture capable of withstanding mechanical stress while maintaining porosity. These characteristics are favorable for biomedical applications, including drug delivery, tissue regeneration, and implantable constructions.

Characterization of Hydrogel with and without Fungal Extract (Taxol)

FTIR analysis confirmed the incorporation of paclitaxel into the sodium alginate–hyaluronic acid hydrogel (Figure a). The unloaded hydrogel exhibited characteristic polysaccharide peaks, including a broad O–H stretching band around 3291 cm–1, a CO stretching band at 1634 cm–1, and COO– symmetric stretching at 1411 cm–1, consistent with alginate. Pure paclitaxel displayed distinct functional group peaks, including an O–H stretching at 3373 cm–1, C–H stretching at 2945 and 2834 cm–1, and carbonyl-associated bands at 1653 and 1419 cm–1. In the Taxol-loaded hydrogel, several characteristic peaks were shifted or broadened, such as the O–H stretching at 3371 cm–1 and the CO band at 1664 cm–1, suggesting hydrogen bonding interactions between paclitaxel and the hydrogel matrix. The retention of both drug- and hydrogel-specific peaks, without the appearance of new peaks, indicates that paclitaxel was incorporated through physical interactions rather than chemical modification, ensuring stable entrapment within the polymer network.

(a) FTIR and (b) XRD analysis of hydrogel with and without Taxol. (c) Drug release profile of Taxol from hydrogel.

XRD analysis further supported these observations (Figure b). Both unloaded and drug-loaded hydrogels exhibited broad halos at 20°–25° 2θ, characteristic of amorphous polymeric systems. − The absence of sharp diffraction peaks confirmed the noncrystalline nature of sodium alginate and hyaluronic acid, consistent with their irregular and hydrated molecular chains. Following paclitaxel incorporation, the diffraction pattern showed a slight increase in overall intensity and a reduction in the halo area, but no crystalline peaks were detected. This suggests that paclitaxel was dispersed in an amorphous or molecularly disordered state within the scaffold rather than forming separate crystalline domains. The amorphous dispersion of paclitaxel within the hydrogel matrix is advantageous for biomedical applications. Amorphous systems enhance the solubility and bioavailability of hydrophobic drugs and ensure uniform drug distribution, which supports controlled and sustained release. These findings confirm that the sodium alginate–hyaluronic acid scaffold provides an effective platform for stable paclitaxel incorporation without disrupting the amorphous polymer structure.

In Vitro Drug Release Assay

Taxol release profile from the 3D-printed sodium alginate–hyaluronic acid scaffolds showed a biphasic pattern, with a continuous release phase after an initial burst (Figure c). During the first few hours, rapid drug liberation occurred, likely due to desorption of paclitaxel loosely associated with the scaffold surface or entrapped within superficial pores. This was followed by a gradual decline in the release rate, consistent with diffusion-controlled transport through the polymeric matrix and relaxation of swollen polymer chains. Over 14 days, the scaffolds achieved a cumulative release exceeding 90% of the encapsulated paclitaxel, indicating high release efficiency. A plateau phase after approximately 240 h suggested that a fraction of the drug was more tightly entrapped within the inner hydrogel network, requiring extended diffusion pathways or partial scaffold degradation for complete release. The biphasic behavior observed is typical of hydrogel-based drug delivery systems, where porosity, swelling, and cross-linking density influence release kinetics. The interconnected porous structure of the printed scaffolds likely contributed to both the initial burst and the subsequent controlled diffusion. Similar release dynamics have been reported in polymeric hydrogels, which maintain therapeutic drug concentrations locally while minimizing systemic exposure. , From a therapeutic standpoint, the biphasic release profile is advantageous in oncology. The initial burst facilitates rapid attainment of cytotoxic paclitaxel levels at the target site, while the sustained phase ensures prolonged drug availability, potentially reducing the dosing frequency. Such localized and sustained release strategies may overcome challenges associated with systemic chemotherapy, including rapid metabolism, multidrug resistance, and systemic toxicity.

Anti-Inflammatory Activity

The anti-inflammatory capacity of the taxol-carrying hydrogel was determined by its performance in prohibiting heat-induced protein denaturation and erythrocyte membrane stabilization. [Figure a­(i)] demonstrates that absorption at 600 nm was drastically reduced in the presence of high concentrations (0.2–1.0 mg/mL) of the hydrogel, denoting a dose effect on its ability to protect proteins against denaturation. The control sample (without the hydrogel) had the highest absorbance (∼0.031), and this indicated the optimization of denaturation. The lowest concentrations (0.2 mg/mL) showed a percentage inhibition of 35.48%, with the highest (1.0 mg/mL) inhibiting the protein denaturation by 85.48% [Figure a­(ii)]. This trend shows that the concentration of hydrogel exhibits a strong correlation with the capability of protecting proteins from a strong thermal load. The recorded inhibition indicates that the taxol-enhanced hydrogel formulation is a strong possessor of anti-inflammatory effects. This effect may be due to Taxol, which is a known microtubule stabilizer, acts on proteins, and decreases thermal denaturation. The hydrogel matrix provides additional assistance, as it can provide a stabilizing microenvironment.

One of the established models of determining anti-inflammatory properties is the stabilization of the erythrocyte membrane. In the current experiments, hemolysis by the taxol-loaded hydrogel was shown to have a concentration-dependent low hemolytic activity, as deduced by a reduction in the absorbance of the hydrogel at 660 nm [Figure b­(i)]. Absorbance in the control sample was greatest (∼0.48), with the lower absorbance values at 1.0 mg/mL (∼0.10), suggesting less lysis of the membrane. As expected, this percentage inhibition was dependent on the concentration [Figure b­(ii)], and the maximum degree of membrane stabilization (∼79%) was obtained at 1.0 mg/mL. These findings validate the RBC membrane stabilization activity of the hydrogel under hypotonic stress in addition to continued evidence of its potential use in anti-inflammatory applications. This effect can be attributed to the component Taxol, which might cause phospholipase inhibition or stabilize lipid bilayers so that they are unable to lyse. Moreover, Taxol retention and bioactivity will bring additional protection maintained by the hydrogel matrix. The two assays confirm that Taxol-loaded hydrogel is effective as an anti-inflammatory agent. The % inhibition observed with dose dependence over the protein denaturation model as well as the membrane stabilizer model highlights the therapeutic promise of the given formulation as a treatment intervention in case of inflammation-related disorders. Together, these findings indicate that Taxol-loaded hydrogels exhibit promising anti-inflammatory activity in vitro. Further research is needed to understand the underlying molecular pathways and assess their potential in vivo or clinical contexts.

Cytotoxicity of Taxol Loaded Hydrogel

The polymer scaffold alone exhibited minimal cytotoxicity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells. Even at the highest tested concentration (100 μg/mL), cell viability remained at ∼57%, and the IC50 value was >100 μg/mL (Figure a). These findings indicate that the sodium alginate–hyaluronic acid hydrogel is biocompatible and does not adversely affect cellular metabolism within the tested range. By contrast, free paclitaxel demonstrated a strong dose-dependent cytotoxic response. Cell viability was reduced to ∼68% at 10 μg/mL and reached complete inhibition at 60 μg/mL, with an IC50 of 19.9 ± 0.05 μg/mL (Figure b). This behavior is consistent with the known mechanism of paclitaxel as a microtubule-stabilizing agent that induces apoptosis and inhibits essential cellular functions. The paclitaxel-loaded hydrogel (polymer + drug) showed enhanced cytotoxicity compared with paclitaxel alone, with an IC50 of 11.63 ± 0.05 μg/mL (Figure c). This represents nearly a 1.7-fold increase in potency relative to that with free drug treatment. Enhanced activity may be attributed to improved intracellular uptake, prolonged release, or polymer-assisted retention within the cells. Similar improvements have been reported with paclitaxel-loaded nanoemulsions, where encapsulation reduced the IC50 compared to free paclitaxel. Notably, the hydrogel-based system demonstrated cytotoxic potency even greater than that of nanoemulsion formulations, suggesting advantages for scaffold-mediated delivery. At 20 μg/mL, the viability decreased to 29%, while a complete loss of viability was observed at 50 μg/mL. These results suggest a synergistic interaction between paclitaxel and the hydrogel carrier, enhancing drug uptake and cytotoxic efficacy while retaining polymer biocompatibility.

Cytotoxic effects of (a) polymers (sodium alginate: hyaluronic acid), (b) Drug alone (only Taxol), and (c) combination of polymers and drug against MCF-7 cells. (d) Invitro scratch assay of drug–polymer combination against MCF-7 breast cancer cells.

The antimigratory effect of the paclitaxel-loaded hydrogel was evaluated using a wound healing assay at the IC50 concentration (11.63 μg/mL). In untreated controls, MCF-7 cells progressively migrated to close the scratch gap over 48 h. In contrast, treatment with the paclitaxel-loaded scaffold markedly inhibited cell migration (Figure d). After 12 h, minimal cellular entry into the wound area was observed, with reduced fluorescence intensity at the wound edges, suggesting early cytotoxic effects and impaired motility. Between 24 and 48 h, the scratch remained largely uncovered, with cell density at the borders reduced compared with controls. At 48 h, fluorescence intensity within the wound area was minimal, indicating significant loss of viability in addition to suppressed migration. The inhibition of migration and reduction in viability are consistent with paclitaxel’s antimitotic mechanism, whereby disruption of microtubule dynamics prevents both proliferation and motility. , These results confirm that the paclitaxel-loaded hydrogel not only enhances cytotoxicity but also effectively suppresses metastatic potential, highlighting its therapeutic promise for localized breast cancer treatment.

Future Directions

This study demonstrates the feasibility of producing Taxol using fungi and delivering it through a hydrogel system; however, several avenues remain to advance this platform. Large-scale production via submerged fermentation or bioreactor systems is necessary to generate clinically relevant amounts of Taxol. Genetic and metabolic engineering of Aspergillus flavus could further improve yield and consistency. In vivo studies are essential to evaluate the pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, biocompatibility, therapeutic efficacy, long-term toxicity, immunogenicity, and hydrogel degradation under physiological conditions. Ensuring reproducibility and stability of the bioprinted scaffoldsincluding print fidelity, batch-to-batch uniformity, and sustained drug releaseis another key consideration. Incorporating stimuli-responsive features, such as pH- or enzyme-sensitive release, could enhance delivery precision. Scaling the system for codelivery of multiple therapeutics or growth factors may broaden its applications. Finally, advanced 3D printing strategies hold promise for designing patient-specific hydrogel architectures, enabling personalized and targeted therapies.

Conclusion

This study establishes that Aspergillus flavus isolated from wastewater can serve as a sustainable microbial source of paclitaxel, expanding the biosynthetic diversity of this important anticancer drug. Embedding fungal-derived Taxol in a bioprinted sodium alginate/hyaluronic acid hydrogel demonstrated that polymer composition directly influences printability, swelling, degradation, and drug release, with a 2:1 SA/HA ratio identified as optimal. Notably, hydrogel-based delivery enhanced the cytotoxicity of Taxol against breast cancer cells compared to the free drug, highlighting the utility of 3D bioprinting for controlled, localized drug administration. Combining microbial biosynthesis with advanced biomaterials addresses two major challenges in chemotherapy: limited drug availability and inefficient systemic delivery. By reducing the reliance on plant-derived paclitaxel, minimizing systemic toxicity, and enabling patient-specific delivery, this approach offers a promising, sustainable route for effective cancer therapy.
