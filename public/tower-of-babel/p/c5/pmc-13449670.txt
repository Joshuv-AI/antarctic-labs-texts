PITX2C deficiency promotes arrhythmogenic remodelling via oxidative stress in atrial myocytes
Authors: Andy Kim, Sébastien Gauvrit, Frederick S Vizeacoumar, Michelle M Collins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic variants upstream of the paired-like homeodomain transcription factor 2 (PITX2) are the strongest risk variants associated with atrial fibrillation. However, the mechanisms downstream of PITX2 are not completely understood. Here, we explore the role of PITX2 in oxidative metabolism and stress as a unifying mechanism of arrhythmogenesis. Transcriptomic analysis of Pitx2c-deficient neonatal rat atrial myocytes indicates oxidative phosphorylation as the top dysregulated pathway. Extracellular flux analysis reveals a functional decrease in oxidative metabolism in Pitx2c-deficient atrial cardiomyocytes which was accompanied by an accumulation of mitochondrial-specific ROS. We additionally assessed cardiomyocyte calcium cycling and observed an increased frequency of pro-arrhythmogenic mechanisms including altered calcium traces. Further, we identified a role of PITX2C in the sarcomere, as Pitx2c-deficient atrial cardiomyocytes display altered Titin localization. Notably, we observe that antioxidant treatment with N-acetylcysteine partially attenuates arrhythmogenic phenotypes including abnormal calcium cycling and Titin organization in Pitx2c-deficient atrial cardiomyocytes. Together, these data suggest that PITX2C deficiency is associated with atrial metabolism defects that ramifies cardiomyocyte dysfunction through oxidative stress.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, PITX2, Neonatal rat atrial myocyte, Mitochondrial dysfunction, Reactive oxygen species

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia. Genome wide association studies have identified genetic variants on chromosome 4q25, near the paired-like homeodomain transcription factor 2 (PITX2) gene, as the most significantly associated with AF [1], [2], [3]. Animal models of PITX2 insufficiency have highlighted its critical role in AF pathogenesis. Studies in Pitx2 heterozygous mice have shown that PITX2 has a transcriptional role in maintaining cardiac electrical stability and structure with targets in ion channels, calcium handling, and intercalated disk formation [4], [5]. Similarly, we have previously demonstrated that pitx2c deficient zebrafish develop sarcomere and metabolic defects that alter cardiac function in early larval stages and which contribute to electrical instability and structural remodelling in adult zebrafish [6]. Although there is a clear role of PITX2 in maintaining components of cardiac rhythm, how these components are linked is incompletely understood.

One central mechanism of PITX2 in AF pathogenesis we propose is through its transcriptional regulation of oxidative metabolism and stress [7]. It has been previously demonstrated that pitx2c-deficient zebrafish have reduced oxidative phosphorylation capacity and Pitx2 transcriptionally regulates redox genes [6]. Additionally, human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) PITX2 knockout models have shown that PITX2 deficiency alters atrial metabolism resulting in a metabolic shift towards glycolysis that contributes to structural and electrical remodelling [8]. Recently, reactive oxygen species (ROS) have been demonstrated as a unifying mechanism of AF susceptibility in Pitx2 insufficient mice; pharmacological targeting of ROS-generated lipid dicarbonyls prevented arrhythmia vulnerability in Pitx2 insufficient mice [9]. However, an interesting question that remains is whether PITX2-dependent alterations in intracellular calcium dynamics are driven by oxidative stress. Therefore, to further support this unifying mechanism, we characterized the role of ROS in Pitx2c deficiency in neonatal rat atrial myocytes. We demonstrate that reduction of ROS ablates arrhythmia susceptibility, restoring aberrant calcium cycling and structural abnormalities.

Materials and methods

Isolation of neonatal rat atrial myocytes (NRAM)

All protocols were in accordance with the guidelines detailed by the Canadian Council of Animal Care by the Animal Research Ethics Board at the University of Saskatchewan (AUP #20220090).

This protocol was adapted from Wu et al. [10]. 2-day old Sprague Dawley (Charles River) neonatal rat pups were euthanized by decapitation and the heart was harvested in PBS supplemented with 5 mM glucose. Ventricles and large vessels were removed, and atria were subjected to enzymatic digestion using a lysis buffer solution containing 20 mM HEPES-NaOH, 130 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 1 mM NaH2PO4, 5 mM glucose, 15 mg/mL DNase I (Roche 104,159), collagenase Type II (ThermoFisher 17,101,015), and pancreatin (Sigma P3292). Multiple rounds of enzymatic digestion were performed where the tissue was incubated with 10 mL lysis buffer at 37 °C for 10 min. Supernatant from the first round of digestion was discarded and subsequent rounds were performed with the supernatant harvested in 5 mL of horse serum. Once the tissue was completely digested, cells were pelleted at 300 x g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was then re-suspended in pre-warmed red blood cell lysis buffer (Sigma 11,814,389,001) and incubated for 3 min at 37 °C after which, 20 mL of DMEM/F-12 (ThermoFisher 11,320,033) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin was added. Cells were pelleted at 300 x g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was then again re-suspended in DMEM/F-12 with 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin and plated on uncoated cell culture dishes for 90 min to remove fibroblasts and endothelial cells. Unadhered cells were then collected, passed through a 70 μm mesh filter, centrifuged at 300 xg for 5 min at 4 °C, re-suspended in culture medium (DMEM/F-12, 10% FBS, 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, 5% horse serum, 3 mM Na-pyruvate, 0.1 mM ascorbic acid, 1:200 insulin/transferrin/Na-Selenite solution, 0.2% BSA, and 20uM cytarabine (Sigma C1768). Cells were seeded at a cell density of 60 × 104 cells/cm2. The medium was replaced the next day to remove dead cells. To assess the effects of oxidative stress, cells were treated using N-acetylcysteine (NAC) at 1 mM for 24 h prior to experiments.

Biological replicates (N) were defined as each independent cardiomyocyte isolation from 10 to 15 neonatal pups and at least 2 biological replicates were performed for each experiment.

Pitx2c siRNA transfection

Cells were left to reach 60–70% confluency and transfected with 25 nM, 50 nM, and 100 nM non-targeting siRNA (Invitrogen 4390843), Pitx2c s132548 siRNA (Invitrogen 439077) or Pitx2c s132549 siRNA (Invitrogen 439077) diluted in OptiMEM (ThermoFisher 31985070) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (ThermoFisher 13778150) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. After 24 h, the transfection medium changed into the culture medium stated above. Cells were harvested at 24, 48, and 72 h post transfection to determine the time of maximal Pitx2c knockdown. Cells were then pelleted and stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction was performed. All functional assays were performed using 100 nM Pitx2c s132548, 48 h post-transfection.

RNA extraction and reverse-transcriptase-qPCR

RNA extraction was performed using the Qiagen RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen 74,034) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. RNA concentration and A260/A280 ratios was assessed by spectrophotometry (Nanodrop One). First strand cDNA was synthesized using Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit for RT-qPCR with dsDNase (ThermoFisher K1641). Gene knockdown was confirmed by RT-qPCR using Actb (B-actin) as the reference gene. Each qPCR reaction was 10 μL containing 1× PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (ThermoFisher A25742), 0.2 μM forward primer, 0.2 μM reverse primer and 2 μL template cDNA. To analyze the fold change of the genes of interest, the delta-delta Ct method was applied which uses the formula 2- ΔΔCt where ΔΔCt = ΔCt (treated sample) – ΔCt (untreated sample) and ΔCt = Ct (gene of interest) -Ct (reference gene). Primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are provided in Supplemental Table 1.

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from quadruplicates of Pitx2c-deficient neonatal rat atrial myocytes and their controls. RNA yield and quality were assessed and samples with sufficient RNA quality (RNA integrity number > 8) were sequenced. Poly A-selected libraries were sequenced by the Centre for Applied Genomics at the University of Toronto and mapped to the rat GRCr8 genome. Quality control was performed using FastQC v.0.12.1. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 v.1.26.0 and principal component analysis performed. Significant differentially expressed genes were classified as log2 (Fold Change) > ±0.85 (|1.5| fold change) and p-adj <0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis was then performed to identify global changes in gene expression patterns using the Hallmark gene set. Significantly enriched pathways were classified as false discovery rate < 0.25.

Identification of genes associated with ChIP-seq peaks

ChIP-seq peaks identified in GSM116257 [5] were obtained from the BED file. Peaks were annotated to the nearest gene using GENCODE mouse genome annotation (GRCm39/mm39). Gene annotations were obtained in GTF format and converted to transcript database objects using the GenomicFeatures package in R. Peak-to-gene associations were performed using ChIPseeker (R library), assigning each peak to the closest transcription start site (TSS) within a ± 3 kb window; peaks located outside this window were annotated to the nearest gene based on genomic distance. Gene identifiers were mapped to official mouse gene symbols using org.Mm.eg.db. A non-redundant list of genes associated with ChIP-seq peaks was generated for downstream analyses.

Seahorse XFp

Seahorse mitochondrial stress test (Agilent 103010–100) and the glycolytic rate assay (Agilent 103,346–100) were performed on neonatal rat atrial myocytes seeded into 8-well XFp microplates at a density of 40,000 cells/well.

MitoStress test

Cells were washed with XF DMEM medium (Agilent 103,575–100) supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, and 10 mM glucose and placed in a 37 °C without CO2 for 45 min. An automated Seahorse XFp protocol was performed consisting of an initial calibration/equilibration, followed by injection of drugs. For the mitochondrial stress test, ports were loaded with (A) 2 μM oligomycin, (B) 2 μM FCCP, and (C) 0.5 μM rotenone/antimycin A.

Glycolytic rate assay

Cells were washed with XF DMEM medium (Agilent 103575–100) supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, and 10 mM glucose and placed in a 37 °C without CO2 for 45 min. An automated Seahorse XFp protocol was performed consisting of an initial calibration/equilibration, followed by injection of drugs. For the glycolytic rate assay, ports were loaded with (A) 0.5 μM rotenone/antimycin A and (B) 2-deoxy-d-glucose (2-DG).

Mitochondrial DNA content

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was isolated extracted using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen 69506). Relative mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number was measured by a qPCR-based method to calculate the ratio of mtDNA/nDNA. mtDNA was quantified using primers for mt-co1 and nDNA was quantified using primers for polg. Each qPCR reaction was 10 μL containing 1× PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix, 0.2 μM forward primer, 0.2 μM reverse primer and 3 μL gDNA. To analyze the fold change, the delta-delta Ct method was applied which uses the formula 2- ΔΔCt where ΔΔCt = ΔCt (treated sample) – ΔCt (untreated sample) and ΔCt = Ct (mt-co1) -Ct (polg). Primer sequences used for qPCR are provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Western blot

Cell lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific 89900) with 1× proteinase/phosphatase cocktail (Thermo Scientific 78442). The protein concentration in cell lysates was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific 23,225). Subsequently, proteins were separated on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Following a 1.5-h blocking step with 5% BSA in Tris-buffered saline-Tween-20 (TBS-T), membranes were incubated with primary antibodies in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were used for 1 h at room temperature and protein signals were visualized using the Biorad Chemi Doc Imager. β -actin served as the internal control. Densiometric analysis was performed in ImageJ. Antibodies used are provided in Supplemental Table 2.

MitoSOX

Neonatal rat atrial myocytes were loaded with 0.5 μM MitoSOX Red (ThermoFisher M3600) diluted in Opti-MEM. The sample was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min after which the cells were washed 3 times with pre-warmed Opti-MEM. Imaging was performed with the Nikon Eclipse Ti2 CSU-X1 using a 60× objective and cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 while imaging. Images were analyzed from three regions of interest (50 px x 50px) within the cytosol and taking the mean of these measurements for each cardiomyocyte using Fiji [11] software.

JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using JC-1. Neonatal rat atrial myocytes were loaded with 2 μM JC-1 (CST 92891) diluted in Opti-MEM. The sample was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min after which the cells were washed with pre-warmed PBS. JC-1 monomers were excited at a wavelength of 485 nm and detected at the emission wavelength of 530 nm whereas JC-1 aggregates were excited at a wavelength of 535 nm and detected at the emission wavelength of 590 nm using the Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader. The ratio of aggregated and monomeric JC-1 was used to quantify changes in mitochondrial membrane potential.

Fluo-8 AM calcium imaging

Calcium imaging was performed in spontaneously beating neonatal rat atrial myocytes without field stimulation. NRAMs were loaded with 5 μM of the calcium indicator Fluo-8 AM (ab142773) diluted in Opti-MEM. The sample was incubated for 90 min at 37 °C. After loading, the cells were washed with Opti-MEM for 5 min, 3 times each. Calcium imaging was performed with the Nikon eclipse Ti2 using a 60× objective. Movies were taken with a size of 1192 × 1192 pixels for 15 s with 50 ms delay. Calcium analysis was performed in ImageJ by drawing a region of interest in the cytosol of the cell and fluorescence values were exported to OriginLabs for analysis using the calcium transient analysis app [12].

Immunocytochemistry

Neonatal rat atrial myocytes cultured in Ibidi dishes (Ibidi 80807) were washed in PBS, fixed in PEM fixative (3% paraformaldehyde (PFA), 100 mM Pipes, 1 mM MgSO4, and 2 mM EGTA in distilled water and adjusted to pH 7.4) for 15 min at room temperature, then washed with ice cold PBS, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature, and blocked with 10% sheep serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody labelling was performed overnight at 4 °C, washed with PBS, and labelled with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 CSU-X1 using a 40× objective. Primary antibodies used were alpha-Actinin (1:100, Sigma A7732), Myomesin (1:20, DSHB mMac) and Titin (1:100, DHSB 9 D10). Secondary antibody used was anti-mouse 647 (1:500).

Images were analyzed in Fiji software for cellular morphology, PatternJ [13] plugin for Titin co-localization, and Ridge Detection [14], [15] plugin for sarcomere density. Sarcomere length and organization was analyzed using SotaTool [16].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. The specific statistical tests used are reported in the figure legends. Results were considered to be statistically significant if the probability (p) value was <0.05 as the threshold to reject the null hypothesis.

Results

The PITX2C gene regulatory network in the post-natal atria

To assess the gene regulatory network downstream of PITX2C in the post-natal heart, we leveraged neonatal rat atrial myocytes (NRAMs) transfected with Pitx2 siRNA. We tested knockdown efficiency of two siRNAs (ThermoFisher PITX2 s132548 and s132549) and observed similar levels of transcript knockdown with both reagents (Fig. S1); therefore, we decided to continue all analyses with the s132548 siRNA. Significant downregulation of Pitx2c mRNA (86.03%) was observed at 48 h post transfection compared to control siRNA treated cells (Fig. S1). Poly(A) bulk RNA-seq was then performed to identify PITX2C-dependent gene expression changes. Principal component analysis identified close and distinct clustering of both Pitx2c deficient and control cardiomyocytes (Fig. 1A). 230 transcripts showed significant differential expression, including significant downregulation of Pitx2c (Fig. 1B). Gene set enrichment analysis identified oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, and glycolysis amongst the top dysregulated pathways, suggesting a metabolic role for PITX2C (Fig. 1C). Transcripts enriched in oxidative phosphorylation encode mitochondrial electron transport chain proteins including complex I and IV subunits and ATP synthase subunits (Fig. 1D). To determine whether these changes are direct transcriptional targets of PITX2, we analyzed a previously published ChIP-seq dataset performed in 12-week-old mouse heart tissue [5]. We identify direct PITX2C targets encoding complex I, complex IV, and ATP synthase in the mouse heart, suggesting a potential transcriptional role for PITX2C in regulating metabolic processes and oxidative stress (Table S3).

PITX2C regulates atrial cardiomyocyte metabolism and oxidative stress

To evaluate if the PITX2C-induced dysregulation of mitochondrial oxidative metabolism gene expression translated to abnormal mitochondrial bioenergetics, we utilized the XFp Extracellular Flux Analyzer to measure oxygen consumption rate (OCR) as an indirect quantification of oxidative phosphorylation. OCR was assessed at multiple time points before and after the addition of different inhibitors (Fig. 2A) of the mitochondrial electron transport chain which allow for the evaluation of OCR based parameters. Pitx2c-deficient NRAMs exhibit decreased non-mitochondrial and basal respiration in comparison to control cells (Fig. 2B). The addition of oligomycin (an ATP-synthase inhibitor) allows us to distinguish whether the changes in basal OCR are subsequent of decreases in ATP-linked mitochondrial OCR or non-ATP linked OCR as a secondary consequence to electron transport chain proton leak. We observe no difference between control and Pitx2c-deficient NRAMs indicating the decrease in basal OCR was primarily due to changes in ATP-linked OCR (Fig. 2B). Following, FCCP (electron transport chain uncoupler) was injected to mimic heightened energy demands inducing maximal respiration capacity to reduce oxygen into water, as a component of normal respiration. Pitx2c-deficient cardiomyocytes show decreased maximal respiration and spare respiratory capacity (Fig. 2B). To assess whether this decrease in mitochondrial respiration was due to changes in cellular mitochondrial content, we quantified the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) content and observed no change between control and Pitx2c-deficient cardiomyocytes (Fig. 2C).

To evaluate whether the proposed transcriptional role of PITX2C within the electron transport chain translated to functional alterations in protein expression, we performed western blot analysis of electron transport chain complex subunits including NDUFS1 (Complex I), SDHA (Complex II), UQCRSF1 (Complex III), MT-CO1 (Complex IV), and ATP5A1 (Complex IV) (Fig. 2D, Fig. S2). We identified a decrease in UQCRSF1 and ATP5A1 expression which likely contributes to the decreased oxidative respiration observed in Pitx2c-deficient cardiomyocytes (Fig. 2E). Interestingly, we observe an increase in MT-CO1 expression (Fig. 2E). In addition to oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis contributes to energy production within the post-natal heart and metabolic switches to glycolysis is observed when oxidative phosphorylation is reduced to compensate for decreased mitochondrial energy production. Therefore, we assessed glycolytic function through the proton efflux rate (PPR) (Fig. S3A). We observe no significant changes in basal glycolysis and compensatory glycolysis with the injection of rotenone and antimycin A (electron transport chain complex I and III inhibitor) in Pitx2c-deficient NRAMs (Fig. S3B).

Direct PITX2 transcriptional targets include electron transport chain complex I and IV which may generate excessive reactive oxygen species under mitochondrial dysfunction [17], and oxidative stress is a known pathological mechanism of atrial fibrillation [18], [19], [20]. Therefore, we assessed mitochondrial-specific ROS (mitoROS) production using MitoSOX, a fluorescent mitoROS probe (Fig. 2F). We observed elevated mitoROS in Pitx2c-deficient cardiomyocytes (Fig. 2G). Further, we assessed whether elevated levels of ROS may alter the mitochondrial membrane potential and subsequently the transmembrane proton gradient driving ATP production. We observed no differences in the ratio of JC-1 aggregates to JC-1 monomers indicating no depolarization of the mitochondria in Pitx2c-deficient cardiomyocytes (Fig. 2H). All together, these data indicate that loss of PITX2C is associated with altered oxidative metabolism gene expression, and the loss of this regulatory network impairs mitochondrial function and subsequently leads to ROS accumulation.

Pitx2c-deficiency alters intracellular calcium cycling and arrhythmic oscillations

Calcium ions play a crucial role in cardiac excitation-contraction coupling with intracellular calcium oscillations during systole and diastole. PITX2 has been shown to directly transcriptionally regulate calcium cycling genes [5] (Table S4) and Pitx2 insufficiency results in abnormalities in calcium release [4], [21], [22], [23] thereby increasing afterdepolarizations, a key arrhythmogenic mechanism. Spontaneous intracellular calcium cycling was assessed in spontaneously beating NRAMs to characterize calcium kinetics in Pitx2c-deficient myocytes (Fig. 3A). Calcium kinetics differences are interpreted in the context of variable cycle length. Pitx2c-deficient cardiomyocytes do not alter beat rates (Fig. 3B) in comparison to control cardiomyocytes yet, are slower to flux calcium during systole (Fig. 3C) and are slower to reuptake calcium during diastole (Fig. 3D, E).

Notably, we observed arrhythmogenic calcium events in Pitx2c knockdown cells (Fig. 3F, G). We used calcium traces as a surrogate to infer afterdepolarization events, as these transmembrane potential dependent mechanisms often coincide with changes in cardiomyocyte calcium handling [24], [25]. Afterdepolarization events were classified as either early afterdepolarization-like calcium events (EADs) (Fig. 3F) if a second peak was detected prior to reaching baseline or delayed afterdepolarization-like calcium event (DADs) (Fig. 3G) if a second peak is detected after reaching baseline under spontaneous calcium activity. We observe a higher frequency of both EAD-like (Fig. 3H) and DAD-like (Fig. 3I) calcium events. Together, these data suggest calcium mishandling as a pro-arrhythmic mechanism in Pitx2c-deficient cardiomyocytes.

Pitx2c-deficiency alters sarcomere structure and titin protein localization

While structural remodelling occurs during AF pathogenesis, an underlying sub-clinical cardiomyopathy has also been suggested to increase AF susceptibility. We previously reported that pitx2c-deficient zebrafish display disorganized sarcomeres with absence of M-band structures [6]. Here, we characterized cellular morphology in control and Pitx2c-siRNA treated NRAMs. We observed no differences in cell size (Fig. S4A) or cell elongation (Fig. S4B, S4C). Additionally, we assessed the sarcomere by Myomesin immunostaining to label the M-band (Fig. 4A). Pitx2c-deficient atrial cardiomyocytes had a shorter sarcomere length (Fig. 4B) with no changes in sarcomere organization (Fig. 4C). In contrast to the zebrafish model, we observe an increase in the density of sarcomere M-bands (Fig. 4D).

Of note, the transcriptomic analysis reveal myogenesis as one of the top dysregulated pathways in Pitx2c-deficiency (Fig. 1C) and a number direct PITX2 transcriptional targets exist at the thick/thin filaments (Myh6, Myh7b, Myh8, Tnni1, Tnni3k, Tnnt2, Tpm2, Tpm4), Z-disk (Neb, Pdlim5, Pdlim4, Capzb, Fmn2, Lmod1, Tmod1), and M-band (Ampd2, Obscn, Capn3) (Table S4). Therefore, we performed immunocytochemistry for Titin, a crucial protein that spans the sarcomere and maintains cardiomyocyte elasticity (Fig. 4E). We observed that Pitx2c-deficient cardiomyocytes display altered Titin/F-actin co-localization (Fig. 4F). We quantified total Titin levels via qPCR targeting exons 49–50 and identified an upregulation of total Titin mRNA expression (Fig. S4D), suggesting that altered Titin levels may contribute to its mislocalization. We also considered the splicing factor RNA-binding motif protein 20 (Rbm20), which acts to alternatively splice Titin. Mutations in Rbm20 have been associated with arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathies with erroneous splicing resulting in high expression of the fetal N2BA Titin isoform [26], [27]. Notably, ChIP-seq analysis identifies Rbm20 as a direct transcriptional target of PITX2 in mouse (Table S4).

N-acetylcysteine reduces oxidative stress and arrhythmia-associated phenotypes in Pitx2c-deficiency

There are several ways by which ROS may induce cardiac arrhythmia, including by oxidizing calcium cycling or sarcomere proteins, which are in line with some of the phenotypes we observe in Pitx2c-deficient atrial cardiomyocytes. To evaluate ROS as a mechanism by which the loss of Pitx2c drives arrhythmogenesis, we treated control and Pitx2c-deficent cells with 1 mM NAC, a potent ROS scavenger, for 24 h. NAC reduced mitochondrial-derived ROS accumulation (Fig. 5A) in Pitx2c-deficient atrial cardiomyocytes. In addition, reducing ROS does not alter beat rate (Fig. 5B) but partially attenuates PITX2C-dependent alterations in calcium cycling decay (Fig. 5D, E). However, while NAC treatment reduces the time to peak (Fig. 5C), it did not restore the prolongation in calcium release (Fig. 5C) back to control levels. Despite this, the frequency of both EAD/DAD-like calcium events were reduced after NAC treatment (Fig. 5G, H). Further, we assessed Titin localization in Pitx2c knockdown cells following NAC treatment and observe an increase in Titin/F-actin co-localization in atrial myocytes. Together, these data suggest that PITX2C-dependent ROS accumulation potentially promotes abnormal calcium cycling and sarcomere instability.

Discussion

Main findings

In this study, our data reveal reactive oxygen species as an early mechanism of intracellular calcium handling and sarcomere structure downstream of PITX2C. We identify altered oxidative metabolism and reactive oxygen species accumulation in Pitx2c-deficient neonatal rat atrial myocytes. Reducing oxidative stress in Pitx2c knockdown NRAMs using NAC partially attenuates aberrant calcium handling and Titin/F-actin co-localization.

PITX2 regulates atrial metabolism

We report an evolving role of PITX2C in regulating atrial metabolism. In this study, we observe Pitx2c-deficient cardiomyocytes display decreased basal and maximal oxidative phosphorylation as well as decreased ATP production. These findings are consistent with pitx2c-deficient zebrafish, murine models of Pitx2 insufficiency [7], [28], [29], PITX2-deficient human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSC) [8], [30], and AF patient derived left atrial tissue [8]. In addition, it is becoming increasingly recognized that decreased oxidative phosphorylation is a key finding in atrial fibrillation [31], [32] which may contribute to energy deficits, oxidative stress, and structural and electrical remodelling that causes arrhythmia. We identify amongst the top differentially expressed genes, electron transport chain complex I and IV subunits which may explain the accumulation of ROS [17]. A limitation to note is that the ChIP-seq experiment was previously performed in 12-week-old mouse heart tissue which are both different species and at a different developmental stage than the neonatal rat model used within this study. While upstream NDUFS1 (Complex I) and SDHA (Complex II) protein expression remain unchanged in the absence of PITX2C, the upstream UQCRFS1 (Complex III) protein expression is diminished which decreases electron transfer from ubiquinol to cytochrome c halting ATP production and may increase ROS generation. Further attenuating ATP production, we observed decreased expression of ATP5A1 (Complex V) in Pitx2c-knockdown cells. Interestingly, we observe an increase in expression of MT-CO1 (Complex IV). This likely reflects an adaptive response in an attempt to increase ATP production and reduce ROS production [33]. We speculate that the role for PITX2C is at the transcriptional level, as we observe an upregulation of transcripts encoding complex III and V. However, at the protein level we observe decreased expression of UQCRFS1 and ATP5A1 suggesting another factor may regulate the expression of the electron transport chain components.

Metabolic deficiency is a key finding in arrhythmia and heart failure with a decrease in oxidative metabolism and a shift towards glycolysis as an initial cardioprotective mechanism [34]. This metabolic switch has been demonstrated in PITX2 deficient atrial hiPSCs [8]. Although we observe transcriptional changes in glycolysis, we observe no changes in basal glycolytic function. Potentially, as our study leverages neonatal rat cardiomyocytes that are already undergoing a dynamic developmental metabolic switch from glycolysis to fatty acid metabolism [35], the loss of PITX2C in NRAMs, rather than decreasing glycolytic function, hinders this metabolic switch. Therefore, without PITX2C that regulates atrial metabolism, neonatal rat cardiomyocytes remain utilizing glycolysis rather than fatty acid metabolism, an abundant energy producing source. This presents an area for further exploration to understand how PITX2C regulates post-natal glucose and fatty acid import and utilization in atrial cardiomyocyte development.

PITX2C regulates calcium cycling and sarcomere structure

The role of PITX2C in transcriptionally regulating calcium handling genes and in turn cardiac rhythm is well established [4], [22], [23]. Surprisingly, we fail to observe significant dysregulation of genes encoding cardiac calcium handling genes including L-type calcium channels (Cacna1d), cardiac ryanodine receptors (Ryr2), and SERCA2a (Atp2a2). However, we observe functional deficits in calcium handling, such as alterations in calcium transients, including both the time to peak and decay which are indicative of RyR2 and SERCA2a calcium flux, respectively. However, concurrent with these findings, it has been previously demonstrated that Pitx2c haploinsufficient mice display RyR2 hyperactivity and subsequently higher AF incidence which is reduced with the administration of a RyR2 antagonist ent-vertilicide [36]. Therefore, we speculate that oxidation of these proteins drives the alterations in calcium flux as treatment with N-acetylcysteine partially attenuates some calcium transient parameters and afterdepolarization-like calcium events [37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43]. However, as we observe that the time to peak remains elevated, there may be an irreversible post-translational modification of the RyR2 leading to its sustained activation [38]. Further, it has been previously shown that augmented RyR2 activity modulates mitochondrial calcium handling and promotes mitoROS emission resulting in a pro-arrhythmic feedback cycle [44]. However, a limitation of this study is that we do not assess the redox state or modification of the receptor (S-nitrosylation, S-glutathionylation, disulfide oxidation) directly. Nonetheless, oxidative stress appears to promote arrhythmic cellular mechanisms.

Titin is an integral sarcomere protein known for its role in maintaining cardiomyocyte elasticity. Truncating mutations in Titin have been largely implicated in dilated cardiomyopathies [45] which subsequently increases the risk of developing arrhythmia. Jiang and Ly et al. demonstrated that a 9-amino acid deletion from Titin's A-band in zebrafish and hiPSC atrial-cardiomyocytes results in electrophysiological remodelling that increases AF susceptibility [46]. We observed altered Titin organization following loss of PITX2C that is partially improved by reducing oxidative stress. Yet, we acknowledge a limitation that these experiments did not assess Titin organization in NRAMs treated with both the scrambled siRNA and NAC, and so we do not know how Titin is modulated by NAC treatment alone. One hypothesis linking Titin and AF is that oxidation of Titin downstream of PITX2C is a cardiomyopathic component that contributes to cardiomyocyte dysfunction. Oxidation of Titin protein is known to increase or decrease compliance depending on site of oxidation, which may impair diastolic function [47]. However, how PITX2C regulates Titin protein localization to the sarcomere remains unanswered. One potential mechanism may be that high levels of ROS and oxidation of the Titin protein activates protein quality control mechanisms [48] that result in protein degradation. PITX2C may also regulate neonatal myogenesis and may transcriptionally regulate Titin isoform switching from a passive N2BA neonatal isoform to a stiff adult N2B isoform [49], a hypothesis in line with our transcriptomic data. This may be further supported by Rbm20, a Titin splicing factor, being identified as a transcriptional target of PITX2. However, our study does not quantify total Titin and Titin isoform protein levels in control and Pitx2c-deficient cardiomyocytes. In addition, PITX2 may regulate other key sarcomere proteins which alter Titin anchoring and therefore results in mis-localization. Although incompletely understood, this interaction presents an exciting avenue for further studies.

It is important to consider the relationship between Titin protein function and intracellular calcium dynamics. In particular, the PEVK segment, rich in E (glutamic acid) residues, binds calcium with high affinity, increasing passive tension with increasing levels of calcium. We identify changes in intracellular calcium dynamics, highlighting a prolonged calcium cycle, which may in part alter the passive forces produced by Titin and other sarcomere components. In addition, direct transcriptional targets of PITX2 lie in Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase and S100 calcium binding protein which have been demonstrated to interact with Titin at the N2B and PEVK regions [50], [51], [52] and N2A region [53], [54], respectively.

Reactive oxygen species in atrial fibrillation

Current anti-arrhythmic drugs commonly used in the treatment of AF act by blocking B-adrenergic receptors or ion channels [55]. While these management strategies are beneficial, they are found to have limited long-term efficacy and induce pro-arrhythmic effects [56]. Therefore, due to the clear implication of oxidative stress in AF pathogenesis, understanding the molecular mechanisms of AF and identifying novel therapeutic targets is necessary. In this study, we identify reducing oxidative stress partially attenuates key components of cardiomyocyte function in atrial myocytes with reduced PITX2 expression. However, clinical implementation of NAC as a treatment for AF has been controversial. Results vary amongst clinical trials in administering NAC as a preventative measure of postoperative AF while some studies suggest reduced incidence, others suggest no benefit [57]. Further, combination therapy of NAC and anti-arrhythmia medication (e.g. amiodarone [58] and carvedilol [59]) do not significantly reduce AF incidence post-surgery. These differences in outcomes may be due in part to the incomplete understanding of ROS and the vast number of intracellular targets and consequences. In addition, how a genetic component contributes to the drug interactions and efficacy is incomplete. It is unlikely that NAC, or antioxidants, will continue in clinical trials and implementation in the treatment of AF. However, understanding the mechanism by which oxidative stress downstream of PITX2 induces arrhythmogenesis is necessary to identify personalized therapeutic targets.

The potential role of PITX2 in the link between inflammation and atrial fibrillation

Although outside of the scope of this study, inflammation is a key contributor to reactive oxygen species in AF pathogenesis. We identify dysregulated pathways in inflammation, TNF-α signaling via NF-KB, and the complement system suggesting that PITX2C transcriptionally activates inflammatory signaling (Fig. 1C). Concurrent with these findings, it has been previously identified that inflammatory gene expression was upregulated in a mouse model with a deletion of an intronic CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) site in the Pitx2 gene leading to reduced Pitx2 expression. The precise downstream mechanisms of PITX2 in regulating inflammation in AF remains unclear. However, ROS and inflammation may act cooperatively to exacerbate atrial electrical and structural remodelling that facilitates AF. Zhou and Liu et al. previously reported mouse models of diabetes mellitus with increased AF inducibility that was primarily driven macrophage IL-1B through mitoROS modulation of the RyR2 [60]. The mechanisms of ROS and inflammation in Pitx2c deficiency warrants further investigation.

Limitations

Neonatal rat atrial myocytes are a powerful tool to study the biology of PITX2C in arrhythmogenesis. Nonetheless, there are certain limitations to note. While two siRNAs were tested to identify knockdown capacity, the functional analyses presented here were performed with only a single siRNA against Pitx2c. Moreover, it remains unclear how these findings translate to adult atrial myocytes where AF develops. However, PITX2C expression is believed to decrease with age and therefore understanding acute changes in cardiomyocyte function in neonatal development downstream of the loss of PITX2C is necessary to understand how these changes may predispose an individual to AF in later stages of life. Further, adult atrial myocytes demonstrate a significantly higher number of spontaneous calcium sparks and waves compared with adult ventricular myocytes, which may be further augmented in neonatal cells. In addition, exploring how EAD/DAD-like calcium traces electrical pacing and whether these traces are secondary to membrane potential changes may strengthen the interpretation of arrhythmia susceptibility.
