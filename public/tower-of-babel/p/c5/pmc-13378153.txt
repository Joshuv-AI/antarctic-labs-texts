Background

Bladder exstrophy (BE) is a rare congenital malformation in which the anterior wall of the bladder is absent, and the posterior wall is exposed externally [1]. The approximate incidence of bladder exstrophy is 1 in 50,000 live births in the United States and Europe. Males are more commonly affected, with a male-to-female ratio of 2.3:1 [2]. Initiated by Trendelenburg in 1906, surgical treatment of classical BE became staged reconstruction toward the last quarter of the 20th Century. Regardless of the technique used during the operation, the main goals of surgical treatment are to safely close the bladder as well as the anterior abdominal wall to provide a competent bladder to maintain urinary continence and cosmetically acceptable genitalia [3–5].

Surgeons around the world widely adopt two contemporary reconstructive procedures for the treatment of BE: the complete primary repair of exstrophy (CPRE) and the modern staged repair of exstrophy (MSRE) including a series of procedures (primary closure in the neonate, epispadias correction at age 6–12 months, and bladder neck reconstruction (BNR) at age 4–5 years) [6].

Incontinence is one of the most important problems affecting the quality of life of these patients. Bladder augmentation (BA) with gastrointestinal tissue accompanied by BNR are important tools for pediatric surgeons in the management of children with Bladder exstrophy for urinary incontinence. The goal of BA is to create a reservoir with an adequate functional capacity with low end-filling pressure. By achieving this, the low intravesical pressure will not interfere with the ureteral delivery of urine to the bladder and will preserve the upper urinary tract from high-pressure damage by vesicoureteral reflux [7]. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and outcomes of ileocystoplasty with modified Young-Dees-Leadbetter bladder neck reconstruction and continent catheterizable channel (Mitrofanoff or Monti) in children born with bladder exstrophy after successful bladder closure and had small bladder capacity with incontinence at the Pediatric Surgery Unit, Children’s Hospital, Faculty of Medicine, Alexandria University, Egypt.

Patients & methods

Patients

This prospective study included 20 patients with bladder exstrophy after successful bladder closure (15 patients were closed in the neonatal period while 5 cases were closed delayed after 6 months) and who had small bladder capacity less than 100 ml with incontinence and needed augmentation cystoplasty and bladder neck reconstruction at Alexandria University Children’s Hospital during the period from August 2022 to April 2024. All cases underwent modern staged repair according to our protocol in BE management. Osteotomy was done in 5 cases who had delayed primary repair.

Study design

All patients were subjected to full routine clinical examination and investigative studies preoperatively to assess their fitness for surgery and detect any associated anomalies. Ultrasound abdomen & pelvis to assess the renal cortical thickness and if there is hydronephrosis or hydroureter and voiding cysto-urethrogram (VCUG) for assessing bladder capacity and incidence of VUR were done for all cases (Fig. 1).

Patients were admitted one day before surgery for routine clinical and laboratory evaluation. They were fasting for 6 h before surgery.

Surgical procedure

After induction of anesthesia, a central venous catheter (CVC) was inserted, and an intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotic was given. Opening the previous midline wound, opening the bladder longitudinally, and both ureteric openings were stented. A modified Young-Dees Leadbetter BNR was done over an 8 F Foley catheter by vicryl 4/0 sutures. This repair was described to create intravesical mucosal strip about 1.5 cm width and 2.5 cm length for lengthening of the bladder neck followed by double layers closure of the bladder after mucosal denudation. An ileal segment about 30 cm long and 20 cm away from the ileocecal valve was harvested on its vascular pedicle to formulate the augment. Then, ileoileal anastomosis with closure of the mesenteric defect (Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

Data was fed to the computer and IBM SPSS software package version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, United States) was used for analysis. Number and percent were used to describe qualitative data, whilst range (minimum and maximum), mean, standard deviation, and median were used to describe quantitative data.

Results

The study was conducted on 20 patients, who were followed up for a mean period of 19 months (range 13–24 months).

This study included 16 males (80%) and 4 females (20%), with a male-to-female ratio of 4:1. The age of patients at the time of the operation ranged from 5 to 11 years, with a mean ± SD (7.8 ± 2) years.

All the studied cases were operated by the same surgical team using an ileal bowel segment 30 cm configured as S-shaped bladder augment with modified Young-Dees-Leadbetter BNR and Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy. The operative time ranged from 3.5 to 5.5 h, with a mean of 4.4 ± 0.6 h. Only 3 (15%) cases needed cephalotrigonal ureteric reimplantation; two of them were males and 1 female. This was due to a narrow trigone in which bladder neck reconstruction cannot be done. The remaining 17 cases (85%) had a wide trigone that allowed us to repair the bladder neck smoothly with no need for ureteric reimplantation. None of our patients had an intraoperative complication.

All cases started oral feeding between the 4th and 6th postoperative days according to the restoration of intestinal motility. The duration of hospital stay ranged from 9 to 19 days with a mean ± SD (10.4 ± 2.2) days. Three cases had post-operative wound infection (Fig. 6). Ureteric stents were removed between 15 and 30 days postoperatively with a mean ± SD (17.9 ± 4.98) days. The abdominal drain was removed between 7 and 15 days with a mean ± SD (8.9 ± 1.7) days. The urethral catheter was removed between 14 and 21 days, with a mean ± SD (16.1 ± 2.3) days. The Mitrofanoff catheter was removed, and clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) started between 25 and 30 days, with a mean ± SD (27.9 ± 1.6) days (Table 1).

Early post-operative evaluation of the studied cases

None of the patients had leakage of urine from the Mitrofanoff stoma in the postoperative follow-up. Stoma stenosis occurred in 2 patients (10%). They were examined under anesthesia and were easily calibrated and maturated. Two (10%) patients developed vesico-cutaneous fistula, both of them closed spontaneously without surgical intervention after good bladder drainage.

Sixteen patients (80%) were compliant on CIC every 3 h, and 4 patients (20%) were not compliant. As regards daytime continence, 16 patients (80%) were dry between CIC sessions, while 4 patients (20%) were wet between CIC at resting time. During stress, 14 patients (70%) were dry, while 6 patients (30%) were wet (4 of them were wet at resting time).

At nighttime, 8 patients (40%) were dry for 6 h without doing CIC, while 8 patients (40%) had to cathetrize themselves every 3 h to stay dry, and 4 patients (20%) were wet before 3 h. Regarding patient satisfaction, 7 patients were not satisfied after the operation, 4 of them were not compliant on CIC and were wet at resting time, and the remaining 3 cases were dry. Still, they were not satisfied with CIC (Table 2).

Continence was defined as dryness for 3 h between CIC sessions, assessed via direct questioning during follow-up. “Resting” referred to non-active periods, while “stress” included physical activity.

Only 1 patient developed a bladder stone a year after bladder augmentation. This stone was extracted in our dedicated stone service in the adult Urology Department. Post-operative urine analysis was positive for asymptomatic bacteriuria in 18 patients; only 2 patients had urine analysis free. Only one case developed a significant urinary tract infection and needed hospital admission and IV antibiotics.

In sonography, there was no hydronephrosis nor hydroureter, and the renal cortical thickness was average in all patients before and after surgery.

Discussion

Bladder exstrophy is a rare lower midline abdominal defect and is part of a larger spectrum of abdominal-pelvic fusion abnormalities, known collectively as the exstrophy-epispadias complex (EEC) [8].

Bladder exstrophy reconstruction necessitates closure of the bladder soon after birth and in some cases where the bladder capacity is so small, delayed closure is performed after 6 months with osteotomy, then repair of epispadias around 6–12 months later [9].

A good bladder capacity and sufficient outlet resistance determine the continence of staged reconstruction of bladder exstrophy. After primary closure, the bladder’s capacity to expand is dependent on multiple factors. The increase in capacity after bladder closure is inhibited by pan-cystitis and detrusor fibrosis, which are caused by delayed bladder closure [10].

McMahon et al. noted that augmentation cystoplasty was required in 63% of cases with delayed bladder closure beyond 7 days of age compared to 36% of patients who underwent closure before 7 days of age [11]. Husmann et al. showed a 40% augmentation rate in patients with bladder closure after 1 year, in contrast to 10% in those who were operated on before 1 year of age [12].

In our study, 15 cases were closed in the neonatal period before 72 h while 5 cases were lately closed after 6 months due to the small bladder capacity.

However, the most challenging post-operative problem for children with bladder EEC is still urine continence. To achieve continence and full voiding, bladder neck reconstruction (BNR) was created to mimic the dynamic outlet management of the bladder reservoir [13].

In children with small bladder capacity or refractory bladder dysfunction, augmentation cystoplasty is the ultimate surgical technique. The primary purpose of this approach is to establish a low-pressure reservoir bladder to safeguard renal function, ensure appropriate continence, and enhance quality of life [14]. Indeed, the most frequently utilized bowel segment for bladder augmentation is the ileum [15].

Lapides et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) for bladder emptying. This critical connection between bladder augmentation and CIC cannot be overstated. Indeed, the single most important factor in planning bladder reconstruction in young patients is ensuring the potential for effective CIC [16].

The most popular method for creating a continent catheterizable channel (CCC )is the Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy, which utilizes the appendix; however, the Yang-Monti approach often employs a transversely tubularized segment of the bowel as a channel [17].

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy, safety, and outcomes of augmentation ileocystoplasty with modified Young-Dees-Leadbetter BNR and CCC (Mitrofanoff or Monti) in children born with bladder exstrophy after successful bladder closure and epispadias repair.

In the current study, there was male predilection among cases (80%). This is consistent with Harris et al. [18] , who reported that males were more prevalent than females (69.3% vs. 30.7%) among 192 patients with bladder exstrophy who underwent BNR. Likewise, Maruf et al. [19], who aimed to assess the continence outcomes after successful bladder reconstruction in bladder exstrophy patients, observed a high prevalence of males (70.8%) compared to females (29.2%) in their study.

The timing of bladder augmentation is an important issue to be considered. In a multi-institutional, retrospective cohort study involving 216 patients, Szymanski et al. [20] reported that the probability of bladder augmentation increased with age; by age 10 years, half of the children with bladder exstrophy underwent augmentation, and this increased to 70% by 18 years.

The mean age of patients who had bladder augmentation in our study was 7.8 years. Bhatnagar et al. [10]. reported the mean age for bladder augmentation in their study was 8.6 years. Defoor et al. [21] reported a mean age for bladder augmentation was 8 years in their study. In Maruf et al. [19] study, the mean age was 5.9 years. We believe at this age the patient can use his voluntary abdominal muscles to complete voiding and can catheterize himself appropriately.

Concerning intraoperative data, the mean operative time was 4.4 h. In comparison, McNamara et al. [22] mentioned that the mean operative time in his study was 7.1 h.

In the present study, the most noted early complication was post-operative wound infection in 3 patients (15%). In the same line, Bozaci et al. [23] mentioned that wound infection was found in 6.7% of their cases; among whom one patient had wound debridement and closure.

Among the main late problems of this procedure are stomal leak and/or stenosis and fistula formation [24]. The Mitrofanoff stoma in our study was continent in all patients, with 0% leakage. Harris et al. [18] agree with our results, reporting a continent stoma in 98% of patients in their study. While Liard et el [25]. reported a higher leakage rate (21%) from stoma in their study.

Mitrofanoff stomal stenosis occurred in 2 patients (10%) in this study. This was in agreement with Harris et al. [18] who reported stomal stenosis in 10% of their patients. Liard et el [25]. reported that 9 patients (39%) out of 23 patients developed stomal stenosis.

On the other hand, we had 2 patients (10%) who developed vesicocutaneous fistula, all of which closed spontaneously. Consistent with our findings, Bhatnagar et al. [26] reported 2 out of 19 patients who developed vesicocutaneous fistula; however, different from our study, they reported that both fistulae needed surgical intervention. Demirkan et al. [27] reported 6 patients (18.75%) out of 32 patients who developed vesicocutaneous fistula, and all of them were repaired primarily.

In the current study, two cases (10%) needed a secondary intervention procedure for the maturation of Mitrofanoff stoma. Similar results were reported in Shekarriz et al. [28] study, in which 14 cases needed Mitrofanoff stoma revision out of 133 cases (10.5%). While Harris et. al. [18] found that 8 patients (16%) required stomal revision.

Asymptomatic bacteriuria occured in 50–100% of bladder augmentations, and the incidence of significant UTI has been reported to be lower (4–43%) [29–31]. In our study, post-operative urine analysis was positive for asymptomatic bacteriuria in 18 patients (90%). In consistence with us, H Demirkan et al. [27] reported in his study that all patients were on CIC and a significant proportion (93.1%) of them had asymptomatic bacteriuria.

Only one case (5%) in our study developed a significant UTI who needed hospital admission and IV antibiotics. In various reports, it is thought that chronic bacteriuria does not play a role in the development of renal failure after BA, and its treatment is not routinely recommended [32, 33].

Stone formation is one of the most common complications after BA, with an incidence of 3–40%. [34] In our study, only one case (5%) developed a bladder stone 1 year after bladder augmentation, he was managed by lithotripsy in the adult urology department. The stone analysis revealed it was struvite stone. Mehmood et al. [14] reported that bladder stones developed in four patients (9.5%), with renal stones further developing in three patients (7.1%). Szymanski et al. [34] assessed the stone composition of 85 cases after BA, and infectious stones represented 69.2% of bladder stones. Maxon et. al. [35] he most common primary stone component was dahllite, followed by struvite for all augments. It has been demonstrated that bladder irrigation procedures using regular saline greatly lower the risk of stone formation and lower the incidence of mucus and bacteriuria [36, 37].

In the literature, the definition of continence is debatable; we considered 3 h of dryness at daytime are sufficient to define continence. Most patients (80%) were compliant on CIC / 3 h, and 4 patients (20%) were non-compliant. As regards the dryness between CIC at daytime, 16 patients (80%) remained dry between CIC sessions with no drippling from the urethra, while 4 patients (20%) were wet between CIC during resting time. During stress, 6 patients (30%) were wet, while 14 patients (70%) maintained dryness.

At night, we had 8 patients (40%) who were dry for 6 h without doing CIC, while 8 patients (40%) needed to do CIC/3 hours to maintain dryness, and 4 patients (20%) were wet before 3 h. The overall continence rate in our study was 80%. All continent patients have a sensation of bladder fullness and can void freely.

Similar results were reported in Demirkan et al. study [27], in which a continence rate of 78.7% with volitional voiding and CIC after bladder augmentation, BNR, and CIC. Also, McMahon et al. [11] noted that 82% continence was achieved in 17 patients with bladder augmentation compared to 56% continence in 16 non-augmented patients. Maruf et al. [19] also assessed the continence rate in their work, showing that after isolated BNR, 64% were continent compared to 93% continent patients after BNR with CCC. Bhatnagar et al. [26] reported that the sensation of reservoir fullness was present in all the patients; 9 patients (47.3%) were completely dry out of 19 patients. Two patients are totally incontinent, and the remaining 8 patients had wetting episodes. In a study conducted in 2004, Shaw et al. [38] reported augmentation was required in 30 of 43 patients (70%) to achieve continence. He also stated that Bladder neck reconstruction alone resulted in continence in only a third of the cases. This showed the importance of bladder augmentation combined with BNR and CCC to achieve adequate continence in these patients (Table 3).

As regards VCUG, the change in bladder capacity after augmentation cystoplasty was marvelous; there was a mean 5-fold increase in the bladder capacity in our study (from 60.2 ± 6.6 ml to 341.5 ± 73.4 ml, respectively; p < 0.001).

Similarly, Bhatnagar et al. [26] reported a mean 7-fold increase in bladder capacity after augmentation cystoplasty. Also, Chang et al. [39] stated that there is a mean 8-fold increase in bladder capacity after augmentation cystoplasty.

In contrast, a study done by Zaragoza-Torres et al. [40] revealed only a mean 1-fold increase in bladder capacity after augmentation cystoplasty. This discrepancy may be related to the different lengths of the bowel segment designed to be an augment, which plays an important role in post-operative bladder capacity.

Augmentation lowers intravesical pressure and increases bladder compliance during the urine storage phase, reversing or minimizing VUR [41].

It was found that all cases in our study had pre-operative mild grades of VUR (grade I to III), either unilateral or bilateral, and all of them showed resolution of VUR in post-operative VCUG done 6 months after bladder augmentation. This is similar to a study done in 2016 by Han-Chao Zhang et al. who reported that reflux was resolved in 24 patients (83%) out of 29 patients, improved in 3 patients (10%), but persisted in two (7%) patients [42]. This is in line with Z. Juhasz et al. who reported resolution of VUR after bladder augmentation in 14 patients (87.5%) out of 16 patients [43]. J Khastgir et al. reported complete resolution of VUR in 66.6% of patients who had VUR before augmentation, 16.6% of cases VUR down from grade IV to grade II and 16.6% of cases did not improve [44] (Table 4).

Conclusion

For patients with bladder exstrophy, bladder augmentation and bladder neck reconstruction remain crucial to maintaining continence and enhancing their quality of life.

We believe that this report may add valuable information to the multidisciplinary team involved in the care of patients with this complex pathology, especially for counseling purposes at the time of prenatal diagnosis, before surgery, and after the primary surgery is done.
