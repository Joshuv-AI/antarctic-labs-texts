Individualized strategy of treatment for ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm using causal inference model: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Youngki Sohn, Youngje Woo, Sangkyun Mok, Eunju Jang, Ki-Yoon Moon, Sun Cheol Park, Sang Seob Yun, Jang Yong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study was performed to predict individualized treatment strategies in ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (rAAA) by estimating the survival benefit of endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) and open surgical repair (OSR) based on anatomical and physiological features using a causal inference model.MethodsThis retrospective study included 45 patients with de novo rAAA who underwent EVAR or OSR between 2012 and 2024. Thirty-three variables were analyzed. The model estimated individualized treatment effects (ITE) for 30-day survival. Model interpretability was assessed using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis. Five-fold cross-validation, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and calibration plots were used for model evaluation. A clinical decision tree was developed to derive simplified decision rules.ResultsThe mean ITE was 0.22 ± 0.42, with 33% of patients classified as OSR-benefit candidates. SHAP analysis revealed that suprarenal angle, infrarenal angle, iliac anatomy, and proximal neck characteristics strongly influenced treatment effects. However, some predictors, such as low hemoglobin and systolic blood pressure favoring OSR, conflicted with clinical intuition. ROC analysis showed an area under the curve of 1.00, but calibration suggested overfitting due to a small sample size. Treatment-matched patients had a higher 30-day mortality rate than mismatched patients, suggesting potential bias or unmeasured confounding. The decision tree identified clinically relevant features but displayed structural inconsistencies and impractical cutoff values due to the limited sample size.ConclusionThe X-learner model demonstrated the feasibility of individualized treatment prediction in rAAA but suffered from overfitting and limited generalizability. Validation with larger multicenter cohorts is necessary to confirm clinical applicability.Keywords: Abdominal/surgery, Aortic aneurysm, Causal inference model, Endovascular aneurysm repair, Ruptured aortic aneurysm

INTRODUCTION

Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (rAAA) remains a life-threatening vascular disease with high mortality. Despite significant improvements in emergency management over recent decades, perioperative mortality for rAAA persists at 20%–30% [1,2,3,4]. This reduction in mortality primarily stems from the introduction of endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR), which established an endovascular-first strategy due to its early survival benefits [5,6,7,8,9]. However, major studies like the IMPROVE trial have demonstrated that while EVAR offers short-term survival advantages, these benefits diminish during long-term follow-up, with open surgical repair (OSR) demonstrating better late survival outcomes [10,11,12,13,14].

A key limitation of EVAR is its restricted applicability. Device manufacturers specify anatomical criteria in their instructions-for-use (IFU), and patients with hostile neck anatomy have higher risks of complications, including type Ia endoleak, 1-year sac expansion, and reintervention. Despite these constraints [15], EVAR is often performed in patients who do not meet IFU criteria (off-IFU EVAR), consistently yielding inferior outcomes compared to cases that follow IFU guidelines [16,17].

However, not all IFU violations carry equal clinical significance. Some minor deviations may minimally affect outcomes, while others, even seemingly minor ones, can have devastating consequences for specific patients. Furthermore, IFU criteria are device-specific and focus only on anatomical considerations, overlooking the patient’s physiological status. Consequently, there is sparse evidence on how to optimally integrate both anatomical and physiological factors when deciding between EVAR and OSR.

Existing research methods, such as randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and traditional cohort studies, primarily reveal population-level trends rather than individualized outcomes. RCTs, in particular, suffer from strict eligibility criteria that limit their ability to represent the diversity of real-world rAAA patients. More importantly, these methods are fundamentally limited by the fact that it is impossible to observe the outcome of 2 different treatments (EVAR and OSR) in the same patient to determine which is truly better for an individual. To overcome these limitations, we propose a novel approach using individualized causal inference to expand the cohort of patients who could benefit from EVAR beyond the standard IFU criteria.

Causal inference is a statistical method used to determine a cause-and-effect relationship beyond simple correlation. It allows us to infer counterfactual outcomes by constructing a hypothetical scenario that did not actually occur. For example, we can infer what the outcome might have been if a patient who actually received EVAR had been treated with OSR instead. Among the various causal inference meta-algorithms, we chose the X-learner because of its specific advantages. It is particularly effective at making more accurate predictions when the data is imbalanced or the sample size is small, as it leverages information from both the treatment and control groups.

By applying the X-learner meta-algorithm, which can estimate individualized treatment effects (ITE), we aimed to analyze a comprehensive set of anatomical, demographic, clinical, and physiological features to quantitatively predict whether EVAR or OSR would offer a better survival benefit for each patient. Thus, this preliminary study was conducted to lay the groundwork for a clinically interpretable, evidence-based, and personalized treatment strategy for the management of rAAA and to demonstrate its value for future expansion with larger datasets.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved and monitored by the Institutional Review Board of The Catholic University of Korea, Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital (No. IRB#KC25RISI0076). The need for informed consent was waived because patients’ data were deidentified.

Data collection and study population

This non-randomized, retrospective observational study examined patients treated for rAAA at The Catholic University of Korea, Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital between January 2012 and January 2024. We reviewed medical records and imaging studies of patients with diagnostic codes I71.3, I71.4, I71.8, or I71.9, selecting those who underwent either EVAR or OSR for de novo rAAA as our final study cohort.

We excluded patients who had previous aortic interventions, aortoenteric fistula, or missing CT imaging at presentation. For all included patients, we performed a comprehensive review of both in-hospital and post-discharge clinical outcomes.

All patients presented to the emergency department with suspected rAAA symptoms, primarily sudden-onset abdominal pain and altered mental status, and underwent contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT imaging for diagnosis. On-call vascular surgeons (SCP, SSY, JYK) evaluated aneurysm morphology from the CT scans. The anatomical variables assessed included continuous variables and binary variables. Continuous variables were aneurysmal neck length, suprarenal angle (SRA), infrarenal angle (IRA), proximal aortic neck diameter, and bilateral common iliac artery (CIA) diameters. Binary variables included the presence of aneurysmal neck calcification, intraluminal thrombus, conical neck morphology, aneurysm shape (fusiform or saccular), CIA aneurysm, CIA calcification, CIA intraluminal thrombus, and short CIA landing zone.

Those variables were assessed from contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT imaging using a standardized protocol. Aortic neck diameter was measured at the most inferior renal artery orifice. Aortic neck length was defined as the distance from the most inferior renal artery orifice to the beginning of the aneurysmal sac. SRA was defined as the angle between the blood flow axis of the suprarenal aorta and the aneurysmal neck axis. IRA was defined as the angle formed by the aneurysmal neck axis and a line from the most inferior renal artery to the aortic bifurcation. Bilateral CIA diameters were measured 10–20 mm distal to the aortic bifurcation. In cases with an aneurysmal change, the CIA diameter was measured at the point of maximal dilatation.

Aneurysmal neck calcification and intraluminal thrombus were defined when the respective feature occupied 50% or more of the aneurysmal neck circumference or the aneurysmal sac volume, respectively. Conical neck morphology was identified if the aortic diameter measured 15 mm below the primary measurement point was 10% or more wider than the aortic neck diameter. Aneurysm shape was classified as saccular if the vessel wall exhibited a focal, localized expansion; otherwise, it was considered fusiform. CIA aneurysm was defined as a maximal CIA diameter of 18 mm or greater in men and 15 mm or greater in women. CIA calcification and intraluminal thrombus were defined using the same 50% threshold as the aneurysmal neck. A short CIA landing zone was defined when the length from the iliac branching to the end of the aneurysmal change was less than 10 mm.

Based on these assessments, we evaluated patients for hostile neck anatomy and complex iliac anatomy, determining any violations of device-specific IFU criteria. During the study period, we primarily used 2 EVAR devices, with their respective IFU criteria detailed in Table 1.

Treatment decisions were made by the on-call vascular surgeon following an endovascular-first strategy, which means EVAR was still preferred even with some violations of IFU criteria, as long as it was technically feasible. For instance, EVAR was actively considered for aortic necks with angulation within 90°. For short necks (less than 15 mm), EVAR was often performed with adjunctive techniques such as endoanchors or chimney grafts. However, OSR was favored in cases with hostile anatomy, particularly when severe angulation was combined with a short neck. Patients with a CIA aneurysm underwent EVAR with internal iliac artery embolization and extension of the iliac limb to the external iliac artery. Finally, OSR was chosen when an appropriate device was not available.

We used standard, off-the-shelf endovascular devices for all EVAR cases, primarily 2 types: Medtronic Endurant II (Medtronic) and Cook Zenith (Cook Medical). We did not utilize any fenestrated or branched endovascular technologies, as these are not typically employed in the emergent setting of rAAA due to their procedural complexity and time-consuming nature. However, a small number of patients underwent chimney EVAR to preserve renal artery perfusion in cases with a short infrarenal neck. This was considered a technically feasible approach under our endovascular-first strategy.

In addition, we collected demographic, clinical, and physiological variables known to influence survival outcomes based on previous literature and scoring systems [11,13,14,18,19,20,21,22,23,24,25]. The recorded features included demographic variables (age, sex, smoking history, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, stroke, and chronic kidney disease), clinical variables (presence of altered mental status before surgery, need for preoperative intubation, and history of cardiopulmonary resuscitation [CPR] before surgery), and physiological variables (systolic blood pressure [SBP], hemoglobin level, serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR], and serum lactate).

The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality following the initial surgical intervention. We determined mortality status by reviewing hospital records and directly contacting patients who were discharged and lost to follow-up before the 30-day mark.

To account for potential variations in surgical performance, we confirmed that all on-call vascular surgeons who performed the procedures had an annual volume of at least 10 EVAR and OSR cases. This case volume satisfies the minimum requirement for proficiency in rAAA repair as recommended by existing guidelines. As all surgeons met this proficiency threshold, we did not include the individual surgeon as a variable in our model.

Feature selection and preprocessing

We identified variables with statistical multicollinearity or strong conditional dependence among the collected features. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis, and features with VIF values above 25 were excluded. This process led to the removal of GFR, CPR status, and short right/left CIA landing zone variables. Rather than keeping short right and left CIA landing zones as separate variables, we created a unified composite variable called “short CIA landing zone.” This was coded as 0 for no short landing zones, 1 for a unilateral short landing zone, and 2 for bilateral short landing zones. After completing feature selection and preprocessing, we included 33 variables in the final model. Table 2 summarizes the excluded variables and their reasons for exclusion.

Individualized treatment effect estimation using X-learner

We applied the X-learner meta-algorithm [26] to derive the ITE by inferring counterfactual outcomes from our observational data. In this study, patients who received OSR were defined as the treatment group, and those who underwent EVAR were considered the control group.

Since we could not observe outcomes for both treatments in the same patient, the X-learner framework allowed us to estimate a patient’s survival probability under both scenarios. The core of the methodology involves 2 key steps. First, the model learns the relationship between variables and outcomes for both the EVAR and OSR groups. Second, it uses this relationship to impute the counterfactual outcomes, estimating what would have happened to each patient had they received the alternative treatment.

The X-learner is particularly effective for this analysis, especially with our imbalanced treatment assignments and moderate-sized dataset with multiple covariates. We estimated the ITE using the X-learner framework as:

where x: covariate vector for each patient (demographic, clinical, anatomical, physiological factors); e(x): propensity score, the probability of receiving OSR given covariates; τ̂EVAR(x): estimated treatment effect if the patient originally received EVAR; τ̂OSR(x): estimated treatment effect if the patient originally received OSR; and τ̂(x): final ITE, where positive values indicate OSR benefit, negative values indicate EVAR benefit.

The final ITE for each patient indicates the difference between the estimated outcomes, allowing us to quantify the survival benefit of one treatment (OSR) over the other (EVAR). All X-learner modeling was implemented using the Python-based econml package. Detailed modeling steps and imputation procedures are provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

To ensure reproducibility and transparency, we explicitly defined all preprocessing steps and hyperparameters. Continuous features were standardized using StandardScaler (with_mean=True, with_std=True). Missing values were excluded before modeling. For the random forest outcome models, we used n_estimators=100, max_depth=10, min_samples_split=10, min_samples_leaf=5, max_features='sqrt', and class_weight='balanced'. Logistic regression models were trained with C=1.0, penalty='l2', solver='liblinear', and max_iter=1,000. All models were trained using 5-fold stratified cross-validation with random_state=42 to preserve the EVAR/OSR distribution in each fold.

Cross-validation and meta-learner benchmarking

To assess the X-learner’s robustness and generalizability, we conducted 5-fold cross-validation on the entire cohort. For each fold, we used 80% of the data for model training and 20% for ITE prediction. We recorded the model’s recommended treatment for each patient and compared it to the actual treatment received. The treatment match rate (the proportion of cases where the model’s recommendation matched the actual treatment) served as the primary performance metric.

In addition to the X-learner, we implemented the S-learner and T-learner algorithms for comparative benchmarking. The S-learner employed a single random forest model that incorporated treatment assignment as an additional covariate, while the T-learner trained 2 separate outcome models, 1 for the EVAR group and 1 for the OSR group, without any adjustment mechanism between them.

We compared the distributional properties of ITE estimates (mean, standard deviation, and extreme values) and treatment match rates across all 3 meta-learning strategies. We also evaluated calibration and discrimination performance using ROC curves and calibration plots for the binary classification target (ITE > 0).

Model interpretability using Shapley Additive Explanations analysis and instance-level explanation

To interpret the model predictions clinically, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis was performed. We trained a binary classifier to predict whether patients would have ITE > 0 (indicating OSR-benefit), and calculated SHAP values to quantify how each covariate contributed to the model output. The input features and model structure were identical to those used in the X-learner modeling.

For individual patients, we created SHAP scatter plots to visualize how feature value changes influenced the ITE direction (ITE, >0 vs. <0). For the entire cohort, we generated a SHAP summary plot by aggregating the mean absolute SHAP values across all patients.

Additionally, we created a waterfall plot for a representative patient with an ITE prediction of 1.0. This visualization demonstrated how each anatomical and physiological covariate influenced, and in which direction, the prediction that this patient would benefit from OSR.

All SHAP analyses were conducted using the shap Python package, and visualizations were created with Matplotlib for static rendering suitable for publication.

Model evaluation with discrimination and calibration analysis

Although the X-learner enables detailed causal inference for individual cases, it was necessary to verify that its complex decision-making process remained consistent and systematic. Therefore, we evaluated the model’s predictive discrimination and calibration performance.

For discrimination analysis, we trained a simple random forest-based binary classifier. Using the same covariates as the X-learner input, this classifier predicted whether a patient’s ITE would be positive (ITE > 0). We evaluated the agreement between the binary classifier’s predictions and the X-learner’s estimated ITE labels using a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) as the primary performance metric.

To assess how well model predictions matched actual clinical decisions, we created a 10-bin calibration curve. The x-axis showed the mean predicted ITE score for each group, while the y-axis displayed the proportion of patients in that group who received OSR. A well-calibrated model would follow the 45° diagonal line (slope = 1), indicating strong alignment between predicted OSR benefits and clinical decisions. Any major deviations from this diagonal line would indicate that the model either overestimated or underestimated OSR benefits compared to real clinical practice.

Clinical concordance analysis: outcome of matched vs. unmatched cases

To evaluate how model-guided treatment decisions might affect clinical outcomes, we compared 30-day mortality rates between 2 groups: patients whose actual treatment aligned with the model’s recommendation (matched group) versus those whose treatment differed (mismatched group).

A lower mortality rate in the matched group would indicate that both clinicians and the model were making appropriate treatment selections. However, if the matched group showed higher mortality, this could suggest either the presence of unmeasured confounding variables not included in the model, or that both clinicians and the model were making suboptimal decisions based on the available information.

Clinical rule extraction via decision tree

To develop an interpretable clinical decision rule from the model predictions, we trained a decision tree classifier with ITE > 0 as the target variable. The input features matched those used in the X-learner modeling. We limited the decision tree to a maximum depth of 4 to balance interpretability with predictive accuracy. Key decision parameters were extracted from the tree to create simplified clinical pathways for identifying patients who would likely benefit from OSR versus EVAR.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of study participants

During the study period, 2,063 patients visited our center with diagnostic codes related to aortic aneurysm. Of these, 127 patients were initially assigned diagnostic codes for aneurysm evaluation but showed no aneurysmal changes upon testing. An additional 859 patients were diagnosed with other conditions such as thoracic aortic aneurysm, thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm, or CIA aneurysm. After these exclusions, 1,075 patients had confirmed abdominal aortic aneurysm.

Among the total cohort, 79 patients were diagnosed with rAAA. Five patients were excluded due to death before surgery (dead on arrival) or being deemed unsuitable for surgical intervention. From the remaining 74 treated patients, we excluded 2 patients who received both thoracic endovascular aortic repair and EVAR, 6 patients who required secondary endovascular interventions (such as coil embolization for endoleak), 4 patients with penetrating aortic ulcer, 2 patients with aortoenteric fistula, 5 patients with impending rupture, and 8 patients with previous EVAR or OSR history.

The final analysis included only de novo ruptured AAA cases. Two additional patients were excluded due to unavailable CT imaging data. This resulted in a final study cohort of 45 patients: 26 who underwent EVAR and 19 who received OSR. Individualized treatment effect estimation using X-learner

The X-learner calculated ITE for each patient, showing the predicted difference in 30-day survival between OSR and EVAR. The cohort’s mean ITE was 0.22 (±0.42). Of the patients, 15 (33.3%) were classified as OSR-benefit candidates (ITE > 0), while 30 patients (66.7%) were classified as EVAR-benefit or no-difference candidates (ITE ≤ 0). Cross-validation and meta-learner benchmarking

We compared predicted optimal treatment modalities from 3 meta-learners (X-learner, S-learner, and T-learner) with the actual treatments administered and calculated treatment match rates.

The S-learner showed a 57.78% match rate but could not differentiate between treatment options; its mean, minimum, and maximum ITE values were all 0.00, indicating no detection of treatment effect variations. While the T-learner achieved the highest match rate at 62.22% with a mean ITE of 0.24 (±0.57), its wide ITE distribution (–1.00 to 1.00) revealed instability and extreme predictions. The X-learner, despite having the lowest match rate (55.56%), delivered the most balanced and stable performance, with a mean ITE of 0.22 (±0.42) and a moderate range (–0.32 to 1.00).

Model evaluation with receiver operating characteristic curve and calibration analysis

To evaluate the validity of the model’s treatment prediction, we generated a ROC curve using a binary classifier and calculated the AUC. The binary classifier, built with a random forest model, was trained on the same covariates as the X-learner to predict whether ITE > 0. When comparing its predictions with the X-learner’s ITE estimates, the resulting AUC was 1.00, indicating perfect agreement between the 2 models. This suggests that the X-learner’s complex estimation was highly structured and consistent across input covariates. However, such a perfect ROC curve likely indicates overfitting due to the small sample size.

The calibration curve showed observed probabilities of 0 for the low predicted probability group and 1 for the high predicted probability group. While the curve generally aligned with the ideal 45° line, the extreme values at both ends indicate bin instability caused by insufficient patient numbers per bin. Based on the X-learner recommendations, patients were categorized into 2 groups: those whose actual treatment matched the model’s recommendation (match group) and those whose treatment differed (mismatch group). Comparing 30-day mortality rates between groups (Table 6, Fig. 6) revealed that the mismatch group (n = 20) had a 15.0% mortality rate, while the match group (n = 25) had a 40.0% mortality rate. Clinical rule extraction via decision tree

A clinical decision rule was developed to classify patients into OSR-benefit (ITE > 0) and EVAR-benefit (ITE < 0) groups based on their anatomical and physiological characteristics. For the 45 patients, SRA was used as the primary decision point, with subsequent classifications based on short iliac landing zone, conical neck shape, age, diabetes mellitus, right CIA diameter, and SBP to determine the optimal treatment strategy for each subgroup.

According to the decision tree, patients with an SRA ≤56.5° initially fell into the EVAR-benefit category. However, those with bilateral short iliac landing zones were reclassified as OSR-benefit, though within this group, patients without diabetes mellitus were subsequently categorized back into the EVAR-benefit group. This logical framework continued throughout the decision tree to determine optimal treatment strategies for all patients. Fig. 7 presents the complete structure of the clinical decision tree.

DISCUSSION

EVAR has improved survival rates in rAAA patients, but it is not suitable for everyone. The procedure is particularly challenging in patients with hostile aneurysmal necks or complex iliac arteries. While the IFU provides clinical decision-making criteria, simply following a binary rule of applying EVAR for cases without IFU violations and OSR for those with violations has significant limitations. First, IFU violations are common in real-world practice, and patients with on-IFU anatomy are relatively rare in Korea [27], especially in rAAA cases. Second, different types and severities of violations affect prognosis in varying ways, meaning some violations may not completely rule out EVAR. Finally, this simplified approach fails to properly consider the different physiological burdens that EVAR and OSR place on patients.

Treatment decisions for rAAA require an individualized approach that considers anatomical, demographic, clinical, and physiological features. Traditional research methods alone struggle to establish suitable decision criteria due to the large number of factors involved. To address these limitations, this study used a causal inference model to estimate whether EVAR or OSR would be more beneficial for each patient and to develop a clinical decision rule based on these estimates.

Analysis showed a mean ITE of 0.22 (±0.42) for the overall cohort, with 33% of patients falling into the OSR-benefit group. One of the 5 folds had a notably higher mean ITE of 0.64 (±0.49), indicating a distinct subgroup that would significantly benefit from OSR. SHAP scatter plot analysis revealed that OSR was more beneficial for patients with larger suprarenal and IRAs, CIA diameters, proximal neck diameters, neck lengths, and aneurysmal diameters, as well as those with bilateral short iliac landing zones. For physiological characteristics, OSR-benefit was associated with lower hemoglobin, creatinine, and SBP. These findings, however, conflict with established clinical wisdom that such hemodynamically unstable patients are poor candidates for open surgery. Furthermore, the model failed to properly account for the clinical necessity of OSR in patients with short aneurysmal necks due to their high risk of type Ia endoleak.

Analysis of the waterfall plot for the representative case with an ITE of 1.0 revealed both hostile neck anatomy and severely compromised physiological reserve; specifically, an SBP of 56 mmHg and hemoglobin of 6.8 g/dL. While this study employed a nonlinear methodology that considered feature combinations holistically rather than evaluating treatment benefits based on individual features, we must be cautious about concluding that OSR would definitively outperform EVAR in such cases. These findings point to potential overfitting from the limited dataset size and underscore the importance of validation through larger-scale studies.

In the clinical decision tree analysis, SRA, short iliac landing zone, and right CIA diameter emerged as the primary splitting criteria. Despite the limited sample size, the appearance of clinically significant features as decision nodes lends some credibility to the model’s explanatory power. However, several limitations became apparent.

First, not all features deemed important in the SHAP analysis appeared as decision points in the decision tree. This discrepancy exists because SHAP evaluates each feature’s overall contribution to predictions, while decision trees choose splits based on maximum information gain at each node.

While these 2 methods serve different statistical purposes and complement each other, clinicians may find it confusing when variables known to be clinically significant do not appear at decision points. This is particularly problematic in emergency situations, where quick decisions must be based on clearly meaningful clinical variables. Future studies should therefore explore using weighted decision trees that either incorporate SHAP results or use regularized decision trees to prioritize clinically important features.

Second, the model had a structural limitation where certain features were only considered in specific contexts and disregarded afterward. For instance, once the SRA exceeded 56.5°, the presence of a short iliac landing zone no longer factored into subsequent decisions. This approach contradicts clinical practice, where surgeons may still opt for EVAR despite minor SRA violations if the iliac anatomy is favorable. This disconnect between the model’s decision-making process and clinical reality undermines its practical utility.

Because almost all early deaths were attributable to aneurysm rupture or perioperative complications, aneurysm-related mortality closely paralleled all-cause mortality in our cohort (24.4% vs. 28.9%). Therefore, our findings and conclusions were not materially altered when aneurysm-related mortality was used as the endpoint.

Third, the cohort size of our study is significantly limited (n = 45). Due to this small sample size, the study suffered from insufficient statistical power. For instance, despite substantial clinical differences in sex distribution (80.8% of males in the EVAR group vs. 57.9% of males in the OSR group) and the prevalence of ischemic heart disease (42.3% in the EVAR group vs. 15.8% in the OSR group), these variables did not reach statistical significance. This lack of statistical power introduces a significant risk of bias; thus, the model’s derived variable-outcome relationships and subsequent clinical decision tree may not be grounded in a sufficient amount of data.

Furthermore, the small sample size led to severe model overfitting. To assess the structural consistency of our methodology, we additionally trained a simple random forest classifier. While the model’s ROC curve showed a perfect AUC of 1.0, the pattern of the calibration curve suggests that this likely reflects overfitting from the limited sample size rather than truly robust and generalizable reasoning. This is particularly evident in the decision tree, which yielded clinically impractical criteria such as an age of 90 years and an SBP of 61.5 mmHg. Such unrealistic thresholds not only diminish the model’s practical utility but also raise questions about its medical validity. This is a classic example of the “curse of dimensionality” [28], a phenomenon in machine learning where the number of data points needed to adequately train a model grows exponentially with the number of variables. In this study, the cohort size was simply too small relative to the diversity and number of features, making these results unavoidable.

Intriguingly, the treatment-matched group exhibited higher mortality rates than the mismatched group. This observation suggests 2 possibilities: either the matched group inherently contained patients with more severe baseline conditions, or both the model and clinicians were influenced by shared biases leading to suboptimal decisions. However, we must be cautious with this interpretation, as it relies on the unproven assumption that alternative treatments might have prevented these deaths. Given that a rAAA is an extremely severe emergency condition with high mortality rates even with optimal treatment, the matched group may simply have included more critically ill patients.

Conversely, the mismatched group demonstrated lower mortality rates, suggesting that clinicians’ treatment decisions may have been more advantageous than the model’s recommendations. One possible explanation is that real-world clinical decisions incorporated a broader spectrum of factors not captured by our model. As a non-randomized, retrospective observational study where treatment decisions relied on clinical judgment, our study faces a significant risk of unmeasured confounding, which could introduce structural bias in ITE estimation. This also raises questions about the model’s generalizability and highlights the necessity for future research. Thus, subsequent studies should collect a broader range of variables to identify potential confounders and implement methodological approaches to address the non-randomized nature of the data.

One of the potential candidates for such an unmeasured confounder is the operating surgeons’ experience or comfort level, which could influence both treatment selection and patient outcomes. Although all surgeons in our cohort performed more than 10 cases of EVAR and OSR annually, we acknowledge that this minimum volume may not capture the full spectrum of surgeon-specific factors. As such, the influence of these unmeasured confounders could introduce bias into the ITE estimation. Therefore, future research may explore and consider incorporating operator effects into the model.

Another limitation of this study is the binary classification of certain anatomical variables, such as aneurysmal neck calcification and intraluminal thrombus. While a 50% threshold is a commonly adopted standard, this approach does not capture the full granularity of these features. More detailed quantification, such as the exact percentage of the vessel circumference occupied by calcification or the precise volume of the thrombus, could potentially provide a more nuanced understanding of their impact on treatment outcomes and long-term prognosis. We suggest that future studies with larger, multicenter cohorts aim to develop and validate more granular scoring systems for these variables to further refine risk stratification models.

Despite the aforementioned limitations, this study serves as a possible proof-of-concept for applying causal inference models to personalize treatment strategies for rAAA patients. Our findings suggest that with further refinement, such a model could become a valuable clinical tool in emergency settings. Comprehensively analyzing a patient’s clinical parameters, it could rapidly assist in determining which treatment (EVAR or OSR) is likely to be more beneficial. A key strength of this approach is its ability to provide an evidence-based rationale for its recommendations, thereby functioning as a reliable decision-support tool for surgeons. Therefore, future multi-institutional studies are essential to validate the model’s performance and to confirm its clinical applicability.

This study showed that personalized treatment approaches for rAAA can be developed by combining anatomical and physiological characteristics using a causal inference model. While we identified meaningful patterns and clinically useful decision rules despite our small cohort, overfitting and limited generalizability significantly constrained our findings. The discrepancies between model predictions and actual clinical outcomes point to unmeasured factors and the inherent complexity of real-world treatment decisions. To validate these initial findings and develop robust, generalizable models for personalized rAAA treatment, we need larger multicenter studies.
