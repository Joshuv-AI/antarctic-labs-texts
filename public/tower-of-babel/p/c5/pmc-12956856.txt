Cell fate acquisition at a de novo developmental boundary in the maize leaf
Authors: Lukas J. Evans, Ruqiang Zhang, Maria Camila Medina, Elizabeth A. Fitzgerald, Anne W. Sylvester, George Chuck, Samuel Leiboff, Michael J. Scanlon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceDevelopmental boundaries are conserved features of plant and animal morphogenesis, comprising cellular borders separating two tissue-types that eventually acquire distinct cell fates. In grass leaves, specialized tissues form at the boundary between the lower sheath and upper blade, including a hinge-like auricle. The auricle contributes to leaf angle as a key domestication trait, yet its development is not understood. Here, we analyzed the cell lineage, patterns of cell division, and cell-specific gene expression of the auricle at three developmental stages. The findings uncover a mechanism whereby the auricle is recruited from previously existing blade tissue to acquire a unique cell fate at the leaf boundary.

The formation of developmental boundaries is a ubiquitous feature of multicellular plants and animals, which diverged prior to the advent of multicellularity (reviewed in ref. 1). Boundaries form between two or more adjacent developmental fields that exhibit differential gene expression, and which will ultimately advance distinct cell fates (reviewed in refs. 2 and 3). In animals, cells are subdivided into developmental fields via asymmetric responses to morphogen signaling. Boundaries demarcating these developmental fields are reinforced by localized cell interactions, cytoskeletal reorganization, and the formation of rigid boundary cell types where cell division is reduced. Developmental compartments in animals are lineage-restricted, whereas plant cell fate is position-dependent. Thus, animal cell fate correlates with cell origin whereas cell fate acquisition in plants depends upon where a cell winds up during organogenesis. Positional-dependent cell fate acquisition in plants invokes mechanisms whereby cells at the boundary between two developmental fields can be diverted, or recruited, to acquire a new cell fate. In this way, cells at plant developmental boundaries may be recruited to acquire novel cell fates, regulated by positional/temporal gene expression (4).

Expression of one or more LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARY (LBD) or NAC (NO APICAL MERISTEM; ARABIDOPSIS TRANSCRIPTION ACTIVATION FACTOR1/2; CUP-SHAPED COTYLEDON) genes mark plant developmental boundaries in repeating, serially homologous ways throughout ontogeny (reviewed in ref. 5). These molecular regulators confer similar properties of restricted cell division and differences in cell rigidity, defined as resistance to deformation, a convergently evolved feature in plants and animals (6, 7). Likewise, across kingdoms, the cells adjacent to these developmental boundaries are morphogenetic, and grow to assume distinct cell fates.

The grass leaf is a model system for analyses of de novo boundary specification and subsequent morphogenesis in plants (reviewed in ref. 8). Maize leaves are plant lateral organs formed successively from stem cell reservoirs known as shoot apical meristems (SAMs). A plastochron is the time interval between the initiation of successive leaves from the SAM (9). The plastochron index is a useful strategy to describe leaf ontogeny, wherein the Plastochron 1 (P1) leaf is the newly initiated leaf and the P2 leaf is the next oldest in development, etc. (10). Plant development is primarily postembryonic and is progressive (11), such that the plastochron index—time since leaf initiation—also describes the ontogenetic progress of a developing leaf primordium. At maturity, the maize leaf comprises a proximal leaf sheath that surrounds the stem, and a distal photosynthetic leaf blade projecting away from the plant axis. The boundary between the leaf blade and sheath is delineated by an epidermally derived, adaxial (dorsal) outgrowth of distal sheath margin called the ligule, and two triangular-shaped wedges of L1-L2 derived auricle tissue that act as hinges to maximize light capture in the blade (Fig. 1 A and B). The morphogenesis of these grass leaf boundary-derived structures is an enduring study (12, 13).

We previously discovered that the P4 stage leaf, i.e., the ontogenetic interval wherein a leaf is the fourth-newest primordium generated from the SAM, contains a proximodistal, transcriptomic prepattern of morphogenetic mRNAs previously implicated in ligule-auricle development, long before any observed morphogenesis at the blade–sheath boundary (14). Morphogenesis at this leaf boundary begins later, with a shift in orientation from anticlinal (perpendicular) to periclinal cell division (i.e., parallel to the tissue plane) in the epidermal layer (Fig. 1D). Subsequent cell division and outgrowth in the P8 leaf generates the Early-Fringe staged ligule (Fig. 1E), creating a notch between the enlarging ligule and the slower-growing auricle. Continued ligule development is seen at the P9 Mid-Fringe stage (Fig. 1F) until the tip of the ligule becomes jaggedly less uniform at the P10 Late-Mid-Fringe stage (Fig. 1G). The mature, Late-Fringe stage is reached at P11, when the ligule is extended and unevenly tipped with numerous projecting hair-like cells (Fig. 1 B and H). Thus, the structures comprising the maize blade–sheath boundary are initiated de novo within already existing leaf primordia, beginning approximately 7 d after they arise from the SAM.

Mutations in the plant-specific transcription factor-encoding gene LIGULELESS1 (LG1) essentially delete the maize ligule and auricle although a distinct blade–sheath boundary is maintained (15–18), resulting in extremely upright leaf angles (Fig. 1C). Thus, LG1 is required for primordial-staged morphogenesis at the maize blade–sheath boundary but is not required for boundary formation (16). LG1 transcripts are first observed in P5 staged leaf primordia, prior to ligule initiation at P7, and persist during later ontogenetic stages (19). Transcriptomic analyses indicate that genes utilized during leaf initiation from the SAM are redeployed at the morphogenetic blade–sheath boundary during ligule initiation (19). Subsequent molecular and genetic analyses support a model where the ligule is homologous to the distal margin of the maize leaf sheath (20). Auricle morphogenesis however, remains unclear and understudied, as are later stages of ligule outgrowth and differentiation.

Here we deploy cell lineage analyses, morphometric imaging of cell division and growth, and single cell–scale multidimensional transcriptomics to generate predictive models for cell fate acquisition and morphogenesis at developmental boundary in the maize leaf. The data suggest that auricle founder cells are recruited from the leaf blade via suppression of blade identity at early stages in blade–sheath boundary development, and subsequently undergo specific patterns of gene expression, cell division & growth, and differentiation to acquire a novel cell fate in the maize leaf.

Results and Discussion

Auricle Cells Are Recruited from Blade Prior to Formation of the Blade–Sheath Boundary.

Auricles form on already existing leaf primordia at a position distal to the sheath and ligule, at the proximal end of the blade. The question arises as to the clonal origin of the cells that will eventually form the auricle; is the auricle derived from sheath or blade founder cells? Cell lineage relationships of tissues/organs can be inferred from analyses of marked, clonal sectors that are induced during early stages in the development of that organ. Clonal sectors derived from a genetically marked progenitor cell can identify the cell fate/destination of primordial cells, i.e. what these founder cells will eventually divide into and become (reviewed in refs. 21 and 22). Previous studies using X-ray-induced hemizygous, albino sectors showed that the leaf edges (margins) of sheath primordial cells are already overlapped at their emergence from the node, whereas blade founder cells do not overlap at initiation. Instead, leaf blade margins overlap via expansive blade growth during later primordial stages (23–30). Importantly, meristematic, clonal sectors will mark the leaf founder cells within the SAM, long before the auricle or ligule are initiated. Such clonal sectors induced on leaf founder cells that will eventually give rise to the leaf margins mark both the left and right sides of the overlapping sheath margins but only mark one side of the nonoverlapping blade margins (Fig. 1 I–K). Subsequent computed-tomographic imaging supports this model where the margins of the maize leaf sheath emerge from the node already overlapping, but blade cells do not (29); (Fig. 1J). Reexamination of these albino sectored plants shows that the auricle is also marked on just one side, in leaves where the sheath is sectored on both margins (Fig. 1H). Thus, clonal sector data suggest that the auricle is recruited from blade founder cells.

The concept of cell recruitment defines the processes whereby existing cells change their prior identity to form different cell types. We next sought to identify the developmental timepoint when recruitment of auricle-cells-from-blade is completed. Grass leaves exhibit vascular anastomosis at the boundary between the blade and the auricle (31), marked by the coalescence/joining of multiple intermediate veins from the blade into single vessels in the auricle and subtending sheath. Confocal imaging using the previously described ZmPIN1a-YFP maize vascular reporter line (32) reveals that anastomosis is established at the P8 stage of leaf blade development (Fig. 1 L–N), after ligule initiation but long before the auricle has assumed its characteristic, triangular-wedge shape (Fig. 1N). Once established, this line of anastomosis demarcating the blade and auricle persists, thereby preventing further recruitment of blade cells into auricle. Otherwise, continued recruitment of existing blade cells to become auricle would necessitate the dismantling of anastomotic vascular figures seen at P8 and subsequently reconstructing them distally at the new blade–auricle boundary, but this is not observed. Once the blade–auricle boundary is established at P8, subsequent cell growth and division in the auricle are predicted to alter the geometry of this boundary. The data suggest that the proposed recruitment of auricle founder cells from blade must be completed prior to the P8 stage of leaf development, prior to anastomosis and formation of this vascular, blade–auricle boundary. We next focused on characterizing the morphological and transcriptomic characteristics of auricle tissues and cells as this developmental field is recruited and elaborated.

Morphometric Cell Division and Growth in the Auricle Is Delayed, Compared to the Blade, Sheath, and Ligule.

To study the cellular dynamics of auricle growth from Early-Fringe until Late-Mid Fringe stages (Fig. 1 E–G), we utilized confocal imaging of a ZmTUBULIN-YFP reporter line that enables analyses of cytoskeletal dynamics during plant cell division (33), in combination with quantification of auricle cell shape and growth using the software MorphoGraphX (34); (Fig. 2).

Plants were dissected to expose the abaxial (ventral) surfaces of the developing blade–sheath boundary at three specific stages, based upon the developmental stage of the ligule fringe on the adaxial side. Images of these curved, auricle structures are amenable to MorphoGraphX analyses of cell size and shape. At the Early-Fringe stage, the auricle comprises a rectangular field approximately 10 to 15 cells tall (~100 um), spanning the boundary between the distal blade and proximal sheath cells (Fig. 2 B and E). Comparatively few mitotic TUBULIN figures are observed in Early-Fringe staged auricle cells relative to the blade or sheath (Fig. 2B), and MorphoGraphX reveals that these auricles cells are likewise significantly smaller (Fig. 2E). One plastochron later when the ligule shows expansive growth (Fig. 1 E and F), the Mid-Fringe auricle has increased in size yet still retains its rectangular shape from midrib to leaf margin (Fig. 2 C and F). Likewise, mitotic figures and cell sizes remained reduced in the Mid-Fringe auricle as compared to the expanding blade and sheath (Fig. 2 C and F).

Changes in auricle morphology are observed at the Late-Mid-Fringe stage. Multiple mitotic figures are detected in the auricle epidermal cells (Fig. 2D), accompanied by proliferative increases in auricle cell size and number (Fig. 2G), especially toward the leaf margins. Because of this patterned gradient of cell division and expansion, the auricle first assumes its characteristic, triangular-wedge shape at the Late-Mid-Fringe stage. At maturity, the widest edge of the auricle comprises the leaf margin, which slopes triangularly to form a narrow point near the midrib (Figs. 1C and 2 D and G).

Remarkably, although the maize auricle develops no stomata (35), parallel files of abaxial, guard-cell stomatal pairs can be traced from the blade primordium down into the sheath of late-staged P11 leaves, a pattern that is interrupted by the auricle (Fig. 2H). Stomatal development begins in the blade at P6 and traverses basipetally (from tip to base); this pattern is not executed within the auricle. Stomatal figures are later observed in the sheath at P8 stage, also proceeding basipetally. The data suggest that a basipetal, stomatal prepattern is interrupted by the auricle, prior to stomatal morphogenesis, resulting in the absence of stomata in the maize auricle [Fig. 2H; (35)]. Last, live-staining comparisons of Early-Fringe and Late-Fringe-staged ligules reveal widespread cell death of the larger, distal cells of Late-Fringe ligules, suggesting that at maturity, maize ligules comprise large, sclerified, nonliving cells distal to smaller, live cells near the ligule base (Fig. 2 I and J).

Multiplexed Transcriptomic Analyses of Auricle and Ligule Ontogeny Suggest Distinct Mechanisms of Cell Fate Acquisition.

We conducted single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of maize protoplasts derived from live samples of Early-Fringe, Mid-Fringe, and Late-Mid-Fringe staged blade–sheath boundaries to examine cell-specific transcript accumulation dynamics during ontogeny and to identify candidate genes for cell fate identity in the developing sheath, blade, ligule, and auricle. In particular, we were looking for insight into mechanisms of auricle recruitment. Blade–sheath boundary tissues were sampled in entirety, from margin to margin and in two replicates, for the Early and Mid-stage and one replicate for the Late-Mid stage. Samples included the expanse of the ligule/auricle regions plus approximately 0.5 mm of the bordering blade or sheath tissue at each stage (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Samples were protoplasted and processed for single-cell RNAseq as described previously (36). Quantifications of scRNA-seq features identified increased numbers of cells and cell clusters as development progressed from Early-Fringe (4,684 and 20) to Mid-Fringe (8, 180 and 21) and Late-Mid (11,946 and 29) staged blade–sheath boundaries (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Increased cell cluster numbers during ontogeny are a predicted consequence as developmental fields subdivide and grow. To correct the transcriptomic data for the effects of protoplasting stress, bulk RNA sequencing controls (i.e., not subjected to protoplasting) were compared to scRNA-seq samples for all morphogenetic stages. Protoplast -specific transcripts were excluded from downstream analysis (Dataset S1).

Cell cluster identities (i.e., vasculature, epidermis, sheath, blade, ligule, auricle) in all the Uniform Manifold Approximation Projections (UMAPs) of scRNA-seq data were assigned according to previous descriptions of transcript accumulation during maize ontogeny (SI Appendix, Figs. S3–S5 and Datasets S2 and S3), and to the multiplexed in situ RNA-targeting analyses presented herein (Figs. 3 and 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Cells implicated in ligule-auricle development are respectively highlighted in red and pink in the UMAPS, and occupy an increasingly expanding number of subclusters during ontogeny (Fig. 3A). We anticipated that as developmental fields subdivide and differentiate cell types during ontogeny, we would detect evidence of serial subclustering of cell/tissue domains in the UMAPs. As predicted, the L1-derived ligule cells comprise growing subclusters of the much larger, epidermal cell clusters as development progresses, whereas a few ligule cells subcluster with sheath. In contrast, auricle cells occupy multiple colonies of UMAP subclusters during ontogeny (Fig. 3 A1–A3), perhaps reflecting the differentiation of positionally distinct auricle cell types as well as formation of the disparate auricle/blade and auricle/sheath (ligule) boundaries within all tissue layers (Figs. 1 D–H and 2). Accordingly, Late-Mid-Fringe stage auricle cells form subclusters adjacent to the blade and sheath, as well as subclusters within the larger L2-derived vasculature and L1-derived, epidermal clusters.

LG1 is a molecular-genetic marker of auricle and ligule morphogenesis (15, 17, 18); transcripts are first detected prior to ligule initiation and their abundance increases throughout later stages in blade–sheath boundary ontogeny (19). Our scRNA-seq data identify LG1-expressing cells localized to auricle and ligule clusters in the Early-Fringe stage (Fig. 3B), later expanding to multiple auricle-ligule subclusters in later stages with peaked expression at Late-Mid-Fringe (Fig. 3J and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). LG1 yields robust signals throughout the developing ligule and auricle at all stages examined, as demonstrated in our in situ RNA-targeting analyses (Fig. 3 K and L4–O4).

Previous analyses in Arabidopsis, rice, and barley show that BLADE-ON-PETIOLE (BOP) genes promote morphogenesis of the proximal leaf domain, repress development of distal leaf domains and regulate development at the intervening leaf boundary (37–43). In grasses, BOP transcripts accumulate in the sheath, ligule, and auricle of maize, barley, and rice during early stages of ligule/auricle initiation (19, 30, 41, 42, 44). In agreement, our scRNAseq data detect transcript accumulation of two-to-four paralogous BOP transcripts (BOP1a, BOP1b, TRU1, and TRL1) in ligule and sheath primordia at all three stages examined, with much reduced accumulation in the blade (Fig. 3 C and J and SI Appendix, Figs. S3D, S4D, and S5D). BOP1a in situ RNA-targeting supports these findings (Fig. 3 K and L3–O3). In the auricle, BOP1a and BOP1b transcripts accumulate at initiation (P7) and the Early-Fringe stage (P8); thereafter these BOPs are not detected in the auricle (Fig. 3 C and J and SI Appendix, Figs. S3–S5). Two other maize BOPs, TRU1 and TRL1, are also highly expressed in the auricle at Early-Fringe but not at Mid-Fringe; these paralogs are again detected in the Late-Mid (P10) staged auricle (Fig. 3J). At all stages, RNA-targeting reveals no detectable BOP1a expression in the blade, distal to LG1 accumulation at the blade–sheath boundary (Fig. 3 K, L3–O3, and L4–O4). We propose that relatively higher expression of BOP paralogs during early stages of auricle ontogeny contributes to the repression of blade identity during primordial-staged recruitment of auricle cells from the blade. We further propose that the relative cessation of BOP expression in later stages prevents the auricle from becoming sheath, thereby enabling the acquisition of a unique, auricle cell fate.

We introduce a CYTOCHROME P450 (CYP450) gene, previously proposed to function in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway (45), as a new marker for leaf blade identity in maize primordia (Fig. 3 D and J and SI Appendix, Figs. S3–S5). CYP450 transcripts are identified in distal, leaf primordial regions throughout ontogeny (Fig. 3 K, J, and L5–O5). Although coaccumulation of CYP450 and BOP1a, BOP1b, TRU1 transcripts is observed in P7-staged initiating auricle cells and persists until the Early-Fringe stage (Fig. 3 L2–M2), later refinement renders CYP450 accumulation to the blade and BOP expression in the sheath and ligule. No CYP450/BOP coexpression is observed after the Early-Fringe stage (Fig. 3 C, D, J, L3–O3, and L5–O5). Notably, CYP450 transcript accumulation is not observed during any stage of ligule or sheath ontogeny (Fig. 3 L5–O5), in support of models where the ligule comprises the distal margin of the leaf sheath (20).

Although our scRNA-seq data reveal that transcripts of the proposed stomatal inhibitor TOO MANY MOUTHS-LIKE 2 [TMM2; (46)] accumulate throughout the developing blade and sheath (Fig. 3G and SI Appendix, Fig. S7), TMM2 transcripts are especially abundant during auricle initiation at P7 (19) and in the Early-Fringe staged auricle (Fig. 3 G and J). Homologous to Arabidopsis stomatal repressor TOO MANY MOUTHS1 (47), genetic analyses show that the maize TMM-like2 is upregulated in mutants that condition reduced stomatal densities (46). We propose that extended expression of TMM-like2 during auricle ontogeny contributes to the inhibition of stomatal morphogenesis in the maize auricle, thereby creating a stomatal gap between the blade and sheath (Fig. 2G).

Identification of Auricle and Ligule Marker Genes.

Although the epidermally derived ligule and the histologically complete auricle both require LG1 function for elaboration, their gene expression profiles and morphometric development are quite divergent, especially later in ontogeny. Ligule and auricle cells occupy different cell clusters at all ontogenetic stages analyzed by scRNA-seq (Fig. 3A). Multiplexed in situ RNA-targeting illustrates these distinct transcriptomic trajectories (Fig. 4). The combination of single–cell transcriptomics and RNA-targeting in situ hybridization presents a powerful tool for the identification of ligule and auricle cell marker genes. The Class 1 KNOX gene family member LIGULELESS3 [LG3; (48)] was previously described as expressed in the P7-staged auricle but not in the initiating ligule (19). Our scRNA-seq analyses confirm LG3 transcript accumulation in the initiating and Early-Fringe staged auricle, but not at later stages nor in the ligule (Fig. 3 F and J and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Although genetic analyses of LG3 function are limited to dominant, overexpression alleles (49), related Class 1 KNOX gene family members are described as regulators of cell indeterminacy (50–54) and LG3 transcripts accumulate abundantly in the SAM and stem (19); (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Our data support speculative models wherein LG3 confers morphogenetic potential to auricle primordial cells (19, 20).

Four ligule-marker genes are presented in Fig. 4 A–C, including the INDETERMINATE DOMAIN (IDD) paralog IDD31 whose function is unknown, although two paralogous maize IDD paralogs are implicated to regulate LG1 [(55, 56); SI Appendix, Fig. S9]. IDD31 transcripts are abundant in the Early-Fringe (Fig. 4A2) and Mid-Fringe ligule but are reduced at Late-Mid stage (Fig. 4B2–C2). VESTIGIAL GLUMES1 (VG1) encodes a fatty-acid synthase enzyme (57) that is strongly expressed in the ligule (but not in the auricle) throughout all stages examined (Figs. 3 H and J and 4 A3–C3 and G and SI Appendix, Figs. S3–S5). Dominant Vg1 mutants have pleiotropic phenotypes including ligule necrosis (58, 59). Moreover, two WOX3 paralogs previously implicated in leaf/ligule initiation and outgrowth (19–20) accumulate in the ligule tip at the Early-Fringe and Mid-Fringe stages (Figs. 3J and 4 A4–C4 and A5–C5). The dramatic drop in WOX3 transcript accumulation at Late-Mid-Fringe staged ligules is presumably reflective of cell death in the Late-Fringe ligule (Fig. 2 H and I).

Four auricle-marker transcripts enriched during auricle but not ligule ontogeny are featured in Fig. 4 D–F. A GRETCHEN HAGEN3-like (GH3) gene predicted to function in auxin conjugation shows transcript accumulation in the internal auricle tissues at P7-staged initiation (19); (Fig. 4D2), at the Early-Fringe stage above the point of ligule insertion (Fig. 4E2) and accumulates strongly at the Mid-Fringe stage (Figs. 3J and 4F2). GH3 transcripts are downregulated in lg1 mutants (19) and homologous, rice misexpression mutants condition ligule/auricle phenotypes that affect leaf angle (60). Two GIBBERELLIN2-OXIDASE paralogs, GA2OX2 and GA2OX9, are predicted to function during gibberellin catabolism (61) and show overlapping but nonidentical accumulation patterns during auricle ontogeny (Fig. 4 D3–F4). GA2OX2 transcripts predominate in the Early-Fringe auricle but are reduced later (Fig. 4 D3–F3), whereas GA2OX9 accumulation is more abundant at Mid-Fringe and Late-Mid-Fringe stages (Fig. 4 D4–F4). Last, TRIACYLGLYCEROL LIPASE1 (TGL1) is a robust new expression marker for all stages of auricle ontogeny [Figs. 3J and 4 D5–F5; (19)]. TGL1 is not detected in the ligule and is predicted to function during lipid metabolism, although no functional-genetic analyses are available.

When analyzed alongside our cell/tissue specific scRNAseq data, RNA-targeting of multiple morphogenetic transcription factors in longitudinal sections of the maize seedling shoot provides insights into their coexpression and potential interactions during shoot ontogeny. Fig. 4G illustrates the simultaneous accumulation patterns of ten maize transcripts selected for their implicated roles in adaxial/abaxial polarity [MILKWEED POD1(MWP1), AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR3a (ARF3a), INDETERMINATE GAMETOPHYTE 1 (IG1), PHABULOSA (PHAB)], boundary specification (CUC2/3), vasculature-mediated transport (AUXIN IMPORT CARRIER1 (AIC1)) and other genes implicated in ligule/auricle morphogenesis in maize (LG1, VG1, TGL1).

A Topological Landscape Model for Cell Fate Transition in the Maize Auricle.

Fate mapping and cell-specific transcriptomic data suggest that the auricle is recruited from existing blade primordial cells to assume a new cell/tissue type (Fig. 1 I–N). To model cell fate acquisition in the maize auricle, we generated a computational topological landscape in which specific cell fates, termed attractors, are estimated based upon the relative uniformity of gene expression in different cells/tissues during blade–sheath boundary development. In such models, cell/tissue types with higher uniformity of gene expression have higher probabilities of acquiring a cell type (attractor). Topological landscapes are computational visualizations of a Waddington’s landscape, which is a metaphor for cell differentiation where progenitor cells transcend a sloping developmental topography before settling into one of multiple attractors (cell types) at the bottom of the landscape. Dynamic gene expression combined with internal and external signaling during cell differentiation determines the specific cell fate trajectories down this topological landscape. Important to our study of auricle development, cells can transition between different attractors, ultimately altering their identity in response to ontogenetic changes in gene expression. Such reprogramming of cell fate is defined as recruitment.

To portray how auricle cells are recruited at the blade–sheath boundary, topological landscapes were generated utilizing tissue/cell-specific transcriptomic data from successive ontogenetic stages including 1) four proximo-distal regions (RB,RC,RD,RE) laser-microdissected from P4-staged leaf primordia prior to auricle initiation (14), 2) microdissected sheath, blade, and ligule/auricle tissue at the P7 ligule initiation stage (19), and 3) single-cell RNA-seq data from P8, where the ligule and auricle are in Early-Fringe stage (Fig. 3A). For each sample, we first investigated the transcriptomic variance/heterogeneity to identify different cell types, attractors, in the landscape (Fig. 5 A–F). Next, these data were utilized to compute topological landscape models for these ontogenetic stages, to identify attractors corresponding to blade, sheath, and/or auricle cell types (Fig. 5 G–I and SI Appendix, Fig. S11).

(B) Transcriptomic comparisons of sheath, blade, and initiating ligule/auricle domains microdissected from P7-staged primordia. (C) Transcriptomic variations within blade–sheath boundary domains of P8-staged Early-Fringe, P9-staged Mid-Fringe, and P10 Late-Mid Fringe samples. (D) Density map comparing expression levels of the sheath marker BOP1a versus the blade marker CYP450 at the P4 prepattern stage reveals two attractors, RB and RC-RE. (E) Dot plot comparing sheath and blade marker genes at the P7-staged primordium shows higher BOP1a expression in the sheath and ligule/auricle than in the proximal blade. (F) Contour plot of blade and sheath marker expression in the P8-staged Early-Fringe shows the auricle occupies an intermediate position, between blade and sheath. (G–I) Computational topological landscapes describing auricle development with two attractors (predicted cell types, X1 and X2) for sheath and blade in the P4-staged primordium (G) and an emerging attractor in the P7-staged primordium (H). At the P8-staged Early-Fringe, this third attractor emerges to form a predicted auricle cell type, distinct from the preexisting blade and sheath attractors. (J) The predicted landscape of the lg1 mutant contains just two attractors (blade and sheath). (K and L) Contour plots of sheath and blade marker genes at Mid-Fringe (K) and Late-Mid-Fringe stage (L), respectively. (M) Husk leaves of the maize mutant tru1 show extensive blade development that is not seen in wild type siblings. CPM = counts per million; nor. = normalized.

At the P4 prepatterning stage (Fig. 5A), transcriptomic variance/heterogeneity increases progressively from the more uniform base (B) toward the more heterogeneous upper (C-E) regions of the developing primordium. Notably, the P4-staged density map of the blade/sheath marker genes BOP1a and CYP450 respectively shown in Fig. 5D reveals just two attractors, one comprising the basal RB region alone whereas the other three regions (RC-RE) cluster together as a second attractor. Based upon transcriptomic data, the resulting topological landscape (Fig. 5G) likewise begins with two attractors at the P4 prepatterning stage (i.e., prior to ligule initiation), corresponding to the deep and narrow topology of the uniform sheath attractor and the shallower and broader topology of the transcriptomically more heterogeneous blade attractor (Fig. 5G). Later at P7, the proximal sheath continues to show higher transcriptomic uniformity than the distal blade (Fig. 5 B and E). At the P8 Early-Fringe stage, contour plots confirm that the blade marker CYP450 displays substantially greater transcript variation than does BOP1a or its paralog TRU1 (Fig. 5F), reflecting the increased heterogeneity of blade versus sheath. During ligule/auricle initiation at P7, the topological landscape shows cells at the shallowest edges of the blade attractor, and bordering the sheath attractor, are beginning to organize a third attractor, the presumptive auricle (Fig. 5H). Later at P8, this third attractor emerges as the auricle initiates at the blade–sheath boundary (Fig. 5I). Last, removal of the auricle identity variable from the landscape enables a test of the model’s ability to predict cell fate acquisition in the lg1 mutant (Fig. 5J). No auricle attractor is recruited in this lg1 landscape, which likewise predicts the formation of mutant sheath and blade attractors that differ from wild type.

Taken together, the data contribute to a model for auricle cell fate acquisition in which cells are recruited from the shallow borders of the large blade attractor by a source–sink relationship involving phased expression and function of BOP genes. This model conforms to our cell lineage data, suggesting that the maize auricle is recruited from blade (Fig. 1 F–H). BOPS are known regulators of leaf boundary formation in Arabidopsis and grasses (39, 41, 42, 44). In detail, AtBOP1 and AtBOP2 are required for positioning of a “proliferative zone” at the boundary between the leaf petiole and lamina in Arabidopsis (39), where BOP function represses laminar cell fate and promotes petiole development. Likewise, an orthologous bop mutant in barley (i.e., cul4) disrupts ligule and auricle development (41). Double bop mutants in rice derepress blade development and reduce sheath and auricle development in a dose-dependent manner, whereas bop triple mutant leaves are composed entirely of blade and make no sheath or auricle (42, 43). Similarly, leaf angle phenotypes are reported for single mutations in the maize BOP homolog TRU1 (44). Our analyses of tru1 mutant husk leaves reveal expansive development of blade tissue that is not observed in wild type siblings, where husk leaves are primarily composed of sheath (Fig. 5M). Thus, the maize BOP homolog also functions to repress blade development. Taken together, the data reveal that a conserved BOP function in grasses is to repress blade identity, promote sheath identity, and promote development of the blade–sheath boundary.

Our multiplexed transcriptomic analyses reveal that maize BOP transcripts are essentially excluded from blade but are highly expressed in sheath/ligule. Moreover, paralogous BOP transcripts accumulate Initiating and Early-Fringe staged auricles but are much reduced in the Mid-Fringe staged auricle and later stages of auricle development (Figs. 3C and 5 K and L3–O3). Our model suggests that auricle cells are recruited from the blade via BOP expression at early stages in auricle development, which represses blade identity in these cells to induce a change in cell fate. At later stages, overall BOP accumulation is downregulated in the auricle, thereby promoting a novel auricle identity that is not blade but is also unique from the continuously BOP1a-expressing-sheath and ligule. Notably, the blade marker CYP450 is coexpressed with BOPs at early auricle stages (Figs. 3 J, L3–O3, and L5–O5 and 5F) but is excluded from later-staged auricle cells (Figs. 3 and 5L), providing additional support to this model for cell fate acquisition at the auricle.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions.

The maize inbred B73 was used in this study, and plants for histology, imaging, scRNA-seq, in situ hybridization, and multiplexed in situ hybridization were grown in 36-well trays in a Percival A100 growth chamber under 16-h days, a day temperature of 29.4 °C, a night temperature of 23.9 °C, and a relative humidity of 50% for 19 to 21 d. Soil consisted of a 1:1 mixture of Turface MVP and LM111.

Histology.

Shoot apices plus leaf primordia of maize B73 seedlings were fixed in FAA, dehydrated, cleared, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained as described (20, 62). Tissue sections were prepared using a rotary microtome. All samples for histology were imaged using an Axio Imager Z10 (Carl Zeiss Microscopy) microscope. Light microscopic imaging of sectioned samples was performed as previously described (29).

Confocal Imaging.

Confocal imaging was performed as previously described (63) on LSM u880 Upright Axio Examiner.Z1 microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy). Details are described in the SI Appendix.

MorphoGraphX.

MorphoGraphX v2.0.1 was used for image processing (34, 64). Stacks were converted to .tiff file format and imported into MorphoGraphX to create a 2.5D mesh of the abaxial surface of the leaf from the ZmTUBULIN-YFP signal, modified for this fluorescent reporter from previously described methods (65). Details are described in the SI Appendix.

Single-Cell Transcriptomic Analysis.

The detailed procedures for single-cell transcriptomic analysis were established previously (20, 66, 67), including protoplast isolation, scRNA-seq library preparation, scRNA-seq read processing, cell barcode filtering, and dimensionality reduction. The B73 inbred line, two replicates of Early-Fringe, two replicates of Mid-Fringe and one replicate of Late-Mid-Fringe blade–sheath boundaries tissues were collected, and 30 tissue samples were dissected for protoplast isolation in each batch as described previously (36, 68). Protoplasts were loaded into the 10X Genomics Chromium platform to generate a Gel Bead-In-Emulsion (GEM). The GEM-captured RNA was reverse transcribed and amplified to make 3′ cDNA libraries, followed by the high-throughput RNA sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 sequencer at the Cornell University Biotechnology Resource Center. Detailed descriptions of data normalization and analyses are found in the SI Appendix.

Multiplexed In Situ RNA-Targeting.

The procedures for multiplexed in situ hybridization are described previously (36), including gene probe design, seedling tissue sections, sample processing, and data analysis are summarized in the SI Appendix. Multiplexed in situ hybridization was performed on the 10× Genomics™ Xenium™ Platform for RNA targeting, at University of Rochester Genomics Research Center, Rochester, NY, following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Topological Modeling.

Methods are described in the SI Appendix, Fig. S11.
