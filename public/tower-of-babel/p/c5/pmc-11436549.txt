Ternary TiO2/MoS2/ZnO hetero-nanostructure based multifunctional sensing devices
Authors: Andrew F Zhou, Soraya Y Flores, Elluz Pacheco, Xiaoyan Peng, Susannah G Zhang, Peter X Feng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNovel sensing applications benefit from multifunctional nanomaterials responsive to various external stimuli such as mechanics, electricity, light, humidity, or pollution. While few such materials occur naturally, the careful design of synergized nanomaterials unifies the cross-coupled properties which are weak or absent in single-phase materials. In this study, 2D MoS2 integrated with ultrathin dielectric oxide layers forms hetero-nanostructures with significant impacts on carrier transport. The ternary TiO2/MoS2/ZnO hetero-nanostructures, along with their individual properties, improve the performance of multifunctional sensing devices. The synthesized hetero-nanostructure exhibits a responsivity of up to 16 mA/W to 700 nm light and responds to 5 ppm ammonia gas at room temperature. These enhancements are attributed to interface charge transfer and photogating effects. The ternary TiO2/MoS2/ZnO hetero-nanostructure is compatible with existing semiconductor fabrication technologies, making it feasible to integrate into flexible, lightweight semiconductor devices and circuits. These results may inspire new photodetectors and sensing devices based on two-dimensional (2D) layered materials for IoT applications.Keywords: Photodetector, Gas sensor, Two-dimensional material, Molybdenum disulfide, Zinc oxide, Titanium dioxide, Ternary hetero-nanostructure, Multifunctional sensor

Introduction

The Internet of Things (IoT) is a rapidly growing field that connects physical devices to the Internet, enabling them to collect and exchange data for various applications [1]. Sensors play a crucial role in IoT by capturing real-world data and transforming it into digital information, including temperature sensors, humidity sensors, light sensors, and gas sensors [2]. However, traditional battery-powered sensors require frequent battery replacements or recharging, which can be impractical in large-scale IoT deployments. Addressing this limitation, current research delves into the self-powered sensors which can reduce the need for external power sources and become more sustainable and environmentally friendly, especially in remote or inaccessible locations due to their self-sufficiency [3–6]. On the other hand, in the rapidly evolving Internet of Things (IoT) landscape, the development of multifunctional sensing extending beyond conventional applications aligns seamlessly with the demands of IoT devices. This innovative facet capitalizes on the unique properties of synergized and carefully engineered 2D nanoheterostructures. Moreover, the photoconductivity, coupled with the electrical conductivity of 2D hetero-nanostructures, contributes to the efficient charge transport within the hetero-nanostructure, thereby amplifying its sensitivity and responsiveness in photoinduced scenarios. This intrinsic ability not only ensures the sustainability of the multiple functional sensing devices but also contributes to the overall energy autonomy of IoT systems. For example, in outdoor or remote IoT deployments, where access to traditional power sources may be limited, the self-powered photodetector option enables continuous and uninterrupted operation, eliminating the need for external power sources in certain sensing modalities.

The significant research and development of photodetectors based on two-dimensional (2D) materials, such as graphene [7–11] transition metal dichalcogenides [12, 13] (molybdenum disulfide, tungsten disulfide, and others), coordination polymer nanosheets [14] and hexagonal boron nitride [15–19] have been witnessed in the past decade. 2D Molybdenum disulfide (MoS2)-based photodetectors [20, 21] have been successfully demonstrated. One particular advantage of this material is that, in its monolayer form, MoS2 undergoes a transition from an indirect to a direct bandgap. This feature makes it a direct bandgap semiconductor with a broad absorption spectrum ranging from ultraviolet to visible [22, 23]. Hence its electronic properties can be optimized by controlling the number of layers or by introducing defects for specific application customization. MoS2 also exhibits fast carrier dynamics and, as a result, MoS2-based photodetectors can have relatively fast response times [24]. In addition, MoS2 is a 2D material with a thin atomic structure, allowing for the fabrication of flexible and wearable photodetector devices, or integration with other materials to form hetero-nanostructures or hybrid nanocomposites [25].

On the other hand, gas sensing devices employing 2D MoS2 materials have emerged as a promising technology with notable sensitivity and selectivity for various gases such as nitrogen dioxide (NO2), ammonia (NH3), methane (CH4), and hydrogen (H2) [26, 27], which is particularly valuable for environmental monitoring and industrial safety applications [28]. Additionally, the tunable bandgap of MoS2 enables tailored sensor responses for specific gases, enhancing its applicability across diverse detection scenarios [29]. Meanwhile, the research on 2D titanium dioxide (TiO2) materials has garnered attention in detecting gases such as nitrogen dioxide (NO2), ammonia (NH3), carbon monoxide (CO), methane (CH4), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) [14, 30], and research on two-dimensional zinc oxide (2D ZnO) materials has demonstrated their effectiveness in detecting gases such as nitrogen dioxide (NO2), carbon monoxide (CO), methane (CH4), and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) [31, 32].

To enhance its performance or enable new functionalities in photodetector and gas sensor devices based on one material-based devices, MoS2 has been integrated with other 2D materials or nanostructures [33–38] to form binary heterostructures based sensor devices, including 2D MoS2/ZnO nanowire heterojunctions [39] and MoS2/TiO2 heterostructure phototransistors [40]. However, the binary sensors have limitations in terms of the specific wavelengths they can detect or the gases they are sensitive to. To overcome these limitations, in this paper, we extended the previous work on the use of binary nano-heterostructure based semiconductors as an active layer to the integration of titanium dioxide (TiO2), MoS2, and zinc oxide (ZnO) to form ternary hetero-nanostructures, as each material has unique properties that can contribute and enhance multiple functional sensing capabilities. The wide bandgap TiO2 is known for its excellent photocatalytic and photovoltaic properties [41]. It can absorb light and generate electron–hole pairs, especially in combination with other materials such as MoS2 into the heterostructure to enhance the sensitivities. ZnO is a wide-bandgap semiconductor commonly used in photodetectors with excellent optical, electrical, and piezoelectric properties, along with its ability to be easily synthesized in various nanostructures [42–45]. The fabrication of TiO2/MoS2/ZnO hetero-nanostructures is achieved using a simple synthesis strategy. The morphology and crystal structure are essential to achieve desired properties and are examined using SEM, EDX, and Raman spectroscopy. The performance of the self-powered photodetector based on TiO2/MoS2/ZnO hetero-nanostructures is characterized to assess the device's sensitivity, response time, and stability under illumination, as well as the sensing properties of NO2 gas at room temperature.

Experiment section

Preparation of TiO2/MoS2/ZnO nanoheterostructures

The TiO2/MoS2/ZnO hetero-nanostructures were prepared using a simple synthesis strategy. Firstly, a Si wafer with a 300 nm thick SiO2 insulating layer used as the substrate was cleaned by ultrasonic for 20 min in ultra-pure water, ethanol, and acetone. Then the TiO2 layer was synthesized for 30 s by using a 60 MHz magnetron sputtering system on the substrate at a constant power of 200 W, in which a pure TiO2 target (99.99%) and an Ar/O2 gas mixture were used, the base pressure of the chamber was 1.5 × 10–7 Torr, the working pressure during deposition was 8.0 × 10–3 Torr, and the Ar/O2 ratio was controlled to 30:1.

After deposition of a 20 nm thick TiO2 layer, the sample was transferred into the quartz tube of the CVD (Chemical Vapor Deposition) furnace where the Argon gas was introduced with a flow of 70 sccm. The sulfur powder (0.5 g) was placed at the entrance of the quartz tube, and the TiO2-Si samples were buckled upside down on a quartz boat containing molybdenum trioxide (MoO3) powders (0.02 g) in the center of the furnace for 30 s at 800 ℃ inside the tube to deposit a MoS2 layer. The formed MoS2 was estimated to be 500 nm thick, but it consisted of a large amount of MoS2 clusters. The surfaces of the samples were rough because each MoS2 nanosheet cluster in the sample appeared in random orientations. The sheet structure was clearly visible. The average thickness and size of a continuous MoS2 sheet is approximately 5 nm and 4 μm2, respectively. Detailed characterization of a single MoS2 sheet by using HRTEM (High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy) can be found in our previous work [38, 46].

Finally, nanostructured ZnO layers were deposited by using a simple low-cost spin coating technique with a commercial nano ZnO dispersion in solvent (dissolved solution with concentration of 1 mg/mL from Fisher Scientific). Prior to deposition, the ZnO solution was ultrasonicated at 200 W for two hours at 60 Hz amplitude in 10 s intermittent pulses, to disperse in solvents and break agglomerates in order to have a distribution uniformity. Each spin coating run was for 20 s with a spin rate of 2500 rev/min. Multiple coatings were used to increase the number of nano ZnO on the surface of the MoS2. Detailed descriptions of syntheses and characterizations of these n-type and p-type ZnO materials could be found in our previous work [39]. After annealing for 2 h at 800 °C, the films were examined by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Raman scattering, and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), respectively. The thickness of the generated ZnO layer was approximately 450 nm, depending on the number of spin coating runs. If the thickness of ZnO on the surface of the sample was over 2 μm, the sensing results were rather poor, as the material properties were dominated by the thick layer of ZnO.

Material characterizations

The surface morphology was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Jeol 6480LV JSM), under a vacuum of approximately 10–5 torr. The SEM operated with an accelerating voltage in the range of 10–20 kV. The elemental composition of the synthesized sample was quantitatively analyzed using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Furthermore, the structural and vibrational properties of the synthesized material were investigated via Raman spectroscopy using a JY Horiba T64000 Raman spectrometer. Raman scattering data were collected using a triple monochromator in subtractive mode equipped with a CCD detector. Excitation for the Raman spectra was achieved with 514.5 nm radiation at 5 mW from a Coherent Innova 70C Ar+ laser. Backscattering mode was employed with an Olympus microscope fitted with an 80X objective on an Olympus BX40.

Fabrication of electrode and sensing prototypes

After material characterizations, Au (~ 70 nm) metal electrodes were deposited by plasma sputtering technique on both sides of the sample to form a metal–semiconductor–metal (MSM) prototype, giving an active area of 0.25 cm2 in size.

Measurements

A photodetector workstation [47] was employed to analyze the performance of the prototype based on TiO2/MoS2/ZnO nanoheterostructures. To further explore its performance, the photocurrent was measured at different light wavelengths, bias voltages, light power densities, and temperatures. The characterizations of gas sensing properties of the prototype were carried out in the home-made characterization station [48] where various components including the thermocouple, heater, power supply, electrical connectors, pollution gas tanks, diagnostics, multichannel Pascal universal interface, as well as various Agilent and Hewlett meters were available.

Results and discussion

Morphology of sample surface

Figure 1a shows a typical SEM image of the rough surface of nanostructured MoS2 deposited on top of the TiO2 coated Si substrate. The sample consisted of numerous isolated 2D sheets which were randomly orientated and partially overlapped. Each continuous MoS2 sheet with an average diameter of ~ 1 μm was embedded in the TiO2 nanoparticles. Figure 1b shows the individual ZnO particles of as prepared sample and aggregates into larger particles. Owing to the irregular shapes, the surface area was largely exposed, indicating a desirable light-harvesting feature for prototypes. Figure 1c shows the optical OMM200 Metallurgical Trinocular (model: 0601446) Microscope image of 2D MoS2 sample. The nanosheets appear to have good optical transparency. Different optical contrasts are related to different number of layers. A high-resolution TEM can be used to directly measure tiny fringes at the edge of 2D MoS2, where each fringe is related to a single atomic layer, and thickness of each atomic layer is around ∼ 0.65 nm. Accordingly, the thickness of the obtained 2D MoS2 sheet could be estimated [38, 46]. The MoS2 sample, consisting of numerous 2D super-thin MoS2 sheets, had an approximate thickness of 500 nm [38].

EDS measurements

The surface chemical compositions of the as-deposited TiO2/MoS2 and TiO2/MoS2/ZnO on the silicon substrate were analyzed via EDS measurements, as shown in Fig. 2. The peaks at 50 eV belong to O, at binding energies of 100 eV, 350 eV, and 860 eV correspond to Zn, whereas the peak at 180 eV corresponds to Si. In Fig. 2a, Si has the highest peak of all the elements present in the MoS2/TiO2 composite. However, with the increase in the total thickness of the hetero-nanostructure, as shown in Fig. 2b, the Si peak from the substrate is considerably weakened compared to that of the first sample, owing to the X-ray absorption. For MoS2, the overlap between the L series peaks of Mo and the S Kα peak is very pronounced, only a broadened peak with a tail towards the high energy end can be seen with EDS [49]. The presence of S and Mo elements peaked at 231 eV indicates the successful synthesis of the MoS2. A low concentration of carbon impurity was also detected, probably from the contaminated quartz tube.

Raman measurements

Raman scattering spectroscopy excited with a 514 nm Ar+ laser wavelength was also used to examine the ternary hetero-nanostructure. As a comparison, Fig. 3a provides Raman spectra of as-synthesized TiO2/MoS2 sample on Si substrate over the spectral range 300–700 cm−1. It exhibits two characteristic Raman bands located at 382 cm–1 and 407 cm–1, which respectively correspond to the in-plane movements of Mo and S atoms (E2g1 mode) and out-plane symmetric movements of S atoms along the c-axis (A1g mode) of typical lamellar MoS2 structure. A wavenumber difference between the E2g1 and the A1g bands is 25 cm−1, which suggests that the average MoS2 sheet thickness is only five atomic layers [50]. The TiO2 displays an obvious band located at 519 cm−1, which is consistent with the specifically characteristic A1g band of the anatase TiO2 phase. As indicated in Fig. 3b, with the addition of the ZnO layer, the band centered at 450 cm−1 becomes noticeable, as reported previously [39]. The decrease of the TiO2 peak at 519 cm−1 further confirms the enhanced absorption of the ternary hetero-nanostructures in the visible spectrum.

Prototype peormance

The prototype performance was tested with the homemade photodetector characterization workstation. Calibrated LED light sources with different wavelengths were illuminated on the active area of the as-synthesized ternary hetero-nanostructure materials connected by one pair of electrodes, and the photoresponse was measured by a two-probe method [47]. Figure 4a illustrates the I–V characteristics of the ternary materials, exhibiting typical diode-like behavior. This electric property permits the passage of positive voltages through the anode while blocking negative voltages, thus maintaining a unidirectional flow of electricity. The prototype under study exhibits a breakdown voltage significantly exceeding − 6 V. These distinctive characteristics are directly linked to the exceptional material properties.

a Typical I–V characteristics of the prototype. Photocurrents of the prototype to light of different colors of the same intensity with b 2 V bias, and c 0 V bias. The estimated d rise time τon, and e recovery time τoff to red light with 0 V bias at room temperature

Figure 4b represents the time-dependent response of the prototype to light of different colors, indicating the device was stable, reliable, and more sensitive to red than blue light of the same intensity when the device operated with a 2 V bias at room temperature. The load resistance is 150 kΩ As shown in Fig. 4c, the wavelength sensitivity was further confirmed with the prototype operating with zero-volt bias. The maximum photocurrent around 40 nA occurs at 700 nm wavelength with 0 V bias while the photocurrent reaches above 20 μA with 2 V bias under the same light intensity. The photocurrent decreased when the wavelength became shorter and was no longer sensitive to wavelengths below 450 nm.

The photocurrents generated by red, blue, and green light differ, indicating varying responsivity of the sensor to different wavelengths. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) typically absorbs UV light, with absorption peaks around 380–400 nm, while Zinc oxide (ZnO) absorbs UV light with peaks around 370–380 nm. Molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) absorbs light in the visible range, with peaks around 620–690 nm. Given that TiO2 is the thinnest layer beneath MoS2, and MoS2 is the thickest layer, it is likely that the largest responsivity would occur at wavelengths corresponding to the absorption peaks of MoS2, which fall within the visible range. When red light strikes the nanoheterostructure, electrons in MoS2 absorb energy, transitioning to higher energy levels. These excited electrons can then transfer to ZnO, enhancing its conductivity. This process leads to an increase in photocurrent, resulting in high responsivity.

Based on Figs. 4d and 4e, the estimated rise and recovery time were 0.33 and 0.35 s, respectively. However, this value was largely limited by the light switch on/off time and the large time step (0.2 s) we used. The actual rise and recovery times could be even shorter. When biased with 2 V, the photocurrent was around 500 times larger than that with 0 V bias. With the increase of bias voltage, the photocurrent was even larger. However, self-powered photodetectors generate their electrical power when exposed to light, which eliminates the need for an external power source for remote or portable applications where access to a continuous power supply is limited. They have the potential to serve as energy harvesters to generate electrical power for other devices. The self-powered nature of these detectors makes them suitable for wireless and autonomous systems operating independently for extended periods in applications like remote sensing or IoT devices. Below we focus on the characterization of the photodetector performance with zero-volt bias.

Response of the prototype to different intensities of red-light illuminations with zero bias was conducted by controlling the light intensity through the variation of the distance between the light source and the prototype. As indicated in Fig. 5a, with the increase of this distance D, the photocurrent decreased as expected. Figure 5b plots the change of photocurrent as a function of illumination density. The decrease of the light illumination intensity on the active layer leads to the decrease of the photocurrent. Correspondingly, the responsivity of the tested self-powered prototype was 16 mA/W.

a Response of the prototype following the variation of the distance D between the prototype and the red light source, and b the corresponding photocurrent as a function of red-light illumination density

Different load resistors can affect the power consumption of the photodetector, especially when the device operates in self-powered mode. By testing with various resistors, we can identify the optimal load resistor that provides the required performance while minimizing power consumption. Figure 6 shows the time, voltage, and current responses of the self-powered prototype to the same intensity of red-light illumination with different load resistances. As indicated in Figs. 6a and 6b, a larger load resistance gave a bigger photovoltage. However, the noise level was almost constant with different load resistances. Hence a large load resistance led to a higher signal-to-noise ratio. However, the photocurrent decreased with the increase of the load resistance as shown in Fig. 6c. Hence a maximum power output was reached when the load resistance matched to the internal resistance of the as-synthesized prototype.

Performance of the self-powered prototype to the same intensity of red light illumination with different load resistances: a Time response, b the output photovoltage, and c the output photocurrent

The present ternary material-based device consisted of highly doped n-type ZnO nanowires on top of an undoped 2D MoS2 nanosheets, and a highly doped p-type TiO2 layer on the bottom. Since nanostructured TiO2, MoS2, and ZnO were partially overlapped with one another, it formed porous-like structures. 3D architectures composed of 0D/1D/2D active materials resulted in sensors with several important advantages, including a large electron-depleted zone that improved the sensing performances. When light of an appropriate wavelength strikes the nanoheterostructure, electrons in MoS2 absorb energy, transitioning to higher energy levels. These excited electrons can then transfer to ZnO, enhancing its conductivity, which leads to an increase in photocurrent and high responsivity.

Surface states and defects also play crucial roles in this mechanism. ZnO's surface states can trap electrons, enhancing its absorption capacity, while defects and edges in MoS2 contribute to efficient charge separation. Surface states in TiO2 facilitate electron transfer. These combined effects enhance the absorption of red light. Additionally, the nanoheterostructure's 3D porous structure creates quantum dots and 1D quantum wells at interfaces, further enhancing charge separation and electron mobility. The synergistic effect of TiO2, MoS2, and ZnO creates an optimal environment for red light absorption. Overall, the high responsivity of this nanoheterostructure to red light arises from a combination of factors, including bandgap energies, surface states, defects, and quantum confinement. This tailored design enables efficient light absorption and electron transfer, making it an excellent red light photodetector.

Response to target gases with different concentrations

Transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) like MoS2 have gained attention in gas sensing applications due to their unique electronic properties and large surface area. Combining MoS2 with other materials, such as TiO2 and ZnO, in heterostructures can lead to enhanced sensor performance. The 2D TiO2 component contributes a high surface area, excellent electron mobility, and chemical stability, while MoS2 brings unique electronic properties and sensitivity to environmental changes, and the introduction of ZnO reduces both two key challenges in gas sensor technology which are long response and recovery times, as well as high operating temperature. The ternary nano-heterostructure exploits the complementary characteristics of the 2D materials, resulting in improved gas sensing capabilities, which are crucial for practical IoT applications.

Besides photoelectronic properties, the prototype was also investigated for its sensing performance toward two distinct target gases, CH4 and NO2, diluted with nitrogen gas to reach the required concentrations. The experimental setup included a chamber operating at standard atmospheric pressure, in which the prototype was placed inside. A consistent gas flow rate of 200–220 sccm to the chamber was maintained throughout the experiments. Regulation of the concentrations of target gases within the chamber was achieved by adjusting the ratio of target gas to nitrogen gas in the mixture.

Figure 7a shows the sensing responses of the prototype at room temperature toward NO2 gas at 10 ppm concentrations during cycling tests. When the as-synthesized prototype encountered NO2 test gas, an increase of conductivity was observed in the period when the target gas was on and then gradually turned to a steady state. After the cessation of the test gas, the sensing response steadily decreased to its initial state. The fluctuation in the response is directly attributed to the physisorption of external gas molecules due to weak bonds. Since experiments were conducted at room temperature, the response time is expected to be slightly longer than that when the device operates above room temperature. It was reported that the response time for most 2D material based NO2 gas sensors was quite long, in the range of 3–9 min when tested at room temperature [51, 52].

a The responses of the prototype at room temperature to 10 ppm NO2 gas, and b the relationship between the response strength and the NO2 gas concentrations at room temperature and 5 V bias

The variation in response times is influenced by numerous factors, including the formed morphology and sensor construction, encountered target gas type and concentration, operating temperature, biased voltage, and situational environmental conditions. Due to delays in achieving the desired concentrations upon activation or deactivation of the target gas source, it might be shorter for the actual response and recovery times. Here response strength is defined as ΔR/Ro = [Rs–Ro]/Ro, where Ro denotes the prototype's original resistance in air and Rs the resistance in the presence of the target gas. As shown in Fig. 7b, a lower concentration of NO2 gas at sub ppm level could be detected with the present prototype. As expected at a higher operating temperature the prototype would generate a stronger and faster response signal.

The identical prototype was also characterized using CH4 target gas at 20 ppm for comparison. In line with the same room temperature, the prototype demonstrated negligible response, in contrast to its response to NO2 gas. Even with an increase in concentration up to 90 ppm, the response strength remained relatively consistent, indicating the prototype’s possesses good selectivity.

In this case, the detection mechanism is credited to the transfer of electrons to the active layer from the target gas molecules, prompting a modification of the surface’s electrical properties. Extensive use has been made of density functional theory (DFT) calculations to analyze the gas sensitivity of NO2 molecules on MoS2 cluster surfaces [53]. This theoretical approach facilitates a thorough examination of electronic properties, adsorption configurations, energy band structures, density of states (DOS), total electron density (TED) analysis, and differential charge density (DCD) analysis. Moreover, molecular dynamics methods were employed to analyze the adsorption mechanism of gas molecules on the doped MoS2 surfaces. These molecular interactions with the cluster induce alterations in the electronic properties of the active layer, resulting in a reduction in the cluster's gap and consequent increase in its electrical conductivity.

In this study, the ternary nature of the nanoheterostructure, combining different semiconductor materials with complementary properties, enhances sensitivity to pollution gases by providing multiple pathways for charge transfer and adsorption. Effective control of the hybrid structure, ingredients, and number densities of nanomaterials to form porous-like 3D architectures composed of low-dimensional active materials are crucial for achieving desired electron-depleted zones that determine the performance of sensing devices operating at room temperature. When gas molecules adsorb onto the surface of nanostructured ZnO, they attract electrons from the material to form adsorbates. This alters the conductivity of the nanoheterostructure, resulting in a sensing signal. Band alignment at the interfaces between these materials facilitates charge transfer and influences the overall conductivity of the nanoheterostructure. Surface morphology, including defects, vacancies, and active sites, also plays a crucial role in gas adsorption and charge transfer processes.

UV light can further enhance gas sensor response by promoting molecular adsorption on the nanoheterostructure surface, facilitating bond breakdown, generating electron–hole pairs, and activating surface sites and defects to enhance reactivity towards target molecules in the sensing process. Since the sample’s surface is dominated by ZnO materials, it is therefore expected that the target gas molecules would be absorbed onto the surface of ZnO. When external gas molecules were absorbed on the surface of nanostructured zinc oxide and attracted electrons from the sensor form NO2− adsorbates. This changed the conductivity of the sensor and translated into sensing signals. Similarly, oxygen (O2) would also adsorb on the sensor’s surface and form adsorbates. Therefore, the external gas molecules would be oxidized by oxygen adsorbates, releasing the electrons back to the sensor and changing its conductivity. Different gas concentrations and operating temperatures directly affect the sensor’s response.

The p–n junction structure further enhances the sensitivity in this device. ZnO NWs was a n-type, whereas TiO2 was a p-type semiconductor and MoS2 was nearly neutral. Because of 3D porous structures, a large amount of quantum dots and 1D quantum wells were formed at the interfaces. When adhered to the surfaces of nanostructured ZnO, electrons transfer from the sensor surface to the external gas adsorbates. This process prompts additional electrons to move from MoS2 to ZnO, thereby increasing the conductivity of MoS2. Such a structure facilitates high mobility of electron transport and enhances gas sensitivity electronically.

Conclusion

Although the synthesis of ternary TiO2/MoS2/ZnO hetero-nanostructure reported in this paper was simple and cost-effective, the newly as-fabricated multiple functional sensing prototype worked as a self-powered photodetector of fast response time, high photocurrent, good baseline stability, very broadband spectral response, and repeatability. The prototype responded to the visible spectrum, with a maximum responsivity to red-light in photovoltaic mode up 16 mA/W and a response and recovery time in the order of 0.3 s.

Meanwhile, the same prototype demonstrated its gas sensing capability to NO2 gas down to 1 ppm at room temperature with the advantage of being high in selectivity. The gas sensing mechanisms are possibly related to the elemental composition and carrier concentrations of the ternary hetero-nanostructure, the formation of p-n junctions and quantum dots and wells at the interfaces, the porous-like 3D architectures, the conductivity changes caused by the gas adsorption, the role of surface morphology such as defects, vacancies, and active sites, and the promoting effect of UV light.

In addition, through the synergistic effects, TiO2/MoS2/ZnO hetero-nanostructure can also be used to develop wireless and multiple functional remote sensors such as gas sensors, biosensors, chemical sensors, and environmental monitoring used for IoT applications. Furthermore, these materials are compatible with existing semiconductor fabrication technologies, making the ternary hetero-nanostructure feasible to integrate into flexible, lightweight, and multifunctional semiconductor devices and circuits. Despite these promising attributes, the investigation of the crystal structure, the optimization of the heterostructure design, the understanding of the underlying multiple functional sensing mechanisms, and the practical challenges to further advance the capabilities of multiple functionalities need to be further investigated.
