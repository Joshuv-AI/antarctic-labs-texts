A characterized promoter suite enables metabolic rewiring of the acetogen Acetobacterium woodii toward ethanol production
Authors: Renaud Eynard, Sephora Justine, Rémi Hocq, Stefan Pflügl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcetogenic bacteria are promising platforms for converting gaseous and liquid one-carbon (C1) substrates such as carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide (CO2), formate and methanol into fuels and chemicals, but their application is limited by sparse genetic tools and poor control over metabolic fluxes. Here, we developed a modular plasmid assembly platform and a suite of characterized native, heterologous and methanol-inducible promoters for the model acetogen Acetobacterium woodii, enabling rapid and tunable control of gene expression together with CRISPR-based genome editing. Using an anaerobe-compatible fluorescent reporter (pFAST), promoter strength was quantified, from medium to high expression levels. Subsequently, a minimal promoter replacement at the native adhE (bifunctional aldehyde-alcohol dehydrogenase) locus along with deletion of the neighboring LysR-type regulator was done employing the newly characterized promoters, demonstrating that a single promoter replacement in the genome was sufficient to shift A. woodii from a primarily acetogenic toward a partly ethanologenic phenotype. The engineered strain was able to produce ethanol not only from fructose but also from the C1 substrates methanol + CO2 and formate + syngas. Together with the promoter replacement strategy, these findings underline that ethanol formation in A. woodii is constrained both by energy conservation and by endogenous transcriptional control. Relieving energetic bottlenecks through the choice of energetically favorable C1 substrates and decoupling adhE expression from its native promoter suffices to enable ethanol production from methanol + CO2 and formate + syngas, directly linking the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway to ethanol formation. These results illustrate how small, targeted genome edits can significantly reshape carbon flux in acetogens and positions A. woodii as a viable chassis for C1-based bioproduction.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13068-026-02790-2.Keywords: Acetogens, Methanol, Formate, Syngas, Fluorescent reporter, CRISPR, Metabolic engineering

Introduction

The transition toward a circular bioeconomy has accelerated the search for robust microbial platforms that can convert gaseous and liquid one-carbon (C1) substrates into value-added chemicals and fuels [2, 56, 71]. Among these, acetogens have emerged as particularly promising biocatalysts because they channel a wide range of C1 substrates through the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway (WLP), enabling direct conversion of gases such as CO, CO2 and formate into acetyl-CoA derived fuels and chemicals [37, 60]. This pathway is one of the seven currently known natural carbon dioxide (CO2) fixation pathways [26, 64] and is widely considered the most energy-efficient, operating at the thermodynamic limit of life [51, 60, 66]. This ancient metabolic route enables acetogens to fix CO2 and reduce it to acetyl-CoA, a central precursor for acetate and other potential products, such as ethanol and butanol. While generally associated with gaseous substrates like H2 and CO from industrial flue streams [40, 44], acetogens exhibit remarkable metabolic flexibility, also utilizing liquid C1 feedstocks such as formate and methanol [59].

Among acetogens, Acetobacterium woodii has emerged as a model organism for studying acetogenesis and energy conservation [66]. A. woodii is metabolically versatile and utilizes a broad range of substrates including formate [48, 50, 52], methanol [41, 45], H2/CO2 [12], CO [8, 50], syngas [54], sugars [30] and ethanol [13] to produce primarily acetate, rendering it a potential robust platform for C1 bioconversion. The genetic toolkit for this acetogen has expanded rapidly, including robust electroporation protocols, plasmid-based expression systems and genome editing methods [4, 35, 61, 69, 70, 73]. Notably, A. woodii possesses a functional endogenous type I-B CRISPR–Cas system that has been repurposed for efficient, scarless genome engineering [61]. Biochemical methods and genetic tools have been used to elucidate the function of key enzyme complexes, including the essential role of the Rnf complex and the electron-bifurcating hydrogenase HydABCD in coupling the WLP to energy conservation [73, 74]. Furthermore, these tools have also been successfully used to engineer the production of industrially relevant chemicals such as acetone [1, 35], isopropanol [1, 34], lactate [47] and butyrate [20], demonstrating the significant potential of this strain as a robust biocatalyst for sustainable production of chemicals.

Some acetogens such as Clostridium ljungdahlii already benefit from well-established constitutive and inducible promoters (pTet, pBgal or pCholine1) that facilitate predictable pathway expression [5, 36, 76, 77]. In contrast, despite recent progress, metabolic engineering of A. woodii has only begun to benefit from a systematically characterized set of promoters covering different expression levels and regulatory behaviors. Key examples include pThlACac (derived from the Clostridium acetobutylicum thiolase gene) and pPta-AckClj, one of the few characterized promoters using a β-glucuronidase reporter activity assay [6], originating from the C. ljungdahlii phosphotransacetylase/acetate kinase operon. Furthermore, several inducible promoters have also been characterized using β-glucuronidase assays in the strain [6] such as the IPTG/lactose-inducible promoter pFac, a lactose-inducible promoter pBgaL, the tetracycline-inducible promoter pTet and a lactate inducible promoter pLctA [68]. This promoter palette already provides an important foundation for genetic control in A. woodii and has enabled the first generation of metabolic engineering studies in this organism. Building on this groundwork, the development of a broader, well-validated promoter suite with graded activities will further simplify fine-tuning of gene expression to facilitate balanced pathway fluxes, thus promoting the construction of high-performing A. woodii strains for sustainable biomanufacturing.

To address this gap in promoter availability for A. woodii, we developed, characterized and exploited a promoter suite. This suite includes native A. woodii promoters which were identified using previously obtained transcriptomic datasets [52], a methanol-inducible promoter (pMeOH_1) and heterologous promoters (pPta-AckClj and pThlACac) previously used in A. woodii [6, 18, 20, 35]. The promoters were characterized using pFAST, a fluorescent reporter protein compatible with anaerobes [7, 15]. Following promoter characterization, we constructed ethanol-producing strains of A. woodii by deleting the LysR‑type regulator located immediately upstream of the bifunctional aldehyde-alcohol dehydrogenase adhE locus and simultaneously replacing the native adhE promoter with either a strong constitutive or a methanol‑inducible promoter. The ethanol production capacity of the engineered A. woodii strains was successfully demonstrated from sugar as well as C1 substrates.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

All strains used in this study are described in Additional File 1 Table S1.

E. coli DH10B was routinely cultivated in LB medium (10 g L−1 tryptone, 10 g L−1 NaCl, 5 g L−1 yeast extract) at 37 °C with carbenicillin (100 mg L−1), kanamycin (50 mg L−1), chloramphenicol (20 mg L−1) or erythromycin (400 mg L−1) as needed. For solid media, 15 g L−1 agar was added.

AWO_CDM, an adapted A. woodii strain that is routinely used as the working strain in our laboratory due to its robust and reproducible growth on chemically defined media with C1 substrates was obtained from a grown DSM 1030 culture in complex carbonate-buffered medium (CY) supplemented with fructose. Cells were sequentially transferred twice in complex phosphate-buffered medium (PY) supplemented with carbon monoxide and formate, then four times in chemically defined phosphate-buffered medium (PY) without yeast extract. The culture exhibiting the highest OD600 and growth rate was transformed with the backbone B007 and subsequently cured.

A. woodii was handled anaerobically in a vinyl anaerobic chamber (Coy lab, Grass Lake, MI, USA) or in a Concept 500 anaerobic workstation (Baker Ruskinn, Sanford, ME, USA). Strains of A. woodii were cultivated at 30 °C in media containing per liter: 2.0 g yeast extract, 0.5 g cysteine-HCl x H2O, 2 mL of trace elements, 10 mL vitamin solution (DSMZ 141) (added after sterilization), other components depending on the medium (see below) and carbon sources as indicated for each experiment. Three distinct media variants were used in this study. The complex carbonate-buffered medium (CY) contains per liter: 10.0 g NaHCO3, 0.45 g K2HPO4, 0.33 g KH2PO4, 1 g NH4Cl, 0.16 g MgSO4 × 7 H2O, 0.002 g CaCl2 × 2 H2O, 2 mL trace element solution and 1.0 mg FeSO4 × 7 H2O. The complex phosphate medium (PY) contains 3.5 g NaCl, 8.44 g K2HPO4, 1.76 g KH2PO4, 1 g NH4Cl, 0.16 g MgSO4 × 7 H2O, 0.002 g CaCl2 × 2 H2O, 2 mL trace element solution and 1.0 mg FeSO4 × 7 H2O. Na+-depleted complex phosphate-buffered medium (PY-Na+) is identical to PY except that NaCl was replaced by 12.01 g KHCO3. The trace elements solution (based on DSMZ 141) contained per liter: 15.0 g nitrilotriacetic acid, 5.0 g MnSO4 x H2O, 1.52 g CoCl2 × 6 H2O, 1.8 g ZnSO4 × 7 H2O, 0.1 g CuSO4 × 5 H2O, 0.1 g H3BO3, 0.1 g Na2MoO4 × 2 H2O, 0.3 g NiCl2 × 6 H2O, 0.0003 g Na2SeO3 × 5 H2O and 0.0004 g Na2WO4 × 2 H2O. The vitamin solution contained per liter: 2 mg biotin, 2 mg folic acid, 10 mg pyridoxine–HCl, 5 mg thiamine-HCl, 5 mg riboflavin, 5 mg nicotinic acid, 5 mg D-Ca-pantothenate, 0.1 mg vitamin B12, 5 mg p-aminobenzoic acid and 5 mg DL-α-lipoic acid.

Plates were made using complex phosphate medium (PY) supplemented with 15 g L−1 Kobe agar (Carl Roth, Karlsruhe, Germany). Thiamphenicol (20 µg mL−1) or erythromycin (50 µg mL−1) was added when required.

12-mL Hungate tubes, 125-mL serum bottles or 24-well plates were used, respectively, for promoter characterization, A. woodii cultivation and colony screening after transformation. Following each inoculation, unless otherwise stated, cultivated flasks were flushed and pressurized to 1 bar overpressure with a gas mixture containing 20% CO2 and 80% N2. Cultures were incubated at 30 °C on a rotary shaker at 100 rpm.

For the cultivation of A. woodii for ethanol production, batch fermentations were conducted in 125 mL anaerobic serum bottles containing 50 mL (20 mL for syngas and CO experiments) of the appropriate medium, with gas phases as described in the text. For all methanol cultivations, the headspace was flushed and pressurized to 1 bar with N2/CO2 (80/20), as methanol utilization in A. woodii has previously been shown to be strictly dependent on the presence of CO2 [41]. Cultures were incubated for up to 13 days. At each sampling point, growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) and HPLC was used to quantify the different substrates consumption (except gases) and the production of metabolites to evaluate metabolic flux redirection. Overpressure was monitored with a manometer.

For strains involving characterization of promoters, OD600 and fluorescence were measured to quantify induction levels. Samples were additionally harvested at all time points to monitor substrate consumption during the methanol-inducible promoter characterization (fructose, formate, ethanol and methanol).

In silico identification of A. woodii promoters

Native promoters of A. woodii were selected and designed based on genome-scale transcriptomic datasets for this strain [52, 67]. In particular, transcription start site (TSS) positions were taken from the TSS mapping reported by [67], which we used to delineate the promoter regions analyzed in this study. Native promoters were selected to cover a wide range of expression, from medium to high expression level on fructose (between ~700 TPM and ~9000 TPM).

After identifying the TSSs, the primers used to amplify the different promoters were designed to amplify the upstream region of each gene including the TSS, the -35 and -10 recognition signal and an additional 50 bp upstream of the -35 signal. Identification of the -35 and -10 signals was done using the σ70 consensus sequence established in Clostridia [33] and performed in silico using CLC Workbench (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

Based on the information related to the methanol operon of A. woodii [41], we identified several -35 and -10 signals for the promoter regulating this operon. Therefore, the entire sequence (430 bp) upstream of the first gene of the methanol operon (Awo_c22820) until the end of the CDS (Awo_c22810) upstream of the gene and the entire sequence (93 bp) localized between two CDS (Awo_c22780, Awo_c22770) were amplified by PCR and cloned in a level 1 plasmid. Throughout this study, these two promoter regions are referred to as pMeOH_1 and pMeOH_2, respectively.

Plasmid construction with Golden Gate assembly

Plasmids and DNA parts used in this study are described in Additional File 1 Table S1. A list of all the primers used and their sequences are available in Additional File 1 Table S2. All plasmids were constructed using Golden Gate assembly.

Each part was amplified by conducting PCRs on the A. woodii genome (B7 High Fidelity polymerase, Biozym, Hessisch Oldendorf, Germany) or synthesized (Twist Biosciences, San Francisco, CA, USA) and cloned in a backbone plasmid with a compatible restriction enzyme (BbsI/BsaI/Esp3I) and appropriate fusion sites (Additional File 1 Table S3). Promoters, coding sequences and terminators were cloned individually in level 1 backbone (BB1) using BsaI assembly using unique fusion sites 1–2, 2–3, and 3–4, respectively.

For the assembly of the plasmids used for the promoters characterization, different transcription units expressing an A. woodii codon-optimized pFAST gene [7] were assembled with the different promoters and a BBa_B1001 synthetic terminator using a BbsI assembly into a level 2 backbone plasmid (BB2_A_1_4_B). Finally, these cassettes were cloned in a level 3 backbone plasmid (B007) with the help of two connectors (BB2_BC and BB2_CD) using a BsaI assembly to assemble an A. woodii expression plasmid. The E. coli–A. woodii shuttle vector B007 was obtained by modifying pMTL83241 [29] through insertion of an annealed primer pair into the BamHI and NcoI sites in order to add two BsaI recognition site with the recipient sites AD, thereby maintaining the lacZ reading frame and enabling blue/white screening.

For the knock-in and knock-out experiments conducted with the endogenous Type I-B CRISPR/Cas system of A. woodii, CRISPR plasmids were assembled according to previously established guidelines [61]. PAM sequences (35 bp downstream of a CCA motif) for the different targets were identified using CLC Workbench (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Spacer sequences were generated by annealing two primers carrying the first and last four nucleotides (AAAT and ATTT) of A. woodii endogenous CRISPSR system type I-B repeat units on their overhangs, resulting in sequences identical to the selected protospacers. These spacers were inserted into a vector expressing the A. woodii CRISPR/Cas system using an Esp3I assembly, which contains the A. woodii CRISPR leader sequence and direct repeat region required for crRNA generation. The resulting plasmids were designated BB2_leader1 to BB2_leader6. Final CRISPR plasmids were assembled by BsaI Golden Gate assembly using the BB2_leader plasmids (linker A–B), the repair DNA template consisting of a left homology arm (PCR fragment, linker B–C) and a right homology arm (PCR fragment, linker C–D for knock-out and linker D–E for knock-in) and where applicable, the knock-in fragment (PCR fragment, linker C–D) positioned between the homology arms. For the adhE promoter replacement and lysR knock-out, PCR fragments were used for assembly because a BsaI site was present within the right homology arm. This site was removed by introducing a single base substitution through custom primer overhangs (site X), while preserving the native amino acid sequence. The use of PCR fragments also eliminated an additional cloning step, enabling faster construction of the level 3 plasmids. The E. coli–A. woodii shuttle vector used for the level 3 plasmids assembly, B040 (linker A–D) and B041 (linker A–E), were constructed by combining several parts by BbsI Golden Gate assembly: a BsaI-compatible A–D/A–E linker [65], the pCD6 Gram-positive replicon from pMTL84422 [29], the thermostable chloramphenicol resistance marker catP (synthesized) and a ColE1 Gram-negative origin of replication [65].

A. woodii transformation

Fifty mL of competent cells were prepared for each transformation with a culture of A. woodii with an OD600 between 0.2 and 0.4 (ONDA V-10 Plus, Giorgio Bormac, Carpi, Italy). After rapidly cooling the culture on ice, the cells were centrifuged at 4 °C at 5000g for 8 min. Supernatant was discarded, cell pellets were washed with 50 mL of ice-cold SMP buffer (pH 5.8, 270 mM sucrose, 1 mM sodium phosphate, 1 mM MgCl2 × 6 H2O) and centrifuged again in the same conditions. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended in ice-cold SMP buffer at a volume of 200 µL per planned electroporation (i.e., 200 µL × number of transformations for each strain transformed). Aliquots of 200 µL of these competent cells were mixed with 4 µg plasmid DNA in 2-mm cuvettes, the cells were shocked (1.0 kV, 200 Ω, 50 µF) using a Bio-Rad Gene Pulser Xcell electroporator (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and were instantly transferred to 10 mL of complex carbonate medium without carbon source or antibiotic in 12-mL glass Hungate tubes. Hungate tubes were flushed and pressurized at 1 bar with 80% N2 and 20% CO2 and incubated at 30 °C on a rotary shaker at 100 rpm. After 6 h of regeneration, the cells were centrifuged at room temperature (5000g, 2 min) and resuspended in 100 µL of complex carbonate medium. 20 µL and 50 µL of cells were then spread on agar plates with the corresponding antibiotic. These plates were then incubated at 30 °C in a 2.5 L anaerobic jar (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK).

Colonies appeared on the plates usually after five (erythromycin/pCB102) or 12 (Thiamphenicol/pCD6) days. These colonies are first screened by re-inoculating them in 1 mL complex carbonate medium without antibiotic for the CRISPR plasmids or with erythromycin for the pFAST overexpression plasmids and were supplemented with fructose and vitamins in a 24-well plate (CytoOne, CC7672-7524, Starlab International GmbH, Hamburg, Germany). Well-plates were incubated in a custom-made anaerobic metal jar pressurized to 1 bar overpressure with 80% N2 and 20% CO2 and incubated at 30 °C. After two days, PCR was performed on cell lysates to verify plasmid presence and to screen for the desired genomic deletion or insertion. 1 µL of cell lysate was used as PCR template. Cell lysates were prepared by mixing 100 µL of cells with the same volume of 10 mM TE buffer (pH 8) and boiled at 100 °C for 20 min. Upon successful validation, 10 µL of culture were streaked another time on agar plates and incubated at 30 °C to cure the cells of residual plasmids. After colony formation, the strains were genotyped again and then validated by Sanger sequencing (Microsynth AG, Balgach, Switzerland) and stored in 10% DMSO (v/v) at − 80 °C.

Analytical methods

Samples for HPLC were centrifuged (16,000g, 15 min, 4 °C) to remove biomass. The supernatant was acidified with 40 mM H2SO4 at a 9:1 ratio to match the mobile phase conditions for accurate and reproducible quantification. Metabolites were separated using an Aminex HPX-87H column (300 × 7.8 mm; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) maintained at 60 °C, with an isocratic flow of 0.6 mL min−1 using 4 mM H2SO4 as the mobile phase. Compounds were detected using a Refractomax 520 refractive index detector (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) mounted on a Vanquish Core Quaternary HPLC system. Analyte concentrations were quantified using external standards (fructose, formate, methanol, acetate, ethanol, pyruvate, and lactate) and data were processed using Chromeleon software (version 7.3.2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Fluorescence of pFAST was measured as described previously [31]. Briefly, 5 OD units of cells were harvested in late exponential phase (starting from OD600 of 0.8), unless otherwise stated. The cells were centrifuged at room temperature (5000g, 2 min), the supernatant was discarded and each pellet were resuspended in 100 µL 70 mM Na+ phosphate pH 7.4 and 5 μΜ HMBR (hydroxymethyl bilane-related bilin chromophore) (Twinkle Factory, Paris, France) and placed in a 96-well plate (ref: 655209, Greiner, Kremsmünster, Austria). Fluorescence measurements were conducted with Tecan (Infinite M200, Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) as described before. Results were normalized using the fluorescence value obtained from cells harboring the empty BB3 backbone used for the pFAST plasmids assembly.

Carbon and electron balances were calculated using the number of carbon atoms and the available electrons for substrates and products: fructose (C6H12O6, 6 carbon atoms, 24 available electrons), methanol (CH3OH, 1 carbon atom, 6 available electrons), formate (CHOO−, 1 carbon atom, 2 available electrons), acetate (C2H4O2, 2 carbon atoms, 8 available electrons), ethanol (C2H6O, 2 carbon atoms, 12 available electrons), pyruvate (C3H4O3, 3 carbon atoms, 10 available electrons), lactate (C3H6O3, 3 carbon atoms, 12 available electrons) and carbon dioxide (CO2, 1 carbon atom, 0 available electrons). Biomass formation was included in the carbon and electron balances by converting OD600 values into biomass concentrations using a previously established correlation of 0.38 g dry cell weight per liter per unit of OD600 [52], an assumed carbon content of 45% (w/w) for A. woodii biomass [27] and a degree of reduction of 4.15 mol electrons per mol of biomass carbon [62]. CO2 production was taken into account using the expected stoichiometry for product formation. In detail, when fructose served as substrate equimolar formation of CO2 was assumed for each mol of acetate or ethanol formed. For methanol + CO2, 2/3 mol CO2 was considered per mol acetate formed and no CO2 production for ethanol. For formate, 2 mol CO2 was assumed per formate consumed. Yeast extract was neglected in the calculation of the carbon and electron balances. Carbon balances were calculated as: Carbon balance [%] = ((∑i​(Ci​*ni​)) + biomass)/(Csubstrate​*nsubstrate,consumed​) × 100, where Ci​ is the number of carbon atoms in product, Ci​⋅ni is the number of moles of product i, Csubstrate is the number of carbon atoms in the organic substrate and nsubstrate,consumed is the number of moles of substrate consumed.

Electron balances were reported as the ratio of total electrons recovered in products (reduced and oxidized) to electrons supplied by the consumed substrate. Electron balances were calculated as: Electron balance [%] = ​((∑i​(vi​​*ni​)) + biomass)/(vsubstrate​​*nsubstrate,consumed​)*100, where vi is the number of available electrons per mole of product I (from its degree of reduction), ni is the number of moles of product i, vsubstrate is the number of available electrons per mole of substrate and nsubstrate,consumed is the number of moles of substrate consumed.

Results

A modular Golden Gate assembly system for A. woodii

Golden Gate cloning enables efficient and modular assembly of multiple DNA fragments by exploiting Types IIS enzymes and T4 DNA ligase in a single reaction [23]. For this reason, we developed a plasmid library designed and based on the GoldenMOCS (Golden Gate derived Multiple Organism Cloning System) [65]. This system consists of three assembly levels (level 1 = BB1, level 2 = BB2, level 3 = BB3) (Fig. 1) and uses the type IIS enzymes BsaI (for assembly of BB1 and BB3 plasmids) and BbsI (for BB2 plasmids). BB1 plasmids are entry vectors for individual genetic parts (promoters, coding sequences, terminators) with unique BsaI restriction sites. Assembly into BB2 vectors yields functional expression cassettes or may be used to clone different CRISPR components (e.g., homology arms, gRNA expression cassettes). The final BB3 vectors integrate multiple expression cassettes into final E. coli/A. woodii shuttle plasmids, enabling the generation of complex constructs such as metabolic pathways for overexpression or CRISPR-based genome editing plasmids. To integrate the established endogenous Type I-B CRISPR/Cas system of A. woodii [61] in our cloning workflow, we generated a BB2 plasmid containing the CRISPR leader sequence followed by two repeat units separated by two Esp3I recognition sites, allowing for efficient spacer insertion to assemble a functional CRISPR array.

Scheme of the multi-level Golden Gate system for assembly of A. woodii. Metabolic pathways expression plasmids or CRISPR-based genome editing plasmids. Level 1: individual genetic parts, including promoters (green), coding sequences (yellow), terminators (red), are assembled into vectors using fusion sites (e.g., 1-2, 2-3, 3-4) (BB1). Level 2: assembly of functional expression cassettes, combining promoter, coding sequence (CDS) and terminator, or CRISPR components into vectors (BB2) containing specific linker sequences (from A–B to E–F) for a modular final assembly of up to five units. Level 3: multiple expression cassettes, or CRISPR components are assembled into final E. coli/A. woodii shuttle plasmids (BB3) for pathway overexpression or CRISPR-based genome editing. Details on plasmid components and overhang sequences can be found in Additional File 1 Table S1 and Table S3, respectively

For transformation of A. woodii, two different BB3 plasmids were developed by adapting the pMTL80000 modular shuttle vectors [29], each tailored for distinct applications. For gene overexpression, we generated a BB3 suite with the ermB erythromycin resistance gene [35] and the pCB102 Gram-positive replication origin, which is highly stable in A. woodii [4]. For genome-editing applications, a second vector set was constructed carrying a thiamphenicol resistance marker catP [19] and the unstable pCD6 origin [4]. Unstable origins of replications can be exploited in systems requiring transient replication, such as CRISPR-mediated genome editing, as plasmid curing is typically required after each round of modification.

Expanding A. woodii promoter toolbox

To expand the promoter toolbox for A. woodii, we characterized six native promoters and two heterologous promoters (pPta-AckClj from C. ljungdahlii and pThlACac from C. acetobutylicum) [6, 20, 35], using our hierarchical Golden Gate assembly system to overexpress pFAST (Fig. 2). The native promoters (pCooS, pAcsE, pAcsD, pFhs, pFdhF2 and pRS05465; respective locus tag: Awo_c10740, Awo_c10730, Awo_c10710, Awo_c09260, Awo_c08210 and Awo_c10770) were selected based on RNA-seq data [52] and transcription start site (TSS) analysis [67]. This selection was made to cover a broad range of expression levels, from medium to high expression levels (expressed in transcripts per million, TPM) on fructose (~ 700 TPM/pFdhF2 to ~ 9,000 TPM/pAcsE), formate (~ 1000 TPM /pFdhF2 to ~ 20,000 TPM /pAcsE) and syngas (~ 600 TPM /pFdhF2 to ~ 30,000 TPM /pAcsE). These promoters (sequences are listed in Additional File 1 Table S4) were then cloned into BB1 vectors and subsequently assembled in BB2 plasmids with the pFAST coding sequence and the BBa_B1001 synthetic terminator (iGEM), frequently used for metabolic engineering of E. coli [24]. These cassettes were assembled in a BB3 E. coli/A. woodii shuttle plasmid. Subsequently, these shuttle plasmids were used to transform an A. woodii strain adapted to grow robustly on C1 substrates in chemically defined medium (referred to hereafter as AWO_CDM). Upon successful strain generation, strains carrying all promoters from the suite described above were grown on fructose and fluorescence was measured in triplicates at three different time points (24, 48 and 72 h).

Out of the eight promoters tested, six promoters (pFhs, pFdhF2, pAcsD, pRS05465, pPta-AckClj and pThlACac) showed fluorescence signals with different strengths while the remaining two (pAcsE and pCooS) did not produce detectable fluorescence signals beyond the background level of the empty vector control. Generally, fluorescence signals appeared after 24 h and reached their maximum at 48 or 72 h. The highest fluorescence was recorded for the promoter pRS05465 (Awo_c10770), expressing a putative zinc transporter in the same operon as the monofunctional carbon monoxide dehydrogenase gene (cooS) of the WLP, with an average 71-fold increase in fluorescence relative to the empty vector control at 72 h. The next highest fluorescence signals were observed for pPta-AckClj, pFhs and pFdhF2 with fold changes of 36, 28 and 32, respectively. pAcsD drove a moderate response with a fold change of 28 and pThlACac emitted the smallest fluorescence with an average fold change of 5. These variations in fluorescence were corroborated by transcriptomics data from fructose-grown A. woodii [52] which showed similar expression variations, except for pAcsD. Specifically, the high fluorescence intensity observed for pRS05465 aligns with the significant upregulation of the Awo_c10770 gene (~ 1,500 TPM) compared to Awo_c09260 (pFhs, ~ 760 TPM) and Awo_c08210 (pFdhF2, ~ 760 TPM) during growth on fructose, which ranks among the most highly expressed gene clusters. The differences in fluorescence signal strength between pRS05465 and pFhs, pFdhF2 reflect the relative mRNA abundance of their respective genes [52], suggesting that the reporter system accurately captures the transcriptional output of the different promoters. These correlations can be found in Additional File 1 Table S5.

A methanol-inducible promoter for A. woodii

Reliable inducible promoter systems for A. woodii are desirable for applications such as controlling the expression levels of individual pathway genes. The methanol-dependent operon in A. woodii (locus tag: Awo_c22810 to Awo_22740), which encodes the methanol-specific methyltransferase system and is strongly induced during growth on methanol, has previously been described [41], making it a prime candidate for characterizing an inducible promoter system responsive to a highly relevant carbon and energy source. Kremp et al. demonstrated the sub-operonic structure of the methanol operon mta, with two putative TSSs within this locus: one (pMeOH_1) located upstream of mtaR (Awo_c22810), a gene encoding a protein containing a PocR-type ligand-binding domain-containing protein, belonging to the AraC/PocR family of transcriptional regulators [11, 17, 19] and one upstream of Awo_c22770 (pMeOH_2), which encodes a predicted B12-binding protein. Transcriptional data suggested differing strengths for these sites [41], ~3000 TPM for pMeOH_1 and 20,000 TPM for pMeOH_2. Therefore, we cloned both promoter regions into a pFAST plasmid to validate their activity. Reporter assays revealed that pMeOH_1 exhibited measurable fluorescence upon induction, whereas pMeOH_2 failed to drive detectable expression.

We subsequently assessed the effect of inducer concentration on pMeOH_1 activity by cultivating the pMeOH_1-pFAST strain in medium containing increasing methanol concentrations (0, 5, 10, 30, 60, 120 and 240 mM) (Fig. 3). Fluorescence intensity increased with increasing methanol concentration up to 120 mM, from 0 to an average fold change of 76. For 240 mM, the fluorescence signal sharply declined along with the OD600, suggesting toxicity at higher methanol concentrations. While 120 mM methanol yielded higher fluorescence (fold change 75.8 ± 6.7) and cell densities (OD600 1.15 ± 0.05) compared to 60 mM (fold change 50.0 ± 2.2; OD600 0.91 ± 0.14), we selected 60 mM as the working concentration for subsequent experiments with methanol to avoid any possible growth inhibition.

A. woodii is known for its metabolic versatility and ability to co-utilize various substrates [52]. However, little is known about the ability of A. woodii to co-utilize methanol together with other substrates. To evaluate the specificity and dose-dependent activation of pMeOH_1 and to determine if methanol acts as an inducer in the presence of alternative carbon sources, we performed a substrate co-utilization analysis (Fig. 4). Growth was observed for all conditions tested but with distinct utilization patterns. Methanol was not consumed until complete depletion of fructose and formate, whereas it was co-consumed when ethanol was used as additional substrate. This consumption pattern is also reflected in the fluorescence signals observed. On methanol only, fluorescence was observed without delay and reached a fold change of around 63 after 3 days of cultivation, whereas only marginal fluorescence was observed for fructose, formate and ethanol in the absence of methanol. In contrast, when methanol was additionally provided, fluorescence immediately appeared when cells were co-fed with ethanol, reaching a maximum average fold change of 60 after 6 days of cultivation. In case of formate, only a negligible amount of methanol was consumed (5.4 ± 1.6 mM), leading to low fluorescence with a maximum average fold change of 5 after 6 days of incubation. For fructose + methanol grown cells, fluorescence was only observed after complete depletion of fructose and reached an average fold change of 29 after 13 days.

Together, these results demonstrate that the methanol-inducible promoter is specifically activated by methanol, with the magnitude of induction depending on the presence of certain co-substrates. While the presence of ethanol as a co-substrate allows induction, the co-substrates fructose and formate suppressed expression of pFAST. The induction was dose-dependent with respect to methanol, with maximal promoter activity at 120 mM methanol (Fig. 3). Furthermore, the analysis showed metabolic repression in A. woodii. Methanol was used by the cells only after fructose was fully consumed, co-consumed when ethanol was the second substrate and was not significantly consumed when co-fed with formate.

Construction of ethanol-producing A. woodii strains

The genome of A. woodii contains eleven alcohol dehydrogenases, including a gene coding for the bifunctional aldehyde and alcohol dehydrogenase AdhE (Awo_c06310), which is critical for growth of A. woodii on ethanol [10] but is involved in ethanol synthesis in other acetogens and bacteria in general [9, 25, 39, 58]. However, expression of adhE is typically low when A. woodii grows on C1 substrates such as formate (~22 TPM) or H2/CO2 (~2 TPM) but also on sugars such as fructose (~100 TPM) because acetate is the dominant metabolic product under these conditions [45, 49, 52]. Leveraging the newly characterized promoters, we used the CRISPR-based genome editing method from Poulalier-Delavelle et al. to precisely replace the native promoter of the adhE gene with a strong constitutive (pRS05465) and an inducible promoter (pMeOH_1) to construct ethanol-producing A. woodii strains (Fig. 5). On the genome of A. woodii, there is a gene coding for a LysR-type transcriptional regulator (LTTR), a widespread family of bacterial transcription factors involved in metabolism and stress response [22], ending close to adhE (separated by only 499 bp). As this regulator might potentially be involved in the regulation of adhE expression, we knocked out its coding sequence in all the ethanol-producing strains.

Under low carbonate and Na+-depleted conditions, the five A. woodii strains exhibited distinct metabolic profiles during fructose fermentation. AWO_CDM∆lysR:pRS05465 was the only strain which completely consumed the fructose supplemented (64.6 ± 2.8 mM), whereas for AWO_WT, AWO_CDM, AWO_CDM∆lysR:pMeOH_1 and AWO_CDM∆lysR fructose consumption was 85, 72, 86, 71%, respectively. Biomass formation was comparable for AWO_CDM, AWO_CDM∆lysR and AWO_CDM∆lysR:pMeOH_1 (YX/Fru = 13.4 to 13.7 g mol−1) but was significantly lower for AWO_WT and AWO_CDM∆lysR:pRS05465, for which the biomass yield was only 38 and 54%, respectively, compared to the other strains. Metabolite production revealed distinct strain-specific profiles. For AWO_WT, acetate production dominated (90.9 ± 2.4 mM), yielding 1.71 ± 0.07 molAcemolFru−1. Additionally, small amounts of ethanol were produced (YEtOH/Fru = 0.09 ± 0.01 mol mol−1). In contrast, AWO_CDM∆lysR:pRS05465 reached the highest ethanol yield (0.87 ± 0.01 mol mol−1), which is 9.7, 3.3, 3.8 and 2.5 times higher than that of AWO_WT, AWO_CDM, AWO_CDM∆lysR and AWO_CDM∆lysR:pMeOH_1, respectively. For AWO_CDM∆lysR:pRS05465, a reversed metabolic profile was observed in which more ethanol (56.5 ± 2.1 mM) than acetate (28.2 ± 1.7 mM) was produced, corresponding to an EtOH:Ace ratio of 2. This result was unexpected, as testing of pMeOH_1 with pFAST showed it to be strictly methanol-inducible (Fig. 4). This observation suggests that one of the other, endogenous alcohol dehydrogenases of A. woodii (e.g., Adh4 and Adh6) might be upregulated under Na+-depleted conditions and thereby contribute to ethanol formation, as previously demonstrated with an ∆adh4 A. woodii mutant [49]. Additionally, pyruvate and lactate were minor products for all strains (≤ 3.3 mM). Balancing of carbon and electrons showed relatively high recovery for all strains, with the notable exception of AWO_CDM∆lysR:pRS05465 which initially showed lower recovery of 73.1 ± 2.4% and 64.9 ± 1.7% for carbon and electrons, respectively. This deficit suggested carbon flux diverted toward unquantified products. Subsequent amino acid analysis identified alanine (7.0 ± 0.2 mM) and valine (4.8 ± 0.8 mM) as additional products of AWO_CDM∆lysR:pRS05465 under these conditions and taking these products into account resulted in an increased carbon and electron recovery of 86.5 ± 2.9% and 80.8 ± 2.3%, respectively. Nevertheless, the balances remain rather low, suggesting that a carbon and electron-containing compound remains unquantified.

The metabolic product ethanol is highly reduced (degree of reduction ᵹ = 6) and can therefore only be generated with high carbon efficiency if the carbon source used for production is highly reduced [59]. As such, methanol was selected as one promising substrate because it is a highly reduced carbon source with rather favorable bioenergetics which might be able to favor formation of ethanol in addition to acetate. On methanol + CO2, the reference strain AWO_CDM did not produce ethanol, whereas the engineered strains redirected part of the carbon flux toward ethanol, with AWO_CDM∆lysR:pRS05465 producing 5.4 ± 0.6 mM ethanol (YEtOH/S: 0.10 ± 0.01 mol mol−1) and AWO_CDM∆lysR:pMeOH_1 reaching 2.1 ± 0.6 mM (YEtOH/S: 0.03 ± 0.01 mol mol−1). Biomass concentrations of the engineered strains (0.2–0.3 g L−1) were slightly lower than AWO_CDM (0.3 g L−1) and acetate yields decreased marginally but insignificantly from 0.69 ± 0.05 mol mol−1 in AWO_CDM to 0.66 ± 0.04 and 0.61 ± 0.08 mol mol−1 in the engineered strains AWO_CDM∆lysR:pRS05465 and AWO_CDM∆lysR:pMeOH_1, respectively, indicating a partial diversion of carbon from acetate to ethanol.

On formate, AWO_CDM and AWO_CDMΔlysR:pRS05465 reached similar biomass concentrations of ~0.07 g L−1 and both strains stoichiometrically converted formate to acetate (YAce/S ~ 0.25 mol mol−1) without detectable ethanol production. In contrast to methanol, formate is a rather oxidized substrate with limited bioenergetics. Therefore, we also tested whether the combination of formate and syngas could enable ethanol production compared to formate alone. On formate + syngas, both strains used all available formate along with part of the syngas. Compared to growth on formate alone, biomass concentrations were increased 4.4 and 3.6-fold for AWO_CDM and AWO_CDMΔlysR:pRS05465, respectively. Ethanol production was observed for both strains, but the final concentration was 8.1-fold higher for AWO_CDMΔlysR:pRS05465 compared to AWO_CDM, with comparable acetate production by both strains.

Collectively, these results successfully demonstrate ethanol production from methanol and show increased production of ethanol from formate + syngas in A. woodii. AWO_CDMΔlysR:pRS05465 showed higher ethanol formation compared to AWO_CDMΔlysR:pMeOH_1 and the parental strain AWO_CDM, which produced ethanol from formate + syngas but not from formate or methanol + CO2.

Discussion

In this work, we characterized a suite of eight constitutive (six native to A. woodii and two exogenous) and a methanol-inducible (pMeOH_1) promoter using the pFAST reporter system. Then, we used the strongest characterized promoter (pRS0465) and pMeOH_1 to replace the native promoter of the bifunctional aldehyde/alcohol dehydrogenase AdhE of A. woodii using the endogenous CRISPR type I-B system. Finally, we conducted small-scale batch fermentations of the engineered strains and demonstrated ethanol production from fructose, methanol and formate + syngas.

Employing characterized promoters is a critical step in the rational engineering of microbial strains, as it enables precise control over gene expression for pathway optimization. While commonly used inducible systems offer temporal control, they also carry inherent limitations, such as the need for antibiotics (e.g., aTc) or metabolizable inducers which might interfere with cell physiology and/or affect process economics. In contrast, the native constitutive promoters characterized in this study provide tunable expression without exogenous inducers. Additionally, the native methanol-inducible promoter provides an inducible system based on a carbon and energy source which is highly desirable for bioproduction. Among the eight promoters characterized, three promoters linked to the WLP surprisingly showed either little (pAcsD) or no fluorescence (pCooS and pAcsE) when tested with pFAST, although the expression of the genes involved in this central pathway is strong and constitutive. Indeed, transcriptomic data [52] revealed that the genes downstream of pAcsD, pCooS and pAcsE are expressed at high levels across a broad range of substrates (e.g., ~ 4000, 2000 and 9000 TPM on fructose, respectively). The gene controlled by the strongest promoter we evaluated (pRS05465) displayed a robust yet generally lower expression profile across conditions (e.g., ~ 1500 TPM on fructose). Taken together, the discrepancy we observed between the RNA-seq expression values and our reporter assays underline the necessity of characterizing promoters experimentally, especially in non-model hosts like A. woodii.

This apparent disparity between transcriptomic data and our promoter characterization assays can be attributed to several factors. First, TPM values reflect not only transcriptional initiation but also mRNA stability; dynamic differences in transcript decay rates can substantially uncouple measured expression from the underlying promoter strength [14, 16, 38, 43, 53]. Second, RNA-seq data only provide insights into the transcriptional landscape, whereas reporter assays incorporate effects of translational regulation that may strongly influence promoter activity. Third, the selected sequences may not fully represent native promoter structures, as selection was based on the σ⁷⁰ consensus of Clostridia [33]. Some target genes could instead be controlled by alternative σ factors or transcriptional regulators, which could have an important impact if the chosen promoters were too short to include relevant binding sites.

Inducible promoters are attractive tools for controlled expression of larger transcription units while mitigating metabolic burden [21, 57]. In addition to constitutive promoters, a methanol-inducible promoter (pMeOH_1) was therefore also characterized in A. woodii using pFAST. pMeOH_1 was activated by methanol in a dose-dependent manner, yet its activity was strongly influenced by other carbon sources: while ethanol permitted induction, fructose and formate caused strong repression. This reflects a clear substrate hierarchy in which methanol utilization is subordinate to preferred carbon sources and is reminiscent of carbon catabolite repression regulation reported in acetogens, where methanol utilization is suppressed in the presence of more favorable substrates such as glucose [46]. The reason for this behavior is most likely linked to the fact that the cultures were performed in batch mode with fructose or formate present in excess, a regime that favors complete repression of methanol utilization until the preferred substrate is depleted. In contrast, co-consumption of methanol and formate was observed in formate-limited chemostat cultures of Acetobacterium sp. [3] and co-consumption of fructose and formate or formate with gaseous feedstocks was observed in substrate-limited chemostat cultures with A. woodii [52]. These observations suggest that methanol catabolism and pMeOH_1-driven expression in A. woodii is dependent both on the type of co-substrate and the cultivation mode but the underlying regulatory network remains to be elucidated.

The regulatory mechanism underlying pMeOH_1 activity likely involves two layers of control. The methanol methyltransferase operon (mta) in A. woodii harbors a transcriptional regulator, mtaR (Awo_c22810), situated directly upstream of the other genes and downstream of pMeOH_1 [41, 45]. MtaR shares 38% identity with PocR from Salmonella typhimurium [41], a regulator known to activate metabolic pathways in response to small-molecule effectors [19, 63]. Because of this structural similarity, MtaR was proposed to activate the expression of the mta operon in the presence of methanol [41]. In addition to MtaR, carbon catabolite repression (CCR) likely accounts for the repression observed in the presence of fructose and possibly with formate as methanol was consumed only after fructose was fully depleted in co-feeding experiments and was not consumed in the presence of formate. CcpA is a global regulator of CCR in Gram-positive bacteria, repressing non-preferred substrate pathways in the presence of rapidly metabolizable sugars [28]. Importantly, the genome of A. woodii bears a gene encoding CcpA (Awo_c13680, sharing 83% and 45% identity with CcpA of Acetobacterium bakii and C. ljungdahlii, respectively). A putative CcpA-binding motif (cre: ATTACAATTGGTTAAT) overlapping the − 10 element of pMeOH_1 was identified using a Clostridia consensus sequence, ATGAAAACGTTTTAAT [55], suggesting direct transcriptional occlusion may occur upon CcpA binding, i.e., when cells are grown with preferred carbon sources. Together, these findings suggest that the regulation of the mta operon is orchestrated by a mechanism in which MtaR mediates methanol-dependent induction, while CcpA ensures repression in the presence of fructose or formate.

A. woodii was previously shown to naturally produce ethanol from fructose with a relatively high yield (up to 0.79 mol mol−1) under Na+ depletion, a condition under which the activity of the WLP is severely inhibited. As a result, CO2 cannot efficiently serve as an electron sink, which stimulates ethanol production via the NADH-consuming AdhE enzyme [49]. In this work, we showed that increasing the expression of the native adhE gene via a precise genome edit is sufficient to significantly increase the ethanol yield from fructose under Na+ depletion (0.87 mol mol−1), demonstrating that promoter strength alone is a key limiting factor for ethanol production in A. woodii. This has also been previously observed in other organisms, such as C. acetobutylicum, where targeted promoter replacement in the native cellulase operon was sufficient to enable growth on cellulose [75]. In our design, lysR, located directly upstream of adhE, was deleted together with the native promoter region and replaced by either pRS05465 or pMeOH_1 to relieve potential local regulation of adhE expression. Because LysR-type regulators can control multiple targets, we additionally constructed a control strain lacking lysR but retaining the native adhE promoter (AWO_CDMΔlysR) to separate effects of lysR deletion from those of promoter replacement. The control strain did not display the pronounced shift toward ethanol observed in the promoter-replacement strains, suggesting that the main driver of the ethanologenic phenotype is increased adhE expression. However, more subtle effects of lysR deletion on other genes cannot be fully excluded, which will require further systems-level analyses.

The biotechnological potential of A. woodii relies on the WLP for the conversion of C1 feedstocks, which requires conditions under which the WLP is fully active. We therefore investigated whether the engineered strains overexpressing adhE could produce ethanol from several C1 substrates (methanol + CO2, formate, formate + CO and formate + syngas). Ethanol is a more reduced product than acetate and its synthesis therefore requires a greater input of reducing equivalents. Moreover, diverting carbon flux toward ethanol comes at the cost of reduced acetate production, which is a major driver of ATP generation via the WLP. Consequently, the feasibility of ethanol production from C1 substrates is expected to depend on two key factors: the ATP yield supported by the substrate and the availability of reducing equivalents. Substrates that satisfy both criteria such as methanol or syngas, which provide high amounts of ATP and flexible routing of reducing equivalents [42, 45] are therefore the most promising candidates.

Consistent with this hypothesis, no ethanol production was detected on formate alone, in agreement with the negative ATP yield predicted by the stoichiometric model (−0.09 ATP mol−1 of ethanol, [59]). In contrast, both methanol and formate + syngas supported ethanol production with a selectivity of 12.6 and 8.1%, respectively, confirming that these substrates provide sufficient bioenergetics to sustain carbon flux toward ethanol, in line with model predictions (0.70 and 0.08 ATP mol−1 of ethanol, respectively, [52, 59]). However, despite favorable bioenergetics (0.36–1.27 ATP mol−1 of ethanol, depending on the formate:CO stoichiometry), only trace amounts of ethanol were produced from formate + CO, which might be attributed to the well-documented inhibitory effect of CO on growth [8, 32, 72]: the resulting low growth rates possibly limit acetyl-CoA availability, reducing the carbon flux that could otherwise be directed toward ethanol synthesis.

Together with our promoter replacement strategy, these findings underline that ethanol formation in A. woodii is constrained both by energy conservation and by endogenous transcriptional control. Indeed, relieving energetic bottlenecks through the selection of energetically favorable substrates and decoupling adhE expression from its native promoter suffice to enable the production of ethanol from C1 feedstocks, illustrating how small, targeted genome edits can significantly reshape carbon flux and unlock latent metabolic traits.

Conclusion

This work establishes a modular plasmid assembly platform and a set of characterized promoters for A. woodii, enabling rapid and standardized control of gene expression and CRISPR-based genome editing. Applying this toolbox by implementing a single promoter swap at the adhE locus, we redirected carbon flux from acetate toward ethanol and demonstrated ethanol production from methanol + CO2 and formate + syngas in A. woodii. Together with its robustness and its ability to convert diverse C1 substrates, these tools position A. woodii as a genetically accessible chassis for sustainable production of chemicals and fuels from CO2-derived and renewable C1 feedstocks.
