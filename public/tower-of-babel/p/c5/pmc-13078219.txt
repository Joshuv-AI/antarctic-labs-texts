Longitudinal assessment of influenza vaccination coverage in Spain: The “Gripómetro” as a real-time monitoring tool
Authors: Raúl Ortiz de Lejarazu-Leonardo, Esther Redondo Margüello, Ángel Gil de Miguel, Federico Martinón-Torres, J Manel Farré Avellà, Paloma I Palomo-Jiménez, María Luz Nieto Nevot, Javier Díez-Domingo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDelayed availability of vaccine coverage statistics can limit within-season situational awareness for seasonal influenza vaccination campaigns. This longitudinal ecological study evaluated influenza vaccination coverage (IVC) in Spain across five seasons (2018/19 to 2022/23) using the real-time monitoring tool Gripómetro, based on weekly landline telephone interviews conducted for six consecutive weeks starting 14 d after campaign launch. Adults aged 18–80 y from the Spanish population and healthcare workers (HCWs) from public primary care centers were surveyed using a semi-structured questionnaire. An overall IVC increase was observed during the COVID-19 pandemic, peaking in the 2020/21 season. Among individuals aged ≥65 y, coverage rose from 56% in 2018/19 to 68% in 2020/21 and remained stable thereafter, below the European stablished target 75%. In 2020/21, a coverage peak (49%) was observed among at-risk adults <65 y, followed by a decline. HCWs achieved 74% IVC during the pandemic, with a smaller decline in subsequent seasons. Despite increased coverage, ~80%–85% of respondents still perceived influenza as a mild illness. However, adherence improved over time, with more participants identifying as “Faithful” to vaccination and fewer as “Not convinced.” The Gripómetro provided early, within-season coverage estimates that closely tracked official national data for older adults and can complement delayed administrative reporting. The findings highlight the positive influence of the pandemic on vaccination uptake, and the importance of sustained public health efforts to maintain influenza risk awareness and preserve high IVC.KEYWORDS: Influenza, influenza vaccine, surveillance, vaccine coverage, COVID-19

Background

Seasonal influenza epidemics affect between 5%–15% of the world’s population each year, with an estimated 3–5 million cases of severe illness and between 290,000 and 650,000 deaths worldwide annually.1 Although it can affect people of all ages, children, pregnant women, older adults and people with chronic illnesses are more likely to experience serious complications.1 For example, the annual incidence of influenza-associated respiratory and cardiovascular hospitalizations in Spain was estimated at 75 cases per 100,000 in the general population, increasing to 335 cases per 100,000 in patients aged ≥ 65 y.2

Influenza has the highest burden of the most frequent infectious diseases in Europe,3 which underscores the importance of preventive measures against seasonal epidemics. Vaccination is the most effective pharmacological method to prevent severe infections, complications, and deaths from the influenza virus, making it a key strategy in Europe’s immunization programmes.1,3 Beyond individual outcomes, seasonal influenza places a considerable burden on healthcare systems and society,4 including increased hospital demand and productivity losses due to absenteeism.2,5–8 This reinforces the need to optimize vaccination campaigns and to track uptake in a timely manner to identify gaps while the campaign is ongoing.

Based on the recommendations from the World Health Organization (WHO) and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), the European Union suggests that member states strive to achieve 75% influenza vaccination coverage (IVC) for the aging population and provide greater IVC for HCWs and patients with chronic diseases.9,10 In p longitudinal design of this study n to general guidelines, individual member states may adapt their immunization strategies to the local context. In Spain, annual influenza vaccination is recommended for adults aged ≥ 65 y, individuals with chronic health conditions, pregnant women, children aged 6 months to 5 y, and healthcare workers, among other risk groups, with implementation coordinated at the level of the Spanish Autonomous Communities (SACs).11 In this context, timely monitoring of IVC is critical to support decision-making during ongoing campaigns. However, official national vaccination coverage reports (e.g., SIVAMIN) are typically available several months after the end of the campaign (approximately 6–7 months) and may not provide sufficiently timely, detailed breakdowns for key risk groups, limiting their usefulness for mid-campaign corrective actions.

Surveillance systems are one of the main tools for assessing the incidence of vaccine-preventable diseases in near real-time each season.12 The development of comprehensive near real-time monitoring tools could boost vaccine management and the success of awareness campaigns. The Gripómetro, a demographic survey, was developed in Spain in 2011 as a tool to monitor IVC in real time nationwide.13,14 By identifying early plateaus or a stagnation in uptake among specific risk groups (e.g., chronic conditions) during a campaign (e.g., by week 3), it may support public health authorities in implementing timely, targeted interventions, such as localized SMS reminders or adjusted media messaging, to improve coverage before the campaign concludes.15 The Gripómetro was designed to be applied each influenza season, and its data can provide insights into the profile of the population surveyed and the potential factors that may influence higher or lower IVC. In this context, the COVID-19 pandemic, through heightened attention to respiratory infections and prevention, may have influenced influenza vaccination uptake, further underscoring the value of timely, within-season monitoring.

The negative impact of influenza is not limited to individual outcomes. Every seasonal epidemic carries a significant burden in terms of the total burden of infectious diseases,4 stressing healthcare systems due to the increased demand for medical care and resource utilization required. For the Spanish health system, the direct annual cost of excess hospitalizations due to respiratory and cardiovascular admissions was estimated at €142.9 million for all ages and €115.9 million for patients aged ≥ 65 y.2 Seasonal influenza also negatively affects other social aspects, such as school and work absenteeism, causing loss of productivity.5–7 Moreover, work absenteeism among healthcare workers (HCWs) adds to the burden on healthcare systems,8 and HCW vaccination can also contribute to protecting vulnerable patients and maintaining healthcare capacity during seasonal peaks.

This study aims to assess the IVC in Spain between the 2018/19 and 2022/23 seasons and to describe the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the IVC using the Gripómetro. This information will allow us to monitor and describe coverage levels, identify coverage gaps (mostly among at-risk groups), and analyze their temporal evolution during yearly campaigns. Consequently, it will complement delayed administrative reporting by providing timely information on in-season vaccination uptake.

Methods

Design and study population

Longitudinal ecological study evaluating influenza seasons between 2018/19 and 2022/23 in Spain. A questionnaire was administered in each season through weekly telephone interviews for six consecutive weeks, starting 14 d after the start of the influenza vaccination campaign (defined by the Ministry of Health and usually between October and November). This observation window was intended to capture early and mid-campaign uptake; therefore, vaccinations occurring after the 6-week fieldwork period may not be fully reflected in the estimates.

Two populations of interest were defined: the general Spanish population (SP) and Spanish HCWs. For the first group, participants were randomly selected from the population aged 18 to 80 y residing in Spain. Regarding HCWs, the survey was conducted among doctors and nurses randomly selected from public primary care centers.

Instrument

The Gripómetro is a semi-structured questionnaire that compiles data on influenza vaccination for both the individual respondent and all household members and has been extensively described elsewhere.13 On an individual level, it also collects sociodemographic characteristics and attitudes concerning influenza and vaccination. Information regarding children aged < 18 y was indirectly obtained by asking participants about the members of the household. The complete questionnaires are available in Supplementary material.

Sampling and sample size

Telephone interviews were conducted using computer-assisted telephone interviewing (CATI). The sampling frame was based on random-digit dialing including landline numbers.

The sampling method estimates sought to represent the SP based on age groups. In all seasons, a margin of error of ±1.62% was estimated for the potential interviewee population (±2.37% for those aged 18 to 64 y and ±2.21% for those aged 65 to 80 y). Surveys of HCWs also aimed to be representative, assuming a margin of error of ±3.5% (±5.0% for doctors and nurses). Given the quota-based design and weighting, these margins of error should be considered approximate.

In both surveys, accuracy was calculated for an infinite universe and a confidence level of 95%, under the assumption of maximum indeterminacy (p = q = 0.5). To ensure representative and comparable results across different Spanish Autonomous Communities (SACs, into which Spain is divided geopolitically) and minimum sample sizes across territories and enable comparability, fixed quotas were established by age groups. To restore proportionality to the sample and thus balance any potential imbalances resulting from the fixed quota, the results were weighted based on the population universe for the SAC (by sex and age group for the SP) and the national universe of primary care workers for HCWs. Achieved sample sizes by season and age group are reported in Supplementary Table S1. Season-specific non-response rates could not be calculated from the available dataset.

Variables collected

Data from five seasons were available: 2018/19, 2019/20, 2020/21, 2021/22, and 2022/23. The sociodemographic variables collected for the target populations were sex (male/female), age, and SAC of residence. Furthermore, individuals from the SP belonging to age-related risk groups (<5 and ≥65 y old) and those belonging to non-age-related risk groups due to health conditions were distinguished. The following conditions were considered as risk factors: presence of chronic disease (immunosuppression, diabetes, cancer, heart disease, respiratory disease, mobility disability, neurological disease, liver disease, kidney disease, anemia, and morbid obesity) and pregnancy. For the HCW group, data were also collected on the type of occupation (medicine or nursing). The IVC for each season was estimated based on the response to the question ‘Have you been vaccinated, or do you plan to be vaccinated against the flu during this campaign?’. To study the validity of the ICV estimates, Spanish government data (SGD) published on the Spanish Ministry of Health’s official website (SIVAMIN)16 were included in this analysis. Given differences in data sources and definitions (survey self-report/intention vs administrative coverage metrics and potentially different denominators), these comparisons were interpreted as convergent validation.

Perception of influenza severity and attitude towards vaccination

To describe participants’ perceptions of the severity of influenza, responses to the item ‘In your opinion, and thinking about your own health, the flu is … ’ were categorized as either a severe (‘a disease that can cause death’ or ‘a disease that can be serious’) or mild condition (‘a troublesome disease that can cause complications’ or ‘a troublesome but tolerable disease’).

Based on the responses to the questions about vaccination in current and past campaigns (‘Have you had the flu vaccination in the last 3 y?’), participants were classified into four groups: ‘Faithful’ (vaccinated against the flu in the past and in the season of the survey), ‘Not convinced’ (did not get vaccinated against the flu in the past nor in the season of the survey), ‘Won’ (did not get vaccinated against the flu in the past but have done so or intend to in the season of the survey) and ‘Lost’ (vaccinated against the flu in the recent past but did not do so in the season of the survey). The ‘last 3 y’ variable refers to a rolling reference period preceding each surveyed season, intended to capture recent vaccination behavior while reducing recall burden.

For each of the attitudes toward vaccination, participants were asked about the reasons for their choice (‘What are the main reasons for your decision?’). For the seasons from 2020/21 to 2022/23, the influence of COVID-19 on the decision to get vaccinated (yes/no) was collected.

Statistical analysis

An ecological analysis was performed on the grouped data. Categorical variables were described through their frequencies and proportions within each season and population group. To assess the IVC, a longitudinal description of the data from each season was performed. To assess the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, multivariate binomial regression was performed, in which the dependent variable was the vaccination status of the SP (vaccinated yes/no), and the proposed explanatory variable was the season of the study, divided into two periods: pre-pandemic period (2018/19–2019/20) and pandemic period (2020/21–2022/23). The selected covariates were the influenza season and the percentage of the SP that perceived influenza as a potentially fatal or serious illness. There were no missing data in these analyses. As a sensitivity analysis, we refitted the model additionally adjusting for age group, sex, and chronic condition status. Region (SACs) could not be included in these models because the data available for this analysis were not sufficiently disaggregated to support a consistent region term across seasons. Full results of the adjusted model are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Given the number of subgroup comparisons across seasons, inferential results were interpreted as exploratory and no formal adjustment for multiple testing was applied.

For the epidemiological measures, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were estimated. Analysis was conducted based on two-sided p-values, with p < .05 indicating statistical significance, using R software v.4.3.3.

Results

A total of 114,432 individuals were interviewed from seasons 2018/19 to 2022/23 (2007 were HCWs). A description of the respondents by age, sex, and SACs is available in the Supplementary material (Table S3 for the SP and Table S4 for HCWs). The IVC over the time series for the SP, HCWs and by target groups is shown in Table 1. In the SP, the IVC increased to a peak of 27% in the 2020/21 season, declining to 24% by 2022/23. Figure 1 shows that this trend was consistent across age and sex groups, and was higher in women than in men. At the SAC level, the coverage among adults aged ≥65 y also increased over time, although the trajectory showed some differences among regions (the detailed IVC is available in the Supplementary Table S5).

Influenza vaccination coverage monitoring at national level

Among the population with age-related risk, the official SGD fell within the 95% CI or deviated by <5% with respect to the Gripómetro coverage estimates. In the case of HCWs, a discrepancy in the estimated values was observed, although the trend over the years was similar.

Overall, IVC was higher among at-risk individuals compared with non-at-risk individuals in the SP group. For those aged ≥ 65 y, IVC rose from 56% in 2018/19 to 68% in 2020/21, remaining relatively stable until the 2022/23 season. A slightly higher IVC was observed in men for most of the study period, which significantly reversed in the last season (see Figure 1(B)). In the SP aged ≥ 65 y with non-age-related risk factors, IVC was 5%–12% higher throughout the season series, reaching a maximum of 81% in the 2020/21 season. Among the SP aged 18–64 y with non-age-related risk, IVC increased from 24% to 49% in the 2020/21 season, and then decreased to 32% in 2022/23. The diagnostic groups with the highest IVC in the 2020/21 season were individuals with immunosuppression (66%), diabetes (61%) and cancer (59%). IVC in pregnant women exceeded 50% in the same season. Estimates for pregnancy should be interpreted cautiously due to small sample sizes and wide confidence intervals in some seasons.

Regarding HCWs, there was an increase in IVC from the third season onwards (74% in 2020/21), with a less pronounced decrease in subsequent periods compared to the SP. There were no clear differences between nursing and medical staff. Among HCWs aged over 60 y, IVC followed a similar trend to that observed in the SP group with age-related risk factors. In HCWs with non-age-related risk factors, coverage close to 90% was achieved in the 2020/21 season, one of the highest levels in this study.

Perception of influenza severity and attitude toward vaccination

Despite nearly 80%–85% of respondents considering influenza to be a mild illness during all seasons, in all groups there was a progressive increase in the proportion of individuals who rate it as the lowest severity level. In contrast, the perception of influenza as a serious or fatal disease showed little variation over time, remaining steady at around 10%–15%. With respect to the attitude toward influenza vaccination, across the series and target groups for all seasons, the most common categories were ‘Not convinced’ and ‘Faithful’. The prevalence of the ‘Not convinced’ category decreased by at least 10% after 2018/19 in both the at-risk and the non-at-risk populations. The minimum value was 16% in the SP aged ≥ 65 y in 2021/22, and the highest prevalence was 80% in 2018/19 among the non-at-risk population. The ‘Faithful’ category showed an increasing general trend and had the highest prevalences among individuals aged 65 or older (over 70% from 2021/22 onwards). The lowest prevalences were observed in the non-at-risk population, although with an increasing trend from 13% to 18% until 2022/23. The attitude toward vaccination among the SP from 2018/19 to 2022/23 is summarized in Table 3. The questionnaire items used to elicit reasons for vaccination decisions within each attitude profile are provided in the Supplementary material (Survey S1). Close to 50% of the SP answered that the COVID-19 pandemic influenced their decision to be vaccinated in the 2020/21 season (in both the non-at-risk and age-related risk groups). These percentages decreased to 30%-35% in the 2022/23 season. The influence of the pandemic was least pronounced among people aged ≥ 65 y but remained relatively stable over the three seasons (20%–30% approximately).

Discussion

This study offers a comprehensive analysis of IVC in Spain from influenza seasons 2018/19 to 2022/23 using the Gripómetro, a real-time monitoring tool. Its results provide insights into the vaccination trends over these five seasons and examines how coverage patterns evolved across COVID-19 pandemic period.

Overall, we observed a 5%–10% increase in IVC from the periods 2018/2019 to 2022/2023, with peak coverage coinciding with the height of the COVID-19 pandemic. This increase has also been reported in other European countries,10 and suggests that the heightened awareness and concern for respiratory illnesses during the pandemic likely influenced individuals’ decisions to receive the influenza vaccine.17 A notable and policy-relevant observation is that coverage increased during the pandemic period while perceived influenza severity remained largely stable, with most respondents continuing to rate influenza as a mild illness. This pattern suggests that the pandemic may have increased general receptivity to vaccination or to respiratory infection prevention behaviors, rather than substantially changing influenza-specific risk perception. Although our design does not allow causal inference on these mechanisms, this finding supports the value of future campaigns that combine risk communication with broader prevention framing and convenient access to vaccination.

The increase in IVC observed among HCWs, peaking in 2020/21, aligns with the expectation that pandemic-era risk salience would affect uptake in patient-facing roles. At the same time, HCW vaccination can be viewed against a higher benchmark than the general population, given its relevance to reducing transmission in healthcare settings and maintaining workforce capacity. The fact that IVC did not approach near-universal levels suggests persistent barriers that may include access/logistical constraints, risk perceptions, safety concerns, organizational culture, or differences in institutional policies. While identifying and testing interventions is beyond the scope of this study, these results highlight the importance of targeted strategies for HCWs, potentially including system-level approaches that have been implemented in other settings.

Although Spain has an official system to monitor vaccination coverage, the Ministry of Health’s Vaccination Information System (SIVAMIN) has provided coverage information at national and SAC level only since 2017,16 and its usefulness for in-season decision-making remains limited. In practice, national-level coverage estimates are not consistently available for multiple age- and non-age-related risk groups (comparable national data were only available for adults aged ≥65 y, pregnant women and HCWs), and consolidated estimates typically become available approximately 6–7 months after the end of the influenza season, with partial information for some SACs. These limitations are partly explained by heterogeneity in the digitization of nominal vaccination registries across SACs.18

In this context, the Gripómetro was developed in 2011 to monitor IVC in near real time in Spain.13,14 Its performance and representativeness, particularly for older adults, have been previously evaluated,12 showing that survey-based estimates were concordant with official administrative data released later. Importantly, the present study reinforces this evidence: a key result is the alignment between Gripómetro coverage estimates and Spanish government data reported through SIVAMIN in the groups for which national administrative data were available. Taken together, these findings support Gripómetro as a complementary tool that can provide timely, in-season coverage signals that are broadly consistent with later national reporting, while also offering earlier visibility for selected risk groups before administrative estimates are released.

By comparison, several Northern European countries operate national immunization registries that electronically transfer vaccination events from electronic health records to centralized systems with minimal delay, enabling near-continuous monitoring.19,20 Nevertheless, even in settings with mature registry infrastructure, coverage estimates for specific clinical risk groups are not always routinely available in official outputs.21 Therefore, in Spain, where administrative reporting may be delayed and may lack granularity for some risk groups, Gripómetro can help fill an information gap by providing earlier estimates and supporting situational awareness during the vaccination campaign. In line with this operational role, near real-time monitoring dashboards have been used in other settings to support targeted outreach (e.g., reminders or focused communication) and improve vaccination uptake.22

Beyond overall coverage, the Gripómetro provides additional descriptive insight through the attitude categories (‘Faithful,’ ‘Not convinced,’ ‘Won,’ ‘Lost’). We observed a decline in the ‘Not convinced’ category after 2018/19 and an increase in ‘Faithful,’ particularly among adults aged ≥65 y, alongside pandemic-period increases in IVC. Importantly, these categories should be interpreted as a pragmatic segmentation of recent vaccination behavior rather than as causal behavioral profiles. Likewise, the reported “reasons” for vaccination decisions are self-reported descriptors and may not represent predictors of behavior; our study was not designed to test whether stated reasons prospectively explain uptake. Nevertheless, tracking these categories can be useful for campaign planning, as it highlights whether changes in coverage reflect consolidation among habitual vaccinators, gains among previously unvaccinated individuals, or losses among previously vaccinated groups.

This study has several strengths. The longitudinal design of this study, spanning five influenza seasons, provides a robust framework for assessing trends in IVC over time and the impact of COVID-19 on these trends. In addition, the inclusion of both the SP and HCW groups and the consideration of some at-risk groups allows for a nuanced analysis of IVC and attitudes concerning vaccination across different segments of society, with a focus on groups of particular interest. The large sample size and the representative nature of the survey population because of the sampling method used (based on age groups, sex, and SAC), enhance the generalizability of the findings to the broader nationwide population. In addition, the comparison with official administrative reporting (SIVAMIN) provides supportive evidence that Gripómetro yields coverage estimates broadly consistent with later national data for the groups where comparable information is available. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, the ecological and repeated cross-sectional nature of the analyses precludes individual-level causal inference23,24 and some subgroup estimates (e.g., pregnancy in certain seasons) are imprecise due to small sample sizes. Second, unmeasured factors may contribute to observed patterns, including drivers of vaccine hesitancy such as low social engagement, unhealthy habits (e.g., smoking), safety concerns, lack of trust and perceived lack of need,25,26 as well as evolving attitudes influenced by misinformation and social polarization since the pandemic period.27–29 Third, telephone-based surveys may be affected by coverage and selection mechanisms (including differential reachability and non-response), which may have increased over time, particularly if sampling relied on landlines; weighting improves alignment with known population distributions but cannot fully remove these biases. In addition, season-specific respondent counts and original quota targets by SAC were not available, limiting assessment of quota implementation across territories.

Conclusions

The Gripómetro provides timely, season-in-progress IVC estimates and complementary information by risk group that may support surveillance and campaign monitoring before delayed administrative reporting becomes available. Its contribution is best framed as surveillance support rather than evidence of intervention effectiveness, and it should be considered complementary to ongoing improvements in administrative immunization information systems.
