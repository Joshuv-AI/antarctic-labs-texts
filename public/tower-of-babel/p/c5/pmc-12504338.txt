Evaluating actual use of outdoor exercise equipment in a community park in Southern California through video-captured behavioral assessment
Authors: Mojgan Sami, Soultana Macridis, Oladele A. Ogunseitan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimTest feasibility of measuring physical activity levels and actual use of outdoor exercise equipment by park users through a human-centered virtual audit in an observational study in southern California.MethodsApproximately 3,000 h of continuous video footage were collected using a stationary, customized, wireless data-transmission-enabled outdoor camera in Eastgate Park, Garden Grove, California. The camera captured images of the outdoor exercise equipment every 30 s for 14 h a day over seven months. A virtual audit was conducted on 300 h of footage by a team of researchers who tracked weather conditions, equipment usage, duration of use, and observed the age, gender, and activity levels (sedentary, moderate, or vigorous) of park users. Equipment use was categorized as intended (correct use) or unintended (e.g., resting on equipment).ResultsPooling observations from all eight exercise machines, adults used the equipment as intended an estimated 77% of the time (95% CI: 76%-79%). Youth used the equipment as intended an estimated 39% of the time (95% CI: 37%-40%). Overall, park users used the equipment as intended an estimated 60% of the time (95% CI: 59%-62%).ConclusionVirtual audits can effectively differentiate between intended and actual use of outdoor exercise equipment in recreational parks. This method can complement field observation methods, such as SOPARC, and aid in planning public health promotion and education programs to increase proper use of exercise equipment, however it is time intensive. With the advent of AI, automated visual audits can provide timely analysis of park improvement impacts.

Introduction

According to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), at least 20% of adults in each US state is living with obesity, with 23 states alone, having more than 35% of adults living with obesity [1]. A sedentary lifestyle is a significant risk factor for obesity, and studies show that sedentary behavior among Americans is increasing [2–4]. Engaging in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) is associated with a decreased risk of obesity [5]. Research indicates that the built environment can either promote or deter physical activity [6–11].

The availability, accessibility, and quality of parks in residential neighborhoods are correlated with increased likelihood of MVPA among park users across the lifespan [12–14]. Municipalities can enhance physical activity levels by investing in park improvements, such as installing outdoor exercise equipment (OEE). Leading a sedentary lifestyle has physiological effects that are distinct from being physically inactive [41]. OEE is recognized as an effective public health intervention to combat sedentary lifestyles at the environmental level [15–17].

Eastgate Park in Garden Grove, California, is located on a 4.5-acre public space accessible to approximately 3,000 residents. The park includes open green space, a children’s playground, a private community center, restroom facilities, and a covered picnic area. As part of the interventions supported by the CDC, the Orange County Partnerships to Improve Community Health (OC-PICH) aimed to enhance the local environment to promote physical activity.

The City of Garden Grove received funding through the Partnerships to Improve Community Health initiative supported by the CDC to install OEE in two parks. As university-based evaluation partners of OC-PICH, our team leveraged this opportunity to conduct an observational study using a virtual audit with a stationary camera system. This study aimed to test the feasibility of measuring physical activity levels and actual use of outdoor exercise equipment by park users through a human-centered virtual audit. More specifically, this technology was used first, to assess whether park users engaged with the OEE park equipment as intended by the manufacturer (Greenfields Outdoor Fitness, Inc.), and second, to evaluate the impact of OEE installation on physical activity levels among park users based on the System for Observing Play and Recreation in Communities (SOPARC) instrument [18, 19]. While our in-person observations of park user activity are reported elsewhere [20, 21], this study focused on understanding actual equipment use through continuous observation using an innovative, custom-built stationary camera system designed by Pixelcaster [22]. The use of cameras, both wearable/personalized and public, is becoming increasingly common [23, 24]. Understanding the actual use of OEE is valuable for public health and public works departments to facilitate collaboration in health promotion and education through environmental change. Moreover, assessing the feasibility of integrating technology in park-based observations requires a multi-dimensional approach, that not only examines the acceptability of such tools among users and stakeholders, but also the demand, implementation capacity, and practicality within real-world settings. This aligns with Bowen and colleagues’ [25] framework for feasibility studies, and highlights the potential for future real-time monitoring, especially given the advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI)-enhanced assessment tools for such settings.

Methods

This observational study contributes to public health researchers’ understanding of the “real world” impacts of interventions aimed at changing environmental conditions for health promotion [26, 27]. To this end, the primary data source for this study was obtained through a custom-built, wireless-enabled outdoor camera, designed and installed to continuously capture visual (still) images of OEE – also referred to as “The Fitness Zone” – every 30 s for 14 h a day (6am to 8pm) [22]. The camera was installed five months after the Fitness Zone was inaugurated and recorded images for seven months. This setup resulted in approximately 3,000 h of visual footage from continuous capture of still images every 30 s of all eight pieces of fitness equipment (Figs. 1, 2 and 3; Table 1). The visual images were uploaded to Pixelcaster’s central servers and downloaded for our research team. For this study, 300 h of complete and reliable visual data from 21 consecutive days (February 23 to March 14, 2016) were analyzed. We selected three time periods to focus our image analysis, aligning with previous SOPARC observational momentary time sampling protocols [20, 21]. The institutional review board of the University of California, Irvine, deemed that this observational study in a public park required no review because it involved no direct interaction with human participants.

Measures

Employing SOPARC protocol [19], a team of researchers manually conducted an image analysis to assess the following variables: (1) identification of equipment used; (2) duration of usage; (3) age group of users (adult (21–51 years of age)/senior (≥60 years of age)) or child (≤12 years of age)/teen (13–20 years of age); (4) observed gender of users (male/female); (5) observed activity level (sedentary –lying down, sitting, or standing still in place, including near or on equipment without active use; moderate – using equipment in a light or causal manner, such as performing 1–2 repetitions or using it intermittently without sustained effort; or vigorous – actively using equipment as intended, with sustained effort, typically performing 3 or more continuous repetitions or movements indicative of sustained physical exertion); and (6) actual use (intended or unintended). Intended use was defined as the correct use of the equipment, regardless of duration, while unintended use included any activity not associated with a specific piece of equipment (e.g., sitting on equipment to socialize). Finally, in this study, “observed gender” refers to the gender presentation perceived and recorded by researchers based on visual or contextual cues during data collection. This categorization does not reflect individuals’ self-identified gender or sex and was used solely for observational consistency in the absence of self-reported data.

Analysis

A total of 32 researchers (undergraduate and graduate students in the Department of Public Health at UCI) divided into eight teams to analyze over 100 h of video recordings per each of the eight equipment stations. Teams analyzed individual images through time-lapse video in 30-second increments (Fig. 2). An inter-rater reliability process was implemented to ensure consistent coding by researchers. Inter-rater reliability was conducted using an iterative process in which groups of student research assistants collaboratively analyzed one day of visual data at a time, refining their coding criteria through discussion and consensus [29]. For example, to ensure interrater reliability of observed activity level, the teams jointly determined the classification of activity level into sedentary, moderate, and vigorous, further adjusting their interpretations across sessions for consistency, as needed.

Once reliability was confirmed (through discussion and consensus), data were entered into a spreadsheet that included observer name, time code (start and stop of observed use), duration of use, observed weather, equipment number (Fitness Equipment 1–8; Fig. 3), type of user (e.g., adult/senior male, adult/senior female, child/teen male, child/teen female), observed activity level (e.g., sedentary, moderate, vigorous), and actual use of the equipment (e.g., intended or unintended). The total counts and percentages of usages observed for each of the eight exercise equipment stations were tabulated by user type and by activity level of the user. Then the proportion of usages during which each machine was used “as intended” was estimated, along with a 95% confidence interval, stratified by user type (age and observed gender) and overall. All data management was performed in Excel, and all statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4. The visual database of images is available by sending a reasonable request to the first author.

Results

The total number of exercise machine usages by count and percentage disaggregated by user type and activity level are shown in Table 2. A total of 5,689 usages were observed of which the top three most utilized machines were machines two (n = 1,148, 20.2%), one (n = 937,16.5%), and six (n = 707, 12.4%). Machines one through seven (see Table 2) were most frequently used by adult or senior males. Machine eight was most frequently used by child or teen males and by adult or senior males. For all machines, the majority of equipment use was by adults and seniors, rather than by children and teens. All exercise machines were most frequently used at moderate or vigorous intensity, except for machine number six and eight, which were most often used for sedentary and moderate activity.

Table 3 depicts the estimated proportion and associated 95% confidence intervals of observed fitness equipment usage during which the machines were used “as intended.” Across all eight machines, a higher proportion of usage was “as intended” when the users were adults or seniors, rather than children or youth. Across all users, “as intended” utilization of the machines was more prevalent for machines one through five than for machines six, seven, and eight.

Pooling observations of all eight exercise machines for park users by age group only, machines were used “as intended” by adults and seniors an estimated 77% of the time, with a 95% confidence interval of 76% to 79%. Machines were used “as intended” by children and youth an estimated 39% of the time, with a 95% confidence interval of 37% to 40%. Across all fitness zone users, exercise equipment was used “as intended” an estimated 60% of the time, with a 95% confidence interval of 59% to 62%.

Discussion

This study’s findings are two-fold: first to test feasibility of measuring physical activity levels, and second, to understand actual use of outdoor exercise equipment by park users through a human-centered virtual audit in an observational study in southern California. Addressing the first aim, this study assessed the feasibility of applying SOPARC protocols to video-captured images for monitoring outdoor exercise equipment use. Although video technology allowed for continuous coverage over extended periods, the manual coding of images was highly time-consuming and required substantial human effort, which limits the scalability and practicality [25] of the method in its current form.

This aligns with Bowen and colleagues [25], who indicate that feasibility studies must consider the practicality of an intervention under real-world constraints, such as time, staffing, and technological capacity. While video-based observation offers broader temporal coverage compared to in-person SOPARC observations, it introduces new challenges in implementation and practicality, which are key dimensions of feasibility. In the context of this study, the demand for an efficient and scalable surveillance method is evident, yet the current manual process may not be viable for broader adoption without further development.

Future research should explore automated or AI-assisted coding to enhance implementation efficiency and accuracy, making this approach more viable for long-term park monitoring and public health evaluation. However, as highlighted in recent literature, such technologies may introduce risks around acceptability and equity, particularly if demographic characteristics are misidentified due to the limitations of AI, which may not align with self-identified racial/ethnic, gender and/or age [30–32].

Addressing the second aim, this study provides novel baseline information that can be used to improve the effectiveness of interventions aimed at promoting physical activity among park users using an observational and innovative visual audit design to collect data post-implementation of a fitness zone [33]. Results from this study show that most park users utilized the equipment as intended and occurring at moderate to vigorous intensity levels, particularly among adults and seniors. However, unintended uses of OEE may result from insufficient public information for use, inadequate variety of equipment, the positioning of equipment within the park, and the overall park characteristics (e.g., limited seating leading people to use equipment to rest). At a broader community level, the lack of other opportunities for park visitors to sit and socialize may encourage unintended use of fitness equipment. These findings highlight the need for adaptation - another key feasibility dimension - to better tailor equipment and park layout to user needs [25]. For example, as observed in this study, machines six, seven, and eight were most frequently used in unintended ways, which may be attributable to their design features that are prone for more sedentary activities (e.g., sitting for rest or standing to socialize; Fig. 3). This study also addresses integration, as findings may inform how physical activity equipment can be more effectively embedded within park infrastructure to align and serve broader community needs and usage patterns [25].

Observed differences in “intended” use with OEE were also found between adults/seniors and children/youth, suggesting differences in interest and appropriateness of the machines across the lifespan, as well as acceptability [25]. Although children may be attracted to OEE in park settings, such equipment is often designed for adult use. As a result, “unintended use” behavior among children is difficult to prevent, especially in non-supervised open parks, which can result in unsafe use and/or injury [34]. Applying the expansion feasibility dimension, there is opportunity to examine “intended” and “unintended” behavior in terms of age-appropriateness, motivations, and perceptions to better accommodate the needs and suitability, while also mitigating risk among all ages and abilities [25].

Strengths

Advancements in technology have enabled more efficient observation methods for capturing real-time physical activity in natural settings. This study demonstrates the use of visual technology to monitor OOE use in context while applying SOPARC – a standardized in-person observational assessment instrument – to enhance data collection and analysis. Compared to in-person observations, which are time- and labor-intensive, video cameras offer a cost-effective solution for continuous, real-time data collection over extended periods of time. They also enable repeated time-over-time review during analysis, thus enhancing accuracy of behavioral assessments. In fact, camera-based observation methods have been found to be more reliable than in-person observations, as they do not result in participant reactivity to an observer’s presence, require less time for data collection, and reduce research staff fatigue and safety concerns, particularly during long or late-night observations [23]. This is approach is particularly appealing for municipalities with limited staff and access to continuous camera systems (e.g., traffic camera). Based on this study’s findings, Pixelcaster presents itself as a practical alternative, as it provides high-resolution, time-lapse imaging that can be installed temporarily or permanently in public spaces, including parks and trails.

By capturing real-time video footage, this study provides actionable insights into both the frequency and demographics of OEE use, as well as intended and unintended use and behavior. These findings benefit municipalities in two ways: first by enabling them to monitor the impact of their investments (e.g., physical activity, economic, social outcomes); and second, by providing evidence to inform educational campaigns, optimize equipment selection and placement, and support data-driven decisions that can enhance safety, accessibility, and community engagement among community members of all ages and abilities. Combined, these contributions not only support informed municipal decision-making, but also demonstrate the feasibility of implementing scalable, technology-driven methods in real-world settings.

Limitations

The authors acknowledge several limitations of this study worth noting. First, visual recordings in natural environments pose challenges for objectively coding demographic details. As such, the authors recognize limitations and potential biases inherent in assigning gender and an age category based on observation. We emphasize that this approach was used cautiously and solely for the purposes of behavioral coding in public settings where self-identification was not feasible. Second, video recordings were captured as still images in 30-second increments. As such, some brief user behaviors may have been missed, potentially limiting the completeness of observational data. Further, the counts presented in our analysis represent observed instances of use and do not account for repeated use by the same individuals during a single visit or across multiple days, however longitudinal tracking was beyond the scope of this study. A more detailed analysis could be achieved by increasing the frequency of image capture from every 30 s, as documented in this study, to every 10 s. This would provide more granular information on equipment use, but would require increased data storage, as well as enhanced video resolution. While in-person observation may offer contextual depth, it is susceptible to human errors such as fatigue or momentary distraction, which can result in missed behaviors [35]. In contrast, video-based methods allow for repeated viewing and more granular analysis, potentially improving the accuracy and completeness of behavioral assessments [23]. Also, due to technical issues with the camera (e.g., loss of power/offline and inconsistent image quality), consistent and usable data were confined to 300 h from 21 consecutive days between February 23 and March 14, 2016. Further, to ensure privacy, limit liability concerns, and address the lack of lighting after dusk, recordings were limited to daylight hours when park use was most active and observable. The authors recognize that this study occurred during the transition from winter to early spring and recommend that future studies assess year-round impacts across all seasons [36]. Although we recognize weather is known to influence outdoor physical activity, we chose not to include it in our analysis due to the transitional nature of the season (late winter to early spring) under observation, which may not have provided sufficient variability in weather conditions to support meaningful correlation analysis. Future studies with longer durations and more precise, localized weather tracking are recommended to more robustly explore this relationship. Finally, the authors recognize that although a pooled approach to our analyses enabled us to evaluate coding consistency and identify methodological challenges, a disaggregated analysis is warranted to better understand patterns of equipment use such as the day of the week or time of day. Despite these limitations, the findings of this study have implications for future methodological research.

Future recommendations

Building on the findings of this study, several avenues for future research and methodological refinement are recommended to enhance the feasibility, accuracy, inclusivity, and utility of observational assessments in public park settings. Namely, future studies can triangulate in-person observations with enhanced imaging techniques, integrate Artificial Intelligence (AI) thoughtfully to increase efficiency of observational data and address its potential biases.

Enhancing observational methods

Future studies should prioritize a deeper understanding of how users interact with OEE. This includes exploring user preferences and challenges—such as likes, dislikes, clarity of instructions, whether equipment is perceived as intimidating or more suitable for one or two users, and patterns of use daily, weekly, monthly, and even seasonally. Such insights can inform the design and placement of equipment to maximize accessibility and engagement.

Additionally, refining visual categorization methods is essential. Current observational tools, such as SOPARC, require coders to infer demographic characteristics such as age and sex based on appearance. While practical in public settings where self-identification is not feasible, this approach carries inherent limitations and risks of misclassification. We acknowledge the potential for reinforcing stereotypes and emphasize that such categorizations were made cautiously and solely for behavioral coding purposes. Observational studies must recognize that visual cues often do not align with individuals’ lived identities or cultural contexts, and that binary or overly broad demographic categories can oversimplify complex identities.

Integrating technology and artificial intelligence

The integration of AI into observational research offers promising advancements but also introduces new ethical and methodological challenges. AI systems can significantly enhance the efficiency and accuracy of visual audits by processing large volumes of video and sensor data to detect patterns in park usage, equipment interaction, and physical activity levels. For example, AI can identify fall risks among older adults or assess children’s activity levels, providing valuable data for targeted health interventions [37, 38].

However, reliance on AI for demographic classification must be approached with caution. Algorithmic models often replicate or amplify societal biases embedded in their training data, potentially exacerbating issues of misclassification and exclusion. As highlighted by Buolamwini [39] in Unmasking AI and Benjamin [40] in Assessing Risk, Automating Racism, the deployment of AI in public health contexts must be critically examined to avoid perpetuating systemic inequities.

To mitigate these risks, we recommend a hybrid approach that combines AI-driven analysis with traditional in-person observations. This triangulation can validate findings, reduce observer burden, and provide a more nuanced understanding of park usage. Moreover, AI tools should be continuously audited for bias and calibrated to reflect the diversity of the communities they serve.

In conclusion, this study explored the feasibility of using video-captured images to monitor OEE use in a public park setting by applying SOPARC protocols to a novel visual dataset. Although video technology offers the advantage of continuous coverage and the potential for cost-effective data collection, our findings highlight limitations of manual image coding, which proved to be highly labor-intensive and time-consuming. These constraints limit the scalability of this method in its current form and underscore the need for more efficient, automated approaches to support wider implementation.

Despite these challenges, the findings also demonstrate that the intervention was effective in promoting moderate to vigorous physical activity, particularly among adults and seniors, suggesting meaningful engagement with OEE and alignment with public health goals. More broadly, this study highlights the value of visual technology as a tool for capturing contextual use of outdoor fitness infrastructure, especially when paired with standardized observational frameworks. To fully realize the potential of this approach, future research should emphasize methodological rigor, ethical sensitivity, and technological innovation, including the integration of AI-assisted coding and analysis to streamline data processing, improve accuracy, and generate more granular insights into park use behaviors. As these tools and technology evolve, it will be critical to recognize and address the biases that may be embedded in algorithmic models, ensuring that analytic methods advance equity rather than exacerbate existing disparities. Together, these efforts can support more inclusive, effective public health interventions and community education strategies centered on equitable use of OEE resources.
