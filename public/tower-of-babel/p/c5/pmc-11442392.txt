Association between highly pathogenic avian influenza outbreaks and weather conditions in Japan
Authors: Yuri FUJIMOTO, Takeshi HAGA
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHighly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) poses a significant threat to animal and public health, with outbreaks occurring globally. HPAI poses significant challenges due to its high mortality rate and public health concerns, with outbreaks spreading globally since the emergence of the H5N1 virus in 2003. In Japan, HPAI outbreaks have been particularly prevalent during autumn and winter seasons, with the 2022–2023 winter experiencing the most severe outbreak to date. However, limited research has directly examined the association between HPAI outbreaks and weather conditions in Japan. Here we show that specific weather conditions are associated with an increased risk of HPAI outbreaks on poultry farms in Japan. By analyzing databases of HPAI cases and meteorological data from 2020–2023, we found that higher average air temperatures two to three weeks prior, lower average wind speeds four weeks prior, and longer sunlight hours two and four weeks prior to outbreaks were significantly associated with increased risk of HPAI outbreaks in Japan. These results suggest that weather may influence environmental survival and transmission of the virus, as well as patterns of wild bird movement that could seed new outbreaks. These findings enhance our understanding of the factors influencing HPAI transmission dynamics and highlight the importance of integrating weather forecasts into disease surveillance and prevention strategies.Keywords: case-control study, highly pathogenic avian influenza, Japan, weather condition

Highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) is a problematic disease due to its high mortality rate and concerns to public health [40]. HPAI has spread rapidly worldwide since the outbreak of the H5N1 virus in Asia in 2003, becoming a topic of global importance [3]. Since 2005, HPAI infections have occurred in 105 countries [56]. Wild birds are reservoirs of the avian influenza virus, and their migration plays an essential role in the transmission of the virus [2, 9, 50]. In addition to the impact of HPAI, the potential for human transmission and the impact on international trade are also issues [7]. The economic impact of HPAI in the US in 2014–2015 was estimated at $3.3 billion [13]. HPAI is a serious threat to animal and public health.

In Japan, HPAI outbreaks continued sporadically after the outbreak in the winter of 2003–2004 [23]. HPAI outbreaks in Japan occur mainly in autumn and winter. In particular, the outbreak in the winter of 2022–2023 was the worst ever, with 84 cases occurring in 26 prefectures and resulting in over 17 million birds culled [24, 56]. All domestic birds raised on farms with confirmed HPAI infection will be culled in Japan. Although financial compensation is provided, the impact of the HPAI outbreak on poultry farms is significant.

Few detailed studies have examined the association between HPAI outbreaks and weather conditions. Biswas et al. [5] used time series models to explore the association between avian influenza A (H5N1) virus outbreaks and meteorological factors in Bangladesh. Liu et al. [21] also studied the association between some H5N1 virus outbreaks and climate conditions worldwide. In Japan, Arikawa et al. [4] examined the association between meteorological factors and the number of migratory waterfowl, which is one of the most common risk factors of HAPI outbreaks [20, 22, 25]. However, no studies directly examined the association between HPAI outbreaks and weather conditions in Japan.

This study aims to investigate the association between HPAI outbreaks and weather conditions in Japan. To this end, we examined the locations of HPAI outbreaks and weather conditions near the outbreaks from the database of the World Organisation for Animal Health and the Japan Meteorological Agency and examined the relationship using cluster analysis and multivariable analysis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

Data on HPAI outbreaks in Japan were collected from the World Animal Health Information System (WAHIS) (https://wahis.woah.org/) of the World Organisation for Animal Health. Considering the change in biosecurity to prevent HPAI infection from the early stage of HPAI outbreaks in Japan, for example, four times of amendments of the Act on the Prevention of Infectious Diseases in Livestock by the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries and the guidance on compliance with the Act published in October 2020, data on outbreaks from July 2020 to June 2023 were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Of the data collected, only data for which the epidemiological unit was farm were used in the analysis.

Highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) outbreaks in Japan on a weekly basis.

Weather condition data was collected from the Japan Meteorological Agency website (https://www.data.jma.go.jp/obd/stats/etrn/). Japan has approximately 1,300 Automated Meteorological Data Acquisition System (AMeDAS) sites. The following two types of data were collected from the AMeDAS closest to the location of each HPAI outbreak: 1) weather conditions from 4 weeks before to the day of the outbreak in the year of the HPAI outbreak (case data), and 2) weather conditions from 4 weeks before to the day of the outbreak in the year 1 to 3 years before the outbreak (control data). The period, 4 weeks, was decided on the previous report in Japan [15], which reported that the HPAI virus was introduced into farms 8.6–24.1 days before the notification of the disease. Cluster analysis of weather conditions

The variables were reduced by cluster analysis to prevent overfitting of the model. For each weather condition, the weekly mean, maximum, or minimum value was calculated for each week from 4 weeks before the event to the event week (Table 1). Histograms were used to check the skewness of the data, and log transformations were performed if necessary. Hierarchical clustering of the calculated statistics was performed using Ward’s method [49]. The optimal number of clusters was determined by the elbow method [44]. From each cluster, one variable was arbitrarily selected as representative based on easiness of interpretation.

Case-control analysis

A matched case-control analysis was performed using the outbreak year as the case and 1 to 3 years before the outbreak as the control. The presence or absence of an HPAI outbreak was used as the response variable. For each variable selected by cluster analysis, calculated statistics of each weather condition for each week from 4 weeks before the event to the event week were used as separate explanatory variables. In addition to weather condition variables, the case farms’ latitude and longitude were also used as explanatory variables to consider the geographical location of the farms. A conditional logistic regression model was used with each HPAI outbreak as the strata. The model with the lowest AIC was selected by stepwise regression. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was drawn for the final model, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Prediction correctness of the final model was evaluated by logistic regression using the prediction correctness (correct or wrong) as the response variable and season of the case start date (January–March: spring; April–June: summer; July-September: autumn; October–December: winter), latitude, and longitude as explanatory variable. The significance level was set at P=0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 [28] with the following packages: cowplot [55], dendextend [10], factoextra [19], flextable [11], ggdendro [8], ggsci [57], gtsummary [36], httr [51], jpndistrict [46], kokudosuuchi [60], MASS [47], pacman [30], readxl [54], rmapshaper [41], ROCR [35], rvest [52], sf [26, 27], spdep [27], survival [42, 43] and tidyverse [53].

RESULTS

Collected data

From July 2020 to June 2023, 174 HPAI outbreaks in Japan were reported to the WAHIS database, of which 169 cases had epidemiological units as farm. The remaining five cases without epidemiological units as farm were excluded from the following analysis. Data were collected from 38 AMeDAS sites for 676 time periods.

Cluster analysis

No skewness was observed in the calculated statistics. Cluster analysis showed that the variables were divided into five clusters (Fig. 2): the first was composed of variables related to atmospheric pressure; the second was composed of variables related to air temperature; the third was composed of variables related to rainfall, cloudiness and relative humidity; the fourth was composed of variables related to wind speed; and the fifth was composed of sunlight hours, snowfall-related variables, the number of days with temperatures over 25°C, and the number of thunder days. From each cluster, atmospheric pressure, average air temperature, total rainfall, average wind speed, and sunlight hours were selected as representative variables, respectively.

Case-control analysis

A summary of the final model is shown in Table 2. The variables significantly associated with the HPAI outbreak were average air temperature two and three weeks before, lower average wind speed four weeks before, and sunlight hours two and four weeks before. The AUC calculated from the ROC curve (Supplementary Fig. 1) of the final model was 0.740. No temporal or geographic trends were observed in the prediction correctness of the final model (Supplementary Figs. 2–4; Supplementary Table 1).

DISCUSSION

In this study, the cluster analysis and the conditional logistic regression model were used to determine the weather conditions associated with the occurrence of HPAI outbreaks in Japan. Higher temperatures, lower wind speeds, and longer sunlight hour were significantly related to HPAI outbreaks. These weather conditions two to four weeks prior were associated with HAPI outbreaks.

Higher average air temperatures were significantly associated with the probability of HPAI outbreaks. For example, the hazard ratio for Temperature_mean_2 is 1.152 (Table 2), indicating that probability of an outbreak of HPAI increases 1.152 times as the average air temperature two weeks prior increases by 1°C. In Japan, HPAI outbreaks occurred mainly in winter [32, 37, 58], and the result of the study suggested that higher air temperatures in winter increase the probability of an HPAI Outbreak in Japan. It has been reported that avian influenza A (H5N1) virus outbreaks in wild birds in Europe were spatially and temporally associated with maximum surface air temperatures [29]. Similarly, a study reported that H5N1 virus outbreaks in poultry increased as temperatures increased [33]. It has also been reported that areas with HPAI outbreaks had higher surface temperatures [14]. Si et al. [34] reported that H5N1 virus outbreaks in Europe were associated with increased minimum temperatures in January. On the other hand, some reports suggest an association between lower temperatures and outbreaks of H5N1 virus, as reported by Biswas et al. [5]. There is also a report of a large temperature drop just before the H5N1 virus outbreak [21]. However, this report only provides a descriptive analysis and no statistical analysis. In Japan, a higher average daily maximum temperature was reported to decrease the number of migratory waterfowl, natural hosts of HPAI [4]. In this controversy about the relationship between temperature and HPAI outbreaks, this study supports a positive relationship between them.

Lower average wind speed was significantly associated with the HPAI outbreaks. The relationship between wind speed and HPAI outbreaks is controversial. Jonges et al. [18] demonstrated that the wind spread particulate matters, such as manure contaminated with the avian influenza virus, into the environment. In India, an academic report reported that avian influenza outbreaks became more common in winter with low wind speed enhancing survivability of virus in environment [38]. For human influenza, there is a report that low wind speed was one of the contributors to increased cases [59]. On the other hand, Ssematimba et al. [39] modeled the wind-borne spread of the HPAI virus in the Netherlands in 2003 but found that wind alone was insufficient to spread the virus. A study reported that wind speed is related to transmission intensity in HPAI outbreak, though it describes an association between wind speed and transmission intensity but not with the outbreak itself [1]. As described above, the association between wind speed and the HPAI outbreak is controversial, but in this study, lower average wind speed was significantly associated with the HPAI outbreak.

Sunlight hour was significantly associated with the HPAI outbreaks. It has been reported that in Japan, the mean value of global solar radiation increased the number of migratory waterfowl, which are natural hosts of HPAI [4]. Sunlight affects the migration and distribution of migratory birds [12, 45]. One possible explanation is that longer hours of sunlight may be related to HPAI occurrence through an increase in the number of wild birds that are natural hosts of the virus. A study also suggested an association between decreased cloud cover and avian influenza A (H5N1) virus outbreaks in Bangladesh [5]. This report suggested that a decrease in cloud cover reduced relative humidity and air temperature, which may increase the H5N1 virus transmission among poultry. On the other hand, for human influenza, it has been reported that a low UV index was correlated with human influenza outbreaks in Northern Europe [17]. In the United States, low/high solar radiation correlated with low/high prevalence of human influenza [31]. This report stated that influenza viruses were inactivated by solar ultraviolet radiation. Taken together, sunlight affects the spread of the virus through migration bird activity but may also inactivate the virus.

In this paper, weather conditions two to four weeks prior were related to HPAI outbreaks. This result is consistent with a study by Hayama et al. [15] that examined the time window between the introduction of the virus into farms and the notification of HPAI outbreaks in the 2020–2021 winter outbreak in Japan. They found that the HPAI virus entered farms 14.0 (min: 8.6, max: 24.1) days before notification. Similar studies in other countries also suggest that the time from HPAI entry into a farm to detection takes approximately 1–3 weeks [6, 16, 48]. Thus, the 2–4 weeks before the outbreaks, when the HPAI was associated with weather conditions, was roughly consistent with the time frame in which the virus is thought to have entered farms.

The major limitation of this study is that the virus outbreaks in wild birds were not included in the analysis. As previously described, wild birds play an essential role in virus transmission. Outbreaks in wild birds were mainly detected by surveillance and might have been overlooked or delayed in detection. Overlook or delay in detection makes the analysis’s result inaccurate; thus, outbreak data of wild birds were not analyzed in this study. Additionally, within this paper, it was impossible to determine whether wild birds, the virus, or something else caused the HPAI-weather association. We can only make inferences about this by referring to other papers. Also, since this paper is a study in Japan, it may not be applicable to other countries. However, it could help clarify the relationship between HPAI-weather conditions.

This study is the first to clarify the relationship between HPAI occurrence and weather conditions in Japan. The results were consistent with a previous paper that examined the association between HPAI outbreaks and the introduction time window and the risk factor, the number of birds in the wild in Japan. More efficient prevention of HPAI may be achieved by paying attention to weather conditions.
