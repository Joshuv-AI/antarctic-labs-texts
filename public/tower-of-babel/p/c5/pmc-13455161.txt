The effects of probiotics duration and type on immunotherapy efficacy in non-small cell lung cancer with or without antibiotics
Authors: Wei-Chi Luo, Zi-Jian Huang, Shi-Qi Mei, Qiu-Yi Zhang, Zhi-Hong Chen, Ping-Ping Chen, Guo-Hua Huang, Fen Wang, Yu Deng, Hai-Yan Tu, Jin-Ji Yang, Chong-Rui Xu, Yan-Qing Le, Jing Li, Jian-Hua Chang, Yi-Long Wu, Qing Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients, antibiotics impair immunotherapy efficacy, while probiotics can enhance it. The relationship between the duration of probiotics and the restoration of immunotherapy efficacy compromised by antibiotics, as well as its potential to enhance immunotherapy outcomes in antibiotic-naive patients, remains unclear. Furthermore, more probiotic types and their mechanisms for improving immunotherapy efficacy need to be identified and elucidated.MethodsThis multicenter study enrolled wild-type NSCLC patients. Clinicopathological features were recorded, focusing on antibiotic and probiotic use and duration. Baseline fecal and blood samples were collected. Gut microbiota were analyzed via 16S rRNA sequencing, and serum metabolites were analyzed through untargeted metabolomics. Flow cytometry was used to explore potential mechanisms.Results524 wild-type NSCLC patients were enrolled in the study, with fecal samples collected from 22 patients. Patients receiving antibiotics alone exhibited poorer PFS and OS. For those receiving antibiotics, the use of probiotics, regardless of duration, led to improved PFS and OS, which were comparable to those of patients in the No antibiotics + No probiotics group. Among the patients who did not receive antibiotics, those who were treated with probiotics for more than 15 days showed improved PFS. Patients with enriched gut microbiota in un-f-Bifidobacterium and roseburia showed better PFS. The frequency of peripheral Th cells was positively associated with the abundance of un-f-Bifidobacterium. There was also a positive correlation between o-Methoxyphenyl sulfate and the gut microbiota that exhibited better immunotherapy efficacy.ConclusionsAntibiotics weaken immunotherapy efficacy in wild-type NSCLC patients. Probiotics, regardless of duration, can restore it. For antibiotic-naive patients, taking probiotics over 15 days can improve immunotherapy efficacy. Certain probiotics, including un-f-Bifidobacterium and Roseburia, may be potential candidates for improving the efficacy of immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08580-6.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Gut microbiota, Metabolomics, Antibiotics, Probiotics

Background

In the past decade, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have exhibited substantial clinical efficacy as first-line standard treatment for patients with wild-type advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1–4]. However, despite the survival benefits conferred by ICIs, the goal of transforming lung cancer into a chronic, manageable disease has yet to be achieved. Some concomitant medications, particularly antibiotics, can impair the efficacy of immunotherapy [5–12]. In clinical practice, patients with lung cancer are prone to pneumonia, and when an infection occurs, the use of antibiotics becomes unavoidable. This presents a challenge: how to restore, even enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy while controlling infections. While for NSCLC patients without antibiotics, there is also an urgent need to find ways to enhance the efficacy of their immunotherapy.

Probiotics may serve as a potential approach. Takada et al. found that patients who took probiotics had longer median progression-free survival (PFS) (hazard ratio (HR): 1.73; 95% confidence interval (95%CI): 1.42–2.11; p = 0.0229) [13]. However, our previous research found that probiotics could not enhance the immunotherapy efficacy of NSCLC [12]. These discrepancies suggest that the effect of probiotics may depend on microbial functions rather than tadministration of probiotics.

A key mechanism by which the gut microbiota regulates antitumor response is through its secreted metabolites [14]. Short-chain fatty acids, tryptophan metabolites, bile acids, and inosine have been found to be closely associated with the efficacy of immunotherapy. These metabolites mainly exert their effects by regulating immune cells. At the cellular level, multiple cells participate in this process, including CD8+ T cells, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, and neutrophils. Among these, CD8+ T cells are the key effector cells mediating antitumor immunity. Study [14] has shown that microbiota-derived metabolites can significantly improve responses to immunotherapy by regulating the stemness maintenance, effector function differentiation, and tumor infiltration capacity of CD8+ T cells. For examples, Routy et al. found that Akkermansia muciniphila enhances the efficacy of ICIs in an interleukin-12-dependent manner by recruiting CCR9+CXCR3+CD4+ T cell [15]. Bifidobacterium bifidum modulated the immunological background by augmenting the production of interferon-γ, likely facilitated by an enhanced synthesis of immune-stimulating molecules and metabolites [16]. In the phase I clinical trial, the PFS of renal cancer patients treated with nivolumab-ipilimumab combined with CBM588 was longer than that of patients who did not receive CBM588 (12.7 months versus 2.5 months, HR: 0.15, 95% CI 0.05–0.47, p = 0.001) [17]. Therefore, integrating analyses of the gut microbiota, metabolites, and immune cells will help to determine whether probiotics exert beneficial effects on immunotherapy efficacy and to explore the potential mechanisms underlying such effects.

However, for probiotics to exert their functions and enhance immunotherapy efficacy, they need sufficient time to colonize the gut. Therefore, the duration of probiotic use may be a critical factor influencing their effectiveness. However, few studies have addressed them. Thus, this study enrolled patients with wild-type NSCLC from three medical centers and recorded the use and duration of antibiotics and probiotics to assess the role of probiotics in lung cancer patients with or without antibiotics during immunotherapy. The gut microbiota were characterized using 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) sequencing to find other potential probiotics. Furthermore, serum metabolites and flow cytometry was used to explore potential mechanisms by which different probiotics enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in wild-type NSCLC patients.

Method

Participants

In this retrospective study, the clinical data from patients treated at three medical institutions: Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, Cancer Hospital Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Shenzhen Center, and Peking University Shenzhen Hospital from December 2017, to July 2022 were recorded. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Diagnosed as advanced and local advanced NSCLC; (2) Age ≥ 18 years; (3) First-line therapy should incorporate ICIs. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Diagnosed as small cell lung cancer; (2) The patient is positive for driver gene mutations. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, No. GDREC2016175H (R2). The research methodology adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical informations

Patient demographics, including age, gender, smoking status, pathology, stage of disease, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS), the frequency of immune cells were documented. We recorded the use of antibiotics and probiotics. The use of antibiotics was defined as within 2 months before and after the first ICI administration. The use of probiotics was defined as the period from 2 months before the first ICI administration to the entire duration of immunotherapy. Tumor responses were evaluated by an independent oncologist following the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1, which classified responses into complete response (CR, total disappearance of tumors), partial response (PR, a reduction of ≥30% in the sum of diameters from baseline), progressive disease (PD, an increase of ≥20% in the sum of the smallest diameters), or stable disease (SD, conditions not meeting CR, PR, or PD criteria). Patients with PR or CR were considered responders; patients with PD or SD were considered non-responders. PFS was defined as the duration from the initiation of immunotherapy to the onset of disease progression, while overall survival (OS) was measured from the start of immunotherapy to death from any cause.

Samples collection

Fecal samples were collected in container with Effcgut solution [18] stored at room temperature before the first ICI injection. Serum sample was collected in an empty container and immediately frozen at − 80 °C. Informed consent was obtained from participants, ensuring their awareness and voluntary participation in the study.

Gut microbiota analysis via 16S rRNA sequencing

Approximately 0.25 g of fecal samples underwent deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction with the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA). DNA quality (concentration, purity, and integrity via 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis) was verified. Qualified samples had their 16S rRNA genes amplified with specific primers and barcodes. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products were evaluated on 1.5% agarose gel, selecting those with a bright main band between 400-450bp. PCR products were mixed and purified using the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific). Sequencing libraries were prepared with the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Kit (Illumina), indexed, and quality-checked on Qubit 2.0 and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Finally, the library was sequenced on an Illumina MiniSeq, generating 150 bp paired-end reads.

Raw paired-end reads were assembled with flash and cleaned by removing primers and low-quality reads using cutadapt. Clean tags underwent chimera checking and operational Taxonomic Unit (OTU) clustering with usearch, including demultiplexing, 97% similarity clustering, and chimera checking with UCHIME. Representative sequences were generated, singletons removed, and the final OTU table created. OTU sequences were aligned to the silva_132_97_16S database for taxonomic classification by RDP, and phylogenetic trees were constructed with FastTree.

Serum metabolites analyses via UHPLC‑MS

100 μL of sample was taken, mixed with 400 μL of extraction solution (MeOH:ACN, 1:1(v/v)), the extraction solution contain deuterated internal standards, the mixed solution were vortexed for 30 s, sonicated for 10 min in 4 °C water bath, and incubated for 1 h at −40 °C to precipitate proteins. Then the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (RCF = 13800(×g),R = 8.6 cm) for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a fresh glass vial for analysis. The quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing an equal aliquot of the supernatant of samples.

Mass spectrometry (MS) analyses were performed using an UHPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) coupled to Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Orbitrap MS, Thermo). The mobile phase consisted of 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia hydroxide in water（pH = 9.75）(A) and acetonitrile (B). The auto-sampler temperature was 4 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer was used for its ability to acquire MS/MS spectra on information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode in the control of the acquisition software (Xcalibur, Thermo). In this mode, the acquisition software continuously evaluates the full scan MS spectrum. The ESI source conditions were set as following: sheath gas flow rate as 50 Arb, Aux gas flow rate as 15 Arb, capillary temperature 320 °C, full MS resolution as 60,000, MS/MS resolution as 15,000, collision energy: SNCE 20/30/40, spray voltage as 3.8 kV (positive) or −3.4 kV (negative), respectively.

Regarding data processing, the raw data were converted to the mzXML format using ProteoWizard and processed with an in-house program, which was developed using R and based on XCMS, for peak detection, extraction, alignment, and integration. Then an in-house MS2 database was applied in metabolite annotation. The cutoff for annotation was set at 0.3. Then, the missing values were filled up by half of the minimum value. Also, internal standard normalization method was employed in this data analysis. The final dataset containing the information of peak number, sample name and normalized peak area was imported to SIMCA16.0.2 software package (Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB, Umea, Sweden) for multivariate analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software (R version 4.2.3, http://www.R-project.org/.). For demographic characteristics, categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as applicable. Continuous variables were assessed through Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on their distribution. Survival times were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis, with differences between groups tested using the log-rank test. Clinical factors with a p value < 0.1 in the univariate analysis, as well as those considered clinically important, were then included in the multivariable Cox regression model to identify independent prognostic factors. We used the corr.test function in the R package ‘psych’ to assess correlations between microbial taxa and metabolites. All tests conducted were two-sided, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 524 patients with unresectable NSCLC treated with immunotherapy were included in this study (Supplemental Fig. 1). The detailed demographic information of the patients was presented in Supplemental Table 1. 28 patients (5.3%) received both antibiotics and probiotics, 70 patients (13.4%) received only probiotics, 80 patients (15.3%) received only antibiotics, and 346 patients (66.0%) did not receive either antibiotics or probiotics. The most common antibiotic regimens were β-lactam antibiotics alone (66/108, 61.1%), a combination of β-lactam antibiotics and fluoroquinolones (27/108, 25.0%), and fluoroquinolones alone (15/108, 13.9%). More than half of the patients (61/108, 56.5%) received antibiotics for more than three days, and the most common indication was pulmonary infection. Details on probiotic use were provided in Supplemental Table 2. Most of them are male (440/524, 84.0%) and were diagnosed at stage IV (453/524, 86.5%). The majority of patients had not brain metastases (435/524, 83.0%) and received combination immunotherapy (419/524, 79.9%). More than half of the patients were smokers (342/524, 65.3%) and had been diagnosed with non-squamous cell cancer (390/524, 74.4%). 56.7% patients (297/524) were PD-L1 positive (PD-L1 ≥ 50%, 151/524, 28.8%; 1–49%, 146/524, 27.9%). Nearly all patients (95.4%, 500/524) were in good condition.

The groups are as follows: antibiotics + no probiotics, antibiotics + 1-15d probiotics, antibiotics + >15d probiotics, no antibiotics + no probiotics, no antibiotics + 1-15d probiotics, no antibiotics + >15d probiotics. In Univariate Cox regression analysis (Tables 1 and 2), excluding the effect of probiotics, the use of antibiotics was associated with poorer PFS (PFS: 7.9 vs. 10.9 months, HR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.26–2.28, p < 0.001) and OS (OS: 16.0 vs. 34.0 months, HR = 2.46, 95% CI: 1.75 - 3.46, p < 0.001). In patients treated with antibiotics, the use of probiotics improved PFS, regardless of the duration of probiotics (1-15d probiotics: PFS: 11.0 vs. 7.9 months, HR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.23 - 0.95, p = 0.035; >15d probiotics: PFS: 16.3 vs. 7.9 months, HR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.17 - 0.93, p = 0.033). The OS of the group with probiotics for more than 15 days was improved (OS: not reached vs. 16.0 months, HR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.04 - 0.61, p = 0.008). The PFS and OS of patients receiving antibiotics + 1-15d probiotics or receiving antibiotics + >15d probiotics were comparable to those not treated with antibiotics and probiotics. In antibiotic-naive NSCLC patients, only the use of probiotics for more than 15 days improved PFS (PFS: 26.3 vs. 10.9 months, HR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.24 - 0.77, p = 0.005). However, it did not result in an improvement in OS. Of note, These findings were confirmed in patients receiving combination immunotherapy (Supplemental Fig. 2 and 3).

Multivariate analysis confirmed the results from univariate Cox regression analysis. Antibiotics were worsen independent factors for PFS (HR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.33 - 2.44, p < 0.001) and OS (HR = 2.47, 95% CI: 1.76 - 3.48, p < 0.001). For patients receiving antibiotics, the use of probiotics, regardless of the duration of probiotic use, improved PFS (1-15d probiotics: HR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.23 - 0.97, p = 0.041; >15d probiotics: HR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.15 - 0.81, p = 0.015). The OS of the group with probiotics for more than 15 days was improved (HR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.03 - 0.50, p = 0.004). In patients without receiving antibiotics, only the use of probiotics for more than 15 days improved PFS (HR = 0.46, 95% CI:0.26 - 0.84, p = 0.011). However, it did not result in an improvement in OS. Other independent favorable factors included PD-L1 ≥ 50% for PFS, and PS ≤ 1 for OS.

Gut microbiota can improve the efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with advanced wild-type NSCLC

Fecal samples were collected from 22 advanced wild-type NSCLC patients. Based on the efficacy of immunotherapy, the patients were divided into responder (R) and non-responder (NR). The baseline clinicopathological characteristics were comparable between R and NR (Supplemental table 3).

To explore the characteristic gut microbiota that can improve the immunotherapy efficacy of patients with wild-type NSCLC, we compared the gut microbiota differences between R and NR using 16s rRNA sequencing. The results showed that the α-diversity of the R was higher than that of the NR, but the difference was not significant (Shannon index, p = 0.091; Simpson index, p = 0.32). There was also no significant difference in β-diversity between them (Supplemental Fig. 4). At the genus level, there were eight differentially abundant gut microbiota between the R group and the NR group. Among them, six gut microbiota were enriched in the R group, including Anaerostipes, Lachnospiraceae_UCG_008, Roseburia, Butyricicoccus, [Eubacterium]_eligens_group, and un_f_Bifidobacteriaceae. Two gut microbiota were enriched in the NR group, including Abiotrophia and Prevotella_6 (Fig. 3A).

Based on the detection of un_f_Bifidobacteriaceae in fecal samples at baseline, patients were divided into the Bifido group and the non-Bifido group. Compared to the non-Bifido group, patients in the Bifido group had a significantly longer PFS (9.9 months vs. 5.4 months, p = 0.038), but there was no significant difference in OS (not reached vs. 14.7 months, p = 0.404) between the two groups (Fig. 4A and B). Based on the abundance of gut microbiota in fecal samples at baseline, Roseburiahigh group had improved PFS (10.4 months vs. 5.1 months, p = 0.037) (Fig. 4C and D). The associations between the abundance of the remaining gut microbiota and PFS and OS were shown in Supplementary Figs 5 and 6, respectively.

The plasma metabolites were associated with the efficacy of immunotherapy for advanced wild-type NSCLC patients

Blood samples were collected from 14 advanced wild-type NSCLC patients. To explore the plasma metabolites associated with the efficacy of immunotherapy in advanced wild-type NSCLC patients, we compared the differential metabolites between the R group and NR group. The results showed that there were 571 differential metabolites between the two groups, of which 15 differential metabolites could be annotated. The R group was enriched in mannitol, gamma-glutamylleucine, citric acid, among others, while the NR group was enriched in valyl-valine, valyl-lysine, among others (Fig. 3B and C).

The relationship between gut microbiota, plasma metabolites, and immune cells

To explore the possible mechanism of gut microbiota affecting the efficacy of immunotherapy in advanced wild-type NSCLC patients, we conducted a correlation analysis of gut microbiota, plasma metabolites, and immune cells. Roseburia exhibits a significant positive correlation with CD4+CD25+ T-cells. Un_f_Bifidobacteriaceae displays a notable positive correlation with Th cells, while Anaerostipes has a significant positive association with CD3+ T-cells (Fig. 3D). We have discovered that in Group R, there is a significant positive correlation between the upregulated gut microbiota and o-Methoxyphenyl sulfate (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This multicenter study, for the first time, explored the impact of probiotics duration on immunotherapy efficacy in wild-type NSCLC patients, stratified by the use of antibiotics. The findings demonstrated that antibiotics weakened the efficacy of immunotherapy, whereas probiotics supplementation restored the survival benefits impaired by antibiotics. Notably, at least 15 days of probiotics supplementation was required to enhance immunotherapy efficacy in antibiotics-naive patients. Un-f-Bifidobacterium and Roseburia may be potential candidates for enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy. To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the effect of probiotics duration on immunotherapy efficacy in both antibiotic-naive patients and those who have been treated with antibiotics.

Our research is consistent with previous research results, confirming that the use of antibiotics weakened the efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with wild-type NSCLC. Antibiotics weaken the efficacy of immunotherapy may stem from two reasons. On one hand, infection-related antibiotics use may suggest poor health; however, in our study, 95.4% patients had a favorable condition (PS score ≤ 1), indicating that this is not the primary factor. On the other hand, the use of antibiotics may induce gut microbiota dysbiosis. Previous studies have shown that the use of antibiotics altered the colonization pattern of the gut microbiota, including the shift of Enterococcus, which downregulates MAdCAM-1, facilitating α4β7+CD4+ Treg17 cell migration to tumors, leading to immune escape and reduced immunotherapy efficacy [19]. It seems that antibiotics-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis is the main reason for the impairment in the efficacy of immunotherapy. In clinical practice, the use of antibiotics is essential when patients are faced with infections such as pneumonia. However, while antibiotics are effective in treating infections in patients with wild-type NSCLC, they concurrently weaken the efficacy of immunotherapy. The issue of how to restore immunotherapy efficacy compromised by antibiotics in wild-type NSCLC patients has emerged as an unsolved clinical concern. The use of probiotics to balance gut microbiota presents a promising approach to addressing this issue. Previous research reported that clostridium butyricum could enhance immunotherapy efficacy in patients with wild-type NSCLC [20]. Preclinical studies have revealed that the use of clostridium butyricum in colorectal cancer cells results in MYC degradation through increased proteasome-mediated ubiquitination, thereby enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy [21]. Sivan et al. [22]. discovered that the combination of an immune checkpoint inhibitor with Bifidobacterium significantly inhibited tumor growth. Bifidobacterium may enhance dendritic cell function, which in turn mediates the accumulation and activation of CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment, thereby exerting an antitumor effect. In our study, the most used probiotic among patients was a live bacteria tablet containing Streptococcus, Clostridium butyricum and bacillus mesentericus. Additionally, this live bacteria tablet promoted the growth of Bifidobacterium. We found that the use of probiotics can restore the efficacy compromised by antibiotics, and their survival outcomes were comparable to those of patients who had not received antibiotics. This discovery offers novel insights and approaches for addressing the conflict between antibiotics and immunotherapy efficacy. In antibiotic-exposed patients, gut microbiota diversity is substantially reduced, which may create numerous vacant ecological niches [23]. This ecological disruption might allow exogenous probiotics to colonize rapidly within a short time. Consistently, a previous study has shown that probiotic colonization levels in antibiotic-exposed mice tended to be higher than those in antibiotic-naive mice [24]. This explains why any duration of probiotic use suffices to restore ICI efficacy in this population.

Antibiotic-naive NSCLC patients need to take probiotics for more than 15 days to enhance immunotherapy efficacy. This finding may be related to microbial colonization and its functions. Probiotic colonization is crucial for the efficacy of immunotherapy, being linked to the characteristics of the probiotics and the state of the gut. Studies [25] have shown that nearly 14% of the annotated genes in Bifidobacterium encode enzymes involved in carbohydrate metabolism, providing a foundation for them to obtain the nutritional energy required for survival in the intestine. In addition, Bifidobacterium can produce extracellular polysaccharides, forming a thick biofilm on the surface of intestinal tissues [26]. This not only helps it resist the effects of intestinal mucus and epithelial renewal but also prevents it from being washed away, thereby effectively promoting microbial colonization. It is noteworthy that different gut microbiota have varying colonization mechanisms, leading to differences in their colonization onset times. For example, previous study [27] reports that continuous supplementation of Clostridium butyricum for seven days can increase its colonization level; whereas other research indicates that some probiotics are unable to establish colonization even after ten days [28]. This variability may be related to probiotic type, metabolic characteristics, and intestinal environment. The FMT-LUMINate trial provided key evidence informing this time threshold [29]. In that study, responders exhibited post-FMT donor-like gut microbiome composition, suggesting that donor engraftment correlates with efficacy. Importantly, responders also showed marked depletion of baseline deleterious taxa, including Enterocloster citroniae, E. lavalensis and Clostridium innocuum. These observations imply that, in patients with an intact microbiome, probiotic-mediated ICI sensitization requires more than successful colonization; it also necessitates the suppression of resident immunosuppressive commensals. Given their ecological resilience, displacing these deleterious taxa without antibiotic pretreatment is inherently a slow competitive process, which likely accounts for the longer intervention duration required to achieve clinical benefit. A previous study has shown that in antibiotic-naïve mice, pre-treatment with probiotics for two weeks more effectively enhanced the efficacy of immunotherapy [30]. To our knowledge, we are the first to explore the application of probiotics in the immunotherapy efficacy of wild-type NSCLC patients who have used or not used antibiotics. This discovery not only provides a new perspective for us to deeply understand the mechanism of action of probiotics in immunotherapy but also offers valuable insights for future clinical practice.

Patients in R group had higher α diversity of gut microbiota, although there was no statistical difference. Jin et al. [31] found that the α diversity of gut microbiota is associated with better immunotherapy efficacy. This finding is consistent with our research results, suggesting the importance of αdiversity in immunotherapy. However, due to the small sample size of our study, there was no significant difference. We found that un_f_Bifidobacteriaceae and Roseburia were significantly enriched in R group, and patients patients harboring these microbiota responded better to immunotherapy. Matson et al. [32] found that Bifidobacteriaceae were enriched in R group in metastatic melanoma receiving immunotherapy. Lee et al. [16] indicated that Bifidobacterium bifidum was enriched in NSCLC patients who responded to immunotherapy, and specific strains within it enhanced INF-γ production by promoting the biosynthesis of immune-stimulating molecules and metabolites, thereby modulating the immune environment and improving the efficacy of immunotherapy. These findings are in line with our observations and further highlight the potential value of Bifidobacteriaceae in the field of immunotherapy. However, current research had focused on Bifidobacterium, while our results suggest that novel species within the Bifidobacteriaceae are also potential microbiota that might enhance immunotherapy efficacy. They are worthy of further exploration. Kang et al. [33] revealed that Roseburia intestinalis, by producing butyrate which binds to TLR5 on CD8 T cells to activate NF-kB signaling, enhances the efficacy of immunotherapy by inducing cytotoxic and immune-stimulating CD8 T cells, restoring gut barrier function, and suppressing tumour growth in mouse models of colorectal cancer. However, no research on the application of this microbiota in immunotherapy for lung cancer. To the best of our knowledge, no study has reported an association between o-Methoxyphenyl sulfate and the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy. Our findings provide a foundation for strategies aimed at enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy in NSCLC patients.

This study has several limitations. First, it is an observational study, and prospective studies are needed to validate the findings in the future. Second, the sample sizes for microbiome and metabolomics analyses were limited due to the retrospective design, and these exploratory findings warrant validation in future prospective cohorts. Third, although we have identified the potential of un_f_Bifidobacteriaceae and Roseburia to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in NSCLC patients, further in vivo and in vitro validation experiments are required.

Conclusion

Antibiotics weaken immunotherapy efficacy in wild-type NSCLC patients. Probiotics, regardless of duration, can restore it. For antibiotic-naive patients, taking probiotics over 15 days can improve immunotherapy efficacy. Certain probiotics, including un-f-Bifidobacterium and Roseburia, may be potential candidates for improving the efficacy of immunotherapy. O-Methoxyphenyl sulfate and Th cells may provide new research avenues for future mechanism studies.
