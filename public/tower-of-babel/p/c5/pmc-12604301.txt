Physician adherence to heart failure with reduced ejection fraction treatment guidelines: a cross-sectional study in Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Tilikalem Fenta Workineh, Dejen Tsegaye Alem, Ashagrie Sharew Iyassu, Wubet Hunegnaw, Bekele Getenet Tiruneh, Tadele Fentabel Anagaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure is currently a major public health challenge worldwide, placing a significant burden on healthcare systems. Implementing guideline-directed medical therapies for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction is crucial to alleviating this burden; however, such practices are not optimally practiced in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aimed to assess physicians’ adherence to guideline-directed therapy for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction at Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital, northwest Ethiopia.MethodsAn institution-based retrospective cross-sectional study design was employed among 278 adult patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction who were treated at Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital between December 1, 2023, to January 10, 2024. Data were extracted from patients’ medical records using a pre-developed checklist. Data entry was performed in EpiData version 3.1 and exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed, and an ordinal logistic regression model was used to evaluate the association between independent variables and the dependent variable.ResultsThe mean age of the study participants was 58.4 ± 14 years, and 50.4% were male. The global class adherence score was categorized as good in 70.9% of patients, moderate in 21.6%, and poor in 8%. The proportion of patients who achieved the target dose was 25.5% for ACEIs, 7.6% for BBs, and 15.8% for MRAs. Longer duration of heart failure (AOR = 0.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.06, p < 0.023), cardiomyopathy as the underlying cause of heart failure (AOR = 3.02, 95% CI: 1.52–6.03, p < 0.002), and lower baseline LVEF (AOR = 5.28, 95% CI: 2.39–11.68, p < 0.001) were independent factors that increase physician’s guideline global class adherence.ConclusionOverall, physician adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy for patients with HFrEF in this study did not address the expected AHA and ESC recommendations. Only 70.5% of patients were prescribed all the indicated drugs. Underdosing of all guideline-directed medications was observed, especially the BB dose was very suboptimal.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05291-6.

Introduction

According to the 2021 universal definition of heart failure (HF), it is a clinical syndrome characterized by key symptoms and/or signs resulting from structural or functional cardiac abnormalities. This is supported by elevated biomarkers, such as natriuretic peptide levels, and/or objective evidence of pulmonary or systemic congestion [1]. HF is classified into four subtypes: heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF), heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), and heart failure with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF) [2].

HFrEF, defined as a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 40%, accounts for nearly half of all HF cases [3–5]. Its prevalence is expected to rise due to therapeutic advances, an aging population, and increasing cardiovascular risk factors. In developing countries like Ethiopia, HFrEF constitutes 31.5% of cardiovascular diseases, reflecting its growing burden [6, 7]. Patients with HFrEF, treatments that target the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and the sympathetic nervous system, including ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), beta-blockers (BBs), and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), significantly lower the risk of hospitalizations, improve symptoms, and enhance survival rates.

Effective treatments targeting the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and the sympathetic nervous system, such as ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), BBs, and MRAs, have been proven to significantly reduce hospitalizations, alleviate symptoms, and improve survival rates. International guidelines strongly recommend these therapies for patients with HFrEF, highlighting the importance of reaching target doses to maximize their therapeutic benefits [8–12].

Despite the availability of these guidelines from organizations such as the American Heart Association (AHA) [2] and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) [13], adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy remains suboptimal [14]. Studies have shown inadequate implementation of these recommendations, especially with the use of BBs and achieving target doses [15, 16]. Global surveys reveal that physician adherence to evidence-based guidelines for HFR is often moderate to poor across various countries [17]. Other global studies, including the MAHLER survey and the HART trial, reveal that physician adherence to evidence-based guidelines for HFrEF is often moderate to poor [18–20]. In countries such as the United States [21–23], Korea [24], Indonesia [25], and South Africa [26], there is notable underuse of BBs and MRAs.

Studies on actual physician adherence are rare in Africa. According to a heart failure survey done in Sub-Saharan Africa, the majority of patients were treated with RAAS blockers rather than β-blockers at discharge [21]. Low rates of BBs and MRA prescriptions have been shown in studies in South Africa [22, 23]. A study from Nigeria demonstrated that physician adherence to evidence-based anti-HF medication classes was variable [24]. Similarly, another study in Sub-Saharan Africa indicated that many HFrEF patients were discharged with RAAS blockers instead of BBs, highlighting low prescription rates for BBs and MRAs in South Africa and Nigeria [25].

In Ethiopia, adherence to international heart failure guidelines, particularly for HFrEF, remains low, as evidenced by studies from Black Lion Specialized Hospital [26]. Factors contributing to this include patient-related issues (e.g., age, comorbidities, drug intolerance), physician-related barriers (e.g., focus on symptom relief, fear of side effects, lack of awareness), and systemic challenges such as affordability and limited healthcare access [27, 28].

Methods

Study area and period

The study was conducted at Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital (TGSH) in Bahir Dar, Ethiopia, from December 1, 2023, to January 10, 2024. The city is located approximately 578 km (360 miles) northwest of Addis Ababa. TGSH is situated about 10 km south of the city center and 23 km from the famous Blue Nile Falls, locally known as Tis Abay (Smoke of Fire). This tertiary university teaching hospital has a total capacity of 500 beds, with 72 beds dedicated to adult medical patients. The hospital serves as a referral center for patients across the Amhara region and Benishangul Gumuz, providing both outpatient and inpatient care across all major departments. The Cardiology Unit, housed within the Department of Internal Medicine, is staffed by cardiologists, internists, and internal medicine residents. It provides outpatient services to approximately 2,000 patients with various cardiac conditions.

Study design

An institution-based retrospective cross-sectional study design was employed to evaluate physicians’ adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy among patients with HFrEF attending follow-up at the adult cardiac referral clinic of TGSH.

Populations

All registered patients with HFrEF at the adult cardiac referral clinic of TGSH served as the source population. The study population comprised patients with HFrEF who had follow-up visits at the adult cardiac referral clinic in TGSH between 2021 and 2023 and met the inclusion criteria.

Eligibility criteria

Patients with HFrEF were included in the study if they met the following criteria: age > 18 years, attended at least four regular follow-up visits at the cardiac referral clinic, had impaired left ventricular function with an ejection fraction (EF) < 40% as determined by a recent echocardiogram performed by a cardiologist within the past two years, and were receiving heart failure medications prescribed by a physician. Patients aged < 18 years, those with fewer than four regular follow-up visits, those whose echocardiography results were not updated within the last two years, and those whose baseline echocardiography was performed by someone other than a cardiologist were excluded from the study.

Study variables

The dependent variable is physicians’ adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy. The independent variables included: Socio-demographic factors: Age, sex, and residence; Clinical characteristics: Duration of heart failure, time since the last heart failure hospitalization, presence of comorbidities, and the underlying cause of heart failure; and Laboratory and echocardiography data: Serum creatinine levels and left ventricular ejection fraction (See Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic presentation of conceptual framework showing adherence of physicians to guideline-directed therapy for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction at northwest comprehensive specialized hospital, Amhara, Ethiopia, 2024

Data collection instruments and procedures

The questionnaire was developed by reviewing various relevant literature and previous similar studies [24, 26, 29, 30]. The reliability of the tool was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which yielded a value of 0.79. To ensure its comprehensiveness and relevance to the study objectives. To facilitate data collection, a structured data retrieval form (checklist) in English was designed, capturing essential information related to physicians’ adherence to the guideline and the clinical characteristics of patients. Data were extracted from patients’ medical records by two trained medical residents during the designated study period. The residents conducted the data collection process during regular working hours, ensuring consistency and accuracy in capturing the necessary information. The use of a structured checklist allowed for systematic and standardized data retrieval, minimizing potential bias and ensuring reliability in the collected data.

Data quality assurance

To ensure the quality of data, the data collectors were thoroughly oriented on the data collection tool and its application before the study. This orientation included detailed instructions on how to use the structured checklist and how to extract accurate and reliable information from patient records. Throughout the data collection process, the data collectors were closely supervised to maintain consistency and adherence to the study checklists. Additionally, the principal investigator conducted daily checks on the collected data for completeness, accuracy, and consistency. Any discrepancies or missing information were promptly addressed to prevent errors from accumulating and to ensure the integrity of the data.

A pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample size at Felege Gion Referral Hospital to assess the functionality of the data collection tool and identify any potential issues before the main study. The pretest helped refine the tool, making sure it was clear and effective in capturing the required data. Any adjustments to the tool were made based on the findings from the pretest, further enhancing the reliability of the data collection process.

Data processing and analysis

Once all the necessary data were collected, the investigators coded the data using a pre-arranged coding sheet. Data entry and cleaning were performed using EpiData version 3.1, ensuring accuracy and consistency. Afterward, the cleaned data were exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the data. Continuous variables were analyzed using the mean and standard deviation, while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages.

An ordinal logistic regression model analysis was done to see the association between the dependent and independent variables. After doing an independent variable with a dependent variable with a 95% confidence interval, those who had a p-value < 0.25 were taken to be analyzed with multivariable ordinal logistic regression to see predictors of global class adherence (to compute p-value and adjusted odds ratio at 95% confidence interval), where a p-value of less than 0.05 was taken statically significant. The model’s fitness was tested using the likelihood ratio chi-square test, and the p-value was 0.00. VIF value for all numeric independent variables was < 10, P-value was 0.24 on the test of parallel line, no outliers observed. Finally, the results were presented by text, tables, bar graphs, and pie charts.

Operational definitions

The global guideline adherence score was constructed based on physicians’ adherence to the guidelines regarding the following four classes of medications: ACEIs, ARBs, BBs, and MRAs. The adherence score was the ratio of the treatment prescribed to the treatment that should theoretically have been prescribed. The theoretical treatment score was calculated for every patient, considering treatment eligibility criteria and the existence of contraindications to drugs or treatments based on international guidelines on the management of HF. The score was calculated for each patient by summing the points attributed as follows: one point for non-prescription in the absence of specified indications, and one point each for the use of ACEIs or ARBs, BBs, MRAs (if indicated), and non-administration of recommended drugs because of specific contraindications or intolerance was scored as adherence to guidelines [26]. The score ranges from 0 (very poor) to 1(excellent), and three levels of adherence were defined:

Results

A total of 278 patient charts were reviewed for this study. The mean age of the patients was 58.37 ± 14 years across the entire study population. There was a slight male predominance, with 50.7% of the patients being male. The average duration of HF among the patients was 19 ± 12 years and months. Regarding functional classification, nearly half of patients (51.8%) were categorized in New York Heart Association (NYHA) class II, followed by 38.8% in class III. A smaller proportion of patients were in NYHA class IV (7.6%), while only 1.8% of patients were in class I. The underlying etiology of heart failure was ischemic in 54% of cases, cardiomyopathy in 43.9%, and valvular in 2.2%. The most common comorbidities observed were hypertension (19.4%), atrial fibrillation (19.1%), diabetes mellitus (9%), stroke (7.2%), asthma/COPD (6.5%), and chronic kidney disease (2.5%) (see Table 1).

The adherence score of physicians to guideline-directed medical therapy was good for 70.9% of the overall study population (95% CI: 0.65–0.76), moderate for 21.6%, and poor for 7.6% (see Fig. 6).Fig. 6Percentage of global class adherence score of sampled patients with HFrEF on follow- up at northwest comprehensive specialized hospital, Amhara, Ethiopia, 2024

Factors associated with physicians’ adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy

Bivariate ordinal logistic regression analysis identified the following variables as being associated with physicians’ adherence to guideline directed medical therapy at a significance level of p < 0.25: age, duration of heart failure, time since the last heart failure hospitalization, presence of cardiometabolic comorbidities, underlying cause of heart disease, baseline serum creatinine, and baseline left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).

Multivariable ordinal logistic regression was then performed to assess how these variables independently influence physicians’ adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy while controlling for potential confounders. In the multivariable ordinal logistic regression, duration of heart failure, time since last hospitalization, underlying cause of heart failure, and baseline LVEF were found to have a significant association with poor treatment outcome, with a p-value of < 0.05.

The odds of physicians prescribing all indicated medication were 1.03 times higher in patients with longer duration of heart failure than their counterparts (AOR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.00–1.06, p = 0.023). Similarly, study participants who were never hospitalized were 60% less likely to be on all indicated drugs than those who were recently hospitalized (AOR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.17–0.95, p = 0.037). From the possible underlying causes of heart failure, the odds of good physician guideline adherence were 3.06 times higher in patients with cardiomyopathy than those with coronary artery disease (AOR = 3.06, 95% CI: 1.54–6.08, p = 0.002). Also, the odds of guideline adherence were 4.96 times higher in patients with a baseline left ventricular ejection fraction less than 30% compared to those with more than 30% (AOR = 4.96, 95% CI: 2.37–10.38, p = 0.001) (see Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, the proportion of patients who achieved the target dose was 25.2% for ACEIs, 7.6% for BBs, and 15.8% for MRA. These findings are comparable to those reported in studies from Turkey (24.4%) [31] and the European registry (27%) [29]. However, the rates observed in our study are notably higher than those reported at Black Lion Specialized Hospital, where target dose attainment was 7.6% for ACEIs, 0.8% for BB, and 1% for MRA [26]. This variation may be explained by several factors, including differences in sample size (364 in the current study versus 278 in the Black Lion study), the study period (2023/24 versus 2018), and the availability of health insurance in our study setting, which may have improved patients’ access to prescribed medications.

On the other hand, the target dose achievement rates in this study are lower than those reported in a study from the United States, where 66.7% of patients reached the target dose [32]. This discrepancy may be partly attributed to differences in the clinical experience of healthcare providers. While the current study included residents, internists, and cardiologists, the U.S. study focused exclusively on cardiologists, whose specialized expertise may have contributed to the higher adherence to treatment guidelines. Furthermore, disparities in patients’ medication knowledge and the economic constraints prevalent in Ethiopia may have also played a role in the lower adherence rates observed in our setting.

The proportion of patients receiving the target dose of BB in our study (9.9%) is similar to that observed in a study from Turkey [31]. However, it is lower compared to studies conducted in the USA (13.6%) [32] Canada (67.5%) [33] and European registry (14.8%). This discrepancy may be attributed to factors such as physicians’ reluctance to escalate BB doses in the presence of multiple comorbidities and polypharmacy due to concerns about adverse effects. Additionally, the increased cost of imported medications, limited access to the recommended drugs, and the predominantly rural population in our study could have contributed to the lower rates. The absence of hospital-specific guidelines in our setting may also influence physician prescribing practices.

The proportion of patients receiving the target dose of MRA in our study was lower than that reported in studies from the USA (25%) [32] and Canada(58.4%) [33]. This difference may be partially explained by variations in sample size. However, the consistently low prescription rates of MRAs at target doses among eligible patients highlight the need for further investigation into physicians’ knowledge, attitudes, and prescribing practices related to this drug class.

In this study, overall adherence to guideline-recommended medications across the three drug classes was categorized as good (70.9%), moderate (21.6%), and poor (7.2%). These findings are comparable to those reported in the European registry, which showed a 67% adherence rate [29]. However, it is important to note that the European registry assessed adherence to five drug classes, including Ivabradine, whereas our study focused on four classes: ACEIs, ARBs, BBs, and MRAs. Additionally, the adherence rate observed in our study is notably higher than that reported in a previous study conducted at Black Lion Specialized Hospital, which found an adherence rate of 36% [26]. This difference may be explained by factors such as variation in sample size, differences in the study period (2018 versus 2023), and the availability of health insurance in our study setting, which may have improved access to medications and facilitated better adherence to treatment guidelines.

Similarly, a longer duration of heart failure was positively and significantly associated with improved overall adherence to guideline-recommended therapies. This finding is consistent with results from a previous study conducted at Black Lion Specialized Hospital [26]. This may be due to that the improved adherence among patients with longer disease duration may be attributed to several factors. Over time, these patients are more likely to have frequent follow-up visits, increasing their exposure to multiple healthcare providers who reinforce the importance of adhering to treatment guidelines. Regular contact with the healthcare system enhances opportunities for medication optimization, dose titration, and patient education. Moreover, as patients gain more experience living with the condition, they may develop a greater understanding of their illness, recognize the benefits of therapy, and become more actively engaged in self-care. On the provider side, increased familiarity with the patient’s clinical history may also contribute to more consistent and individualized application of guideline-directed therapies.

Furthermore, patients with cardiomyopathy as an underlying cause of heart failure were more likely to receive all indicated medications compared to those with coronary artery disease. This association is supported by evidence from both the Black Lion Specialized Hospital study [26] and a study conducted in Indonesia [34]. One possible reason is that patients with cardiomyopathy are typically younger and present with more severely reduced baseline LVEF. Younger patients with severe HFrEF are often considered ideal candidates for optimal, full-dose guideline-directed medical therapy due to several interrelated factors. They are expected to gain greater clinical benefit from such therapy, given their higher potential for improvement. Additionally, younger individuals tend to have fewer comorbidities, making them more tolerant of medication up-titration and less susceptible to adverse effects. The severity of their condition often necessitates closer clinical follow-up, allowing for regular monitoring and timely dose adjustments. Moreover, younger patients are generally more health-literate, motivated, and actively engaged in their care, which enhances adherence and enables clinicians to pursue more aggressive and optimized treatment strategies.

Additionally, patients with a baseline LVEF below 30% were significantly more likely to receive all indicated guideline-recommended medications. This association may be attributed to the more advanced nature of their condition, as 64% of these patients were classified as NYHA class III or above, compared to 36.5% in other groups. The increased severity likely prompted greater clinical attention and more aggressive management by physicians. Conversely, patients who had never been hospitalized were less likely to receive all indicated medications compared to those with a history of recent hospitalization. This disparity may be due to differences in the level of clinical oversight; hospitalized patients often receive more intensive evaluation and individualized care, particularly from senior physicians, whereas patients managed in busy outpatient referral clinics may not receive the same level of attention due to time constraints and high patient volumes.

Conclusion and recommendations

Physician adherence to GDMT for patients with heart failure with HFrEF in this study fell short of AHA/ESC recommendations. Only 70.5% of patients received all indicated medications, with particularly suboptimal dosing observed for BBs. Key factors influencing adherence included the duration of heart failure, time since last hospitalization, underlying etiology, and baseline LVEF. These findings highlight the need for targeted efforts by physicians to improve treatment quality. TGSH and the Ministry of Health should prioritize quality improvement initiatives focused on optimizing HFrEF management. Further research is warranted to explore barriers to guideline adherence and inform future interventions.

Limitations

Being an institution-based study, the generalizability of the findings is confined to this single facility. Incomplete or inadequate documentation posed challenges in compiling a comprehensive list of patients with HFrEF, potentially leading to the exclusion of some eligible patients. Additionally, reliance on secondary data sources may have introduced inconsistencies and gaps in the information collected. The absence of blinding also raises the possibility of information bias. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insights that can inform quality improvement efforts at the hospital and guide future research within the country.
