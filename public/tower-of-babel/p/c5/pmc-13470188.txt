The ubiquitin ligase LONRF2 inhibits glycolysis and malignant phenotypes in endometrial cancer through aldolase a degradation
Authors: Juan Shao, Cui Jiang, Yingchun Duan, Li Chen, Jiashi Gu, Huanmei Sun, Congjian Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pathogenesis of endometrial cancer (EC) remains poorly understood. This study aimed to delineate the expression profile of LON peptidase N-terminal domain and ring finger 2 (LONRF2) in EC and to elucidate its mechanistic role in modulating the proliferative, metabolic, and invasive capacities of EC cells. LONRF2 expression was analyzed in EC tissues and cell lines. EC cells overexpressing LONRF2 were evaluated for malignant phenotypes. Aldolase A (ALDOA) was identified as a LONRF2-interacting protein via immunoprecipitation and nano-liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Their regulatory relationship was examined by assessing ALDOA stability and ubiquitination. Functional exploration of the LONRF2/ALDOA axis involved combined LONRF2 knockdown, ALDOA knockdown, ALDOA knockout, or treatment with the ALDOA inhibitor itaconate in EC cells to assess malignant phenotypes, along with a xenograft model to evaluate tumor growth. LONRF2 was downregulated in clinical EC tissue and cell lines. LONRF2 overexpression inhibited the growth, glycolysis, migration, and invasion of KLE cells. LONRF2 interacted with ALDOA and induced its ubiquitination and degradation. In line with this mechanism, the enhanced malignant phenotypes caused by LONRF2 silencing were reversed by either knocking down ALDOA or administering itaconate. ALDOA-knockout cells confirmed that itaconate suppresses EC cell growth, glycolysis, migration, and invasion by targeting ALDOA. Consistently, itaconate abolished the tumor-promoting effect of LONRF2 silencing in a xenograft model. This study identifies the LONRF2/ALDOA axis as a key driver of EC progression, primarily by regulating glycolysis. Targeting this axis, particularly LONRF2, presents a promising therapeutic strategy for EC.Keywords: ALDOA, endometrial cancer, LONRF2, tumor growth, ubiquitination

The rising global incidence of endometrial cancer (EC), a common malignancy of the female reproductive system, represents a critical and growing challenge to women’s health (1). Advances in early diagnosis, surgery, and adjuvant therapy have led to favorable outcomes for patients with early-stage EC (2). Ongoing research on the pathological mechanisms of cancer progression has driven the development and refinement of treatment strategies for advanced, recurrent, or metastatic EC (3, 4), underscoring the importance of exploring the molecular mechanisms of EC.

Tumor occurrence and development are closely linked to metabolic reprogramming, particularly the abnormal enhancement of glycolysis (i.e., the “Warburg effect”), which not only supplies rapid energy but also provides precursors for biosynthesis, serving as a key metabolic feature that drives malignant phenotypes such as migration, proliferation, and invasion (5, 6). Therefore, gaining deeper insights into the upstream regulatory mechanisms of glycolysis and other malignant phenotypes in EC holds great scientific and clinical significance for uncovering the essence of the disease and developing new targeted strategies.

LON peptidase N-terminal domain and ring finger 2 (LONRF2) is a ubiquitin E3 ligase involved in protein quality control. Research indicates that it is highly expressed in senescent cells, where it helps maintain cellular homeostasis by recognizing and ubiquitinating misfolded or damaged proteins. Loss of its function has been linked to pathologies such as neurodegeneration (7). However, its function and mechanism in EC remain unknown. Notably, LONRF2 methylation has been detected in colon cancer tissues, suggesting its association with tumor progression (8, 9). Additionally, genetic variations in LONRF2 have been found in patients with germ cell tumors (10). Despite these findings indicating a significant role for LONRF2 in tumor occurrence, the exact expression profile, biological function, and the molecular mechanisms by which it modulates tumor metabolism in EC remain unclear.

Using a combination of bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation, our data revealed that LONRF2 is generally expressed at low levels in EC tissues and cell lines; however, its overexpression suppresses EC cell proliferation, glycolysis, and invasion. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we identified the key glycolytic enzyme aldolase A (ALDOA) (11, 12) as an interacting protein of LONRF2 through proteomics screening and immunoprecipitation, and confirmed the regulatory relationship between the two involving ubiquitination and degradation. This study is the first to reveal LONRF2 as an EC tumor suppressor and to clarify its novel mechanism of regulating tumor metabolism and progression through the LONRF2/ALDOA axis. These findings provide a potential theoretical basis and new intervention targets for metabolic-targeted EC therapy.

Results

LONRF2 was downregulated in EC

By comparing LONRF2 mRNA expression in thirty-three tumor types and control tissues using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) website, we found that LONRF2 expression was not significantly changed in some tumors (such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [DLBC], invasive breast cancer [BRCA], and cholangiocarcinoma [CHOL]), was upregulated in thyroid carcinoma, and was downregulated in others (such as uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma [UCEC], adrenal cortical carcinoma [ACC], and bladder urothelial carcinoma [BLCA]) (Fig. 1A).

Further analysis showed that LONRF2 mRNA expression was downregulated in UCEC tumor tissues, as indicated by data from the GEPIA website (Fig. 1B). This downregulation in UCEC tumors compared with normal samples was also confirmed using the University of Alabama at Birmingham Cancer Analysis (UALCAN) database (Fig. 1, C and D). We determined the expression levels of LONRF2 in an EC tissue microarray and in EC cell lines. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining analysis showed weaker positive staining for the LONRF2 protein in EC samples compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1E).

Based on LONRF2 immunoreactivity, the cohort was divided into high- and low-expression groups using a score of 6 as the cutoff. Statistical analysis indicated that, in the EC samples, the proportion of low LONRF2 expression was higher than that of high expression, while in the adjacent normal samples, the ratio of high to low expression remained consistent (Fig. 1F). The relationship between LONRF2 expression and clinical features of patients with EC in the tissue microarray is shown in Table S1. LONRF2 expression was associated with tumor stage but not with age, tumor size, grade, or lymph node metastasis. In the EC cell lines (RL-952, KLE, SK-UT-1, Ishikawa), LONRF2 mRNA and protein expression were noticeably lower compared to human endometrial epithelial cells (hEECs) (Fig. 1, G and H).

LONRF2 overexpression inhibited EC cell growth, glycolysis, migration, and invasion

To assess the effect of LONRF2 on the malignant phenotypes of EC cells, we overexpressed LONRF2 in KLE and RL-952 cells by vector transfection (Fig. S1, A–D) and observed changes in cell proliferation, invasion, and glycolysis. The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) results indicated reduced cell viability in response to LONRF2 overexpression (Fig. 2A), and the colony formation assay showed a marked decrease in colony numbers (Fig. 2B), demonstrating that increased LONRF2 expression inhibited cell proliferation. LONRF2 overexpression caused a significant accumulation of cells in the G1 phase and a corresponding reduction in the proportion of cells in the G2 phase, as determined by flow cytometry (Fig. 2, C and D), suggesting a potential cell cycle arrest. The analysis of glycolysis-related indicators showed that LONRF2 overexpression reduced extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) (Fig. 2E), ATP production (Fig. 2F), and lactate production (Fig. 2G), suggesting that LONRF2 overexpression suppresses glycolysis. Results from both scratch wound-healing and Transwell assays collectively indicated that LONRF2 overexpression compromised the migratory and invasive capabilities of KLE and RL-952 cells (Figs. 2, H–J, S1, E and F), suggesting that the invasive capacity of EC cells was attenuated upon LONRF2 upregulation.

LONRF2 interacted with and induced the ubiquitination and degradation of ALDOA

To explore potential downstream target proteins of LONRF2, we enriched LONRF2-interacting proteins via immunoprecipitation, followed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and silver staining (Fig. 3A). Following nano-liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (nanoLC-MS/MS) analysis of the corresponding protein bands, ALDOA was identified as a candidate interacting partner of LONRF2, and their interaction was confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays (Fig. 3B). Glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assays further confirmed the direct binding between LONRF2 and ALDOA (Fig. 3C). Subsequently, we overexpressed LONRF2 in KLE cells via vector transfection and knocked down LONRF2 in RL-952 cells using specific shRNAs (shLONRF2-1, shLONRF2-2, shLONRF2-3) transfection (Fig. S2, A and B) to investigate whether LONRF2 affects ALDOA expression in these cells. For subsequent experiments, RL-952 cells transfected with shLONRF2-1 and shLONRF2-2, which demonstrated efficient knockdown, were used. ALDOA mRNA expression was unaffected by LONRF2 overexpression or knockdown (Fig. 3D); alternatively, ALDOA protein expression decreased with LONRF2 overexpression and increased following its knockdown (Fig. 3, E and F). Additionally, the decrease in ALDOA protein levels caused by LONRF2 overexpression was reversed by MG132 (Fig. 3G) but not by chloroquine (Fig. S3, A and B); LONRF2 promoted ALDOA protein degradation in KLE cells cotreated with cycloheximide (CHX) (Fig. 3H). The data suggest that LONRF2 accelerates ALDOA protein degradation via the proteasome-dependent pathway. The immunoblotting of ubiquitin in the immunoprecipitation of ALDOA was higher in LONRF2-overexpressing cells than in the vector group (Fig. 3I), indicating that LONRF2 overexpression promoted the polyubiquitination of ALDOA protein. IHC analysis was performed on the EC tissue microarray to further examine the relationship between LONRF2 and ALDOA protein expression in clinical samples (Fig. 3J). Spearman correlation analysis revealed a negative correlation between LONRF2 and ALDOA expression (Fig. 3K).

ALDOA knockdown suppressed cell growth, glycolysis, migration, and invasion in LONRF2-silenced EC cells

The knockdown efficiency of ALDOA shRNAs (shALDOA-1, shALDOA-2, and shALDOA-3) in RL-952 cells was confirmed by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blot assays (Fig. S4, A and B). To investigate the involvement of the LONRF2/ALDOA axis in malignant phenotypes of EC cells, LONRF2 and ALDOA were simultaneously knocked down in RL-952 and KLE cells (Figs. S2, A–D and S4, A–D), and the effects on cell proliferation, invasion, and glycolytic capacity were assessed. ALDOA knockdown reversed the increases in cell viability (Fig. 4A) and colony formation (Fig. 4B) caused by LONRF2 knockdown, indicating that it abolished the enhanced cancer cell proliferation induced by LONRF2 knockdown. Cell cycle profiling confirmed that LONRF2 knockdown also slowed the accelerated G1/S transition. (Fig. 4, C and D). ALDOA knockdown inhibited the effects of LONRF2 knockdown, restoring the increased ECAR, ATP, and lactate production induced by LONRF2 knockdown to normal levels (Fig. 4, E–G). Consistent with the abovementioned findings, wound healing and Transwell experiments showed that ALDOA knockdown abolished the increased cell migration and invasion caused by LONRF2 knockdown (Figs. 4, H–J, S5, A and B). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ALDOA acts downstream of LONRF2, and together, the two molecules regulate the proliferation, glycolysis, and invasion of EC cells.

Itaconate restrained growth, glycolysis, migration, and invasion in LONRF2-silenced EC cells

Itaconate inhibits the enzymatic activity of ALDOA (13). To further validate ALDOA as a downstream target of LONRF2, we treated RL-952 cells with itaconate in combination with shLONRF2 and assessed its effects on the malignant phenotypes of EC cells. Cell proliferation assays showed that itaconate alone suppressed cell viability and colony formation, while LONRF2 knockdown significantly enhanced both. Notably, cotreatment with itaconate and shLONRF2 significantly reduced cell viability and colony formation compared to shLONRF2 treatment alone, indicating that itaconate partially reversed the pro-proliferative effect induced by LONRF2 knockdown (Fig. 5, A and B). Cell cycle analysis revealed a markedly higher proportion of G1-phase cells following co-treatment with itaconate, compared to shLONRF2 treatment alone, suggesting that itaconate partially counteracts the cell cycle progression promoted by LONRF2 knockdown (Fig. 5, C and D). Itaconate reduced the increase in ECAR and the production of ATP and lactate caused by LONRF2 knockdown (Fig. 5, E–G), indicating that itaconate attenuates the enhanced glycolysis resulting from LONRF2 loss. Similarly, itaconate inhibited the cell migration and invasion induced by LONRF2 knockdown (Figs. 5, H–J, S5, C and D). These results further support that ALDOA may act as a downstream effector mediating the antitumor function of LONRF2.

Itaconate restrained EC cell growth, glycolysis, migration, and invasion by targeting ALDOA

Knockout experiments were conducted to test the hypothesis that itaconate inhibits EC cell growth, glycolysis, migration, and invasion via ALDOA, thereby exerting antitumor effects. First, ALDOA-knockout (ALDOA-KO) KLE cells were generated and confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blot, which showed the absence of ALDOA expression (Fig. S4, E and F), as well as by functional assays. In ALDOA-KO KLE cells, the inhibitory effects of itaconate on cell viability, colony formation, and cell cycle progression were abolished (Fig. 6, A–D). In wild-type KLE cells, itaconate significantly suppressed ECAR, ATP, and lactate production. In ALDOA-KO cells, ECAR, ATP, and lactate levels were reduced accordingly, and itaconate treatment did not affect these levels (Fig. 6, E–G). Finally, Transwell analyses showed that itaconate significantly inhibited cell migration and invasion in wild-type KLE cells. Conversely, itaconate did not affect cell migration and invasion in ALDOA-KO KLE cells (Fig. 6, H–J). These results indicated that itaconate exerted its antitumor function specifically by inhibiting ALDOA.

Itaconate blocked the growth of tumor xenografts derived from LONRF2-knockdown EC cells in vivo

Using a mouse xenograft model, we investigated the impact of combining the ALDOA inhibitor itaconate with shLONRF2 on tumor progression. Tumor volume monitoring revealed that although LONRF2 knockdown alone significantly enhanced tumor growth compared to the shNC control, co-administration of itaconate markedly suppressed this accelerated growth, with tumors in the itaconate co-treatment group growing more slowly than those with LONRF2 knockdown alone (Fig. 7A). After 33 days of tumor growth, analysis of tumor size and weight showed that the xenografts in the itaconate cotreatment group were significantly smaller than those in the LONRF2 knockdown group (Fig. 7B). These results suggest that itaconate reverses the tumor-promoting effects of LONRF2 knockdown. Immunofluorescence staining of tumor tissues showed that the itaconate co-treatment group had a lower proportion of Ki67-positive cells compared to the LONRF2 knockdown group (Fig. 7C), and a higher proportion of terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL)-positive cells (Fig. 7D), indicating that itaconate inhibits cell proliferation and promotes apoptosis in LONRF2-knockdown tumors. However, neither LONRF2 knockdown nor itaconate treatment affected the body weight of mice, and no toxicities from LONRF2 knockdown or itaconate were found in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues of EC-bearing mice (Fig. S6, A and B). Glycolysis-related measurements showed that ATP and lactate production were suppressed in the itaconate co-treatment group compared to the LONRF2 knockdown group (Fig. 7, E and F). Moreover, LONRF2 expression was prominently decreased, and ALDOA expression was elevated in the LONRF2-knockdown tumor, while itaconate treatment did not affect the expression of either LONRF2 or ALDOA (Fig. 7G).

Discussion

This study elucidates the biological functions and mechanisms of LONRF2 in EC. We discovered for the first time that LONRF2 is generally expressed at low levels in EC, and that its overexpression suppresses the proliferation, glycolysis, and invasion capabilities of EC cells. Mechanistic studies revealed that LONRF2 binds to the key glycolytic enzyme ALDOA and promotes its ubiquitination and degradation via its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, thereby blocking metabolic reprogramming and malignant progression in EC (Fig. 8). In vitro and in vivo functional rescue experiments demonstrated the relevance of the LONRF2/ALDOA axis, confirming that targeted modulation of this axis can effectively reverse tumor growth. These findings enhance our mechanistic understanding of EC pathogenesis.

Mechanism by which LONRF2 inhibits EC progression. LONRF2, via its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, binds to the key glycolytic enzyme ALDOA and promotes its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby inhibiting metabolic reprogramming and malignant progression in EC. Itaconate suppresses metabolic reprogramming and the malignant progression of EC by targeting ALDOA.

LONRF2 is a protein quality control ubiquitin E3 ligase that selectively binds and ubiquitylates abnormally structured TDP-43 and hnRNP M1 and artificially misfolded proteins (7). LONRF2 expression is strongly induced in senescent cells (14), and loss of LONRF2 contributes to motor neuron degeneration and motor deficits (7). Previous studies have shown that LONRF2 expression is increased in patients with Sertoli cell-only syndrome (15) and decreased in patients with rectal adenocarcinoma (8). Aberrant methylation patterns for LONRF2 have been noted in rectal adenocarcinoma (8) and colorectal cancer (9). Moreover, the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA crosstalk between MBNL1-AS1, miR-429 and LONRF2 is validated to play important roles in improving overall survival of patients with rectal cancer (16). However, these studies do not thoroughly investigate the specific function and mechanism of LONRF2 in particular diseases and tumor types. Through systematic bioinformatic analysis, molecular biology, and biochemical experiments, the present study is the first to demonstrate that LONRF2 is downregulated in EC and that its low expression is closely related to tumor proliferation, metabolic activity, and metastatic potential in EC patients. These findings provide direct functional evidence supporting earlier bioinformatic or epigenetic hypotheses and further expand the research on LONRF2 from cellular senescence and neurodegeneration (7) to solid tumors, especially gynecological tumors.

Metabolic reprogramming, characterized by a preference for aerobic glycolysis even in oxygen-rich environments, is a well-established hallmark of cancer cells (17, 18, 19). The metabolic alteration provides cancer cells with ample energy and biosynthetic materials necessary for their rapid proliferation, invasion, and survival in the tumor microenvironment (20, 21, 22). Previous extensive research has confirmed that ALDOA, a key enzyme in the glycolysis pathway, is overexpressed in various tumors, driving tumor growth and metastasis (23, 24, 25), and is associated with poor overall survival and metastasis in patients (26, 27, 28). ALDOA exerts oncogenic activity in cancers through various signaling pathways. It serves as a key regulator of HIF1α, c-Myc, γ-actin, YAP, and the EGFR/MAPK and AMPK signaling cascades, promoting cancer cell proliferation, G1/S transition, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and metastasis (23, 29). However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms of ALDOA protein levels remain incompletely understood, with current knowledge primarily focused on transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation (30, 31).

In this study, we found that the E3 ubiquitin ligase LONRF2 binds to ALDOA and promotes its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby negatively regulating ALDOA protein abundance at the posttranslational level. The current study provides a mechanistic explanation for why ALDOA protein levels are abnormally elevated in some ECs, at least in part due to the absence of its upstream ubiquitin ligase LONRF2. This not only offers a new molecular explanation for abnormal glycolysis activity in ECs, but also establishes, for the first time, a direct regulatory pathway from the E3 ligase (LONRF2) to the metabolic enzyme (ALDOA) in ECs, providing a precise target for intervening in this metabolic vulnerability.

Based on the established LONRF2-ALDOA regulatory relationship, this study further confirmed the pharmacological value of targeting this axis. Previous literature has reported that itaconate inhibits ALDOA during tumor progression (32, 33). Consistent with these findings, the current study observed that pharmacological inhibition of ALDOA with itaconate produced a phenotypic reversal similar to that observed with ALDOA knockdown. This finding mechanistically confirms that ALDOA enzymatic activity is necessary for downstream signaling of LONRF2. Animal experiments further confirmed that targeting this pathway can effectively inhibit tumor growth. Together, these results provide preclinical evidence supporting the development of targeted therapy based on the LONRF2/ALDOA axis.

This study has some limitations. First, although we confirmed ALDOA degradation by LONRF2 ubiquitination, the specific interaction domains, ubiquitination sites, and involvement of other adaptor proteins remain unresolved. Second, although this study primarily focused on the ALDOA-mediated glycolytic pathway, LONRF2 (an E3 protein degradation enzyme) may target other important substrates. A more comprehensive proteomic screening is needed to reveal the broader network through which LONRF2 regulates metabolic reprogramming in EC. Third, the animal models used in this study mainly verified the tumor-promoting effect, but the simulation of more complex phenotypes, such as metastasis, remains insufficient. Finally, LONRF2 expression was associated with ALDOA expression and tumor stage but not with other clinicopathological features such as tumor size, tumor grade, or lymph node metastasis. Furthermore, the prognosis is not available in the EC tissue microarray used in this study. Therefore, future research should validate the association between LONRF2/ALDOA expression and clinicopathological features using a larger, adequately powered sample size.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study has advanced our understanding of the mechanism by which LONRF2, acting as an oncogene suppressor, regulates EC progression. At the regulatory mechanism level, it reveals for the first time that the key glycolytic enzyme ALDOA is precisely regulated posttranslationally by LONRF2-mediated ubiquitination, thereby expanding its upstream regulatory network. The proposed targeting of the LONRF2/ALDOA axis offers a promising therapeutic approach for EC. Thus, the findings of this study provide a deeper understanding of disease pathogenesis and present a new conceptual framework for targeted intervention.

Experimental procedures

Bioinformatics analysis

A pan-cancer analysis of LONRF2 gene expression was conducted across all tumor samples and their paired normal tissues using the GEPIA database. Specifically, we used the GEPIA and UALCAN databases to analyze LONRF2 mRNA and protein levels in EC tissues.

IHC staining

The microarray (F080Ur01), containing 74 EC and 6 normal endometrial tissue samples, was obtained from Bioaitech. After 24 h of fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, tumor tissue was embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 5-μm-thick slices, and subjected to immunohistochemistry. Briefly, the tissue section was deparaffinized and subjected to 10 min of antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (10 mM; pH 6.0) at 98 °C. After a 10-min incubation with 3% H2O2 to inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity and a 1-h incubation with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA)/phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to minimize background signal, the section was incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-LONRF2 primary antibody (ab229210; Abcam) and anti-ALDOA primary antibody (11217-1-AP; Proteintech). This was followed by a 30-min incubation with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody, DAB (Beyotime) development, and hematoxylin counterstaining. An Olympus bright-field microscope was used for imaging. Immunoreactivity was quantified as the percentage of positively stained cells and staining intensity (range 0–12). Based on LONRF2 immunoreactivity, the cohort was divided into high- and low-expression groups using a score of 6 as the cutoff.

Cell culture

EC cell lines (RL-952, KLE, SK-UT-1, and Ishikawa) and hEECs were obtained from Procell and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The incubation conditions were set to a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C, with the medium refreshed every 48 h. EC cells stably expressing shLONRF2 or shNC were treated with 1 mM of the ALDOA inhibitor itaconate (S3095; Selleck) or water as vehicle to assess the involvement of ALDOA in LONRF2-mediated EC progression. Cell lines were periodically tested for mycoplasma contamination by PCR and authenticated through short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, compared with reference STR genotypes from ATCC.

Cell transfection

Lentiviral constructs for overexpression (LONRF2 cloned into pLVX-puro) and knockdown (shLONRF2 and shALDOA in pLKO.1-puro), along with corresponding negative controls (empty vector and shNC), were commercially obtained from OBIO Technology (Shanghai, China). The shRNA sequences are shown in Table S2. For transfection procedures, HEK293 cells at approximately 60% confluency were transfected with recombinant lentiviral vectors using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher). Recombinant lentivirus harvested 48 h post-transfection was subsequently used to infect EC cells. Following a 72-h selection with 5 μg/ml puromycin to establish stable cell lines, the cells with high transduction efficiency (>90%) were used for detecting gene knockdown and overexpression efficiency.

Genome editing with CRISPR-Cas9

CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing was used to knock out the ALDOA gene. In brief, sgRNAs and the pSpCas9 BB-2A-Puro vector were designed by CrispRGold via their website (https://crisprgold.mdc-berlin.de/).

CCK-8

EC cells were plated in a 96-well plate (3 × 103 cells per well) and cultured overnight at 37 °C. Following treatment, cells in each well were incubated with 10 μl of CCK-8 solution for 1 h, as recommended by the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Colony formation

EC cells were cultured in 6-well plates for 14 days at a seeding density of 5 × 102 cells/well. After 15 min of fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, China) at room temperature, the cells were stained with 2% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich) for 20 min. After imaging, the ImageJ software was used to count colonies containing more than 50 cells.

Cell cycle

The treated cells were collected by centrifugation (1000g, 5 min), and the resulting pellet was fixed with 700 μl of ice-cold 100% ethanol. Cellular RNAs were degraded into small fragments by exposure to RNase A (1 mg/ml, 100 μl) for 30 min in the dark and stained with propidium iodide (50 μg/ml, 400 μl) for 10 min. Cell cycle distribution was assessed using a Becton Dickinson FACScan flow cytometer. Data were subsequently collected and analyzed using the integrated Cell Quest software.

ECAR

ECAR was measured using an XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer. Briefly, EC cells (1 × 104/well) were seeded in an XF-24 culture plate (Agilent Technologies) and cultured conventionally for 24 h. XF Base Medium (Agilent Technologies) was used I h before the assay. ECAR was then evaluated by sequentially adding glucose (1 μm), oligomycin (1 μm; to inhibit ATP synthesis), and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG; 0.5 μm; to inhibit glycolysis) at pre-specified time intervals.

Measurement of lactate and ATP

After plating cells at 3.5 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate and allowing them to adhere overnight, the medium was refreshed with 50 μl of DMEM per well, followed by a 24-h incubation. The culture supernatant was harvested by centrifugation for lactate release detection using a Lactic Acid Assay Kit (S0208S) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For ATP detection, EC cells were allowed to adhere in 6-well plates for 12 to 24 h and then lysed in 200 to 300 μl of buffer (with brief vortexing). The supernatant was collected for ATP level determination by combining it with the detection solution from the ATP Assay Kit (A095-1-1) and normalizing the readout to the total protein concentration. The kits used were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute.

Wound healing

EC cells were grown to 80%–90% confluency in each well of a 24-well plate, starting from a density of 2.5 × 105 cells. A linear wound was created across each well using a sterile 10 μl pipette tip, and any detached cells were gently washed away. Subsequently, the complete medium was replaced with serum-free medium to initiate the migration assay. Wound images were photographed under a microscope at 0, 24, and 48 h of wound healing. The extent of wound closure was quantified using the following formula: [(Width0 – Widtht)/Width0] × 100%, where 0 denotes the measurement at 0 h, and t represents either 24 or 48 h.

Transwell assay

The migratory and invasive potential of EC cells in response to transfection and itaconate treatment was examined using Transwell assays. In both assays, cells were suspended in serum-free DMEM at a density of 5 × 104 cells per 200 μl and loaded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with DMEM containing 10% FBS to establish a chemoattractant gradient. The key distinction in the invasion assay was the precoating of the upper chamber with Matrigel (30 μg; Corning) prior to cell seeding. Cells that had migrated for 24 h were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.05% crystal violet for 30 min. The migrated cells were quantified under a light microscope (Olympus) by averaging counts from five random fields per membrane.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was first isolated from EC cells with TRIzol reagent (Beyotime) and then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq Kit (Takara). The resulting cDNA was amplified using SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher) on a real-time PCR instrument, with ACTB as the normalization control. The primers used (obtained from Shanghai Sangon Biotech) were as follows: LONRF2-forward 5′-CTGGAGCTGGCTCCTGAT-3′, LONRF2-reverse 5′-AGCTGCACTGGCATCTTGG-3′; ALDOA-forward 5′-ATGTGACCGAGAAGGTGCTG-3′, ALDOA-reverse 5′-CTTCTGAGTGCAAGCATGGC-3′; ACTB-forward 5′-ATGATGATATCGCCGCGCTC-3′, ACTB-reverse 5′-CCACCATCACGCCCTGG-3′. The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to analyze the relative mRNA levels.

Western blotting

Protein samples from EC cells or tumor lysates were prepared using RIPA reagent with the protease inhibitor phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (1 mM; Thermo Fisher). The samples (40 μg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. Specific proteins were visualized using the following steps: 1 h of blocking with 5% skimmed milk; successive overnight incubations with primary antibodies targeting LONRF2 (ABIN531152; antibodies-online Inc), ALDOA (11217-1-AP; Proteintech), and β-actin (66009-1-Ig; Proteintech); a 1-h incubation with horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibodies; and development with an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Thermo Fisher). The band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Mass spectrometry analyses

Stable Flag-LONRF2-expressing and control HEK293 cells were lysed in high-salt NETN buffer, followed by a Co-IP. The resulting samples were subsequently analyzed by Biotree Biotech Co, Ltd using mass spectrometry. The buffer comprised Tris–HCl (pH 8.0; 20 mM), NaCl (300 mM), EDTA (1 mM), and 0.5% Nonidet P-40, and included inhibitors (10 mM NaF, 50 mM β-glycerophosphate, 1 μg/ml pepstatin A, and 1 μg/ml aprotinin). After dilution with NET buffer (1:1), the lysates were incubated with anti-Flag affinity beads overnight at 4 °C. The specifically bound proteins were competitively eluted at 4 °C using a Flag peptide. The eluates were separated by SDS-PAGE, and peptides were extracted from the gel piece, eluted with a linear gradient of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid, and sprayed into a quadrupole time-of-flight tandem mass spectrometer (Q-Tof-2, Micromass). The mass spectrometer adopts data-dependent acquisition (DDA) PaSEF mode for DDA data acquisition. Raw MS/MS data were processed with MaxQuant (version 2.2.0) using the UniProt/Swiss-Prot human protein database (release of November 13, 2021). Label-free quantification was performed to acquire the relative abundance of immunoprecipitated proteins. p values were calculated using the two-tailed Student’s t test.

Co-IP and ubiquitination analysis

The interaction between LONRF2 and ALDOA was assessed by Co-IP. In brief, cells were lysed on ice for 20 min with an IP lysis buffer. After centrifugation (10,000g, 10 min), the cleared supernatant (lysate) was incubated with anti-LONRF2 (ABIN531152; antibodies-online Inc), anti-ALDOA (12842-1-AP; Proteintech), or control IgG (sc-515946; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) antibodies for 1 h, followed by overnight rotation with Protein A/G Plus agarose beads. After four washes with IP buffer, the beads were boiled in 2 × Laemmli buffer for 5 min. The levels of the target proteins in the eluted samples were assessed by immunoblotting.

GST pull-down assay

GST pull-down assays were conducted using Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, UK). Beads were washed with PBS containing 0.05% Tween (PBST) and incubated with 100 μg of purified GST or GST–LONRF2 fusion protein (GeneCreate Biological Engineering, Wuhan, China) at 4 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, purified His–ALDOA protein (100 μg, GeneCreate) was added and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. The beads were then washed, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 1 × loading buffer, followed by analysis via immunoblotting using anti-His and anti-GST antibodies.

Protein stability assay

EC cells were treated with 0.1 mg/ml CHX (Selleck, Shanghai, China) to assess ALDOA protein stability. The cells were harvested and lysed at designated time points after treatment. ALDOA degradation was then examined by Western blot using a specific antibody against ALDOA, with β-actin expression serving as the control.

Animals

To establish a xenograft model, 5 × 106 RL-952 cells stably transduced with shLONRF2 or shNC were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flank of 6-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (Hangzhou Ziyuan Experimental Animal Technology Co, Ltd). Housing conditions included a controlled environment with 45%–65% humidity, a temperature of 22–26 °C, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. Twelve days after inoculation, mice bearing shLONRF2 tumors were randomly assigned to three groups, including untreated group, itaconate group, and vehicle group (n = 6 per group), while the shNC group (n = 6 per group) served as the control. Itaconate was given daily by intraperitoneal administration at 50 mg/kg. At the start of itaconate treatment on day 12, the baseline mean tumor volumes in the shNC, shLONRF2, shLONRF2+Vehicle, and shLONRF2+Itaconate groups were 20.89g, 21.29g, 20.84g, and 21.57g, respectively. Tumor dimensions were recorded three times per week until day 33, when the mice were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep pentobarbital anesthesia. The tumors were harvested, photographed, weighed, and subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for the assessment of tissue cell apoptosis and proliferation. All procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and received formal approval from the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Pudong Hospital, Fudan University Pudong Medical Center (approval number 2023-QKW-06).

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence analysis of Ki67 and apoptosis, 5-μm-thick paraffin-embedded tumor sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and blocked with 5% BSA. They were then incubated overnight with an anti-Ki67 primary antibody (AF0198; Affinity). After PBST washes, the sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Apoptosis was detected using a commercial One-Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (C1088; Beyotime). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Beyotime) for 3 min prior to imaging. Fluorescence images were captured using an Olympus microscope.

Histological analysis

Liver, heart, lung, kidney, and spleen tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), according to the protocols (34). Histopathology was evaluated by two observers blinded to the intervention and imaged using a Nikon microscope (Y-THS, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.4.2, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The normality of the variable’s distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The variables were normally distributed; therefore, group comparisons were made using the Student’s t test (for two groups) or one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons post hoc test (for three or more groups). All experiments were conducted independently in triplicate. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
