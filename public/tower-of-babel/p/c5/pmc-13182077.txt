NUF2 interacts with BUB3 and drives lung adenocarcinoma progression through activation of the NF-κB pathway
Authors: Dingqiang Zhu, Jinghui Liang, Jiacheng Li, Wenhao Yu, Luyuan Ma, Zhanpeng Tang, Rongyang Li, Chenghao Qu, Jingyi Han, Dingxin Wang, Lin li, Hui Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common form of lung cancer and is often diagnosed at advanced stages. NUF2, a key component of the NDC80 kinetochore complex, is highly expressed in several malignancies, but its function and regulatory mechanism in LUAD remain unclear.MethodsExpression profiling using TCGA, GEO datasets, and LUAD tissue microarrays revealed NUF2 dysregulation in LUAD. Functional assays (CCK-8, EdU, colony formation, Transwell, and xenograft models) were performed to assess the effects of NUF2 gain- or loss-of-function. Protein interactions were examined using immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry. Transcriptomic sequencing and Western blotting were used to evaluate downstream signaling pathways.ResultsNUF2 expression was significantly elevated in LUAD tissues and cell lines and was positively associated with tumor stage and poor overall survival. NUF2 knockdown inhibited LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and tumor growth, whereas NUF2 overexpression enhanced malignant behaviors. Mechanistically, NUF2 interacted with and stabilized the spindle checkpoint protein BUB3 by preventing proteasomal degradation. Additionally, NUF2 activated the NF-κB signaling pathway, and BUB3 was required for this activation.ConclusionsNUF2 promotes LUAD progression by stabilizing BUB3 and activating NF-κB signaling. Targeting the NUF2–BUB3 axis may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04319-1.Keywords: NUF2, BUB3, NF-κB, Lung adenocarcinoma, Tumor progression

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide. The overall 5-year survival rate varies significantly by disease stage and geographic region, ranging from approximately 4% to 17% [1]. Lung cancer is broadly classified into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC), with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) being the most common subtype of NSCLC [2]. Because early-stage LUAD is typically asymptomatic and lacks specific clinical manifestations, many patients are diagnosed at advanced stages. Therefore, the identification of reliable biomarkers for early detection, prognostic evaluation, and disease monitoring is urgently needed [3].

The NDC80 complex, comprising NDC80 (also known as HEC1), NUF2, SPC24, and SPC25, serves as the primary microtubule receptor at the kinetochore [4]. It plays a crucial regulatory role in cell cycle progression. In normal tissues, the expression level of NUF2 is generally low, whereas significant upregulation has been observed in various malignancies, including lung cancer, breast cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, gastric cancer, thyroid cancer and ovarian cancer [5–11]. Numerous studies have shown that aberrantly elevated expression of NUF2 is closely associated with accelerated tumor cell proliferation, enhanced invasive and metastatic capabilities, and poor clinical outcomes, suggesting that NUF2 may function as a potential oncogene. Mechanistically, NUF2 promotes tumor cell proliferation by facilitating the transition from the G2/M phase to mitosis. Overexpression of NUF2 stabilizes the attachment between spindle microtubules and kinetochores, thereby reducing the activation of cell cycle checkpoints and promoting chromosomal instability (CIN) [12]. Chromosomal instability is a major driver of tumor initiation and progression, and NUF2 is thus considered an important regulator of CIN. In addition, studies have indicated that NUF2 can activate signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and ERK, thereby enhancing cell migration and invasion [10, 13]. Suppression of NUF2 expression significantly reduces tumor cell proliferation and colony-forming ability, induces cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and highlights NUF2 as a promising therapeutic target. Clinically, high NUF2 expression is frequently associated with advanced clinical stage, increased tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and shorter overall survival [5–11]. Analyses of gene expression databases, such as TCGA and GEO, have also demonstrated that NUF2 often serves as a marker of poor prognosis and is positively correlated with proliferation markers such as Ki-67 [5–11, 14]. Moreover, high NUF2 expression has been linked to alterations in the tumor immune microenvironment, including increased infiltration of immunosuppressive cells [15–17].

Despite the well-established roles of NUF2 in multiple cancer types, its specific function in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) progression and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely characterized. Furthermore, while NUF2 is known to function within the NDC80 complex alongside BUB3—another key component of the spindle assembly checkpoint—the potential interplay between NUF2 and BUB3 in LUAD, and whether BUB3 contributes to NUF2-driven oncogenic effects, has not been previously explored.

In the present study, we sought to address these gaps by comprehensively investigating the expression pattern, biological function, and mechanistic contribution of NUF2 in LUAD, with a particular focus on its relationship with BUB3. Our results demonstrate that NUF2 is significantly upregulated in LUAD and promotes tumor growth by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, and further reveal that NUF2 exerts its oncogenic effects, at least in part, through stabilizing BUB3. These findings not only elucidate a previously unrecognized NUF2/BUB3 axis in LUAD progression but also suggest that targeting this axis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for LUAD.

Methods

Bioinformatic analysis and clinical samples

A lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) tissue microarray (HLugA180Su11) was purchased from Shanghai Xinchao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (SHYJS-CP-2206001). All patients provided written informed consent, and all samples were anonymized. The array contained 88 LUAD tissues and 88 matched adjacent noncancerous tissues, accompanied by clinical data including age, gender, stage, and survival. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Xinchao Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Cell culture and treatment

Human LUAD cell lines (BEAS-2B, H1299, A549, H1975, and PC9) were obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank Research Center (Chinese Academy of Sciences). Cell line authenticity was confirmed by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and revalidated every three months. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco; for H1299 and H1975) or Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco; for BEAS-2B, A549, and PC9), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Mycoplasma contamination was assessed every three months using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza).

Transient transfections were performed using jetPRIME reagent (Polyplus, Cat# 101000046) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each well received 200 µL buffer, 4 µL transfection reagent, and either 5 µL siRNA or 2 µg plasmid DNA. Cells were harvested 48–72 h post-transfection. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs; Suzhou Gemma Gene Co., Ltd.) and plasmids (Shandong Scientific Research Cloud Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were used as indicated.

Lentiviral transduction was performed using Sh-NUF2 lentivirus in A549 cells (MOI = 20) and NUF2-overexpression lentivirus in H1299 cells (MOI = 10), with corresponding negative controls. Stable cell lines were selected with puromycin. All lentiviruses were purchased from Jikai Co. The siRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western Blotting

Cells were lysed on ice for 30 min in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Cat# P0013B) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay (Beyotime, Cat# P0010). Equal amounts of protein (15 µg) were resolved by SDS–PAGE (10–12% gels) and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, washed, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit. GAPDH served as a loading control. Antibody information is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) cell proliferation assay

Transfected cells were seeded in 96-well plates (2,000 cells/well) and incubated for 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h. Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (APExBIO, Cat# K1018). After adding CCK-8 reagent and incubating for 2 h in the dark, absorbance was measured at 450 nm in a microplate reader. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate.

EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine) assay

Cells (20,000/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated overnight after transfection. EdU staining was conducted using the EdU In Vitro Kit (Beyotime, Cat# C0076S) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted microscope, and EdU-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ. Experiments were performed in triplicate.

Colony formation assay

Transfected cells were plated in 6-well plates (1,000 cells/well) and cultured until visible colonies (> 50 cells) appeared. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 25 min, stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 25 min, washed three times with PBS, and air-dried. Colonies were imaged and counted using ImageJ. All experiments were independently performed three times.

Wound-healing assay

Confluent cells in 6-well plates were scratched with a 200 µL pipette tip and rinsed with PBS three times. Cells were cultured in serum-free medium and imaged at 0 h and 24 h under a microscope. Wound closure was quantified using ImageJ. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Transwell assay

Cells were seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (serum-free medium), while the lower chamber contained 700 µL medium with 20% FBS. After 24 h, non-migrated cells were removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 25 min, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and imaged under a microscope. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate.

In vivo experiments

Stable PC9 (Sh-NC, Sh-NUF2) cells (2 × 10⁶ cells in 0.1 mL PBS) and A549 (Vector, oe-NUF2) cells (5 × 10⁶ cells in 0.1 mL PBS) were injected subcutaneously into the axillae of 4-week-old nude mice (Huafukang Co.). Mice were randomized into four groups (n > = 3/group). Tumor size was measured weekly using calipers, and volume was calculated as:

After 35 days, mice were euthanized via CO₂ asphyxiation, and tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed. H&E and Ki67 immunohistochemistry (GB121141-100, 1:300) were performed by Sevier Corporation. All animal procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University (DWLL-202500260).

Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE) staining

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24–48 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Section (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated with 1% acid alcohol, blued in alkaline solution, counterstained with eosin for 2 min, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral resin. Images were captured using a light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

LUAD and murine tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were then deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 120 °C for 10 min in a pressure cooker. incubated at 60 °C for 1 h, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Samples were incubated with peroxidase blocking solution(Beyotime, Cat# P0100A) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by blocking with 5% normal goat serum in PBST for 2 h at 37 °C. Primary antibodies (NUF2, Ki67) were applied overnight at 4 °C. For negative controls, the primary antibody was omitted and replaced with non-immune serum. Lung tumor tissue sections with known positive expression for NUF2 and Ki67 were used as positive controls. Slides were washed with TBST, incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, developed with DAB, counterstained with hematoxylin, and imaged under an inverted microscope. Antibody information is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

IHC scoring was performed independently by one board-certified pathologist and two trained scorers, blinded to clinical data. The H-score was calculated based on staining intensity and percentage of positive cells. Inter-observer variability was minimized through a predefined scoring protocol.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) assay

LUAD and 293T cells were lysed in IP buffer(Beyotime, Cat# P0013), and the supernatants were incubated with antibodies against NUF2, BUB3, or IgG controls, as well as tag antibodies (Flag, Myc, His), on a rotating platform at 4 °C for 1 h. Protein A/G magnetic beads were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed five times, resuspended in loading buffer, and analyzed by Western blotting using primary antibodies from a different species to avoid heavy/light chain interference. Antibody details are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using the Cellular RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (SparkJade, Cat# AC0205). Reverse transcription was performed using the Hifair®III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen, Cat# 11141ES60) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was carried out to detect the mRNA expression levels of NUF2, BUB3, and GAPDH. Each qPCR reaction was performed in a 20 µL volume containing 10 µL of 2× SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, Cat# 11201ES08), 0.4 µL of each primer (10 µM), 2 µL of cDNA template, and 7.2 µL of RNase-free water. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Melt curve analysis was performed to verify primer specificity. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. All reactions were performed in triplicate. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Protein stability assay

A549 cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX, 50 µg/mL; MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-12320) or DMSO (vehicle control) to assess NUF2-mediated effects on BUB3 stability. Cells were harvested at 0, 0.5, 1, and 2 h post-treatment. Protein levels were analyzed by Western blotting.

Analysis through transcriptomic sequencing, mass spectrometry, and bioinformatics

Transcriptomic sequencing was performed by Majorbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Differentially expressed mRNA transcripts between NUF2-knockdown PC9 cells and control PC9 cells were identified through RNA sequencing. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was subsequently conducted using the Majorbio Cloud (www.majorbio.com) to visualize functional pathways associated with the identified genes. Data analysis was performed using the Majorbio Cloud (www.majorbio.com). [18]

Mass spectrometry analysis was carried out by Jikai Co. Briefly, immunoprecipitation was performed using A549 cells stably overexpressing NUF2, and the precipitated magnetic beads were collected by centrifugation for subsequent mass spectrometric analysis. The resulting data were used to identify proteins interacting with NUF2.

The GEPIA platform (https://gepia3.bioinfoliu.com/) was used to assess NUF2 expression across multiple cancer types. All datasets were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). For transcriptomic data analysis, NUF2 RNA-Seq expression data (HTSeq-FPKM) for TCGA-LUAD were retrieved from the UCSC Xena platform (https://xena.ucsc.edu/), which provides uniformly processed and normalized TCGA datasets. In addition, GEO datasets GSE31210, GSE75037, GSE116959, GSE117049, and GSE19804, containing expression profiles of lung adenocarcinoma and normal lung tissues, were downloaded from the GEO repository. Raw data were preprocessed and normalized at the probe level using the limma package by R (4.5.2) to ensure consistency across datasets.

Immunofluorescence (IF) Assay

Transfected cells were seeded onto poly-D-lysine–coated coverslips placed in 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min, and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with CoraLite 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Details of the antibodies used are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism. Each experiment was conducted at least three times independently. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. For non-normally distributed data, we now present results as median [IQR] and use non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test for two groups; Kruskal–Wallis test for multiple groups). Spearman’s correlation was used where appropriate. Two-group comparisons were conducted using two-tailed Student’s t-tests, while multiple-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. Kaplan–Meier, Pearson’s χ², and Fisher’s exact tests were applied where appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For survival analysis, overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from initial diagnosis to death from any cause. Patients who were lost to follow-up, withdrew from the study, or were alive at the last follow-up were censored at the date of last contact. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify independent prognostic factors and to calculate hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Variables with P < 0.1 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate Cox model. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

NUF2 is highly expressed in lung adenocarcinoma and associated with poor patient prognosis

To investigate the role of NUF2 in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), we first analyzed data from the GEPIA platform and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). NUF2 expression was markedly elevated in LUAD tissues compared with normal lung tissues (Fig. 1A, B). Analysis of TCGA clinical data further revealed that high NUF2 expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival (OS) in LUAD patients (median OS: 35.77 months in the high‑expression group vs. 53.33 months in the low‑expression group; Log‑rank P = 0.0012) (Fig. 1C). Correlation analyses indicated that elevated NUF2 expression was significantly related to T stage (P = 0.018), N stage (P = 0.017), M stage (P = 0.015), and TNM stage (P = 0.001, Table 1). Univariate Cox regression identified NUF2 as a high-risk factor for LUAD (HR = 1.092, P = 0.018), and multivariate analysis confirmed that NUF2 expression (HR = 1.088, P = 0.0257) was an independent predictor of poor OS (Table 2). To validate these findings, multiple GEO datasets (GSE31210[HR = 3.024, P = 0.0026], GSE75037, GSE116959, GSE117049, GSE19804, and GSE30219[HR = 2.102, P < 0.0001]) were analyzed, consistently showing elevated NUF2 expression in LUAD tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1D). High NUF2 expression was also correlated with unfavorable survival outcomes High NUF2 expression was also correlated with unfavorable survival outcomes (GSE30219: median OS: 28 months in the high‑expression group vs. 104 months in the low‑expression group, Log‑rank P < 0.0001; GSE31210: median OS was not reached in either group, Log‑rank P = 0.0026) (Fig. 1E). Next, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was performed on a tissue microarray containing 88 LUAD specimens. In the H-score system, NUF2 expression was significantly higher in LUAD tissues than in adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1F, G), and Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed its association with poor prognosis (median OS: 28.5 months in the high‑expression group vs. 69.5 months in the low‑expression group; Log‑rank P = 0.0027) (Fig. 1H). In this cohort, high NUF2 expression was significantly correlated with TNM stage (P = 0.003, Table 3), while univariate (HR = 1.00, P < 0.001) and multivariate (HR = 1.00, P = 0.005) Cox analyses again demonstrated its independent prognostic value (Table 4). Consistently, western blot analysis of four paired LUAD and adjacent normal tissues revealed elevated NUF2 protein levels in tumor tissues (representative blots in Fig. 1I; quantification in Supplementary Fig. 4A). Moreover, both western blot and qRT–PCR analyses showed that NUF2 expression was markedly upregulated in LUAD cell lines (H1299, A549, NCI-H1975, and PC-9) compared with the normal bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B (representative blots and qRT–PCR analyses in Fig. 1J; western blot quantification in Supplementary Fig. 4B). Collectively, these findings indicate that NUF2 is aberrantly overexpressed in LUAD and functions as a potential oncogenic driver.

NUF2 promoted the proliferation and migration of LUAD cells in vitro

Western blot and qRT–PCR analyses revealed that among the four LUAD cell lines examined, NUF2 expression was highest in PC9 and H1299 cells and lowest in A549 cells (representative blots in Fig. 1J; quantification in Supplementary Fig. 4B). Four siRNA sequences targeting NUF2 were screened in PC9 and H1299 cells, and the most effective sequence was selected for constructing lentiviral vectors (Supplementary Fig. 1). Stable NUF2-knockdown cell lines were established in PC9 and H1299 cells, while a NUF2-overexpressing cell line was generated using A549 cells. The efficiency of NUF2 silencing and overexpression was confirmed by western blot (Supplementary Fig. S1). Functional assays were then performed to assess the biological effects of NUF2 on LUAD cell behavior. CCK-8, EdU, and colony formation assays demonstrated that NUF2 knockdown significantly suppressed LUAD cell proliferation, whereas NUF2 overexpression markedly enhanced proliferative capacity (Fig. 2A–C). Moreover, transwell migration and wound healing assays revealed that NUF2 depletion reduced migratory ability, while NUF2 overexpression promoted cell migration (Fig. 2D–F). Collectively, these results indicate that NUF2 enhances the proliferative and migratory potential of LUAD cells in vitro.

NUF2 promotes the proliferation and metastasis of LUAD cells in vivo

To further investigate the role of NUF2 in tumor growth and metastasis in vivo, subcutaneous xenograft models were established by implanting stable NUF2-knockdown PC9 cells and NUF2-overexpressing A549 cells into the flanks of BALB/c nude mice. NUF2 knockdown significantly suppressed tumor growth, resulting in reduced tumor volume and weight compared with control groups (Fig. 3A), whereas NUF2 overexpression exerted the opposite effect, markedly promoting tumor progression (Fig. 3B). Immunohistochemical analysis further supported these findings, revealing substantially lower Ki-67 expression in the sh-NUF2 group and higher Ki-67 expression in the OE-NUF2 group relative to controls (Fig. 3C). To evaluate the metastatic potential of NUF2, a lung metastasis model was established by intravenous injection of the modified LUAD cells. In vivo bioluminescence imaging and histological examination of lung tissues demonstrated that NUF2 knockdown markedly inhibited hematogenous dissemination and pulmonary colonization of LUAD cells (Fig. 3D–F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that NUF2 promotes LUAD cell proliferation and metastasis both in vitro and in vivo.

NUF2 interacts with BUB3 and activates the NF-κB signaling pathway

To identify molecular interactors mediating the oncogenic activity of NUF2, we performed immunoprecipitation followed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (IP–LC-MS/MS) in LUAD cells ectopically expressing NUF2. Comparative proteomic analysis identified 139 proteins significantly enriched in NUF2 immunoprecipitates compared with IgG controls. Notably, Budding Uninhibited by Benzimidazoles 3 (BUB3)—a key mitotic checkpoint protein essential for accurate chromosome segregation during cell division—was among the top hits (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays confirmed both endogenous and exogenous interactions between NUF2 and BUB3 (Fig. 4A, B). Confocal microscopy further demonstrated co-localization of NUF2 and BUB3 in LUAD cells (Fig. 4C). Moreover, NUF2 knockdown markedly decreased BUB3 protein levels without affecting its mRNA expression (representative blots in Fig. 4D; quantification in Supplementary Fig. 4C), suggesting a post-translational regulatory mechanism. Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 restored BUB3 protein levels reduced by NUF2 silencing (representative blots in Fig. 4E; quantification in Supplementary Fig. 4D). Moreover, we found that knockdown of NUF2 led to a decrease in the ubiquitination level of BUB3 (Fig. 4H). Consistently, cycloheximide chase assays showed that NUF2 overexpression significantly prolonged the half-life of BUB3, as evidenced by slower degradation rates in the presence of cycloheximide (Fig. 4F, G). Together, these results indicate that NUF2 interacts with and stabilizes BUB3 by preventing its proteasomal degradation.

NUF2 promotes LUAD oncogenesis and activates the NF-κB signaling pathway through BUB3

To explore the downstream signaling pathways through which NUF2 promotes LUAD progression, we performed transcriptomic analysis following NUF2 knockdown. Differential gene expression and pathway enrichment analyses revealed that the NF-κB signaling pathway was the most significantly affected among tumor-associated pathways (Fig. 5A). Among the downregulated genes, NF-κB2 (p100) ranked among the most prominently reduced transcripts (Fig. 5B). Both loss- and gain-of-function experiments demonstrated that NUF2 acts as a positive regulator of the NF-κB pathway (representative blots in Fig. 5C-E; quantification in Supplementary Fig. 4E-G). To further determine whether BUB3 mediates NUF2-induced NF-κB activation, we silenced BUB3 in NUF2-overexpressing cells. Knockdown of BUB3 abolished NUF2-driven activation of the NF-κB pathway (representative blots in Fig. 5F; quantification in Supplementary Fig. 4H), indicating that BUB3 is required for this effect. Functionally, CCK-8 assays revealed that silencing BUB3 abrogated the NUF2-induced increase in LUAD cell proliferation (Fig. 5G). Similarly, colony formation assays confirmed that BUB3 depletion significantly reduced the clonogenic advantage conferred by NUF2 overexpression (Fig. 5H). EdU incorporation assays further demonstrated that BUB3 knockdown attenuated the NUF2-induced increase in proliferative cell fractions (Fig. 5I). Cell motility assays showed consistent results: transwell migration assays indicated that BUB3 silencing reduced NUF2-driven cell migration (Fig. 5J), and wound healing assays revealed that BUB3 knockdown mitigated NUF2-induced acceleration of wound closure (Fig. 5K). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that NUF2 promotes LUAD cell proliferation and migration by stabilizing BUB3 and activating the NF-κB signaling pathway in a BUB3-dependent manner (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Our study identifies NUF2 as a novel regulator of BUB3 in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). We demonstrate that NUF2 physically interacts with BUB3 and stabilizes its protein level, thereby promoting activation of NF-κB signaling pathway and driving LUAD initiation and progression. Clinically, NUF2 expression was markedly elevated in LUAD tissues and correlated with poor patient prognosis. High NUF2 expression was significantly associated with advanced clinicopathological features, suggesting its potential as a biomarker of tumor aggressiveness. Functional assays, both in vitro and in vivo, further confirmed that NUF2 enhances the proliferative and migratory capacities of LUAD cells, underscoring its critical role in tumor development. Future investigations should focus on elucidating the role of NUF2 in drug resistance and tumor initiation in LUAD. As our current study utilized established LUAD cell lines and xenograft models, we cannot yet infer the effects of NUF2 on tumor initiation in vivo. This limitation is acknowledged, and subsequent studies employing early-lesion models and lung organoid systems will be essential to define NUF2’s function during the earliest stages of tumorigenesis.

Mass spectrometry analysis suggested that NUF2 may interact with BUB3 in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). We further confirmed this interaction through both endogenous and exogenous co-immunoprecipitation assays. These findings demonstrated that NUF2 physically associates with BUB3 and enhances its protein stability by preventing proteasome-dependent degradation. However, several questions remain to be addressed. First, the precise mechanism of BUB3 degradation remains unclear—whether NUF2 inhibits ubiquitin-mediated or lysosome-mediated degradation. Previous studies have reported that BUB3 undergoes ubiquitination during mitosis [19] ; therefore, it will be important to determine which ubiquitin linkage (e.g., K48- or K63-linked) predominates in this context. Second, to delineate the structural determinants of NUF2 function, future studies should perform domain-mapping analyses to evaluate how specific NUF2 regions contribute to its interaction with BUB3. Structurally, NUF2 contains four major domains: residues 1–385 interact with the N-terminus of NDC80, residues 386–464 interact with the C-terminus of NDC80 and the SPBC24–SPBC25 subcomplex, and two coiled-coil regions (147–345 and 389–459) facilitate complex formation [20–22]. Such studies will clarify whether NUF2’s structural integrity is essential for its role in promoting LUAD progression. Finally, complete NUF2 knockout using CRISPR/Cas9 should be employed to rigorously validate its function in LUAD development. Partial depletion by RNA interference only moderately decreases BUB3 stability and NF-κB activity, leading to reduced proliferation and migration but allowing partial recovery over time. In contrast, genetic ablation of NUF2 is expected to eliminate its function entirely. Residual NUF2 in knockdown cells may permit compensatory activation of alternative survival pathways, whereas knockout cells would lack this capacity, resulting in more pronounced and sustained biological effects.

BUB3 belongs to the conserved budding uninhibited by benomyl (BUB) protein family, which is known to function in the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC) pathway, from yeast to mammals [23, 24]. Through this surveillance mechanism, BUB3 preserves genomic integrity and prevents aneuploidy — a hallmark of many cancers. Increasing evidence indicates that dysregulated BUB3 contributes to tumorigenesis. Elevated BUB3 expression has been reported in hepatocellular carcinoma, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and lung adenocarcinoma, where it correlates with higher pathological grade, enhanced proliferative capacity, and unfavorable clinical outcomes [25–28]. Excessive BUB3 activity can exacerbate chromosomal instability, promoting uncontrolled cell division and metastatic potential [25]. Conversely, BUB3 deficiency impairs SAC function, resulting in chromosome mis - segregation, aneuploidy, and apoptotic cell death [29]. These findings demonstrate a context-dependent dual role of BUB3: it is indispensable for preserving genomic stability under physiological conditions, yet its aberrant overexpression can paradoxically promote malignant progression [30]. While BUB3 has been extensively characterized for its canonical role in mitosis, its involvement in oncogenic signaling pathways, such as the NF-κB pathway, has not been reported. In our study, we found for the first time that NUF2 knockdown inhibits NF-κB signaling. This suggests that NUF2 and BUB3 may form a regulatory complex that modulates NF-κB activity. We hypothesize that overexpression of either NUF2 or BUB3 induces CIN, which subsequently activates a DNA damage–NF-κB signaling axis [31]. CIN is a potent trigger for NF-κB activation [32]. When NUF2 or BUB3 is abnormally upregulated, SAC fidelity becomes compromised, leading to chromosome missegregation and accumulation of DNA damage. The resulting DNA damage activates ATM, which further stimulates the IKK complex, promotes IκBα degradation, and ultimately results in persistent NF-κB activation [33]. Sustained NF-κB signaling drives oncogenic transcriptional programs that enhance tumor cell proliferation, survival, and invasiveness. However, we acknowledge that the direct mechanistic link between BUB3 and NF-κB activation remains to be fully elucidated, and the proposed signaling cascade via CIN and DNA damage represents a hypothetical model that warrants further experimental validation. These observations suggest that targeting BUB3, or disrupting its interaction with NF-κB pathway components, may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for tumors characterized by mitotic checkpoint dysregulation and chronic NF-κB activation.

The three LUAD cell lines employed herein—PC9 (EGFR exon 19 deletion), H1299 (TP53-mutant), and A549 (KRASG12S)—recapitulate distinct oncogenic drivers representative of human LUAD heterogeneity. Irrespective of their divergent mutation spectra, all lines exhibited elevated NUF2 expression (Fig. 1J), indicating that NUF2 upregulation operates independently of these canonical driver alterations—a finding concordant with our TCGA and GEO cohort analyses. Mechanistically, BUB3 was validated as a downstream effector of NUF2 through rescue experiments in NUF2-overexpressing A549 cells. Reintroduction of BUB3 significantly reversed NUF2-induced phenotypic alterations (Fig. 5), confirming that BUB3 partially mediates NUF2 oncogenic function in this cellular context. Future investigations employing isogenic cell systems, patient-derived organoids, or genetically engineered murine models will be instrumental in delineating context-dependent functions of NUF2/BUB3 within molecularly defined LUAD subsets.

In summary, our study identifies NUF2 as a novel upstream regulator of BUB3 in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). We demonstrate that NUF2 physically interacts with BUB3 and stabilizes it at the protein level, thereby promoting activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway and driving LUAD initiation and progression. Clinically, NUF2 expression was markedly elevated in LUAD tissues and correlated with poor prognosis and advanced clinicopathological features, suggesting its potential as a biomarker of tumor aggressiveness. Functional assays in vitro and in vivo confirmed that NUF2 enhances the proliferative and migratory capacities of LUAD cells, underscoring its critical role in tumor development. Collectively, our findings unveil a previously unrecognized NUF2/BUB3/NF-κB signaling axis with therapeutic implications for LUAD.
