Blood-labyrinth barrier disruption associates with hypercholesterolemia-induced hearing loss
Authors: Zheng Yang, Yuqing Li, Zeyu Zheng, Bo Yue, Xiaofeng Zhai, Wei Ren, Shiming Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHypercholesterolemia is a recognized risk factor for sensorineural hearing loss. This study investigated the mechanistic link between blood-labyrinth barrier (BLB) disruption and hypercholesterolemia-associated cochlear damage. Low-density lipoprotein receptor knockout (Ldlr−/−) mice fed a high-fat diet for 8 weeks were used as the experimental model. Auditory assessments showed elevated auditory brainstem response (ABR) thresholds and distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAEs), especially at high frequencies, consistent with hearing loss driven by outer hair cell dysfunction. BLB disruption occurred before hearing impairment, manifest as increased vascular permeability, damaged tight junctions and downregulated junctional protein levels. Cochlear histopathology confirmed outer and inner hair cell loss. These findings demonstrate a sequential and identify BLB as a key therapeutic target for preventing hypercholesterolemia-associated hearing impairment.

Introduction

Hypercholesterolemia, a prevalent metabolic disorder characterized by abnormally high levels of cholesterol in the blood, represents a significant and growing global health burden.1,2 Its escalating prevalence, fueled by the widespread adoption of diets rich in saturated fats and increasingly sedentary lifestyles, has made it a major public health concern worldwide.3,4,5 While hypercholesterolemia is most commonly recognized for its role in promoting cardiovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis coronary heart disease,6,7 emerging evidence suggests it also contributes to a range of non-cardiovascular complications including impairment of the auditory system.8,9,10 Recently, epidemiological studies have suggested a potential association between dyslipidemia and sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL).10,11,12,13 This risk is particularly pronounced for high-frequency hearing loss a type of hearing impairment that often affects the ability to perceive speech in noisy environments and typically precedes more severe forms of auditory dysfunction.14,15 The correlation suggests that dyslipidemia may exert a direct and detrimental effect on the auditory system. However, establishing a definitive causal link in human studies remains challenging, as clinical observations are frequently confounded by comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and age-related degeneration, which are highly prevalent in the same patient populations.16,17,18,19 Consequently, the critical question of whether hypercholesterolemia acts as an independent risk factor for SNHL, and if so, through what specific pathological mechanisms, remains a subject of intense investigation and debate. Resolving this ambiguity is crucial for developing targeted preventive and therapeutic strategies.

The cochlea, responsible for converting sound waves into neural signals, is a highly metabolically active organ critically dependent on a stable microenvironment for normal function.20,21 This delicate homeostasis is maintained by the blood-labyrinth barrier (BLB), a specialized vascular structure that tightly regulates the selective exchange of ions, nutrients, and toxins between the systemic circulation and the cochlear fluids. It is noteworthy that the ultrastructure and physiological function of the BLB share significant similarities with the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Both barriers are composed of endothelial cells interconnected by complex tight junctions, supported by pericytes and astrocyte-like cells, and they play a crucial role in protecting sensitive neural tissues from fluctuations in blood composition.22,23 Particularly, the BLB is primarily located in the stria vascularis and spiral ligament, which tightly regulates the exchange of ions, nutrients, and potentially harmful substances between the systemic circulation and the cochlear fluids, thereby preserving the unique endocochlear potential (EP) and the ionic composition of the endolymph that are fundamental for normal auditory transduction.23,24,25 Given the cochlea’s high metabolic demand and minimal energy reserves, its dependence on a stable blood supply is absolute. It is therefore highly plausible that systemic metabolic disturbances, such as hypercholesterolemia, could disrupt this delicate equilibrium.

Researches have demonstrated that hypercholesterolemia is a potent disruptor of the BBB, the proposed mechanisms include inducing endothelial dysfunction, promoting oxidative stress, triggering chronic neuroinflammation, and degrading key tight junction proteins such as occludin and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), leading to increased vascular permeability.26,27,28 Given this established causality in the cerebrovascular system, we hypothesized that the BLB might be a similarly vulnerable target of lipid-mediated damage. Given the significant similarities in structure and function between the BLB and the BBB, we hypothesize that the BLB represents a similarly vulnerable target of lipid-mediated damage. We propose that chronic hypercholesterolemia compromises BLB integrity, thereby breaching the immune-privileged environment of the cochlea. This breach would allow the influx of cytotoxic molecules, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and reactive oxygen species into the cochlear compartments, subsequently inducing oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and ultimately culminating in the apoptosis of irreplaceable sensory hair cells. This mechanism represents a plausible and direct pathway linking a systemic metabolic insult to localized cochlear pathology.

To illustrate the potential mechanistic link between hypercholesterolemia and cochlear pathology, we utilized the Ldlr−/− mouse model. This well-characterized model enables the induction of profound and isolated hypercholesterolemia through a controlled high-fat diet.29,30,31 Over an 8-week dietary intervention, we conducted a comprehensive investigation designed to systematically evaluate several key aspects of this relationship. We firstly assessed the development of auditory functional deficits using comprehensive measurements of ABR thresholds to evaluate overall neural sound transmission and DPOAE to specifically probe the integrity of outer hair cell (OHC) amplification. Secondly, we examined the structural and functional integrity of the BLB through a combination of Evans Blue permeability assays, western blot analysis of tight junction proteins, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Finally, we quantified concomitant histopathological changes within the cochlea, focusing on the potential loss of sensory hair cells. These results of this investigation provide preclinical insights into a plausible mechanism by which hypercholesterolemia may contribute to hearing loss, highlighting the BLB as a critical structure of interest for future therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving auditory function in metabolically compromised individuals.

Results

High-fat diet in Ldlr−/− mice induces weight gain and liver steatosis

To investigate the impact of hypercholesterolemia on the auditory system, we first established a mouse model of diet-induced metabolic dysfunction. Ldlr−/− mice were fed a high-fat diet were set as hypercholesterolemia group (HC), while wild type (WT) mice received a standard normal diet were set as the control group (Ctrl). As illustrated in the experimental timeline (Figure 1A), a significant increase of body weight showed in HC mice, compared to Ctrl mice (Figure 1B), confirming the induction of obesity. Next, the development of hepatic steatosis, as a hallmark of metabolic syndrome, was assessed. Histological analysis of liver sections revealed substantial lipid accumulation in HC mice, as visualized by Oil Red O staining (Figure 1C). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significantly greater percentage of Oil Red O positive area in the livers of the high-fat diet (HFD) group compared to Ctrl group (Figure 1D). Consistent with the onset of liver injury, serum levels of the liver enzymes aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were markedly elevated in HC mice (Figures 1E and 1F). High-fat diet induces severe dyslipidemia in Ldlr−/− mice

Having confirmed the development of obesity and hepatic steatosis, we next characterized the systemic dyslipidemic phenotype of HC mice. After 8 weeks of dietary intervention, serum lipid profiles were analyzed. Macroscopically, serum of HC mice appeared markedly lipemic and turbid compared to the clear serum from Ctrl mice (Figure 2A), indicating a high concentration of circulating lipids. Biochemical analysis confirmed severe dyslipidemia in HC mice. Serum levels of total cholesterol (Figure 2B) and triglycerides (Figure 2C) were significantly elevated in the HC group compared to the Ctrl group. Furthermore, the diet intervention specifically altered the lipoprotein profile. While both high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, Figure 2D) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, Figure 2E) concentrations were significantly increased in HC mice, the profound rise in LDL-C, a key atherogenic lipoprotein, is particularly notable. High-fat diet induces progressive, frequency-dependent hearing loss in hypercholesteremic mice

To comprehensively evaluate the impact of hypercholesterolemia on auditory function, we longitudinally measured both the auditory brainstem response (ABR), which reflects the integrity of the entire neural auditory pathway, and distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAEs), which specifically assess the active amplification function of OHCs, in the Ctrl and HC mice over an 8-week dietary intervention period.

At the outset of the study (0 weeks) and after 2 weeks of the high-fat diet, both ABR (Figures 3A and 3B) and DPOAE (Figures 4A and 4B) thresholds were comparable between the Ctrl and HC groups across all tested frequencies (5.6–32 kHz), indicating normal baseline hearing and OHC function. The first signs of auditory impairment emerged after 4 weeks of the diet. The HC group began to exhibit significant elevations in ABR thresholds compared to the Ctrl group, with the deficit initially manifesting at the higher frequencies (16, 22.6, and 32 kHz) (Figure 3C). Crucially, a nearly identical pattern was observed in the DPOAE thresholds at the 4-week time point (Figure 4C), where the HC group showed significant threshold shifts specifically at the high frequencies. The auditory dysfunction progressed in severity and frequency range with continued dietary intervention. At 6 and 8 weeks, the HC mice displayed substantially elevated ABR thresholds that extended to mid-frequencies (8 and 11.3 kHz), in addition to the persistent high-frequency loss (Figures 3D and 3E). Representative ABR waveform traces also demonstrate reduced peak amplitudes and delayed latencies in HC mice, consistent with the elevated ABR thresholds we quantified (Figure S1). Similarly, the DPOAE thresholds in the HC group worsened markedly, showing profound deficits across almost the entire frequency spectrum by 8 weeks (Figures 4D and 4E). The persistent, parallel deterioration of both ABR and DPOAE thresholds provides compelling evidence that OHC damage is the principal mechanism underlying the progressive hearing loss in this model. However, the observation that the ABR threshold shifts were more severe than the DPOAE shifts at later time points suggests that prolonged hypercholesterolemia may also involve additional damage to other cochlear structures or the auditory nerve, which would not be captured by DPOAE measurements.

Hypercholesterolemia induces hair cell loss through apoptosis

To investigate the histopathological basis for the observed functional deficits in ABR and DPOAE, we next examined the integrity of sensory hair cells in the organ of Corti. Immunofluorescence staining for Myosin VIIa, a specific marker for hair cells, revealed a striking difference between groups. After 8-week dietary intervention, in the Ctrl mice, the organ of Corti exhibited the characteristic orderly array of a single row of IHCs and three rows of OHCs in the apical, middle, and basal turns. By contrast, the mice of the HC group showed a clear segment-specific pattern of hair cell degeneration. In the apical and middle turns, the overall architecture of the organ of Corti was largely preserved, with only scattered OHC loss and mild disruption of the regular row organization. In striking contrast, the basal turn displayed extensive degeneration, characterized by large gaps in the OHC rows, and near-complete loss of OHCs, while IHC loss was also evident (Figures 5A and 5B). Apoptotic markers including cleaved caspase-3, Bax, and Bcl-2 were also detected by western blot analysis of cochlear lysates from both groups of mice. Hypercholesterolemia disrupts the structural and functional integrity of the blood-labyrinth barrier

To investigate the upstream mechanism underlying the observed hair cell loss and functional hearing impairment, we assessed the integrity of the BLB, which is critical for cochlear homeostasis. We hypothesized that BLB breakdown, allowing the influx of toxic blood-derived components, could be a primary event leading to the subsequent pathology.

Functional assessment using an Evans Blue permeability assay revealed a stark contrast between groups. Cochlear tissues from the HC mice showed BLB disruption after 8 weeks HFD while the Ctrl mice exhibited normal BLB integrity (Figure 6A). Ultrastructural analysis by TEM corroborated this functional deficit. At the molecular level, western blot analysis revealed a mechanism for the barrier breakdown. A significant downregulation of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Claudin-5 were showed in the HC mice. Concurrently, upregulation of PLVAP, a marker of vascular permeability, and ICAM-1, a key inflammatory mediator, indicated a pro-permeability and pro-inflammatory state within the cochlear vasculature (Figures 6C and 6D).

To definitively establish the temporal sequence of BLB disruption relative to auditory dysfunction, we also analyzed cochlear tissues from mice at the 2-week timepoint, when ABR and DPOAE thresholds remained entirely normal. Evans Blue permeability assay at this early stage revealed mild but detectable dye leakage in the HC group, whereas the Ctrl group showed no evidence of extravasation (Figure S2A). Corroborating these functional findings, western blot analysis of 2-week cochlear lysates demonstrated reduced expression of the ZO-1 and Claudin-5, alongside increased levels of PLVAP and ICAM-1 in HC mice (Figures S2B and S2C). These results confirm that BLB disruption is an initiating event that precedes the onset of measurable hearing loss.

To address whether hypercholesterolemia-induced cochlear pathology is driven by local inflammation secondary to BLB disruption or systemic low-grade inflammation, we further characterized inflammatory responses in both the systemic circulation and cochlear tissue. Consistent with the observed ICAM-1 upregulation, ELISA analysis revealed significantly elevated serum levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) in the HC mice compared to the Ctrl (Figures S3A and S3B). Concurrently, qPCR of cochlear tissue lysates demonstrated a marked upregulation of Il6 and Tnf, transcripts in the HC mice (Figures S3C and S3D).

Collectively, these findings demonstrated that hypercholesterolemia disrupts the BLB, both structurally and functionally. This breach could compromise the cochlear microenvironment, which we propose initiates the impairments that ultimately lead to the hair cell degeneration and the consequent functional hearing deficits.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that diet-induced hypercholesterolemia in Ldlr−/− mice triggers a pathogenic cascade within the cochlea, initiated by the disruption of the BLB, which subsequently leads to OHC dysfunction, sensory cell loss, and ultimately, progressive high-frequency hearing loss. Our results provide a mechanistic explanation for the long-observed but poorly understood epidemiological link between dyslipidemia and SNHL by identifying BLB integrity as a critical early target of metabolic insult.

While the association of hypercholesterolemia with cardiovascular disease is well-established, its role as an independent risk factor for SNHL has been debated, primarily due to confounding comorbidities in clinical studies.8,32 By utilizing the Ldlr−/− mouse model, our study effectively isolates the effects of hypercholesterolemia. The successful induction of systemic dyslipidemia, evidenced by elevated serum cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL-C, alongside hepatic steatosis, created a controlled metabolic challenge. The emergence of auditory dysfunction specifically in this group, while wild-type controls on normal diet remained unaffected, strongly argues for a direct causal role of hypercholesterolemia in cochlear pathology, independent of aging or other common systemic diseases.

Our work elucidated that BLB disruption might be the pivotal early event of cochlear pathology in hypercholesterolemia. We demonstrated that hypercholesterolemia compromises the BLB through multiple lines of evidence: functional leakage of Evans Blue tracer, ultrastructural damage to endothelial tight junctions observed via TEM, and a corresponding molecular profile of downregulated tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Claudin-5) and upregulated permeability markers (PLVAP). This finding is conceptually significant as it extends the principle of vascular barrier vulnerability from BBB to analogous BLB.22,33 Given that BBB dysfunction is a recognized consequence of hypercholesterolemia, leading to neuroinflammation and neuronal damage, it is plausible that similar mechanisms, such as oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, are at play in the stria vascularis.26,28,34,35 The concurrent upregulation of ICAM-1 in our model supports the involvement of inflammatory processes. We proposed that BLB breakdown breaches cochlear immune privilege, permitting influx of cytotoxic mediators that disrupt ionic homeostasis essential for hair cell survival, as schematically summarized in our graphical abstract depicting this pathological cascade. Notably, the precise contribution of the innate immune system, particularly resident macrophages within the stria vascularis, to this inflammatory cascade remains an important area for future investigation.36 As key regulators of lipid metabolism and local inflammation, stria vascularis macrophages may sense elevated lipid levels in the hypercholesterolemic microenvironment, undergo phenotypic activation, and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines that further amplify BLB disruption and cochlear injury.37 Although our present study did not directly assess macrophage phenotypes, future work will be needed to characterize the activation state, lipid-handling capacity, and spatial distribution of strial macrophages in hypercholesterolemic cochleae, and to determine whether targeted modulation of these cells can mitigate BLB breakdown and subsequent hair cell loss.

We also proposed that BLB breakdown and the resulting disturbance of ion and fluid homeostasis in the lateral wall may lead to a chronic reduction in the EP, which is essential for maintaining the electrochemical driving force for OHC function and survival. A sustained decline in EP might contribute to OHC degeneration, acting as an upstream event in the degenerative cascade.38,39,40 It is also important to acknowledge that hypercholesterolemia may exert direct effects on OHC membrane function that act in parallel with BLB disruption, representing a complementary mechanism of auditory dysfunction. Previous study demonstrated that cholesterol modulation can alter OHC membrane properties and electromotility by regulating prestin distribution.41 Our current data do not rule out the possibility that such direct cholesterol-mediated effects on OHCs contribute to the observed hearing impairment, alongside the BLB disruption-driven pathogenic cascade we have characterized. This dual mechanism framework is consistent with the complex nature of metabolic insults, where systemic dyslipidemia may target both vascular barriers and sensory cells directly to exacerbate cochlear pathology. Future studies should aim to dissect the relative contributions of these two pathways, for example by evaluating OHC membrane function and prestin localization in models where BLB integrity is preserved despite hypercholesterolemia.

Our data supported a sequential pathological process. The initial functional impairment, reflected by the parallel elevation of ABR and DPOAE thresholds after diet intervention, points specifically to OHCs dysfunction as the primary defect. This is a critical observation because DPOAEs are a direct measure of the electromechanical activity of OHCs.42 The fact that ABR thresholds, which measure the entire neural pathway, showed a nearly identical pattern at this early stage suggests that the hearing loss was predominantly cochlear in origin.43,44 However, whether OHC dysfunction or EP reduction is the primary initiating event remains unclear. An alternative interpretation is that a chronic reduction in EP occurs first as a consequence of BLB disruption and lateral wall dysfunction, and that this sustained EP decline subsequently triggers secondary damage to OHCs, as suggested in previous work on EP-related cochlear degeneration.38,39,40

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence that hypercholesterolemia is not merely associated with but is a direct cause of hearing loss through a mechanism involving the structural and functional disruption of the BLB. This breach in cochlear vascular homeostasis initiates a degenerative cascade culminating in hair cell death and auditory dysfunction. By positioning the BLB as a critical mediator of metabolic hearing loss, our work not only deepens the understanding of the pathophysiology but also identifies a promising new target for the development of otoprotective strategies against this common yet overlooked complication of dyslipidemia.

Limitations of the study

Our study, while providing mechanistic insights, has certain inherent limitations that warrant consideration. The Ldlr−/− mouse model, although well-suited for studying systemic hypercholesterolemia, does not allow for the precise attribution of BLB dysfunction to specific cellular components, such as endothelial cells or pericytes. Future investigations utilizing cell-type-specific knockout models could more effectively delineate the relative contributions of distinct BLB constituents to the observed barrier breakdown. Furthermore, the specific inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways that are activated downstream of BLB compromise have not been fully elucidated in this work. A more detailed temporal analysis of cytokine dynamics and markers of oxidative damage within the cochlea would help to clarify the key mediators responsible for initiating hair cell apoptosis. Importantly, future studies incorporating direct EP measurements and time-resolved assessment of lateral wall pathology and hair cell integrity will be necessary to rigorously test whether EP reduction indeed precedes and drives secondary OHC damage in hypercholesterolemia.

An important direction for subsequent research would be to explore whether targeted pharmacological or lifestyle interventions are capable of preserving BLB integrity and, consequently, preventing the progression of hearing loss in this experimental context.

This study used only male mice, sex-related differences were not analyzed, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

In vivo mice studies, male Ldlr−/− and WT mice on a C57BL/6J background were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. All mice were 8 weeks of age at the start of experiments. Animals were housed in a specific pathogen–free facility with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, controlled temperature (22 ± 1 °C), and humidity (50% ± 10%). Standard food and water were provided ad libitum. WT mice received a control diet (D12450B, Research Diets), and Ldlr−/− mice were fed a high-fat diet (D12492, Research Diets) for 8 weeks. Body weight and food intake were monitored weekly. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (No. 2023-X16–51). This study used only male mice. Sex differences in hypercholesterolemia-induced cochlear injury were not assessed, and the generalizability of findings to female mice is limited, which is acknowledged as a limitation of this research.

Method details

Hepatic lipid analysis by Oil-Red-O staining

Following the dietary intervention period, animals were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a ketamine/xylazine solution (120 mg/kg and 24 mg/kg body weight, respectively) prepared in 0.9% saline. For hepatic lipid accumulation assessment, liver were carefully dissected out and were immediately embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) compound and rapidly frozen on dry ice. Serial sections were cut at a thickness of 8–10 μm. Sections were fixed in 10% formalin, rinsed in 60% isopropanol, and incubated in a filtered 0.5% Oil Red O solution for 15 min. After differentiation in 60% isopropanol, sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Lipid droplet area (red) was quantified as a percentage of total area from five random fields per sample using ImageJ software.

Serum biochemical analysis

Following an 8-h fasting period, blood samples were obtained from mice via retro-orbital bleeding under mild anesthesia. The samples were allowed to clot at room temperature and then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min to separate serum. The resulting serum was aliquoted and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Biochemical assessments were performed using an automated Chemray 800 analyzer (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., China). The measured parameters included liver function markers—alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)—as well as key lipid profile components: total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). All assays were conducted following the manufacturer’s standardized protocols.

Auditory brainstem response measurement

Auditory function was assessed by recording the auditory brainstem response (ABR) as previously described at baseline (0 weeks) and then at 2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks following the initiation of the dietary intervention.45,46 Mice were anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of a ketamine/xylazine mixture (100 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg, respectively) to ensure immobility and eliminate muscle artifacts. All recordings were conducted in a double-walled acoustic chamber under thermostatically controlled conditions (37°C). ABR thresholds were elicited using tone-burst stimuli at frequencies of 5.6, 8, 11.3, 16, 22.6, and 32 kHz, delivered monaurally through a high-frequency magnetic speaker placed close to the animal’s left ear. Subdermal needle electrodes were positioned at the vertex (active), below the pinna of the stimulated ear (reference), and at the tail (ground). The bioelectrical signals were amplified, filtered (300–3000 Hz band-pass filter), and averaged over 512 presentations for each stimulus level. The ABR threshold was defined as the lowest sound intensity level (in dB SPL) at which a reproducible wave pattern, specifically wave I or wave IV, could be clearly identified. To minimize bias, all ABR measurements and threshold determinations were performed by an investigator who was blinded to the experimental group assignments.

Distortion product otoacoustic emissions

DPOAEs were assessed as previously described at baseline (0 weeks) and then at 2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks following the initiation of the dietary intervention.47,48 Mice were anesthetized using an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine/xylazine (100 mg/kg and 2.5 mg/kg, respectively). The 2f1–f2 DPOAE response was evaluated in the left ear at f2 frequencies of 5.6, 8, 11.32, 16, 22.6, and 32 kHz. Acoustic stimuli were delivered using two MF-1 speakers (Tucker-Davis Technology, Alachua, FL, USA), while responses were captured with an ER10B+ low-noise microphone system (Etymotic Research, Elk Grove Village, IL, USA). Probe fit was verified using strict criteria: ear canal volume between 0.1 and 0.3 mL, acoustic leakage≤5 dB SPL at 1 kHz, and background noise floor ≤ −10 dB SPL across 5.6–32 kHz; the probe was repositioned until these criteria were met. For DP-gram analysis, the 2f1–f2 distortion product paradigm was used with a fixed frequency ratio f2/f1 = 1.22, and f2 frequencies were swept across 5.6, 8, 11.32, 16, 22.6, and 32 kHz; primary tone levels were set at L1 = 65 dB SPL and L2 = 55 dB SPL (L1-L2 = 10 dB), with continuous stimuli presented for 500 ms per frequency step (50 ms rise/fall time). Signal generation and data acquisition were controlled by a custom MATLAB script (v24.1, MathWorks) coupled with a National Instruments USB-6346 A/D interface; responses were digitized at 100 kHz. For the FFT parameters, we performed the fast Fourier transform with a 1024-point length, a 50 ms time window, and a Hanning window to optimize the signal-to-noise ratio and frequency resolution. The noise floor was calculated as the mean amplitude of spectral bins ±12 Hz adjacent to the 2f1–f2 frequency bin, and a strict data rejection strategy was applied: online rejection of sweeps with peak amplitudes exceeding ±3 SD from the mean, and post-processing rejection of data points with a coefficient of variation (CV) > 15% across sweeps (these measurements were repeated). A measurable DP response was defined as a DPOAE amplitude at the 2f1–f2 frequency that was at least 3 dB above the noise floor (SNR ≥3 dB), and DPOAE thresholds were the lowest stimulus level (L1) at which such a response was detected; DPOAE data were discarded and retested if any of the following conditions were met: the probe fit failed to meet the aforementioned validation criteria; the noise floor exceeded −10 dB SPL at any tested frequency; the animal exhibited excessive movement during the testing procedure. All measurements and analyses were performed independently by two investigators blinded to experimental group assignments to minimize observer bias.

Transmission electron microscopy

Cochleae were harvested immediately after euthanasia for ultrastructural analysis of BLB as previously described.49 The otic bulla was dissected, and the surrounding bony capsule was meticulously removed under a stereomicroscope to expose the cochlear tissue. The samples were initially fixed by immersion in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 48 h at 4°C. Subsequently, decalcification was carried out in a 10% EDTA for approximately two weeks at 4°C, with the solution changed every 2days. Following thorough rinsing with 0.1 M phosphate buffer, the decalcified cochleae were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h at room temperature. Samples were then dehydrated through a graded series of acetone (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%). Dehydration was followed by a gradual infiltration process using an epoxy resin embedding medium, first with a mixture of acetone and resin (2:1) for 3 h, then acetone and resin (1:2) overnight, and finally pure resin for 2 h at 37°C. The infiltrated tissues were embedded in fresh resin and polymerized in a sequence of 37°C overnight, 45°C for 12 h, and 60°C for 24 h. For preliminary orientation, semi-thin sections (1.5 μm) were cut using a glass knife, stained with 1% toluidine blue, and examined under a light microscope to identify the region of interest. Subsequently, 50 nm ultra-thin sections were prepared using a diamond knife, mounted on copper grids, and double-stained with 3% uranyl acetate and Reynolds’ lead citrate. The sections were observed and imaged using Hitachi H-7650 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) operated at 80 kV. The integrity of the BLB was assessed by examining the morphology of capillary endothelial cells, pericytes, and the surrounding basement membrane.

Western blot

Western Blot was conducted as previously described with modification.50 Samples were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA Lysis Buffer (Beijing, China) and incubated on ice for 30 min to facilitate protein extraction. The resulting lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for protein quantification using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). Protein concentrations were normalized to ensure equal loading across samples. Equal amounts of protein from each sample were resolved by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Proteins were first concentrated on a 6% stacking gel and then separated on a 12% resolving gel. Subsequently, the separated proteins were electrophoretically transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature to prevent nonspecific binding. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-Claudin-5 (Proteintech, Cat No. 29767-1-AP), ZO-1 (Proteintech, Cat No. 21773-1-AP), PLVAP (Proteintech, Cat No. 14452-1-AP), ICAM-1 (Proteintech, Cat No. 10831-1-AP), β-actin (CST, Cat No. 4967). After three 10-min washes with TBST, the membranes were probed with appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit, CST, Cat No.7074; anti-mouse, CST, Cat No. 7076) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system.

Cochlear immunofluorescence

Cochlear Immunofluorescence was conducted as previously described with modification.51 Following dissection, cochleae were immediately fixed by immersion in 4% paraformaldehyde (in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) overnight at 4°C. Decalcification was then performed using a 10% EDTA for 5 days at 4°C. After thorough washing with PBS, the decalcified cochleae were micro-dissected into three turns, apical, middle, and basal, for subsequent processing. The dissected cochlear turns were permeabilized and blocked with a solution containing 5% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Tissues were then incubated overnight at 4°C with a rabbit polyclonal primary antibody against Myosin VIIa (1:200, Proteus Biosciences, Cat No.25-6790), a specific marker for auditory hair cells. Following three washes in PBS, the samples were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Negative control samples, processed in parallel with the omission of the primary antibody, showed no specific staining. Imaging was performed using a ZEISS Axio Observer 7 epifluorescence microscope equipped with an Apotome 3 structured illumination unit for optical sectioning (Carl Zeiss AG, Jena, Germany). For quantitative analysis of hair cell survival, the number of Myosin VIIa-positive IHCs and OHCs was counted along the entire length of the basilar membrane for each turn. The counts were normalized and expressed as the number of cells per view. To minimize bias, the cell counting was performed by one investigator who was blinded to the experimental groups, while a second investigator conducted the subsequent data analysis and graphing using ImageJ software (Version1.46r, National Institutes of Health, USA).

Blood-labyrinth barrier permeability evaluation using evans blue assay

The integrity of the BLB in the stria vascularis was assessed by evaluating the extravasation of the non-permeable tracer Evans Blue, as previously described with modifications.52 Briefly, Two hours prior to sacrifice, 2% (w/v) solution of Evans Blue (Sigma-Aldrich, E2129) in saline was administered to mice via the tail intravenous injection at a dosage of 0.5 mL per 100 g of body weight. Following the circulation period, the animals were euthanized. The stria vascularis tissues were meticulously dissected out following the same as the protocol described above. The harvested tissues were fixed and processed accordingly. The prepared samples were then examined under an Olympus IX81 inverted confocal microscope equipped with an Olympus Fluoview FV1000 imaging system. The degree of Evans Blue leakage, observed as fluorescence signal diffusion around the capillaries, was qualitatively assessed and quantitatively analyzed. Fluorescence intensity and distribution were processed and quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) to provide a semi-quantitative measure of BLB permeability.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0.2). Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SD. The exact value is provided in each figure legend and represents the number of biological replicates (mice) per group. For representative images (including confocal microscopy, TEM, and Western blots), the number of independent experimental replicates is indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Significance is denoted as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001,∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. Data normality was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test prior to parametric statistical testing; unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used for comparisons between two independent groups; two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was used for body weight gain analysis over time; two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was used for ABR and DPOAE threshold comparisons across multiple frequencies and time points; two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for hair cell quantification across cochlear turns, and two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for Western blot densitometric quantifications. All statistical details, including the specific tests used, exact n values, definitions of biological replicates, mean ± SD, and significance levels, are fully reported in the corresponding figure legends.
