Reacting CO2 with Light Alkanes to Value-Added Products
Authors: Yong Yuan, Jingguang G. Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Catalytic conversion of anthropogenic carbon dioxide (CO2) into value-added products is a promising strategy to mitigate global carbon emissions. Concurrently, the shale gas revolution has provided an abundant supply of light alkanes (methane, ethane, propane, and butane), presenting a unique opportunity to employ these underutilized hydrocarbons as an effective, low-cost hydrogen source for CO2 reduction. In this Perspective, we summarize past efforts, current state, and future opportunities for reacting CO2 with light alkanes to generate a diverse range of value-added products. Compared with direct alkane conversion, the introduction of CO2 fundamentally alters reaction thermodynamics and kinetics, enabling selective C–H and C–C bond activation while suppressing catalyst deactivation from coke formation. Building on decades of research in dry reforming and CO2-assisted dehydrogenation, recent advances in catalyst design have enabled CO2-assisted dehydrogenation processes that approach chemical equilibrium for the selective production of olefins and syngas. Importantly, advances in catalyst design and reactor engineering have further expanded the product scope beyond gas-phase (syngas and olefins) to include liquid-phase (oxygenates and aromatics), and solid-phase products (carbon nanomaterials). We highlight key catalyst design principles for controlling reaction pathways and discuss major challenges and opportunities in developing selective and versatile platforms for the simultaneous upgrading of CO2 and light alkanes.

Introduction

The rapid increase in atmospheric CO2 concentration has drawn global attention due to its adverse effects on climate change and ocean acidification. Converting CO2 into value-added products represents a promising strategy for mitigating environmental impacts while simultaneously meeting the growing demand for carbon-based chemicals, fuels, and materials. − Consequently, significant research efforts have been devoted to carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technologies, − with catalytic CO2 conversion emerging as a particularly attractive pathway. − However, a fundamental challenge in large-scale CO2 utilization is identifying a sustainable and low-carbon source of hydrogen to drive the reduction reactions. Currently, approximately 95% of the global commercial H2 supply is “gray hydrogen”, derived primarily via methane steam reforming or coal gasification. These processes are highly energy-intensive and generate substantial CO2 emissions. As a result, relying on molecular H2 for CO2 hydrogenation is unlikely to achieve net reductions in CO2 emissions under current industrial conditions. Developing alternative hydrogen sources that can drive CO2 reduction without introducing additional carbon emissions therefore remains a critical challenge for practical CO2 utilization.

In parallel with the increasing interest in CO2 conversion, the shale gas revolution offers a viable alternative to crude oil for meeting energy and resource demands. , Advances in horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing have enabled the large-scale production of natural gas, resulting in an abundant supply of light alkanes such as methane (CH4), ethane (C2H6), propane (C3H8), and butane (C4H10), a substantial fraction of which remains underutilized or is directly flared. Efficient upgrading of these abundant hydrocarbons into value-added products such as olefins, oxygenates, aromatics, and materials has therefore become an important research direction. − Importantly, light alkanes can serve as both carbon and hydrogen sources in reactions with CO2, providing a route to integrate hydrocarbon upgrading with carbon utilization. , Coupling CO2 with light alkanes offers a pathway toward carbon-efficient, and potentially carbon-neutral chemical production.

However, the combined reactions of CO2 and light alkanes to value-added products pose significant catalytic challenges. Both reactants are thermodynamically stable and kinetically inert, requiring catalysts capable of selectively activating strong C–H bonds in alkanes while simultaneously facilitating CO2 reduction. Conventional alkane upgrading processes, including direct alkane dehydrogenation and alkane dehydroaromatization, typically operate at high temperatures owing to the thermodynamic constraint, and frequently suffer from rapid catalyst deactivation caused by coke formation. , Introducing CO2 as a coreactant provides a promising pathway for overcoming these challenges. , Compared with traditional nonoxidative alkane activation or O2-based oxidative processes, CO2 acts as a milder oxidant that enables controlled dehydrogenation while suppressing the undesired overoxidation of hydrocarbon intermediates. In addition, CO2 can directly participate in C–C bond formation and facilitate carbon incorporation into products. From a mechanistic perspective, the selectivity of CO2-assisted alkane conversion is governed by the interplay among C–H, C–C, and C–O bond activation steps. By tuning this balance through catalyst design and reactor engineering, reaction pathways can be directed toward different products.

Over the past decades, CO2-assisted alkane activation has evolved from a traditional reforming chemistry into a versatile platform that integrates hydrocarbon upgrading with carbon utilization (Figure ). Early studies focused on dry reforming to produce syngas (H2 and CO), followed by CO2-assisted oxidative dehydrogenation for olefin production. More recently, advances in catalyst design and reactor engineering have expanded the product scope to oxygenates, aromatics, and carbon nanomaterials such as carbon nanofibers (CNFs) and carbon nanotubes (CNTs). Despite this progress, the field remains largely catalyst-centric, with limited attention to separation challenges arising from increasingly complex product mixtures and their downstream utilization. The incorporation of CO2 as a coreactant generates more complex effluent streams than direct alkane conversion, making product separation a critical yet often overlooked bottleneck. Tandem strategies that directly upgrade primary products without intermediate separation offer a promising pathway to overcome this limitation and improve overall efficiency. Products such as liquid oxygenates and aromatics, as well as solid carbon nanomaterials, are particularly well suited for such integrated approaches. In this Perspective, we focus on three key aspects of CO2-assisted light alkane conversion: thermodynamic constraints governing reaction feasibility, atomic-level catalyst design for selective bond scission and formation, and tandem catalytic strategies for products that cannot be easily achieved using a single reactor (Figure ). By integrating insights from mechanistic studies, in situ characterization, and computational modeling, we aim to provide a unified framework for understanding CO2-assisted alkane chemistries and to guide the development of next-generation catalytic systems for simultaneously upgrading CO2 and light alkanes.

Reacting CO2 and Light Alkanes to Syngas and Olefins

Combining CO2 reduction with alkane conversion fundamentally alters the thermodynamics of hydrocarbon activation. Compared with conventional alkane upgrading processes, the presence of CO2 introduces additional reaction pathways that influence both reaction equilibrium and product selectivity. Early studies of CO2-alkane chemistry primarily focused on dry reforming reactions, in which hydrocarbons react with CO2 to produce syngas that can be used for downstream processes such as Fischer–Tropsch synthesis and methanol production. − Although dry reforming remains an important route for syngas generation, the reaction network typically favors extensive C–C bond cleavage. As a result, it is less suitable for pathways that preserve C–C bonds required for the synthesis of multicarbon hydrocarbons and oxygenates. Recently, increasing attention has shifted toward CO2-assisted alkane dehydrogenation, which enables the selective formation of olefins while simultaneously reducing CO2 to CO. The CO2-assisted alkane conversion generally proceeds through two distinct routes: (1) oxidative dehydrogenation of alkanes mediated by lattice oxygen that can be regenerated from CO2 dissociation and (2) tandem coupling between direct alkane dehydrogenation and reverse water–gas shift (RWGS) reactions (Figure ). These two catalytic paradigms provide a conceptual framework for understanding CO2-assisted alkane activation. These pathways can be distinguished based on conversion trends and product selectivities. In oxidative dehydrogenation, CO2 conversion is typically comparable to or higher than alkane conversion, reflecting its role in generating reactive oxygen species. In contrast, the tandem dehydrogenation-RWGS pathway shows alkane conversion comparable to or higher than that of CO2 conversion, with high olefin selectivity and near-stoichiometric CO formation from CO2. Further insight can be obtained by comparing alkane conversion in the presence and absence of CO2: a strong dependence indicates oxidative dehydrogenation, whereas similar conversion levels suggest a tandem dehydrogenation-RWGS mechanism.

Thermodynamics Analysis and the Role of CO2

Thermodynamic analysis provides a quantitative understanding of the role of CO2. As illustrated in Figure a, equilibrium calculations for the CO2–C2H6 system, a representative case involving C–H, C–C, and C–O bond transformations, reveal that the presence of CO2 alters the equilibrium composition of the reaction mixture as a function of temperature. , In particular, when dry reforming is excluded, CO2-assisted oxidative dehydrogenation of ethane can be regarded as the coupling of direct ethane dehydrogenation with the RWGS reaction, in which CO2 reacts with H2 to form CO and H2O. Since H2 is continuously consumed through this reaction, the equilibrium of alkane dehydrogenation can be shifted toward olefin formation. This coupling provides a key advantage for CO2-assisted alkane activation. At elevated temperatures, the system evolves toward a mixture of olefins, CO, and H2. In CO2-assisted alkane conversion, CO2 plays three fundamental roles: removing H2 that shifts the equilibrium of alkane dehydrogenation, acting as a mild oxidant that removes coke via the reverse Boudouard reaction (CO2 + C → 2CO), and serving as a carbon source that participates in the formation of carbon-containing molecules.

Compared to direct light alkane conversion, the incorporation of CO2 not only alters the thermodynamic equilibrium but also modulates catalyst structure and reaction pathways. Under reaction conditions, the oxidative environment created by CO2 or H2O (a byproduct of the reaction) together with hydrocarbon reactants can induce dynamic structural evolution of catalytic active sites. For example, metallic clusters formed upon reduction may undergo partial oxidation (Figure b), while alloy catalysts can restructure to form metal–metal oxide interfacial sites. In-situ characterization techniques have been utilized to identify these changes under reaction conditions. Recent studies have highlighted that such dynamically generated interfaces play a critical role in controlling the reaction pathway by facilitating CO2/alkanes activation and stabilizing reaction intermediates. ,,

The reaction mechanisms differ fundamentally from those of conventional direct dehydrogenation pathways. As shown in Figure c, one common pathway in CO2-alkane systems involves direct alkane dehydrogenation, which proceeds through sequential C–H bond cleavage followed by hydrogen desorption, with CO2 participating only via the RWGS reaction in the gas phase. In contrast, CO2-assisted oxidative dehydrogenation follows a Mars-van Krevelen (MvK) mechanism, in which lattice oxygen from the oxide component directly participates in hydrogen abstraction from the alkane. The resulting oxygen vacancies are subsequently replenished through CO2 dissociation, completing the catalytic redox cycle. These mechanistic differences highlight the distinct role of CO2 in alkane activation.

CO2-Assisted Oxidative Dehydrogenation

Thermodynamic analysis defines the overall feasibility of CO2-assisted alkane conversion, while catalyst design determines which reaction pathways are kinetically accessible. Selective conversion of light alkanes requires catalysts capable of controlling the activation of C–H, C–C, and C–O bonds. Early studies focused primarily on metal or alloy catalysts supported by reducible oxides, such as CeO2. Catalysts including Pt/CeO2, PtCo/CeO2, and PtNi/CeO2 have demonstrated high activity for dry reforming reactions, with bimetallic alloys typically exhibiting higher CO2 and light alkane conversions. − In these systems, metal-oxide interfacial sites play a critical role in activating CO2 through the oxygen vacancies on the oxide surface. CO2 dissociation at these sites generates reactive oxygen species that remove carbon deposits and facilitate hydrogen abstraction. However, while these catalysts are highly effective for syngas production through reforming reactions, they often suffer from poor selectivity toward dehydrogenation products. Metallic surfaces capable of efficiently activating C–H bonds typically also possess low activation barriers for C–C bond cleavage, leading to reforming pathways rather than selective olefin formation.

To address this challenge, recent research has focused on engineering metal–metal oxide interfacial sites that selectively promote C–H activation while suppressing C–C bond scission. As shown in Figure a,b, the catalytic performance of NiFe/CeO2 for CO2-assisted ethane dehydrogenation strongly depends on the Ni/Fe ratio. Increasing Fe content shifts the reaction pathway from reforming toward selective dehydrogenation, leading to significantly higher olefin selectivity at the expense of activity. Notably, product selectivity is strongly dependent on ethane conversion and should be only compared at similar conversion levels. In Figure b, this is achieved by adjusting the space velocity of Ni3Fe1/CeO2 to match the conversion of Ni1Fe3/CeO2. In-situ characterization studies reveal that the Ni1Fe3/CeO2 catalyst forms an Fe-rich NiFe alloy under reductive conditions, but undergoes structural evolution in a CO2 atmosphere, where Fe in the alloy is partially oxidized to form FeO x species. These transformations generate FeO x -Ni interfacial sites that facilitate CO2 activation and stabilize reaction intermediates. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further indicate that on pure Ni surfaces, the activation barriers for C–H bond cleavage and C–C bond scission are similar, making it difficult to control product selectivity. In contrast, interfacial FeO x -Ni structures increase the kinetic barrier for C–C bond scission while maintaining low barriers for C–H activation (Figure c,d). As a result, ethane activation preferentially proceeds through dehydrogenation rather than reforming pathways.

Similar design principles have also been demonstrated in other catalytic systems. − As shown in Figure e–h, Pd-based bimetallic catalysts such as Pd1Fe3/CeO2 and Pd1In3/CeO2 exhibit high C2H4 selectivity in CO2-assisted ethane conversion, where in situ characterization confirms the formation of metal-oxide interfacial structures under reaction conditions. Descriptor-based analysis further identifies two key parameters governing selectivity across Pd-based systems: the formation energy of the PdM x alloy surface and the binding energy of oxygen species. These descriptors determine whether the catalyst stabilizes an alloy surface or reconstructs into a metal-oxide interface during reaction. Catalysts favoring stable alloy surfaces tend to promote oxygenate intermediates and C–C bond cleavage for dry reforming, whereas metal-oxide interfaces weaken oxygen binding and promote C–H activation via oxidative dehydrogenation. Mapping these descriptors across a wide range of Pd-based bimetallic catalysts enables the identification of catalyst compositions that preferentially promote C–H activation or C–C bond scission, thereby providing a rational guideline for designing selective catalysts in CO2-assisted alkane conversion.

Tandem Dehydrogenation-RWGS Pathway

Although CO2-assisted oxidative pathways can activate alkanes through redox cycles, competitive adsorption between hydrocarbons and CO2 on the catalyst surface limit effective reactant activation, while the presence of reactive oxygen species may promote undesired C–C bond scission and reforming reactions. As a result, achieving high olefin selectivity at practical conversion levels remains challenging in CO2-assisted alkane dehydrogenation systems. An alternative strategy is to design bifunctional catalysts that combine direct alkane dehydrogenation with the RWGS reaction, in which CO2 primarily consumes H2 rather than oxidizing alkanes.

Zhai et al. developed PtSn/SiO2 catalysts (Figure a–c) and demonstrated that, in the presence of CO2, propane conversion can exceed the equilibrium limitation of direct propane dehydrogenation, enabling higher olefin yields at similar reaction temperatures. Structural characterization and DFT calculations reveal that two types of active sites are present under reaction conditions: interfacial PtSn/SnO x and Pt/SnO x sites. These sites provide distinct catalytic functions, where the PtSn alloy favors propane dehydrogenation while Pt-SnO x interfacial sites promote RWGS activity. Such catalyst design has also been demonstrated through the development of multimetallic alloy systems. Xing et al. showed that carefully combining several metal components within a single catalyst can balance the requirements of alkane activation, CO2 activation, and reaction selectivity. , Typically, Pt serve as the primary centers for C–H bond activation, transition metals such as Co or Ni promote CO2 activation, and other metals such as Sn, In, or Ga introduce ensemble effects that suppress undesired side reactions. This strategy led to the development of Pt–Co–In catalysts and high-entropy intermetallic systems such as PtCoNiSnInGa, which exhibit both high propane conversion and high propylene selectivity. The incorporation of multiple metal components allows the catalyst to balance the rates of propane dehydrogenation and RWGS reactions while maintaining structural stability under reaction conditions.

Another strategy to couple alkane dehydrogenation with the RWGS reaction is to construct tandem catalytic systems in which the two reactions occur on spatially separated catalytic sites. In such configurations, dehydrogenation catalysts first convert alkanes into olefins and H2, while a RWGS catalyst subsequently consumes the generated H2 through reaction with CO2. Compared with multifunctional catalysts, tandem architecture enables independent control over the catalytic sites responsible for alkane activation and CO2 reduction. The performance of these systems, however, is influenced by the spatial proximity between the two catalytic domains. Tian et al. reported a tandem catalytic system in which PtSn/Al2O3 catalyzes propane dehydrogenation to generate propylene and hydrogen, whereas adjacent CeO x /Al2O3 activates CO2 and catalyzes RWGS (Figure d–f). The catalytic performance was found to depend strongly on the distance between the two components: decreasing the separation from millimeter to nanometer scale significantly increased propylene formation rates while suppressing undesired dry reforming. Mechanistic studies further revealed that H atoms generated during dehydrogenation spill over from PtSn sites to neighboring CeO x domains, where it reacts with CO2 through RWGS. This hydrogen-spillover-mediated coupling consumes hydrogen generated during dehydrogenation, shifting the equilibrium toward olefin formation and enabling propylene selectivity approaching 99%.

Overall, the two reaction pathways require distinct catalyst compositions and exhibit different advantages and limitations. For CO2-assisted oxidative dehydrogenation, catalysts containing metal–metal oxide interfacial sites, such as Ni-FeO x , typically provide high olefin selectivity. Although nonprecious metal catalysts can achieve high alkene selectivity, alkane conversion typically maintains far below thermodynamic equilibrium. In contrast, tandem pathways that couple direct alkane dehydrogenation with the RWGS reaction rely on bifunctional catalyst systems consisting of highly active dehydrogenation sites and separate RWGS-active components. Achieving near-equilibrium alkane conversion often requires highly active Pt-based dehydrogenation catalysts, while maintaining catalyst stability in the presence of CO2 remains a major challenge because CO2 can alter surface structures and accelerate catalyst deactivation. Despite these challenges, tandem dehydrogenation-RWGS pathways provide a promising strategy for achieving efficient CO2-alkane upgrading with improved carbon utilization.

Another important factor influencing the preferred reaction pathway is the type of light alkane. As the carbon number increases from ethane to propane and butane, C–H bond activation becomes easier, allowing dehydrogenation reactions to proceed at lower temperatures. At the same time, higher hydrocarbons become increasingly susceptible to undesired cracking via C–C bond cleavage, especially under oxidative conditions. Consequently, tandem dehydrogenation-RWGS pathways are more suitable for higher alkanes because they better preserve carbon chains while still enabling CO2 utilization. The key challenge is therefore to design catalytic sites that selectively activate C–H bonds while minimizing C–C bond scission and dry reforming side reactions.

Reacting CO2 and Light Alkanes to Liquid Products

While current studies of CO2-assisted light alkane conversion primarily focused on the production of olefins and syngas, recent advances have expanded toward a broader range of value-added products. By coupling the initial high-temperature reaction of CO2 and alkanes with downstream transformations, tandem catalytic systems enable the synthesis of liquid products such as oxygenates and aromatics. These emerging examples highlight that CO2-assisted alkane reactions serve as a modular platform for producing a diverse group of value-added fuels and chemicals.

Oxygenates

Oxygenated compounds are an important class of chemicals widely used as solvents, fuels, and chemical intermediates, with C3 oxygenates such as propanal and 1-propanol being particularly valuable due to their applications in fuel blending, polymer synthesis, and pharmaceutical production. , Traditionally, these compounds are produced through hydroformylation (oxo synthesis), in which olefins react with externally supplied syngas under high pressure using homogeneous Rh- or Co-based catalysts. − This process typically involves multiple upstream steps, including alkane cracking or dehydrogenation to generate olefins and fossil-based routes to produce syngas. In contrast, CO2-assisted alkane activation enables the in situ generation of olefins, CO, and H2 within a single reactor. When coupled with heterogeneous hydroformylation catalysts in tandem reactor configurations, these intermediates can be directly converted into oxygenates without gas-phase separation.

Thermodynamic analysis (Figure a) reveals that hydroformylation is favorable at low temperatures (below 200 °C), whereas upstream CO2-assisted light alkane activation requires much higher temperatures (above 600 °C), necessitating tandem reactors running at different temperatures. In such systems, the upstream reactor generates an appropriate ratio of C2H4, CO, and H2, while hydroformylation proceeds in a downstream reactor operating at lower temperatures. The performance of the CO2-assisted C2H6 conversion over an Fe3Ni1/CeO2 is illustrated in Figure b. By tuning the upstream temperature, the product composition can be adjusted, , with an approximately 1:1:1 ratio of C2H4, CO, and H2 obtained at 750 °C. This balance is critical, as insufficient CO limits carbonyl insertion, whereas excess H2 promotes undesired hydrogenation. −

Conventional homogeneous hydroformylation processes using Rh- or Co-based catalysts typically require high reaction pressures (e.g., 10 or 20 bar) and present challenges in product separation and catalyst recycling. To address these challenges, Xie et al. developed a heterogeneous Rh1Co3/MCM-41 catalyst that enables efficient ethylene hydroformylation at ambient pressure. As shown in Figure c, Rh–Co bimetallic catalysts exhibit significantly higher oxygenate yields than their monometallic counterparts. The tandem results are summarized in Figure d, with the highest ethane-based yield of C3 oxygenates (4.7%) being achieved using Fe3Ni1/CeO2 at 800 °C in the first reactor and Rh1Co3/MCM-41 at 200 °C in the second reactor. These results highlight that CO2 not only promotes ethane activation but also participates in downstream C–C bond formation by supplying the carbonyl group required for hydroformylation.

A key challenge, however, is the development of hydroformylation catalysts that are highly active and selective under ambient pressure. Yuan et al. constructed isolated Rh–Co sites confined within a ZSM-5 zeolite (Figure e–g) to enhance ethylene hydroformylation activity and selectivity while maintaining catalyst stability under ambient conditions. The presence of Co prevents the aggregation of isolated Rh atoms into nanoparticles. Isolated Rh1Co3 clusters confined in ZSM-5 exhibit higher hydroformylation rates and selectivity toward C3 oxygenates (Figure f,g), showing a volcano-type dependence on Co coordination. Other promoters, such as metal oxides (e.g., ReO x and WO x ), have also been reported to enhance hydroformylation selectivity. , Although a broad range of catalysts is currently under development, , practical integration with upstream CO2-assisted alkane activation requires hydroformylation catalysts that remain active and selective under ambient pressure and can tolerate fluctuating reactant ratios and impurities in upstream effluents.

Aromatics

Aromatics, particularly benzene, toluene, and xylenes (known as BTX), are essential platform chemicals widely used in the production of polymers, resins, and synthetic fibers. − Traditionally, aromatics are produced through catalytic reforming of petroleum-derived naphtha or through nonoxidative dehydroaromatization of light alkanes over zeolite catalysts. , However, these processes often suffer from thermodynamic limitations, rapid catalyst deactivation caused by coke deposition, and limited selectivity toward desired aromatic products. Consequently, developing alternative strategies for converting abundant light alkanes into aromatic hydrocarbons remains an important research topic.

Introducing CO2 into alkane aromatization networks can potentially improve reaction thermodynamics, suppress coke formation, and regulate reaction pathways. Thermodynamic analysis indicates that CO2-assisted C2H6 dehydroaromatization exhibits higher equilibrium conversions than conventional dehydroaromatization pathways (Figure a). This improvement arises from the ability of CO2 to remove hydrogen generated during alkane dehydrogenation through the RWGS reaction, thereby shifting the equilibrium toward the formation of unsaturated intermediates. In zeolite-based aromatization systems, light alkanes are first dehydrogenated to olefin intermediates on metal sites, followed by oligomerization, cyclization, and dehydrogenation within the acidic micropores of zeolites such as ZSM-5 to form aromatic molecules. However, conventional zeolite or metal-zeolite catalysts often suffer from rapid deactivation due to coke deposition and micropore blockage. The presence of CO2 promotes the reverse Boudouard reaction, which removes surface carbon species and helps mitigate catalyst deactivation. −

In CO2-assisted ethane aromatization, Gomez et al. developed Ga/ZSM-5/P catalysts that achieve close to 14% liquid aromatic yield with 24% ethane conversion (Figure a). The introduction of CO2 significantly enhances both ethane conversion and aromatic yields compared with those in the absence of CO2 (Figure b,c). This is due to the synergistic interaction between CO2 activation and alkane dehydrogenation, which promotes the formation of olefin intermediates and suppresses undesired hydrogen transfer reactions. Phosphorus modification of Ga/ZSM-5 further enhances catalyst performance by improving the hydrothermal stability and reducing coke formation through tuning of the acid–base properties of the zeolite framework. DFT calculations suggest that CO2 can modify the energetics of key dehydrogenation steps by enabling alternative hydrogen removal pathways that avoid direct H2 formation (Figure c), thereby facilitating the overall aromatization process.

In addition to CO2-assisted ethane aromatization, similar strategies have been extended to other light alkanes such as propane − and n-butane. , Recent studies by Liu and co-workers demonstrate that CO2 strongly influences both reaction pathways and product distributions during propane and n-butane aromatization over metal-zeolite catalysts. ,− Reaction temperature plays a key role in controlling hydrocarbon distributions, with higher temperatures promoting dehydrogenation and cyclization reactions that favor aromatic formation (Figure d). The partial pressure of CO2 also affects alkane conversion and product selectivity. Moderate CO2 concentrations enhance aromatic selectivity by suppressing cracking products, whereas excessively high CO2 partial pressures may inhibit light alkane activation due to competitive adsorption on active sites (Figure e,f). Recent mechanistic studies suggest that CO2 may also participate directly in the catalytic cycle rather than only regulating thermodynamics and hydrogen balance. Liu and co-workers ,, reported that approximately 10–25% of the converted CO2 can be incorporated into aromatic molecules through pathways involving oxygen-containing intermediates, as evidenced by isotopic labeling experiments using 13CO2.

Overall, CO2-assisted aromatization is a coupled reaction network involving alkane activation, hydrogen transfer, olefin transformation, and zeolite-mediated aromatization. By regulating hydrogen balance, modifying reaction pathways, and potentially contributing carbon atoms to aromatic products, CO2 introduces new opportunities for upgrading light alkanes into value-added aromatics. The formation of liquid aromatics in CO2-assisted light alkanes conversion also facilitates downstream separation from the gas-phase reaction mixture.

Reacting CO2 and Light Alkanes to Solid Carbon Nanomaterials

The CO2-assisted light alkane conversion can also be directed toward the formation of solid carbon nanomaterials. CNTs and CNFs are particularly attractive products because of their exceptional mechanical strength, electrical conductivity, and thermal stability, enabling applications in advanced composites, energy storage devices, and electronic components. , Conventional CNT/CNF synthesis typically relies on chemical vapor deposition (CVD) at 600–1000 °C using fossil-derived hydrocarbons, , an energy-intensive process associated with significant carbon emissions. In contrast, converting CO2 and light alkanes into solid carbon nanomaterials provides a promising pathway for producing functional carbon materials while enabling longer-term carbon sequestration.

Reaction of CO2 with Methane

The simultaneous conversion of CO2 and CH4, often referred to as biogas conversion, into solid carbon materials provides a promising pathway to achieve net-zero or even net-negative CO2 emissions by permanently storing carbon in solid form. , A typical CO2–CH4 conversion over Fe-, Co-, or Ni-based catalysts at ∼800 °C can achieve nearly 90% CH4 and CO2 conversion, producing syngas as the dominant product with an H2/CO ratio close to 1.0 (Figure a–c). Regardless of catalyst composition, the formation carbon nanomaterials remain limited in CO2–CH4 systems, with CNT yields typically below ∼10%. Liquid metal catalysts have also been explored for converting CO2–CH4 into solid carbon materials; however, the carbon yield remains low due to thermodynamic constraints.

To overcome these limitations, Xie et al. proposed a tandem strategy in which dry reforming of methane is first catalyzed at 600 °C over a Ni/MCM-41 catalyst to generate CO and H2, which are subsequently fed into a second reactor at 450 °C containing a Co12K5/CeO2 catalyst (Figure d,e). This tandem configuration improves carbon fixation efficiency, as confirmed by the ratio of catalyst weight gain to the initial catalyst weight (CWG/Initial) increasing to 7, compared with only 1.1 using a single reactor at 600 °C. Notably, syngas with higher H2/CO ratios (2–3 vs <1.5 in single-reactor systems) is obtained as the byproduct (Figure d), which is beneficial for downstream reactions such as Fischer–Tropsch synthesis and methanol production. Overall, these findings highlight the potential of tandem reactor systems for efficient conversion of CO2 and CH4 into solid carbon nanofibers.

Reaction of CO2 with Other Alkanes

To achieve both high carbon yield and controllable carbon morphology, Yuan et al. demonstrated the direct conversion of CO2–C2H6 into solid carbon in a single reactor, achieving carbon yields exceeding 50% over a broad temperature window of 400–800 °C. , For example, using Co/Al2O3 as the catalyst, CO2–C2H6 conversion produces CNTs at 750 °C and CNFs at 450 °C, respectively, with H2-rich syngas (H2/CO ≈ 1.5) as a coproduct. Notably, participation of CO2 not only enhances the durability of the catalyst, but also contributes about 30% of the CNTs production. In addition, bimetallic CoFe catalysts can enhance CNT production from CO2–C2H6 reactions by an order of magnitude compared with monometallic catalysts (Figure f). The active sites and resulting CNT morphologies strongly depend on catalyst composition: Co-rich catalysts (Co/Fe ≥ 5) form stable face-centered cubic (fcc) CoFe alloys that produce cylindrical CNTs (Figure g), whereas Fe-rich catalysts (Co/Fe ≤ 2) favor body-centered cubic (bcc) alloys that transform into carbides, yielding bamboo-like CNTs (Figure h). These studies demonstrate that CO2-assisted alkane conversion provides a versatile platform for producing solid carbon nanomaterials, where catalyst composition, reactor configuration, and reaction conditions together determine carbon yield, morphology, and process efficiency.

Past Efforts, Current State, and Future Opportunities

Past Efforts

Over the past two decades, research on reactions between CO2 and light alkanes has evolved from reforming-centered chemistry into a broader platform for selective hydrocarbon upgrading and carbon utilization. Early efforts were dominated by dry reforming reactions, particularly CO2–CH4, − and later extended to CO2–C2H6, ,,, CO2–C3H8 systems, and CO2–C4H10. In these processes, CO2 primarily served as an oxidant for syngas production, with both CO2 and alkanes contributing to CO formation under high-temperature conditions. These studies established the fundamental thermodynamic and catalytic framework for coconverting CO2 and alkanes, while also revealing key limitations of conventional reforming catalysts, including poor selectivity, carbon deposition, and rapid deactivation.

In contrast to CO2–CH4 systems, which predominantly lead to syngas formation, CO2–C2H6, CO2–C3H8 and CO2–C4H10 systems enable alternative reaction pathways leading to olefin production. In these systems, CO2 serves as a coreactant to promote alkane dehydrogenation to produce olefins as valuable chemical building blocks. This shift marked a transition from complete reforming toward selective dehydrogenation. During this period, extensive studies on Cr-based catalysts established the foundation for CO2-assisted oxidative dehydrogenation, with systematic investigations into support effects, active site structures, oxidation state evolution, size-dependent behavior, and the roles of promoters. − Motivated by environmental concerns associated with Cr, numerous Cr-free catalysts were subsequently explored, including V-, − Ga-, − Zn-, − Fe-, − and other transition-metal-based systems, − as well as metal-free materials. Comprehensive reviews of these developments are available in the literature. − These studies established CO2 as a soft oxidant that improves dehydrogenation thermodynamics, facilitates hydrogen removal via RWGS pathways, and suppresses coke formation relative to direct dehydrogenation. Collectively, these advances moved the field beyond a reforming-dominated view toward a more detailed understanding of selective C–H, C–C, and C–O bond activation in CO2-assisted alkane conversion. ,

As demonstrated in the literature references quoted in this Perspective, many of these advances have been extensively disseminated through ACS journals, which have played a central role in shaping mechanistic understanding, catalyst design, and emerging reactor concepts in this field. These ACS publications have been critical in promoting studies and identifying the current framework of CO2-assisted alkane conversion.

Current State

Building on these developments, CO2-assisted alkane conversion involves two distinct roles of CO2. It can act as a soft oxidant that modulates reaction thermodynamics and catalyst structure while being converted to CO, or as an active reactant that contributes carbon and/or oxygen to the final products. This dual functionality of CO2 expands the range of accessible products, spanning gas, liquid, and solid phases, with the type of products being governed by the catalyst architecture and reactor configuration.

For gaseous products, including syngas and light olefins, CO2-assisted alkane conversion is governed by the coupling between alkane dehydrogenation and CO2 reduction. Current efforts primarily focus on selective C–H bond activation while avoiding dry reforming, which requires balancing C–H activation, CO2 reduction, and suppressing C–C bond cleavage. Catalyst design has therefore shifted toward interfacial environments, such as bimetallic (multimetallic) systems and metal–metal oxide interfaces, where alkane and CO2 activation can be tuned simultaneously. ,,, Tandem catalyst systems that couple dehydrogenation with RWGS have attracted considerable attention, as they integrate two well-established reaction chemistries with extensively studied catalysts. ,, However, the optimal combinations and spatial arrangements remain underexplored, particularly in ensuring that distinct catalytic functions operate independently without undesired interactions. These factors determine whether the system favors selective olefin formation or proceeds toward reforming reactions.

For liquid products, including oxygenates and aromatics, CO2-assisted alkane conversion involves more complex reaction networks that couple alkane activation with downstream C–C and/or C–O bond formation, which typically require tandem catalytic systems. In hydroformylation-related pathways, current efforts focus on developing catalyst systems that tolerate variations in reactant composition, as hydroformylation involves a three-component reaction that is highly sensitive to the partial pressures of each species. , For CO2-assisted aromatization, ongoing research aims to improve catalyst structural stability and product selectivity. , Given that the presence of H2O can degrade zeolite frameworks and affect catalytic performance, the introduction of phosphorous (P) has shown promise in enhancing hydrothermal stability, while continued efforts aim to balance activity, selectivity, and stability within these systems. ,,

For solid carbon products, CO2-assisted alkane conversion introduces a distinct reaction paradigm in which carbon formation becomes a controllable synthesis pathway. Carbon growth proceeds through dynamic processes involving dissolution, diffusion, and precipitation on metal catalysts. In this system, CO2 plays a dual role by contributing to both carbon formation pathways and enhancing catalyst durability. , Catalyst composition, particularly in bimetallic systems, strongly influences carbon solubility, diffusion, and carbon growth behavior. These systems highlight the potential for coupling carbon utilization with materials synthesis, while ongoing efforts focus on improving catalyst stability and expanding the range of carbon sources to include other relevant and abundant carbon-containing feedstocks.

Future Challenges and Opportunities

Despite the rapid progress in CO2-assisted alkane conversion, most current studies remain at the proof-of-concept stage. Translating these advances from laboratory demonstrations to practical applications will require coordinated progress in catalyst design, mechanistic understanding, and process engineering. Because CO2-assisted alkane activation involves highly stable reactants, complex reaction networks, and multiple catalytic environments, key challenges are no longer simply demonstrating feasibility but achieving the levels of activity, selectivity, stability, energy efficiency, and economic viability required for large-scale implementation.

Challenges

Controlling Activity, Selectivity, and Stability

Achieving high activity and selectivity while maintaining catalyst stability remains a central challenge in CO2-assisted alkane conversion. Unlike nonoxidative dehydrogenation, this chemistry involves a more complex reaction network, requiring the simultaneous activation of inert alkanes and thermodynamically stable CO2 while suppressing competing pathways such as deep dehydrogenation and coking, C–C bond cleavage, and CO2 methanation. Controlling these pathways requires precise tuning of metal–metal and metal-oxide interfacial sites that govern C–H, C–C, and C–O bond activation. This challenge becomes more pronounced for higher alkanes (C3+), where cracking is more facile and catalysts should selectively activate different types of C–H bonds while suppressing undesired C–C bond scission. At the same time, catalyst stability remains a major concern, as CO2-containing environments can induce metal sintering, phase transformations, and, in zeolite-based systems, hydrothermal degradation due to steam formation. Developing catalyst systems that balance activity, selectivity, and stability under realistic reaction conditions therefore remains a key priority.

Mechanistic Understanding of Dynamic Changes in Active Sites and Reaction Intermediates

Developing a comprehensive experimental toolbox to resolve catalyst structure and reaction pathways under operating conditions remains a critical challenge. As emphasized throughout this Perspective, active sites in CO2-assisted alkane activation are highly dynamic: metal particles formed under reductive conditions can be partially reoxidized by CO2, while alloy catalysts may restructure through surface segregation or dealloying. As a result, ex-situ characterization alone is often insufficient to identify the catalytically relevant surface structures. Advances in situ and operando techniques, such as X-ray absorption and diffraction techniques, are therefore essential for tracking active-site evolution and establishing structure-performance relationships, as highlighted in some of the examples discussed in this Perspective. Identifying surface intermediates remains challenging due to the difficulty of probing adsorbed hydrocarbon and oxygenated species under reaction conditions, and mechanistic insights remain limited because of the complex reaction network and high-temperature conditions involved. In-situ Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) and Raman spectroscopy are effective for distinguishing key intermediates. Coupling these techniques with isotope-labeling measurements should enable unambiguous tracing of carbon sources in the products.

Energy Efficiency and Process Integration

Energy efficiency and process integration represent critical challenges for CO2-assisted alkane conversion. Compared with conventional light alkane conversion, the introduction of CO2 leads to more complex product streams containing CO, H2O, and unreacted CO2, complicating downstream separations and potentially offsetting the benefits of CO2 utilization. In addition, the overall carbon footprint depends critically on how heat is supplied, making energy input a central factor in process sustainability. Recent analyses across multiple CO2-assisted systems, ,,, including ethane and propane dehydrogenation and tandem routes to oxygenates and carbon materials, indicate that external heating accounts for a large fraction of total energy consumption. This highlights the importance of efficient heat management and the integration of alternative energy sources, such as industrial waste heat or renewable electricity. Achieving net-neutral or net-negative CO2 emissions further depends on the carbon intensity of the energy supply; under current grid conditions, significant reductions in carbon intensity are required to fully realize the benefits of CO2 utilization. Integrating tandem catalytic steps that directly convert intermediates into value-added products without intermediate separation offers a promising strategy to improve overall efficiency. However, rigorous energy, techno-economic, and life-cycle analyses remain essential to evaluate the viability of these approaches relative to existing technologies.

Product Scale and Carbon Utilization

Another important consideration is the mismatch between the market scale of potential products and the magnitude of global CO2 mitigation targets. Many attractive products demonstrated in laboratory studies, such as oxygenates, aromatics, or carbon nanomaterials, have relatively small market sizes compared with the scale of global CO2 emissions. While these products may serve as important early applications for CO2-assisted alkane chemistries, meaningful carbon utilization will require extending these concepts toward larger-volume products such as fuels, commodity chemicals, or durable carbon materials. Addressing this challenge will require integrating reactor engineering, catalyst development with techno-economic analysis and lifecycle assessment to identify reaction pathways that can deliver both environmental and economic benefits.

Opportunities

The challenges discussed above also highlight several promising directions for advancing CO2-assisted alkane conversion.

Data-Driven Catalyst Discovery

The design space of CO2-assisted alkane conversion is highly complex, involving multimetallic active phases, redox-active supports, promoters, tandem architectures, and numerous reaction variables. Exploring this space using conventional trial-and-error approaches is inefficient. Integrated workflows that combine automated synthesis, high-throughput experimentation (HTE), and machine-learning (ML)-guided optimization offer a systematic approach to catalyst discovery. Recent studies have revealed structure-performance relationships that are difficult to access through conventional approaches. For example, decision-tree analysis of CO2-assisted oxidative dehydrogenation of propane (CO2–ODHP) has identified Cr–Ni–Mo-ZrO x quaternary compositions that outperform binary and ternary systems. Similar workflows have also been applied to oxidative dehydrogenation of n-butane. A key limitation, however, is that most ML models rely on descriptors derived from the as-synthesized catalyst rather than working states under reaction conditions. Incorporating operando descriptors, such as oxidation states from XAS or adsorbate coverages from infrared spectroscopy, offers a promising route to improve predictive reliability.

Dynamic Catalytic Systems

Most studies of CO2-assisted alkane conversion rely on steady-state cofeeding of CO2 and alkanes, which complicates reaction optimization and downstream separation. Decoupling alkane activation and CO2 utilization therefore represents an attractive alternative. Chemical-looping schemes, in which a solid oxygen carrier (e.g., CeO2 or Fe2O3) is alternately reduced by alkanes and reoxidized by CO2, are particularly promising for this chemistry. These approaches offer several advantages. First, spatial separation of the two half-reactions can improve conversion by eliminating competitive adsorption between reactants and suppressing undesired pathways (e.g., dry reforming) that limit selectivity in cofeed systems. Second, the CO2 reoxidation step can remove surface carbon species and regenerate the active phase, thereby extending catalyst lifetime. Third, separating reaction steps can simplify the product distribution. However, these benefits come with important challenges. For example, oxygen carriers should provide sufficient lattice oxygen capacity, fast redox kinetics, and mechanical stability over repeated cycles, while also maintaining high selectivity for dehydrogenation over deep oxidation.

Advanced Reactor Engineering

Reactor engineering provides another important opportunity for improving CO2-assisted alkane conversion. Many of the reaction pathways discussed above involve multiple catalytic steps that require different reaction environments, making reactor design as important as catalyst design. Advanced reactor configurations, such as tandem reactors and membrane reactors, can help coordinate these steps by controlling the transport and conversion of reaction intermediates. For example, selective removal of species such as H2 or H2O may shift reaction equilibrium and suppress undesired side reactions. Similarly, delivering olefin-rich intermediates directly to downstream catalytic zones can enable sequential reactions while minimizing undesired reactions in the gas phase. These strategies may therefore provide more effective control of complex reaction networks than conventional fixed-bed reactors.

Expanding the Product Landscape

Another important opportunity is to expand the range of target products toward applications with larger market demand and stronger decarbonization impact. While C3 oxygenates and aromatics provide useful proof-of-concept demonstrations, their market size remains relatively small compared with the scale of global CO2 emissions. Future research should therefore consider high-volume products such as fuels and other commodity chemicals. One promising direction is the synthesis of longer-chain hydrocarbons and sustainable aviation fuel precursors through tandem C–C coupling strategies. Another is the controlled formation of durable solid carbon materials, where CO2-assisted alkane conversion can combine carbon utilization with longer-term carbon storage in advanced materials or structural composites.

Overall, reacting CO2 with light alkane activation to value-added products has evolved from a reforming-oriented reaction into a broader catalytic platform for producing syngas, olefins, oxygenates, aromatics, and carbon nanomaterials. In these systems, CO2 can modify thermodynamic driving forces, influence catalytic active sites, and alter reaction pathways, while also serving as a carbon source when combined with appropriate catalyst and reactor design. Continued advances in catalyst design, reaction engineering, and process evaluation will determine the practical potential of this chemistry.
