Chronic inflammation-responsive hydrogel restores myeloid-T cell crosstalk to reinvigorate antitumor immunity against metastatic colorectal cancer
Authors: Xinchao Li, Wangxian Fu, Hao Le, Rui Luo, Yingjie Li, Xiaorong Kou, Tao He, Liping Bai, Yangsong Xu, Jiayi Cao, Yixi Wang, Xinmao Yu, Peng Mi, Zichao Luo, Changyang Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic inflammation in intermediate/advanced tumors drives burdensome protumor immune cell communication, thereby weakening immunotherapy. Conventional anti-inflammatory therapies focus on alleviating chronic inflammation whereas ignore dysfunction and scarcity of myeloid and T cells, which hinder their intercellular communication restoration. To address the dilemma, an inflammatory condition-triggered protumor inflammation-immunosurveillance shift hydrogel (TRANS) is developed to initiate adaptive immune responses mediated by intercellular communication. Triggered by inflammatory conditions, TRANS releases celecoxib (CXB) to inhibit the COX-2/PGE2 pathway, thereby reprogramming tumor-associated macrophages and mitigating protumor inflammation. Furthermore, TRANS incorporates FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligands (Flt-3L) and 4-1BB agonists (α-CD137) to respectively recruit type 1 conventional DC (cDC1) and revitalize tumor-infiltrating T cells, to rejuvenate immunosurveillance. TRANS inhibits 87.50% and 88.74% of primary and secondary colorectal tumors, generates antitumor immune memory to resist tumor rechallenge, and significantly reduces lung and liver metastases. Rechallenge model shows TRANS leads to antitumor immune memory formation. Single-cell RNA sequencing is preform to elucidate the mechanism of TRANS, which exhibits that TRANS exerts antitumor effects by optimizing the crosstalk between myeloid cells and T cells via CXCL9/10-CXCR3/DPP4. TRANS further gains better control of colorectal cancer when combined with immune checkpoints inhibitors. This study offers a novel perspective on immunotherapy by rebalancing inflammation-immunity dynamics.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents approximately 10% of global cancer cases and is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. More than 50% of patients with localized tumors eventually develop metastatic disease [2]. Although fluoropyrimidine- and platinum-based systemic chemotherapy remains the cornerstone therapy for metastatic CRC, it offers limited survival benefits and results in poor outcomes for most patients. Complex and highly organized tumor microenvironment (TME) shaped by tumor cells and various non-malignant cells contributes to the incurable status of metastatic CRC [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Cell-cell communication, mediated through direct contact and the release of soluble factors, plays a pivotal role in regulating the reciprocal interactions within the TME [7]. These dynamic exchanges continuously influence cell behavior, drive TME evolution, and promote tumor progression and metastasis [8]. Among these processes, inflammatory signaling is a key determinant of TME complexity, selectively modulating cell fate to establish tumor-supportive niches and suppress host immune responses [9].

Chronic inflammation is considered a well-established driver of tumor progression, often described as creating “wounds that do not heal”. It plays a pivotal role in all stages of tumor development, progression, and metastasis, earning it the term “protumor inflammation” [10,11]. Through mechanisms such as direct mutagenesis and activation of inflammatory mediators, protumor inflammation generates high levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), bringing about genomic instability in malignant cells. This drives their evolution into highly aggressive phenotypes with enhanced proliferation, invasion, and resistance, while also producing neoantigens in less harmful cells. The presence of neoantigens in advanced tumors indicates that immunosurveillance and protumor inflammation can coexist [12]. However, without external intervention, cancer immunoediting disrupts this balance, shifting cell-cell communication toward protumor inflammation and weakening antitumor immune responses [[13], [14], [15]]. Thus, anti-inflammatory strategies offer a promising approach to restoring the equilibrium by targeting the formation, dissemination, and effects of chronic inflammation [16].

Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), a key mediator in the complex network of protumor inflammation, is commonly overexpressed in various human cancers such as colorectal, breast, and lung cancer, and is strongly associated with poor prognosis [[17], [18], [19]]. PGE2 is produced from arachidonic acid by cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, primarily COX-2, and plays a dual role in promoting protumor inflammation while suppressing immunosurveillance [20]. It achieves this effect by triggering the release of inflammatory cytokines and binding to prostaglandin E receptors (EP) on immune cells, generating a tumor-friendly environment. Previous studies in COX-2-deficient tumor models demonstrated ablation of COX-2 could promote dendritic cell (DCs) and type 1 classic DCs (cDC1s) infiltration and antigen presentation, repolarize tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) from M2 to M1, and boost interferon-γ (IFN-γ) secretion by NK and T cells [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. However, the selective COX-2 inhibitor, celecoxib (CXB), has shown limited antitumor effects in preclinical and clinical trials, probably due to its short-lived suppression of PGE2, which quickly rebounds in tumors [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]]. Additionally, while COX-2/PGE2 inhibition potently reduces protumor inflammation, it has minimal impact on restoring immunosurveillance, as advanced tumors often lack functional antitumor immune cells and effective cell-cell communication. Therefore, combining anti-inflammatory strategies with enhanced immunosurveillance is crucial for addressing this challenge.

Here, we have developed a chronic inflammatory condition-triggered protumor inflammation-immunosurveillance shift hydrogel (TRANS) to address chronic inflammation and its role in tumor growth. TRANS actively shifts the balance from pro-tumor inflammation to immunosurveillance, restoring communication among anti-tumor immune cells and triggering strong systemic anti-tumor immune responses (Fig. 1). In response to the high oxidative stress of chronic inflammation at tumor sites, TRANS actively releases celecoxib, which could reduce ROS levels and block the COX-2/PGE2 pathway, thus reprogramming TAMs from M2 to M1 type. Subsequently, we encapsulated Flt-3L and α-CD137 into the TRANS hydrogel to recruit immature cDC1s and promote both the proliferation and activation of tumoral T cells, respectively. This multifunctional TRANS hydrogel significantly inhibited the primary and secondary tumor growth, generated antitumor immune memory to resist tumor rechallenge, and dramatically reduced lung and liver metastasis burden, demonstrating a strong systemic anti-tumor immune response. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that the TRANS hydrogel could increase both the abundance and functionality of anti-tumor immune cells by promoting intercellular communication. It restored CXCL9/10-CXCR3/DPP4-mediated interactions between myeloid cells (DCs, M1, and M2 macrophages) and T cells in colorectal cancer models. Results and discussion

Tumor-derived PGE2 is associated with chronic inflammation

Chronic inflammation often accompanies cancer development and worsens as tumors progress [33]. To explore the relationship between chronic inflammation and cancer, we analyzed the expression of key inflammatory factors (IL-1b, IL-6, CXCL-8, IL-11, TGF-β1, OSM, and LIF) across multiple tumor types was analyzed, including BRCA, COAD, GBM, LUAD, OV, PAAD, PRAD, READ, SKCM, and STAD (Fig. 2A). The results showed that chronic inflammation is closely associated with cancer development in diverse tumor types. Further analysis compared the expression of these cytokines in normal colon tissue and colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD) (Fig. 2B and Fig. S1A). Notably, the levels of IL-1β, CXCL-8, and LIF were significantly higher in tumor tissues, indicating more aggressive chronic inflammation in tumors compared to normal tissue [34]. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), the primary product of Prostaglandin endoperoxide synthase 2/Cyclooxygenase 2 (PTGS2/COX2) expression and activation, significantly impacts immune cells in tumor environments, driving inflammation [36]. Tumor tissues typically exhibit higher PGE2 levels than normal tissues, correlating with tumor progression and poor prognosis. We then investigated the relationship between Ptgs2 expression and chronic inflammation factors across various tumor types (Fig. 2C). The results revealed a strong positive correlation between Ptgs2 and various chronic inflammation factors. In colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD), Ptgs2 showed a particularly strong correlation with Il1b, Il6, Cxcl8, Il11, and Tgfb1 (Fig. 2D, Fig. S1B and S1C). Next, we quantified PGE2 levels using ELISA in CT26 tumor-bearing mice across a range of tumor sizes. The results showed that PGE2 content correlated positively with tumor weight (Fig. S2).

As a PTGS2/COX-2 selective inhibitor, CXB is clinically used to block PGE2 synthesis. To investigate the impact of reducing tumor-derived PGE2 on immune cells, we treated BMDCs, BMDMs, and splenic T cells with CXB-mediated tumor-conditioned medium in vitro (Fig. 2E). First, the IC50 of CXB for CT26 tumor cells was determined to be 88.06 μM using the MTT assay (Fig. 2F). At this concentration, CXB did not induce apoptosis but caused cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase (Fig. 2G and Fig. S3). Next, we measured PGE2 levels in the supernatant of CT26 cells treated with various CXB concentrations for 24 h. At 80 μM, CXB reduced PGE2 release by approximately 93.14% compared to the medium group (Fig. 2H).

We next studied the effect of blocking tumor-derived PGE2 by CXB on bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). CXB-pretreated tumor medium (40 or 80 μM CXB) significantly increased the population of CD103+ DCs, a migratory subtype of cDC1 (Fig. 2I). It also significantly boosted CD86 expression, while reducing CD80 levels on BMDCs (Fig. 2J and Fig. S4). Additionally, this blocking PGE2 effect potently enhanced BMDCs’ antigen-processing ability (Fig. 2K).

PGE2 is known to promote tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), the most abundant immune cells in colorectal cancer, toward the M2 phenotype [37]. To evaluate the impact of CXB-treated tumor-conditioned medium (TCM) on TAM, we cultured Raw 264.7 cells with TCM. CXB-treated TCM (40 μM) potently reduced the M2 ratio without affecting M1 macrophages. At 80 μM, CXB-pretreated TCM significantly decreased M2 while increasing M1 macrophages (Fig. 2L–N). Moreover, qPCR analysis showed lower Il1b gene expression in macrophages cultured with CXB-treated TCM, indicating reduced chronic inflammation due to PGE2 pathway blockade (Fig. S5).

To assess the impact of PGE2 blockade on T cells, we first isolated splenic T cells, stimulated them with CD3/CD28 antibodies for 48 h, and then incubated them with CXB-treated TCM for another 24 h (Fig. S6). CD69 and CD137 expression on CD8+ T cells significantly increased with higher CXB concentrations (Fig. 2O and P), probably due to the restored IL-2 signaling from PGE2 blockade [38,39]. Furthermore, CXB-TCM (at 40 or 80 μM CXB) stimulated splenic T cells to produce higher IFN-γ in vitro (Fig. 2Q).

Sustained PGE2 blockade activates immune cells in vitro

CXB has been shown to transiently induce cancer inhibition genes in preclinical trials, complicating dosing regimens and limiting its clinical trial efficiency [32]. To achieve the sustained CXB release, we designed an immunologic adjuvant-based hydrogel, as previously reported [40]. The hydrogel was formed by linking oxidized mannan (OM, 10 mg/mL) and chemically modified chondroitin sulfate (CS, 30 mg/mL) (Fig. 3A), both confirmed by 1H NMR and FT-IR (Figs. S7 and S8). SEM showed the hydrogel's porous microstructure (Fig. 3B), and rheological tests confirmed that CXB loading did not alter its storage modulus (Fig. 3C and Fig. S9). HPLC analysis showed ROS-responsive CXB release, with sustained release lasting at least 144 h in PBS containing 0.5 mM H2O2 at 37 °C (Fig. 3D). We next evaluated the hydrogel's immunostimulatory effects on BMDCs and Raw264.7 cells. CS strongly promoted DC maturation, while OM effectively polarized macrophages toward the M1 type. Next, CT26 tumor cells were treated with Med, Gel, CXB, or Gel-CXB (GC) for 24 h to collect different tumor-conditioned media (TCM). ELISA assays showed that both CXB and GC significantly reduced PGE2 production by 97.37% and 98.00%, respectively, compared to Medium (Fig. S11). To investigate the impact of PGE2 blockade on DCs, BMDCs were co-cultured with ovalbumin (OVA) peptides and TCM for 24 h. The results showed that PGE2 blockade by GC enhanced the antigen-processing ability of BMDCs, compared to Gel alone. Additionally, CXB- and GC-TCM promoted a higher proportion of CD11c+ DCs compared to the Gel-TCM group (Fig. S12). And the increased BMDCs in the CXB- and GC-TCM groups were primarily the migratory CD103+ DC subset (Fig. 3E and F).

We next evaluated the impact on BMDCs maturation. Notably, CD103+ DCs showed higher maturation markers (CD80+, CD86+, and CD80+CD86+) than CD103- DCs across all groups (Fig. 3G and H, and Fig. S13A). The proportion of CD103+ DCs largely determined the overall maturation level of DCs (Fig. S13B and S13C). As a rare but critical antigen-presenting cell, cDC1 plays a key role in cross-presenting tumor antigens to CD8+ T cells and is essential for inducing tumor antigens-specific immune responses [41]. To obtain pure cDC1s, we cultured bone marrow-derived myeloid cells with Flt-3L and GM-CSF for 15 days [42]. These cDC1s were co-cultured with TCM, and we then evaluated their secretion of CXCL9, a key T-cell recruitment cytokine (Fig. 3I). The results showed that blocking tumor-derived PGE2 by GC increased the proportion of CXCL9+ cDC1s. Additionally, the PGE2 blockade promoted cDC1 maturation, particularly enhancing CD86 and CD40 expressions (Fig. 3J). Overall, sustained PGE2 blockade effectively promotes cDC1 and overall DC maturation.

TAMs, the most abundant immune cells in the TME, are known for their impaired antigen-presenting capacity and strong immunosuppressive effects [[43], [44], [45]]. To investigate whether sustained PGE2 blockade could reprogram TAMs toward an anti-tumor phenotype, we treated Raw 264.7 cells with tumor-conditioned medium (TCM) under different conditions. The GC group significantly enhanced the expression of co-stimulatory molecules (CD80, CD86, and CD40) on Raw 264.7 cells compared to controls (Fig. S14). Further validation using bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) showed that GC treatment markedly increased CD86 expression, a marker of M1 polarization, compared to both Gel and CXB alone (Fig. 3K). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CD86 followed a similar trend, supporting the shift toward M1 phenotype (Fig. S15A). Conversely, GC treatment reduced the proportion of CD206+ M2 macrophages (55.49% ± 1.17%) compared to the medium group (81.18% ± 0.64%) (Fig. 3K and S15B).

To assess the functional impact of GC on macrophage activity, we next evaluated its expression of MHC-II, a critical molecule for antigen presentation [46,47]. GC treatment significantly increased MHC II expression on BMDMs (59.78% ± 2.87%) compared to medium group (10.26% ± 0.42%) (Fig. 3L). Furthermore, GC also enhanced the ability of BMDMs to present tumor antigens, as evidenced by a 4.44-fold increase in SIINFEKL-H-2Kb complex expression (41.89% ± 4.10%) compared to medium group (7.70% ± 0.64%) (Fig. 3M). To evaluate the impact of sustained PGE2 blockade on cytokine production, we analyzed the supernatant of macrophages and cDC1 treated with different TCM. GC treatment significantly reduced the secretion of chronic inflammatory cytokines, IL-1β and IL-6, from macrophages. In contrast, GC prompted the release of IL-12p70, a key immunosurveillance-associated cytokine, from cDC1 (Fig. S16). These results demonstrate that sustained PGE2 blockade via GC treatment effectively reprograms macrophages toward an anti-tumor phenotype, enhancing their antigen-presenting capacity and further supporting their role in anti-tumor immunity.

To assess the impact of sustained PGE2 blockade on T cell activation, we co-cultured splenic T cells with tumor cells treated with Med, Gel, CXB, or GC in a transwell co-culture system. Gel significantly enhanced CD69 expression on both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3N and Fig. S17A), due to its polysaccharide-based hydrogel structure modulating T cell glycolytic pathways [48]. Notably, GC exhibited a synergistic effect, but this was restricted to CD8+ T cells, with no significant impact on CD4+ T cells. Further analysis of 4-1BB (CD137), a costimulatory receptor indicative of T cell activation, showed that CXB markedly upregulated CD137 expression on CD8+ T cells, an effect further amplified by GC treatment (Fig. 3O and Fig. S17B). These findings highlight distinct and synergistic roles of GC in modulating T cell activation, particularly in CD8+ T cells.

Sustained PGE2 blockade contributed to immune microenvironment remodeling in vivo

The biodegradation of the hydrogel was then explored in vivo and the result displayed that the hydrogel could be maintained for at least 5 days (Fig. S18). To explore the difference between CXB and GC in vivo, we constructed CT26 subcutaneous tumor models that were peritumorally administrated with PBS, CXB, and GC. Subsequently, tumor masses were collected and analyzed with flow cytometry at 1-, 5-, and 9-days post-administration, respectively (Fig. 3P). As the principal component of antitumor immune responses, T cell infiltration level is directly linked to tumor prognosis. Therefore, we checked the infiltration of T cells within TME. CXB-treated mice exhibited a significant surge 24 h post-administration, followed by a precipitous decline after 5 and 9 days. However, GC could maintain a higher CD8+ T cell infiltration, rather than CD4+ T cells, within 5 days post-administration (Fig. 3Q), which suggests that continuously blocking the PGE2 pathway in TME would be conducive to higher T cell infiltration. We also evaluated the infiltration and maturation of CD11c+ DCs within TME (Figs. S19 and S20). GC contributed to more DC infiltration trends than CXB and significantly promoted DC maturation at 5-, and 9-days post-administration. cDC1, including lymphoid-tissue resident CD8α+ cDC1s and migratory CD103+ cDC1s, had been widely reported to be an important APC associated with antitumor immune response. GC could improve the proportion of CD8α+ cDC1s within TME at 5 days post-administration. Within 9 days, the proportion of CD103+ cDC1 in the CXB group experienced a rapid decline, while that in the GC group demonstrated an initial increase followed by a decrease. The phenomenon indicated that sustained inhibition of the PGE2 pathway facilitated the recruitment of CD103+ cDC1 into TME.

After detecting sustained inhibition PGE2 pathway in colorectal tumor models, we further explored whether it would lead to effective tumor growth in vivo. Therefore, CT26 tumor-bearing mice were peritumorally injected with PBS, Gel, CXB (7.5 mg/kg), or GC (containing CXB at 7.5 mg/kg) when the tumor volume when tumor size fell within the range of 50 to 100 mm3. The tumor growth curves were recorded and presented in Fig. 3R, and the result displayed that both CXB and GC could inhibit tumor growth to some extent. However, CXB demonstrated a 32.38% tumor growth inhibition rate while GC achieved 49.15%, which indicated that sustained inhibition of the PGE2 pathway contributed to better antitumor efficacy. The individual tumor growth curves were shown in Fig. S21. Furthermore, we tested the phenotype of tumor-infiltrating T cells. GC elevated the proportion of CD8+ T cells within TME while did not influence CD4+ T cells (Fig. S21). We further detected the expression of 4-1BB (CD137) on both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and the results demonstrated that GC could improve the expression of CD137 on CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3S) rather than CD4+ T cells (Fig. S21), which indicated that the blockade of the PGE2 pathway might lead to the activation of CD8+ T cells within TME. In order to explore whether CXB and GC could activate the adaptive immune response, the proportion of T cells and the expression of CD137 on splenic T cells were analyzed. Nevertheless, they both had no impact on the proportion and CD137 expression of T cells (Fig. S21), which suggested that CXB and GC could only regulate local immunosurveillance within TME.

Considering that CXB and GC upregulated CD137 expression on CD8+ T cells within TME, we further explored whether CD137 agonists could be synergistic with GC. We first separated splenic T cells in C57BL6 mice and cultured them in RPMI 1640 medium containing αCD3/CD28 for 72 h with or without CD137 agonists. CD137 could promote the proliferation of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Their activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells after CD137 agonists treatment also confirmed that CD137 agonists could effectively drive T cell activation in vitro. In addition, CD137 agonists did not show promotion of CD44 expression on T cells (Fig. S22).

TRANS inhibits tumor growth of colorectal cancer in vivo

Although sustained inhibition of the PGE2 pathway by GC alleviated chronic inflammation, its direct impact on tumor suppression was limited. GC increased cDC1 counts both in vitro and in vivo, but the infiltration of cDC1, a key antigen-presenting cell for cross-presentation, remained insufficient within the TME. Additionally, GC enhanced CD137 expression on tumor-infiltrating T cells, which could be further activated by CD137 agonists to improve proliferation, activation, and effector functions. These findings suggest that amplifying cDC1 and T cell activity in colorectal cancer may restore cell-cell communication among antitumor immune cells, thereby reinforcing immunosurveillance while maintaining PGE2 pathway blockade.

To achieve this, we developed an autonomous condition-activated hydrogel scaffold (TRANS) by co-encapsulating Flt-3L and 4-1BB agonist (α-CD137) within a CXB-loaded hydrogel. This platform aims to shift the balance from protumor inflammation to immunosurveillance and restore communication among antitumor immune cells, eliciting potent systemic antitumor immune responses (Fig. 4A). To determine whether sustained PGE2 blockade enhances antitumor immunity by simultaneously boosting immune cell infiltration and function, we treated CT26 tumor-bearing mice as outlined in Fig. 4C. Mice were administered PBS, Gel, GC (CXB: 7.5 mg/kg), GCF (CXB: 7.5 mg/kg and Flt-3L: 5.0 mg/kg), or TRANS (CXB: 7.5 mg/kg, Flt-3L: 5.0 mg/kg, and α-CD137: 2.0 mg/kg) when tumor volumes reached 50-100 mm3. Additionally, to evaluate the antitumor efficacy of TRANS, control groups including Gel + Flt-3L, Gel+αCD137, Gel + CXB+αCD137, and Free CXB + Flt-3L+αCD137 were employed. The results demonstrated that TRANS more effectively inhibited tumor growth compared to the other groups (Fig. S24). Specifically, TRANS treatment achieved an 87.50% tumor growth inhibition rate relative to the PBS control (Fig. 4D and Fig. S25A). Consistent with this, tumor weights were markedly reduced in the TRANS group (Fig. 4E), indicating effective tumor inhibition. Notably, TRANS did not affect mouse body weight, underscoring its favorable safety profile (Fig. S25B).

To delineate the immune landscape of TME, we analyzed tumoral immune cells 5 days post-treatment. TRANS significantly increased CD45+ leukocyte infiltration compared to other groups (Fig. 4F), suggesting enhanced immune cell recruitment. To identify specific immune subsets, we quantified dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and T cells. CD11c+ DCs were significantly elevated in the GC group (11.05% ± 1.48%) compared to PBS (5.70% ± 1.76%), consistent with the in vitro result. GCF and TRANS, which include Flt-3L, further increased DC infiltration to 17.49% ± 1.44% and 16.77% ± 2.35%, respectively, with TRANS achieving a 3.68-fold higher DC proportion than PBS (Fig. 4F and Fig. S25C). Similar trends were observed for CD103+ cDC1 (Fig. S25D), highlighting the role of Flt-3L in promoting DC recruitment.

Macrophage infiltration also increased in the TRANS group (Fig. S25E and S25F). Phenotypic analysis revealed that TRANS significantly elevated M1 macrophages (CD86+) while reducing M2 macrophages (CD206+), resulting in a higher M1/M2 ratio compared to PBS (43.34% vs. 69.52%; Fig. 4G and Fig. S25G). This shift toward a pro-inflammatory macrophage phenotype underscores the immunomodulatory potential of TRANS. Furthermore, TRANS enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration more effectively than other treatments (Fig. 4H), while CD4+ T cells showed a modest increase (Fig. S25H and S25I). The limited effect of GC only on CD8+ T cells highlights the synergistic benefits of combining sustained PGE2 blockade with immune cell modulation. These findings demonstrate that TRANS amplifies immune cell infiltration and reprograms the TME to favor antitumor immunity, providing a robust strategy for enhancing immunosurveillance.

We further analyzed cytokine profiles within tumor lesions to evaluate the immunomodulatory effects of TRANS compared to PBS. TRANS significantly enhanced the secretion of antitumor cytokines, including IL-2, IL-12p70, and IFN-γ, which are critical for promoting T cell activation and antitumor immunity (Fig. S25J). Furthermore, we quantified PGE2 levels on days 1, 5, and 9 post-administrations. The NS group exhibited a rapid increase in PGE2. A single dose of CXB initially reduced PGE2 but was followed by a rebound. In contrast, both the GC and TRANS groups maintained significantly suppressed PGE2 levels up to day 5. A slight elevation was observed by day 9 in these groups, likely attributable to near-complete drug release amid persistent residual tumor tissue (Fig. S26A).

We further evaluated the correlation between tumor growth inhibition rate and relative PGE2 content across time points. A strong positive correlation was evident in the GC and TRANS groups, underscoring that sustained PGE2 suppression is critical for therapeutic efficacy. The correlation was weaker in the CXB group, aligning with its transient suppression profile. Notably, the correlation in the TRANS group was slightly attenuated compared to GC, possibly due to additional antitumor mechanisms mediated by immunomodulatory agents beyond PGE2 blockade (Fig. S26B). Together, these results confirm that prolonged PGE2 suppression is essential, and its duration strongly predicts treatment outcome. Conversely, TRANS reduced chronic inflammatory cytokines secretion, such as PGE2, IL-6 and IL-1β, which are associated with tumor progression and immunosuppression. These results demonstrate that TRANS not only fosters a pro-inflammatory antitumor immune response but also mitigates chronic inflammation.

TRANS initiates systemic antitumor immune responses

To determine whether TRANS induces systemic antitumor immunity, we evaluated the quantity and function of immune cells in peripheral immune organs (lymph nodes, spleen) and blood in CT26 tumor-bearing mice treated with different hydrogels (Fig. 4C). In lymph nodes, which are critical for immune activation, GCF and TRANS significantly increased dendritic cell (DC) proportions, reasonably due to the presence of Flt-3L (Fig. S27A). TRANS also enhanced DC maturation, as evidenced by elevated CD80 and CD86 expressions (Fig. 4I), an effect not observed with CXB alone, indicating the hydrogel's immunostimulatory role. TRANS further boosted the proportion of CD8α+ cDC1 (Fig. 4J), a key subset for cross-presentation, and increased CD8+ T cell counts (Fig. 4K). Additionally, TRANS promoted the activation of both CD8+ and CD4+ T cells in lymph nodes (Fig. 4L and Fig. S27B).

In the spleen, TRANS elevated CD11c+ MHC-II+ DCs and CD8α+ cDC1 proportions (Fig. 4M and N), confirming the Flt-3L's role in expanding DC populations. TRANS also enhanced CD8+ T cell proliferation without affecting CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4O). Notably, TRANS maintained the highest levels of T cell activation and effector function, as measured by IFN-γ production (Fig. 4P and Q, and Fig. S28).

To assess systemic immune cell migration, we analyzed blood T cell counts. TRANS uniquely increased CD8+ T cell proportions in circulation (Fig. 4R and S, and Fig. S29A), suggesting its potential to prevent tumor metastasis by targeting circulating tumor cells. Serum cytokine analysis revealed that TRANS significantly elevated IFN-γ levels without inducing a cytokine storm, as IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β remained unchanged (Fig. 4T and Fig. S29B). Finally, splenic T cells from TRANS-treated mice exhibited higher IFN-γ production upon PMA/ionomycin stimulation and anti-CD3/CD28 activation (Fig. 4U). When co-cultured with CT26 tumor cells (at an Effect/Target ratio of 20:1 or 40:1), splenic T cells from the TRANS group induced significantly higher tumor cell apoptosis compared to PBS (Fig. 4V). These findings demonstrate that TRANS enhances local antitumor immunity and initiates robust systemic immune responses, highlighting its potential as a comprehensive immunotherapeutic strategy.

The robust systemic immune responses prompted us to investigate whether TRANS could inhibit distant tumor growth. Using a bilateral CT26 subcutaneous tumor model (Fig. 5A), we evaluated the antitumor efficacy of TRANS on both treated and untreated tumors. TRANS significantly suppressed tumor growth on the treated side, achieving an 87.30% inhibition rate compared to PBS (Fig. 5B and Fig. S30). Notably, TRANS also potently inhibited secondary tumor growth on the untreated side, with an 88.74% inhibition rate (Fig. 5C and Fig. S30), indicating its ability to elicit systemic antitumor immunity. Note that the enhanced T cell infiltration is often associated with improved antitumor outcomes. In untreated tumors, TRANS increased the proportion of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 5D and E), consistent with the elevated CD8+ T cell levels in lymph nodes (Fig. 4K), spleen (Fig. 4O), and blood (Fig. 4R). Immunofluorescence staining of primary tumors revealed deeper and broader immune cell infiltration in the TRANS group, indicating a favorable remodeling of the TME (Fig. S31). Additionally, we identified multicellular immune hubs--clusters of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and T cells in close proximity--which are associated with antigen-specific T cell expansion and tumor inhibition [49,50]. TRANS generated more abundant and larger immune niches compared to other treatments (Fig. 5F and Fig. S32), suggesting enhanced cell-cell communication among antitumor immune cells. These niches likely underpin the potent antitumor immune responses observed with TRANS.

Metastasis is a major driver of mortality in cancer patients, with PGE2 playing a pivotal role in facilitating lung and liver metastasis in colorectal cancer [51,52]. To evaluate whether TRANS mitigates metastasis, we established CT26 lung and liver metastasis models. In the lung metastasis model, TRANS significantly inhibited primary tumor growth and reduced metastatic node number by approximately 93.80% compared to PBS (Fig. 5G–K). Lung weights in the PBS group were 4.38 times higher than those in the TRANS group (Fig. 5L), and H&E staining confirmed fewer metastatic nodes in TRANS-treated mice (Fig. 5M). The results suggested that TRANS treatment is associated with significant control of the primary tumor and a marked reduction in lung metastatic burden. In the liver metastasis model, created via splenic injection of CT26 cells followed by spleen resection (Fig. 5N), TRANS treatment is also associated with a marked reduction in liver metastatic burden, as evidenced by reduced average radiance in InVivo Imaging (Fig. 5 O and P, and Fig. S33) and lower liver weights (PBS: 7.27 ± 1.05 g vs. TRANS: 1.84 ± 0.64 g; Fig. 5Q and R). H&E staining further confirmed fewer metastatic nodes in the TRANS group (Fig. 5S).

To assess whether TRANS induces anti-tumor immune memory, we surgically excised tumors from CT26-bearing mice 5 days post-treatment with PBS or TRANS (Fig. 5T). Two weeks later, mice were rechallenged with CT26 or 4T1 tumor cells. TRANS conferred significant long-term protection, with 88.89% (8/9) of mice showing no tumor regrowth upon rechallenge. Bystander antitumor effects against 4T1 tumors were also observed in the TRANS group (Fig. 5U and Fig. S34A). Additionally, TRANS potently increased the proportion of CD44+CD62L− effector memory T cells (TEM) in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations (Fig. 5V and Fig. S34B), explaining its potent ability to prevent tumor recurrence. These findings demonstrate that TRANS not only inhibits metastasis but also establishes durable antitumor immune memory, highlighting its potential as a multifaceted immunotherapeutic strategy.

Analysis of DCs revealed that TRANS upregulated pathways related to interferon responses (alpha and beta), MHC class Ib receptor activity, and myeloid DC activation (Fig. S38A and S38B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further highlighted enhanced phagosome formation, antigen processing and presentation, oxidative phosphorylation, and complement cascades in TRANS-treated DCs (Fig. S38C). Next, Volcano plots identified upregulated genes in TRANS-associated macrophages, including those involved in phagocytosis (Cybb, Itgb2), antigen presentation (B2m, Tap1, Tapbp, H2-T23), M1 polarization (Cd86, Cd40, Stat1, Stat2), interferon signaling (Isg15, Irf1, Irf7), and chemokine production (Cxcl10, Cxcl11) (Fig. S39A and S39B). Conversely, genes linked to M2 polarization (Apoe, Trem2, Csf1r, Mrc1, Arg1) and complement cascades (C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, C3ar1, C5ar1) were downregulated. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses of M1 macrophages suggested that TRANS enhanced cellular responses to interferon-b, innate immunity, and immune system processes (Fig. S39C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways such as phagosome formation, endocytosis, NOD-like and Toll-like receptor signaling, TNF signaling, and antigen processing were upregulated (Fig. 6E). GSEA further confirmed enrichment in NOD-like receptor and JAK-STAT signaling pathways, underscoring the role of TRANS in enhancing macrophage phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and maturation (Fig. 6F).

GO analysis of M2 macrophages revealed that TRANS modulated cellular responses to interferon-b, innate immunity, protein binding, and glycolytic processes (Fig. S39D). KEGG analysis showed that TRANS upregulated amino acid biosynthesis, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, antigen processing and presentation, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, while downregulating the Wnt signaling pathway, focal adhesion, and ribosome-related processes (Fig. 6G). GSEA further confirmed the upregulation of glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and antigen processing and presentation in TRANS-treated macrophages (Fig. 6H), indicating a conversion from M2 to M1 phenotype, consistent with previous studies [[53], [54], [55]].

To validate these findings, M0 macrophages were incubated with either free medium or TRANS-treated tumor supernatant for 24 h in vitro. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that the TRANS-treated medium increased the proportion of M1 macrophages while reducing M2 populations (Fig. 6I and J), due to the effect of CXB on PEG2 pathway blockade, as supported by a recent study [56]. Western blot analysis further confirmed that TRANS-treated tumor supernatant enhanced STAT1 expression by macrophages, a key regulator of M1 polarization (Fig. 6K). In a separate experiment, Raw 264.7 cells were polarized to M2 macrophages using tumor-conditioned medium (TCM) and IL-4, followed by treatment with TRANS-treated tumor supernatant. TRANS significantly reduced M2 populations and increased M1 populations (Fig. 6L and M), demonstrating its ability to repolarize macrophages. To further assess antigen-processing capabilities, macrophages pre-incubated with TRANS-treated tumor supernatant were co-cultured with ovalbumin (OVA, a model antigen). TRANS-treated macrophages exhibited a higher ratio of H-2Kb/SIINFEKL positivity, indicating enhanced antigen processing and presentation (Fig. 6N).

Gene expression analysis revealed that TRANS upregulated genes associated with antigen presentation (Tap2), interferon signaling (Isg15), and M1 polarization (Il12a, Nos2), while downregulating pro-inflammatory and M2-related genes (Il1b, Il6, Il10, Arg1) (Fig. 6O and P). Furthermore, macrophages treated with TRANS-treated medium enhanced the activation and effector functions of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in vitro (Fig. 6Q and Fig. S40), confirming the role of TRANS in promoting T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. These results demonstrate that TRANS reprograms macrophages toward an M1 phenotype, enhances their antigen-presenting capabilities, and potentiates T cell activation, contributing to its robust antitumor effects.

Furthermore, we identified a distinct IFN T cell characterized by expression of IFN-related genes (Fig. S44A). Next, we investigated the immunomodulatory effects of TRANS on NKs and Tregs within TME. Notably, TRANS treatment led to a reduction in NK cell proportions (Fig. S45A), but it significantly modulated the expression of genes associated with NK cell functions. Specifically, TRANS upregulated genes related to costimulatory molecules (Slamf7, Tnfrsf18), effector functions (Gzma, Gzmc, Rap1b, Ets1), migration (Cxcr6), and proliferation (Il2rb, Stat1, Cdk4, Maf), as well as antigen processing and presentation (Pdia3, B2m, Calr, H2-D1, H2-K1) in NK cells (Fig. S45B). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that TRANS enhanced antigen processing and presentation, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and chemokine signaling (Fig. S45C). Additionally, we observed that TRANS upregulated genes associated with activation (Icos, Il18rap), effector function (Gzmc, Lef1), migration (Cxcr6, Pten), and proliferation (Stat3, Stat5b, Fosb) in proliferating NK cells (Pro NK) (Fig. S46).

Regarding Tregs, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis between TRANS and PBS groups indicated that TRANS suppresses Treg activation and promotes their exhaustion (Fig. S47A). TRANS downregulated genes associated with activation (Hdgf, Lef1, Git2) and survival (Gpx4 and Stat2), while upregulating exhaustion-related genes (Tox, Lag3, Klf2) (Fig. S47B). These findings suggest that TRANS not only reduces Treg abundance within the TME but also inhibits their suppressive functions.

To assess cell-cell communication among different cell clusters, we utilized the CellphoneDB database to analyze interactions between myeloid cells (DCs and macrophages) and T cells (CD8 Teff, Pro CD8 Teff, IFN T, and CD4 Tregs) between PBS and TRANS groups (Figs. S48 and S49). The results demonstrated that TRANS recruited more anti-tumor T cells (CD8 Teff, Pro CD8 Teff, and IFN T) than PBS, via CXCL10/CXCL11-CXCR3 and CCL3/CCL4-CCR5 interaction pathways (Fig. 7H). Furthermore, TRANS intensified CD28−CD80/CD86 and CXCR3-CXCL9 pathways compared to PBS, as evidenced by ligand-receptor interaction analysis between T cells and myeloid cells (Fig. S50). To elucidate the specific antitumor mechanism of TRANS, we evaluated its combined effects with either a CCR5 inhibitor (CCR5i, TAK-779, 7.5 mg/kg, intraperitoneal) or a CXCR3 inhibitor (CXCR3i, AMG487, 5 mg/kg, administered twice subcutaneously). In vivo imaging results demonstrated that TRANS mediates antitumor efficacy predominantly via the CXCL9/10-CXCR3 axis, with no significant reliance on the CCR5 pathway (Fig. S51). These results collectively demonstrate that TRANS reshapes the TME by enhancing NK cell functionality, suppressing Treg activity, and promoting pro-inflammatory cell-cell communication.

TRANS-mediated antitumor efficacy relies on early infiltrating T cells and is enhanced by TIM3 blockade

To determine whether the antitumor effects of TRANS depend on tumor-infiltrating T cells, we employed FTY720, a sphingosine-1-phosphate analog that inhibits T cell egress from lymph nodes [57]. Mice receiving TRANS treatment were administered FTY720 daily via intraperitoneal injection (Fig. 7I). Tumor growth was monitored, and growth curves revealed that TRANS + FTY720 effectively inhibited tumor growth, albeit to a lesser extent than TRANS alone (Fig. 7J). These results demonstrated that TRANS primarily exerted its antitumor effect via early-infiltrating T cells, rather than depending on continuous T cell recruitment from lymph nodes. Body weights remained stable across treatment groups, indicating minimal systemic toxicity (Fig. S52A).

Analysis of the TME revealed that TRANS increased the absolute counts of Ki67+ proliferative T cells compared to PBS (Fig. S52B). Both TRANS and TRANS + FTY720 promoted CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration, with TRANS showing higher T cell proportions due to additional recruitment from lymph nodes (Fig. 7K). Although the proportions of IFN-γ+ CD4+ T or CD8+ T cells and Granzyme B+ CD4+ T or CD8+ T cells did not significantly increase, their absolute numbers rose markedly (Fig. 7L and Fig. S52C). Notably, TRANS did not alter PD-1 expression but increased the proportions of TIM3+CD4+ T and TIM3+CD8+ T cells, as well as PD-1+TIM3+CD8+ T cells, indicating a shift toward an exhausted phenotype driven by 4-1BB signaling (Fig. 7M, Fig. S52D and S52E) [58].

To further enhance TRANS efficacy, we evaluated the impact of TIM3 or PD-1 blockade. TIM3 blockade, but not PD-1 blockade, synergized with TRANS to significantly delay tumor growth. Two out of seven mice remained tumor-free for approximately 40 days following treatment (Fig. 7N–P, and Figs. S53A–S53C). Splenic T cells from TRANS-treated mice exhibited increased proportions of IFN-γ+CD8+ T and IFN-γ+CD4+ T cells after ex vivo stimulation with PMA and ionomycin (Fig. 7Q and R, Fig. S53D, and S53E). While TIM3 blockade alone did not further elevate IFN-γ+ T cell proportions, it enhanced IFN-γ secretion in splenic T cells upon CD3/CD28 stimulation (Fig. 7S). These findings demonstrate that TRANS exert potent antitumor effects primarily through early infiltrating -T cells, with additional contributions from recruited T cells. Combining TRANS with TIM3 blockade further augments therapeutic efficacy, highlighting a promising strategy for fostering cancer immunotherapy efficiency.

Conclusion

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a highly heterogeneous and dynamic ecosystem where chronic inflammation and immunosurveillance coexist, often shifting toward a pro-inflammatory state due to immunoediting pressures [[59], [60], [61]]. Traditional anti-inflammatory therapies targeting single cell type or processes frequently fail to address the complexity of the TME, leading to unpredictable outcomes. Most COX-2 inhibitors, including celecoxib (CXB), have yielded suboptimal clinical outcomes in various cancers, despite promising preclinical data. This disconnect may stem from several interrelated challenges. Primarily, the lack of precise predictive biomarkers leads to a mismatch between drugs and cancer types. Secondly, due to its short half-life and associated cardiovascular risks, systemic CXB therapy has constrained dose escalation potential, limiting intratumoral drug exposure. Finally, the pharmacodynamic interactions between CXB and diverse chemotherapeutic agents are complex and variable, further complicating treatment efficacy. Here, we propose a paradigm shift toward targeting interconnected cellular networks and cell-cell communication within the TME to achieve more effective antitumor responses.

Different from naturally progressing tumor lesions, TRANS effectively inhibits primary colorectal tumor growth by establishing antitumor immune niches within the TME, which has been reported to be associated with stronger immune cell communication and better prognosis [62]. It also induced systemic immune responses, controlling secondary tumor lesions in a CD8+ T cell-dependent manner. Of note, there were no systemic immune responses in the Gel-CXB or free CXB group, which might be because localized CXB administration could only induce local PEG2 blockade while exhibiting mere effects on circulating T cells [63]. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that TRANS increased the proportions of M1 macrophages, dendritic cells, and T cells while reducing M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells (Tregs). Mechanistically, TRANS both activated the JAK-STAT pathway and antigen processing in macrophages and modulated glucose metabolism in T cells.

Metastatic lesions in advanced colorectal cancer determine the prognosis of patients, and the efficacy is often related to the status and effector function of T cells [64,65]. While local hydrogel delivery cannot directly target distant metastases, effective localized therapies (e.g., radiotherapy or intratumoral immunotherapy) have been shown to elicit systemic immune responses and abscopal effect. In this study, TRANS-induced systemic immunity significantly suppressed metastatic growth in colorectal cancer models, particularly in lung and liver metastases. These results suggest that localized TRANS application may help eliminate residual micro-metastases and establish long-term immunosurveillance. For diffuse metastatic disease, local hydrogel delivery may function as an effective adjunct to systemic therapies, enhancing overall treatment efficacy. Furthermore, the exhausted state of CD8+ T cells detected via FTY720 treatment indicated that TRANS stimulated more cytotoxic but exhausted intratumoral T cells within the primary tumor microenvironment for tumor progress control. It also implied the potential of combining immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) for better tumor elimination. When synergizing with immune checkpoint inhibitors, TRANS achieved a tumor-free status in 2/7 tumor-bearing mice for approximately 40 days post-treatment, which might bring new therapeutic opportunities for the current clinical situation of low response rates to ICI [66].

These findings highlight TRANS as a promising strategy to address the imbalance between protumor inflammation and immunosurveillance, offering a novel perspective for cancer immunotherapy. By targeting multiple cell types and their interactions within the TME, TRANS represents a transformative approach to overcoming the limitations of conventional therapies.

Although TRANS represents a strategy that could be realized in immunotherapy to modulate the inflammation-immunity dynamic game, its feasibility in vivo in clinical patients has not been explored. This study aims to explore whether the TRANS system can modulate the tumor immune microenvironment by attenuating chronic inflammation via PGE2 blockade while potentiating anti-tumor immunosurveillance. The CT26 cell line was selected based on its intermediate immunogenicity, higher than that of triple-negative breast cancer models such as 4T1, yet lower than commonly used colorectal cancer models such as MC38. With its high tumor mutational burden and relatively robust immunogenicity, the CT26 model serves as a sensitive experimental system to evaluate whether the TRANS system can elicit antitumor immunity through a “chronic inflammation-to-immunosurveillance shift” mechanism. Although findings from the CT26 model may not be directly generalizable to all human colorectal cancers, they suggest the potential of the TRANS system to remodel the immune microenvironment in tumors with preexisting immune infiltration. These results provide a conceptual foundation and rationale for further validation in more clinically relevant models, such as patient-derived organoids co-cultured with human immune cells, or low-immunogenicity patient-derived xenografts in humanized mice, paving the way for more rigorous and translational experimental studies.

The translational gap from preclinical promise to clinically viable delivery remains a major hurdle. While the peritumoral injection of hydrogels used here represents a more invasive route, it is well-adapted for image-guided precision interventions. The integration of real-time imaging modalities such as ultrasound, CT, or MRI, together with minimally invasive techniques such as endoscopy, could further facilitate application in deeper solid tumors. This system is also ideally applied in intraoperative or postoperative settings, direct application to the tumor resection bed may eradicate residual microtumors and modulate the local immune milieu to inhibit recurrence, offering a clinically feasible translational pathway. Furthermore, although no adverse effects were detected in mice, a thorough translational assessment remains essential. This includes evaluating the chronic biocompatibility of the carrier material, the safety profiles of its degradation products, and potential risks from robust local immune activation, including cytokine release syndrome. Furthermore, we will further explore the feasibility of TRANS in patients receiving adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy. The game between chronic inflammation, represented by PGE2, and immunosurveillance during radiotherapy or chemotherapy is often variable and difficult to control, and several clinical studies have demonstrated the lack of synergistic effects of current anti-inflammatory therapies on chemotherapy. The involvement of TRANS in the modulation of the inflammation-immunity dynamic game and its promising preclinical antitumor effects may bring a novel perspective to the synergistic effects of classical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and chemotherapy.

Materials and methods

Mannan and chondroitin sulfate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Methyl 3-mercaptopropionate was obtained from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Celecoxib (T0466) was sourced from TargetMol (USA). Recombinant cytokines, including GM-CSF, M-CSF, and IL-4, were acquired from Proteintech (USA). Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, M4647) and ionomycin (M3621) for T cell activation were purchased from AbMole (USA). FTY720, AMG 487, TAK-779, D-Mannitol, and hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HP-β-CD) were provided by MedChemExpress (USA). ELISA kits for mouse IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12p70, TNFα (BioLegend, USA), PGE2 (Cayman Chemical Company, USA), IL-2 (Dakewe Biotech, Shenzhen, China), IFN-γ, and TGF-β (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were used for cytokine quantification. RNA concentration was measured using the StarLighter RNA HS Assay Kit (FS-T1006, Beijing Foreverstar Biotech, China), and qPCR was performed with the SYBR Green Premix Pro Tag HS qPCR Kit (AG11701, Accurate Biotechnology, China). Antibodies for flow cytometry were provided by Biolegend, USA. Anti-CD3e (16-0031-82) and anti-CD28 (16-0281-82) for T cells induction were obtained from Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). Anti-STAT1 antibodies (ab281999) were purchased from Abcam (UK). ECL Super Sensitive Luminescent Liquid (E1080) was sourced from Beijing LABLEAD Inc. (China). Transwell inserts (1102110, SAINING, China) were used for T cell and tumor cell co-culture experiments. For in vivo studies, the following reagents were used: InVivoMAb anti-mouse 4-1BB (CD137, BE0239), recombinant Flt-3L-Ig (BE0098), anti-mouse TIM-3 (CD366, BE0115) from BioXCell (USA), and anti-mouse PD-1 (CD279, A2212) from Selleck (USA).

Cells and animals

CT26 cells and Raw 264.7 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). Female BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks) were obtained from Laboratory Animal Center, Sichuan University (Chengdu, China). All animal experiments were conducted according to the experimental guidelines of the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee of Sichuan University.

Preparation of the hydrogel

Methyl 3-mercaptopropionate was dissolved in acetone, catalyzed by concentrated sulfuric acid at 70 °C to reflux for 12 h. Subsequently, the reaction was monitored via thin layer chromatography (PE: EA = 8:1). The solvent was removed by rotary evaporation to gain the crude products, which were reconstituted by EA, and extracted with saturated sodium bicarbonate and saturated sodium chloride to remove residual concentrated sulfuric acid. Finally, product A was gained via rotary evaporation. Product A was dissolved in methanol and reacted with hydrazine hydrate at room temperature for 12 h. The solvent was then removed by rotary evaporation and lyophilized to gain dry raw products. Finally, they were purified via column chromatography to obtain S-ADH. Chondroitin sulfate was connected with S-ADH via an acyl hydrazide reaction. Chondroitin sulfate was dissolved in MES buffer (pH 5.5) and reacted with S-ADH at room temperature for 48 h at the ratio of 1:2 with the catalyst of EDCI and NHS. CS was obtained after dialysis and lyophilization.

Mannan was oxidized by sodium periodate to gain oxidized mannan (OM). In detail, mannan was reacted with sodium periodate at the ratio of 1:0.3 in PBS buffer for 12 h. Subsequently, the products were collected, dialyzed, and lyophilized to gain OM.

The hydrogel was crosslinked via the Sniff base reaction. Specifically, the hydrogel was screened by orthogonal experiments that gradient concentrations of compounds OM were crosslinked with various concentrations of CS. Finally, the hydrogel was obtained by mixing 10 mg/mL OM and 30 mg/mL CS.

Release behavior detection

CXB or BSA-FTIC-loaded hydrogel was shaken in PBS or PBS containing 0.5% H2O2 at 37 °C, 90 rpm for 144 h. Samples were obtained at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144 h. HPLC was utilized for CXB detection and MicroplateReader was utilized for BSA-FITC detection.

qPCR

RNA was extracted via Trizol and then displaced into chloroform after centrifugation at 4 °C. Subsequently, RNA was precipitated into isopropanol from chloroform after centrifugation, washed with 70% ethanol twice, and dissolved into double distilled water. The concentration of RNA was tested by Nanodrop.

According to the qPCR SYBR GREEN reagent instructions, RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA and detected. RNA primer-template sequences were listed in Table S1, supplementary information.

BMDCs maturation

BMDCs were isolated from the femur and tibia of 6-8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in RPMI 1640 complete medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL GM-CSF, 10 ng/mL IL-4, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol, and 10% heat-inactivated FBS for 6 days. Fresh medium was replenished on day 3. On day 6, BMDCs were harvested and incubated with either free medium, Gel, CXB, or GC-treated CT26 tumor-conditioned medium (TCM). Following incubation, the cells were collected and stained with anti-CD11c-FITC, anti-CD103-APC, anti-CD86-PECy7, anti-CD80-PE, or anti-CD11c-FITC and anti-CD40-APC for flow cytometry analysis.

BMDMs polarization

Bone marrow-derived cells were separated from the femur and tibia of C57 BL6 mice, which were cultured at 37 °C in RPMI 1640 complete medium containing 20 ng/mL M-CSFμM β-mercaptoethanol, and 10% heat-inactivated FBS for 6 days. On the 3rd day, the aforesaid fresh medium was replenished. On the 6th day, the medium was changed into free medium, Gel, CXB, or GC pretreated CT26 TCM for 24 h. Thereafter, those cells were collected and stained by anti-CD11b-FITC, anti-F4/80-PE, anti-CD86-PECy7, and anti-CD206-APC for flow cytometry analysis.

Local antitumor assay in vivo

1 × 106 CT26 cells were injected into the right flank of 6-8-week-old female BALB/c mice. When tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were administered peritumorally with either PBS, Gel, GC, Gel + CXB + FLT-3L (GCF), or Gel + CXB + FLT-3L + α-CD137 (TRANS). In another tumor model, mice received peritumorally injection of Gel + Flt-3L (without CXB or α-CD137), Gel + α-CD137 (without CXB or Flt-3L), Gel + CXB + α-CD137 (without Flt-3L), Free CXB + Flt-3L + α-CD137 (administered in solution, without hydrogel). The dosages were as follows: CXB at 7.5 mg/kg, FLT-3L at 5.0 mg/kg, and α-CD137 at 2.0 mg/kg. Mouse weights and tumor volumes were monitored until the tumor volume reached 2000 mm3, at which point the mice were sacrificed. On day 19, mice were euthanized, and tumor weights were measured and recorded for each group. Additionally, T cells and cDC1 from the spleen were harvested and analyzed.

Single-cell suspension preparation and scRNA sequencing

CT26 tumor tissues were separated from PBS or TRANS-treated tumor-bearing mice at Day 5 post-treatment and transferred into an RNA-free culture dish, where they were shredded and then dissociated into single-cell suspension with an overall cell viability of over 85%. 10X Genomics Chromium instruments were utilized to capture single cells within the aforementioned single-cell suspension. Subsequently, cDNA amplification, library construction steps, and scRNA sequencing were performed by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd, (Hangzhou, China).

cDC1 induction and maturation

Bone marrow-derived cells were isolated from the femur and tibia of 6-8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in RPMI 1640 complete medium supplemented with 5 ng/mL GM-CSF, 200 ng/mL Flt-3L, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol, and 10% heat-inactivated FBS for 9 days. Fresh medium was replenished on day 5. On day 9, cells from the medium supernatant were collected and further cultured under the same conditions for an additional 6 days. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with free medium, Gel, CXB, or GC-pretreated CT26 tumor-conditioned medium (TCM). Finally, cDC1 cells were harvested, and single-cell suspensions were prepared and stained with anti-CD11c-FITC, anti-CD103-APC, anti-CD86-PECy7, and anti-CD80-PE for flow cytometry analysis.

T cells proliferation, activation, and effector function detection in vitro

Splenic T cells were isolated from 6-8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice and prepared as single-cell suspensions. The cells were resuspended in Lymphocyte Isolation Solution and centrifuged. For proliferation analysis, T cells were counted and stained with CFSE-FITC. The CFSE-labeled T cells were then cultured with anti-CD3/CD28 (1.0 μg/mL) ± α-CD137 (10.0 μg/mL) for 3 days. Afterward, the cells were collected and stained with anti-CD4-APC and anti-CD8-PE-Cy7 for detection. For activation and effector function analysis, splenic T cells were stimulated with anti-CD3 (1.0 μg/mL) and anti-CD28 (0.5 μg/mL) ± α-CD137 (10 μg/mL) for 3 days. The T cells were then collected and stained with anti-CD4-APC, anti-CD8-PE-Cy7, anti-CD44-FITC, anti-CD69-PE, or anti-IFN-γ. For the co-culture assay with CXB, splenic T cells were incubated with CXB-pretreated TCM for 24 h after stimulation with anti-CD3 (1.0 μg/mL) and anti-CD28 (0.5 μg/mL) for 2 days.

Tumor-infiltrating immune cells analysis in vivo

CT26 cells were inoculated onto the right flank back of 6-8-week-old Balb/c female mice. The mice were accepted peritumoral administration of PBS, CXB (7.5 mg/kg), or Gel-CXB, when the tumor volume was approximately up to approximately 100 mm3. Subsequently, tumors from different groups were collected for detection of tumoral immune cells on the 1st, 5th, and 9th days post-administration. In detail, the tumor mass was cut into pieces and digested into a single-cell suspension with type I collagenase (1 mg/mL) and nuclease (5 U/mL) at 37 °C for 1.5 h. After the lysis of red blood cells, the single cells were washed and stained with anti-CD3-PE-Cy7, anti-CD4-FITC, anti-CD8-APC for T cells analysis, anti-CD11c-FITC, anti-MHC II-APC, anti-CD80-PE, and anti-CD86-PECy7 for detection of DC maturation and anti-CD11c-FITC, and anti-CD8α-PE, anti-CD103-APC for cDC1 detection.

The analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells landscape

CT26 cells-bearing mice received peritumoral administration of PBS, Gel, GC, GCF, or TRANS once the tumor volume reached 200 mm3. 5 days post-administration, mice were sacrificed and their tumors were cut, digested, lysed, and washed into a single-cell suspension. Subsequently, they were stained with anti-CD11c-FITC, anti-MHC-II-PE, anti-CD80-APC, and anti-CD86-PE-Cy7 for DC detection, anti-CD11c-FITC, anti-CD8α-PE, and anti-CD103-APC for cDC1 detection, anti-CD11b-FITC, anti-F4/80-PE, anti-CD86-PE-Cy7, and anti-CD206-APC for macrophage detection, anti-CD3-PE-Cy7, anti-CD4-FITC, anti-CD8-APC and anti-CD69-PE for activated T cells detection, anti-CD3-PE-Cy7, anti-CD4-FITC, anti-CD8-APC, and anti-IFN-γ-PE for cytotoxic T cells detection, anti-CD45-APC, anti-CD11b-FITC, and anti-Gr-1-PE for MDSC detection, and anti-CD3-PE-Cy7, anti-CD4-FITC, anti-CD25-PE, and anti-Foxp3-Alexa Fluor 647 for Treg detection. In addition, the blood was collected for T cells analysis, and the serum for ELISA detection.

Systemic antitumor assay in vivo

CT26 cells were inoculated on the right flank back of female mice, while half counts of CT26 were injected on the left back four days later. The mice were received peritumoral administration of PBS, Gel, GC, GCF, or TRANS, when the volume of the primary tumor was approximately up to 100 mm3, approximately. Subsequently, the body weights and tumor volume of mice were monitored and recorded every other day until the primary tumor volume was up to 2000 mm3. On the 19th day, the mice were sacrificed and the secondary tumors were harvested for tumor-infiltrating T-cell analysis.

Rechallenge of CT26 and 4T1

CT26 cells were inoculated on the right back ofBal b/c female mice. Tumor bearing mice were treated with PBS, Gel, GC, GCF, or TRANS, when the tumor volume around 100 mm3. The tumors were resected at Day 12. Two weeks post-surgery, 1 × 106 4T1 and CT26 were inoculated onto opposite flanks back of the mice. Subsequently, the tumor changes of 4T1 and CT26 were monitored. The mice were sacrificed and the tumor weights of 4T1 and CT26 were recorded at Day 44. In addition, CD44+ memory T cells were detected within the tumors and spleens.

Metastasis models construction

For lung metastasis, CT26-Luc cells were inoculated on Bal b/c female mice. Two days later, those mice received another 1 × 106 CT26-Luc cells via intravenous injection. Mice received PBS or TRANS treatment at primary tumor lesions on the 7th day. Tumor lesions were detected by the Invivo imaging system on the 9th, 13th, and 16th day. For liver metastasis, CT26 cells were inoculated on Bal b/c female mice. The mice were injected with 1 × 106 CT26-Luc cells from the spleen within 10 min and then removed the spleen 3 days post-primary tumor inoculation. Mice received PBS or TRANS treatment at primary tumor lesions on the 7th day. Tumor metastasis lesions were detected by the Invivo imaging system on the 6th, 10th, 12th, and 14th day.

TRANS combined with CCR5 or CXCR3 blockade in vivo

CT26-Luc cells were inoculated onBal b/c female mice. For CXCR3 blockade, mice received 5 mg/kg of AMG487 (s.c., dissolved in 20% of hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin) twice daily from Day 6 post tumor inoculation until the end of the experiment. For CCR5 blockade, CT26-Luc tumor-bearing mice received 7.5 mg/kg of TAK-779 (i.v., dissolved in 5% of D-mannitol) daily from Day 6 post tumor inoculation until the end of the experiment. TRANS was peritumoral injected to the CCR5i + TRANS, CXCR3 + TRANS, and TRANS groups at Day 7. Tumor progress was monitored with In Vivo Imaging system.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The concentration of PGE2, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α in serum or culture supernatant were detected using ELISA kits according to the standard protocol.

Statistical analysis

All the data were analyzed using Prism 9.0 GraphPad, and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Unless otherwise stated, the differences between two groups was analyzed using Student's t-test. One-way ANOVA was used to determine the statistical significance of the difference among three or more groups. Two-way ANOVA was employed to assess the statistical differences of tumor volume among different treatments. Significant difference was denoted as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
