Epicatechin suppresses cancer stem-like cell phenotype and radioresistance in non-small cell lung cancer via targeting CXCL8
Authors: Tianqian Li, Hongmei Han, Meiyan Liu, Yongmei He, Sihan Zhou, Youchuan Xiao, Nan Huang, Na Peng, Chongxin Li, Hongying Xu, Jie Xia, Zengbo Lv, Yao Wu, Meifang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiotherapy remains a cornerstone for non‑small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). However, its efficacy is frequently limited by radioresistance, a phenomenon closely associated with cancer stem-like cells (CSCs). Although our previous work demonstrated that epicatechin (EC) sensitizes NSCLC cells to ionizing radiation (IR), whether this effect involves modulation of CSCs and the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated.MethodsA radioresistant NSCLC cell line (A549RR) was established via fractionated irradiation and employed in both in vitro assays and murine xenograft models. To assess its radiosensitizing potential, EC was administered prior to IR exposure. Cell viability and colony formation capacity were measured to evaluate radiosensitivity. Putative targets of EC were predicted using network pharmacology and validated through molecular docking. The direct interaction between EC and CXCL8 was examined by cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA). The functional relevance of CXCL8 in EC-mediated effects was assessed through both loss-of-function (shRNA-mediated knockdown) and gain-of-function (CXCL8 overexpression) approaches. CSC properties were evaluated by tumor sphere formation assays and immunoblotting for stemness markers.ResultsEC significantly enhanced NSCLC radiosensitivity both in vitro and in vivo, concomitant with marked suppression of CSC stemness. CXCL8 was identified as a direct functional target of EC: its expression was substantially upregulated in radioresistant cells but downregulated upon EC treatment. CETSA confirmed a direct interaction between EC and CXCL8 in cells. Functionally, CXCL8 knockdown suppressed cell viability, colony formation capacity, and CSC properties in A549RR cells. Conversely, overexpression of CXCL8 abrogated EC-induced radiosensitization and restored CSC phenotypes in both cellular and xenograft models.ConclusionEC overcomes NSCLC radioresistance by directly targeting CXCL8, thereby disrupting CSC-like traits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13062-026-00881-6.

Introduction

Lung cancer persists as the foremost cause of cancer-related mortality globally, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) representing approximately 85% of all diagnoses [1]. Radiotherapy serves as a fundamental treatment modality for NSCLC in both curative and palliative settings, conferring significant survival benefits [2]. Nevertheless, the frequent emergence of radioresistance substantially undermines therapeutic outcomes, promoting local failure and distant dissemination. Consequently, strategies to overcome radioresistance constitute a critical unmet need in NSCLC therapeutics.

Naturally derived bioactive compounds have increasingly gained recognition as promising sources of radiosensitizers and adjunctive anti-cancer agents. Among these, golden buckwheat (Fagopyrum dibotrys)—a medicinal plant native to China—has attracted attention due to its historical use in managing pulmonary diseases [3]. Epicatechin (EC), a principal bioactive flavonoid isolated from golden buckwheat, possesses well-documented anti-tumor and anti-oxidative properties [4]. Our previous work further demonstrated that EC enhances NSCLC radiosensitivity both in vitro and in vivo [5, 6]. However, the precise underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) represent a phenotypically distinct subpopulation within tumors, endowed with self-renewal capacity and tumor-initiating potential [7]. CSCs are widely recognized as pivotal drivers of tumorigenesis, disease progression, therapeutic resistance, and metastatic dissemination [8]. Their intrinsic resilience to conventional modalities, including radiotherapy, enables even a residual CSC population to instigate tumor regrowth and systemic spread [9]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies aimed at eradicating CSCs have garnered substantial interest. Notably, accumulating evidence indicates that various plant-derived compounds can attenuate radioresistance, at least in part, through CSC-targeting mechanisms [10, 11]. Although EC has been shown to suppress CSC-like phenotypes in several cancer models [12], whether its radiosensitizing effect in NSCLC is mediated via CSC modulation remains to be validated.

To systematically investigate the molecular basis of EC action in NSCLC, we employed an integrated approach combining network pharmacology and molecular docking. This strategy identified C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8) as a putative nodal target bridging EC treatment, radioresistance, and CSC biology. CXCL8, a prototypical pro-inflammatory chemokine, is consistently upregulated in NSCLC and correlates with poor prognosis [13]. Functionally, CXCL8 signaling has been implicated in fueling CSC self-renewal and sustaining tumor progression [14, 15]. Moreover, emerging evidence further links CXCL8 to the acquisition of radioresistance across multiple malignancies [16].

In the present study, we investigated whether EC enhances NSCLC radiosensitivity by targeting the CSC compartment via CXCL8 modulation and sought to validate the direct interaction between EC and CXCL8. Our findings uncover a novel mechanism by which EC counteracts NSCLC radioresistance and position the EC–CXCL8 axis as a promising therapeutic avenue for improving radiotherapy outcomes.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

A549 human NSCLC cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

To establish radioresistant sublines, A549 cells were subjected to fractionated irradiation over six months. The regimen consisted of three cycles of 4 Gy, followed by four cycles of 6 Gy, and finally three cycles of 8 Gy. Surviving cells were expanded and validated as the radioresistant A549RR subline. For EC treatment, 20 µg/mL EC (purity ≥ 98%, Solarbio, IE0120) was added to the cultured cells for 24 h prior to irradiation [6]. Radioresistance was validated by cell viability and colony formation.

Cell transfection

For CXCL8 overexpression, the full-length cDNA encoding human CXCL8 was cloned into a plasmid vector (GeneChem, China). An empty vector was used as the negative control. Transient transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting CXCL8 (shCXCL8) and a negative control shRNA (shNC) were constructed into lentiviral vectors (GeneChem, China). A549RR cells were infected with lentivirus according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for subsequent analyses.

Animals and tumor xenograft assay

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kunming Medical University. Female BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old, 15 g) were housed under specific pathogen‑free conditions. To establish subcutaneous xenografts, 4 × 10⁶ parental A549 or transfected A549RR cells were subcutaneously injected into the left dorsal side of each mouse. Tumor formation was monitored daily; successful engraftment was confirmed by palpable nodules.

When tumors reached approximately 100 mm³, mice were randomly assigned to the following six treatment groups (n = 6 per group): (1) A549 + IR; (2) A549RR + IR; (3) A549RR+ Vehicle + IR; (4) A549RR + EC + IR; (5) A549RR-vector + EC+ IR; (6) A549RR-CXCL8 + EC + IR.

To assess the radiosensitizing effect of EC, mice received daily oral gavage of EC (50 mg/kg) or vehicle for 7 consecutive days prior to IR [6]. On day 8, tumors were locally irradiated with a single dose of 8 Gy. EC administration was continued daily after irradiation until the end of the experiment. Tumor growth was monitored every 3 days by caliper measurement, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (length × width²)/2.

Western blotting

Cultured cells or tumor tissues were collected, rinsed twice with ice‑cold phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS), and lysed on ice in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE, transferred onto PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% non‑fat milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CXCL8 (Thermo Fisher, MA5‑23697, 1:1000), ALDH1 (Abcam, ab227948, 1:1500), NANOG (Abcam, ab21624, 1:1000), SOX2 (Abcam, ab97959, 1:2000), CD133 (Abcam, ab19898, 1:1000), OCT4 (Abcam, ab19857, 1:1000) or GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004‑1‑Ig, 1:3000). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and detected with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit‑8 (CCK‑8; Beyotime, C0038) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transfected or treated cells were seeded into 96‑well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well. After the designated treatment period, CCK‑8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 1 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the control group.

Colony formation assay

Transfected or pretreated cells were seeded into 60‑mm culture dishes at a density of 3 × 10³ viable cells per dish and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then exposed to IR. Following treatment, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, and washed gently with PBS to remove excess stain.

Tumor sphere formation assay

For tumor sphere culture, transfected or treated cells were maintained in serum‑free DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF), 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), and B27 supplement (1×). Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10³ cells per well in ultra‑low attachment 24‑well plates. Sphere formation was assessed using a light microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

CETSA was performed to assess direct binding of EC to CXCL8. Cell lysates with normalized protein concentrations were incubated with EC or vehicle control for 30 min at room temperature, then heated at 37–72 °C, followed by cooling at 25 °C. After centrifugation, soluble fractions were analyzed by western blotting with antibodies against CXCL8. Band intensities were quantified, normalized to the 37 °C sample, and thermal stability curves were generated.

Network pharmacology analysis

To identify potential targets of EC, the compound structure was queried against the BATMAN-TCM database. Transcriptomic datasets GSE197236 (A549 vs. radioresistant A549RR cells) and GSE192790 (A549 vs. A549-spheres) were downloaded from the GEO database. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using |log2FC| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 as cutoffs. Overlapping genes between the two datasets and predicted EC targets were selected for further analysis. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database and visualized in Cytoscape. Hub genes were identified using the CytoNCA plugin with degree centrality > 15. Molecular docking between EC and CXCL8 was performed using Discovery Studio 2019.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Comparisons between two groups were evaluated by unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. For multiple group comparisons, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. All in vitro experiments were independently repeated at least three times, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For in vivo experiments, data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) with n = 6 mice per group. A.

P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Results

Radioresistant NSCLC cells exhibit enhanced CSC‑like properties

A radioresistant NSCLC subline, designated as A549RR, was established via fractionated irradiation as previously described. Compared with parental A549 cells, A549RR cells displayed significantly increased resistance to IR, as determined by CCK‑8 and colony formation assays (Fig. 1A and B). Given the established role of CSCs in therapy resistance, we next examined whether acquisition of radioresistance was accompanied by enrichment of CSC‑like traits. Western blot analysis revealed marked upregulation of canonical CSC markers—including ALDH1, NANOG, SOX2, CD133 and OCT4 [17]—in A549RR cells relative to A549 controls (Fig. 1C). Consistently, tumor sphere formation assays confirmed substantially enhanced self‑renewal capacity in the radioresistant subline (Fig. 1D).

EC sensitizes radioresistant NSCLC cells to irradiation and suppresses CSC properties

To assess whether EC directly counteracts radioresistance, A549RR cells were pretreated with EC or vehicle control prior to IR exposure. EC pretreatment significantly reduced post-irradiation cell viability, as determined by CCK‑8 assay (Fig. 2A). Colony formation assays further revealed that EC sensitized A549RR cells to IR (Fig. 2B). We next examined whether this radiosensitizing effect was accompanied by suppression of CSC traits. EC treatment substantially downregulated ALDH1, NANOG, SOX2, CD133 and OCT4 protein levels (Fig. 2C) and robustly impaired tumor sphere formation capacity (Fig. 2D), indicating functional attenuation of stemness.

To extend these findings in vivo, we established subcutaneous xenografts using A549, A549RR, or A549RR cells pretreated with EC or PBS. Following local tumor irradiation, A549RR-derived tumors grew faster than their A549 counterparts, confirming their enhanced tumorigenic potential and intrinsic radioresistance (Fig. 2E). EC administration profoundly suppressed the growth of irradiated A549RR xenografts and restored sensitivity to radiotherapy (Fig. 2E).

Network pharmacology identifies CXCL8 as a candidate target of EC in radioresistant NSCLC

Given the established link between radioresistance and CSC enrichment, we sought to identify genes commonly dysregulated in both radioresistant and CSC-enriched NSCLC populations. We analyzed two public transcriptomic datasets: GSE197236, which compares gene expression between parental A549 and radioresistant A549RR cells, and GSE192790, which profiles A549 cells and A549-derived spheres—a well-established in vitro model of CSC enrichment. Differential expression analysis revealed 633 DEGs associated with radioresistance (Fig. 3A) and 6,408 DEGs associated with CSC stemness (Fig. 3B). Intersection of these two gene sets yielded 152 commonly dysregulated genes (Fig. 3C). Concurrently, putative EC targets were retrieved from the BATMAN-TCM database (PubChem CID: 72276), generating a set of 19,041 candidate genes. Overlap analysis between the predicted EC targets and the 152 radioresistance/CSC-associated genes identified 126 overlapping candidates as potential mediators of EC action (Fig. 3D).

A PPI network was constructed using the STRING database and visualized in Cytoscape. Topological analysis with CytoNCA highlighted several hub nodes with degree centrality > 15, including CXCL8, CCL2, IL1A, and JUN (Fig. 3E). Molecular docking simulations performed using Discovery Studio 2019 generated 15 binding poses for the EC-CXCL8 complex, with the highest LibDockScore, indicating strong and stable binding (Fig. 3F).

CXCL8 is upregulated in radioresistant NSCLC and directly targeted by EC

Integrated network pharmacology and molecular docking identified CXCL8 as a putative direct target of EC. To validate this interaction, we first examined CXCL8 expression levels in radioresistant cells. CXCL8 was upregulated in A549RR cells compared with parental A549 controls (Fig. 4A), consistent with its reported role in therapy resistance. Importantly, EC treatment significantly suppressed CXCL8 expression in A549RR cells at the protein level (Fig. 4B). CETSA further demonstrated the direct binding between EC and CXCL8. CXCL8 exhibited greater thermal stability in the EC-treated group than in the vehicle control (Fig. 4C).

Knockdown of CXCL8 reverses radioresistance in A549RR cells

Having identified CXCL8 as a candidate mediator of EC-induced radiosensitization through bioinformatics analysis, we sought to validate its functional role in NSCLC radioresistance. Stable CXCL8 knockdown was achieved in A549RR cells via lentiviral shRNA delivery, with efficient silencing confirmed by Western blotting (Fig. 5A). Functional assays revealed that CXCL8 depletion enhanced cellular sensitivity to IR, evidenced by decreased cell viability (Fig. 5B) and reduced colony formation capacity (Fig. 5C). Consistent with the link between CXCL8 and stemness, CXCL8 knockdown also impaired tumor sphere-forming capacity (Fig. 5D) and downregulated stemness-associated markers, including ALDH1, NANOG, SOX2, CD133, and OCT4 (Fig. 5E). These results indicate that CXCL8 is essential for maintaining the radio-resistant phenotype in NSCLC cells. To directly test whether EC-mediated radiosensitization requires CXCL8, we performed rescue experiments in CXCL8-overexpressing cells.

CXCL8 is functionally required for EC‑induced radiosensitization and CSC suppression

To determine whether CXCL8 serves as a functionally essential downstream effector of EC, we established A549RR cells stably overexpressing CXCL8 (A549RR-CXCL8; Fig. 6A). In contrast to vector‑transfected controls, CXCL8‑overexpressing cells were resistant to EC‑mediated radiosensitization: EC pretreatment failed to suppress post‑irradiation viability (Fig. 6B) or colony formation capacity (Fig. 6C) in A549RR-CXCL8 cells. Consistently, CXCL8 overexpression largely abrogated EC‑induced downregulation of CSC markers—ALDH1, NANOG, and SOX2 (Fig. 6D)—and restored tumor sphere formation capacity (Fig. 6E). Importantly, the suppressive effect of EC on tumor growth in vivo was also attenuated in CXCL8‑overexpressing xenografts (Fig. 6F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CXCL8 is a critical functional mediator of EC action and that targeting CXCL8 is both necessary and sufficient for EC‑induced radiosensitization and CSC elimination in NSCLC.

Discussion

In this study, we established a radioresistant NSCLC subline (A549RR) with enhanced CSC-like properties and demonstrated that EC effectively attenuates CSC traits and restores radiosensitivity. Through network pharmacology and molecular docking, we identified CXCL8 as a candidate target of EC. CETSA further confirmed direct binding between EC and CXCL8. Functional validation using complementary loss- and gain-of-function approaches confirmed the essential role of CXCL8. Knockdown of CXCL8 in A549RR cells enhanced radiosensitivity, suppressed tumor sphere formation, and downregulated CSC markers. Conversely, CXCL8 overexpression abrogated EC-induced radiosensitization. Collectively, these findings establish CXCL8 as a functionally essential mediator of EC action and position the EC–CXCL8 axis as a promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming radioresistance in NSCLC.

Radiotherapy remains a cornerstone of curative and palliative treatment for NSCLC, yet acquired radioresistance frequently limits its efficacy and contributes to disease relapse [18]. Naturally derived bioactive compounds have attracted increasing interest as adjunctive agents to overcome therapy resistance. Golden buckwheat, a medicinal plant, possesses anti‑inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial activities [19, 20] and has long been employed in the management of pulmonary diseases [3]. EC, the principal bioactive flavonoid isolated from golden buckwheat, has demonstrated broad pharmacological effects, including cardioprotective [21], hepatoprotective [22], and anti‑obesity properties [23]. In the oncology setting, EC has been shown to induce apoptosis in breast and prostate cancer cells [24], suppress triple‑negative breast tumor growth with efficacy comparable to doxorubicin [25], and serve as an effective adjunct in acute myeloid leukemia therapy [26]. Beyond its direct anti‑tumor effects, emerging evidence suggests that certain phytochemicals confer radioprotective or radiosensitizing activities [27]. We previously reported that EC enhances the radiosensitivity of NSCLC cells in both in vitro and in vivo models [5, 28]. Extending these observations, the present study demonstrates that EC reduces the survival of radioresistant NSCLC cells (A549RR) and potentiates the response of A549RR xenograft tumors to IR. These findings position EC as a promising naturally derived radiosensitizer for improving radiotherapy outcomes in NSCLC.

CSCs represent a phenotypically distinct subpopulation within tumors, endowed with self-renewal capacity and tumor-initiating potential [29]. Their intrinsic resilience to conventional therapies, including radiotherapy, positions CSCs as pivotal drivers of treatment resistance and disease relapse [30]. Accumulating evidence has established a causal link between CSC enrichment and acquired radioresistance. HOTAIR‑mediated maintenance of stemness promotes radioresistance in hepatocellular carcinoma [31], while enhanced CSC phenotypes are closely associated with radioresistance in NSCLC [32]. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies aimed at eradicating CSCs are increasingly recognized as a rational approach to radiosensitization. The present study demonstrated that radioresistant A549RR cells exhibit enriched CSC‑like properties. Although prior work has reported that EC suppresses stemness in various cancer models [12], we provide the evidence that EC effectively attenuates CSC traits specifically in the context of acquired radioresistance. These findings suggest that EC‑mediated radiosensitization is functionally linked to the elimination of CSC‑like characteristics. However, the molecular circuitry through which EC dismantles CSC stemness in radioresistant NSCLC has remained largely undefined.

Although bioactive constituents derived from medicinal plants have been extensively investigated for their radiosensitizing effects in lung cancer, their inherent multi-target pharmacology poses substantial challenges to mechanistic deconvolution. Network pharmacology offers a systematic framework to address this complexity by constructing “disease–target–drug” interaction networks, thereby enabling holistic assessment of compound action within biological systems. Applying this approach, we identified CXCL8 as a putative nodal mediator through which EC suppresses CSC traits and overcomes radioresistance in NSCLC. CXCL8 is a well-established proinflammatory chemokine implicated in multiple aspects of tumor progression. Liu et al. reported that DDX17‑driven CXCL8 upregulation promotes hepatocellular carcinoma aggressiveness [33], while Ren et al. demonstrated that CXCL8 facilitates M2 macrophage polarization in ovarian cancer [34]. Beyond its pro-tumorigenic functions, accumulating evidence links CXCL8 to therapeutic resistance. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, CXCL8 has been identified as a chemoresistance‑associated gene [35]; in cervical cancer, CXCL8 silencing sensitizes otherwise resistant cells to IR [16]. Importantly, CXCL8 has also been functionally implicated in the maintenance of cancer stemness [36, 37]. These well‑documented roles collectively support the biological plausibility of CXCL8 as a critical effector of EC‑mediated radiosensitization and CSC elimination in NSCLC.

Guided by these bioinformatic predictions, we performed systematic in vitro and in vivo validation. CXCL8 was upregulated in radioresistant A549RR cells, and this elevation was robustly suppressed by EC treatment. To determine whether EC directly engages CXCL8, we performed CETSA. EC treatment significantly enhanced the thermal stability of CXCL8 protein in cell lysates, indicating direct physical binding. To assess the functional role of CXCL8 in radioresistance independent of EC, we established A549RR cells with stable CXCL8 knockdown using shRNA. CXCL8 depletion enhanced radiosensitivity, suppressed tumor sphere formation, and downregulated CSC markers. These results demonstrate that CXCL8 is essential for maintaining the radioresistant and CSC-like phenotypes in NSCLC cells. Further, we established A549RR cells stably overexpressing CXCL8. Overexpression of CXCL8 attenuated EC‑induced suppression of cell viability and colony formation, and partially restored CSC phenotypes—including upregulation of stemness markers and enhanced tumor sphere formation capacity. Consistent with these in vitro observations, CXCL8 overexpression also diminished the tumor‑suppressive efficacy of EC in A549RR xenografts. Collectively, these results demonstrate that EC directly binds to CXCL8 and overcomes NSCLC radioresistance through targeted suppression of this chemokine, positioning the EC–CXCL8 axis as a promising therapeutic strategy for radiosensitization.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we provide evidence demonstrating direct binding between EC and CXCL8 (CETSA), the precise binding mode and stoichiometry remain to be resolved by co-crystallization or surface plasmon resonance analysis. Second, our data suggest that EC may regulate CXCL8 through dual mechanisms: CETSA demonstrates direct physical binding, while EC treatment also reduces CXCL8 protein levels, suggesting potential transcriptional or post-transcriptional regulation. The relative contributions of direct binding versus transcriptional downregulation to EC’s radiosensitizing effect remain to be fully dissected. Third, while our rescue experiments establish CXCL8 as a functionally essential mediator, the downstream signaling pathways through which CXCL8 drives stemness and radioresistance in NSCLC were not fully dissected. Fourth, our findings are primarily based on A549 cells. Although A549 is a widely used and well-characterized NSCLC cell line for radioresistance and CSC research, future validation in additional NSCLC cell lines will be important to establish the generalizability of the EC–CXCL8 axis. Fifth, our xenograft models were established using cell lines rather than patient-derived tissues. Future validation in PDX models and assessment of EC bioavailability in clinical specimens will be essential to establish translational relevance. Addressing these questions will further solidify the therapeutic rationale for targeting the EC–CXCL8 axis in radioresistant NSCLC.
