Mediating role of childbirth readiness in the relationship between social support and psychological birth trauma in primiparous women: a nationwide cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Dandan Ju, Ke Zhang, Mengmei Yuan, Bingbing Li, Tieying Zeng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the relationship between social support and psychological birth trauma among Chinese primiparous women and the mediating effects of childbirth readiness.DesignA cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2021 and March 2022.Setting10 general tertiary hospitals and 3 specialised maternity hospitals in 7 provinces in China.ParticipantsA total of 751 primiparous women were recruited for this study.Outcome measuresThe questionnaires consisted of questions on demographic characteristics, the Birth Trauma Scale, the Chinese Mandarin version of the Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey and the Childbirth Readiness Scale. Hierarchical multiple regression was used to explore the associated factors and mediating role of childbirth readiness in the relationship between social support and psychological birth trauma. The mediating model was examined by the PROCESS macro for SPSS.ResultsAfter adjusting for demographic variables, social support was negatively associated with psychological birth trauma (r=−0.242, p<0.01). Childbirth readiness had a positive correlation with social support (r=0.206, p<0.01) and a negative correlation with psychological birth trauma (r=−0.351, p<0.01). The hierarchical multiple regression model indicated that social support and childbirth readiness explained 3.9% and 7.7% of the variance in psychological birth trauma, respectively. Childbirth readiness partly mediated the association between social support and psychological birth trauma for primiparas.ConclusionsChildbirth readiness plays a mediating role between social support and psychological birth trauma among primiparas. Strategies and interventions to enhance childbirth readiness levels may be expected to improve the impact of social support on women’s psychological birth trauma.

Introduction

Psychological birth trauma (PBT) refers to the severe psychological harm caused by events that occur before and during childbirth1 2 and is characterised by intense fear, loss of control, powerlessness and helplessness during labour and birth.3 The incidence rate is high, and its effects are far-reaching. The prevalence of traumatic psychological distress experienced during childbirth in Iran, the Netherlands, Australia, the UK and the USA was reported to be between 9.0% and 55.5% in several studies.49 The negative impacts of PBT are mainly focused on the mother and spread in a ripple-like manner,10 having an influence on the mother’s health, the infant’s well-being, the relationship between mother and infant, the partnership, the medical staff and even subsequent pregnancies. As for mothers, PBT may bring about the emergence of post-traumatic stress. This is shown as traumatic memories, negative cognitions and exhibited avoidance behaviours. Moreover, it may also develop into post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)11 and even lead to adverse events such as suicide. In terms of infants’ health, breastfeeding is scant for infants due to maternal alienation and aversion, which affects their nutritional status and mental health during adolescence.12 13 A previous study highlighted that mothers with poor attachments with their infants might adopt harmful infant care and be less dedicated to childcare, thus affecting their children’s physical, cognitive and behavioural growth.14 Regarding their partner, they may blame themselves for nothing they can do to release the pain of maternal pregnancy and childbirth,15 and those traumatic memories can strain the marital relationship.4 For medical staff, secondary traumatic stress may appear because they witnessed and participated in the whole distress birth process, manifesting as powerlessness and helplessness and doubting their professional faith.16 17 Additionally, the women who had experienced negative traumatic births might avoid subsequent pregnancies, affecting their future fertility choices.14 18 Given the importance of PBT to women, exploring how to prevent women’s PBT is crucial to promoting positive birthing experiences and reducing the negative consequences of childbirth in women.14 19 However, there is scant evidence on prevention.

Prior studies indicated that PBT was affected by multiple factors, including age, income, history of sexual abuse, excruciating labour pain, previous trauma and high expectations.2022 Research has found a strong correlation between social support and PBT.23 Social support, defined as the practical, emotional, information, tangible, affectionate support perceived by individuals from their social networks,24 was confirmed to be a protective factor for PBT,22 which may reduce negative birth experiences, enhance physical and mental fitness and thereby prevent the PBT of the women. Previous studies have demonstrated that continuous support from healthcare providers during pregnancy, labour and birth reduces negative maternal psychological experiences,25 26 decreases the rate of emergency caesarean section, the interventions of instrumental vaginal delivery27 and improves maternal outcomes.28 Insufficient support further compounds women’s negative birth experiences and may lead to PBT.16 However, while the importance of the level of social support received by women is recognised, few studies have aimed to identify the mechanism underlying how social support affects the PBT of women.

In addition to social support, poor childbirth readiness levels may connect with the women’s PBT. Childbirth readiness refers to women’s level of childbirth readiness during pregnancy, which is a comprehensive assessment of their ability before childbirth and their perception of whether they are ready for childbirth.29 Inadequate childbirth readiness can endanger maternal and infant health and affect childbirth outcomes. Research has shown that insufficient childbirth readiness can cause prolonged labour, preterm labour, postpartum haemorrhage30 31 and even increasing maternal and infant mortality.32 Moreover, inadequate childbirth readiness may increase the incidence of birth trauma, lead to negative childbirth experiences, postpartum depression and even progress to PTSD.30 33

Childbirth is a developmental transition,34 and the transition theory by Schumacher and Meleis35 suggests that individuals in transition may face changes in physical health, role relationships, expectations and competencies that predispose them to risk during this vulnerable period. Social support as a facilitator of transition and childbirth readiness as interventions that influence transition outcomes may ultimately combine to influence patterns of primiparous response. Based on this, this study proposes the hypothesis that childbirth readiness partially mediates the relationship between medical social support and PBT in order to provide a basis for improving PBT, promoting healthy pregnancy and increasing women’s fertility intentions.

Considering that primiparous women would not be disturbed by past negative birth experiences, we determined to select the primiparous women as the research population. To sum up, this is the first study to examine the impact of social support on PBT and the relationship between PBT, social support and childbirth readiness. This study aims to verify the following three hypotheses among primiparous women: (1) social support has a negative effect on PBT, (2) childbirth readiness has a negative effect on PBT, (3) childbirth readiness mediates the association between social support and PBT.

Methods

Study design and sample

A cross-sectional study of women in obstetric wards. The National Bureau of Statistics of China divided economic regions into four regions. Based on these regions, we selected seven provinces at random (Hainan, Zhejiang, Shanxi, Hubei, Guangxi, Xinjiang and Jilin). The study included 10 comprehensive hospitals and 3 specialised hospitals from these provinces in mainland China. After that, we contacted the principals of the tertiary and secondary hospitals in the seven regions that were sampled. A pilot study was conducted to choose 30 participants to assess the questionnaires. The results showed that the participants understood the questions in the instruments. It showed good reliability. After the pilot study, we seldom revised the questionnaires.

This research was conducted from November 2021 to March 2022. The sample size was calculated using Kendall’s rough estimate: the sample size was 5–10 times the number of independent variables, and then 10% of invalid participants were considered.36 The questionnaires in the study included 61 independent variables, and 339–678 participants were selected. 763 questionnaires were distributed in this study. Participants were recruited using a convenient sampling method. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) primiparous women aged ≥18 years with a singleton pregnancy; (2) willing to participate in the study; (3) having given birth within 72 hours. The exclusion criteria were women who had psychological diseases (eg, diagnosis of depression, PTSD, bipolar disorder) or severe obstetric complications recorded in the hospital medical record system (eg, rupture of the uterus, postpartum haemorrhage, amniotic fluid embolism). The incomplete questionnaires were excluded. A total of 763 questionnaires were distributed, and 751 valid questionnaires were returned, with a valid return rate of 98.43%. The process for data selection is shown in online supplemental figure 1. We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology cross-sectional checklist when writing our report.37

Patient and public involvement

It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in our research’s design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans.

Measurements

The Birth Trauma Scale (BTS), a 15-item self-report scale, was used to assess the PBT levels of the women.38 Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (not meet at all) to 5 (exact match). The total score is between 15 and 75. A higher total score indicates stronger PBT levels. The PBT score consists of four dimensions: being neglected (four items, Cronbach’s α=0.878), out of control (four items, Cronbach’s α=0.804), physiological emotional response (four items, Cronbach’s α=0.863) and cognitive behavioural response (three items, Cronbach’s α=0.904). Cronbach’s α for the present study was 0.937. The Scale-level Content Validity Index (S-CVI) of the BTS was 0.942, and the Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) was 0.870~1.000.

Social support

The Chinese Mandarin Version of the Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey (MOS-SSS-CM)39 was used to assess the women’s perception of social support; this scale has 20-item and includes 19 items assessing the availability of certain types of social support in emotional/informational, tangible, affectionate and positive social interaction domains and 1 item evaluating the support network. It is a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (none of the time) to 5 (all of the time), with a higher score indicating greater social support. The Cronbach’s α and 2-week retest reliability coefficient for the MOS-SSS-CM were 0.98 and 0.84, respectively.39 Cronbach’s α for the present study was 0.973. The overall model was supported by the goodness-of-fit measures, with χ2/df=2.69, Normed Fit Index (NFI)=0.87, Non-Normed Fit Index (NNFI)=0.90 and Comparative Fit Index (CFI)=0.91.

Childbirth readiness levels

The Childbirth Readiness Scale (CRS) was used to assess the childbirth readiness levels.29 The CRS is an 18-item scale with four dimensions: self-management, information literacy, birth confidence and birth plan. It is a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The total score is between 18 and 90. A higher total score indicates greater childbirth readiness levels. The CRS has good reliability (split-half reliability=0.88, Cronbach’s α=0.94), and was validated in Chinese pregnant women.29 In the present study, the CRS’s Cronbach’s α=0.957. The S-CVI and I-CVI were calculated as 0.971 (>0.9) and 0.880–1.000 (>0.78), respectively.

Demographic characteristics

The demographic characteristics included eight questions on participants’ age, education, marital status, residence area, employment status, monthly household income (Renminbi, Chinese Yuan), pregnancy sleep status and pregnancy exercise status.

Statistical analyses

IBM SPSS Statistics V.26.0 (IBM, Asia Analytics Shanghai) was used to conduct the statistical analysis. Two-tailed p<0.05 was set as the significant level. All continuous variables were tested for normality, and they all confirmed normality. Independent t-test and one-way analysis of variance were used to describe group differences in demographic characteristics of continuous variables. Pearson’s correlations were used to examine the correlations between PBT, childbirth readiness and social support. The hierarchical multiple regression analysis was performed to explore childbirth readiness as a potential mediating role in the association between social support and PBT. Control variables were entered in model 1. In model 2, social support was added, and in model 3, childbirth readiness levels were added as a mediator. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) of <5 indicated that there is no severe multicollinearity. All the VIF values were <5 in this study.

The mediation model was conducted using PROCESS V.4.0 for the SPSS macro-program. The selection of control variables was based on the statistics of the univariate analysis. Social support was modelled as the independent variable, with PBT as the dependent variable and childbirth readiness as the mediator. The total effect (path c), the direct effect (path c') and the indirect effects (path a×b) were examined. To assess the indirect effect, 5000 bootstrapped samples based on bias-corrected CIs were used.40 The effect is considered significant if the 95% bootstrap CI does not include zero.

The mediating role of childbirth readiness in the relationship between social support and PBT in primiparous women

Table 4 shows the results of the mediation analysis. The association between social support and PBT (path c) was examined. The total effect of social support on PBT (c=−0.164, p<0.01) was initially evaluated. Then, the indirect effect of social support on PBT via childbirth readiness was observed (path a×b, a=0.138, b=−0.312, a×b (95% CI)=−0.043 (−0.074, –0.020)). The CI for indirect effect did not contain zero, suggesting that childbirth readiness mediated between social support and PBT. When childbirth readiness was entered into the model as a mediator, the direct effect of social support on PBT (path c') was still significant (c'=−0.121, p<0.01). Therefore, childbirth readiness had a partial mediating effect on the relationship between social support and PBT for primiparas. To understand the effect size of the mediating pathway, we calculated the proportion of the total effect of social support on PBT that was mediated by childbirth readiness using the formula (a×b)/c. The proportion of childbirth readiness was 26.22%. Discussion

Results from this study support the hypothesis that social support was negatively associated with PBT, and childbirth readiness was the positive predictor of PBT among primiparous women. Furthermore, we found that childbirth readiness mediated in promoting the effects of social support on reducing PBT. The current research provides evidence of potential mechanisms for the association between social support and PBT and provides an empirical basis for PBT among primiparous women.

In the current study, we found that compared with the primiparous women who were in well-pregnancy sleep status, those in moderate or poor sleep status tended to have a higher risk of PBT. Poor sleep status during pregnancy may lead to adverse pregnancy and childbirth outcomes19 41 and even negative pregnancy and childbirth experiences,42 which may increase the probability of PBT. The current study also found that the primiparous women with young age and lower monthly household income tended to experience PBT. This finding is corroborated by Herman’s research43 that those who were more vulnerable in society (young and poor) may be more likely to experience trauma.

As we expected, there was a negative relationship between social support and PBT, consistent with previous studies. Taheri et al44 reported that social support such as providing continuous care during labour and birth, implementing music therapy and reducing medical intervention can reduce the traumatic experience during labour and birth. Additionally, research indicated that the positive relationship between women and healthcare providers is crucial in creating a sense of control, providing women with safe and supportive birthing environments and influencing childbirth outcomes.45 On the contrary, prior studies showed that most women shared their experiences of medical staff betraying their trust, neglecting them and being apathetic and dismissive.14 Such behaviours of medical staff contributed extensively to women’s childbirth trauma. Elmir et al,46 integrating 10 qualitative studies on birth trauma, found that the key concerns of women were communication, being respected and emotional support. Also, good social support is a protective factor for birth trauma. Moreover, spousal support is a crucial factor during pregnancy and the perinatal period, as it can improve maternal self-esteem, be a buffer against pregnancy tensions and stresses and is essential for maintaining a positive emotional experience.47 48 Adequate social support can influence women’s childbirth outcomes, enhance childbirth safety and improve the childbirth experience.16 Therefore, it is suggested that effective interventions to promote social support for women should be implemented.

This study found a negative correlation between childbirth readiness and PBT in primiparous women, which was consistent with previous studies.49 50 Promoting self-management during pregnancy could help women maintain a favourable physical and psychological state for childbirth and improve childbirth experiences,51 reducing obstetric complications.52 Additionally, skill-based childbirth preparation can increase childbirth experience for first-time mothers and improve their self-efficacy and birth confidence.53 Many studies have shown that women’s psychological factors directly impact childbirth outcomes. Furthermore, a pregnant woman’s belief in her ability to childbirth has been associated with numerous positive childbirth experiences. Hence, helping women a better birth preparedness will be a protective factor against PBT.

More importantly, this study confirmed the significant indirect effect of social support on PBT through childbirth readiness, which was consistent with the transition theory by Schumacher and Meleis.35 Social support can be a transition facilitator, and childbirth readiness can be an intervention that collectively affects birth outcomes. Simply put, social support is both directly affected and indirectly affected via childbirth readiness and PBT. The overall level of women’s childbirth readiness is described as four dimensions: self-management, information literacy, birth confidence and birth plan.29 Social support is understood as the help that women receive from and perceive within their social networks.54 Individuals with a high level of social support were more likely to adopt better childbirth readiness, which further affected the PBT. These findings suggest that PBT could be improved by enhancing the level of social support and developing better childbirth readiness strategies. Crowe and von Baeyer55 also identified that childbirth and parenting knowledge from childbirth education lectures, higher birth confidence and less anxiety would enhance the birth experience. Moreover, a positive birth can represent a ‘peak’ experience.56 Therefore, adequate social support and childbirth preparation training are suggested to be supplied for the women to prevent PBT.

Based on the findings of this study, we offer the following suggestions, hoping to help primiparous women prevent or reduce PBT. First, women and their families should be informed about risk factors and the adverse consequences of PBT from preconception until the end of the perinatal period.50 Family members should provide the mothers enough material and spiritual support to maintain good physical and mental fitness to ensure their health.57 Healthcare providers should also pay more attention to the feelings and communication needs of the mothers, and help them in discussing and expressing their doubts and concerns individually.49 Second, efficient interventions for enhancing childbirth readiness should be developed. Medical staff should provide guidance on women’s daily behaviour and self-protection practices, promote self-management and assist in creating a sensible birth plan. Encouraging women to know more about pregnancy and childbirth and improving information literacy are also crucial. Moreover, medical staff and family members should help the women believe in themselves and build birth confidence.

Some limitations of this study should be discussed. First, a cross-sectional study did not allow us to draw a causal relationship between social support, childbirth readiness and PBT. Longitudinal studies need to confirm the relationship between the three variables. Second, the study variables were evaluated using self-report questionnaires. Nevertheless, all the scales have good reliability and validity and have been verified in Chinese women. Third, the data on childbirth readiness was collected within 72 hours after the women gave birth; recall bias may exist. Future studies could focus on collecting data on women’s childbirth readiness during their pregnancy. Finally, this study is limited to primiparas. The study also ignored the importance of childbirth phobia. Future studies could extend this research to include multiparas and assess the childbirth phobia among pregnant women.

Conclusions

In conclusion, for primiparous women, social support and childbirth readiness were negatively correlated with PBT, and childbirth readiness played a mediating role between social support and PBT. This study draws out the implication of focusing on childbirth readiness when exploring the mechanism of how social support affected PBT. Strategies and interventions to enhance childbirth readiness levels may be expected to improve the impact of social support on women’s PBT.
