Genetic predisposition and the impact of missense genetic variants on the structural and functional protein dynamics in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Anitha Mohanraj Barney, Barney Isaac, Devasahayam J Christopher, Balamugesh Thangakunam, Janani Balaji, Venkata Raghava Mohan, Christhunesa Soundararajan Christudass, Debashish Danda, Sumita Danda
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive lung disease characterised by an altered extracellular matrix and destruction of alveolar architecture.[12] Approximately 3 million people worldwide are affected, highlighting the urgent need for research and effective treatments.[3] In India, IPF prevalence among interstitial lung diseases is estimated at 17%.[4] Diagnosis is based on HRCT findings, such as honeycomb cysts and reticulation patterns, particularly a usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern. If the HRCT shows a UIP pattern, a clinical-radiological diagnosis is sufficient; otherwise, a biopsy via cryo-TBLB or VATS is needed for confirmation.[567]

The aetiology of IPF is unknown, but it is believed to result from genetic and environmental factors. Risk factors include older age, male sex, smoking, gastroesophageal reflux, obstructive sleep apnea, air pollution, herpesvirus infection, and certain occupational exposures.[68] Mutations in TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1 genes, and variations in DSP, AKAP13, CTNNA, and DPP9 genes confer a predisposition to IPF.[6] Genome-wide association studies in IPF have detected several genetic polymorphisms in the MUC5B, Toll-interacting protein (TOLLIP), or even the Toll-like receptor (TLR) 3 that might confer an increased risk of developing fibrosis.[2910] Mutations within surfactant protein genes that codify SP-A and SP-D have a crucial role in the pathogenesis of IPF.[11]

Research on the genetic basis of IPF in India is limited, as all whole-exome sequencing and genome-wide association studies have been conducted abroad. A US study identified 39 differentially expressed genes associated with IPF, but similar research has not been done in India.[12] Additionally, there is no global research on the analysis of genetic variants or protein prediction models for missense variants in IPF identified by whole-exome sequencing. Therefore, we proposed to conduct exploratory whole-exome sequencing and 3D protein prediction modelling among Indian IPF patients.

We screened all patients between February and June 2024 with confirmed ILD attending the pulmonology outpatient clinic. Patients fulfilling the IPF diagnostic criteria according to the 2022 ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT IPF clinical practice guidelines update[1314] were confirmed by the interstitial lung disease multidisciplinary discussions (ILD MDD). We obtained written informed consent from the willing participants and administered a structured questionnaire to collect the necessary sociodemographic, clinical, and laboratory details. In addition, 2 mL of blood was collected in an EDTA tube for whole-exome sequencing analysis.

For the whole-exome sequencing analysis, we extracted DNA from blood by using a custom capture kit to perform targeted gene capture. The libraries were sequenced to a mean depth of >80–100X on the Illumina sequencing platform. We annotated the clinically relevant mutations in coding and non-coding regions by using published variants in the literature and a set of disease databases: ClinVar, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), Human Genome Mutation Database (HGMD), Leiden Open Variation Database (LOVD), DECIPHER (population Copy number variant), and SwissVar. All the identified variants were reconfirmed with the Human Genome Mutation Database (GRCh38 assembly in Ensembl) and Varsome (The Human Genomics Community Database). Variants were described according to the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) guidelines.

We developed a 3D structure of both wild-type and mutant types via the SWISS-MODEL Repository (http://swissmodel.expasy.org/repository/), a database of 3D protein structure models generated by the SWISS-MODEL homology-modelling pipeline. The models were selected based on the sequence identity and high Global Model Quality Estimate (GMQE) value, and the predicted models were validated using PROCHECK software (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/software/PROCHECK9/). Functional and physio-chemical effects of point mutation were analysed using HOPE software (https://www3.cmbi.umcn.nl/hope/), and the genomic variants were mapped onto the Missense 3D software tool (https://missense3d.bc.ic.ac.uk/missense3d/).[151617] We analysed the data by using SPSS V 21 (License Number (Customer ID): 200699; Vendor: SPSS South Asia Pvt Ltd. Bangalore). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation or as median and interquartile range (IQR) based on their distribution. This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB min number: 12698 dated 25.03.2020).

Variants identified in the whole exome sequencing of the IPF cohort

We analysed the VUS variants by using Varsome for clinical relevance. Case 1 had a variant in the TERT gene (c.3208G > A; p.Val1070Met) classified as benign supporting (BP4). Cases 3 and 4 had MUC5B gene variants (c.3004G > A; p.Val1002Met and c.11224T > G; p.Ser3742Ala), both classified as benign moderate to strong (BP4). Case 5 had compound heterozygous variants in CFAP74 (c.2810G > C; p.Arg937Thr and c.2020G > C; p.Ser674Gly), classified as benign strong (BP4). Cases 2, 6, and 7 had pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants. Case 2 featured a TTR gene variant (c.34G > C; p.Ala12Pro), classified as pathogenic moderate (PP3). Case 6 had a frameshift variant in DSP (c.4687_4688del; p.Leu1563GlufsTer63), classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic. Case 7 revealed a variant in ECT2 (c.1616A > G; p.Tyr539Cys), reclassified as pathogenic very strong (PP3) without literature support. Caution is advised when correlating clinically.

We have summarised the structural and functional impacts of various variants, including images of wild-type and mutant proteins, in Table 3. The missense 3D prediction tool indicated structural damage in the ECT2 gene, disrupting all side-chain hydrogen bonds, while the HOPE tool suggested that this mutation introduces an amino acid with different properties, potentially impairing function. The mutation in CFAP74 [c.2810G > C (p.Arg937Thr)] results in a loss of charge and interactions, introducing a more hydrophobic residue that may disturb protein folding. Similarly, a variant in MUC5B [c.11224T > G (p.Ser3742Ala)] also introduces a hydrophobic residue, impacting hydrogen bonds and folding. Root mean square deviation (RMSD) analyses showed that none of the variants exceeded 2Å, indicating no major structural deviations.

In our study, the mean age of our population was 63 years, and the mean age of onset was 59 years, similar to the subjects of the other Indian studies.[418] Our study had a male predominance (93%) [Table 1]. Though IPF is globally male-predominant, in India, studies have reported both male (73%) and female-predominance (59%).[418] They manifested a mild restrictive ventilatory defect (mean FVC: 2.01L, 63%) [Tables 1 and 2], and nearly half of our patients were smokers. Other studies also had patients with similar characteristics.[48]

WES identified benign missense variants c.3004G > A (p.Val1002Met) and c.11224T > G (p.Ser3742Ala) in the MUC5B gene in two patients without a cough, a key feature of IPF. One had stable disease at diagnosis and was not started on antifibrotics, but both variants suggest susceptibility to pulmonary fibrosis. IPF was incidentally found in case 6 during a cardiac arrhythmia evaluation. WES also revealed a heterozygous frameshift variant c.4687_4688del (p.Leu1563GlufsTer63) in the DSP gene, potentially linked to arrhythmogenic right ventricular dysplasia-8 (OMIM#607450), supporting the connection between cardiac arrhythmias and IPF.[19] The patient did not have ARVD8. Another patient with early-onset disease had a missense variant c.1616A > G (p.Tyr539Cys) in the ECT2 gene, an oncogene linked to a hyperplastic lung epithelial cell phenotype in IPF. The variant’s significance for IPF susceptibility is unknown, and he will be monitored for lung malignancies. Our cohort also had variants in TERT, TTR, MUC5B, DSP, ECT2, and CFAP74, consistent with the literature.[16920] We found structural damage in the ECT gene and functional alterations in MUC5B, ECT2, and CFAP74, though these were not significantly harmful. There is no relevant literature on this topic. This study is the first molecular analysis of IPF from India and globally reports structural and functional alterations from missense variants in IPF identified through WES.

We encountered several limitations during our study. Sanger sequencing was not performed, and we did not recruit control subjects to validate the variants, as this investigation is intended to be exploratory. A comprehensive, hypothesis-driven study is being planned for future implementation to further validate and expand upon the preliminary findings. Due to a lack of evidence in the literature, we were unable to classify the variants as pathogenic or likely pathogenic. Although segregation analysis, particularly parental validation, was suggested for some variants, we could not carry this out due to the parents’ passing. Additionally, the small sample size may be insufficient to accurately estimate the spectrum of genetic variations in IPF in India.

In conclusion, this exploratory research has laid the groundwork for exciting future investigations into the genetic predispositions and the structural and functional dynamics of proteins associated with IPF in India. While the clinical significance remains partially understood, there’s great potential for deeper exploration ahead.
