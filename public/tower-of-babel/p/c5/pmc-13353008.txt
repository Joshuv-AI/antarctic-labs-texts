Hand hygiene compliance among healthcare workers at a tertiary maternity hospital in Sierra Leone: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sia Morenike Tengbe, Ibrahim Franklyn Kamara, Mary Jane Koroma, Bobson Derrick Fofanah, Desta Betula Ali, Braima Munda Koroma, Joseph Mustapha Macarthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal and neonatal mortality remains a global health problem, with infections accounting for a significant proportion of this burden. Major predisposing factors include poor aseptic techniques and contaminated hands of healthcare workers (HCWs). Hand hygiene (HH) is the most effective and simplest infection prevention and control (IPC) measure to minimise infection transmission. This study aimed to assess HH compliance and identify barriers among HCWs at a tertiary hospital in Sierra Leone.MethodsThis hospital-based cross-sectional study utilised a standardised World Health Organization (WHO) HH observation form to assess HH compliance among HCWs in the labour ward of the Princess Christian Maternity Hospital (PCMH). A root cause analysis was also used to determine the barriers to HH compliance. Data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel, with findings presented in frequencies and proportions.ResultsA total of 404 HH opportunities were observed at the labour ward. The overall HH compliance rate was 32%, and HH compliance for nurses, midwives, doctors, and nursing students was 37%, 34%, 27%, and 0%, respectively. Based on the WHO “5 moments” for HH compliance, the highest adherence was observed for moment 3 (83%), followed by moment 4 (68%). Conversely, moment 2 had the lowest compliance (6%). Identified barriers to HH compliance included organisational concerns, availability of IPC materials and equipment, staffing, environmental factors, cultural influences, and existing practices.ConclusionHH compliance among HCWs at PCMH was found to be low. To enhance HH practices and patient safety, promoting HH practices through investing in IPC supplies, strategic placement of workplace reminders, training, mentorship, and coaching should be prioritised.Keywords: Hand hygiene compliance, Healthcare associated infection, Infection prevention and control, Health care workers, Hospitals, Sierra Leone

Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) pose a major public health concern, leading to increasing morbidity, mortality, hospital stay, and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [1, 2]. It is estimated that approximately 15% of patients in low and middle-income countries would acquire an HAI, with one-tenth of them dying from it annually [3–5]. In Africa, the pooled prevalence of HAI is 12.9%, with a higher prevalence in Western Africa (15.5%) [6]. Furthermore, it varies across countries, with a prevalence of 8.2% in Ghana and 13.7% in Libya [5, 7–9]. This significant prevalence of HAI in Africa suggests poor hand hygiene (HH) compliance and a substantial AMR burden, as a strong correlation exists between poor HH compliance, HAIs, and AMR [10].

Given the high prevalence of HAIs and weak HAI and AMR surveillance systems in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), implementing infection prevention and control (IPC) interventions, such as HH practices, is critical for preventing infection transmission and enhancing patient safety [11–13]. While everyone accessing healthcare services is at risk of HAI, women during childbirth face heightened risk due to several invasive procedures, including frequent vaginal examinations, caesarean sections, intravenous cannulation, and catheterisation [14, 15]. Fortunately, appropriate HH practices in hospitals can mitigate this risk [16–18].

Studies have shown that HH compliance among healthcare workers (HCWs) is higher in Europe and North America compared to sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), including Sierra Leone [19, 20]. A recent systematic review highlighted low HH compliance (21%) in SSA countries [21]. In Sierra Leone, a study across two non-maternity tertiary hospitals revealed a wide range of compliance rates (32–60%) [22].

HH compliance also varies across different wards, with lower rates in the labour ward (38%) and general care wards (40%) as compared to critical and intensive care units (60%) [23, 24]. Additionally, it also varies among HCW cadres, with nurses demonstrating better HH compliance than medical doctors in most studies [11, 25–27]. Based on the World Health Organization (WHO) five Moments of HH, HCWs often tend to perform HH practices for self-protection rather than adopting a patient-centric approach [21, 28, 29].

Several studies have identified factors that influence HH compliance rates among HCWs. Healthcare workers’ education and training seem to go hand in hand with HH compliance. Increased knowledge and awareness of HH practices among HCWs have been shown to improve compliance [2, 11, 30]. In addition to training, staff motivation, adequate health workforce, and supervision result in appropriate HH compliance rates [31, 32]. Conversely, HH compliance also depends on having an enabling environment and infrastructure. On the other hand, high workload, understaffing, overcrowding, and frequent stockouts of IPC supplies are strong predictors of poor HH compliance [26, 33].

In Sierra Leone, studies have documented sub-optimal IPC status across hospitals [2, 34, 35]. HH compliance rate among HCWs in two non-maternity tertiary hospitals was also found to be low [29]. Considering the services frequently provided at maternity hospitals, there is an increased risk of infection transmission if HH practices are not performed appropriately. Furthermore, no study has evaluated the HH compliance rate and barriers among HCWs in a maternity tertiary hospital. To address this knowledge gap, this study evaluated HH compliance among HCWs and barriers that affect its practices at a tertiary maternity hospital in Sierra Leone. Findings from this study are relevant as they contribute to the growing body of evidence highlighting the need to strengthen hospital IPC initiatives to reduce maternal mortality, reduce the spread of infection, and AMR.

Methods

Study design

This was a hospital-based cross-sectional study that utilised a standardised WHO hand hygiene observation form [36].

Study site

General setting

Sierra Leone, located on the west coast of Africa, is bordered by Guinea and Liberia [37]. The country comprises five regions and 16 districts, with a population of 7.5 million [38]. The healthcare delivery system has public and private facilities, with the government’s public facilities divided into tertiary hospitals [6], secondary hospitals [23], and peripheral health units (1185) [35, 39]. The tiered structure facilitates upward referrals from primary to secondary to tertiary with maternity health services at all levels.

Site-specific setting

Princess Christian Maternity Hospital (PCMH) is Sierra Leone’s sole tertiary and referral hospital providing obstetric and gynaecological services. It operates 24 h, with a bed capacity of 140 beds, handling over 7800 deliveries annually. It offers free healthcare services to pregnant women, lactating mothers, and children under the age of five [2, 40, 41]. It has a hospital IPC programme, committee, focal point, and team responsible for implementing IPC interventions, which include regular hand hygiene observation and other IPC monitoring activities [2].

WHO hand hygiene observation form

The WHO HH observation form was used for data collection. This is the gold standard for assessing HH compliance during routine patient care [42]. This form was developed to observe HH compliance based on the “five moments” concept and is divided into two sections.

The first section calculates basic compliance, which involves monitoring opportunities for HH and the type of HH action taken (performed action: hand rubbing with alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) formula, handwashing with soap and water, and missed action) across different healthcare workers' professional categories. The second section of the form is used to calculate compliance related to the “five moments” indications for HH (Moment 1: Before touching a patient; Moment 2: Before a clean/aseptic procedure; Moment 3: After body fluid exposure risk; Moment 4: After touching a patient; and Moment 5: after touching the patient’s surroundings).

Data collection

Data was collected at the labour ward from 5 to 20th February 2024 by the IPC link personnel, the hospital’s IPC focal person, and cross-validated by the principal investigator. All data collectors were trained in the use of the tool and possess expertise in conducting HH observation. 69 HCWs were discretely observed using the WHO HH observation form while they had direct contact with patients or their surroundings at the labour ward, with all possible HH opportunities and actions recorded. Each observation session lasted for about 20 min, allowing sufficient time for the HCWs to revert to their normal behaviour, thereby minimising the Hawthorne effect [43]. Not more than two HCWs were observed at the same time. Observations were conducted across all three shifts (early, late, and night) to capture HH practices over 24 h. There was no direct interaction with healthcare workers. A total of 404 opportunities were observed, which is above the WHO threshold of 200 opportunities per unit. Positive hand hygiene action was recorded according to the method used (rubbing with ABHR, hand washing with soap and water, or a combination of both), whereas negative hand hygiene actions (zero action) were recorded, including glove use. The data collection process did not interfere with patient care, as HCWs were observed during service delivery.

To identify barriers to HH practices, a brainstorming session was conducted with the HCWs (one medical doctor and three midwives) in the labour ward and the hospital IPC team (one IPC focal point and three link personnel) to discuss the root causes of poor HH compliance. We used the round‑robin method, wherein everyone contributes one idea per turn; this prevents dominant voices from taking over and ensures junior staff or quiet members' participation. The brainstorming session lasted for one hour, wherein 10 min were allocated to defining a precise problem, followed by 40 min to discuss the causes, and 10 min for prioritisation and validation. During the session, HCWs listed several barriers to HH compliance, which were grouped into categories and constructed into a fishbone diagram. Since we were addressing a health-related issue, we used standard cause categories such as staff (skills, training), organization (protocols, workflows), culture/practice (guidelines compliance, monitoring), Environment (location of ABHR, facility design), Materials (stock, logistics), and Equipment (functionality, availability). This ensures consistency and avoids random or duplicated causes. Furthermore, they suggested solutions to the barriers identified, which will, in turn, improve HH compliance in the unit and the facility as a whole.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel Version 16.92. The compliance rate was calculated by using the formula: Compliance (%) = (Actions)/(Opportunities) × 100%, as stated in the WHO hand hygiene technical reference manual [44]. HH compliance was analysed by professional cadres, and according to the WHO Five Moments of HH [44]. The barriers to HH compliance were classified under different sections on a fishbone diagram to illustrate the various factors that influence adherence. Solutions to barriers identified were presented in a tabular format.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 69 HCWs were observed for HH practices, comprising 27 (39%) midwives, 21 (31%) nurses, 14 (20%) medical doctors, and 7 (21%) student nurses.

Hand hygiene compliance among healthcare workers

A total of 404 HH opportunities were observed, and the overall HH compliance rate was low (32%) (130 HH actions performed out of 404 HH opportunities). Compliance rate varied among the different professional cadres, with nurses demonstrating the highest compliance (37%), followed by midwives (34%), doctors (27%), and least among nursing students (0%) (Table 1).

Barriers to HH compliance and suggested solutions

Several barriers contributing to HH noncompliance in the labour ward were identified by medical doctors, midwives, and the IPC team; they were then categorised into organisational, materials/equipment, staff, environment, and culture/practice. Key barriers include organizational (lack of mentorship and orientation training of newly posted staff); lack of materials and limited equipment (stockout of hand hygiene supplies such as liquid soap and ABHR, non-functional hand basins); staffing (forgetfulness, understaffing, lack of knowledge on hand hygiene); hospital environment (water supply interruptions, increased workload, and high patient load) and workplace culture and practices such as glove use. Discussion

This study is the first comprehensive assessment of HH compliance and associated barriers at a tertiary maternity hospital in Sierra Leone. Our findings indicate that HH compliance among healthcare workers was low and the identified barriers to appropriate HH practices were lack of mentorship and orientation training of newly posted staff, stockout of hand hygiene supplies such as liquid soap and ABHR, non-functional hand basins, forgetfulness, understaffing, lack of knowledge on hand hygiene, water supply interruptions, increased workload, and high patient load and workplace culture and practices.

Hand hygiene compliance among HCWs was low (32%). Our finding is similar to a study conducted in a non-maternity tertiary hospital (32%) in Sierra Leone [22] and consistent with a systematic review of HH compliance among birth attendants in LMIC (1.3–38%) [22, 24]. In contrast to our findings, studies conducted in Ethiopia (21%), Rwanda (23%), and Nigeria (29%) have all shown lower HH compliance among HCWs [21, 45, 46]. This indicates that while our findings are concerning, they are not the lowest in the region and call for tailored interventions to improve the situation in Sierra Leone. Given that HH compliance is a key factor in preventing and controlling the spread of HAI and AMR, our findings provide evidence to advocate and encourage the senior hospital management and HCWs to prioritize interventions that promote good HH practice to improve patient safety and outcomes.

It was observed that nurses and midwives were more compliant with HH practices than medical doctors, as their HH compliance scores were higher. In keeping with our findings, studies conducted in Bangladesh, China, Ethiopia, and Sierra Leone report a similar trend [22, 47–49]. This suggests that poor HH compliance among medical doctors is not unique to Sierra Leone but is a global problem [50]. The low HH compliance rate among medical doctors in our study might be attributed to the fact that IPC training and implementation at national, sub-national, and healthcare facility levels have been mainly centred around the nursing profession. This indicates the need for a paradigm shift: IPC training should be expanded to all HCW cadres, striving for a culture of HH compliance that embeds IPC knowledge across varying professional cadres. Efforts to improve compliance must include medical doctors as key stakeholders in the healthcare environment to ensure a holistic approach to infection prevention.

Regarding the WHO's 5 moments of HH, we observed an interesting finding that serves as a call to action for behavioural change among HCWs. Across the different cadres of HCWs, HH was performed more frequently when HCWs considered themselves at risk of infection (HCW protection) rather than when providing health care services to patients (patient protection). The highest level of compliance was seen for moment 3, followed by moment 4, and the lowest for moment 2. A similar pattern was seen in a study conducted in a tertiary hospital in Nigeria, with the highest HH compliance level in moment 3 [21]. Furthermore, a study conducted in Japan also documented that body fluid exposure risk and after-touching patient compliance rates were higher compared to other moments [51]. This is a concern as both HCWs, and patients should be protected to ensure quality and safe health service provision. We recommend that HCWs should be trained and mentored on the importance of the WHO’s 5 Moments of HH and that all the different moments should be given the same priority, and not only focus on the moment when they are at risk of infection. Additionally, patient education on HH might help to improve HH compliance among HCWs, as patients might motivate and prompt HCWs to perform HH [52].

This study found that glove use was a high contributor to HH non-compliance among HCWs (39%). This is similar to studies conducted in India, Iran, and Nigeria [11, 30, 33]. Although glove use is important in the prevention of HAIs and AMR, it does not replace hand hygiene practices [53]. Furthermore, the false sense of security that comes with glove use often results in a risk of contamination of HCWs' hands [54]. We, therefore, recommend training of HCWs in the labour ward at PCMH on the role and appropriate use of gloves to prevent HAIs and AMR.

The major barriers to HH compliance at PCMH were frequent stockouts of HH supplies (ABHR and liquid soap), interrupted water supply, poor positioning of sinks, lack of HH reminders, staffing shortages, and high workload. These findings resonate with the recent global report on IPC status which found that only 40.3% of tertiary hospitals in LMIC have systems in place to respond to staff needs [10]. An assessment of the built environment, materials, and equipment of IPC further revealed that only 24% of healthcare facilities in LMIC have hand washing stations and 68% have a continuous water supply [10]. These deficits in the core components of IPC may justify the sub-optimal HH compliance among HCWs in our study. This calls for concerted action for the mobilisation of the required human and financial resources to ensure the achievement of minimum IPC standards at PCMH. The hospital IPC programme, committee, and team should be empowered to carry out their annual work plan to ensure the minimum requirements are achieved.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, this study used a WHO-standardised hand hygiene observation form to assess HH compliance among HCWs, ensuring comparison of our findings with other HH compliance studies done nationally and internationally. Second, data were collected by the hospital IPC focal point and IPC link personnel, and cross-validated by the principal investigators, all of whom have expertise in conducting HH observation. This helped to reduce errors, ensure high-quality data, and improve internal validity. Third, we use the strengthening of the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to report our findings [55].

Despite these strengths, our study faced some limitations, including observation bias (Hawthorne effect), in which HCWs may have modified their behaviour because of their awareness of being observed [56]. Furthermore, since the data collectors were IPC focal persons and link personnel, there may be observer or social desirability bias. This might impact the result even though the IPC personnel have been trained to implement HH compliance as a quality improvement process.

Also, given that the study was focused on HH compliance, we did not assess whether the proper techniques of hand wash or hand rub were used. Our study is purely descriptive as aligned with the study objectives. Furthermore, it was not powered for inferential analysis. Finally, even though tertiary hospitals in Sierra Leone have similar IPC structures, the generalizability of these findings should be done with caution, as the study was conducted in only one tertiary hospital in the country.

Conclusion

Hand hygiene compliance among the different cadres of HCWs at the labour ward at PCMH was low. Frequent stockouts of ABHR and liquid soap, interrupted water supply, poor positioning of sinks, lack of HH reminders, staffing shortages, and high workload were barriers to HH compliance. The low HH compliance might impact patient safety and increase HAI and AMR burden. We recommend in-service IPC training, mentorship, and coaching programs for HCWs with a preference for medical doctors and student nurses. Additionally, prepositioning of HH supplies and workplace reminders could positively influence HH compliance rates. Also, the design and implementation of regular hand hygiene audits by the hospital’s IPC team would be beneficial to HCWs and patients.

Before data collection, permission was obtained from the senior hospital management, and ethical approval was secured from the Sierra Leone Ethics and Scientific Review Committee (SLESRC No: 011/01/2024). There was no need for informed consent as there was no direct interaction with the healthcare workers during the observation. For the brainstorming exercise, all the healthcare workers agreed to participate as part of the facility improvement initiatives.
