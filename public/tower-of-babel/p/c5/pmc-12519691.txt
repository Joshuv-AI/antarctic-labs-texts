Leishmaniases in Kenya: a scoping review subanalysis of vectors
Authors: Katherine O’Brien, Grace Grifferty, Hugh Shirley, Caroline Wanjiku, Abigail Binkley, Abigail L Williams, Philip Espinola Coombs, Morgan G Wong, Sophia Roe, Fiona Wallace, Kathleen Tschoepe, Adrienne M Orriols, Jason L Hirsch, Richard Wamai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesVector control is imperative for eliminating leishmaniasis as a public health problem in Kenya. As elimination efforts expand in East Africa, it is crucial to understand the current research landscape. To address that need and identify gaps, a scoping review was conducted to characterise the landscape of leishmaniasis vector research in Kenya.DesignBuilding on a previously published scoping review by this team, we updated database searches in PubMed, Embase via Embase.com, Web of Science Core Collection, the Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) and the Pan African Clinical Trials Registry to incorporate literature up to 4 January 2024 and focused on vector-related papers. Studies classified as ‘prevention’ in the original scoping review were included due to overlapping definitions.ResultsA total of 95 studies were included in the analysis. Although a wide range of sandfly species have been documented, most of the research is outdated, having taken place 20–40 years ago. Existing studies are mostly epidemiological with little focus on basic and clinical research. There are also no studies on post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis despite its potential contribution to the disease transmission cycle. The geographical scope of the research is largely limited to traditional transmission foci with little attention to new disease hotspots such as North Eastern Kenya.ConclusionsThese research gaps need to be addressed to better inform the country’s leishmaniasis prevention and vector control strategy.Keywords: Neglected Diseases, Review, Infection control

Introduction

The vast majority of the world’s population has been exposed to new and re-emerging vector-transmitted infectious diseases of the last few decades, precipitating the launch of the Global vector control response 2017–2030.1 Among these are neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) which include the leishmaniases, a group of vector-borne infections caused by a diverse range of Leishmania parasites and transmitted by over 90 species of female phlebotomine sandflies.2 The leishmaniases are endemic in 99 countries in the tropical regions, endangering the lives of 1.7 billion people and contributing significantly to disease burden in these regions.3,5 The spectrum of the leishmaniases includes four distinct clinical entities: visceral leishmaniasis (VL, also known as kala-azar), cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL) and post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis (PKDL).6 Over 2 70 000 cases of CL and almost 12 000 cases of VL were reported to the WHO in 2023, although these are likely underestimates as only 61% of CL endemic countries and 65% of endemic VL countries reported to the WHO in the most recent update.1,33 PKDL is an infectious sequela of treated VL that occurs in 5–60% of patients.7 The East African region, particularly Kenya, is considered an eco-epidemiological hotspot for VL, compounded by the endemicity of CL and occasional MCL and PKDL.3

As a growing country of 47 million people, Kenya is continuously impacted by the leishmaniases.8 Since 2020, nine counties have reported over 2000 cases of VL, including counties outside of the historical distribution of the disease in Kenya’s arid north and northeastern regions.9 Disease outbreaks highlight the necessity of ongoing control, diagnostic and therapeutic efforts, as VL is fatal if untreated.10 In 2022, Kenya had 1564 cases of VL and 90 cases of CL. There is some epidemiological overlap between VL and CL, including the Rift Valley region,10 and vector control strategies are similar across these disease entities.

However, elimination efforts are impeded by factors that include a complex parasite life cycle, multiplicity of parasite strains and variable disease manifestations that depend on the parasite species and host immune response and a diversity of sand fly species. Leishmania parasite requires two hosts: mammalian and insect vector (sand flies). In the sand fly, the parasite exists as a flagellated promastigote that migrates from the gut to the proboscis and is injected into the mammalian host during blood feeding.11 Once in the puncture wound, the promastigotes are phagocytised by macrophages and other mononuclear cells, where they transform into amastigotes (tissue stage of the parasite), multiply and infect other cells, causing reticuloendothelial dysfunction and disease.12 Following several rounds of replication, the host cell lyses, releasing the amastigotes into the bloodstream and infecting the next sandfly that takes a bloodmeal from the infected host. Presently, there are over 20 documented Leishmania parasite species.13 Circulating strains in Kenya include L. donovani, L. major, L. tropica and L. aethiopica10 13 and are linked to different clinical forms of leishmaniasis. For example, L. donovani is associated with VL, while L. tropica is associated with CL.12 Globally, Leishmania parasites are transmitted by about 90 sand fly species. In Kenya, the genus Phlebotomus constitutes the main vector group with P. duboscqi and P. guggisbergi implicated in the transmission of L. major and L. tropica, respectively, and P. pediffer, P. longipes and P. elgonensis as vectors of L. aethiopica.10 14 15

The sandflies primarily reside in termite hills, acacia trees and houses with mud-cracked walls.1116,21 In addition to humans, sandflies use several animal species as hosts and reservoirs, including various rat, hyrax and dog species in the wider East African region.22,24 While people with asymptomatic infection are not thought to contribute significantly as reservoirs, those with active infection or with PKDL can infect sandflies.25 26 Therefore, the sandfly, its environment and human and animal reservoirs are all crucial targets for disease control.

The elimination of leishmaniasis as a public health problem in East Africa is a critical priority for both the WHO and local governments, with vector control being a current focus outlined in the strategic framework for the elimination of visceral leishmaniasis as a public health problem.10 27 In Kenya, where the incidence of VL has been increasing and spreading beyond previously defined epidemiologic bounds,22 understanding its vectors is an important first step in designing research and intervention efforts. This project builds on a full scoping review of the leishmaniases in Kenya completed in 202228 and presents an updated subanalysis of vector-specific research in the country. Concurrently, this team conducted a subanalysis of diagnostics research in order to identify gaps and priorities in the diagnostics field, published separately. This review aims to provide a thorough overview of the literature published on vectors work in the leishmaniasis field. This is in an effort to aid research prioritisation efforts in the era of VL elimination in East Africa.

Methods

This scoping review is reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (online supplemental appendix 1).29 This scoping review replicates the methodology used by this team for the broader scoping review of the leishmaniases in Kenya, published in 2023.28 We replicated the search strategies, searching PubMed, Embase via Embase.com, Web of Science Core Collection, the Cochrane Library, ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP and the Pan African Clinical Trial Registry up to 4 January 2024. Search strings can be found in online supplemental appendix 2. Articles were eligible for inclusion if they contained an explicit and thorough discussion of leishmaniasis, concerned or took place in Kenya, and focused on vectors research. Full details of the replicated methodology can be found in the original publication.28 Dual blinded screening was conducted at both title/abstract and full text stages, with conflicts resolved by the first author. Data extraction was conducted in a dual-blinded fashion as well. The comprehensive codebook used in the previous scoping review was used for this study (online supplemental appendix 3), with additional data analysis focused on various vector-related factors: study aims, methodology, outcomes, study design (Appendix online supplemental appendix 4), setting (field or laboratory), sandfly species, bloodmeal analysis, reservoirs/hosts, Leishmania strain, county in Kenya, duration of study (in months), sample size and sample description. In the original review, interventions for vector control were classified under the ‘prevention’ theme. These articles were included in our analysis. Data analysis was descriptive and was done using Microsoft Excel V.16.88.

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in this study.

Results

Study characteristics

Of 1067 abstracts screened, 200 full texts were reviewed in the updated search, with only one additional vectors study included. Combined with the original scoping review, 93 studies were thematically classified as ‘vectors’ according to the data analysis codebook. One duplicate was found at a later stage and was removed. Additionally, three of the four articles classified originally as prevention were included because they discussed vector control. In total, 95 articles were included in this analysis (figure 1). Of these, 63 (66.3%) were epidemiological, 19 (20.0%) were basic science research, seven (7.4%) were clinical studies and six (6.3%) were secondary research. Of epidemiological studies, 39 (41.1% of all studies) were monitoring/surveillance studies, 15 (15.8%) were molecular epidemiology, seven (7.4%) were categorised as other, and two (2.1%) studies were method development studies. Of basic science research, nine studies (9.5%) were genomics/genetics/computational studies, seven (9.5%) were animal studies, two (2.1%) were categorised as other and one (1.1%) was a method development paper (figure 2).

Study categories and subcategories by number of studies.

Types of leishmaniases studied were evenly represented. 30 studies (31.6%) focused on VL, 29 (30.5%) on CL and 22 (23.2%) on both VL and CL. 13 studies (13.7%) did not specify type of leishmaniasis, and one study focused on multiple types of leishmaniases beyond a combination of VL/CL, including PKDL minimally. 13 Leishmania strains were represented in the studies, primarily L. donovani (n=19, 20.0%), L. major (n = 18, 18.9%) and L. tropica (n=12, 12.6%). Most studies took place in both field and laboratory settings (n=49, 51.6%), followed by field only (n=21, 22.1%) and laboratory only (n=19, 20.0%). The average study duration was 13.2 months (range of 0–54 months), and the average sandfly sample size was 4337 (range of 1 to 45 949). The largest contributors to vector research were the United Nations Development Programme/World Bank/WHO Special Programme for Research and Training in Tropical Diseases with 12 studies (12.6%), followed by the International Centre of Insect Physiology and Ecology (n=11, 11.6%). The average number of citations was 44 (range of 0–1240).

Sandflies examined

The most frequently studied sandfly species belonged to two genera: Sergentomyia and Phlebotomus. The Sergentomyia group consisted of S. schwetzi (n=39, 41.1%), S. antennatus (n=39, 41.1%) and S. bedfordi (n=36, 37.9%), whereas P. martini (n=31, 32.6%), P. duboscqi (n=23, 24.2%) and P. rodhaini (n=11, 11.6%) were the most frequently studied in the Phlebotomus group. Most studies examined more than one sandfly species. The full breakdown of sandfly species can be found in figure 3. 50 unique species were mentioned at least once. Study locations

If a study took place in more than two counties, it was classified as ‘various.’ The most frequent study location was Baringo County (n=27, 28.4%), followed by Bungoma County (n=8, 8.4%) and Kitui County (n=7, 7.4%). Seven (7.4%) studies took place in various counties. The distribution of studies can be seen in figure 4.

Studies by year published

Most studies (n=28, 29.5%) were published between 1980 and 1989, followed by 1990–1999 (n=24, 25.2%). Research on vectors for leishmaniasis in Kenya steadily increased from 1950 to 1979, then rapidly increased in 1980–1989, followed by a slight decrease in 1990–1999, and finally a significant slowdown since 2000 (figure 5).

Distribution of studies by year published.

Research in each county in Kenya followed this similar chronological trend, and no specific county has been the focus of recent research. Generally, the timeline of the vector research field mirrors the overarching leishmaniasis research field in Kenya. The original Kenya scoping review found that most papers were published between 1980 and 1999 and identified an increase during 2010–2014 (29); however, these trends were not observed when examining vector work.

Study designs

Studies were classified as descriptive, observational or interventional using a defined system (online supplemental appendix 4). Most studies were observational (n=68, 71.5%). Within observational studies, almost all (n=64, 94.1% of observational studies) were cross-sectional. Only one cohort study and three ecological studies were found. 17 (17.9%) studies were interventional, with 15 (88.2% of interventional studies) being non-randomised trials. Only one randomised controlled trial was identified. Finally, four studies were descriptive (4.3%; all case reports) and six (6.5%) were secondary research. This can be seen in figure 6.

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to characterise the leishmaniasis vectors research landscape and determine existing gaps. 95 studies were included in the analysis. Most research on vectors occurred 20–40 years ago, likely due to constrained financing and technical capacity, competing public health goals and geographical challenges that render most leishmaniasis foci difficult to reach and logistically demanding for research.10 Given the role of vector research in the regional and national elimination and control strategies10 27 and the evolving nature of vector-ecosystem interactions, especially in the context of climate change,1 31 32 more up-to-date research on the vectors of leishmaniases would be useful. Climate change has been shown to impact VL through changes in temperature and rainfall patterns,33 with potential impacts on vector distribution. Other important environmental correlates include vegetation, a resource that is central to the survival of adult male and female sand flies and some of their reservoir hosts.4 34 Updated vector research is needed to reflect the changing environment.

Overall, the number of epidemiological studies outnumbered vector-related basic science and clinical studies. This is expected given the cost-effectiveness and immediate relevance of epidemiological data to public health decision making. Existing vector-related basic science focused on sand fly infection biology35 36 and behaviour, primarily plant feeding habits.34 Understanding infection biology is essential in elucidating transmission mechanisms and formulating targeted therapies including and not limited to transmission blocking vaccines. Research on plant feeding offers insights into the olfactory system of sand flies and provides unique ways to leverage plants from which they obtain their sugar meals. Other areas of vector research that could contribute to elimination efforts include sand fly bionomics, focusing on species compositions and distribution and key behavioural traits (oviposition, mating and blood feeding) as well as infection with Leishmania parasites, profiling of insecticide resistance and the identification of novel formulations to address resistant populations. Understanding the chemical and molecular underpinnings of vector-parasite and vector-host associations is essential, including interactions with animal reservoir hosts. Research on sandfly bionomics is in part hampered by methodological complexities relating to species identification and infection status determination.37 38 However, innovative laboratory platforms such as matrix assisted laser desorption ionisation-time of flight mass spectrometry, a protein profiling spectral technology, slowly gaining traction for rapid, high-throughput, multidetection analysis of arthropods, including sand flies, and could revolutionise the field.39 40

While few clinical research studies were identified, that category does not share significant overlap with vectors research, so it should not be considered a gap. Many of the studies at this intersection focused on prevention for vector control, likely because the original definition of clinical study included social interventions to improve health outcomes. No studies examined PKDL specifically, and only one study mentioned PKDL. PKDL is a complex piece of the leishmaniasis transmission cycle. Greater attention is needed towards understanding its transmission by sandflies given its prevalence in Kenya and East Africa at large.3 41

The spatial focus of the studies represents another gap (figure 4). While the major transmission areas have been studied, recent research indicates that transmission may be shifting in Kenya. A 2022 study by Mewara and Gadisa9 identified several outbreaks of leishmaniasis between January 2020 and November 2022 in the counties of Wajir, Garissa, Mandera, Isiolo, Marsabit, Kitui, Tharaka-Nithi, Baringo and West Pokot. Additionally, cases reported to the WHO from 2017 to 2023 primarily originated from northeastern and northwestern Kenya,41 where limited research on vectors has been done (figure 7).

Comparison of vector research distribution with reported cases. (left) Distribution of vector research for leishmaniasis in Kenya (figure 4). (right) Cases reported to the WHO from 2017 to 2023, as identified in the recent Weekly Epidemiological Record report on elimination of VL in Kenya.41

Research on sandfly vectors has not been conducted where these recent outbreaks and cases have occurred, especially from northeastern counties of Mandera, Wajir and Garissa, which presents a gap in research. With the influence of climate change, human population movement due to armed conflict and/or in search of land for agriculture, water and pasture, transmission zones could expand beyond traditional foci, and vector research should reflect this trend.22 33 42

Most of the studies published were cross-sectional. While this study did not perform a quality review, the design of the studies can give some indication of the level of quality. Cross-sectional studies have several drawbacks, including difficulties establishing causality.43 Although randomised-controlled trials are considered the gold standard in research,44 only one was found in this scoping review. Studies focused on interventions were non-randomised trials. Only a few interventional studies were found, identifying a need for research with more robust study designs.

One area of strength in the literature includes the number of sandfly species covered in the collection of studies. There are about 90 sandfly species that transmit leishmaniasis, and over 50 species were discussed throughout the literature examined. However, despite it being the most examined area of leishmaniasis research, as described previously, many gaps remain.10 27

Recommendations

In view of the existing gaps, we recommend conducting vector-focused research, employing appropriate study designs, in counties with recent leishmaniasis outbreaks. This will help discern the drivers of transmission and triggers for recent outbreaks, helping inform prevention and vector control efforts and will help support the WHO’s strategy for integrated vector management and vector surveillance outlined in the recent strategic framework.27 Research should also include PKDL as an important stage of transmission with patients acting as potential reservoirs.

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations. During the full-text screen, articles were excluded if they did not explicitly and thoroughly discuss leishmaniasis in Kenya, which is ultimately subjective. This is minimised by a dual, blind review, and our requirement for leishmaniasis in Kenya to be mentioned at least twice, except for secondary research. Additionally, some classification bias exists in the study, primarily regarding the application of labels for study design. The authors defined the categories prior to classification, and two reviewers applied these labels to each study, with a third reviewer resolving conflicts. Another limitation was that Leishmania species were used as a proxy for type of leishmaniasis if it was not explicitly provided. Finally, we excluded non-English publications, which could have been relevant literature.

Strengths

This is the first study to examine vector research specifically for leishmaniases in Kenya. This subfield is of increasing importance as climate change affects the distribution of disease. Based on our comprehensive screening strategy, we are confident we identified the full body of literature on vectors in Kenya. Additionally, the dual, blind methodology used in the abstract, full-text and data extraction phases helped ensure that labels were applied correctly.

Conclusion

Characterising research and identifying gaps on leishmaniasis vectors is crucial because it allows for the conceptualisation and design of interventions and prevention efforts. Here, we have highlighted key gaps which, if addressed, could contribute to improved vector control and disruption of transmission cycles for disease elimination.
