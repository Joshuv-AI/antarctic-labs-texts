Systemic corticosteroids for severe community-acquired pneumonia: a narrative review of mechanisms and critical appraisal of recent trials in ICU
Authors: Benjamin Eastwood, Isis Terrington, Rebecca Young, Olivia Cox, Elizabeth Webb, Cathrine McKenzie, Kordo Saeed, Andrew Conway-Morris, Michael P.W. Grocott, Ahilanandan Dushianthan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinical trials investigating the effect of systemic corticosteroids as an adjunctive therapy for severe community-acquired pneumonia (sCAP) requiring admission to the intensive care unit have produced heterogeneous results over recent decades. The most recent trials, despite being significantly larger than their predecessors, have displayed similarly heterogeneous results. This narrative review aims to explore the possible underlying reasons for this heterogeneity of treatment effect, providing detail for each of the proposed immune- and nonimmune-mediated mechanisms through which corticosteroids might offer therapeutic benefit for patients with severe pneumonia and providing an in-depth critical appraisal of the most recent large-scale multicentre randomised controlled trials, namely ESCAPe (2022), CAPE COD (2023) and REMAP-CAP (2025). We discuss the importance of recognising sCAP as a heterogeneous disease, in which local processes in the lung are not always reflected in systemic features, and the need for further characterisation of endotypes of the disease.

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) can be defined as symptoms of a lower respiratory tract illness with associated focal signs on chest examination and systemic features of infection acquired outside of a hospital setting [1, 2]. Severe CAP (sCAP) is a subset where the management often requires admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) or high-dependency unit (HDU) for advanced respiratory and/or cardiovascular support. Infectious Disease Society of America (IDSA)/American Thoracic Society (ATS) consensus guidelines provide specific diagnostic criteria detailing multiple variables for sCAP diagnosis [3]. Illness severity scores such as the Pneumonia Severity Index or CURB-65 (C denotes new-onset confusion, U denotes urea >7 mmol·L−1, R denotes respiratory rate ≥30 breaths·min–1, B denotes blood pressure with systolic <90 mmHg or diastolic ≤60 mmHg and 65 denotes age ≥65 years) score are also used to classify disease severity and estimate mortality risk [4–6]. Comparison and synthesis of corticosteroids trials in sCAP is challenging due to these inconsistent definitions of “severity” across studies as discussed in detail in the literature [7]. Recognising that no single definition is applied uniformly across the literature, we adopted ICU/HDU admission as the primary operational definition of sCAP in this narrative review and detailed studies that have used or meet established severity criteria (e.g. IDSA/ATS). This combined approach allowed us to retain clinically rigorous trials while facilitating cross-trial comparison.

CAP is the leading communicable cause of death worldwide [8]. In the UK, incidence estimates range between 2.81 and 21.81 cases per 1000 person-years depending on methodology and risk increases with advanced age and male sex [9, 10]. sCAP is the leading cause (60%) of sepsis-related ICU admissions [11]. Around 10% of patients hospitalised with CAP will require ICU admission, with those aged ≥65 years being most susceptible [12, 13]. A British Thoracic Society audit of adult CAP reported that around 25% (n=2291) of hospital admissions with CAP were classified as “severe” according to the CURB-65 criteria, whilst 5.2% of all cases were admitted to ICU [14]. ICU sCAP patients have a higher mortality, typically 30–50% [15–24], although for the population recruited into clinical trials 90-day mortality is closer to 20% [25]. Poor functional status at baseline has been shown to be a key predictor of sCAP mortality with a significant proportion of hospital deaths occurring in patients over 70 years and those with high frailty scores [26, 27].

For sCAP, other than COVID-19 pneumonia, there are no specific treatment options available beyond standard antibiotics and supportive care. Corticosteroids as an adjunct for sCAP have been investigated over the past three decades. Most recently, dexamethasone was associated with a substantial survival benefit in hospitalised patients with severe COVID-19 requiring supplemental oxygen [28]. This was surprising as previous observational studies of corticosteroids in other severe viral infections (e.g. influenza) found unfavourable outcomes [29–32]. Recently, three large multicentre trials investigated the use of corticosteroids in patients with sCAP and demonstrated contrasting results [33–35]. In this review, we provide an overview of the pathological processes related to sCAP and discuss the potential mechanisms that may explain the efficacy of corticosteroids beyond the standard “hyperinflammatory” hypothesis, where excessive dysregulated host inflammatory response can lead to adverse outcomes. We also critically appraise the most recent large clinical trials and explore the latest mechanistic perspectives including microbiome diversity and pathogen variance, nonimmunological treatment effects and immunological endotypes (a sub-phenotype with a specific identifiable pathophysiological mechanism [36]). This aims to offer a unique perspective on the reasons behind the heterogeneity of treatment effect (HTE) observed in this population which may help explain the conflicting results.

Pathophysiology of sCAP

Pneumonia develops when invasive pathogens damage the distal airways and alveolar spaces, triggering an acute inflammatory response. In health, silent immunological surveillance of the lung microbiome is facilitated by resident macrophages and dendritic cells; but, if the capacity of these cells is exceeded, a sequence of signalling events occurs, triggering inflammatory pathways [37, 38]. Early “alarm” cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1β are released following activation of the inflammasome, mediating de novo synthesis and release of downstream cytokines such as IL-6 and chemokines including C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL)-8 [39, 40]. These provoke both local and systemic responses, inducing migration of circulating immune cells, most notably neutrophils. Neutrophil recruitment is aided by monocyte ingress [41] and often an increase in circulating numbers is observed (“neutrophilia”). If these are insufficient to resolve the inflammatory stimulus, then factors such as IL-6 and granulocyte colony stimulating factor drive emergency granulopoiesis, switching the bone marrow to neutrophil production and reducing output of lymphocytes, monocytes and other granulocytes such as eosinophils. This produces the characteristic “left shift” seen in severe inflammation, reflecting the release of immature, less effective neutrophils, as well as lymphopenia, monocytopenia and eosinopenia [42].

When inflammatory cells infiltrate the airspace and become activated, they release a range of cytokines and chemokines that sustain inflammation [41] driving further recruitment of immune cells and setting up self-reinforcing cycles. Neutrophils degranulate releasing a range of lytic enzymes [43] which, together with oxidants and extruded nuclear material (neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)), damage the delicate alveolar barrier and allow leakage of plasma across the barrier. This impairs gas exchange, producing the characteristic hypoxia and breathlessness of severe pneumonia. Changes to the lung surfactant composition and function occur early in the disease process and contribute to respiratory failure [44]. Platelet activation, damage to the endothelial glycocalyx, NETosis, endotoxin effects and inflammatory cytokine milieu conspire to cause capillary microthrombosis in the lung. These further affect gas exchange and extend to extrapulmonary tissues beds contributing to multiorgan dysfunction [45, 46]. These effects are summarised in table 1.

Mechanisms of corticosteroids in sCAP

Corticosteroids have a broad range of mechanisms of action, encompassing both genomic (transcriptional) and nongenomic (direct pathway modulation) effects. Although commonly considered “anti-inflammatory”, many of the therapeutic and indeed adverse effects of corticosteroids arise from nonimmune mechanisms. In this section, we explore the effects of corticosteroids further beyond the established anti-inflammatory properties (figure 1).

Corticosteroids as immunomodulators

The dominance of immunopathology in driving CAP severity has led to repeated attempts at immunomodulation to improve outcomes. Corticosteroids have the strongest track record in this area, although the evidence for benefit has remained inconsistent, and is likely confined to only some endotypes. Indeed, one recent individual patient data meta-analysis identified C-reactive protein (CRP) >204 mg·L−1 predicted a favourable mortality profile when treated with systemic corticosteroids [47]. Corticosteroids with glucocorticoid effects have a wide range of immune functions, which can be divided into genomic and nongenomic mechanisms.

Genomic or transcriptional mechanisms affect disease processes through alteration of gene expression. Glucocorticoids bind to glucocorticoid receptors which translocate to the nucleus and directly suppress activity of key immune transcription factors, including NF-κβ and activator protein 1 complexes, resulting in reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-8 [48–50]. Indirect suppression also occurs, whereby glucocorticoids increase the transcription of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1 receptor antagonist, IL-10 and NF-κβ inhibitor [51, 52]. Circulating neutrophil counts are initially increased by corticosteroids with the return of “marginated” neutrophils loosely adherent to the endothelium and mobilisation from the bone marrow through altered integrin expression, although infiltration into target tissues is simultaneously curbed [53–55].

Nongenomic or direct immunological mechanisms of corticosteroids alter cellular functions independent of transcription. Vasomotor tone is modulated in a context-specific manner mediated by direct action on endothelial nitric oxide synthase counter-balanced by transcriptional mechanisms. Overall, this improves haemodynamics and has downstream immunomodulatory effects [7, 56–58]. Corticosteroids inhibit the synthesis of prostaglandins (pro-inflammatory mediators) by altering phospholipid metabolism to restrict the lipid precursor [59]. Corticosteroids also directly reduce the pulmonary inflammatory response by rewiring metabolism in resident macrophages to suppress cytokine release [60]. Subsequent recruitment of neutrophils into the lung is tempered and excessive damage from immune cells is limited by reducing activation and degranulation [55].

Whilst neutrophils are considered critical to the pathophysiology of pneumonia and acute lung injury [61], it is also recognised that acute lung injury develops in patients that lack circulating neutrophils (i.e. neutropenia arising from bone marrow failure) [62] and that pneumonia consists of distinct patterns of alveolar inflammation including non-neutrophil driven endotypes [63]. Distinct patterns of alveolar inflammation, driven by divergent immunological mechanisms, suggest that different immunomodulatory therapies may be required for different endotypes [63]. Future design of clinical trials based on precision medicine will require an understanding of the detailed mechanisms that contribute to individual endotypes and their responsiveness to corticosteroids.

Corticosteroids modulate oxidative stress and alveolar hyperoxia

Pneumonia causes increased oxidative stress and depletes redox storage [64]. Reactive oxygen and nitrogen species generated by activated inflammatory cells contribute to alveolar epithelial injury through lipid peroxidation, protein nitration and mitochondrial dysfunction [65]. The heightened oxidative stress is worsened by the presence of a higher partial pressure of inspired oxygen often required to mitigate systemic hypoxaemia [66]. Generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) also indirectly leads to depletion of intrinsic antioxidant reserves in epithelial cells [66, 67]. Corticosteroids can potentially attenuate the oxidative stress burden through various mechanisms.

Corticosteroids may increase the ability of the lung tissue to mitigate cellular damage in the presence of ROS. The antioxidant capacity measured by the ferric reducing ability of plasma and glutathione/dimer (GSH/GSSG) ratio is significantly reduced in CAP [64]. Acute corticosteroid administration increases antioxidant capacity in rat lungs and therefore may help restore this antioxidant buffer [68]. Markers of free radical damage by ROS such as serum 8-isoprostane (8-isoPGF2a) are correlated in humans with severity of pneumonia and mortality and corticosteroids have been consistently shown to reduce airway 8-isoprostane levels in asthma [69–71]. Moreover, in a rat oleic acid lung injury model, dexamethasone was shown to be effective in the upregulation of antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase and GSH levels, suggesting improved antioxidant capacity [72]. Despite these clinical and preclinical studies, direct human clinical confirmation in sCAP is lacking and future trials should consider this as a potential mechanistic plausibility beyond inflammation.

Corticosteroids may hasten injury recovery and the repair of alveolar epithelium

Alveolar type II (AT-II) pneumocytes are implicated in tissue repair following an acute lung injury such as sCAP [73]. Damaged alveolar type I pneumocytes are repopulated by stem-cell-like actions of the AT-II cells in adults, especially during acute lung injury [74]. In rat models of acute lung injury, dexamethasone protects the population of AT-II cells by reducing rates of apoptosis through a reduction in intracellular free Ca2+, serum TNF-α and intracellular apoptosis mediators Bax and caspase-8 [75, 76]. The presence of higher concentrations of cultured alveolar epithelial cells (A549) is protective against the hyperpermeability of pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells seen in sepsis [77]. In rat alveolar epithelial cells, corticosteroids can also restore TNF-α-mediated alveolar epithelial barrier dysfunction [78]. However, the effect on airway epithelium is contextual, where corticosteroids can also induce epithelial cell apoptosis, hindering recovery. In vitro models have shown that corticosteroids can delay epithelial migration and wound repair if given prior to injury, but not after [79, 80]. This is noteworthy as timing of corticosteroids administration in lung injury produces heterogeneous effects. In clinical trials, the timing of initiation varies widely with different intent; early to limit the initial inflammatory response [33] or later to regulate myofibroblast activation, extracellular matrix deposition and attenuate ongoing low-grade inflammation [34, 73, 81]. This requires further evaluation in clinical settings.

Corticosteroids may improve lung surfactant synthesis

Surfactant deficiency with compositional and functional abnormalities are common in sCAP leading to premature alveolar collapse, worsening hypoxaemia [44, 82, 83]. Lung surfactant is a complex mixture of lipoproteins that are essential for surface tension reduction at the air–liquid interface. While primary surfactant deficiency seen in premature neonates responds to surfactant replacement [84], several animal models of prematurity suggest that prenatal corticosteroids can enhance surfactant synthesis [85–87]. Moreover, isotope labelling studies of isolated AT-II cells and animal explants suggest corticosteroids can also directly stimulate surfactant synthesis in the fetal lung [88, 89]. Similarly, corticosteroid treatment can augment surfactant protein expression and synthesis of surfactant proteins such as SP-A, SP-B, SP-C and SP-D in premature lungs [90–92]. However, while lung models of prematurity point to corticosteroids augmenting surfactant synthesis, specific studies investigating the direct effect of corticosteroids on lung surfactant synthesis in injured adult lungs are lacking. Further studies are needed to evaluate the impact of corticosteroids on surfactant metabolism in injured lungs in adult humans.

Corticosteroids enhance alveolar fluid clearance

During severe pneumonia, patients develop alveolar epithelial and endothelial injury, predisposing them to nonhydrostatic pulmonary oedema [93]. Proteinaceous plasma translocates across the alveolar interstitium worsening lung injury and impairing gas exchange. Alveolar fluid clearance (AFC) is impeded due to damage to the alveolar epithelium and failure of the tight junctions between damaged epithelial cells allows for further fluid influx [94, 95]. The ability to concentrate and clear this excess fluid is associated with improved clinical outcomes [94]. Both glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids increase expression of the apical epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) in the alveolar epithelium as well as the basolateral Na+/K+ ATPase [96, 97]. These enable clearance of fluid by facilitated or active sodium transport into the interstitium, where fluid follows and is cleared by the circulation. The function of these channels is directly impeded by local inflammatory milieu, alveolar hyperoxia, hypoxia and mechanical ventilation [98, 99]. In animal models, corticosteroids improve AFC and pulmonary oedema through upregulation of ENaC and Na+/K+ ATPase expression [100–102]. However, detailed studies investigating the effect of corticosteroids on AFC in human lung injury are lacking.

The corticosteroid trials in sCAP

Although there have been several trials of corticosteroids in hospitalised patients with CAP, these studies vary with regards to the severity of patients included in their population [103, 104]. As a result, confidence in the effect of systemic corticosteroids on mortality of sCAP patients has previously been limited due to small sample sizes. Recently, three large trials each involving more than 500 patients in the ICU or HDU setting have been published, namely CAPE COD (n=800) [33], ESCAPe (n=584) [34] and REMAP-CAP (n=658) [35]. While only the ESCAPe trial explicitly used the ATS/IDSA sCAP criteria, CAPE-COD and REMAP-CAP were conducted in the ICU setting. As ATS/IDSA defines sCAP by the presence of at least one major criterion (requirement for mechanical ventilation or vasopressors for septic shock) and many patients in these trials required such organ support, most participants would likely meet ATS/IDSA definitions of sCAP despite the criteria not being explicitly used. Therefore, this narrative review will focus on comparing these most recent large randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to allow detailed appraisal.

The ESCAPe trial mostly consisted of military veteran males (95%). The CAPE COD and REMAP-CAP male proportions were 69% and 60.8%, respectively. A subgroup analysis of the CAPE COD study signals towards greater benefit in females (risk difference −4 (CI −8.7–0.8) in favour of hydrocortisone for males and −9.5 (CI −16.7–−2.3) in favour of hydrocortisone for females). Although such subgroup analyses are not adjusted for multiplicity, sex-specific differences in corticosteroid treatment outcomes are noted in the ADRENAL study (septic shock) and others [105, 106]. This may partly explain the ESCAPe study finding.

CAPE COD and ESCAPe were both underpowered, with CAPE COD recruiting 800 (powered at 1146 to detect 25% relative risk reduction estimating 27% control group mortality) and ESCAPe recruiting 584 (powered at 1420 to detect 7% absolute risk reduction estimating 28% control group mortality). Underpowered trials can lead to inaccurate and imprecise results due to over- or under-capturing the event of significance [107]. Although both reports provide a reasonable rationale as to why this occurred (COVID pandemic and difficulty recruiting) fewer than pre-defined numbers were recruited. For context, the ADRENAL trial of hydrocortisone in septic shock would have shown a statistically significant (p=0.03) mortality benefit had it been stopped after recruiting 1000 patients [108]. This is echoed in the published interim analysis after 950 patients (p=0.08) [109]. The effect was not observed after recruiting the full cohort of 3800 patients [109]. Through this lens, smaller trials could also be capturing variance rather than providing an accurate effect estimate. Similarly, the stopping rule for REMAP-CAP may have been prematurely triggered, especially in view of the low control-group mortality (see the “Comparator mortality” section).

There were differences in baseline CRP across the studies. Smit et al. [47]. used individual patient data meta-analysis to predict and verify that baseline CRP >204 mg·L−1 was associated with improved 30-day mortality including the ESCAPe and CAPE COD cohorts (OR 0.71, 95% CI 0.5–0.99). A higher proportion (58.6%) of the CAPE COD trial participants exceeded this threshold than the ESCAPe trial (29.4%), which may represent enrichment of the CAPE COD cohort with a steroid-responsive sCAP endotype and help explain the difference in outcomes between these trials. Baseline CRP was not reported for the REMAP-CAP study. Although the CRP-based HTE was assessed and externally validated using data from large RCTs, we advocate for further validation through large-scale prospective trials using longer-term mortality outcomes before using CRP >204 mg·L−1 as a treatment rule for systemic corticosteroids in sCAP. The authors themselves note that the optimal cut-off may lie within a broader range, and timing and certain comorbidities may affect CRP measurement and therefore treatment decisions, so interpretation in the clinical context remains important even if this treatment rule were corroborated by further evidence. A broader understanding of the mechanisms and markers of steroid-responsive sCAP are required to aid clinician judgement. Longer-term mortality outcomes should be sought, particularly in view of the significantly increased risk of re-hospitalisation in the corticosteroid group (OR 1.95, 95% CI 1.24–3.07).

CAPE COD excluded patients with vasopressor-dependent septic shock at randomisation, whereas ESCAPe only excluded multiple or higher-dose vasopressors. REMAP-CAP and ESCAPe not only included shock but also included CAP with cardiovascular shock alone as a severity criterion. Systemic corticosteroids are only conditionally recommended in refractory septic shock [110]. For benefit to be found in the CAPE COD trial (the only study with shock excluded) is therefore somewhat surprising and suggests that effects of corticosteroids in sCAP may extend beyond mechanisms related to septic shock such as vasopressor sensitivity.

CAPE COD and REMAP-CAP excluded healthcare-associated pneumonia (HCAP), whereas 34% of ESCAPe patients met HCAP criteria. The pathophysiology, microbiome effects and causative organisms of HCAP differ from CAP and the response to corticosteroid therapy may be altered (see the “Variations in pathological virulence and lung microbiome in sCAP” section). Evidence specifically investigating corticosteroid use in HCAP is lacking. Influenza pneumonia was excluded from CAPE COD but comprised 5–10% of the REMAP-CAP and ESCAPe cohorts.

Studies have also included patients with airways disease (asthma or COPD). A significant proportion of CAPE COD patients had pre-existing airways disease (27% intervention; 31% control) [33], as did ESCAPe (COPD: 43% intervention; 47% control) with many using anti-inflammatory medications at baseline (81% intervention; 77% control) [34]. REMAP-CAP included 34–35% patients with “respiratory disease” at baseline [35]. Corticosteroids are often recommended for hospitalised patients with acute infective exacerbations of airways disease [111, 112]. Inclusion of such patients with pre-existing airways disease who might benefit from systemic corticosteroids during their infective exacerbation with sCAP raises methodological concerns. The clinical validity of the findings may be compromised due to varying degrees of airway inflammation and different responses to steroids in this group. This variation could lead to inflated effect sizes, underestimation of harm or diluted treatment effects, highlighting the need for stratification or subgroup analysis to ensure clinical validity.

Intervention

Although initial corticosteroid doses were balanced in terms of daily prednisolone equivalents (50 mg·day−1) [113], the interventions varied in duration and were not identical for several other reasons. Firstly, the type of corticosteroid varied (CAPE COD and REMAP-CAP: hydrocortisone; ESCAPe: methylprednisolone). Hydrocortisone has both mineralocorticoid and glucocorticoid activity, whereas methylprednisolone is predominantly a glucocorticoid [113]. Trials in septic shock suggest that hydrocortisone both alone and in combination with fludrocortisone can lead to faster shock reversal [109, 114, 115]. However, it is unclear if the positive effects seen in sepsis trials are primarily due to its mineralocorticoid or glucocorticoid properties. Recent meta-analyses [116, 117] suggest that the mortality benefit from corticosteroids in sCAP is almost entirely driven by hydrocortisone rather than others with less mineralocorticoid action, although these were published before the publication of REMAP-CAP trial, which may have influenced their meta-analysis. Conversely, an earlier meta-analysis prior to CAPE COD found the opposite result (i.e. “not hydrocortisone” drove the mortality benefit), demonstrating the significant impact of these large studies [118].

Secondly, the timing of initiation differed. REMAP-CAP and CAPE COD required first administration within 24 h, whereas ESCAPe allowed 72–96 h after admission, although 65% started within 48 h. While different timing of corticosteroid initiation may produce heterogeneous effects as pathological processes change throughout the disease, early administration may confer potential benefits. Defining “early” versus “late” intervention is challenging as the precise time of disease onset is rarely known and clinical timelines such as hospital admission, ICU admission and randomisation do not necessarily reflect the underlying biological stage. However, a recent individual patient data meta-analysis finding that the administration of corticosteroids within 24 h of hospital admission was associated with significantly greater benefit (and that initiation after 48 h may attenuate benefit or signal potential harm) provides supportive evidence for early administration, which may have implications in designing future clinical trials [47]. Importantly, this analysis was based solely on the CAPE COD study data because it was the only trial which recorded precise time in hours between hospital admission and treatment initiation.

Thirdly, the mode of delivery and the duration of treatment differed. Both ESCAPe and CAPE COD delivered the corticosteroid by continuous infusion, whereas REMAP-CAP used intermittent infusions every 6 h (see the “Variations in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of corticosteroids” section). The ESCAPe protocol was 7 days methylprednisolone with an additional 13-day taper (total duration of 20 days). The actual median dose was not reported. The REMAP-CAP protocol was 7 days hydrocortisone without taper and cessation was at hospital discharge. The actual median dose given was 5 days (interquartile range (IQR) 3–8). The CAPE COD protocol was 4 days hydrocortisone with tapering for an additional 4 or 10 days (total duration of 8 or 14 days) and cessation at ICU discharge. The actual median dose given was 7 days (IQR 4–8) (table 3).

An adaptive treatment duration strategy was used in the CAPE COD trial. The decision to shorten therapy from 14 to 8 days was guided by pre-specified criteria assessed on day 4, as follows: 1) spontaneous breathing; 2) partial pressure of arterial oxygen/fraction of inspired oxygen ratio >200 mmHg; 3) Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score on day 4 ≤SOFA score on day 1; and 4) a high clinician-estimated probability of ICU discharge by day 14 (figure S1 of [33]). Of note, fewer than 25% of patients in the trial received more than 8 days of corticosteroids and over 75% stopped prematurely, primarily due to ICU discharge.

Compliance also varied across the studies. Of the 297 patients in the ESCAPe intervention group, 51 (17%) did not receive more than 6 days of the intervention in the first 20 days. Noncompliance in the placebo group was similar. A per-protocol analysis demonstrated that excluding these patients reduced the mortality of each group (16% to 15% intervention versus 18% to 16% control). This may reflect that more unwell or complex patients are those in whom compliance is an issue due to conflicting factors.

Finally, there was crossover, with 23% (28/122) of the REMAP-CAP comparator group receiving corticosteroids for a median of 4 days (IQR 2–5.3) and 7.7% (23/395) of the CAPE COD comparator group having an indication for open-label corticosteroid, which could reduce the effect size.

Comparator mortality

The baseline mortality of the control groups differs significantly between the studies (figure 2) and these were much lower than the real-world expected mortality rates for sCAP [15–21]. In large observational studies, 28-day sCAP mortality is ∼25% and can range from 30–50% when including longer-term outcomes [23, 24]. Within interventional trials of sCAP, control group 90-day mortality is estimated at 19.3%, as illustrated in figure 2 [25]. These trials do not always capture the most unwell patients due to limitations in the specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. This may be due to not recruiting or excluding patients with a higher mortality risk, such as those who have a do-not-intubate order (CAPE COD), and nursing/care home residents (REMAP-CAP), but also who would be inappropriate to recruit due to treatment futility. A “trial effect” [119, 120] favouring patients who have been enrolled in a clinical trial is debated and seems unlikely to affect care in an ICU setting, but should be considered.

The control group of the REMAP-CAP trial is a particular outlier due to its small size (n=122) with a low 90-day mortality of 9.8% overall (13% with vasopressor-dependent shock). One possible explanation is that this group may have been less systemically compromised – the median Extended Cardiovascular (EC)-SOFA score was 1 (IQR 0–3) compared to the intervention group medians of 3 and 2 (IQR 0–3), respectively, for the whole cohort and subset where both intervention and control were offered [35]. By contrast, the CAPE COD median SOFA scores were 4 (IQR 3–6) for both groups and the ESCAPe mean±sd SOFA scores were 6.68±3 and 6.29±2.85 for intervention and control, respectively [33, 34]. Given the low scores for both REMAP-CAP groups, it is possible that these values are on the scale of 0–4 for the cardiovascular system only. Even if this is the case, it may represent a significantly higher risk of mortality in the intervention group, as small changes in SOFA score predict dramatically different mortality. For example, a mean SOFA score of 1.1–2.0 predicts a mortality risk of 5%, compared to 2.1–3.0 predicting a risk nearer to 20% [121]. The EC-SOFA score is an adaptation of the original SOFA score incorporating broader markers of cardiovascular dysfunction and predicts mortality more accurately [122].

Underlying causes for HTE

Such stark differences in outcome between trials is likely to reflect the underlying clinical heterogeneity of the treatment and disease process. There are a multitude of contributory factors which have not been fully explored above, and these can be divided into host factors, pathogen factors and treatment factors. While it is not an exhaustive list, we have provided a few patient variations and treatment variations as potential candidates contributing to this heterogeneity. Moreover, inclusion of patients with severe respiratory failure from corticosteroid-responsive sCAP mimics into clinical trials can introduce clinical heterogeneity and distort the estimated treatment effect.

In which pneumonias are corticosteroids effective?

Beyond COVID-19, there are various inflammatory pneumonias that are known to be responsive to corticosteroids. These include organising pneumonia (which can be secondary to viral or bacterial infections), eosinophilic pneumonia (acute or chronic), acute or subacute hypersensitivity pneumonitis, other idiopathic pneumonias such nonspecific interstitial pneumonia, pulmonary vasculitis, drug-induced pneumonitis and Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia in the context of immunocompromised hosts secondary to HIV/AIDS (table S1). Some patients admitted to ICU with a clinical diagnosis of sCAP may in fact have one of these mimics. Despite their comparative rarity, the population admitted to ICU with respiratory failure refractory to usual treatment is likely to be enriched with these. Assessment should include reviews of occupational and environmental exposures, recent prescribed and over-the-counter medications, and an analysis of the onset and progression of symptoms. Additionally, investigations such as bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) analysis, differential cell count, autoimmune panel and high-resolution computed tomography scans may be necessary. The inadvertent inclusion of these conditions to varying extents likely contributes to the HTE seen in clinical trials.

Patient factors

Variations in systemic and local host immune response: inflammatory endotypes

One likely source of clinical heterogeneity between patients is variations in the degree and nature of the host response to the infection. Shapiro et al. [123] refute the widely held paradigm that sepsis is simply a result of hyperinflammation based on extensive investigations into anti-inflammatory interventions not yielding benefit, failure to reproduce clinical sepsis with TNF-α administration even in levels far exceeding natural sepsis and poor characterisation of the “hyperinflammatory” cytokine profile [124].

While resources are heavily invested in exploring the inflammatory response in sepsis, many questions remain. Firstly, failure of TNF-α administration to produce profound organ failure demonstrates that TNF-α alone is insufficient and, although a pivotal inflammatory cytokine, does not prove that immune dysregulation is not involved in the pathophysiology of sepsis. Secondly, the effects of immunomodulatory therapies in sepsis are heterogeneous rather than ubiquitously negative and differences between individual immune responses to infection may also explain this phenomenon. Thirdly, particularly for sCAP, it is apparent that systemic cytokines do not always reflect the local inflammatory process [63].

Jeffrey et al. [63]. characterised three endotypes of pneumonia (including sCAP) based on direct investigation of lung tissue. These “pneumotypes” had different immune cell infiltrates and different prognoses and did not correlate with any particular identified pathogen. They did not represent different stages of the same disease as evidenced by serial sampling and were not exclusive to particular patients; those with subsequent admissions may have switched between different pneumotypes. Patients with balanced immune response and epithelial–endothelial repair signatures had faster time to resolution, as well as a signal towards reduced 1-year mortality which did not meet statistical significance.

It seems unlikely therefore that the immunological contribution to pathophysiology of sepsis comes from simply “too much” inflammation, but rather from an incompletely characterised, dysregulated immune response. The immunomodulatory, as opposed to immunosuppressive, effect of interventions such as systemic corticosteroids, without specific patient selection, would be expected to produce heterogeneous outcomes. Understanding which patients benefit from corticosteroids will require measurement and reporting of relevant systemic biomarkers (e.g. CRP, procalcitonin (PCT), cytokines). For example, rapid temporal variability of cytokines in CAP [125] may provide insight into the early versus late disease processes, identify inflammatory endotypes and enable more precise patient selection, particularly in critical illness. Direct interrogation of the localised lung inflammasome is also necessary to build a complete picture.

However, CRP has been used as a peripheral marker of systemic inflammation to identify patients with sCAP who may benefit from adjunctive corticosteroid therapy. In a landmark RCT, Torres et al. [126] demonstrated that methylprednisolone significantly reduced early treatment failure in patients with sCAP and CRP >150 mg·L−1, supporting the concept that CRP may enrich for steroid-responsive endotypes. The large contemporary trials such as CAPE COD, REMAP-CAP and ESCAPe did not stratify therapy according to CRP. Recently, an individual patient data-driven meta-analysis (IPDMA) identified adjunctive corticosteroid therapy is beneficial for hospitalised patients with CAP and patients with high baseline CRP of >204 mg·L−1 were more likely to benefit from systemic corticosteroid therapy based on studies prior to 2022 and validated on the available CAPE COD and ESCAPe trials [47]. This stratification may allow therapeutic enrichment in further trials. Importantly, CRP lacks specificity for lung-specific inflammatory pathways [63] and can be influenced by co-morbid illness, hepatic function or infection burden (although the 2025 IPDMA accounted for major comorbidities and found none to be predictive of corticosteroid benefit, either individually or in combination with CRP) [47]. CRP emerged as the only variable identified as an effect modifier, supporting the interpretation as a simple, feasible, peripheral enrichment marker for precision corticosteroid therapy in CAP and sCAP. We recommend these results are evaluated in large prospective clinical trials as a standalone marker or in combination with other variables to assess corticosteroid-responsiveness.

Variations in vascular endotypes

sCAP and sepsis pathophysiology includes microvascular damage through degradation of the endothelial glycocalyx leading to fluid leakage and parenchymal oedema as well as capillary microthrombosis [127]. This contributes to multi-organ dysfunction (heart, lungs, kidneys, liver and brain) and overall to the significant morbidity and mortality associated with critical illness [128]. Human studies measuring serum levels of major glycocalyx components syndecan-1 and hyaluronan during sepsis show that there is a clear trend of elevated circulating levels, but also significant heterogeneity between populations [127]. Circulating syndecan-1 is associated with increased glycocalyx damage and has been inversely correlated with survival [129, 130]. Dexamethasone administration significantly decreases syndecan-1 release in lipopolysaccharide models of sepsis in rats [131].

Multi-omics approaches to identifying endotypes of endovascular dysfunction in sepsis patients are developing and may yield a deeper understanding of the heterogeneity of the disease and treatment effects of interventions such as corticosteroids [128]. Immune-mediated alveolar capillary endothelial dysfunction, driven by neutrophil–platelet interactions and microthrombus formation, contributes to microvascular obstruction and impaired gas exchange at the alveolar level. This process is difficult to measure and quantify and the extent to which endothelial and microvascular injury contributes to disease severity in sCAP or responds to corticosteroid therapy remains uncertain.

Variations in pathological virulence and lung microbiome in sCAP

Heterogeneity of microbial factors in sCAP can be divided into pathogen, host and microbiome effects, which have a complex interplay, discussed in this section. There are myriad causative organisms of pneumonia, although in approximately half of sCAP cases no pathogen is isolated [33–35]. Virulence factors vary between organisms and clinical outcomes may be influenced by the microbes themselves, such as through exposure to by-products or endotoxins, and the host response to the organism and its metabolites [132]. Certain microbes are particularly pathogenic when host immune response is inadequate, such as P. jirovecii, while others are enhanced by strong immune responses, such as Mycoplasma pneumoniae [132]. Notably, corticosteroids are associated with mortality benefit in P. jirovecii pneumonia for people living with HIV [133, 134].

In health, the lung microbiome primarily reflects upper respiratory tract flora, but also lung-specific bacteria, fungi and viruses, from inhalation of airborne microbes and oropharyngeal aspiration [135–138]. Local conditions such as humidity, pH, oxygen tension and microbial competition can affect the composition and can vary through pre-existing pathological changes such as in COPD, allergy, infection and cancer. These changes can lead to different prognoses and responses to treatment [136, 139, 140].

The composition of the lung microbiome could affect susceptibility to pneumonia. The lung microbiome of advanced HIV subjects shows dysbiosis that persists for years despite treatment [141] and may be associated with increased pneumonia susceptibility in patients with uncontrolled HIV infection. Alteration of the microbiome due to recent antibiotic therapy increases the risk of HCAP [142]. In ventilated patients colonised with P. aeruginosa, a decrease in the diversity of the lung microbiome under antibiotic treatment was associated with the development of pneumonia [143]. This change in the microbiome is the result of selection pressure and airway dysbiosis, and subsequent manipulation of immune regulation [143].

Corticosteroid treatment may have significant effects on the composition, diversity, burden and abundance of the lung microbiome [144]. The interaction between the lung microbiome, microbial virulence and immune response is ever-changing and can be altered by systemic corticosteroids, which may play a role in disease progression and outcome in CAP. In addition to this, in severe pneumonia there is added complexity due to inter-treatment effects in the ventilated patient, anti-anaerobic antibiotic effects (e.g. co-amoxiclav, piperacillin with tazobactam) and long-term oxygen therapy, which is associated with depletion of certain bacteria (mainly anaerobes) which can negatively impact survival [145].

Corticosteroid treatment factors

Variations in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of corticosteroids

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (PK-PD) of systemically administered corticosteroids in patients with critical illness are under-investigated and under-scrutinised. HTE may be introduced with modest changes to dosing regimens due to variations in PK-PD. For instance, pharmacokinetic modelling suggests that different dosing regimens of equivalent daily quantities alter the efficacy of methylprednisolone [146]. Several conditions with an indication for corticosteroids, such as kidney transplant and acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, are associated with higher incidence of rejection and relapse respectively with lower corticosteroid exposure by pharmacodynamic parameters, but these relationships are less established in critical illness including sCAP [147].

Variability of the corticosteroid molecule between trials may lead to varied exposure due to differences in the biological half-life between molecules (table 5) [147]. In addition, protein binding varies greatly in intensive care and only the free fraction of the corticosteroid can bind to the glucocorticoid receptor at the site of action and initiate a biological effect [148].

Corticosteroid dosing is also problematic. Each of the trials used a corticosteroid dose equivalent to 200 mg·day–1 hydrocortisone. However, this does not account for variations within the study populations. For instance, a 120 kg younger man, after major trauma with a high likelihood of augmented clearance receives the same dose as a 50 kg, frail older woman with congestive cardiac failure and reduced clearance [7, 110]. These variations may affect outcomes in subpopulations due to differences in corticosteroid exposure [149]. Further, this strategy takes no account of the altered PK-PD with wide inter-individual variation in intensive care which is well-described in the literature [147]. Future research should focus on characterising the relationship between pharmacokinetics of corticosteroids and efficacy in sCAP and intensive care patients.

Future trial recommendations

Future trials should incorporate mechanistic evaluations to identify biological heterogeneity that is either responsive or unresponsive to corticosteroid therapy. The approach should utilise multi-omics endotyping, integrating transcriptomics, proteomics and cellular functions to create a streamlined, clinically applicable signature that can effectively stratify patients in real time. Additionally, it is essential to combine this host-response data with rapid molecular pathogen identification with complete microbial metagenomic sequencing, as the causative organism can significantly impact clinical variability in treatment responses. Trials should also clarify the PK-PD relationship of corticosteroids in sCAP, where altered drug handling in critical illness is likely to influence optimal dose, timing, frequency and duration. Future trials should therefore evaluate different steroid types and regimens, incorporate PK-PD monitoring and relevant biomarkers (e.g. PCT, CRP, IL-6), include pathogen-specific analyses, follow patients beyond 90 days to capture metabolic sequelae, such as post-ICU diabetes, and apply consistent definitions of sCAP. By taking these steps, we can develop a fully integrated platform that includes pathogen profile, the host's inflammatory response and drug metabolism, enabling personalised and precision-based corticosteroid therapy in sCAP.

Conclusion

We have presented evidence from pathophysiological and clinical studies that may explain the underlying mechanisms leading to such heterogeneous outcomes observed for systemic corticosteroids in adult sCAP. Further characterisation of these mechanisms in human patients will allow for future developments in exploring inter-individual variations in treatment response and ultimately better trial design to identify whether specific treatments are favoured in particular endotypes. Multi-omics approaches and noninvasive techniques to interrogate localised pathophysiological mechanisms in the lung may facilitate developments in this area.
