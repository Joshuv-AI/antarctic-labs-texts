PDPN+ cancer-associated fibroblasts correlate with the neoadjuvant immunotherapy response in gastric cancer
Authors: Mi Jian, Zhensong Yang, Yutao Tang, Fangjie Jiang, Li Cai, Aina Liu, Jinchen Hu, Xixun Wang, Shuguang Liu, Dawei Zhao, Miaomiao Li, Hongbing Chen, Menglai Zhang, Zengwu Yao, Rongbao Jia, Ruyue Chen, Lixin Jiang, Yifei Zhang, Xicheng Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe achievement of pathological complete response (pCR) with neoadjuvant therapy can significantly improve prognosis in patients with gastric cancer (GC). GC tissues demonstrating pCR after immunotherapy exhibited increased stiffness and proliferation of fibroblasts within the stroma. Specific subpopulation cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) may serve as potential markers for predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy. We screened CAFs-related genes as candidate predictors for immunotherapy using the TCGA-STAD, PRJEB25780, GSE27342, and GSE54129 databases. Tissue specimen from GC patients enrolled in the clinical trial (NCT04208347) was used to evaluate its clinical significance. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were obtained from GSE163558, GSE183904, and GSE184198 datasets and analyzed through Seurat v3 R software and iTALK. GC patient-derived organoids (GC-PDOs) modeling verified the effect of CAFs subpopulations on immunotherapeutic response in vitro. Podoplanin (PDPN) has been identified as a candidate marker related to CAFs for predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy. Western blot analysis indicated that lower PDPN expression was observed in GC samples with pCR. Functional and pathway enrichment analysis indicated PDPN was associated with numerous malignancy-related pathways in gastric cancer. Using the iTALK algorithm, scRNA-seq datasets further verified the interaction between a subpopulation of PDPN+ CAFs and immune cells. The results of multiple immunohistochemistry/immunofluorescence suggested a negative correlation between PDPN+ CAFs and pCR to anti-PD-1 treatment (p < 0.01). Notably, using the GC-PDO model, we determined that PDPN + CAFs hinder the activation, thereby reducing immune response in GC patients. PDPN+ CAFs subpopulation has a potential correlation with the efficacy of immunotherapy for GC patients.

INTRODUCTION

In China, gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most common malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related death.1 Radical surgery remains the effective curative therapy for GC.2 Even with advances in surgical techniques and adjuvant therapy, the recurrence of GC is still an alarming issue due to late diagnosis.3 In addition to targeted therapies, advances in immunotherapy that block programmed death 1 (PD-1) protein or its ligand programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) have brought major improvements to the treatment of advanced or metastatic gastric cancer.4 According to the recent research, neoadjuvant immunotherapy for advanced GC maximizes T cell activation using the primary tumor as an antigen source, as well as removing micro-metastases systemically.5 Patients with metastatic GC receiving anti-PD1 immunotherapy, including camrelizumab, have shown good efficacy and low adverse effects in preliminary studies.6,7

Prediction and evaluation of pathologic responses following neoadjuvant immunotherapy has been shown to be an effective early predictor of treatment response and survival.8 Neoadjuvant immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) treatment is different from chemotherapy in that it directly kills tumor cells through cell toxicity. By activating tumor-specific T cells, ICB therapy indirectly eradicates tumors, which may help improve anti-PD-1/L1 response. Currently, the neoadjuvant immunotherapy response is still lacking effective predictive and prognostic indicators. In recent years, a number of key driver genes have been discovered with the progress of high-throughput sequencing technology and transcriptomic research. However, there is still a need to identify additional key driver genes, especially those that affect the tumor immune microenvironment and may be related to the immunotherapy prognosis.9

Notably, in clinical practice, GC tissues demonstrating pathological complete response (pCR) after immunotherapy exhibited increased stiffness. In the tumor microenvironment (TME), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) makeup the vast majority of stromal cells and are critical to the progression of the disease.10,11 As activated CAFs interact with tumor cells and other stromal cells, they release numerous regulatory factors and synthesize and remodel extracellular matrix (ECM), and thus potentially impose selective pressure on cancer clones and may hold the key to curing the malignancy. Furthermore, hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining of dissected GC tissue specimen showed a heightened proliferation of fibroblasts within the stroma. Our observation suggested that genes associated with CAFs may be candidate predicting factors of immunotherapy. Our preliminary analysis led to the identification of podoplanin (PDPN), one of the CAFs-related genes, as a candidate predictor for immunotherapy. Previous studies have revealed that PDPN serve as the biomarker of activated CAFs, as well as a potential therapeutic target for these cells.12 PDPN expression have been identified in a variety of malignancies and are reported to correlate with poor prognosis in patients with breast cancer or lung cancer.13 However, the source and function of PDPN in GC remains debated. Therefore, our study highlighted the potential relationship between PDPN and immunotherapy response and determined the novel potential interaction between PDPN+ CAFs subpopulation and T cells in the TME.

RESULTS

The abundance of CAFs was increased after anti-PD-1 therapy in GC tissues

During surgery, we found that GC tissues achieving pathological remission exhibit significant stiffening. Considering stromal collagen organization correlating with ECM stiffness, we first investigated the proportion of fibroblasts in resected gastric cancer tissue samples. Typical histomorphology of cancer-associated fibroblasts CAFs was shown in Fig. 1(a). Upon comparing gastric cancer tissues before and after immunotherapy, a notable increase in the abundance of CAFs was observed [p < 0.001, Figs. 1(b) and 1(c)]. In the exploratory phase of our study, we utilized immunofluorescent staining with alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), fibroblast activation protein (FAP), and vimentin (VIM) antibodies to selectively label and quantitatively analyze the presence of CAFs within the GC tissue samples. However, based on the classification of treatment response into pCR and non-pCR groups, we did not observe any significant differences in the α-SMA, FAP, and VIM expression levels [p >0.05, Fig. 1(d)]. Here, a detailed flow chart for further exploration was shown in Fig. 2.

WGCNA and screening of CAFs-related DEGs to predict response for immunotherapy

By clustering the 373 samples in TCGA-STAD, we found that the five samples on the left and the 10 samples on the right were quite different and formed two clusters [Fig. S1(a)]. The soft threshold power value was set to 6 for subsequent analyses [Fig. S1(b)]. A dendrogram was used to quantify module similarity based on the correlation [Fig. S1(c)]. Associations between clinical traits and modules were identified based on these correlations. MeBlack module was positively correlated with inferior immunotherapeutic response [correlation = −0.54, p < 0.001, Fig. S1(d)]. Defined based on a combined gene significance >0.8 and module membership >0.8 among genes in the brown module, 193 total genes that were highly connected in the MeBlack module were identified as candidate hub genes.

Four datasets were downloaded from the Metascape, ENA, TCGA, and GEO databases. After data collection, the microarray data were normalized. When identifying DEGs, a threshold fold change of 2 and a p-value of 0.01 were used. The limma package was used to analyze the DEGs in GSE27342 and GSE54129 datasets. In addition, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to evaluate the diagnostic power of candidate biomarkers in ENA database (PRJEB25780). These genes with area under curve (AUC) value higher than 0.60 were considered to have good predictive value [Fig. S1(e)]. A total of 7244 genes were identified as potential biomarkers to predict response for immunotherapy.

Finally, the intersection of predictive biomarker, WGCNA, DEG, and CAFs marker data are displayed in the Venn diagram. The six most significant genes, ASPN, COL1A2, COL3A1, COL5A1, PDPN, and SPARC, were obtained [Figs. S1(e) and S1(f)].

Correlation between PDPN, SPARC, and OS in the TCGA-STAD cohort and its preliminary verification in predictive value for pCR response

Using univariate Cox regression analysis, we verified the prognostic value of these six candidate key genes, and we identified that PDPN (p = 0.047) and SPARC (p = 0.025) mRNA expression were correlated with the overall survival in GC patients [Figs. 3(a)–3(c)]. PDPN expression exhibited a negative correlation with pCR, whereas SPARC expression demonstrated no significant correlation. Therefore, PDPN may be related to the efficiency of immune checkpoint inhibitors therapy.

PDPN expression was associated with CAFs and its enriched functions and pathways in GC

Analysis using TCGA-STAD data in heatmap and Timer 2.0 database demonstrated that high expression of PDPN correlated well with CAFs infiltration level [p < 0.001, Figs. S2(a) and S3(A)–S3(I)]. Moreover, we also observed that the CAFs infiltration positively correlated with monocyte, macrophage, myeloid dendritic cell, endothelial cell infiltration, T stage, stroma score, and microenvironment score [p < 0.001, Fig. S2(a)], and negatively correlated with T cell, CD8+ T cell, B cell, neutrophil infiltration, cytotoxicity score, and immune score [p < 0.05, Fig. S2(a)]. The heatmap showed that PDPN can serve as a biomarker of CAFs or activated CAFs in addition to the immunosuppressive microenvironment in GC.

In order to predict the function of PDPN, including associated pathways, we analyzed TCGA-STAD data to identify PDPN correlations with other genes in gastric cancer [Figs. S4(a)–S4(i)]. Functional enrichment and Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed that PDPN was mainly associated with TME-related gene sets, including organization of extracellular matrix (ECM), structure, and external encapsulating structure [Fig. S2(b)]. Molecular function (GO-MF) analysis indicated an enrichment of the top 300 genes in extracellular matrix structural constituent, receptor ligand activity, glycoaminoglycan binding, and sulfur compound binding. Using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, an enrichment and crosstalk of these top 300 genes mainly focused on focal adhesion, protein digestion and absorption, and ECM-receptor interaction. According to Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) in KEGG pathways, the PDPN-related pathway mainly focused on the pathways in cancer, the MAPK signaling pathway, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and neuroactive ligand receptor interaction were significantly enriched [Fig. S2(c)]. In addition, neutrophil degranulation, signaling by interleukins, and Gpcr Ligand binding were significantly enriched using GSEA in Reactome pathway analysis [Fig. S2(d)]. According to these results, PDPN appears to be associated with numerous malignancy-related pathways in gastric cancer, particularly immune-related pathways.

scRNA-seq data quality control and annotation

For scRNA-seq data, we used the following data: (1) features > 1200; (2) total RNA counts between 500 and 75 000; and (3) percent of mitochondrial gene expression >10%. The cells were classified into clusters based on the UMAP algorithm [Fig. S5(a)], and volcano plots were generated using PlotMultiGroupVolcano [Fig. S5(B)]. Additionally, different types of cell marker genes were identified through the FindAllMarker analysis [Fig. S5(c), Table S2]. Based on marker genes, the clusters were annotated into GC tumor clusters, immune cell clusters (including monocyte clusters, macrophage cluster, mast cells clusters, dendritic cell clusters, T-cell clusters, and B-cell clusters) and stromal cell clusters (including fibroblast clusters and vascular endothelial cell clusters). Cell proportion difference analysis was conducted using the scBar application. Sub-clustering analysis of different cells was performed using RunSeurat. The proportion of fibroblasts was similar in GC tissues compared to non-tumor tissues [8.7% vs 9.1%, p > 0.05, Fig. S5(d)]. Furthermore, custom metadata (such as clinical information) can be imported into the target single-cell expression profiles via the ToMetadata application for subsequent personalized analyses. The target gene PDPN is only expressed in CAFs, and cell subpopulations were annotated based on PDPN expression in each cluster [Fig. 6(e)]. Then, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE) dimensional reduction was conducted using the RunTSNEfunction. Fibroblasts were annotated with four marker genes, including ACTA2, FAP, PDGFRB, and NOTCH3. The fibroblasts were re-clustered with the same algorithm as FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. tSNE dimensionality reduction was further performed on fibroblast subpopulation clusters. Marker genes of each PDPN+ CAFs subpopulation cluster were identified using FindAllMarkers function by comparing one cluster with other clusters with logFC = 0.5, minpct = 0.35, and p < 0.05 [Fig. S5(f)]. The top three subpopulations of PDPN+ CAFs were CXCL14+, THBS2+, and TGFBR1+ subpopulations. Ligand–receptor interactions across CAFs subpopulations in GC

Using iTALK analysis, the LR interactions among CAFs subpopulations, tumor clusters, immune clusters, and stromal clusters were identified [Figs. S6(c)–S6(e)]. Among the PDPN+ CAFs subpopulations, the LR interactions predominantly communicated with other cell types in the CXCL14+, THBS2+, and TGFBR1+ subpopulations (Table S3). Especially for PDPN+CXCL14+ CAFs subpopulation, enriched functions or pathways were mainly focused on focal adhesion, pathways in cancer, regulation of cytoskeleton, ECM-receptor, protein processing of endoplasmic reticulum, and T cell receptor signaling pathway. All of these results suggest that PDPN+CAFs may be related to tumor metastasis and immune infiltration. However, whether PDPN + CAFs really affect immunotherapy through these pathways still needs further experiments to confirm.

Association between PDPN expression and therapeutic effect with ICB therapy in GC tissue section labelled with mIHC/IF

To assess the specificity of the association between the quantitative fluorescence (QIF) based on PDPN expressions and therapeutic effect with ICB therapy, we measured the levels of PDPN expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration and exhaustion in GC tissue pre- and post-immunotherapy [Figs. 4(a)–4(c)]. MIHC/IF staining verified a strongly negative association between the pre-immunotherapy PDPN expression and pCR of anti-PD-1 treatment for GC [Spearman's R = −0.815, p = 0.025, Figs. 5 and S7(a)]. Similarly, PDPN expression was positively associated with the PD-L1 and Tim-3 expression [Spearman's R = 0.504, p < 0.001, Fig. S7(b); Spearman's R = 0.634, p < 0.001, Fig. S7(c)]. Our mIHC/IF staining also showed that the levels of PDPN+VIM+ cells was also negatively associated with pCR [Spearman's R = −0.927, p = 0.003, Fig. S7(d)] and positively associated with the PD-L1 or Tim-3 expression [Spearman's R = 0.536, p = 0.003, Fig. S7(e); Spearman's R = 0.421, p = 0.004, Fig. S7(f)]. However, the level of pre-immunotherapy VIM+ cells and post-immunotherapy PDPN expression was not associated with the pCR [Figs. S7(g) and S7(h)]. The level of pre-immunotherapy VIM+ cells was not directly associated with CD8, PD-L1, FOXP3, and Tim-3 expression [Figs. S7(i)–S7(l)].

PDPN+ CAFs regulated the response of ICB therapy in in vitro co-culture of human GC-PDOs, CAFs, and T cells

We next examined the impact of CAFs with different PDPN phenotypes on the response to anti-PD-1 therapy for GC cells. Based on current research, PDO models can mimic clinical responses to clinically approved immune therapies or combinations. We aim to obtain the effect of PDPN+/-CAFs on gastric cancer organoids after immunotherapy by knocking out and amplifying PDPNs in CAFs. Unfortunately, our knockout results were not ideal. Therefore, as an alternative, we utilized fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to isolate subpopulations of CAFs based on PDPN positivity and negativity. Subsequently, we established an in vitro co-culture model to assess the response of PDPN+/- CAFs to ICB therapy [Figs. 6(a) and 6(b)]. FACS analysis was also utilized to assess the CD8+ subpopulation of T cells, and a higher increased CD8 expression in T cells was observed in in vitro co-culture of GC-PDOs with PDPN- CAFs compared to PDPN+ CAFs, regardless of whether they were treated with anti-PD-1[Figs. 6(c) and 6(d), Table S4, p < 0.01]. Additionally, higherincreased IFN-γ secretion was also observed in supernatant of in vitro co-culture model of GC-PDOs with PDPN-CAFs compared to PDPN+ CAFs, regardless of whether they were treated with anti-PD-1 [Fig. 6(e), Table S4, p < 0.01].

DISCUSSION

The low response rate to ICB therapy in patients with GC remains an intractable clinical problem.14,15 To deliver proper treatment, it is critical to stratify patients according to their sensitivity to or resistance to first-line ICB therapy. GCs with DNA mismatch repair defects, high microsatellite instability, or PD-L1+ (CPS ≥ 1) have previously been found to respond better to PD-1 blockade therapy.16,17 A growing focus has been placed on the involvement of the immune system in neoadjuvant treatment. Previous studies on immunotherapy in GC have focused on the role of the immune contexture, including tumor-infiltrating TIL cells, macrophage cells, neutrophils, B cells, and natural killer cells. In our study, PDPN has been confirmed as a promising biomarker for neoadjuvant ICB therapy responsiveness in GC. Furthermore, we also found that there is a potential association between the PDPN+CAFs subset and the suppression of regulated CD8 T cells, which may contribute to ICB resistance.

Originally, PDPN was used to mark lymphatic vessels in various cancers, including GC. We found that it was able to not only label lymphatic endothelial cells, but also was closely related to poor prognosis and immune evasion status in GC patients with high PDPN expression. Since immune cell infiltration and activation are critical for the immunotherapy response of patients with GC,18–20 we investigated the relationship between PDPN expression and immune cell infiltration level, and found that PDPN+ CAFs are lower in GC tissue specimens with pathological complete response (pCR). These findings provide potential directions for subsequent investigations. Using public databases, we performed functional enrichment analysis and single-cell RNA sequencing. Functional analysis revealed that PDPN is primarily associated with high infiltration of CAFs. Furthermore, PDPN showed strong correlations with pathways involved in cell adhesion, immune infiltration, and immune cell activation in GC tissues. iTALK analysis further identified an interaction between PDPN+ CAFs and immune cells. Overall, our results found a link between PDPN, CAFs, GC, and immune cells, suggesting a possible role for PDPN+ CAFs in immune infiltration within gastric cancer. However, whether PDPN+ CAFs indeed interact with immune cells through these pathways—and whether they functionally contribute to GC immunotherapy—requires further investigation.

Functional studies conducted by different laboratories have laid a certain theoretical basis for the role of PDPN as a promoter of malignant tumors, which is partly in line with our observations.12,21 According to previous studies, PDPN is also expressed in the membrane of fibroblast-like reticular cells present in thymic conduits, and its interaction with CCL21 is associated with T cell differentiation. These findings have suggested the potential role of PDPN+ CAFs in the tumor microenvironment and their possible association with T cells. Since PDPN may act as a key factor in T cell regulation, its deficiency in CD8+ T cells was verified to cause high expression of IFN-γ, which contributes to favorable immunotherapy response. In our specimens, a negative correlation was consistently observed between PDPN and TIM-3 expression,22 indicating that the PDPN+ CAFs subpopulation may have a correlation with dysfunctional T cells. Subsequent in vitro experiments further confirmed that exposure to PDPN+ CAFs subpopulation resulted in reduced activation rates of CD8+ T cells and decreased IFN-γ expression in the co-culture model. However, the association between TIM-3 and PDPN alone is insufficient to fully elucidate the mechanisms by which PDPN+CAFs suppress CD8+ T cells. More detailed mechanistic validation is essential and critical for advancing the exploration of PDPN as a therapeutic target.

Furthermore, we also explored the correlation between the PDPN expression and the immune checkpoint-related genes, including PD-L1, TIM-3, and FOXP3. In our study, GC specimens with pCR showed lower PDPN and TIM-3 expression, and higher FOXP3 expression. Further analysis in mIHC/IF staining proved that PDPN+ CAFs negatively correlated with PD-L1 and TIM-3 expression in GC, suggesting that PDPN+ CAFs may have a potential relationship with immunosuppression of T cells. In part, this explains that specific CAFs subpopulations partially account for immunosuppression, leading to an inferior response to ICB therapy. On the other hand, no correlation was observed between PDPN+ CAFs and Foxp3 expression in our GC tissue specimen. As previously reported, Foxp3+ Treg frequencies were significantly higher in lung cancer with PDPN+ CAFs than those with PDPN- CAFs. Further investigation with a larger sample size is needed to confirm the correlation between PDPN and FOXP3 expression.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, our findings provide insights into the mechanisms involved in the formation of the immunosuppressive microenvironment in GC. Specifically, we highlighted that PDPN expression in endoscopic biopsy may have a strong relationship with inferior immunotherapy response for GC. Our findings indicated specific CAF subpopulation plays a potential role in remodeling the ECM and regulating the activation of CD8+ T cells, thereby it can affect the effectiveness of anti-PD-1 therapy. Further analysis revealed that the proportion of PDPN+ CAFs was related to worse efficacy of immunotherapy for GC patients. This provides the fundamental experimental evidence for developing strategies that target specific CAF subpopulations to enhance the tumor response to immunotherapy.

METHODS

Patient selection

Endoscopic biopsy specimen of pretreatment primary tumor and surgical specimens of post-treatment primary tumor were collected from 16 advanced GC patients who were performed D2 plus radical resection combined with perioperative chemotherapy (SOX regiment, including S-1, Oxaliplatin) with anti-PD-1 (Camrelizumab) therapy at the Yantai Yuhuangding Hospital Affiliated to Medical College of Qingdao University, Yantai, China.7 Camrelizumab was administered intravenously in three doses (200 mg per dose) every 3 weeks, followed by surgery within 84–98 days of the first dose. The medical records system was used to extract information about clinical characteristics, imaging, and treatment history.

Clinical staging, pathologic examination, and histologic assessments

Endoscopic biopsy specimens of pretreatment tumors and matched surgical dissected specimens of post-treatment tumors were collected from GC patients in our clinical cohort.23 Briefly, for the assessment of clinical staging, tumor response was evaluated according to the RECIST v1.1, using enhanced CT at baseline and after neoadjuvant treatment before radical surgery. Pathological regression was assessed by identifying and measuring the tumor bed. In cases of small tumor beds (≤3 cm), the entire tumor bed was sampled. In cases where the tumor bed was >3 cm, a minimum of one section per diameter of the tumor bed was sampled. In case there was less than 10% residual viable tumor in the initial sampling, it was necessary to sample all remaining beds. Slides stained with H&E from the main tumor bed were examined histologically.24

Common immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF)

Both IHC and IF staining were performed on 6-μm-thick sections. Rabbit anti-PDPN (ab236529, Abcam), rabbit anti-Vim antibody (ab92547, Abcam), rabbit anti-FAP antibody (ab40778, Abcam), rabbit anti-αSMA antibody (ab124964, Abcam), and mouse anti-EpCAM antibody (BF2019, Affinity) were used as the primary antibodies to identify the CAFs and tumor cells. Goat anti–mouse IgG AlexaFluor 633 conjugate and Goat F(ab′)2 anti–rabbit IgG AlexaFluor 488 conjugate were used as secondary antibodies. The nucleus was stained with DAPI (F6057, Sigma) and sealed.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry/immunofluorescence (mIHC/IF), image acquisition, and quantitative analysis

mIHC/IF staining was carried out following the addition of the secondary antibody, and a heat induction and cooling step was used to repair the antigen. The nucleus was stained with DAPI and sealed. For the quantification of positively stained cells in mIHC, Pannoramic MIDI® slides were scanned and imaged, and HALO® and R scripts were used for batch analysis.25 We could therefore quantify the number of positively stained cells with a single (e.g., PDPN or VIM only), double (e.g., PDPN+VIM+), or triple (e.g., CD8+ PD-L1+TIM-3+) marker.

The following primary antibodies were used in this study: rabbit anti-PDPN (ab236529, Abcam), rabbit anti-VIM (ab92547, Abcam), mouse anti-CD8 (70306S, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-PD-L1 (13684S, Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-Tim-3 (45208S, Cell Signaling Technology), and rabbit anti-FOXP3 (ab20034, Abcam). The secondary antibody was Opal™ polymer HRP Ms+Rb (ARH1001EA, Perkin Elmer). Fluoroshield with DAPI (F6057, Sigma) was used to stain nuclei and seal the slices.

Western blot analysis

Western blot (WB) analysis was performed according to the standard protocol as previously described.26 The following primary antibodies were used in WB analysis: rabbit anti-PDPN (ab236529, Abcam) and rabbit anti-SPARC (ab207743, Abcam).

Data collection and analysis

RNA expression and clinical data were obtained from the TCGA-STAD database, PRJEB25780(European Nucleotide Archive, ENA), GSE27342 (Platform: GPL570), and GSE54129 (Platform: GPL570). Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were obtained from GSE163558, GSE183904, and GSE184198. TCGA tumor abbreviations are listed in Table S1. For samples from the same patients, gene expression was averaged by the Bioconductor limma package of R software (version 4.0.2).

Calculation of CAFs score

The MCP-counter algorithm, provided by TIMER 2.0 (http://timer.cistrome.org/), was used to calculate the CAFs score of patients recruited from both the TCGA-STAD databases.

TideSCORE analysis of mRNA expression in GC

From the TCGA-STAD dataset, raw counts in RNA-sequencing data and corresponding clinical characteristics were obtained, in which acquisition and application of the database complied with the guidelines and policies. The TIDE algorithm was used to predict the potential ICB response.27,28

Immune cell infiltration

Infiltration scores of TCGA-STAD data that were estimated using CIBERSORT were downloaded.29,30 A median CAFs score level was used to divide samples from the TCGA-STAD into two groups (high CAFs and low CAFs) to compare immune cell infiltration.

Correlation and enrichment analyses

The Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated using TCGA-STAD data to measure the correlation between PDPN and other mRNAs in gastric cancer. We selected the top 300 genes most positively correlated with PDPN for enrichment analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the ClusterProfiler R package's EnrichGO function.30 Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis was performed using the ClusterProfiler R package's EnrichKEGG function. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the ClusterProfiler R package's gseGO, gseKEGG, and gsePathway functions.30,31

For scRNA-seq data, a correlation heat map to show the expression of CAFs subpopulation markers was constructed to demonstrate the connections using the clusterProfiler R package. Enrichment analysis was also performed on GO and KEGG pathways to determine the potential functions of each cell subpopulation. Additionally, enrichment statuses were visualized using bubble charts created by the PlotClusterBubble. Functions or pathways with p < 0.05 are considered significant.

scRNA-Seq data processing and cell annotation

An analysis of single-cell transcriptome data from 30 GC tumor tissues and 12 non-tumor tissues was performed (GSE163558, GSE183904, and GSE184198). Individual data were merged, and low-quality cells were excluded based on the types of genes detected, the total number of detected genes, and the percentage of mitochondrial genes. The eligible data were normalized, and batch effects were removed. Furthermore, the scRNA-seq data were subjected to uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis for dimension reduction and statistically divided into different clusters with a resolution of 1.0 (Refs. 32 and 33). Using the FindAllMarkers function, we obtained markers among different clusters and annotated their cell types based on known cell markers.34 The statistical significance was considered when the p-value was less than 0.05 (*), 0.01 (**), 0.001(***), and 0.0001 (****). In tumor scRNA-seq datasets, we chose differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with p < 0.001 and then removed candidates with normalized expression levels below a threshold (1 for GC).35

Cell communication analysis

We focused on two aspects of the tumor environment surrounding tumor cells. First, we undertook a more thorough analysis of tumor and non-tumor cell clusters. We rearranged their markers and annotated them with reported tumor-related subtype markers. Second, ligand-receptor (LR) interaction analysis was applied to demonstrate the interaction between CAFs subpopulations and other cell types using the iTALK package.36 The LR interaction of interest was assessed based on the expression of the ligand in the GC cluster and the expression of the corresponding receptor in another cell cluster. Thus, we selected the LR interactions that were biologically relevant to the CAFs subpopulations.

Establishment of GC-PDOs co-cultured with CAFs and T cells

Tumor tissues were minced finely on ice. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) were isolated from dissected tumor tissue and cultured using the Organoid Culture Medium Kit (abs9449). Primary cancer-associated fibroblasts were isolated from the tumor specimens using the outgrowth method.37 CAFs-conditioned supernatant was collected, and cells were counted and adjusted to a density of 1 × 106 cells/ml. Cells were fixed and permeabilized using a commercial buffer, then incubated for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. After resuspension in 100 μl of sterile 1% FBS/PBS, flow cytometry antibodies (PDPN-1 and an isotype control) were added at a 1:500 dilution, followed by 30 min of incubation at 4 °C in the dark. We isolated PDPN+/- CAFs subsets using flow cytometry. Subsequent immunofluorescence staining confirmed a significant difference in PDPN expression between PDPN+ and PDPN-CAFs subpopulations.

T lymphocytes were isolated and washed twice with RPMI 1640 medium via centrifugation (2500 rpm for 10 min, then 2000 rpm for 10 min). After supernatant removal, cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium and plated in a 60-mm dish with RPMI 1640 containing 15% FBS. The culture was maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Pieces of minced tissue generated in the process of organoid isolation were separated and incubated with fibroblast medium containing RPMI Medium 1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 20% fetal calf serum (Gibco), 200 mM GlutaMAX (Gibco), and 1×Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco).38 CAFs were passaged approximately every 5–7 days and their identity was confirmed by morphological assessment. All CAFs tested negative for mycoplasma contamination and were used for experiments within 3 to 7 passages after isolation. CAFs cells were sorted into PDPN+ and PDPN- groups (APC anti-Human Podoplanin, Biolegend) by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Peripheral blood was processed into peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs),39 and PBMCs were then cultured in T cell medium (PriMed-iCell-026). GC-PDOs co-culture model was established, referring to a previous study.38–40

To recapitulate the in vivo immune microenvironment, we implemented a staged coculture system beginning with a 5-day preconditioning phase where PDOs were cocultured with CAFs to establish communication and mimic natural matrix–tumor interactions as closely as possible prior to immune cell introduction. This preparatory phase ensures more physiologically relevant responses to subsequent PD-1 inhibitor treatment. Technical optimizations included maintaining PDOs within optimal size ranges (with routine 7–14 days subculture cycles) to prevent central necrosis due to absent vasculature. T cells were introduced on day 5 to allow stabilized interactions, followed by PD-1i addition on day 7 when the system reached a responsive steady state. This standardized protocol effectively balances experimental reproducibility with faithful simulation of dynamic in vivo immunotherapy responses.

Assay for anti-PD-1-dependent T cell activation

T cell samples were washed twice in ADMEM/F12, triturated with a P1000 pipette to dissociate further, and passed over a 40 mm filter. Single cells were then pelleted, resuspended in 100 ml FACS Buffer (PBS plus 2 mM EDTA and 0.1% BSA), and stained for FACS.38 FACS staining cocktail contained Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD), anti-CD3 (UCHT1, BD), anti-CD4 (L200, BD), and anti-CD-8 (RPA-T8, BD). T cells were sorted on a BD Aria II flow cytometer. IFN-γ in supernatant samples in the co-culture model was tested by the ELISA Kit (mlbio).

Statistical analysis

Biological replicates are indicated by the indicated sample size (n) and are graphed as mean ± s.d. A parametric Student's two-tailed unpaired t-test was used for the comparison of two groups with normally distributed data. The nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test (for unpaired groups) was used if the data were not normally distributed. GraphPad Prism version 7.0.1 or R software (version 4.0.2, http://www.rproject.org) was used to determine statistical significance. The survival analysis was conducted using the “survminer” and “survival” R packages. In all datasets, tumor samples were divided into two groups based on the half-separation cutoff value for mRNA or protein level to plot Kaplan-Meier survival curves and perform multivariate Cox regression analysis to evaluate the risk significance of each variable for OS. Statistical significance was evaluated using two-tailed t tests: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.
