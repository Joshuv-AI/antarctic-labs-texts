Videoflow: Uroflowmetry in Children Exploiting Standard Care Video Urodynamic Imaging
Authors: Wouter van Dort, Peter F W M Rosier, Ruud C Wortel, Rogier P J Schroeder, Thomas R F van Steenbergen, Bernard J Geurts, Laetitia M O de Kort
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPressure‐flow study, consisting of flow rate combined with detrusor pressure, is the gold standard to determine bladder outflow obstruction and evaluate the detrusor voiding contraction. In very young children, not able to sit on a uroflowmetry device, the flow rate cannot be measured. This results in an incomplete evaluation of bladder and urethral functions. The aim of this study is to derive the flow rate using standard care video‐urodynamic study X‐ray images.MethodsWe retrospectively included 50 video urodynamic studies in children able to void on a uroflowmetry toilet, to correlate the outcome with standard uroflowmetry. The X‐ray images taken during voiding were manually segmented and algorithmically converted into a flow rate, called “videoflow”. This videoflow was compared with the measured (standard) uroflowmetry.ResultsAn excellent cross‐correlation of 0.98 was found between the videoflow and normal uroflowmetry outcomes. The videoflow maximum flow rate (Q max) was accurate with a not significant bias of +0.1 mL/s difference with standard uroflowmetry.ConclusionsIn conclusion, the proposed videoflow is found feasible and accurate in children who are able to void in a uroflowmetry toilet. This method paves the way to measure urine flow rate in very young children who cannot yet sit upright. For the first time, this new technique will enable the validation of a pressure‐flow‐based urethral resistance measurement in this very young age group.Trial RegistrationClinical Registration Number: 23U‐0621.Keywords: bladder outflow obstruction, pediatric urology, urodynamics, uroflowmetry

Introduction

The pressure‐flow study (PFS), part of the urodynamic study (UDS), is the gold standard in assessing lower urinary tract (LUT) function [1]. Using the combination of the detrusor pressure with urine flow rate, the urethral resistance (UR) and bladder contractility (BC) can be derived [2, 3]. These relations between pressure and urine flow rate are well‐studied in adults, resulting in several metrics for the classification and quantification of bladder outflow obstruction (BOO) [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. In this way, clinically significant obstruction can be identified, and treatment may be initiated.

Although UR is well studied in—especially male—adults, BOO cut‐offs are not well defined in the pediatric population and consensus about measures is lacking [11]. Existing measures for adults to detect BOO are considered inadequate for the pediatric population, due to anatomical and physiological differences [12]. The International Children's Continence Society (ICCS) standard does not comment on the quantification or qualification of BOO. It only reports that p det pressures during voiding in children with normal neurologic function significantly exceed adult values [13]. The diagnosis BOO is defined as a clinically significant UR with clinical relevance, and is based on simultaneously measured pressure and flow rate. Therefore, p det alone is inadequate for diagnosing BOO. Hence, measuring a reliable flow rate is mandatory for an adequate assessment of the LUT function during voiding.

The measurement of a reliable flow rate is challenging in infants. Before potty training, they can not void voluntarily, resulting in an unclear distinction between the cystometry and the PFS. Moreover, the youngest infants cannot sit upright due to the lack of core stability. Therefore, standard uroflowmetry equipment cannot be used in this population. Without a flow rate, the UDS is not complete, and statements about UR and BC cannot be made.

As direct urine flow measurement is not possible in the targeted category of young children, the flow rate is currently estimated indirectly from other measured parameters. Some approaches include the calculation of the mean flow by dividing the measured (weighted) voided volume in the diaper by the observed voiding time [14]. Although giving some general indication, this approach misses possible (and likely common) fluctuations in an infant's flow, which could have a major influence on the reported clinical outcome.

In case X‐ray images are made during UDS to evaluate bladder and urethra anatomy and vesicoureteral reflux (video urodynamic study (VUDS)), these images can also be used to estimate the flow rate. By determining the change in bladder surface area during micturition as a measure of change in bladder volume, the flow can be derived. A feasibility study in five children established an excellent correlation of the flow rate based on the X‐ray images with normal uroflowmetry [15]. The current study aims to further establish the feasibility and accuracy of this new technique in a larger population, using retrospective VUDS data.

Methods

To validate the calculation of the flow rate by X‐ray images, VUDS performed in children between 2016 and 2020, who were able to sit on a uroflowmetry toilet, were retrospectively included. This population was included to be able to compare the outcome with standard uroflowmetry. VUDS was performed according to the ICCS recommendations. During a VUDS, the bladder was filled with Urografin 30%, and the uroflowmetry was corrected for the difference in mass density compared to water. During voiding, a sagittal X‐ray image was taken at least once per second, as per local protocol. Sometimes, the fluoroscopy was interrupted while taking a fluorographic image. In those cases, a time difference between successive images of maximally 4 s was accepted. The UDS was performed with the Ellipse water‐filled urodynamic system with Audact software (Andromeda Medizinische Systeme GmbH) with a 6Fr urodynamic catheter, while X‐ray images were taken with the Philips MultiDiagnost Eleva FD (Koninklijke Philips N.V.).

Data Selection

The VUDS were selected based on the quality of the UDS and the availability of the X‐ray images during the voiding episode. The UDS quality was considered sufficient when the uroflowmetry parameters were considered physiological, with voided volume > 50 mL and 2 < Q max < 40 mL/s. For this study, VUDS were excluded if there was vesicoureteral reflux > Grade 1 (International Classification Grade 1–5).

Methodology Testing

To calculate the flow rate from the X‐ray images, a method which was earlier found feasible was used with minor modifications [15]. The bladder on the X‐ray images was manually segmented, and the area of the bladder on those images was determined in pixels, see Figure 1. Previously, this area in pixels was raised to the power of 1.5, to create a hypothetical corresponding volume in voxels [15]. This voxel volume was successively converted into a real volume in milliliter using a investigation specific factor n 1, which depends on the settings of the X‐ray machine. The power of 1.5 converts a two‐dimensional area to a three‐dimensional volume. This power can only be used if there is a constant and linear relation between the area measured on the X‐ray images and the unknown third “depth” dimension.

Example of an original VUDS X‐ray image (A) and the result of the performed segmentation (B).

This postulation was tested using nonlinear regression analysis. For all images, the area of the bladder was normalized by dividing by the full bladder area, for example, the area of the first image (A), resulting in a list of fractions to the full bladder area between 1 and 0. In addition, the bladder volume according to the uroflowmetry at the same timestamp as the X‐ray image was also normalized by dividing by the bladder volume of the full bladder (B). Nonlinear regression was performed using the following “fitting formula”: B=n1*An2. Here, n 1 is an investigation‐specific correction factor for the full bladder, based on individual anatomy and X‐ray machine settings. This n 1 converts the volume in voxels (which is something in three dimensions, but with an arbitrary unit) to volume in milliliters, and should be recalculated for each individual patient as machine settings differ and patient anatomy varies. This is done by using the X‐ray image of the full bladder and the total bladder volume, calculated at the end of the UDS using the voided volume and post‐void residual. This factor is constant for an individual during the investigation when no machine settings are changed during voiding. As all volumes are normalized for the postulation testing, this factor should be close to 1. Moreover, n 2 is the conversion factor of interest for testing the conversion from a two‐dimensional area to a three‐dimensional volume, and is compared to the dimensionally correct value of 1.5.

Methodology Application

After testing the hereabove mentioned correction factors, we applied the methodology to the X‐ray images of the included patients. After manual determination of the areas of the segmented X‐ray images, a Whittaker–Eilers smoother was used with a λ of 3000 and an order of 2, while directly upsampling to a sample rate of 10 Hz [16]. Using the formula described above, the observed X‐ray bladder areas were converted into an approximate bladder volume. These successive bladder volumes were differentiated in time, while taking into account the exact timestamps of the respective images, resulting in the “videoflow”. In our approach, n 1 was calculated by dividing the real total voided volume + eventual residual urine by the volume in voxels of the full bladder according to the X‐ray images.

The resulting videoflow was compared with the standard uroflow rate, measured with the Eclipse uroflowmeter. To compare the uroflowmetry curve shapes with the videoflow results, the cross‐correlation metric was used to assess similarity. In addition, the weighted mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) was used for assessing the relative differences between the videoflow and the uroflowmetry curve shapes. Moreover, the differences between the uroflowmetry Q max and the videoflow Q max were tested on normal distribution and assessed using the paired‐samples t‐test and visualized using a Bland–Altman plot. Dependency of the absolute error in Q max according to the videoflow on the actual Q max, age, voiding time, voided volume and MAPE was investigated using the Pearson correlation test. Data selection and analysis were performed in Python 3.12.3 and statistical analysis was performed in SPSS, version 30 (IBM).

Results

We retrospectively included 50 VUDS performed in children (mean age 10.7, range 5–18; 48% male) in this study. In total, 1147 X‐ray images (about 23/child) taken during the UDS were manually segmented by a pediatric urodynamicist. Basic uroflowmetry parameters can be found in Table 1. The female children were significantly older, had a higher Q max and a larger voided volume than the male children (Mann–Whitney U p < 0.05).

Nonlinear regression of the normalized X‐ray bladder areas with the normalized bladder volume using the formula described above, resulted in a n 1 of 1.014, close to the expected 1.00 due to the normalization, and a n 2 of 1.504, which is very similar to the theoretical 1.5. Comparison between the standard uroflowmetry and the videoflow curve shape yielded an excellent median cross‐correlation of 0.981 (range 0.935–0.995), see also Figure 2. The median MAPE was found to be 19.1% (range 6.6–37.9).

No significant correlation was found between either the cross‐correlation or the MAPE with voiding time, voided volume, age and Q max (Pearson's correlation p > 0.36) or sex (Mann–Whitney U test p > 0.162), so the dependency of the videoflow accuracy on these factors was not significant.

The difference between the uroflowmetry Q max and the videoflow Q max was found to be normally distributed according to the Shapiro–Wilk test of normality (p = 0.129). No significant mean differences were found between the uroflowmetry Q max and the videoflow Q max, although a standard deviation of the difference of 1.8 mL/s was observed, see Table 2. No correlation between the absolute Q max error and voided volume, voiding time and age was observed (Pearson p > 0.322). There was a moderate correlation of the MAPE with the absolute Q max error (+0.380, p = 0.007), indicating that a less accurate curve representation (represented by a larger MAPE) is more likely to result in a larger error in the Q max estimation.

Discussion

This study presents a first‐of‐its‐kind method to accurately and reliably calculate a flow rate curve based on X‐ray images taken during a routine VUDS. This method enables the conversion of those images into a flow rate curve in young children who cannot perform standard uroflowmetry. The combination of the calculated videoflow with detrusor pressures measured during the VUDS can be used for diagnosing BOO in young children.

The nonlinear regression analysis to compare the theoretical formula with the real‐world values showed no meaningful difference between the theoretical values and the real‐world values. We can therefore conclude that the postulation that the unknown third dimension has a linear relationship with the two measured dimensions is true. This also means that there is no difference for the calculation of the videoflow for making X‐ray images both anterior‐posterior as well as saggitally. This makes the videoflow widely applicable in the current clinical workflow.

A part of the method which can have a major influence on the result is the applied filtering. As the uroflowmetry curves are reconstructed from X‐ray images with a low sample rate of 1 Hz, frequencies above 0.5 Hz can never be reconstructed according to the Nyquist theorem. This results in inaccuracies with regard to variations with a higher frequency than 0.5 Hz which are present in the uroflowmetry. To prevent the suggestion of higher frequencies in the videoflow and remove inaccuracies due to segmentation and timestamp errors, successive time‐based moving average filters were previously used. Those filters are currently replaced by a single Whittaker‐Eilers smoother, which has less influence on the curve shape [16]. It has an additional advantage in handling missing data and concurrent upsampling, which makes the postprocessing of the videoflow less complex.

No clinically and relevant or statistically significant differences were found for the difference in Q max of standard uroflowmetry compared to videoflow, with an absolute difference of only 0.1 mL/s. This results in a high accuracy for the Q max estimation by the videoflow. The precision of the videoflow Q max showed a standard deviation of the difference between the uroflowmetry and the videoflow Q max of 1.83 mL/s. Comparing these values to the expected variation in Q max could help interpret this precision. Although stated as a knowledge gap by the International Continence Society [17], no study has been performed regarding the test–retest reliability of uroflowmetry. Therefore, only studies describing the intra‐patient variability can be used for comparison. A study performed in healthy children found a standard deviation of 6.8 mL/s of the differences in Q max between the children, after correcting for the voided volume and only including the bell‐shaped uroflowmetry curves [18]. This is substantially larger than the standard deviation of the difference in Q max between the uroflowmetry and videoflow indicating that the precision of the videoflow to determine Q max is acceptable.

A moderate correlation was found between the MAPE, which is an indicator of the overall performance of the algorithm to reproduce the uroflowmetry curve, and the estimation of the error in Q max. This indicates that the improvement in the accuracy of any step in the conversion method from images to flow rate will likely result in a smaller Q max error. Two potential inaccuracies were identified in the method. The first is the manual segmentation of the X‐ray images. Small variations can result in artificial fluctuations, which can affect the calculated Q max. Using machine learning for the segmentation of the bladder area may result in a reduction of these fluctuations.

Another source of potential inaccuracy involves the timestamps of the X‐ray images used to convert bladder volumes to flow rate. As this is a retrospective study, timestamps were given in seconds, and smaller differences could not be determined. Implementation of the algorithm in a prospective way, with direct capturing of the X‐ray images with time accuracies in milliseconds will eliminate this inaccuracy.

This study has a few limitations. First, the current method is only applicable if the complete voiding phase is visible on the X‐ray images. In this study, 193 of the 272 screened VUDS were excluded because the voiding phase was not completely captured. Good coordination between the urodynamicist and the radiologist or radiology nurse is, therefore, essential. If performed prospectively, the usability will likely increase significantly. The second most common reason for exclusion was vesicoureteral reflux Grade 2 or higher. As high‐grade reflux likely has a major influence on the algorithm, correction factors should be calculated to make the videoflow also applicable in children with high‐grade vesicoureteral reflux. In addition, for validation purposes, the current study was performed in older children than the population for which the videoflow is necessary to create a uroflowmetry curve. However, in the studied group, we did not see an age dependency of the accuracy. In addition, the factor of n 2 of 1.5 in the conversion formula will result in a larger error for large bladders than for small bladders, given that the percentual error is similar. Therefore, we suppose that the videoflow will be a reliable tool in younger children.

An additional important point of consideration is the use of radiation. The applied radiation dose should be as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA principle). Therefore, performing VUDS with the sole purpose of urine flow calculation should be avoided, and the calculated videoflow should be a “bonus” while performing VUDS or voiding cystographies for other indications. Other imaging methods, using nonradiation methods, would be preferable for urine flow rate measurements in very young children. Such methods should be used when there is no indication for VUDS or voiding cystography.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the proposed videoflow is found feasible and accurate in children who are able to void in a uroflowmetry toilet. This method paves the way to measure urine flow rate in very young children who cannot yet sit upright. For the first time, this new technique will enable the validation of a pressure‐flow‐based UR measurement in this very young age group.
