HIF-1α mediated placental ischemic signaling in the development of early-onset preeclampsia
Authors: Kai You, Yujing Tong, Tingting Sun, Dan Wang, Qifang Liu, Xuewen Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly-onset preeclampsia is a severe placental disorder driven primarily by impaired uteroplacental perfusion and early placental ischemia. Increasing evidence identifies HIF-1α–mediated hypoxic signaling as a central regulator linking placental ischemia to downstream molecular and clinical manifestations. During early gestation, tightly controlled hypoxia guides normal trophoblast differentiation and vascular remodeling. Persistent or excessive hypoxia stabilizes HIF-1α, leading to defective trophoblast invasion, impaired spiral artery remodeling, and progressive placental ischemia. Sustained HIF-1α activation drives mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, amplifying placental injury. At the transcriptional level, HIF-1α promotes antiangiogenic programs, including sFlt-1 upregulation, resulting in systemic angiogenic imbalance and maternal endothelial dysfunction. These processes explain the severity, early onset, and rapid progression of the maternal syndrome. This review integrates current evidence on hypoxia sensing, trophoblast biology, mitochondrial stress, and angiogenic dysregulation to define HIF-1α as a unifying mechanistic hub in early-onset preeclampsia. Understanding HIF-1α–centered signaling provides a framework for improved biomarker development and targeted therapeutic strategies.Keywords: HIF-1α, Early-onset preeclampsia, Placental ischemia, Hypoxia signaling, Trophoblast invasion, sFlt-1/PlGF ratio

Introduction

Pre-eclampsia is a pregnancy-related hypertensive disorder characterized by placental hypoxia, increased oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and endothelial as well as metabolic dysfunction [1,2]. It exhibits proteinuria before the 34th week of pregnant patients due to placental ischemia and vascular endothelial cell dysfunction [3,4]. This definition of time not only reflects the time of onset. Premature diseases show unique biological characteristics, more severe placental involvement and faster clinical progression [5,6]. Obstetrics believes that premature pre-eclampsia is the main cause of premature birth due to medical reasons worldwide. This situation seriously affects maternal health, neonatal mortality and long-term health consequences. These consequences make premature pre-eclampsia the focus of maternal health problems around the world. Despite advances in prenatal care, the burden of premature pre-eclampsia on pregnant women remains high [6,7]. Affected women often experience severe hypertension, renal dysfunction, liver damage, or neurological complications, which typically advance rapidly. Clinicians frequently confront difficult choices to induce early delivery in order to protect maternal safety. While this can reduce maternal risks, it subjects the fetus to extreme prematurity, usually necessitating neonatal intensive care. Survivors face elevated risks of respiratory diseases, neurodevelopmental delays, and lifelong metabolic disorders. This dual burden emphasizes the clinical urgency of understanding the mechanisms behind preterm birth disorders.

Epidemiological data show that premature pre-eclampsia accounts for a small proportion of the total number of cases, but the impact on adverse outcomes is disproportionately large. Late hair disease is more common, but it is usually milder. Premature disease usually reflects serious placental pathological changes [8,9]. This distinction explains the high incidence of fetal growth restriction and placental insufficiency in premature cases. The health system is under great economic pressure due to prolonged hospitalization and newborn care. These realities further emphasize the need to carry out research on the mechanism of early pre-eclampsia. The lack of effective disease modification therapy further exacerbates this burden. The current treatment mainly depends on symptomatic treatment and termination of pregnancy [10]. Antihypertensive agents are effective in lowering blood pressure; however, they do not address the underlying placental dysfunction. Although magnesium sulfate can prevent eclamptic seizures, it does not halt disease progression. These therapeutic limitations underscore the urgent need to identify upstream molecular drivers. A deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms may facilitate earlier detection and enable the development of targeted interventions. Consequently, early-onset preeclampsia represents not only a significant clinical challenge but also a critical focus of ongoing scientific research.

Early and late pre-eclampsia have the same diagnostic criteria, but their mechanism of occurrence is different. The gestational week provides a useful clinical boundary, but pathophysiology is the real basis for distinguishing the two [11,12]. Premature hair disease is mainly caused by placental dysfunction. Delayed disease reflects the greater contribution of maternal cardiovascular vulnerability to the disease. This difference determines the severity of the disease, biomarkers characteristics and fetal outcome. There is a significant difference in the placental development of these two phenotypes. Premature pre-eclampsia usually involves insufficient infiltration of trophoblast cells and incomplete reshaping of spiral arteries [13,14]. These abnormalities can restrict uteroplacental blood flow in early pregnancy, resulting in sustained placental hypoxia and nutrient deprivation. In contrast, late-onset disease typically develops after placentation has been completed, and the placental structure often appears morphologically intact. In such cases, limited maternal hemodynamic reserve becomes the primary constraint. These pathophysiological differences help explain why fetal growth restriction is more prevalent in early-onset disease and less common in late-onset cases.

The biomarker model further supports this distinction. Early-type diseases are manifested by a significant increase in anti-angiogenic factors, such as soluble FMS like Tyrosine kinase 1 (sFlt-1), while a significant decrease in placental growth factor (PLGF) [15,16]. These alterations often emerge several weeks before the onset of overt clinical symptoms. Late-onset disease is typically characterized by a relatively mild angiogenic imbalance, in which maternal hypertension and metabolic stress play more prominent roles. These distinct pathophysiological features highlight the need for differential therapeutic targets and tailored management strategies. A single treatment method cannot effectively solve these two phenotypes. There are also differences in the clinical course of the disease. Premature preeclampsia usually progresses rapidly and is difficult to predict [17,18]. Maternal organ dysfunction in early-onset disease may deteriorate rapidly, sometimes within a matter of days, whereas late-onset disease typically follows a more indolent course. This clinical heterogeneity underscores the importance of precise phenotypic classification. Elucidating the biological basis of early-onset disease enables clinicians to better stratify risk and implement appropriate surveillance strategies. Moreover, this distinction provides the conceptual framework for emphasizing placental ischemia–related signaling pathways in early-onset preeclampsia.

Placental ischemia is the core initiating factor of premature pre-eclampsia. Insufficient spiral artery remodeling limits the mother's blood flow to the developing placenta. This restriction hinders the normal transition from the high-resistance cycle to the low-resistance cycle [19,20]. The placenta is continuously exposed to the fluctuating oxygen supply. With the progress of pregnancy, chronic ischemia gradually develops. This environment exerts continuous metabolic and oxidative stress on the placental tissue. The ischemic placenta responds through adaptive but ultimately dysregulated signaling pathways. For example, hypoxia activates HIF-1α, upregulates sFlt-1 and pro-inflammatory factor TNF-α, and simultaneously inhibits VEGF, resulting in an imbalance in angiogenesis and a synergistic deterioration of the inflammatory response [21,22]. These factors are subsequently released into the maternal circulation, where the vascular endothelium becomes a principal downstream target. This cascade of events illustrates how localized placental abnormalities can precipitate widespread maternal systemic dysfunction. Placental ischemia can also destroy the normal differentiation of trophoblast cells [23,24]. Extravillous trophoblasts fail to fully invade the decidua and remodel the uterine spiral arteries within the myometrium. This defect results in persistently elevated vascular resistance and establishes a vicious cycle: inadequate perfusion exacerbates ischemia, while ischemia further impairs trophoblast invasion. This self-perpetuating process distinguishes early-onset preeclampsia from its late-onset counterpart. In this context, the placenta is no longer a passive participant but rather the principal driver of pathology. The placental ischemia model is highly consistent with clinical observations. Early-onset preeclampsia is frequently accompanied by fetal growth restriction, abnormal uterine artery Doppler findings, and reduced placental volume—features that reflect impaired placental perfusion. The model also explains why maternal symptoms often improve rapidly after delivery of the placenta: removal of the ischemic source alleviates endothelial dysfunction. Thus, placental ischemia provides a unifying framework for understanding the onset and progression of early-onset preeclampsia.

In the molecular reaction of placental ischemia, HIF-1α has become a core regulatory hub. As a principal transcription factor, HIF-1α can sense oxygen supply [25,26]. Under normal early pregnancy conditions, short-term hypoxia will stabilize HIF-1α and promote trophoblast cell differentiation. With the improvement of placental perfusion, the activity of HIF-1α will decrease [27]. This time regulation helps to maintain healthy placental development. In the early pre-eclampsia, this regulatory mechanism fails. Persistent ischemia leads to the continuous stability of HIF-1α. The transcription factor remains active outside its physiological window period. Sustained activation of HIF-1α signaling alters trophoblast behavior by suppressing their invasive capacity and promoting a metabolic shift toward hypoxic adaptation. These alterations impair placental maturation, leaving the placenta in a relatively immature developmental state. Such persistent and dysregulated activation represents a critical pathogenic shift in disease progression. HIF-1α also directly regulates the balance of angiogenesis [28]. The transcription factor induces the expression of sFlt-1, which is a soluble receptor that can neutralize VEGF and PLGF. This induction effect links hypoxia perception with angiogenesis disorders. Excessive sFlt-1 can destroy the signal conduction of maternal endothelial cells and lead to hypertension, proteinuria and organ damage. Therefore, HIF-1α links placental ischemia with maternal vascular disease through a clear molecular pathway. In addition to angiogenesis, HIF-1α also affects mitochondrial metabolism, oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling. These processes will further damage the placental tissue and amplify the stress response. The convergence of these pathways makes HIF-1α a key node in the early pre-eclampsia. Targeting this hub is expected to bring a clear conceptual understanding and treatment prospects. Understanding HIF-1α signal conduction provides a coherent framework to integrate placental biology with systemic maternal outcomes.

This review aims to comprehensively evaluate the current evidence regarding the role of HIF-1α–mediated placental ischemic signaling in early-onset preeclampsia. It focuses on upstream hypoxia-sensing pathways and their downstream molecular and cellular consequences, with particular emphasis on defective trophoblast invasion, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and angiogenic imbalance. Each of these components contributes to the initiation and progression of the disease. Importantly, this review distinguishes physiological hypoxia—an essential feature of early placental development—from pathological ischemia. This distinction helps clarify when HIF-1α signaling supports normal developmental processes and when its persistent activation becomes maladaptive. By integrating molecular, cellular, and clinical evidence, the review delineates the mechanistic differences between early- and late-onset preeclampsia, highlighting how sustained HIF-1α activity can remodel placental structure and function. In addition, emerging biomarkers and their therapeutic implications are discussed. HIF-1α–regulated pathways generate measurable signals that may facilitate early detection, including alterations in angiogenic factors, metabolic markers, and imaging correlates of placental ischemia. Understanding these associations may improve risk stratification and enable more precise monitoring strategies. Finally, this review summarizes key knowledge gaps and outlines future research priorities. A deeper understanding of the HIF-1α–centered signaling network may support the development of targeted interventions before irreversible placental injury occurs. This mechanism-driven approach aligns with the broader goal of precision obstetrics. By focusing on placental ischemic signaling, this review seeks to advance both scientific insight and clinical management of early-onset preeclampsia.

Physiological and pathological hypoxia in early placental development

Normal placental oxygen gradients and developmental hypoxia

The early development of the placenta occurs in a unique hypoxia environment, which supports the orderly differentiation of trophoblast cells. In the early stage of pregnancy, the flow of maternal blood into the villi gap is deliberately restricted. The spiral artery remains narrow and has high resistance. This restriction causes a state of physiological hypoxia in the placental tissue. At this stage, the oxygen pressure of the placenta is much lower than that of most adult tissues [29,30]. This situation is not a pathological state, but a strictly regulated developmental signal. Trophoblast progenitor cells rely on hypoxic pressure to maintain their proliferation ability [31,32]. The hypoxic state can maintain the stem cell-like characteristics of the trophoblast cell group. These cells respond by activating transcriptional procedures, which are conducive to controlling growth rather than premature differentiation. The stabilization of HIF-1α plays a central role in this process. Under hypoxic conditions, the activity of prolyl hydroxylase is reduced. The reduction of hydroxylation prevents the proteasome degradation of HIF-1α. Stable HIF-1α enters the nucleus and regulates the genes involved in metabolism, survival and angiogenesis. This signaling pathway supports the expansion of the placenta in early pregnancy.

Physiological hypoxia also affects the metabolic process of trophoblast cells. Early placental cells mainly rely on glycolytic metabolism rather than oxidative phosphorylation [33,34]. This metabolic preference reduces mitochondrial oxygen consumption and limits the production of reactive oxygen. Therefore, the placenta protects itself from oxidative damage during the fragile development stage. HIF-1α sustains this metabolic state by upregulating glycolytic enzymes and suppressing mitochondrial biogenesis. This coordinated adaptation enables rapid placental growth while minimizing the accumulation of oxidative damage. As pregnancy progresses, however, the placenta gradually transitions from a hypoxic to a more oxygen-rich environment, accompanied by a corresponding shift in metabolic and signaling profiles. Around the end of early pregnancy, the spiral artery undergoes extensive remodeling. Extravilli trophoblast cells replace vascular smooth muscle and elastic tissue [35,36]. These changes transform the uterine circulation into a low-resistance system. Maternal blood flow into the intervillous space progressively increases, leading to a steady rise in oxygen tension. Concurrently, HIF-1α activity declines. This temporal regulation marks a critical developmental transition. Successful placentation depends on the precise coordination between oxygen availability and trophoblast differentiation. Accordingly, the concept of developmental hypoxia is fundamentally distinct from pathological ischemia. Physiological hypoxia supports normal placental formation, whereas pathological hypoxia disrupts placental structure and function. This distinction is determined by timing, duration, and adaptive capacity. Early gestational hypoxia is transient and tightly regulated, allowing placental tissues to adapt effectively. In contrast, dysfunction arises when hypoxia is prolonged beyond its normal window or is exacerbated by persistent inadequate perfusion.

Transition from physiological hypoxia to placental ischemia

The transformation from physiological hypoxia to pathological placental ischemia is the core event of early preeclampsia. Normal pregnancy requires a timely change of oxygen pressure from low level to high perfusion state. This transformation depends on the successful invasion of trophoblast cells and the remodeling of spiral arteries. If this process fails, it will prevent the expected increase in oxygen supply. Placental tissue is continuously exposed to a chronic hypoxic environment [19,37]. This environment is becoming more and more unfavorable. Persistent hypoxia is different in nature from early developmental hypoxia. Chronic hypoxia will trigger a stress response, not a differentiation process [38,39]. Trophoblast cells activate survival pathways, but this comes at the expense of functional maturation. HIF-1α becomes abnormally stabilized, and prolonged activation alters its transcriptional program. This persistent signaling upregulates genes involved in inflammation, anti-apoptosis, and anti-angiogenic pathways. Collectively, these changes signify a critical shift from adaptive to maladaptive responses.

Placental ischemia can also cause fluctuations in oxygen supply. Insufficient remodeling of the spiral artery will lead to intermittent perfusion. This pattern makes the placental tissue go through the cycle of hypoxia and reoxygenation (Fig. 1). These cycles will produce oxidative stress [40,41]. Reactive oxygen species accumulate within the mitochondria of trophoblast cells, overwhelming the antioxidant defense system. Oxidative stress damages lipids, proteins, and mitochondrial DNA, impairing cellular function and amplifying stress signaling pathways. In response, the ischemic placenta alters its secretory profile, increasing the production of anti-angiogenic factors such as sFlt-1 and soluble endoglin. These molecules enter the maternal circulation, with the maternal vascular endothelium becoming the primary target. Endothelial cells are deprived of VEGF and PLGF signaling [42,43], leading to impaired vascular tone regulation, systemic hypertension, and compromised renal filtration. These downstream effects collectively constitute the maternal syndrome of preeclampsia.

Placental ischemia–reperfusion injury and oxidative stress in preeclampsia. Chronic ischemia resulting from inadequate spiral artery remodeling exposes placental tissue to repeated hypoxia–reoxygenation cycles. These cycles increase reactive oxygen species production, cause oxidative damage to lipids and DNA, and overwhelm antioxidant defenses.

Placental ischemia is self-reinforcing through a pathogenic feedback loop. Hypoxia inhibits trophoblast invasion, and shallow invasion maintains high vascular resistance, which in turn perpetuates ischemia. HIF-1α plays a central role in this loop: sustained HIF-1α signaling suppresses genes required for extravillous trophoblast differentiation [44,45], preventing the proper remodeling of spiral arteries and locking the placenta in an immature state. Consequently, the transition from physiological hypoxia to pathological ischemia reflects a failure of normal developmental progression. The placenta remains trapped in its early hypoxic program, with molecular signaling stuck in an adaptive mode that no longer meets the metabolic and functional demands of pregnancy. This maladaptive mismatch underlies the pathogenesis of early-onset preeclampsia. Understanding this transformation clarifies how early placental defects can ultimately trigger systemic maternal disease later in pregnancy.

Early gestational timing of hypoxia-related vulnerability

Early pregnancy represents a critical window during which the fetus is highly vulnerable to hypoxia-related disturbances. At this stage, placental structure and function remain highly plastic, and even minor perturbations can have lasting consequences. Successful placental formation relies on precise temporal regulation; delays or disruptions during the early weeks cannot be fully compensated later in gestation. During early pregnancy, the uteroplacental interface is established as extravillous trophoblasts invade the maternal decidua and myometrium. These trophoblasts interact closely with uterine immune cells, the extracellular matrix, and vascular endothelial cells [45,46]. Oxygen availability tightly regulates these interactions: excessive hypoxia can alter trophoblast behavior, whereas insufficient hypoxia can impair their differentiation. Both extremes carry significant risks. Consequently, the placenta depends on a finely tuned oxygen gradient during this formative period to ensure proper development.

Maternal factors strongly affect this fragile period. Past diseases, such as chronic hypertension, diabetes and autoimmune diseases can damage uterine vascular function before pregnancy begins. These diseases limit the adaptability of the placenta. The reduction of vascular reserves will increase the susceptibility to ischemia [47]. Environmental exposure will also have an impact. Smoking, air pollution and nutritional deficiency can affect oxygen delivery and mitochondrial function. These influences work in the early stage. The placenta has been affected by these before the clinical detection of symptoms. The time of stimulation of ischemia determines the phenotype of the disease. Persistent hypoxia in early stage is conducive to the occurrence of early preeclampsia [47]. Later or milder stress may initially allow the placenta to fully develop, but in late pregnancy, it will overwhelm the adaptability of the mother's blood vessels. This schedule explains why premature disease shows more serious placental pathological changes. In these cases, the placenta has dysfunction during its formation. Late hair disease reflects the different balance between placental function and maternal adaptability.

The experimental model supports the importance of early time. Exposure to hypoxia in the early stage of placental formation can lead to more serious phenotype than exposure in the late stage of pregnancy. Early damage will permanently change the gene expression pattern. Epigenetic modification locks cells in a state of maladjustment. HIF-1α-dependent transcription programs will be activated for a long time. Even if the infusion is partially improved, it is difficult for these procedures to return to normal. This persistence explains the limited reversibility of early development of the disease [48,49]. Clinical observations are consistent with these mechanisms. Early preeclampsia usually has abnormal uterine artery Doppler ultrasound results during pregnancy. These results reflect the early defects of spiral artery remodeling. Placental volume and blood vessel distribution are reduced. Fetal growth restriction usually occurs in the early stage. These characteristics indicate that the course of the disease began long before the symptoms appeared. Therefore, early pregnancy is both a risk period and an opportunity. Intervention at this stage may help prevent the disease from progressing to irreversible ischemia. Recognizing the vulnerability of early placental development to hypoxia provides a theoretical foundation for strategies aimed at early screening and prevention. This perspective shifts the focus from maternal symptoms to placental biology, framing preeclampsia as a developmental disorder rooted in maladaptive placental responses during the early stages of pregnancy.

Molecular regulation of HIF-1α in the ischemic placenta

Oxygen-dependent stabilization and degradation of HIF-1α

The regulation of HIF-1α mainly depends on the supply of oxygen to cells. Under normal oxygen conditions, placental cells maintain a low basic level of HIF-1α protein. Oxygen-dependent enzymes, that is, prolyl hydroxylase domain proteins, will actively hydroxyl specific proline residues on HIF-1α. This modification will create an identification site for the identification of the von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. The complex targets HIF-1α and makes it rapidly degraded by proteasomes. This mechanism ensures that the HIF-1α signaling pathway remains inhibited when the oxygen supply meets the metabolic needs [50,51]. Therefore, placental tissue avoids unnecessary activation of hypoxic response genes in the late stage of pregnancy. Hypoxic conditions will destroy this degradation pathway. Reduced oxygen supply will limit the activity of prolyl hydroxylase. Insufficient hydroxylation will prevent the binding of VHL. HIF-1α protein accumulates in the cytoplasm [52,53]. The stable HIF-1α protein is transferred to the nucleus and dimerized with HIF-1β. The heterodimer binds to the hypoxic reaction element in the target gene promoter. This combination initiates the transcription program that supports cells to adapt to the hypoxic environment. These procedures include genes involved in glycolysis, angiogenesis, cell survival and iron metabolism. During the early development of the placenta, this reaction is strictly regulated and short-lived (Fig. 2).

Schematic of oxygen-dependent control of HIF-1α stability. Under normoxia, PHDs hydroxylate HIF-1α proline residues, enabling recognition by the VHL E3 ubiquitin ligase complex and rapid proteasomal degradation. Under hypoxia, reduced PHD activity limits hydroxylation, allowing HIF-1α stabilization, nuclear translocation, dimerization with HIF-1β/ARNT, and transcriptional activation of hypoxia-responsive genes.

Placental ischemia alters both the duration and magnitude of HIF-1α stabilization. In early-onset preeclampsia, incomplete remodeling of the spiral arteries restricts maternal blood flow, subjecting placental tissue to persistent hypoxia rather than the transient low-oxygen signals of normal development. This sustained hypoxia drives HIF-1α stabilization beyond its physiological range. Prolonged nuclear presence of HIF-1α modifies transcriptional outputs [52,53], shifting expression from adaptive genes to stress-response programs. Consequently, the placenta transitions from a regulated growth mode to a survival-oriented state under adverse conditions. This maladaptive shift represents a pivotal step in the pathogenesis of early-onset preeclampsia. The ischemic placenta will also experience fluctuations in oxygen tension, rather than a stable hypoxia state. Reshaping incomplete arteries allows intermittent perfusion. These cycles of hypoxia and reoxidation will produce oxidative stress. Reactive oxygen can change the regulatory mechanism of HIF-1α. Even if the oxygen level rises briefly, oxidative stress will inhibit the activity of prolyl hydroxylase [54,55]. This inhibition allows the stabilization of HIF-1α to be maintained despite the presence of partial reperfusion. Therefore, the placenta loses the ability to properly turn off the hypoxic signal. This disorder will amplify the downstream pathological reaction. The specificity of placental cell types further affects the regulation of HIF-1α. Trophoblast cells, fusion trophoblast cells, and extravillous trophoblast cells show different sensitivity to oxygen tension. Extra-villi trophoblast cells need to accurately regulate HIF-1α to complete invasion and vascular remodeling. Continuous HIF-1α activity will inhibit gene expression related to differentiation and movement. These changes will damage the transformation of the spiral artery, resulting in placental ischemia. Therefore, the oxygen-dependent regulation of HIF-1α directly links molecular induction with placental structural defects.

Crosstalk with inflammatory and metabolic signaling pathways

The HIF-1α signaling pathway in the ischemic placenta does not operate independently (Fig. 3). Inflammatory and metabolic pathways strongly regulate HIF-1α activity. Placental ischemia activates the innate immune response in both trophoblasts and resident immune cells, leading to elevated local concentrations of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β [56,57]. These cytokines influence HIF-1α at both transcriptional and post-translational levels: inflammatory signals upregulate HIF-1α mRNA, while cytokine-activated kinases enhance HIF-1α protein stability. Notably, these effects persist even under moderate oxygen conditions. Under ischemic stress, the placenta shifts toward a pro-inflammatory microenvironment, with chronic activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway [58,59]. NF-κB directly binds to HIF-1α promoters, increasing transcriptional output, and also acts synergistically with HIF-1α on shared target genes. This synergy amplifies the expression of inflammatory mediators and anti-angiogenic factors, establishing a positive feedback loop: inflammation stabilizes HIF-1α, and HIF-1α in turn reinforces the inflammatory response. As a result, the placenta remains in a state of sustained stress activation.

AMPK, mTORC1, and HIF1 in hypoxia. Hypoxia elevates ROS and Ca2+ to activate AMPK, and AMPK suppresses mTORC1 (with parallel hypoxia inputs such as REDD1 and BNIP3); hypoxia also stabilizes HIF by inhibiting PHD-dependent hydroxylation and pVHL-mediated degradation, linking oxygen sensing to translational control and transcriptional adaptation.

The metabolic signaling pathway also has a strong regulatory effect on HIF-1α. Placental ischemia can change mitochondrial function. The reduction of oxygen supply will limit oxidative phosphorylation. Trophoblast cells are more dependent on aerobic glycolysis. HIF-1α actively promotes this metabolic reprogramming. The transcription factor induces the expression of glycolytic enzyme and glucose transport protein. This transformation supports the generation of ATP under hypoxic conditions [60,61]. This transformation also reduces mitochondrial oxygen consumption. However, long-term dependence on glycolysis will lead to low metabolic efficiency. Lactic acid accumulates in the placental tissue, leading to local acidosis. These conditions will further damage the function of trophoblast cells. Mitochondrial dysfunction will in turn affect the regulation of HIF-1α. Damaged mitochondria produce excessive active oxygen. These molecules will inhibit the activity of prolyl hydroxylase. This inhibition can stabilize HIF-1α even when the oxygen level fluctuates. Therefore, mitochondrial reactive oxygen acts as a secondary hypoxic signal. The placenta is becoming more and more sensitive to ischemic stress. This sensitivity explains why small changes in perfusion lead to disproportionate molecular reactions in early preeclampsia.

The nutritional perception pathway will also further regulate the activity of HIF-1α. AMP-activated protein kinase responds to energy stress by changing cell metabolism. The activation of AMPK can regulate the translation and transcription activity of HIF-1α [62,63]. The mTOR signaling pathway also interacts with the regulation of HIF-1α. Reduced nutrient supply will inhibit the activity of mTOR and change the translation priority. These metabolic checkpoints integrate oxygen, energy and nutritional signals. This integration reaction determines the adaptability or inadaptability of the placenta. The combined effect of inflammation and metabolism transforms HIF-1α from a developmental regulatory factor to a pathological driving factor [64,65]. This transcription factor begins to promote the anti-angiogenesis process, not placental growth. The expression of genes such as sFlt-1 was significantly upregulated. This induction association links local placental stress with systemic maternal endothelial dysfunction. Therefore, the interaction between hypoxia, inflammation and metabolism explains the severity and duration of continuous activation of HIF-1α in premature preeclampsia.

Epigenetic and transcriptional control of HIF-1α activity

Epigenetic regulation plays a key role in shaping the activity of HIF-1α in ischemic placenta. Placental cells undergo extensive epigenetic remodeling in the early development process. DNA methylation mode and histone modification guide cell spectrum specialization and gene expression. Hypoxic stress can change these epigenetic maps. Persistent hypoxia will change the chromatin accessibility of the HIF-1α target gene. These changes will affect the duration and intensity of the transcription response. Placental ischemia can change the DNA methylation of the promoter region involved in hypoxic signal transduction [66,67]. Low methylation enhances the transcriptional reactivity to HIF-1α. This modification increases the expression of genes related to angiogenesis imbalance and inflammation. These changes still exist after the initial hypoxic damage. Placental cells retain the memory of early stress exposure. This kind of memory leads to irreversible pathological programming in premature diseases.

Histone modification further regulates the transcription output of HIF-1α. Histone acetylation increases the chromatin accessibility in the hypoxic reaction element. The acetyltransferase recruited by HIF-1α enhances the transcription efficiency [68]. On the contrary, changes in histone methylation will affect the inhibition or activation of genes. The balance between activation markers and inhibitory markers determines which HIF-1α target genes dominate. In premature preeclampsia, the chromatin state is conducive to the expression of anti-angiogenesis and inflammatory genes. Development-promoting genes are still inhibited. Transcriptional co-regulation factors also affect the function of HIF-1α. Co-activating factors such as p300 and CBP enhance the transcriptional activity of hypoxic-reactive promoters. Placental ischemia increases the availability of these co-activating factors. The increase in availability enhances the signal conduction of HIF-1α. At the same time, the inhibition mechanism is weakened. This imbalance amplifies the expression of pathological genes.

Non-coding RNA adds another layer of regulation. Hypoxia-induced microRNA regulates the stability and downstream signal conduction of HIF-1α. Some microRNAs enhance the translation of HIF-1α (Table 1). Others inhibit the negative regulatory factors of hypoxic signal conduction. Placental ischemia changes the expression spectrum of microRNA. These changes enhance the continuous activity of HIF-1α. Long-chain non-coding RNA is also involved by constructing transcription complexes at hypoxic reaction sites [73]. The epigenetic persistence of HIF-1α signal conduction explains the limited reversibility of early preeclampsia. Even partial recovery of perfusion cannot completely normalize the expression of placental genes. Exposure to hypoxia in the early stage will leave lasting molecular imprints. These imprints make trophoblast cells fall into a state of maladjustment. The placenta still cannot complete the normal mature process. This epigenetic framework provides important clinical insights. The damage in early pregnancy has greater long-term consequences than the source of stress in the later stage. The timing of hypoxia determines whether it will trigger a reversible adaptive reaction or an irreversible pathological change. HIF-1α acts as both a receptor and an executor in this process. Therefore, understanding the epigenetic regulation and transcription regulation mechanism of HIF-1α helps to clarify why premature preeclampsia becomes a unique and serious placental disease.

HIF-1α and defective trophoblast invasion

Regulation of trophoblast differentiation and EMT programs

Successful placental formation depends on the orderly differentiation of trophoblast cell lines. Cell trophoblasts must proliferate, differentiate, and gain the ability to invade in order to form extravillous trophoblast cells. This transformation allows trophoblast cells to invade the maternal decidua and myometrium, a process that depends on the tightly regulated epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [8]. EMT enables cells to lose epithelial polarity and acquire migratory properties [74], facilitating vascular remodeling and stable placental anchoring. HIF-1α plays a key physiological role in regulating trophoblast differentiation during early pregnancy. Short-term activation of HIF-1α promotes early trophoblast proliferation while preventing premature differentiation, ensuring that progenitor cells expand sufficiently before invasion begins. As oxygen availability increases, HIF-1α activity typically declines, which in turn promotes EMT and the maturation of extravillous trophoblasts. This temporal regulation maintains a critical balance between cellular growth and invasive capacity.

Persistent placental hypoxia will disrupt this process. Continuous HIF-1α stability will interfere with normal EMT signal conduction. Trophoblast cells retain epithelial characteristics and cannot obtain complete invasion ability. HIF-1α changed the expression of EMT-related transcription factors (such as SNAIL, TWIST and ZEB family members) [75]. These changes lead to partial or stagnant EMT states. Cells are trapped between epithelium and mesenchymal cells. This incomplete transformation limits the motility of trophoblast cells. The ischemic placental environment can also change the interaction of the extracellular matrix. HIF-1α signal inhibits the expression of matrix metalloproteinase, which is necessary for tissue invasion. The reduction of matrix degradation further limits the migration of trophoblast cells. At the same time, HIF-1α promotes the expression of adhesion molecules, which anchor trophoblast cells prematurely. These molecular changes together damage the invasion phenotype. Therefore, the placenta cannot establish sufficient maternal contact. EMT defect is an early molecular sign of early preeclampsia. This defect does not stem from the lack of hypoxia signals, but from its persistent existence. HIF-1α is transformed from a developmental regulatory factor to a pathological invasion inhibitor. This transformation shows that the time and duration of signal transmission determine the result. The changed differentiation trajectory lays the foundation for downstream vascular defects.

Impaired spiral artery remodeling in early-onset preeclampsia

Spiral artery remodeling is an important feature of normal placental development. Extravillous trophoblasts replace endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle cells in the maternal spiral artery [76,77]. This substitution effect transforms narrow and high-resistance blood vessels into dilated and low-resistance blood vessels. The remodeled artery provides stable blood flow for the villi space. Full remodeling ensures an adequate supply of oxygen and nutrients throughout pregnancy. Premature preeclampsia shows serious defects in spiral artery remodeling [78,79]. The invasion of trophoblast cells is shallow and incomplete. Vascular smooth muscle persists in the arterial wall. Arteries retain their contractile ability. These structural features maintain high vascular resistance. Placental infusion is restricted. As pregnancy progresses, the placenta may experience chronic ischemia. Persistent activation of HIF-1α directly impairs spiral artery remodeling by inhibiting the trophoblast differentiation pathways required for arterial transformation. This transcription factor downregulates genes involved in vascular mimicry and endothelial replacement, preventing trophoblasts from fully integrating into maternal blood vessels. In addition, HIF-1α disrupts signaling between trophoblasts and maternal endothelial cells, further interrupting coordinated vascular remodeling and exacerbating placental dysfunction.

The ischemic placenta also shows changes in immune interaction. Natural killer cells and macrophages of the uterus usually support spiral artery remodeling. HIF-1α-driven inflammatory signal conduction destroys this supportive environment. Excessive cytokine production causes immune cells to dysfunction. Immune cells show pro-inflammatory phenotypes, not play the role of supporting remodeling. These changes aggravate the defects of trophoblasts and worsen vascular lesions. Histological studies consistently show that these abnormalities exist in early preeclampsia. The spiral artery shows smooth muscle residue and lumen stenosis. The penetration of trophoblast cells is limited to the superficial membrane. There is very little or no invasion of the uterine muscle layer. These findings are related to the abnormal pattern of uterine artery Doppler during pregnancy. The high resistance index reflects potential structural defects. Spiral artery remodeling disorder is a key link between molecular dystony and macroscopic placental pathology [80,81]. This defect keeps the placental perfusion at a sub-excellent level. The placenta cannot be compensated in the later stage of pregnancy. Therefore, the signal conduction mediated by HIF-1α forms a structural bottleneck, which defines early preeclampsia. Once formed, this defect is basically irreversible.

Feedback amplification between shallow invasion and ischemia

The invasion of extravillous trophoblasts and placental ischemia are mutually reinforcing through a potent feedback loop. Insufficient trophoblast invasion increases uterine vascular resistance, limiting maternal blood flow. Reduced perfusion perpetuates placental hypoxia, which stabilizes HIF-1α. This stabilized transcription factor further suppresses trophoblast invasion, creating a self-sustaining cycle. As pregnancy advances, this feedback loop intensifies: rising fetal demands require progressively greater placental blood flow, but inadequately remodeled spiral arteries cannot meet these needs. Relative ischemia worsens, amplifying placental stress signals. HIF-1α drives increased expression of anti-angiogenic and pro-inflammatory mediators, which enter the maternal circulation and impair endothelial function. In this way, the placenta functions both as the origin and amplifier of pathology.

This feedback loop also explains the rapid clinical deterioration often seen in early-onset preeclampsia. Placental function may initially appear marginally sufficient, but small decreases in perfusion can suddenly exceed adaptive capacity. Once the ischemic threshold is crossed, HIF-1α signaling escalates rapidly, and maternal symptoms can progress swiftly. This dynamic behavior distinguishes early hair disease from slow-progressing late hair disease. Mitochondral dysfunction helps to amplify feedback [82,83]. Hypoxia can damage the mitochondrial respiration of trophoblast cells. The dysfunctional mitochondria produce excessive reactive oxygen. These molecules stabilize HIF-1α independently of oxygen supply. Therefore, even during a short period of reperfusion, oxidative stress will maintain hypoxia signals. The placenta has lost its ability to respond flexibly to environmental changes.

Epigenetic mechanisms further reinforce this feedback loop. Early hypoxic exposure alters the chromatin landscape of genes involved in trophoblast invasion, and these modifications are maintained through subsequent cell divisions. As a result, trophoblasts retain a maladaptive transcriptional program, preventing the placenta from resetting its developmental trajectory. This epigenetic “memory” helps explain why later interventions often fail to restore normal invasive capacity [84,85]. The reciprocal interaction between shallow trophoblast invasion and placental ischemia defines the core pathology of early-onset preeclampsia, with HIF-1α at the center of this cycle. This transcription factor integrates oxygen sensing, metabolic stress, and inflammatory signals, producing an output that inhibits placental maturation. Disrupting this cycle remains a major therapeutic challenge: interventions must be applied early to restore invasion or mitigate ischemic signaling, whereas later interventions contend with entrenched structural and epigenetic defects. This understanding underscores the critical importance of early detection and preventive strategies targeting placental biology. Consequently, HIF-1α–driven invasion defects represent not only a mechanistic insight but also an important clinical warning signal.

HIF-1α–driven mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress

Hypoxia-induced metabolic reprogramming in trophoblasts

Placental cells rely on flexible metabolic procedures to adapt to changes in oxygen supply. In early pregnancy, trophoblast cells tend to be glycolytic and metabolized to support rapid proliferation under hypoxic tension. This metabolic pattern is closely related to developmental hypoxia. HIF-1α is the key regulator of this process. The transcription factor induces gene expression to enhance glucose uptake and glycolysis flum [86,87]. These changes enable trophoblast cells to maintain the production of ATP while minimizing oxygen consumption. Under physiological conditions, this metabolic state is short-lived. Placental ischemia can change the duration of metabolic reprogramming. Persistent hypoxia will maintain the long-term activity of HIF-1α. Trophoblast cells still maintain the state of glycolysis after the normal development window period [88,89]. This persistence limits metabolic flexibility. With the progress of pregnancy, cells cannot change to balanced oxidative phosphorylation. Energy efficiency in the placenta declines, leading to lactic acid accumulation and local acidosis. These changes disrupt cellular homeostasis and impair trophoblast function.

Nutrient delivery to the ischemic placenta is also compromised. Reduced perfusion limits the supply of glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids. In response, HIF-1α shifts cellular priorities toward survival rather than growth-supporting metabolic pathways, redirecting resources to maintain basic cellular integrity. As a result, biosynthetic activity declines, impairing placental growth and vascular development. The mismatch between metabolic demand and nutrient supply further exacerbates placental insufficiency. Metabolic reprogramming also affects trophoblast differentiation. Extravillous trophoblasts rely on efficient mitochondrial function to support invasion and vascular remodeling, but prolonged dependence on glycolysis restricts this capacity. Consequently, cells remain in a metabolically immature state, which worsens defects in shallow invasion and spiral artery remodeling. Thus, HIF-1α serves as a critical link between hypoxia sensing, metabolic restriction, and structural placental pathology.

Imbalance between mitochondrial ROS generation and redox

Mitochondria are primary targets of hypoxia-induced stress in the ischemic placenta. Reduced oxygen availability impairs the efficiency of the electron transport chain, causing electrons to accumulate within mitochondrial respiratory complexes. This accumulation increases the leakage of electrons to molecular oxygen, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS). While moderate ROS levels function as signaling molecules in normal placental development, excessive ROS production leads to cellular damage [89,90]. Placental ischemia further amplifies mitochondrial ROS generation. Inadequate perfusion creates repeated cycles of hypoxia and reoxygenation, which destabilize the mitochondrial membrane. Reintroduction of oxygen into damaged mitochondria triggers bursts of ROS that overwhelm the antioxidant defense system. The resulting oxidative stress accumulates in trophoblast cells, with lipid peroxidation compromising membrane integrity, protein oxidation impairing enzymatic function, and mitochondrial DNA damage undermining respiratory capacity.

The HIF-1α signaling pathway is closely related to the redox equilibrium of mitochondria. ROS inhibits the activity of prolyl hydroxylase and stabilizes HIF-1α independently of oxygen tension. This effect can maintain the hypoxia signal even during a short period of reperfusion [90,91]. HIF-1α further suppresses mitochondrial biogenesis by downregulating transcriptional regulators of oxidative metabolism. This inhibition reduces both mitochondrial number and respiratory capacity, limiting ROS detoxification and exacerbating oxidative stress. The ischemic placenta also exhibits impaired mitochondrial quality control. Under normal conditions, mitophagy removes damaged mitochondria to maintain cellular health, but persistent hypoxia disrupts this process. As a result, dysfunctional mitochondria accumulate in trophoblasts, continuously generating ROS and establishing a self-perpetuating cycle of oxidative damage. Cellular stress is thus amplified, and the effects of redox imbalance extend beyond trophoblasts. Fused trophoblasts release oxidative stress signals into the maternal circulation, where ROS and lipid peroxides induce systemic endothelial dysfunction, reduce nitric oxide bioavailability, increase vasoconstriction, and elevate blood pressure. In this way, mitochondrial ROS link placental pathology to maternal disease.

Oxidative stress as an amplifier of placental injury

Oxidative stress acts as a potent amplifier of placental injury in early-onset preeclampsia. ROS modify signaling proteins and transcription factors, altering cellular responses to hypoxia. HIF-1α becomes more stable and transcriptionally active, driving enhanced expression of stress-related genes [92,93]. Anti-angiogenic mediators such as sFlt-1 are upregulated, while angiogenic signaling weakens, further impairing placental perfusion. Excess ROS also affect trophoblast survival and differentiation. High ROS levels activate apoptosis pathways, increasing cell turnover and disrupting placental villous structure. Reduced trophoblast renewal impairs barrier function and nutrient transport, diminishing overall placental efficiency and accelerating functional decline.

Under oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling is amplified. ROS activate redox-sensitive pathways such as NF-κB, increasing cytokine production. Inflammation further disrupts trophoblast behavior and vascular remodeling, and immune cell infiltration intensifies. The placental microenvironment becomes hostile to normal development. This inflammatory state synergizes with hypoxic signals to maintain pathological HIF-1α activation. ROS also affect epigenetic regulation by altering DNA methylation and histone modifications [94,95], impacting genes involved in metabolism, invasion, and angiogenesis. These epigenetic changes persist, allowing placental cells to retain a “memory” of oxidative damage, explaining why dysfunction continues even after temporary improvements in perfusion.

Hypoxia, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress form a tightly interconnected pathogenic network, with HIF-1α at its center. This transcription factor integrates oxygen sensing, metabolic cues, and redox signals, producing an adaptive response that prioritizes survival over function. Placental development stalls, structural defects persist, and initial perfusion deficits can trigger disproportionate molecular responses. Oxidative stress amplifies hypoxic signaling [96], mitochondrial dysfunction sustains redox imbalance, anti-angiogenic products increase, and maternal endothelial damage ensues. The placenta thus becomes a driver of systemic maternal disease. Targeting oxidative stress and mitochondrial health represents a potential therapeutic strategy. Restoring redox balance can limit excessive HIF-1α activation, while improving mitochondrial function enhances trophoblast resilience. Timing is critical: early intervention offers the greatest potential, as accumulated oxidative and epigenetic damage substantially reduces reversibility.

HIF-1α–mediated angiogenic dysregulation

Transcriptional induction of sFlt-1 and antiangiogenic programs

Vasogenic balance plays a central role in maintaining the health of the placenta and maternal blood vessels. Normal pregnancy depends on the coordinated signal conduction between VEGF, PLGF and their endothelial receptors. This signal conduction supports endothelial integrity, vasodilation and placental vascular development. Premature pre-eclampsia fundamentally destroys this balance. Ischemic placenta tends to be in an anti-angiogenic state. HIF-1α is the key transcription driver of this transformation. Persistent hypoxia makes HIF-1α stable in the nucleus of the trophoblast. The transcription factor directly activates the FLT1 gene, which encodes the membrane-bound VEGFR-1 and its soluble isomer sFlt-1 [21,97]. Under hypoxic stress, selective splicing is conducive to the production of soluble forms. The increase in sFlt-1 secretion has become a prominent feature of premature disease. The cyclic sFlt-1 binds VEGF and PLGF with high affinity. Therefore, the signal conduction of functional angiogenesis is weakened.

Ischemic placenta produces a large amount of sFlt-1. The main source is the fusion trophoblast cells. Continuous HIF-1α activity increases the release of transcription products and proteins. Inflammatory signal conduction and oxidative stress further aggravate this process. The placenta becomes a major endocrine organ, releasing anti-angiogenic signals into the mother's blood. This release links local placental stress with systemic vascular dysfunction. HIF-1α also induces other anti-angiogenic mediators. The expression of soluble endothelin increased synchronously with sFlt-1 [71,98]. This molecule will interfere with the transmission of TGF-β signals in endothelial cells. The combined effect of a variety of anti-angiogenic factors aggravates endothelial damage. The angiogenic environment has become unfavorable. The maternal blood vessels lose their adaptability. The blood pressure regulation function deteriorates. The time point of this transcriptional transformation explains the severity of the disease. Early continuous HIF-1α activation will produce a high level of sFlt-1 long before clinical symptoms appear. Maternal endothelial cells are exposed to high levels of sFlt-1 for a long time. The compensation mechanism is exhausted. This early exposure distinguishes between early preeclampsia and late preeclampsia, which develops more slowly.

Inhibition of VEGF and PLGF signaling pathways

The increase of anti-angiogenic media leads to effective inhibition of VEGF and PLGF signal transmission in the parent tissue. Endothelial cells need VEGF signal conduction to maintain the production of nitric oxide, the stability of the cytoskeleton and the integrity of the barrier. sFlt-1 neutralized VEGF and PLGF in the cycle before the receptor binds [21,99]. Therefore, the signal conduction of endothelial cells is weakened. Vascular tension tends to contract. Decreased VEGF signal conduction will change the metabolism and survival of endothelial cells. Endothelial cells can't get survival-promoting signals. Increased susceptibility to cell apoptosis. The connection between cells is unstable. Increased capillary permeability. These changes directly lead to proteinuria, edema and vascular leakage. The kidney is a particularly sensitive target organ. Glomerular endothelial cells rely heavily on VEGF provided by foot cells. sFlt-1 will destroy this paracrine interaction.

The inhibition of PLGF further aggravates vascular dysfunction. PLGF usually supports placental angiogenesis and endothelial repair. The absence of PLGF signal conduction limits compensatory vascular growth [100,101]. The placenta cannot effectively expand its microvascular network. Placental perfusion decreased further. This decline will in turn aggravate ischemia and the activation of HIF-1α. Therefore, angiogenic defects will form a vicious circle. VEGF pathway inhibition will also affect the mother's heart and cerebral vascular system. The stiffness of endothelial cells increases. Vasodilation reserve is reduced. The self-regulation function of cerebral blood vessels is weakened. These effects can lead to neurological symptoms such as headaches and visual impairment. Severe inhibition will increase the risk of eclampsia. Due to systemic vasoconstriction, the heart load increases. Long-term vascular remodeling may last until postpartum. The degree of inhibition of VEGF and PLGF is related to the severity of the disease. The sFlt-1/PLGF ratio of patients with premature preeclampsia is higher than that of patients with late eclampsia. The ratio reflects the degree of angiogenesis imbalance. This ratio provides a biochemical index of HIF-1α-driven transcriptional activity. The measured value reflects the downstream effect of placental ischemia on the maternal vascular system.

Systemic transmission of angiogenic imbalance to the mother

The placenta transmits ischemic stress signals to the mother through the circulating angiogenic factor. sFlt-1 is the main messenger molecule. After sFlt-1 is released into the mother's blood, it will play a wide role on the endothelial surface. This molecule is not limited to the uterine placental unit. General exposure can lead to a wide range of vascular effects. Maternal endothelial cells respond quickly to angiogenesis imbalance. The bioavailability of nitric oxide decreased, the vasoconstriction pathway dominated, and blood pressure increased [102]. Kidney endothelial damage leads to glomerular dysfunction and proteinuria. Hepatic sinus endothelial cells are prone to ischemic damage, liver enzyme levels are elevated, and platelet activation is enhanced. These characteristics constitute maternal syndrome in pre-eclampsia.

The systemic characteristics of unbalanced angiogenesis explain the phenomenon of multiple organ involvement in early preeclampsia. The placenta releases a single molecular signal, which acts on different vascular beds. Organ-specific performance reflects local susceptibility. The kidney, brain, liver and heart are most affected. This model supports the view that preeclampsia is a vascular disease driven by placental signals. HIF-1α is located upstream of this systemic cascading reaction [103]. The transcription factor integrates hypoxia, inflammation and metabolic stress in the placenta. This integration reaction increases the production of anti-angiogenic factors. Maternal symptoms are downstream consequences, not primary defects. This hierarchical relationship explains why the removal of the placenta after childbirth can quickly improve the maternal condition. Removing the signal source can restore the balance of angiogenesis.

The whole body transmission model also explains the time dynamics. Imbalanced angiogenesis may be compensated for a period of time. The maternal endothelial reserve can cushion early stress. Progressive exposure will overwhelm the compensation mechanism and cause symptoms to appear suddenly. This kind of delayed attack often surprises clinicians. Biomarkers can capture this asymptomatic stage. The increase in sFlt-1 levels occurs before hypertension or proteinuria. It is of great clinical significance to understand the mechanism of systemic transmission. Therapy to reduce the production or neutralization of sFlt-1 can stabilize the maternal vascular system. These methods aim to block the signal pathway from the placenta to the mother. The timing is crucial. Early intervention has the greatest potential benefits. Therefore, HIF-1α-mediated angiogenesis disorder is the core mechanism that connects placental ischemia with maternal disease. This pathway explains the severity of the disease, the time of onset and the scope of systemic impact. This understanding supports the development of risk stratification and targeted therapeutic drugs based on biomarkers. Continuous research on angiogenesis regulation may change the treatment of preeclampsia (Fig. 4).

HIF-1α–sFlt-1–mediated anti-angiogenic signaling links placental hypoxia to maternal endothelial dysfunction in early-onset preeclampsia. Placental ischemia/hypoxia sustains HIF-1α activity and promotes FLT1/sFlt-1 upregulation, sequestering VEGF/PLGF and weakening endothelial survival and barrier signaling, with concomitant oxidative stress and inflammatory activation. Endothelial apoptosis, junction instability, and increased capillary permeability contribute to the multisystem maternal syndrome, including hypertension, cerebral edema/visual impairment, proteinuria, and liver dysfunction.

Integration of hypoxia, immune activation, and endothelial injury

HIF-1α regulation of placental inflammatory responses

Placental ischemia creates a microenvironment that favors sustained inflammatory activation. Hypoxic stress alters trophoblast signaling and modulates immune cell behavior, with HIF-1α playing a central coordinating role. This transcription factor directly regulates genes involved in innate immunity and cytokine production. Under prolonged hypoxia, trophoblasts upregulate TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, reshaping the placental immune environment. In contrast to the controlled inflammation required for normal pregnancy—supporting implantation and vascular remodeling [104,105]-early-onset preeclampsia disrupts this balance. HIF-1α promotes a shift toward a pro-inflammatory state, causing trophoblasts to adopt a stress-response phenotype rather than fulfilling developmental functions, thereby exacerbating local tissue injury.

Placental immune cells are highly responsive to hypoxia-driven signals. Macrophages and uterine natural killer (uNK) cells typically support trophoblast invasion and spiral artery remodeling, but sustained HIF-1α activation alters the cytokine gradients that guide their function. Immune cells adopt a pro-inflammatory phenotype, diminishing their supportive role, increasing cytotoxicity, and amplifying oxidative stress, collectively impairing placental vascular adaptability. HIF-1α also enhances inflammasome-related pathways, with hypoxic stress upregulating inflammasome components and damage-associated molecular patterns [77,106]. These signals trigger innate immune cascades in trophoblasts and resident immune cells, driving chronic sterile inflammation. As long as ischemic signaling persists, this inflammatory state continues. Cytokine-mediated NF-κB activation further stabilizes and amplifies HIF-1α transcription, reinforcing the pathological signaling loop. Inflammation and hypoxia thus potentiate each other, locking the placenta in a self-sustaining stress response that limits functional recovery.

Hypoxic-immune interaction in the placental microenvironment

The placental microenvironment is a dynamic interface between fetal and maternal tissues, shaped profoundly by hypoxia, which influences immune cell recruitment and activation. Persistent ischemia alters chemokine gradients, attracting inflammatory rather than regulatory immune cells, thereby shifting placental cellular composition. HIF-1α regulates chemokine expression, promoting the infiltration of monocytes and neutrophils. These immune cells generate ROS and proteases [107,108], intensifying local oxidative stress and causing cumulative structural damage to villous tissue.

Hypoxia–immune interactions also affect adaptive immunity. Inflammatory conditions enhance antigen presentation and reduce regulatory T cell function, tilting immune balance toward non-tolerance and heightening maternal immune activation at the placental interface. Endothelial cells further mediate immune crosstalk: hypoxia increases adhesion molecule expression, promoting leukocyte attachment and vascular inflammation, which exacerbates blood flow deficits and ischemia. In response, the placenta alters its secretory profile. Extracellular vesicles from hypoxic trophoblasts carry inflammatory mediators and microRNAs into the maternal circulation, transmitting placental stress signals systemically. This positions the placenta as a signaling hub, disseminating local inflammation beyond the uterine environment. The interplay between hypoxia and immune activation explains why early-onset preeclampsia exhibits both vascular and inflammatory pathologies. HIF-1α integrates these signals and amplifies downstream effects.

Maternal endothelial dysfunction and severe clinical syndrome

Maternal vascular endothelium is the ultimate target of placental stress signals. Circulating anti-angiogenic and inflammatory mediators—including sFlt-1, cytokines, and oxidative molecules—disrupt endothelial homeostasis [109,110]. Endothelial cells lose survival and vasodilatory signals, impairing vascular tone regulation. Clinically, this manifests as hypertension, proteinuria, and multi-organ damage. Reduced nitric oxide bioavailability promotes vasoconstriction, while increased vascular permeability leads to plasma protein leakage. Renal glomerular endothelium is particularly vulnerable, causing proteinuria. The liver and brain are also affected: hepatic endothelial dysfunction reduces sinusoidal perfusion and damages hepatocytes, elevating liver enzymes and activating platelets; cerebral endothelial injury compromises blood-brain barrier integrity and autoregulation, leading to headache, visual disturbances, or seizures—hallmarks of severe early-onset preeclampsia.

The immune component exacerbates endothelial damage. Circulating inflammatory mediators activate endothelial cells, upregulating adhesion molecules and coagulation factors, which may promote microvascular thrombosis and further reduce tissue perfusion, accelerating organ injury. This cascade explains the rapid deterioration observed in some patients. HIF-1α centrally orchestrates this systemic signaling [111], transmitting placental ischemic signals through circulating mediators. Endothelial dysfunction is thus a downstream consequence rather than the primary cause, which accounts for the rapid maternal symptom resolution following placental delivery. Removing the source of ischemic signaling restores vascular balance.

The integration of hypoxia, immune activation, and endothelial injury determines the severity of early-onset preeclampsia. The placenta coordinates this integration via HIF-1α–dependent pathways, with the resulting maternal syndrome reflecting systemic consequences of local placental dysfunction. Understanding this integration identifies potential intervention points: strategies that reduce placental inflammation or protect endothelial function may slow disease progression [112]. This framework also supports biomarker development: circulating markers of inflammation, angiogenic imbalance, and endothelial activation reflect disease activity, enabling early monitoring and intervention. Early detection remains the most effective opportunity to prevent irreversible maternal and fetal injury. The complex interplay of hypoxia, immunity, and vascular dysfunction underscores HIF-1α′s role as a molecular coordinator. Continued research into this integrated network may reveal novel therapeutic approaches and improve outcomes in affected pregnancies.

Therapeutic implications of targeting HIF-1α pathways

Modulation of placental hypoxia and oxidative stress

Therapeutic strategies targeting placental hypoxia aim to reduce upstream stress signals that stabilize HIF-1α (Fig. 5). Early-onset preeclampsia arises from inadequate uteroplacental perfusion, making interventions to improve oxygen delivery in early pregnancy a rational preventive approach. Enhancing uterine blood flow can limit persistent hypoxia and dampen downstream molecular cascades. These strategies emphasize prevention rather than reversal.

Therapeutic strategies targeting HIF-1α pathway in early-onset preeclampsia. The potential therapeutic approaches aim to modulate HIF-1α signaling. Strategies include enhancing uterine blood flow, antioxidant therapy, reducing circulating sFlt - 1 levels, and molecular therapies targeting angiogenesis and HIF-1α expression.

Low-dose aspirin exemplifies upstream modulation [113,114]. Aspirin improves placental perfusion by supporting early spiral artery remodeling and reducing platelet aggregation. It also inhibits inflammatory signaling and oxidative stress, indirectly limiting sustained HIF-1α activation. Clinical studies demonstrate that initiating aspirin in early pregnancy reduces the incidence of early-onset preeclampsia, with timing coinciding with the placenta's most vulnerable developmental window.

Antioxidant strategies have also attracted attention. Excessive ROS amplify hypoxic signaling and stabilize HIF-1α; restoring redox balance can attenuate this amplification. Experimental models indicate that improving mitochondrial function can suppress pathological HIF-1α signaling. Mitochondria-targeted antioxidants are theoretically advantageous, as they address the source of oxidative stress, although clinical translation remains limited due to safety concerns in early pregnancy.

Metabolic regulation represents another upstream approach. Activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) enhances mitochondrial efficiency, reduces oxidative stress, and modulates HIF-1α transcriptional activity. Interventions that improve metabolic flexibility may increase placental adaptability under hypoxic conditions. Nutritional and metabolic strategies require careful evaluation, as placental development relies on precise metabolic control.

Collectively, these upstream strategies share a common goal: preventing excessive stabilization of HIF-1α by optimizing the placental microenvironment. Once structural and epigenetic changes are established, reversibility diminishes, highlighting the critical importance of early intervention.

Targeted downstream angiogenesis imbalance

Downstream therapeutic strategies aim to mitigate the consequences of HIF-1α–induced angiogenic dysregulation (Fig. 5). Excessive sFlt-1 production is a central mediator of maternal disease; reducing circulating sFlt-1 or neutralizing its effects directly protects maternal endothelium [21,98]. Plasma-based removal of sFlt-1 provides proof-of-concept: selective adsorption techniques lower circulating sFlt-1, temporarily improving blood pressure and renal function, and in some cases prolonging pregnancy. These findings confirm sFlt-1's pathogenic role but are limited by high cost, procedural complexity, and the need for repeated treatments, restricting use to severe cases.

Molecular therapies offer a more targeted approach. siRNA-mediated inhibition of placental sFlt-1 reduces its production at the source. Preclinical studies show that silencing sFlt-1 transcripts restores angiogenic balance without disrupting essential VEGF signaling. Placenta-targeted delivery systems minimize fetal exposure, representing a key step toward mechanism-based therapy, though clinical translation remains early. Pharmacological modulation of angiogenesis, including the use of statins, is under investigation. Statins exhibit pleiotropic effects, improving endothelial function and reducing inflammation; experimental models suggest they may lower sFlt-1 production indirectly via HIF-1α and oxidative stress pathways [115]. Early clinical trial results are mixed, with safety, dosage, timing, and patient selection remaining critical considerations.

Restoring VEGF and PLGF signaling is another downstream strategy to counter systemic vascular effects. Approaches using modified ligands or receptor-specific targeting may offer safer options, though these remain experimental. Each of these strategies highlights the challenge of correcting angiogenic imbalance without disrupting physiological vascular regulation. Downstream therapies primarily alleviate maternal symptoms rather than addressing the root placental pathology. They can stabilize maternal condition and prolong pregnancy, but cannot fully resolve placental dysfunction. Combining upstream interventions—targeting hypoxia, oxidative stress, and metabolism—with downstream approaches may produce synergistic effects, offering a more comprehensive strategy for managing early-onset preeclampsia.

Future directions and translational perspectives

Systems-level mapping of HIF-1α regulatory networks

Future advances in preeclampsia research will rely on a systematic understanding of the HIF-1α regulatory network. Single-pathway models are insufficient to capture the complexity of placental ischemia signals. The ischemic placenta activates interconnected pathways involving hypoxia sensing, metabolism, inflammation, angiogenesis, and epigenetic regulation. Integrated analysis can reveal how these pathways interact dynamically over time. Systems biology approaches provide tools to reconstruct these interactions and identify key regulatory nodes. Multi-omics technologies have laid the groundwork for this work: Transcriptomics defines the HIF-1α–regulated hypoxic response gene program; Proteomics quantifies downstream mediators that shape placental function and maternal response; Metabolomics reveals changes in energy utilization and redox balance; Epigenomics identifies persistent chromatin modifications that lock trophoblast cells into maladaptive states. By integrating these layers, researchers can move beyond descriptive studies to construct causal network models.

Computational modeling complements experimental data [116,117]. Network-based simulations can predict how disturbances in oxygen delivery alter HIF-1α activity and downstream signaling, identifying critical thresholds where adaptive responses shift to pathological pathways. Such predictions can guide experimental design and prioritize therapeutic targets. A systems-level framework also facilitates translational applications, particularly in biomarker discovery. Network analysis can identify combinations of markers that reflect pathway activation, rather than isolated signals, improving early detection of placental ischemia. The objective is to capture disease trajectories over time, rather than relying on static measurements. Therefore, system-wide mapping of the HIF-1α network represents a critical next step for both mechanistic insight and clinical application.

Accurate phenotype analysis of premature preeclampsia

Preeclampsia is a heterogeneous disorder, with diverse molecular drivers among patients. Accurate phenotype analysis aims to classify disease subtypes based on underlying biological mechanisms rather than clinical features alone. The HIF-1α–centered signaling axis provides a logical framework for this classification. Variations in hypoxia severity, inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and angiogenic output can define distinct phenotypes [118]. Biological stratification requires integration of angiogenic biomarkers, imaging data, and maternal risk factors. For example: High sFlt-1/PLGF ratios may indicate an angiogenesis-dominant phenotype; Elevated inflammatory markers may point to an immune-driven subtype; Metabolic signatures may highlight mitochondrial vulnerability. These models inform individualized monitoring and intervention strategies.

Phenotype-based stratification is also critical for therapeutic research. Patients with predominantly hypoxia-driven pathology may benefit from upstream interventions to enhance placental perfusion, whereas those with pronounced angiogenic imbalance may respond better to downstream sFlt-1–targeted therapies. Tailored treatment improves efficacy while minimizing unnecessary exposure, echoing the precision medicine frameworks established in oncology and cardiology [119]. Longitudinal analysis further strengthens phenotype characterization. Serial biomarker measurements throughout pregnancy reveal disease trajectories: early deviations may predict rapid progression, while stable patterns suggest compensatory adaptation. Such information guides the timing of interventions and delivery planning. Accurate phenotype analysis thus advances mechanistic understanding and enhances clinical decision-making, laying the foundation for precision management of early-onset preeclampsia.

Conclusion

Pre-eclampsia is fundamentally a placental disorder rooted in early developmental adaptation failure. This review emphasizes HIF-1α as the central molecular regulator linking early placental ischemia to downstream pathological cascades. During normal pregnancy, tightly controlled hypoxia supports trophoblast proliferation and placental formation. HIF-1α activity is transient at this stage, consistent with normal developmental programming. In pre-eclampsia, the placenta fails to exit this hypoxic program. Continuous HIF-1α stabilization shifts placental signaling from adaptive to maladaptive. Persistent activation of HIF-1α alters multiple aspects of placental biology [58,120], including inhibition of trophoblast invasion and disruption of spiral artery remodeling. These defects produce high-resistance placental perfusion, chronic ischemia, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and dominant inflammatory signaling. HIF-1α integrates these pathways into a unified stress response. This model explains the fundamental distinction between early- and late-onset pre-eclampsia: structural defects established in early pregnancy cannot be fully corrected later, and the placenta acts as the primary driver rather than a passive victim. HIF-1α is thus more than a marker of hypoxia—it functions as a decision-making node that determines placental fate.

A key contribution of this review is linking placental ischemia to systemic maternal disease. Continuous HIF-1α activation drives potent anti-angiogenic programs in trophoblasts [121], increasing sFlt-1 and related mediators, which disrupt VEGF and PLGF signaling. These signals propagate beyond the placenta, impairing maternal vascular endothelium. Angiogenic imbalance explains hallmark features of early pre-eclampsia. Endothelial dysfunction underlies hypertension, proteinuria, and multi-organ injury, with the kidney, liver, brain, and cardiovascular system displaying differential sensitivity. The placenta generates a singular molecular signal, while maternal vasculature interprets it in tissue-specific ways, culminating in systemic syndrome.

This framework reconciles previously independent clinical observations. Maternal deterioration often follows a prolonged silent phase; angiogenic biomarkers rise before symptoms; and rapid postpartum recovery reflects removal of the placental signal source. These findings align with the HIF-1α–dependent model of placental-to-maternal signal transduction and angiogenesis disorder [69]. Early and sustained exposure to anti-angiogenic factors overwhelms maternal compensatory mechanisms. In contrast, late-onset disease progresses more slowly due to weaker or delayed placental signaling. Recognizing these differences allows earlier risk stratification and more precise monitoring. This integrated perspective also highlights treatment opportunities, targeting upstream hypoxic signaling, downstream angiogenic imbalance, or maternal endothelial function. Effective therapy may require a coordinated, multi-level strategy guided by biological insights.

The analysis presented underscores a clear path for precision obstetrics centered on placental biology. The HIF-1α–centered pathway provides a coherent framework to explain disease heterogeneity. Not all early-onset pre-eclampsia cases share the same molecular drivers; variations in hypoxia severity, inflammatory activation, and metabolic dysfunction define distinct subtypes. Future research must embrace this complexity. Advances in multi-omics, imaging, and computational modeling will accelerate progress. Systems-level approaches can identify regulatory nodes that govern disease progression, which can serve as biomarkers or therapeutic targets. Early identification of high-risk pregnancies remains a priority, as interventions before irreversible placental damage offer the greatest potential benefit.

Clinical translation requires both caution and innovation. Placental development relies on precise signaling balance. Targeting HIF-1α must limit pathological activation while preserving physiological hypoxia, with timing being critical. Early pregnancy represents both a therapeutic opportunity and a challenge, necessitating careful ethical and safety considerations. Despite these constraints, the potential impact is substantial. Focusing on HIF-1α–mediated placental ischemia fundamentally reshapes our understanding of early pre-eclampsia, shifting emphasis from late clinical manifestations to early developmental disorders. This paradigm opens new avenues for prevention, monitoring, and targeted intervention.

In summary, early-onset pre-eclampsia reflects placental adaptive failure driven by sustained HIF-1α activation. The resulting cascade links hypoxia, inflammation, metabolism, angiogenesis, and endothelial dysfunction into a unified disease model. Ongoing integration of basic science and clinical research will determine whether this knowledge can effectively improve maternal and fetal outcomes. The placenta now stands at the center of both mechanistic understanding and therapeutic strategy.
