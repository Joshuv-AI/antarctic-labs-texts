Revised mechanism of hydroxyurea-induced cell cycle arrest and an improved alternative
Authors: Alisa E. Shaw, Mattias N. Mihelich, Jackson E. Whitted, Hannah J. Reitman, Adam J. Timmerman, Muhammad Tehseen, Samir M. Hamdan, Grant D. Schauer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceHydroxyurea (HU) is a drug used in the clinic but also as a tool to stall cells in their cell cycle. Among other research questions and applications, researchers arrest cells to understand how they respond to challenges during DNA replication. It was thought that HU primarily works by depleting the nucleotides necessary for replicating DNA, but we show that the drug also works by producing copious reactive oxygen species (ROS), which directly inhibit DNA polymerase complexes by oxidizing iron–sulfur (Fe-S) clusters in DNA polymerases, causing them to disassemble, rendering them unable to bind DNA. We also provide the community with engineered yeast cells that are a superior and less toxic alternative to HU for inducing cell cycle arrest.

DNA replication is plagued by a multitude of challenges, collectively known as replication stress, which threaten genomic stability and place cells at risk of mutagenesis and/or death. Physical barriers to normal replication in the S-phase are numerous and include endogenous obstacles like DNA secondary structural elements and DNA-bound proteins, as well as DNA lesions directly caused by genotoxins including metabolic intermediates, exogenous chemicals, and ultraviolet (UV) light, to name a few (1, 2). Another important source of replication stress includes the depletion of deoxynucleotide triphosphate (dNTP) pools, causing polymerase stalling during elongation—accordingly, dNTP levels are strictly regulated during the S-phase (1, 3–6). One common thread in replication stress is the occurrence of helicase–polymerase uncoupling: The eukaryotic helicase CMG (Cdc45, Mcm2-7, GINS) encircles the leading strand and normally forms a stable complex with the leading strand Polymerase (Pol) ε (7, 8), but this complex rapidly separates upon Pol ε stalling (9, 10). This functional uncoupling of helicase and polymerase activity has been shown to be the primary activator of the S-phase replication checkpoint, a pathway that has evolved to stabilize replication forks in the presence of replication stress.

In the S-phase checkpoint response, Mec1, the primary replication checkpoint sensor kinase in budding yeast (homologous to ATR in humans) is activated by stretches of Replication Protein A (RPA)-coated single-stranded DNA resulting from runaway CMG helicase (1, 11–14). Activated Mec1 is recruited to replication forks to phosphorylate Mrc1, the Mediator of the Replication Checkpoint (Claspin in humans), a mediator that travels with the replisome during elongation and which is required for both normal replication speed and checkpoint activation (15–22). When activated by the checkpoint, Mrc1 helps Mec1 activate the effector kinase Rad53, which slows and stabilizes replication forks with the help of the Tof1/Csm3 pausing complex (Tipin/Timeless in humans) by phosphorylating CMG helicase and Mrc1 itself (23–28) (Fig. 1A). Mrc1 null yeast strains are viable but progress through the S-phase slowly and demonstrate increased sensitivity to chemically induced replication stress (23). Interestingly, the roles of Mrc1 in replication speed and checkpoint signal transduction appear to be separable: Yeast expressing only Mrc1AQ, a version of Mrc1 containing Ala mutations of all 17 SQ/TQ residues [a motif demonstrated to be the primary target of the Mec1 family (29)], were shown to be completely nonfunctional in S-phase checkpoint activation while maintaining a normal S-phase duration (15). Thus, yeast strains bearing mrc1AQ can be used to study the effects of replication stress under surveillance of a disabled replication checkpoint apparatus with otherwise fully functional replication and cell cycle progression.

Hydroxyurea (HU) is a chemotherapeutic in clinical use and is to date the most widely utilized drug to induce cell cycle arrest in the laboratory. HU usage is particularly prevalent in budding yeast, where concentrations up to 0.2 M are routinely used to achieve full cell cycle arrest. The primary mechanism of HU is considered to be an inhibition of ribonucleotide reductase (RNR), the enzyme complex responsible for converting nucleotide diphosphates (NDPs) to deoxynucleotide diphosphates (dNDPs), which are required to produce deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs) (30, 31). RNR is a heterotetramer composed of a dimeric large catalytic subunit and a dimeric small regulatory subunit: Rnr1 is the main catalytic subunit, while Rnr3 is a nonessential paralog catalytic subunit; both these large subunits require the small subunits, Rnr2 and/or Rnr4 for activity (32). Inhibition of RNR by HU has been demonstrated to occur by electron transfer from HU to a catalytically requisite diferric tyrosyl radical center, thus limiting dNTP pools and stalling polymerases (31–35). However, there remain unexplained phenomena surrounding this mechanism as a primary source of cell cycle arrest. For instance, it was observed that HU can still arrest replication forks in archaea which use a rare and separate RNR class that does not depend on this tyrosyl radical, without reducing dNTP pools (36). Further, it was shown in budding yeast, which cannot use nucleotide salvage pathways and thus strictly require RNR for dNTP supply, that HU does not significantly reduce the basal pools of dNTP (retaining ~79% of G1 dNTP levels) and that HU can arrest rad53 mutants without exhausting dNTPs (retaining ~69% of G1 dNTP levels) such that yeast would have to sense minor changes in dNTP levels and halt replication elongation through an as yet undescribed mechanism (37). A further complication to the usage of HU to induce replication arrest has been increasingly described in the literature, whereby HU results in the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as H2O2, possibly caused by radical chain reactions initiated by its hydroxylamine group (33, 38, 39). ROS can be teratogenic by directly damaging DNA, and DNA oxidation accumulates during HU administration. These effects can be partially reversed by antioxidants (40–42). Besides being linked broadly to oxidative stress, many other potential targets of HU have been discovered, including catalase, carbonic anhydrase, and matrix metalloproteinases. It is indeed progressively more understood that ROS and its potential downstream targets are responsible for much if not most of the acute cytotoxicity of HU (33, 43, 44).

Increasingly, iron–sulfur (Fe-S) clusters have been discovered in various protein structures: Hundreds of Fe-S-containing proteins have been described in humans and yeast, including many enzymes required for DNA metabolism. Fe-S clusters most frequently appear in the form of [4Fe-4S], a cubane-type arrangement of four iron ions and four sulfide ions tightly coordinated by four negatively charged thiolate sulfurs of surrounding cysteine residues. Although the precise role(s) that these Fe-S clusters play are not fully understood for every protein, it is established that they are often enzymatic cofactors, can separately play roles in protein complex stability, and are broadly sensitive to the redox state of the cell (45–47). Intriguingly, it has been shown that primase regulatory proteins (Pri2 in yeast and p58 in humans), part of the 4-subunit Pol α holoenzyme responsible for synthesizing RNA/DNA primers de novo during replication, possess a Fe-S cluster in their C-terminal domain (CTD) (48–53), and there is evidence that the Fe-S cluster in the human primase may act as a redox switch for DNA binding and primer handoff (54). It was demonstrated by Fe incorporation in vivo and electron paramagnetic spectroscopy in vitro (55) that all three eukaryotic replicative polymerases (Pols α, δ, and ε) contain an integral Fe-S cluster in their CTD, coordinated by a cysteine-rich metal-binding motif called CysB (55) and an adjacent motif, called CysA, is thought to coordinate Zn2+ by default; although whether CysB in Pols α and Pol ε coordinate an Fe-S cluster or a Zn2+ metal remains unclear (46). An integral Fe-S cluster was recently observed in the N-terminal domain (NTD) of Pol2 (the polymerase subunit of Pol ε, the 4-subunit leading strand polymerase), coordinated by the CysX motif (56), and mutation of this domain was observed to abrogate polymerase and exonuclease activity by disruption of DNA binding (57). Further, disruption of the Fe-S coordination by CysB mutation in the CTD of Pol3 (the polymerase subunit of Pol δ, the 3-subunit lagging strand Pol) destabilized the complex and similarly compromised both its polymerase and exonuclease activities (55, 58). The effect on Pol δ was also observed in vivo by Vernis et al., where mutation of its CTD CysB domain resulted in genome instability (59), corroborating earlier results from a genetic screen (60). This group has also demonstrated that HU activates the oxidative stress response pathway, which attenuates the deleterious oxidative effects of HU in part by upregulation of two critical genes involved in Fe-S biosynthesis, and additionally that H2O2 inhibits Leu1, an isopropylmalate isomerase containing a Fe-S cluster (61); these findings collectively led to speculation that various stress and/or anticancer drugs could be responsible for similar effects by oxidizing Fe-S clusters in cells (59, 61).

Mrc1 knockouts in yeast exhibit slow progression through the S-phase compared to WT and are sensitive to HU (15, 23) as we also observe (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). However, mrc1AQ cells, in which Mrc1 has been replaced by the Mrc1AQ mutant, are defective in checkpoint activation and are significantly less sensitive to HU compared to mrc1Δ cells, but unlike mrc1Δ cells still exhibit timely progression through the S-phase (15). During replication stress, Rad53 is responsible for recovery from replication stress by stabilizing stalled forks and preventing late origin firing (11, 62–64), but is also increasingly observed to arrest elongation on both the leading and lagging strands by directly targeting fork components (27, 28, 65). It has been previously shown that rad53 mutants can be arrested by HU, raising the possibility that cell cycle arrest by HU might occur independent of Mec1/Rad53 signaling (37, 63). However, Rad53-deficient cells are profoundly affected beyond a simple checkpoint deficient phenotype, and it was also recently reported that Rad53 acts as a transcription factor that can bind promoters independent of Mrc1 signaling (66), making it possible that altered expression underlies this observation. Conversely, it was recently reported that mrc1AQ cells were permissive through the S-phase when released from G1 into 50 mM HU (67), so whether the replication checkpoint is required for cell cycle arrest in HU remains unclear. Since mrc1AQ cells are unable to activate Rad53 and stabilize replication forks during replication stress (15, 26), and since basal dNTP levels are largely preserved during HU treatment (37), our working hypothesis was that mrc1AQ cells would be unable to stall in the presence of HU as they lack the ability to orchestrate global fork arrest via the S-phase checkpoint (Fig. 1A). Instead, we observed HU-induced cell cycle arrest in yeast independent of the replication checkpoint and/or oxidative signaling mediated by Mrc1 (Results). In an effort to explain this result, we probed whether disruption of the various integral Fe-S clusters in eukaryotic Pol complexes could be responsible for the chemically induced replication arrest we observed in the absence of programmed fork stalling via Mrc1 signaling. We therefore sought to quantify the extent to which HU induces ROS formation in yeast nuclei, testing the prediction that HU and/or H2O2 may partially inhibit the eukaryotic replicative Pols α, δ, and ε. During our attempt to understand the off-target effects of HU, we also developed a superior system in budding yeast called RNR-deg that directly targets the RNR apparatus with greatly improved specificity.

Results

Cell Cycle Arrest by HU Independent of the S-Phase Checkpoint and Psk1 Signaling.

To test our hypothesis that mrc1AQ cells treated with HU would be permissive through the S-phase, we synchronized WT or mrc1AQ cells in G1 with alpha factor (αF) and released them into media containing 0 or 200 mM HU, assessing cell cycle state by flow cytometry. Surprisingly, we found that cells exposed to HU were arrested at the G1/S interface (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A; scatter plots for this experiment and representative gating criteria used for all cytometry studies are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S3), so we titrated HU into asynchronous WT or mrc1AQ cells growing in YPD to see whether the stalling we observed was concentration dependent. We observed reasonable agreement with Duch et al. at 50 mM HU, the maximum used in their study: mrc1AQ cells appeared to be partially permissive through the S-phase. However, at higher HU concentrations, we observed a distinct accumulation of cells at the G1/S interface (Fig. 1B; quantified in SI Appendix, Fig. S2B), indicating an S-phase independent arrest caused by incubation with more than 50 mM HU. It was recently discovered that Psk1 kinase, having been identified in the oxidative stress response (68), is the primary kinase that targets Mrc1 in response to oxidative stress, at separate sites from those targeted by Mec1, resulting in delayed progression through the S-phase under oxidative conditions (67). We therefore wondered whether Psk1 could be responsible for signaling to Mrc1 under oxidative stress and activating the checkpoint. We hypothesized that knocking out psk1 might release the HU-dependent stalling we observed in the absence of Mec1/Mrc1 signaling. To the contrary, cells with a mrc1AQ psk1Δ background released into 200 mM HU also stalled in G1/S in the presence of HU (Fig. 1C), indicating that the pausing we observed was entirely independent from Mec1/ATR signaling, in agreement with studies showing that Rad53 is not required inhibition of bulk DNA replication in the S-phase (37, 63), but also from any oxidative stress response transduced via Mrc1. Taken together, these results indicate that HU arrests cells in their cell cycle independent of the Mec1/Mrc1 and/or Psk1/Mrc1 pathways, and thus this drug likely acts outside of Mrc1-mediated S-phase checkpoint and/or oxidative signaling.

A Direct Relationship between HU Concentration and ROS Generation in Yeast Cell Nuclei.

If the canonic effect of HU is dNTP depletion, HU-induced cell cycle arrest does not appear to be initiated by activation of the replication checkpoint at stalled forks as mediated by Mrc1. The other frequently cited possibility is that cells arrest because replication forks simply run out of dNTPs and can no longer synthesize DNA, where checkpoint activation (or lack thereof) would not be consequential for cell cycle progression. However, since yeast preserve basal dNTP levels during HU treatment (37), and since HU arrests archaea lacking Class I RNR with no detectable change in dNTP concentration (36), we wondered about the possibility of an additional mechanism of cell cycle arrest. Particularly, since it has been shown that HU generates ROS in yeast, we wanted to query the extent to which ROS generation might be responsible for stalling cells. To detect nuclear ROS, we used dihydroethidium (DHE), an indicator that is converted to ethidium in the presence of ROS, consequently intercalating with DNA and exhibiting a strong red shift in fluorescence emission from 420 to 610 nm (Fig. 2A). We grew yeast in the log phase for 1 h in the presence or absence of 200 mM HU, treated cultures with DHE for 1 h, washed cells with Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS), and then examined fluorescence in cells by flow cytometry. In agreement with another study (61), we show a dramatically increased ROS signal from yeast cell nuclei in the presence of HU (Fig. 2B). To accurately assess the relationship between the amount of HU and ROS produced in yeast cells, we switched to a plate assay to quantify our results more robustly. As expected, we saw an asymptotic increase in the fold change of red-shifted fluorescence in yeast nuclei with increasing amounts of H2O2 (the most common cellular ROS), likely plateauing due to a saturation of the conversion of nuclear-stained DHE to ethidium; we also observed a strikingly similar trend when titrating HU into yeast (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). When directly correlating these values, we quantified a direct relationship between the fold change in fluorescence intensity caused by increasing [HU] versus that seen by increasing [H2O2] (r = 0.85). Although DHE is an established reporter of oxidation by ROS like H2O2 and superoxide, it also reacts to nonspecific oxidation (69). We wanted to rule out the possibility that HU was causing red-shifted DHE fluorescence directly, so we performed an in vitro assay containing DHE and plasmid DNA, in the presence of HU or H2O2. Critically, we observed that H2O2 activated the stain (i.e., caused its fluorescence to red shift), while HU did not (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Interpreting the curves from yeast in Fig. 2C within their quasi-linear ranges using an in vitro standard curve of DHE fluorescence vs. H2O2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), we roughly estimate that incubation with 0.2 M HU results ~20 mM of active (i.e., unquenched) ROS at minimum in the nucleus, with an indeterminate upper bound due to signal saturation. Because DHE measures active nuclear ROS, this is an estimate of the ROS that has not been quenched by H2O2 scavengers in yeast such as catalase, superoxide dismutase, and redoxins. Yeast cells are more permeable to H2O2 in the log phase, with a permeability constant measured at R = 0.64 (70). Our DHE intensity vs. [H2O2] curves (Fig. 2C) agree well with this in the quasi-linear range. Considering that HU does not activate our ROS probe (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), and that HU was previously observed to inhibit Leu1 (an unrelated cytosolic enzyme containing a Fe-S cluster) in vivo but not in vitro (61), we conclude as others have that HU must be generating radical species through one or more cellular metabolic intermediates, possibly initiated by its hydroxylamine group (38, 61). Having observed a direct relationship between the amount of HU cells were incubated with and the amount of ROS consequently generated in the nucleus, we next wondered whether this ROS could be in part responsible for arresting the cell cycle of replication checkpoint deficient cells. Indeed, we observed that release of mrc1AQ psk1Δ cells into millimolar concentrations of H2O2 was sufficient to cause arrest at G1/S (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S6), indicating that concentrations of ROS comparable to those produced by HU treatment can arrest cells independent of the replication checkpoint and oxidative signaling through Mrc1.

Strong Inhibition of Eukaryotic Replicative Polymerases by ROS.

After observing cell cycle inhibition by HU or H2O2 irrespective of S-phase checkpoint activation, we wondered whether either HU or the abundant ROS generated by HU might be responsible for inhibiting enzymes that were critical to cell cycle progression. Since there was speculation in the literature about the possibility of HU-mediated inhibition of proteins containing Fe-S clusters (33), we focused on the polymerase complexes responsible for copying DNA: Pols α, δ, and ε. Performing in vitro primer-extension assays that utilize a radiolabeled primer annealed to an oligonucleotide template to monitor DNA replication, we first asked whether HU can directly inhibit DNA polymerases. In these assays, we loaded all substrates with Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen (PCNA) sliding clamp using the Replication Factor C (RFC) clamp loader and coated the template with RPA, an ssDNA binding protein. Pol δ requires PCNA clamp for activity, Pol ε can use clamp but does not require it, and Pol α does not work with or require clamp; however, we wanted to present all polymerases with identical reaction conditions for a more direct comparison. We first observed that DNA replication activity of both Pol δ and Pol ε was unaffected by the presence of up to 200 mM HU (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Because we observed that HU generates ROS in yeast nuclei, we next asked whether H2O2 could inhibit DNA Pols α, δ, and ε. Indeed, we saw that all three polymerases were strongly inhibited by concentrations as low as 10 mM H2O2. We compared these results with Klenow Pol, a commercially available proteolytic product of Escherichia coli DNA polymerase that possesses full polymerase and 3′-5′ exonuclease activity, and, like all prokaryotic polymerases, also lacks an integral Fe-S cluster. Importantly, we did not observe inhibition of Klenow at 50 mM H2O2, five times the amount that was inhibitory for the yeast polymerase complexes (Fig. 3A; example gel shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). The most significant effect for all Pols was between 1 and 10 mM H2O2; we further show linear dose–response curves in this range for Pols α and δ, where we calculated IC50 values of 6.9 and 5.3 mM H2O2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 C–F). Since our earlier results indicate that incubation with 200 mM HU is expected to yield roughly 20 mM H2O2 or more in cell nuclei, we conclude that replicative Pols are likely to be directly inhibited by the ROS generated by the high HU concentrations routinely used to arrest cells.

DNA Template/Primer Binding by Pol α Is Inhibited by ROS.

We next asked whether the inhibition of polymerase activity via Fe-S cluster oxidation that we saw was localized to the polymerase active site or could be traced to other properties like DNA substrate binding, as was recently demonstrated with Pol ε (57). Pol α is responsible for synthesizing the hybrid RNA/DNA primers required for DNA replication and can also substitute for Pol ε and/or Pol δ (71), making it a good candidate as a central link to the cell cycle. We measured equilibrium binding of Pol α and Klenow Pol to DNA substrates by recording changes in fluorescence polarization of a fluorescein-labeled template/primer substrate and increasing amounts of Pol α. To force Pol α into the polymerase-competent mode of DNA strand extension, the primer was dideoxy terminated and reactions included the next cognate dNTP (dTTP). Both Pol α and Klenow Pol bound this substrate with reasonably high affinity (Kd = 21.0 nM and 45.6 nM, respectively). Strikingly, the presence of 50 mM H2O2 resulted in complete abolishment of the DNA binding of Pol α (Fig. 3B) while this amount of H2O2 did not measurably alter substrate binding of Klenow Pol (Fig. 3C). Because the DNA binding clefts of Pol α and Klenow Pol are fundamentally similar, we attribute this difference to the existence of a labile, oxidation-sensitive Fe-S cluster in Pol α, but not in Klenow Pol. We next wanted to assess whether inhibition of Pol α DNA binding by H2O2 was due to a change in DNA binding affinity or by a precipitation or unfolding of the enzyme, so we measured the effect of increasing NaCl levels on the Pol α:DNA interaction in the presence or absence of H2O2. Without H2O2, salt titration affected the result as expected by reducing both apparent Kd and by reducing the amount of apparent binding saturation. A similar effect could be seen in the presence of H2O2, albeit at a reduced dynamic range, suggesting that Pol α remains soluble in the presence of H2O2 as evidenced by apparent but weak equilibrium binding that can be screened by salt (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). At this point, however, we were unsure whether oxidation of the Fe-S cluster was responsible for disruption of local protein:DNA interactions or of the global structure of Pol α. ROS-induced dissociation of polymerase complexes with integral Fe-S clusters.

Based on reports that Fe-S cluster oxidation may lead to polymerase and/or primase complex dissociation (36, 55, 58), as well as observations we have made during purification of Pols δ and ε in which oxidative conditions lead to complex dissociation, we hypothesized that disruption of the Fe-S cluster may lead to disassembly of polymerase holoenzymes. Using mass photometry to test this, we found that the Pol αPol δ complexes almost fully dissociated in the presence of >3 mM H2O2 (Fig. 3 D and E; see SI Appendix, Fig. S9 for data acquired at additional H2O2 concentrations and for a quantification of Pol α dissociation during H2O2 titration). The Pol ε complex was also observed to dissociate in the presence of H2O2 but was considerably less sensitive to H2O2 than Pols α or δ, remaining largely intact even at 45 mM H2O2 (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S9C; See Discussion regarding the roles of CysB and CysX in Pol ε).

CysA and CysB of POL1 Are Dispensable for Pol α Activity In Vivo.

Having observed dissociation of Pol α, we were interested to find that Pol α was so highly sensitive to H2O2 in terms of DNA binding and activity, particularly since our reaction scheme forced Pol α binding to DNA in a polymerase-competent mode, i.e., not in its primase mode, implying either a direct modulation of the polymerase subunit (Pol1) and not the primase subunits (Pri1/Pri2) or a reliance on the primase domain for Pol1 binding to DNA. Pol α contains conserved CysA and CysB motifs in the CTD of its polymerase domain (Pol1), but it remains unclear whether they coordinate Fe-S clusters (46, 55). This uncertainty is in large part due to the difficulty of purifying these complexes while retaining labile cofactors; however, genetic studies like those performed for Pol δ (59, 60) and Pol ε (56) can be informative regarding the importance of these metal binding sites. Pol α is essential, making it highly sensitive to changes in activity. To test whether the activity of Pol1 requires the coordination of Fe-S and/or Zn2+ by CysA and/or CysB, we first decided to degrade endogenous Pol1 using a C-terminal auxin-inducible degron (AID) tag (72) on POL1, which was lethal in the presence of the plant hormone 1-Naphthaleneacetic Acid (NAA) and the ubiquitin ligase OsTIR1 as determined by spot assays. We were then able to rescue this degradation of endogenous Pol1-AID with an integrated copy of Pol1 under Gal control. Surprisingly, given the previously observed importance of CysB to Pol δ function, we were also able to rescue lethality by expressing Pol1 that had CysB and/or CysA metal binding motifs mutated by either Ser or Ala substitutions of the cysteines. At higher temperature (37 °C), the Ala mutations of the CysB motif were unable to rescue degradation of endogenous POL1, which we consider likely to be a misfolding phenotype (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). As such, these data indicate that Pol α does not require its CysA or CysB motifs for primase and/or polymerase activity in vivo. Based on this, we consider it likely that the Pri1/Pri2 domain, in which Pri2 contains an Fe-S cluster, is largely responsible for DNA template binding during DNA elongation (Discussion).

Antioxidants Rescue HU Cytotoxicity.

If cells arrest in HU due to oxidation from ROS, this should be reversible by scavenging ROS with antioxidants. Indeed, we observed that the antioxidants N-Acetyl Cysteine (NAC) and Ascorbic Acid (Asc) partially reversed the profound cytotoxicity of 0.2 M HU on spot assays (Fig. 3G and SI Appendix, Fig. S11), indicating that yeast can replicate for many generations in the presence of even very high HU concentrations when ROS is mitigated. Since antioxidants should not interfere with the mechanism of RNR inhibition by HU (reduction of the tyrosyl radical) and rather would be predicted to protect HU as a reducing agent, we conclude that HU-generated ROS plays a direct role in cell cycle arrest that is entirely independent of dNTP regulation.

ROS Generation and Polymerase Dissociation in the Human System.

We tested whether the ROS generation we measured in yeast translated to human cells at the effective HU concentrations used for arresting cells. Indeed, we observed by cytometry that HU treatment of human epithelial carcinoma (HeLa) cells caused a significant increase in DHE fluorescence, similar to the signal increase we observed in yeast, and often with much higher increases (SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A and B) in agreement with recent work showing excessive ROS generation in HeLa cells treated with HU (73, 74). From this, we conclude that the amount of ROS generated in HeLa cells with 2 mM and 5 mM HU was comparable or greater than that observed in yeast cells. We also tested whether human Pol δ that was purified from human cells and characterized as containing the Fe-S cluster (75) was similarly susceptible to dissociation by ROS. As predicted, we saw that this four-subunit complex dissociated in the presence of H2O2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S12D).

An Additional Mechanism of HU-Induced Replication Stress.

Collectively, our results indicate that HU-generated ROS causes disassembly of polymerase complexes into their subunits, inhibiting both DNA binding and polymerase activity. The effect is relevant in both yeast and human cells. We propose an additional mechanism of replication stress from HU whereby this polymerase inhibition directly causes cell cycle arrest (Fig. 3H).

RNR-deg: A Superior Alternative to HU-Induced Cell Cycle Arrest.

These studies demonstrated that the oxidative toxicity of HU was high enough—and the specificity against RNR low enough—to warrant a different approach to cell cycle arrest, so we decided to directly target the RNR complex. Using an N-terminal AID tag on Rnr1 combined with an Rnr3 knockout and integrated OsTir1 (OsTIR1, AID-rnr1, rnr3Δ) hereafter called RNR-deg (Fig. 4A), we observed lethality in the presence of both OsTIR1 and the auxin 1-Naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) by survival spot assays (Fig. 4B). Assessing by western blot, we observed that Rnr1 was rapidly and thoroughly degraded after 30 min (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). We note that a C-terminal degron tag on Rnr1 was not viable even in the absence of NAA and OsTIR1 (not shown), likely because it prevented oligomerization, and that a strain bearing an N-terminal degron tag on Rnr2 was unaffected by the addition of OsTIR1 and NAA (SI Appendix, Fig. S14A). Importantly, when we synchronized cells in G1 and released them into media containing HU or NAA, we saw that RNR-deg arrests cells at G1/S in a manner than is indistinguishable from that of HU, using three orders of magnitude less NAA than HU (Fig. 4C). We observed a clear dose–response curve of the percentage of cells arrested in G1/S upon titration from 0 to 250 µM NAA (SI Appendix, Fig. S14B). Scatter plots from this experiment are shown in (SI Appendix, Fig. S14C), demonstrating that RNR-deg closely resembles HU treatment and does not significantly change cell shape. Treating with NAA at various timepoints after release from G1, we also demonstrate that RNR-deg can be used as a robust method of arresting cells in the S-phase (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Further, we show that RNR-deg is more quickly reversible than HU; cells washed after a 4-h treatment with RNR-deg reached G2 after 90 min, comparable to the speed of untreated cells, while cells washed after HU treatment were slower and had only partially reached G2 by 3 h post wash (Fig. 4D). This difference may reflect the relative inefficiency in removing three orders of magnitude more HU than NAA from cells (including the ROS that it generated) and could also be related to the time required to resynthesize (or reassemble) and reload polymerases after HU treatment following ROS clearance and reduction of Fe-S clusters. Further work is needed to understand these mechanisms. Critically, we demonstrate that RNR-deg generates significantly less ROS in yeast nuclei than HU (Fig. 4E; compare RNR-deg signal to the HU signal in Fig. 2A). We note that a specific shutdown of dNTP production likely reduces the metabolic ROS load in cells, as we observed RNR-deg to have a decreased ROS signal compared to untreated cells (Fig. 4E). Taken together, we demonstrate that RNR-deg is a superior alternative to HU resulting in robust, rapidly reversible cell cycle arrest without the unnecessary production of harmful ROS.

Materials and Methods

Strain Construction.

Our base strain, OY01, is derived from W303: ade2-1 ura3-1 his3-11,15 trp1-1 leu2-3,112 can1-100 bar1Δ MATa pep4::KANMX6) (76). The RNR-deg strain (AES38) was created by integrating Gal/OsTIR1 into the Ura3 gene into OY01. A miniAID1 tag (77) was added to the N-term of endogenous RNR1 using CRISPR/Cas9. RNR3 was replaced by a HygR cassette. The remaining strains used in this study (mrc1AQ, mrc1AQ psk1Δ, mrc1Δ, deg-POL1, and various POL1 rescue constructs) were created using standard replacement of genes with various marker cassettes. Yeast cultures were grown in YPG/Gal media at 30 °C unless otherwise stated. Further details for all strains can be found SI Appendix, SI Materials and Methods.

Flow Cytometry.

Cytometry experiments followed previously described methodology using Sytox Green to monitor DNA content of living cells (78) and were performed on a Cytek Aurora spectral cytometer. Reference samples of Sytox stained and unstained cells were performed for each experiment for live unmixing. Data were analyzed with FlowJo software using optimized gates that were static across each experiment, after first excluding small debris and large aggregates using forward scatter (FSC) vs. side scatter (SSC) plots and excluding non-DNA-containing debris with FSC vs. Sytox plots.

ROS Detection Assay.

All DHE experiments in yeast were performed with the RNR-deg strain (AES38). Cells were stained with 25 µg/mL DHE for 1 h, washed, resuspended, and diluted 10-fold in PBS and measured by spectral flow cytometry or in a 384-well plate reader (BMG Clariostar). Fluorescence intensity of red-shifted probe was measured as Ex: 488 nm and Em: 584 nm for cytometry experiments and Ex 360/40: Em: 600/40 in the plate assay. In vitro experiments in SI Appendix, Fig. S5 were 25 µL/well and were performed in the presence 1 ng/µL of supercoiled pAS1 plasmid DNA.

Protein Purification.

The purification of Pol α, Pol δ, Pol ε, RFC, PCNA, and RPA has been previously described (71, 76, 79). The purity of protein complexes was assessed by Coomassie-stained sodium-dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and all enzymes were assessed for activity in vitro before use in assays.

Strand Extension Assays.

Reaction volumes were 25 µL and contained 1.5 nM of the 85DS substrate, a 243 nt template primed with a 5′-32P-primer (construction described in SI Appendix, SI Materials and Methods). Reactions contained 25 mM Tris-OAc pH 7.5, 5% (vol/vol) glycerol, 50 μg/mL Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA), 2 mM (tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine) (TCEP), 3 mM Dithiothreitol (DTT), 10 mM Magnesium Acetate (Mg-OAc), 50 mM Potassium glutamate, and 0.1 mM Ethylenediaminetetraacetic Acid (EDTA and were carried out at 30 °C. DNA was first loaded with 20 nM RPA for 1 min, followed by 40 nM PCNA with 2 nM RFC and 1 mM ATP for 5 min. Pol α, δ, and ε were each added at 10 nM in the presence of the indicated HU and/or H2O2 concentrations. Reactions were initiated by the addition of 60 mM dNTPs, allowed to proceed for 5 min, and stopped with a STOP solution containing 1% SDS and 40 mM EDTA. Reactions were run by 7.5% denaturing PAGE. Gels were backed with Whatman 3MM filter paper, exposed to a storage phosphor screen for 6 h, and visualized on a Typhoon FLA 9500 PhosphorImager (GE).

DNA-Binding Assay.

Fluorescence polarization reactions were 20 μL and contained 25 mM Tris-OAc pH 7.5, 5% (vol/vol) glycerol, 50 μg/mL BSA, 2 mM TCEP, 3 mM DTT, 10 mM Mg-OAc, 125 mM NaCl (unless otherwise noted), 0.1% Tween-20, and 3 nM probe template/primer DNA, where the primer contains a 5′-fluorescein and is 3′ dideoxy terminated, in the absence or presence of 50 mM H2O2. Pol α titrations were scanned on a 384-well plate reader equipped with polarizing filters (BMG Clariostar; Ex: 482/16 nm, Em: 530/40 nm). Polarization is expressed in mP (Px10−3), where P = (IVV-IVH)/(Ivv+IVH); IVV is the fluorescence intensity with vertical Ex/Em polarizers, and IVH is the fluorescence intensity with vertical and horizontal Ex/Em polarizers. Binding curves were fit to a single-site binding equation using GraphPad Prism.

Mass Photometry.

Mass photometry was performed on a Refyn Two MP as previously described (80). Stocks of purified pol complexes were diluted to 50 nM in 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM DTT, 5% glycerol (vol/vol) and 250 mM NaCl were incubated in the indicated amounts of H2O2 for 5 min at 30 °C. Proteins were diluted to 5 nM in the 9 µL of the same buffer and deposited on the coverslip, and movies were acquired for 1 min. MW histograms were generated with Refyn DiscoverMP software.

Serial Spot Dilution Assays.

Yeast spot assays were performed with yeast harvested in the exponential phase. Cells were serially diluted to 104, 103, 102, and 10 cells/µL with sterile water; spots were 5 µL.

Western Blot.

Primary antibodies: rabbit anti-RNR1 (Agrisera #AS21 4608) at 1:3,000 or mouse anti-α-tubulin (ABM # G094) at 1:8,000 for a loading control. Secondary antibodies: DyLight goat anti-mouse 680 nm or goat anti-rabbit 680 nm at 1:15,000. SI Appendix, SI Materials and Methods for full details.

Discussion

In this report, we found that HU can arrest cells independent of the S-phase checkpoint and/or oxidative stress signaling via Mrc1 (Fig. 1). A previous study concluded that yeast must sense moderate changes in basal dNTP levels in order to arrest cells and that this mechanism did not depend on the replication checkpoint; however, such a mechanism has yet to be elucidated (37). In trying to uncover the basis for our finding, we found that HU generates excessive nuclear ROS that is likely inhibitory to the replicative polymerases required for S-phase progression, particularly at the high concentrations of HU (~50 to 200 mM) used to arrest yeast cells: The direct relationship between HU and H2O2 implies a level of H2O2 that is not only well above the ~0.1 mM H2O2 that elicits the cellular oxidative stress response (67, 81) but also the ~1 to 10 mM H2O2 range that we observed to arrest cells in vivo and to fully inhibit replication and cause polymerase dissociation in vitro

It is well established that HU can inhibit RNR; however, given that dNTP pools are not appreciably reduced in yeast (37), that HU can arrest and kill archaea without a reduction in dNTP levels (36), that HU generates amounts of ROS in yeast that we showed was sufficient to arrest cells and disassemble polymerases, and that antioxidants reversed HU cytotoxicity, we consider it unlikely that RNR inhibition is the sole mechanism of cell cycle arrest by HU. It is also possible that the predominant mechanism of fork decoupling may depend on HU concentration. For instance, a moderate amount of replication into the S-phase has been reported in 100 mM HU with overexpression of RNR1, which increases dNTP levels, although this also improves unperturbed replication rates (82). Importantly, the only studies to our knowledge that have reported RNR inhibition to be the sole mechanism of HU action are from bacteria (83–86), where Fe-S clusters are not coordinated in polymerases, and where significantly less HU is required. Keeping this in mind, only one study to our knowledge has claimed that HU is not a direct inhibitor of DNA synthesis in vivo, and this study was also performed in E. coli (87). Instead, at the high concentrations of HU routinely employed in yeast, we predict that polymerases are likely one of a multitude of classes of Fe-S-containing enzymes that are inhibited in the strong oxidative environment seen with HU incubation, that cellular pathways related to redox sensing are likely also activated, and that the molecular mechanisms of cytotoxicity by HU are likely manyfold. For instance, it was recently shown that the ROS generated from HU may be responsible for inducing replication:transcription collisions (74). Further work will be required to disentangle these mechanisms. At minimum, our findings cast doubt on the designation of HU as a specific inhibitor of RNR. Critically, we found that antioxidants, which would be predicted to make HU better at reducing the catalytic tyrosyl radical in RNR, instead allowed cells to grow in 200 mM HU, indicating that there were enough dNTPs present in yeast nuclei during HU treatment to copy their genomes for many generations: This directly challenges the current model that reduction in dNTP pools is primarily responsible for halting replication with HU. The fact that colonies did not fully grow to normal size may be due to a reduction of dNTP levels by HU but could also be attributed to poor permeability of antioxidants as well as the relative inefficiency of scavenging of H2O2 and associated radicals. Taken together, the reversibility of HU through scavenging points to the high likelihood that ROS generation and polymerase dissociation is at least among the causative effects, if not the most prominent effect, underlying cell cycle arrest by HU in yeast (Fig. 3G).

Since yeast and human polymerase complexes are highly structurally and functionally homologous and since human polymerases also contain integral Fe-S clusters, it was reasonable to expect that they are similarly susceptible to H2O2. We indeed determined that hPol δ, which also contains an Fe-S cluster, is susceptible to dissociation during exposure to ROS. Further, the ~1 to 5 mM concentrations of HU used to arrest human cells generated sufficient ROS to dissociate polymerases (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). It is interesting that 2 to 5 mM HU in human cells appeared to yield similar amounts of ROS seen in yeast treated with 200 mM HU. This seems plausible since multiple ROS like OH• can be generated by a single oxidant, often with the help of redox-active metal centers (88). Thus, it is possible that the differences in HU absorption and metabolism between human cells accounts for the differences in effective HU concentrations commonly used at the bench. Further work is required to understand these differences. Does HU work primarily through dNTP reduction in human cells or is arrest predominately due to oxidation? This question will be more difficult to answer given that, unlike yeast, metazoans can utilize the nucleotide salvage pathway to generate dNTP. Still, relevant studies in humans may provide insight. For instance, HU added to human fibroblasts results in only ~70% reduction in deoxyadenosine triphosphate (dATP) and deoxyguanosine triphosphate (dGTP), and a ~30% increase in deoxycytosine triphosphate (dCTP) and deoxythymidine triphosphate (dTTP) (89). In chronic myelogenous leukemia patients, HU infusion in the 1 to 6 mM range arrests DNA replication but reduces dATP levels by 60% and does not change dTTP, dCTP, or dGTTP levels (90). Human cells, unlike yeast, can uptake dNTPs, thus, dNTP absorption would be predicted to reverse HU treatment if RNR is the primary target. However, most studies show only a moderate rescue of HU inhibition with the addition of deoxynucleotides or deoxynucleosides. The first study to our knowledge to suggest that deoxynucleotides at high (1 mM) levels could reverse HU inhibition in mammalian cells, as measured by 3H-thymidine incorporation into DNA, was performed at 2 mM HU (91), but this could not be repeated in human fibroblasts using identical concentrations (92). Subsequent studies in human cells show only partial rescue and were performed at relatively low [0.05 to 0.1 mM HU ranges (93–95)]. Thus, as in yeast, it remains possible that RNR inhibition is not the primary effect of HU in human cells.

In this study, we observed that Pol complexes containing Fe-S clusters (Pols α, δ, and ε) were inhibited by H2O2, while Klenow Pol was not. Because the active sites of eukaryotic and prokaryotic DNA polymerases are structurally and functionally homologous (96), we attribute the stark contrast observed between the H2O2 sensitivity with the existence of oxidation-sensitive Fe-S clusters in the eukaryotic Pol complexes that do not exist in prokaryotic Pols. Notably, the fact that both DNA binding and polymerase activity of Klenow Pol were unfazed by profuse H2O2 also rules out the possibility that oxidation of enzymatic residues, the DNA substrate, or the dNTPs in the reaction was responsible for inhibition of either DNA polymerase and/or substrate binding activities of Pol α, δ, or ε. We note that in theory, inhibition of any of the replicative polymerases would be enough to stall the cell cycle, can The fact that perturbation of CysB in Pol δ and CysX in Pol ε inhibits these critical polymerases in vivo (57, 59, 60), and similarly that primase activity relies on Fe-S coordination (48, 51) indicates that oxidization of the integral Fe-S cluster in these complexes would be sufficient to stall cells. Critically, it was shown that mutation of the Cys residues that coordinate Fe-S in the CTD of human Pri2 (the regulatory subunit of primase) results in defective Pol α loading and initiation, with a temperature-sensitive phenotype of G1/S arrest (97). Thus, oxidation-driven inhibition of just one of the three enzyme complexes we observed in this study is in fact sufficient to cause cell cycle arrest.

In an attempt to elucidate the mechanism of Pol inhibition by H2O2, we found that both CysA and CysB were dispensable for Pol α activity, indicating that a Fe-S and/or Zn2+ cofactor is likely not required for the polymerase activity of Pol1. A recent report describes a possible mechanism in human Pol α primase whereby primase may hand off to the polymerase subunit via a charge transfer between two Fe-S clusters, implying that the CTD of the Pol1 subunit would need to have a reduced Fe-S cluster in its CTD order to facilitate primase-to-polymerase handoff (54); however, we do not see evidence for coordination of a charge transfer donor in Pol1 by either CysA or CysB. Recently, O’Donnell et al. published a structure of yeast Pol α comprising all four subunits, where a Fe-S cluster was not observed in the Pol1 CTD, corroborating our results (98). Because we were not able to abrogate Pol α activity in vivo by disrupting the CysA or CysB domains in Pol1 CTD, but were able to strongly inhibit template/primer binding and elongation of Pol α with H2O2 in vitro, we predict that most of the DNA binding affinity of Pol α in its DNA elongation mode might arise from the primase regulatory subunit, Pri2, previously observed to possess Fe-S clusters in both yeast and humans (48–53) and to remain bound to the template during the DNA polymerization step (98). Still, given the conservation of the CysA and CysB motifs in Pol1, and the fact that Fe-S was previously observed to associate with the Pol1 CTD (55), further study is warranted to understand the functional role, if any, of these motifs in Pol α.

How exactly does the 4Fe-FS cluster modulate polymerase activity? Based on our studies and work from other labs (36, 55, 58) it appears that these clusters may play mostly a structural role in helping maintain polymerase complex assembly and/or function. The Fe-S cluster seemingly plays a different role in each polymerase: The dissociation of Pol α subunits results in reduced DNA binding affinity, possibly by the loss of Pri2 binding as discussed above, while in Pol δ, the loss of the small subunits (Pol31 and Pol32) would eliminate its affinity for PCNA sliding clamp, which is required for activity (55, 99). In this work, Pol ε dissociated in the presence of H2O2 but was more stable than Pol α or δ. It remains unclear whether Pol ε retains an Fe-S cluster in its CTD (46); however, a separate Fe-S cluster target of H2O2 seems more likely: Mutagenesis of the CysX motif of the Pol2 NTD, which has been observed in structures to coordinate a Fe-S cluster, resulted in loss of DNA binding and activity as consequence of disrupting the P-domain, part of the catalytic core shown to be critical for processivity (56, 57, 100). If Pol ε indeed only retains an Fe-S cluster in the Pol2 NTD, it would appear this location is much less critical for Pol complex stability than Pri2 for Pol α or the CTD of Pol3 for Pol δ. Thus, all three polymerases are vulnerable to oxidation but by separable mechanisms. It is possible that coordination of Fe-S clusters by polymerases evolved as a “kill switch” to prevent DNA from being replicated in a potentially damaging environment by triggering rapid complex disassembly and/or unfolding under oxidizing conditions. Interestingly, it is even possible that these cofactors are communicating their redox status through DNA via charge transfer signaling (101). It will take further structural and genetics studies to fully understand their role(s) in replication. Regardless of the precise mechanism of inhibition by HU, the abundant ROS that it generates in cells should be recognized as a major confounding variable to present and past studies in eukaryotes utilizing HU-induced cell cycle arrest. Here, we demonstrate that RNR-deg is a superior alternative to HU that is significantly more specific against RNR and allows cells to recover from arrest faster than in HU, all in the absence of toxic ROS generation. We hope that RNR-deg can be a useful tool to investigate the S-phase checkpoint, genome maintenance, DNA damage repair, and countless other processes in the cell without the confounding presence of HU-induced oxidative stress.
