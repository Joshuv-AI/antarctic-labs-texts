Rectal smooth muscle loss as a predictor of severe postoperative complications in rectal cancer surgery
Authors: Tatsuhiko Kume, Shuang Liu, Shigehiro Koga, Motohira Yoshida, Satoshi Akita, Hiroki Sugishita, Yusuke Ogi, Masaki Mogi, Taro Oshikiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe influence of rectal smooth muscle (RSM) mass on the surgical outcomes of rectal cancer surgery remains inadequately understood. This study investigated smooth muscle depletion during rectal cancer surgery, evaluated the perioperative association between skeletal muscle mass and RSM, and assessed the impact of RSM on postoperative complications. This retrospective study analysed 107 patients who underwent rectal cancer surgery, of whom 102 were included in the analysis of postoperative outcomes. Preoperative computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging scans were utilised to quantify skeletal muscle using the psoas muscle index (PMI) and quantify RSM using the newly established rectal smooth muscle index (RSMI). The RSMI was an independent predictor of severe postoperative complications. The RSMI demonstrated superior predictive accuracy over the PMI for Clavien-Dindo grade III–IV complications, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.792 for the RSMI compared with 0.609 for the PMI, and for postoperative ileus, with an AUC of 0.700 for the RSMI versus 0.560 for the PMI. Patients exhibiting reductions in both the PMI and RSMI were significantly more susceptible to skeletal muscle loss 6 months postoperatively. The significant correlation between skeletal muscle and RSM mass underscores the clinical value of the RSMI in predicting postoperative outcomes and informing tailored management strategies.Keywords: Rectal smooth muscle index, Psoas muscle index, Skeletal muscle, Smooth muscle, Rectal cancer, Postoperative outcomeSubject terms: Rectal cancer, Colorectal surgery

Introduction

Rectal cancer accounts for approximately 730 000 of the 1.9 million newly diagnosed colorectal cancer cases annually1,2. Demographic trends, including population growth and ageing, project a 71.5% increase in rectal cancer-related deaths worldwide by 20353. Skeletal muscle mass at the initial diagnosis of rectal cancer is reportedly a critical factor affecting oncological outcomes4. Sarcopenia, a systemic state defined by progressive and generalised loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function, is associated with longer postoperative hospitalisation, increased intraoperative blood loss, increased incidences of postoperative ileus, surgical site infection, and hypoalbuminemia, higher 30-day and 90-day mortality rates, and significantly worse overall survival and recurrence-free survival5,6.

The motor function of intestinal smooth muscle is essential for various intestinal functions, including transport and absorption. However, surgical manipulation of the intestine can activate mast cells, leading to increased mucosal permeability, bacterial translocation, and activation of intestinal macrophages. Consequently, inflammatory mediators are released, which reduces the contractile force of intestinal smooth muscle7,8. Furthermore, the complete functional rectal smooth muscle (RSM) is critical for blood supply, energy metabolism, and structural integrity, which are essential for proper healing of the anastomotic site9.

Although smooth muscle is critical in the formation and function of various hollow organs, including those in the gastrointestinal tract and arterial system, the effects of systemic sarcopenia on intestinal smooth muscle, as well as the interplay between skeletal and smooth muscle, remain inadequately understood. Consequently, the influence of intestinal smooth muscle mass on postoperative complications in patients with rectal cancer has not been thoroughly investigated. In the present study, we introduce a novel metric, the rectal smooth muscle index (RSMI), to quantitatively assess the RSM mass at the initial diagnosis of rectal cancer. We analysed the correlation between the RSMI and a conventional index of skeletal muscle mass, the psoas muscle index (PMI), and evaluated the impact of the RSMI on clinical postoperative outcomes.

Results

Establishment of the RSMI as an invasive measure of the smooth muscle status

A total of 107 patients with complete medical records and images were recruited for the study. High-quality axial computed tomography (CT) images and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images were obtained for PMI and RSMI calculation (Fig. 1a and d). In the analysis of the relationship between the PMI and RSMI, the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) was 0.543, which indicated a moderate linear relationship between the two variables (Fig. 1e). Moreover, the p-value associated with this correlation was < 0.001, indicating that the relationship between the PMI and RSMI was significant.

To further verify whether the RSMI accurately reflected the smooth muscle status, the correlation between the RSMI and the RSM thickness obtained from resected specimens was evaluated. Representative images of the RSM wall in patients with normal and low RSMI are presented (Fig. 1f). The RSM thickness differed significantly between the normal and low RSMI groups (2206 μm, interquartile range (IQR): 1992–2584 vs. 1307 μm, IQR: 1172–1500, p < 0.01). A Pearson correlation coefficient (r) of 0.857 indicated a strong linear relationship between the two variables (Fig. 1g).

Taken together, the RSMI was established as an invasive measure for evaluating the RSM status, which is closely related to the skeletal muscle status indicated by the PMI.

Diagnostic performance of the RSMI in systemic sarcopenia

According to the diagnostic criteria of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia, we selected the PMI as the primary measure to define low muscle mass10. The study population was categorised based on sex-specific PMI thresholds into the normal PMI group (n = 48) and low PMI group (n = 54) using cutoff values of 6.36 cm²/m² for men and 3.92 cm²/m² for women (Table 1).

Comparison of major clinical characteristics including postoperative outcomes between groups divided by the PMI or RSMI

A comparison of the patient characteristics between the patients divided by the PMI or RSMI is shown in Table 1.

Several differences in patterns were observed in both the PMI- and RSMI-divided groups, comprising differences in the visceral fat area (VFA), and body mass index (BMI), albumin concentration, Clavien-Dindo grade III–IV complications, and postoperative ileus. There were significant differences in sex, smoking status, and cardiovascular disease between the normal and low PMI groups, but no differences between the normal and low RSMI groups. Compared with the normal RSMI group, the low RSMI group had significantly higher incidences of postoperative anastomotic leakage, abdominal infection and abdominal haemorrhage; however, there were no differences between the normal and low PMI groups.

Predictication performance of the RSMI for severe postoperative complications

To identify factors that independently predicted a low RSMI, univariate analysis was first performed to identify factors associated with the RSMI (Table 2). The results indicated that the BMI, VFA, albumin concentration, and low PMI were significantly associated with the low RSMI. In multivariate logistic regression analysis adjusted for potential confounders, low PMI was significantly associated with low RSMI (OR: 6.885, 95% CI: 2.310–20.500, p < 0.001). Given the moderate correlation between PMI and RSMI (r = 0.543), this association should be interpreted as reflecting a shared muscle depletion phenotype.

We further explored potential clinical factors that might be closely related to severe postoperative complications classified as Clavien-Dindo grade III–IV. In univariate analyses, the BMI, VFA, low PMI, and low RSMI were associated with severe postoperative complications. However, in multivariate analysis, only a low RSMI remained statistically significant (OR = 15.034, 95% CI: 1.576–143.418, p = 0.019). This finding suggests that a low RSMI, which can be assessed preoperatively, may serve as a novel and reliable predictor of severe postoperative complications.

The predictive performance of the RSMI and PMI for postoperative complications was evaluated using ROC analysis and XGBoost decision boundary (Fig. 2c and f). For predicting severe postoperative complications (Clavien-Dindo grade III–IV), the RSMI achieved an AUC of 0.792 (95% CI: 0.673–0.911), while the PMI obtained an AUC of 0.609 (95% CI: 0.468–0.749). Similarly, for postoperative ileus, the RSMI showed superior predictive power (AUC: 0.700, 95% CI: 0.573–0.827) compared with the PMI (AUC: 0.560, 95% CI: 0.424–0.696). The illustrated decision boundary identified critical values at which the risk of severe complications was significantly increased. A clearer boundary for the RSMI compared with the PMI indicates that the RSMI was more effective in distinguishing high-risk patients. However, the presence of some uncertain regions, particularly for postoperative ileus, suggests that additional clinical assessment is required. Taken together, these results indicate that the RSMI provides better predictive accuracy than the PMI for both Clavien-Dindo grade III–IV complications and postoperative ileus.

Relationship between a low RSMI and low PMI and progressive skeletal muscle loss postoperatively

At 6 months postoperatively, the participants were categorised based on the presence or absence of a low PMI and RSM according to the cutoff values defined in Table 1 into Group A (normal PMI/normal RSMI), Group B (low PMI/normal RSMI), Group C (normal PMI/low RSMI), and Group D (low PMI/low RSMI) (Fig. 3).

Changes in body composition parameters, comprising the (a) body mass index (BMI), (b) psoas muscle index (PMI), and (c) visceral fat area (VFA), at 6 months postoperatively compared with preoperative values. Participants were categorised into four groups based on the presence or absence of a low PMI and rectal smooth muscle index (RSMI). Results are presented as median (interquartile range).

There were no significant differences in the BMI among the groups. However, the PMI demonstrated a general trend of decrease across all groups. The IQR for PMI changes was − 1.247% (− 11.631%, 1.988%) for Group A, − 6.478% (− 14.783%, − 0.959%) for Group B, − 2.361% (− 14.863%, 5.135%) for Group C, and − 11.501% (-19.774%, − 7.891%) for Group D. Notably, there were significant differences in PMI reductions between groups with and without low muscle mass (Group D vs. Group A: p = 0.004).

Interestingly, although the VFA did not differ among the groups (p = 0.198), with an IQR of − 11.291% (− 31.25%, 8.476%) for Group A, 3.698% (− 30.686%, 41.075%) for Group B, 4.659% (− 3.475%, 15.234%) for Group C, and 12.704% (− 25.682%, 37.123%) for Group D, there was a trend of increased VFA in patients with a low RSMI postoperatively.

These findings suggest that the presence of both a low RSMI and a low PMI may play a critical role in postoperative body composition changes.

Discussion

This study assessed the prognostic significance of RSM mass at the time of initial diagnosis, quantified using the RSMI, in predicting surgical outcomes in patients with rectal cancer. While the conventional PMI is influenced by various systemic factors, the RSMI directly reflects the condition of RSM, as confirmed through histological analysis, and is specifically associated with perioperative gastrointestinal function. Our findings suggest that the RSMI is independently associated with severe postoperative complications and long-term alterations in body composition. Although these associations remained significant after adjustment for several clinical variables, residual confounding, including differences in type of surgical procedure, cannot be completely excluded. Although rectal cancer surgery is primarily dictated by oncologic principles, preoperative identification of patients with low RSMI may assist in perioperative risk stratification. For example, patients with low RSMI could be considered for intensified perioperative nutritional support, structured prehabilitation programs, closer monitoring for postoperative ileus, and, in selected cases, consideration of a protective diverting stoma when technically appropriate. Importantly, the RSMI is not intended to alter oncologic surgical indications but may provide additional information to optimize perioperative management in high-risk patients.

In patients with rectal cancer, excision and disruption of gastrointestinal tissue can lead to several postoperative complications, including anastomotic leakage, haemorrhage, infection, and bowel dysfunction. These complications can significantly impact both short-term recovery and long-term survival, resulting in delayed recovery, prolonged hospitalisation, increased morbidity and mortality, and increased risk of cancer recurrence11,12. This underscores the importance of preoperatively identifying patients at risk of postoperative complications. Integrating the RSMI into preoperative assessments could serve as an early warning for the onset of complications, prompting the surgical team to take pre-emptive measures. In high-risk patients, as predicted by our XGBoost model (Fig. 3), a protective colostomy or diverting ileostomy may be warranted to further mitigate the risk of postoperative complications.

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols are widely implemented to reduce postoperative morbidity and accelerate recovery in colorectal surgery13–15. In this context, RSMI may serve as an adjunctive marker to identify patients who could benefit from more individualized optimization (e.g., intensified nutritional support, closer postoperative monitoring, or tailored rehabilitation strategies) within standard perioperative care pathways.

During the initial phase of this study, we encountered challenges in segmenting intestinal smooth muscle on MRI. The differential detectability of smooth muscle loss between the jejunum and rectum may be attributed to variations in the thickness of the muscularis propria across the intestinal tract. The muscularis propria is thicker in the rectum than in other regions, making smooth muscle loss more readily detectable16. Consequently, our histological verification confirmed that the RSMI was closely correlated with the actual thickness of RSM. Based on these quantitative findings, the definition of the RSMI was established.

The mechanisms underlying intestinal smooth muscle atrophy and its relationship with skeletal muscle atrophy remain unclear. Our study identified the PMI as an independent predictor of the RSMI, suggesting that the two muscle types may share common molecular pathways of atrophy. Like skeletal muscle, smooth muscle undergoes atrophy due to disuse and responds to catabolic stimuli by upregulating ubiquitin ligases16,17. In skeletal muscle, reduced PI3K/AKT signalling induces the expression of the muscle-specific E3 ubiquitin ligases, MuRF1 and Atrogin-1, which drive protein degradation and atrophy18. Similar mechanisms may operate in smooth muscle, as Atrogin-1 and MuRF-1 are also upregulated during smooth muscle atrophy, such as in postpartum uterine involution16,19. Further single-cell transcriptomic analysis may provide deeper insights into the signalling networks between skeletal and smooth muscle cells and their surrounding cellular environment.

The present exploratory study has several limitations. First, this was a single-centre study, and differences in surgical techniques, patient management protocols, and demographics across institutions may affect the generalisability of the RSMI as a predictor of complications. Second, the accuracy of the RSMI as a predictor may be influenced by MRI and measurement techniques. Variability in image acquisition, analysis protocols, and operator expertise could introduce inconsistencies, potentially leading to measurement errors. Third, although surgical procedures were heterogeneous, they were not significantly associated with severe complications. However, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Fourth, the number of explanatory variables in the logistic regression analysis was large relative to the sample size, increasing the risk of overfitting and potentially affecting the reliability of the estimates. Consequently, the observed relationship between skeletal and intestinal smooth muscle should be interpreted with caution. To address these limitations, future prospective studies with larger patient cohorts are required to validate these findings across different segments of the intestinal tract and to establish clearer causal relationships.

In conclusion, we propose the RSMI as a quantitative index for assessing RSM mass at the initial diagnosis of rectal cancer. The RSMI not only demonstrates strong diagnostic performance in evaluating RSM status but also exhibits exceptional predictive ability for severe postoperative complications. We recommend including the RSMI in preoperative assessments, as its integration into clinical practice could enhance risk stratification, enabling more targeted interventions to improve surgical outcomes and patient prognosis. Future research should focus on validating the RSMI across diverse populations and exploring its potential role in long-term patient management and recovery.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-centre, retrospective, cohort study was conducted. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethical Review Committee, met the ethical guidelines of the Japan Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects, and conformed to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board, and an opt-out recruitment method was employed to allow all patients the opportunity to decline participation in the study.

The eligibility criteria were (1) rectal cancer surgery between April 2010 and March 2024, (2) age ≥ 18 years, and (3) both preoperative CT and MRI. The exclusion criteria were: (1) age < 18 years, (2) marked oedema and thickening of the rectal wall due to intestinal obstruction, (3) difficulty assessing the rectal muscularis propria on MRI, (4) refusal of study participation, and (5) lack of clinical data.

A total of 107 patients with complete medical records and imaging data were initially enrolled. Among these, five patients who underwent total pelvic exenteration were excluded from the analysis of surgical outcomes due to the highly invasive nature of the procedure and its inherently elevated risk of postoperative complications. Consequently, 102 patients were included in the primary analysis evaluating postoperative outcomes. Of these 107 patients, we excluded 16 patients who underwent postoperative follow-up at another hospital, 2 patients who had undergone surgery less than 6 months ago, and 2 patients who died within 6 months postoperatively. Thus, 87 patients were analysed for percentage changes in the PMI, VFA, and BMI 6 months postoperatively.

Body composition assessment

Among several methodologies, including bioelectrical impedance analysis, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, CT/MRI analysis, anthropometric measurements, and creatinine excretion analysis, the PMI derived from CT scans is considered highly accurate with a reference standard derived from extensive research, and was therefore used for the assessment of skeletal muscle mass20. The PMI was calculated from preoperative CT images acquired for the purpose of rectal cancer evaluation and was adopted as the skeletal muscle index21. Single CT images at the third lumbar vertebra (L3) level were selected, and the psoas muscle area was calculated automatically using a SYNAPSE VINCENT® medical imaging system (Fujifilm Medical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The PMI was calculated as the sum of the bilateral psoas muscle areas at the L3 level divided by the square of the height (cm2/m2). Sarcopenia was defined as PMI < 6.36 cm2/m2 for men and PMI < 3.92 cm2/m2 for women22. The VFA was determined using CT images at the umbilical level and calculated automatically using SYNAPSE VINCENT®.

To our knowledge, no existing index specifically quantifies the RSM mass. Therefore, we developed a new metric, the RSMI, which was derived from MRI scans. T2-weighted MRI is routinely used in clinical practice to assess the depth of rectal cancer invasion and is particularly effective in evaluating the muscular layer. In these images, the submucosa appears as a hyperintense area, while the muscularis propria is depicted as a hypointense area, facilitating clear delineation of the muscle layers for accurate measurement23. The measurement site was selected in a section of the rectum located more than 1 cm from the cancerous lesion. The anatomical reference for the rectum was defined by identifying its starting point at the sigmoid take-off. The sigmoid take-off and anorectal junction were identified on cross-sectional imaging where the mesocolon elongated, which was visualised as the transition of the sigmoid colon to a ventral and horizontal course on axial and sagittal views24. The RSMI was defined as the semi-automatically measured cross-sectional area of the rectal muscularis propria, normalised by the square of the patient’s height (mm²/m²).

CT and MR images were acquired by certified radiologic technologists at our institution. For extraction of the smooth muscle layer, image segmentation was performed by a dedicated specialist with 10 years of experience (Fujifilm Medical Co., Ltd.). Subsequent quantitative analyses, including RSMI calculation, were independently conducted by two board-certified gastrointestinal surgeons, each with 11 years of post-training experience. All procedures were performed blinded to clinical outcomes. Quantitative measurements were obtained using standardized semi-automated segmentation with SYNAPSE VINCENT® software, minimizing operator-dependent variability. No additional study-specific training was required beyond routine clinical imaging practice.

Histological evaluation

Resected sections were obtained from paraffin-embedded tissue blocks used for pathological classification from a randomly selected cohort of 30 patients. The sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin and visually assessed using a microscope (BZ-X800; Keyence Corporation, Osaka, Japan). The smooth muscle layer thickness was quantified as the mean of three arbitrarily chosen points in each rectal section, located 1–3 cm oral to the tumour. Three sections from each patient were used for quantification.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the correlation between the PMI and RSMI. The secondary endpoints were the associations between the RSMI and various clinical covariates. We evaluated the impact of the RSMI on postoperative complications, as well as the changes in the PMI, VFA, and BMI at 6 months postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using JMP version 14.2.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University), a graphical user interface for R (www.r-project.org).

Comparison between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationship between the PMI and RSMI, and between smooth muscle thickness and the RSMI. ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate the ability of the RSMI to predict sarcopenia, with the optimal cutoff determined by the Youden Index. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with the RSMI and complications, incorporating variables with p < 0.1 in univariate analyses. ORs and CIs were reported. ROC analysis and XGBoost Decision Boundary were used to assess the predictive performance of the PMI and RSMI for complications. Comparison across four groups postoperatively using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by the Steel-Dwass test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
