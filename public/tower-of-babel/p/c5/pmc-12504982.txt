Optimization of astaxanthin extraction from red (Gracilaria corticata) and brown (Sargassum polycystum) macroalgae through ultrasonication and microwave processing
Authors: Parisa Feizi, Seid Mahdi Jafari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Carotenoids, Ionic microemulsion, Extraction efficiency, Algae

Introduction

Macroalgae appear red, green and brown based on their external color and are classified as Rhodophyceae, Chlorophyceae and Phaeophyceae, respectively [1]. Macroalgal natural pigments are an excellent alternative to harmful chemical dyes used in the production of commercial products. Not only are pigments attractive, but also, they are valuable for human health (acting as antioxidant, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, anti-obesity, and neuroprotective agents) [2]. The antioxidant activity (AxAc) of these pigments helps in the elimination of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, which play a vital role in the development of various diseases and metabolic disorders such as cancer, diabetes, cataracts and aging. Carotenoids are unsaturated hydrocarbons belonging to the tetraterpene group (terpenoids with 40 carbon atoms). About 700 types of carotenoids have been reported, 30 types of which contribute to photosynthesis. Carotenoids are divided into two categories: xanthophylls and carotenes. Astaxanthin (ASX) is a pigment of the xanthophyll family and is one of the most famous carotenoid pigments. Structurally, ASX has 13 double bonds, making it a strong antioxidant [3]. ASX is found in various microorganisms such as algae, bacteria, and yeast.

The brown algae Sargassum polycystum (SP), an edible seaweed, is rich in dietary fiber, vitamins, minerals, and polysaccharides. This seaweed is a common food source in Asian countries. Recently, various bioactive compounds have been identified in SP with antioxidant, antibacterial, and antitumor properties. Taken together, these properties make this seaweed very useful and functional for a wide range of biomedical applications [4]. In another study, AxAc of phenolic compounds from three brown macroalgae species (Colpomenia sinuosa, Cystoseira myrica, and Sargassum swartzii) collected from the coasts of the Persian Gulf in the Assaluyeh region was investigated [5]; the results showed that S. swartzii was a rich source of phenolics having AxAc. On the other hand, among red algae, the genus Gracilaria contains a wide variety of valuable contents for human nutrition and is one of the most widely cultivated and valuable seaweeds in the world. Its lipid content is low (1–5 % of dry weight), but it contains docosahexaenoic acid, known as the most important unsaturated fatty acid for reducing the risk of cardiovascular diseases, acting as an excellent antioxidant, strengthening cell membranes and repairing damaged cells and tissues, improving heart function and fighting cancer [6,7]. Nevertheless, the nutritional profile of seaweeds such as Gracilaria is influenced by various factors such as seaweed species, habitat, maturity stage, season, water temperature, and sampling conditions and methods [8]. Gracilaria corticata (GC) is widely distributed on the coasts of Iran in the Persian Gulf and has good potential for antioxidants extraction.

Various methods have been used to extract ASX from algae; but nowadays, conventional extraction techniques have been replaced by advanced methods e.g., microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), supercritical fluid extraction, Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE), and pressurized liquid extraction, which are more efficient and require less solvent [9]. These extraction techniques are environmentally friendly, and able to extract a large number of samples in a shorter extraction time (t) than conventional methods [10]. MAE is based on the absorption of microwave energy by polar molecules [11]. For MAE, the moisture in the material is used as a target for microwave heating. When exposed to high thermal stresses, local high pressure, and microwave heating, moisture in the food is heated inside the cells and evaporated. The pressure resulting from the evaporation of moisture causes the cell wall to rupture, facilitating the leakage of bioactives from the damaged cells into the surrounding solvent, thereby improving the extraction efficiency (EE) [12]. The main mechanism of UAE is cavitation and the formation of very small bubbles in the liquid, which then grow rapidly and burst. The mechanical effects of ultrasonic waves and the cavitation phenomenon caused by these waves increase the penetration of the solvent into the sample, enhance mass transfer, and subsequently ascend EE at lower temperatures [13].

On the other hand, in recent years, to reduce the effects of volatile and toxic solvents, hydrophilic ionic liquids (ILs) have been used as dispersing solvents and hydrophobic ILs as extracting solvents. ILs are a salt in liquid form, considered among green solvents due to their tunable physicochemical properties, high chemical and thermal stability, and negligible vapor pressure at room temperature, and have a molecular structure consisting of various cations and anions [14]. However, the viscosity of most ILs is higher than that of organic solvents (ORS), which consequently leads to a decrease in the mass transfer rate. Microemulsion (MEL) is a promising solvent that enables selective extraction of biomolecules in the food and chemical industries [15]. MELs are a type of emulsion system that, unlike common emulsions, are thermodynamically stable and their dispersed phase size is about 10–100 nm. MELs have desirable properties such as thermodynamic stability, low viscosity, and high solubility capacity for hydrophilic and lipophilic compounds [16]. In this research, tributyloctylphosphonium bromide (TBOPB) was used as a source of MEL.

Regarding toxicity, phosphonium ILs (like TBOPB) are generally more toxic than ammonium or imidazolium-based ILs, both to human cells and to aquatic organisms [17]. Their toxicity often increases with longer alkyl side chains (hydrophobicity enhances interaction with lipid membranes, leading to membrane disruption and cytotoxicity) [18]. Phosphonium ILs have been shown to cause cytotoxicity in mammalian cells, primarily via mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress [19]. On the other hand, onion contribution (bromide) itself is not highly toxic in moderate concentrations, but the overall toxicity is governed mainly by the phosphonium cation [20]. In case of environmental concerns, TBOPB and similar phosphonium ILs are typically poorly biodegradable; in fact, they are structurally resistant to microbial breakdown due to their bulky, hydrophobic alkyl chains and stable phosphonium core [21]. Their lipophilic nature means they may partition into biological membranes and potentially bioaccumulate in aquatic organisms [19]. As to safety concerns of TBOPB, it is classified as an irritant and may be harmful if inhaled, ingested, or in contact with skin; high concentrations can lead to acute toxicity in lab workers (so gloves, goggles, and fume hoods are recommended). To date, MELs have been used to extract proteins, pigments, and trace elements. [22] extracted ASX from shrimp waste using UAE and ILs (MEL). MEL containing TBOPB significantly increased ASX extraction (ASXEX) due to stronger electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding [23]. Feizi et al. [24] also did ASXEX from shrimp and crustacean waste using ILs-MEL (MELL), oil solvent and ORS and reported that MELL had higher EE than other solvents and is a suitable option for ASXEX compared to common solvents.

Shaeri et al. [25] aimed at using the percolation method and MAE to extract phenolic, carotenoid and chlorophyll compounds from red algae. In this study, the variables of solvent type (water, ethanol and methanol), t (10, 20 and 30 min), extraction power (P; 90, 180 and 270 W) and solvent to sample ratio (SSRatio) (5:1, 12.5:1 and 20:1) were used in MAE. SSRatio and the square power of the solvent type had significant effects (p < 0.05) on the chlorophyll content of the extracts. For the extraction of carotenoids by MAE, the solvent type and SSRatio had a significant effect on EE (p < 0.05). Zou et al. [26] investigated the use of UAE for the effective ASXEX from Haematococcus pluvialis. Some parameters such as extraction solvent, liquid-to-solid ratio, extraction temperature and t were optimized by single factor experiment and response surface methodology (RSM). The optimal extraction conditions were 48 % ethanol in ethyl acetate, 20:1 liquid-to-solid ratio (mL/g), and 16 min (t) at 41.1 °C under 200 W UAE. The results obtained are useful for the full utilization of H. pluvialis, and also showed that UAE is a very useful method for ASXEX from marine organisms. In another study by Aslanbay Guler et al. [27], ASXEX from H. pluvialis using MAE was investigated. The MAE process at 75 °C and 700 W for 7 min resulted in the highest pigment EE (12.24 mg/g cells).

Therefore, considering the importance of ASXEX, this study intended to optimize ASXEX by MAE and UAE using an ORS (a mixture of ethanol and ethyl acetate), a green solvent (MELL: TBOPB as the hydrophobic part in water, where phosphonium forms the cationic part and bromide forms the anionic part), and vegetable oil (sunflower oil, SFO) from red (GC) and brown (SP) algae. Also, this study aimed at comparing the efficiencies of MAE and UAE methods as well as efficiencies of different solvents in ASXEX. In addition, the antioxidant activity and other properties of the extracted ASX were analyzed. This study is the first to investigate the use of red (GC) and brown (SP) algae, which contain an acceptable number of carotenoids and other valuable substances such as proteins and essential fatty acids, as a source for the extraction of ASX. The combination of new extraction methods with suitable solvents has shown promising potential to improve extraction efficiency.

Materials and methods

Materials

SP and GC were obtained from the Persian Gulf Research Institute after molecular identification and species confirmation. Commercial ASX (purity > 98 %), DPPH (2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) and BHT (Butylated hydroxytoluene) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co., Germany. Ethanol, propanol and ethyl acetate (chromatographic purity) and TBOPB and Triton X-100 (purity > 99 %) and n-butanol (purity 99.9 %) were bought from Sigma-Aldrich Co., Germany. Refined SFO without antioxidants was also prepared from Hayat Company, Iran.

Sample and solvent preparation

After being transported to the lab, the SP and GC were washed several times with tap water and finally with distilled water to remove sand and impurities. Then, the samples were dried without crushing for 48 h at lab temperature (22 °C) and away from sunlight. At the end, they were completely crushed using an electric grinder and the resulting powder was kept at 4 °C after sieving until extraction. The green solvent used in this research was a MELL in water, which was prepared by stirring only for the spontaneous formation [22]: TBOPB:Triton X-100– n-butanol (3:1 ratio): water was mixed in mass ratios of 0.13:0.25:0.62, respectively. These compounds constituted the non-polar phase, surfactant-cosurfactant, and polar phase of the MEL, respectively. This solvent was stored at room temperature, with ethanol being used for the control sample.

Extraction by sonication and microwave treatment

The method of Zou et al. [26] was used for UAE. Extraction with different solvents was performed in an ultrasonic bath at a frequency of 40 kHz and a temperature of 41.1 °C at different SSRatio, P, and durations. Then, the extract was centrifuged at room temperature and 8500 rpm for 10 min, followed by separating the supernatant, and re-extracting the precipitated part according to the previous method. After centrifugation, the upper part was combined with the previous part and filtered by a 0.45 µm Teflon filter. For MAE, after mixing the sample powders at room temperature in the specified ratios with the solvents, MAE was performed at a frequency of 2.45 GHz and different powers and durations. After extraction, when the solvent was an ORS, the solution was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at room temperature, and then the supernatant was separated and filtered with a 0.45 µm filter. However, when the extraction was carried out using a green solvent or through dissolution in SFO, the extract was first diluted with ethanol and then filtered [28].

Evaluation of physical properties of microemulsion

To measure the conductivity and density of MEL, a conductivity meter (inoLab, Germany) and a pycnometer (Hubbard, Germany) were used. The diameter of MEL was determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Horiba, Japan).

Evaluation of total carotenoid content (TCC)

TCC was determined using spectrophotometric measurements [29]. To determine TCC, the absorbance of 4 mL extracted solutions was measured by UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) at 450 nm; a specific extinction coefficient of ASX = 2100 was used. Statistical analysis

To obtain and optimize the extraction method with the highest ASX content, RSM was used, because in this method, the optimal extraction conditions can be introduced by examining the main and interactive effects of variables. In UAE, SSRatio (X1) of 5 to 20, the P (X2) of 60–200 W and the t (X3) of 5 to 30 min were the independent variables; for MAE, SSRatio (X1) of 5 to 20, the P (X2) of 100–300 W and the t (X3) of 5 to 110 s were the independent variables. The range of time, power and solvent-to-sample ratio were selected as averages from similar studies with acceptable extraction results and were determined through literature regarding ASXEX from various sources. The amount of extracted ASX, measured by a spectrophotometer, was considered as the dependent variable. The central composite design of RSM was applied in this study. Each of the independent variables was coded at five levels as 0, ±1 and ± α values, increasing the accuracy of the work. Using these values, Design Expert software version 13 estimated extraction conditions for each solvent in both UAE and MAE, with six replicates at the central point (for detection of the experimental errors). Results and discussion

Physical properties of microemulsions

In this study, the density of the MELs was 0.97 g/cm3, their diameter was 15.80 nm (Fig. 1), and their conductivity was 312 µS/cm at 27.1 °C. Studying the physical properties of the solvent and their temperature dependence is important for the proper design and operation of extraction processes. However, the physical properties of MELL are scarce and ignored in previous studies. The high conductivity of MEL compared to the ethanol:ethyl acetate (1:2) mixture is due to the presence of dissolved ionic species which are freely present in the solution and tend to move. On the other hand, the small size indicates the stability of the solvent. The high conductivity of MELL compared to ethanol:ethyl acetate (1:2) mixture results in better solvent-sample interaction and higher pigment extraction [22]. Nevertheless, the viscosity of most ILs is higher than that of ORS, which leads to a decrease in the mass transfer rate. MEL is a promising solvent that allows the selective extraction of biomolecules in the food and chemical industries [15].

As shown in Fig. S1 and S2 (Supplementary Data), TCC extracted from GC and SP using ORS, MELL and SFO increased as SSRatio, P or t increased. The high SSRatio activates the diffusion movement of the intracellular matrix to the extracellular matrix, ultimately increasing the extracted carotenoids. The underlying reason is that the effect of the cavitation phenomenon is more pronounced at higher solvent volumes [33]. Moreover, increasing the P of sonication enhances the amount of extracted compounds due to easier tissue breakdown, which, in turn, causes the rapid release of pigments and intratissue materials as targets [34]. During extraction, the solvent diffuses into the sample and dissolves compounds with similar polarity; thus, it seems obvious that EE increases with t, but can eventually reach a maximum at some point in time. Determining the end t is also an important factor in the type of extracted component because raising the t can lead to increased pigment decomposition and/or the conversion of cis isomers to trans forms and vice versa [35]. In the study of Liu et al. [36], different methods of electric fields, UAE, high-pressure microfluidization, hydrochloric acid with acetone, and ILs were used to extract ASX. The results showed that ILs and the combined method of hydrochloric acid and acetone led to the most favorable results, part of which was similar to the study of Park et al. [37] who investigated the effect of UAE on Haematococcus pluvialis. The results showed that UAE acted as a stress source in H. pluvialis cells containing ASX and led to the production of more ASX.

According to our results, TCC was extracted more by MELL than by ORS or SFO. This issue can be explained by several physicochemical factors related to solubility, microstructure, and interaction of the solvents with the macroalgae matrix and carotenoid: (i) ILs are known for their tunability; their polarity, hydrogen bonding ability and hydrophobicity can be designed for specific targets like carotenoids [38,39]. In our MELL, TBOPB is a hydrophobic IL, meaning it likely has high affinity for lipophilic carotenoids. The phosphonium cation can interact with the conjugated double bond system in carotenoids via π-cation interaction, stabilizing the molecules in solution [40]. (ii) MELs are thermodynamically stable systems of oil, water, and surfactant, forming nano-sized droplets that provide large interfacial surface area for mass transfer, ability to penetrate algal cell walls more effectively, and improved solubilization of hydrophobic compounds (like carotenoids) within the micellar or droplet core [41]. Thus, carotenoids embedded in algal members are more easily solubilized into the hydrophobic core of MELL droplets. (iii) Synergistic effect with ultrasound: UAE caused cavitation, which disrupts cell walls and increases solvent penetration [42,43]. MELL, due to their low viscosity and nano size, can infiltrate cells more effectively when assisted by ultrasound than bulkier ORS or SFO [44]. This synergy increases the carotenoid release and solubilization. (iv) Though ORS has moderate polarity and can extract carotenoids, it is less structured and less tunable than MELL. Moreover, ORS may evaporate quickly during sonication, affecting EE [45]. Also, SFO is very lipophilic and good at dissolving carotenoids, but has high viscosity, which limits mass transfer, has large molecular size, which hinders penetration into the algal matrix, and has no amphiphilic properties to assist in diffusion or cell wall disruption [46].

The results of ANOVA for the quadratic polynomial model terms (not shown) indicated that the fitted models for ASX extracted by UAE from GC and SP using all solvents were significant. Accordingly, the R2 for all solvents were > 0.5672. For ASXEX from SP by MELL and SFO, the linear effects of SSRatio, P and t were significant, and for ORS, the linear effect of SSRatio was significant. On the other hand, the results of ASXEX from GC showed that with MELL, the factors of SSRatio and t were significant while for SFO, the effect of P, the interaction of SSRatio and P, and the interaction of P and t were significant. Meanwhile, for ORS, the linear effect of P, and t, the interaction of SSRatio and P, the interaction of SSRatio and t, and the interaction of P and t were significant.

Fig. 2 and Fig. S3 shows that the amount of ASX increased with the simultaneous enhancement in extraction factors. Sharayei et al. [47], also, reported that increasing the t from 5 to 15 min ascended the extracted ASX content. Sahin et al. [48] reported that the effective extraction of bioactives by UAE, as a function of time, occurs in two main stages: (i) “washing”, occurs in the first 10 to 20 min by dissolving the soluble components on the surface of raw materials. In this stage, up to 90 % of the effective compounds is exposed to extraction process. (ii) “slow extraction”, is the mass transfer from the surface of raw material to the solvent by diffusion. Dahmoune et al. [49] claimed that the extraction of bioactives from the leaves of P. Lentiscus intensified after 10 min. The higher ASXEX from brown macroalgae (SP) compared to GC during UAE can be attributed to higher and more accessible ASX content in SP, softer cell wall structure (alginates, cellulose) of SP compared to dense (agar, carrageenan) cell wall structure of GC, better ultrasound response of SP (better cavitation and disruption) in comparison with limited ultrasound response of GC (due to rigid gel network) and more efficient solvent penetration in SP compared to hindered solvent penetration (by dense wall matrix) in GC [50,51].

Microwave-assisted extraction

The results of ANOVA for the quadratic polynomial model terms indicated that the fitted models for TCC by MAE from GC and SP using all three solvents were significant, with R2 > 0.6456. For SP, the interaction effect of SSRatio and P as well as the quadratic effect of P for MELL, the linear effect of SSRatio, the interaction of SSRatio and P, the interaction of SSRatio and t for SFO and the linear effect of SSRatio and P for ORS were significant. On the other hand, for GC, the linear effect of SSRatio, P and t for ORS, the linear effect of SSRatio and the interaction of P and t for SFO were significant; at the same t, for MELL, except for the linear effect of P, all other model parameters were significant. According to Fig. 4, with increasing SSRatio and decreasing P, TCC increased. Simultaneously, Fig. S5 shows that the higher SSRatio and the shorter the t, the higher the TCC. In materials containing some moisture, as the microwave energy is absorbed and, subsequently, converted into thermal energy, the moisture begins to evaporate, causing pressure to be created inside the cell wall and leading to the disruption of the cells and ultimately the release of bioactives into the surrounding solvent, and enhancing EE. Using higher microwave P for a longer t might be a more effective strategy for extracting phenolics [52].

The mixture of polar and non-polar solvents (water and TBOPB) used in microwave irradiation exhibited a higher tendency toward the phospholipids like PUFAs from the microalgal cell wall. Furthermore, the use of polar and non-polar solvents for recovery prevented losses of PUFAs by a reduction in the oxidation. Further, a highly polar solvent, like water with a dielectric constant of 80 at 20 °C, when in contact with solvent, tends to absorb microwave energy and expand to the dielectric loss which may result in the conversion of microwave energy into heat [53]. These two factors may amend the IL-based MAE in terms of efficiency and performance. Such improvement can trigger the PUFA production as a result of microalgae cell disruption. PUFAs are considered as the main component of microalgae polar lipids (i.e., glycolipids and phospholipids) [54]. The highest mount was extracted by IL in water medium-based microwave irradiation. Hence, this potential of MAE leads to a high percentage of health advantageous compounds [55,56]. These outcomes proved the high proficiency of IL-based microwave irradiation as a heating medium for the extraction by preserving the premier quality of the pigments.

TCC was extracted more from SP than from GC. This can be due to: (i) Natural pigment composition differences; SP is rich in fucoxanthin, a major carotenoid that plays a key role in light-harvesting [57]. Fucoxanthin content in brown algae can be significantly higher than TCC in red algae [58]. Daylight emits blue and green lights, which can be harvested by beta-carotene, lutein, and violaxanthin pigments commonly found in microalgae [59]. Additionally, blue light can disrupt photosynthetic efficiency by affecting chlorophyll absorption, leading to more light-induced damage. This disruption further prompts algae to produce more carotenoids to counteract oxidative damage, maintain cellular function, and protect the photosynthetic machinery and cellular membranes by quenching excess energy. Moreover, light affects the photosynthetic system by changing how energy is distributed between photosystem I and photosystem II. This imbalance can cause PSII to become overexcited, leading to photoinhibition and more production [60]. To counter this imbalance, algae are seen to increase carotenoid production, which helps absorb the excess light energy and safely dissipate it as heat, protecting the photosynthetic system from damage [61].

GC, on the other hand, mainly contains phycobiliproteins (e.g. phycoerythrin) as primary pigments, with lower levels of carotenoids e.g., β-carotene, zeaxanthin, and lutein. These carotenoids are present in smaller amounts and may be less extractable under the same conditions [62]. (ii) Microwave treatment heats materials by dielectric heating – interacting with polar molecules, especially water, causing rapid internal heating. This leads to cell wall disruption via stream pressure, enhanced solvent diffusion into cells, and improved pigment release from intracellular compartments [63]. SP has more microwave susceptible cell structures (alginates, cellulose) than GC, whose cell walls are rich in dense, gel-like polysaccharides (agar, carrageenan) that resist solvent penetration and heat diffusion [64]. (iii) In brown algae, fucoxanthin and other carotenoids are mainly located in chloroplast membranes, which are easier to disrupt with heat [65]. In red algae, carotenoids may be more tightly bound to proteins or structural matrices, requiring more intense or prolonged treatment to be released [66].

The results of ANOVA (not shown) indicated that the fitted models for microwave-extracted ASX from GC and SP using all solvents were significant, with R2 > 0.6557. For brown macroalgae and using ORS, the linear effects of SSRatio, P and t were significant, but for MEL, the linear effects of SSRatio and P were significant while using SFO, in addition to the linear effects, the interaction of extraction factors was also significant. For red macroalgae, the linear effects of SSRatio and P for ORS, the linear effects of SSRatio for SFO and the linear effect of SSRatio, the interaction of SSRatio and P, the interaction of SSRatio and t, the interaction of P and t for MELL were significant. According to Fig. S6 and S7, as SSRatio enhanced, and P and t decreased, the amount of extracted ASX increased. Aslanbay Guler et al. [27] extracted ASX from H. pluvialis algae and reported that MAE process at 75 °C and 700 W for 7 min led to the highest EE (12.24 mg/g cells).

The higher ASXEX from brown macroalgae by MAE compared to UAE can be explained by key differences in the mechanisms of cell disruption, heating efficiency, and mass transfer enhancement provided by each method: (i) Microwave heating causes rapid internal heating and cell rupture. Microwave irradiation generates heat directly inside cells by interacting with polar molecules (like water) in the biomass. This causes localized high temperatures and pressure inside cells, cell wall rupture due to stream generation and pressure buildup, faster and more complete release of intracellular compounds like ASX [67]. Ultrasound mainly disrupts cells via acoustic cavitation (microbubble collapse), which is mechanical and more localized and gradual [68]. (ii) Better solvent penetration and diffusion with microwave. The rapid heating from microwaves reduces solvent viscosity, increases solute diffusion rates, and improves solvent-matrix interaction, especially in viscous or complex systems like MELL and oils [69]. Ultrasound, while effective, is less efficient in deep tissue penetration, especially with thick-walled macroalgae cells. (iii) Macroalgae cell wall resistance. SP has cell walls made of cellulose, alginate, and fucoidans, which can be thermally softened by microwaves [70]. MAE helps overcome the resistance of this matrix more effectively than the mechanical effects of ultrasound, especially when aiming to extract carotenoids tightly embedded in plastids or membranes [71].

In conclusion, MAE was more effective for extracting ASX from brown algae due to its ability to: (I) Rapidly disrupt cells from the inside, (II) improve solute–solvent interactions, (III) overcome structural resistance of algal tissues more efficiently than ultrasound.

The results of ANOVA for the quadratic polynomial model terms (not shown) indicated that the fitted models for the EE by MAE from GC and SP using all three solvents were significant, with R2 > 0.6048. Considering SP, the linear effect of SSRatio and t for MELL, the linear effect of P, the interaction of P and t, and the quadratic effect of SSRatio for SFO, the linear and quadratic effect of SSRatio and the interaction of SSRatio and t for ORS were significant. On the other hand, for GC, the quadratic effect of P, the interaction of SSRatio and P, as well as the interaction of SSRatio and t for MELL, the linear effect of t and the quadratic effect of SSRatio for SFO, and the linear effect of P and t for ORS were significant. Fig. S8 and S9 shows that the lower the P and t and the higher SSRatio, the higher EE. In MAE, EE increases when a sample with an appropriate solvent ratio is exposed to microwave waves. Tsiaka et al. [33] optimized the extraction of carotenoid pigments from shrimp waste using MAE and reported an optimum SSRatio = 20, which is in line with the findings of the present study. Due to the oxidative damage effect of microwave radiation on carotenoid pigments, the shorter the t, the better the pigments preservation.

Optimization results

Considering the high importance of ASX pigment, the aim of this study was to optimize the conditions for ASXEX through UAE and MAE via ORS, SFO and MELL from GC and SP by maximizing ASXEX, TCC and EE. According to UAE results, a SSRatio = 20:1, P = 200 W and t = 30 min were selected as optimal conditions for extracting ASX from GC and SP. Under these conditions, for SP by MELL extraction, TCC was 86.7 mL/g, ASX 84.0 mg/mL, and EE 98.4 %; for ORS extraction, TCC = 52.8 mL/g, ASX = 50.4 mg/mL, and EE = 95.3 %. Finally, for SFO extraction, TCC = 54.1 mL/g, ASX = 48.0 mg/mL, and EE = 95.5 % with a desirability of 92.7 % (Fig. 5a). For GC and using MELL as the solvent, TCC = 64.8 mL/g, ASX = 54.5 mg/mL, and EE = 94.3 %. For ORS extraction, TCC = 33.9 mL/g, ASX = 33.2 mg/mL, and EE = 87.1 %. At last, for SFO extraction, TCC = 7.7 mL/g, ASX = 7.6 mg/mL, and EE = 72.9 % with a desirability of 93.4 % (Fig. 5b).

According to MAE results, for extracting ASX from GC and SP, SSRatio = 20:1, P = 100 W, and t = 5 s were selected as the optimal conditions. Under these conditions, for SP by MELL extraction, TCC = 93.5 mL/g, ASX = 89.7 mg/mL, and EE = 99.6 %. For ORS extraction, TCC = 78.8 mL/g, ASX = 54.5 mg/mL, and EE = 96.9 %. For SFO extraction, TCC = 54.1 mL/g, ASX = 50.8 mg/mL, and EE = 97.0 % with a desirability of 94.1 % (Fig. 5c). For GC by MELL extraction, TCC = 72.8 mL/g, ASX = 60.1 mg/mL, and EE = 98.5 %. For ORS extraction, TCC = 33.9 mL/g, ASX = 12.9 mg/mL, and EE = 90.2 %. For SFO extraction, TCC = 9.5 mL/g, ASX = 7.2 mg/mL, and EE = 83.6 % with a desirability of 89.0 % (Fig. 5d). The findings showed that MAE was a more efficient method than UAE for extracting ASX because the amount of ASX extracted in MAE was higher. Furthermore, among the solvents used, MELL had a higher EE than the other two solvents. Among the two sources used for extracting ASX, SP resulted in a higher amount of ASX than from GC. Khoo et al. [30] used ILs as a preferred solvent to penetrate the cell wall of H. pluvialis algae and extract ASX, and showed that the use of ILs increased EE of ASX.

Antioxidant activity

As shown in Fig. 6, AxAc of ASX extracted by UAE was higher than that by MAE, which may be due to less thermal damage in the former method. The results also showed that AxAc of ASX extracted in both methods by SFO was higher than other solvents. Although the pigment extracted by SFO had AxAc close to BHT, AxAc of BHT was always higher. In general, the increased AxAc of ASX extracted by antioxidant-free SFO compared to other solvents can be attributed to the better protection of the pigment in SFO. In addition, the ASX pigment is better protected in SFO and is kept away from contact with ambient oxygen and retains its AxAc longer. In MELL solvent, despite having the highest EE, it had lower AxAc than SFO owing to partial oxidation or possible subsequent damage during the storage period. According to the results of this study, depending on the type of solvent, AxAc will also vary. ASX has very specific chemical properties due to its molecular structure and plays an important role in the removal of free radicals and heavy metals. The presence of keto moieties on the ionic ring is responsible for the high AxAc of this compound. ASX traps free radicals both in the unsaturated conjugated chain (poly-ene) and in the terminal rings (C3 rings) [72].

Conclusion

In this study, UAE and MAE were used for ASXEX from brown (SP) and red (GC) macroalgae, which decrease the volume of solvent used and enhance EE. Due to the problems caused by the separation of solvents (through conventional solvents e.g. ORS and SFO) from the products, ASX can be effectively extracted using green solvents (e.g., ILs), as carried out in this research. Our results showed that MELL solvent performed more effectively in ASXEX due to the hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions with ASX. The higher ASXEX from SP compared to GC during UAE can be attributed to higher and more accessible ASX content of SP in comparison with low ASX content of red algae, softer cell wall structure (alginates, cellulose) of SP compared to dense (agar, carrageenan) cell wall structure of GC, better ultrasound response of SP (better cavitation and disruption) in comparison with limited ultrasound response of GC (due to rigid gel network) and more efficient solvent penetration in SP compared to hindered solvent penetration (by dense wall matrix) in GC. Moreover, UAE (compared with MAE) preserved the structure and AxAc of ASX due to the lower temperature during extraction. However, the amount of extracted ASX, TCC, and EE in MAE were higher than those in UAE method. MAE was more effective for extracting ASX due to its ability to rapidly disrupt cells from the inside, improve solute–solvent interactions, and overcome structural resistance of algal tissues more efficiently than UAE. Based on our results, red (GC) and brown (SP) macroalgae can be suggested as accessible and inexpensive sources for extracting valuable compounds such as bioactives and the natural pigment astaxanthin.
