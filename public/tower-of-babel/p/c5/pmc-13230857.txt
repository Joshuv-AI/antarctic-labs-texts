Extensive genetic interactions (epistasis) linked to alcohol use disorder in a high-risk population
Authors: Stanislav Listopad, Qian Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlcohol use disorder (AUD) is known to have a significant genetic component, yet there remains a gap between its heritability and findings from genome-wide association studies. One potential explanation for this could be genetic interactions, or epistasis, which remain largely unexplored in the context of AUD. We investigated the role of epistasis in AUD susceptibility among 742 American Indians. By analyzing 467 K variants in 3,736 genes and regulatory elements linked to AUD, we identified 97 interacting gene pairs significantly associated with AUD severity in an American Indian cohort. Five of these gene pairs: CNTNAP2-GRM8, CSMD1-DLGAP1, CSMD1-ERBB4, CSMD1-MAML2, and KCNQ5-ROBO2 - were replicated in All of Us research American Indian cohort (N = 5,037). These genes were enriched for immune system, cell adhesion, neuronal, and disease pathways. Their expressions were particularly enriched in midbrain GABAergic neurons. This large-scale epistasis study of AUD suggests that epistasis may contribute to the development of AUD.Subject terms: Diagnostic markers, Addiction, Genome-wide association studies, Quantitative trait

Introduction

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a medical condition characterized by an impaired ability to stop or control alcohol use despite experiencing adverse social, occupational, or health consequences (National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, 2020). According to the 2023 National Survey on Drug Use and Health, approximately 28.9 million people aged 12 and older - representing 8.7% of the U.S. population - had AUD in the past year. Rates of AUD also vary across different populations and ethnic groups, with American Indians (AI) showing the highest prevalence in the U.S. (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2023). Like many other complex diseases, the risk of developing AUD is influenced by both environmental and genetic factors, with twin studies suggesting that heritability is about 50%1.

With increasingly large sample sizes, meta-analyses of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have begun to yield more robust results for AUD2. While GWAS is a powerful tool for genetic mapping, most of the variants identified thus far have shown only small effects, explaining a limited portion ( < 10%) of the AUD heritability2. A number of factors have been proposed to account for this issue of “missing heritability”, with genetic interactions being one potential key contributor3–5. GWAS typically tests genetic effects of single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) one at a time using linear or logistic regression, assuming additive and independent effects across loci. However, this approach does not account for potential combinatorial effects that go beyond the linear model. When the phenotypic effects from two genetic variants deviate from the expected additive value of the individual mutations, the variants are said to exhibit a statistical epistatic interaction6. Meanwhile, when two or more biological components physically interact, this is referred to as biological epistasis7,8. A classic example of epistasis is albinism, in which dysfunctional mutations in the gene responsible for pigment production render the pigment genes themselves irrelevant9. Once treated as an exception, epistasis is now increasingly recognized as a common and important phenomenon in genetics10,11. Epistasis has been observed in a variety of complex human traits and diseases, including body mass index (BMI) and Alzheimer’s disease12–14. Studies have shown that disease-related genes have a high propensity to interact with each other15. Furthermore, many genetic variants linked to complex diseases are located in non-coding regions, suggesting that they may exert their effects through regulatory interactions16.

Genetic interactions, however, have rarely been explored systematically in studies on the genetics of alcohol use and use disorder. Previous research into epistasis in AUD cohorts has mainly focused on a few candidate genes17–24, such as those related to alcohol metabolic enzymes (ADH and ALDH) and dopamine receptor (DRD2)25,26. One of the interactions identified in relation to AUD was between ADH1B and ADH7 in a Han Chinese population27. There is also one notable study from 2016 that conducted epistasis analysis in relation to alcohol dependence symptom count that revealed a small number of potentially significant interactions28. This limited focus likely stems from the assumption that statistical power is insufficient for detecting genetic interactions on a large scale. More recently, large-scale multi-omics studies have begun to identify regulatory interactions, spanning methylation, transcriptional regulation, and chromatin accessibility, that impact AUD or alcohol use traits29–35. A single-cell multi-omics study of postmortem human brain tissues identified five ligand–receptor signaling interactions between distinct cell types associated with AUD35. These findings further highlight the importance of interaction-based biological mechanisms of AUD and motivate genetic analyses that move beyond single-variant approaches to model gene–gene interactions. It has been shown that while the number of potentially interacting gene pairs is enormous, the overall genetic interaction density is low, and not all statistical interactions are necessarily biologically meaningful36. In this light, we hypothesize that AUD-related genes are more likely to be involved in genetic interactions than previously thought. By focusing further on functionally connected genes and regulatory elements known a priori, we can significantly increase the statistical power to detect these interactions. We applied these insights to an American Indian cohort, which exhibits a notably high lifetime prevalence of AUD, particularly in its severe form. In doing so, we conducted a large-scale epistasis analysis on an AUD phenotype. We began with a set of genes broadly associated with alcohol dependence and use disorders and expanded this list by leveraging biological networks. This approach led us to a comprehensive set of approximately 2300 genes and 1400 regulatory elements, encompassing nearly half a million SNPs. In this study, we focused on pairwise (2nd order) interactions and performed an exhaustive search for epistasis among all selected SNPs. We then applied a bi-clustering algorithm to exploit the correlations among neighboring SNPs, successfully identifying many pairs of significantly interacting genes associated with the severity of AUD. We further conducted a replication study in American Indian participants within the All of Us (AoU) research cohort37.

Results

Interacting SNP pairs

Interacting SNP pairs associated with AUD severity in the American Indian cohort (AI) (n = 742) were identified using rapid epistatic mixed model association analysis (REMMA)38. Out of 476,850 SNPs analyzed, 150,676 could be considered effectively independent due to linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure39. Using the number of effectively independent SNPs, we set p value at 4.4e-12 (Wald-Chi Square test) as the significance threshold for SNP x SNP interactions using Bonferroni correction. None of the interacting SNP pairs passed this significance threshold. Several SNP pairs were suggestively significant at p value < 4.4e-11 (Wald-Chi Square test) (Table 1). The top interacting SNPs are between one SNP on the insulin receptor gene (INSR) and multiple SNPs located on the cholinergic receptor nicotinic gamma subunit gene (CHRNG).

Significantly interacting gene and regulatory element pairs

Bi-clustering of the 1539 interacting SNP pairs identified 97 significant (p value < 1.22e-16, hypergeometric one-tailed test) and 3 suggestively significant (p value < 1.22e-15, hypergeometric one-tailed test) gene-gene interaction pairs associated with AUD severity (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1). In addition, 146 regulatory elements contained interacting SNPs, all located near or within the identified interacting genes. Reported p values were derived from hypergeometric one-tailed (enrichment) tests with correction for multiple testing. Of 175 genes and 146 regulatory elements involved in significant and suggestively significant interactions, 58 have been previously connected to alcohol use disorder. About half of the interacting pairs form small networks together with other interacting pairs. The largest interacting network contains 12 genes. For 31 gene and/or regulatory element pairs there existed prior evidence of potential biological interactions in scientific literature and/or pathway databases. Several interacting pairs were present within same neuronal system pathways such as Reactome’s transmission across chemical synapses40. The rest were present in various disease, immune system, and signaling pathways. The interacting pairs were distributed across all chromosomes except chromosome 22 (Fig. 2).

Pathway enrichment analysis of interacting genes

The significantly and suggestively significantly interacting gene pairs were enriched for, cell adhesion molecule (CAM), disease, immune system, and neuronal pathways (Supplementary Table 2)41. CAMs play a role in a variety of processes including homeostasis, immune response, inflammation, and development of neuronal tissue. CAMs have also been implicated in addiction due to their impact on neuroplasticity42–44. The enriched disease pathways encompassed autoimmune thyroid disease and diabetes pathways. The immune system pathways belonged to three categories: cytotoxicity regulation, major histocompatibility complex, and antigen processing. These immune system pathways are essential for the function of adaptive immune system. While the adaptive immune system is not traditionally considered to play a direct, primary role in AUD, it is heavily impacted by alcohol use45. Meanwhile, neuroinflammation as a whole, mediated by interleukins, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and toll-like receptors, plays a substantial role in the progression of AUD46–52. Additionally, the tissue specificity analysis demonstrated significant upregulation of genes for most brain regions including frontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, basal ganglia, hypothalamus, hippocampus and amygdala (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). The cell-type analysis identified enrichment for midbrain GABAergic neurons and neuroblasts and fetal lung visceral neurons (Supplementary Fig. 2). Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) is an inhibitory neurotransmitter that is known to play a major role in alcohol use disorder53. The genes regulated by interacting regulatory elements were enriched for gene transcription, cancer, neuronal, and various signaling pathways (Supplementary Table 3).

Replication in All of Us Research

We conducted replication analysis in the American Indian subset (NAUD = 744, NControl = 4293) of the All of Us cohort (AoU). Plink was used for epistasis analysis. Bi-clustering was performed twice, with input p value thresholds of 1e-8 (t-test) and 1e-9 (t-test) applied to Plink --epistasis output. The interacting interval significance threshold was 6.34e-16 (hypergeometric one-tailed test). Interacting intervals passing this threshold were mapped onto gene pairs. Out of 100 interacting gene pairs identified in the AI cohort, we replicated 3 of them in AoU with p < 1e-9 (t-test) bi-clustering configuration: CNTNAP2 x GRM8, CSMD1 x DLGAP1, and CSMD1 x ERBB4. Two additional gene pairs were replicated in AoU with p < 1e-8 (t-test) configuration: CSMD1 x MAML2 and KCNQ5 x ROBO2. Five of these 8 genes have been previously connected to AUD: CNTNAP2, GRM8, CSMD1, KCNQ5, and ROBO22,54–57.

Discussion

Understanding gene interactions is crucial to deciphering the structure and function of complex genetic systems that contribute to diseases. The first step in this process is identifying the scope of these interactions. To achieve this, we performed a large-scale epistasis analysis to investigate the genetic factors associated with AUD severity in an American Indian cohort, which has a notably high AUD prevalence of 70%, with half of those affected experiencing severe AUD. We then replicated the findings in the American Indian subset of All of Us cohort.

We identified a total of 100 interactions involving 175 genes and 146 regulatory elements that are significantly or suggestively significantly associated with the AUD severity phenotype in this AI population. Five of the significant gene-gene interactions were replicated in the AI subset of the All of Us cohort for AUD diagnosis. These interactions may occur across various biological contexts. While statistical epistasis does not always directly translate into biological epistasis, many of the epistatic pairs we identified have been previously proposed to interact biologically, either directly or through a few intermediaries. Notably, five of the gene pairs we found to interact had been previously suggested in the literature as potentially interacting: ASIC2 x KIF5B, CSMD1 x ERBB4, MTA3 x CDH2, MYCN x LDHA, and SF3B1 x HLA-B58–62. The ASIC2 and KIF5B may interact indirectly via Syntabulin. Meanwhile, CSMD1 may regulate epidermal growth factors, of which ERBB4 is a member. Another two gene pairs, CSMD1 x DLGAP1 and NRXN1 x CTNNA2, have been implicated as interacting by the protein-protein interaction database StringDB63. Notably, CSMD1 x DLGAP1 and CSMD1 x ERBB4 interactions were also replicated in AoU dataset. We did not replicate the ADH1B x ADH7 interaction previously identified in relation to AUD in the Han Chinese population27. This is not surprising, as the ADH1B allele rs1229984 implicated in the reported interaction has a MAF of 26% in the Han Chinese population64 but only 3% in our American Indian cohort.

Over a quarter of the interacting gene pairs are co-located within various biological pathways, suggesting potential mechanisms of interaction. The most common pathways involve neuronal pathways, followed by signaling pathways, human disease pathways, and immune system pathways. Notably, tissue- and cell-type specific analyses revealed that the interacting genes were most significantly enriched in the frontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, caudate, nucleus accumbens, and developmental midbrain GABAergic neurons, which play key roles in the development and progression of alcohol and substance use disorders. Interacting gene pairs that show both statistical and biological evidence of epistasis may represent particularly promising candidates for further investigation in future studies of AUD.

We have also examined potential impact of environmental factors such as socioeconomic status on AUD. The SES variables included years of education, current employment, work full time status, gross income, marriage, religion, and religious attendance (Supplementary Table 4). Only years of education demonstrated significant, p value = 1.4e-4, (Spearman Rho test) (negative) correlation with the AUD severity in this AI population, consistent with our previous findings57.

To overcome the limitations of statistical power in SNP-level epistasis analysis and to enhance the interpretability of the results, we adopted a strategy that integrated both hypothesis-driven and discovery-driven approaches. By prioritizing nearly half a million SNPs located in genes and regulatory elements linked to alcohol use and use disorders—both directly and indirectly—and accounting for SNP correlations, we were able to achieve significant findings, even with a moderate sample size.

The results of this study should be interpreted with several limitations in mind, however. First, the analyses were not designed to generate a comprehensive model of AUD in this community but rather to explore specific genetic interactions associated with AUD phenotypes. As such, a larger sample size, powered to assess genome-wide epistasis, would be necessary for more robust conclusions. Additionally, American Indians represent a diverse group both genetically and environmentally, meaning our findings may not be applicable to other American Indian populations. Prevalence of AUD, for instance, varies among tribes, which may limit the generalizability of our results65,66. This diversity however underscores the importance of identifying risk and resilience factors within specific communities, as these insights can help guide targeted prevention and intervention efforts. While we were able to conduct a replication analysis in AoU and replicated several gene-gene interaction findings, there were several major differences between our primary and replication cohorts. Our primary AI cohort was recruited from eight geographically contiguous reservations, while the American Indian participants of AoU cohort were primarily urban, self-identified AI across the United States, instead of specific tribal communities. Additionally, the primary cohort’s phenotype is a quantitative trait reflecting AUD severity that was derived from the clinical course of AUD67 based on Semi-Structured Assessment for the Genetics of Alcoholism (SSAGA)68,69, while the replication cohort AoU’s phenotype is based on electronic health record diagnosis of AUD. There is also a substantial difference in AUD prevalence—and likely severity—between the two cohorts. In the AI cohort, ~70% meet criteria for AUD with half of them classified as severe AUD, whereas AUD prevalence in the AI participant within AoU is substantially lower ( ~15%), and comparable data on AUD severity are not available. Given that alcohol and substance use risks in American Indian populations are generally understudied, this gap in research highlights the need for further exploration. While our specific findings may not be generalizable to other populations, the methodology used and the gene interactions identified for AUD in this study may be highly relevant to other groups, warranting further investigations.

In summary, this study represents a large-scale investigation into epistasis in the context of alcohol use disorders. By systematically studying how genetic interactions influence AUD traits and expanding the boundaries of traditional GWAS, we open the door to new insights into how genes work together and interact to drive the disorder, offering a more nuanced understanding of the complex genetic architecture of AUD that could lead to more effective prevention and treatment strategies. While some of our findings may be specific to an American Indian population, overall, our results suggest that genetic interactions could play a substantial role in the development and progression of AUD.

Methods

Participants

American Indian participants were recruited from eight geographically contiguous reservations. The reservations had a total population of about 3000 individuals. To be included in the study, participants had to be between the ages of 18 and 70 years, and mobile enough to be transported from their home to The Scripps Research Institute (TSRI). The cohort used in this study contained 742 individuals that have both the phenotype and genotype data. Full demographics of the population are listed in Supplementary Table 5. Notably, the participant cohort exhibited high degree of relatedness. The protocol for the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of TSRI, and the board of the Indian Health Council, a tribal review group overseeing health issues for the reservations where the recruitment was undertaken. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant after the study was fully explained. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Phenotype and genotypes

Participants were interviewed based on the Semi-Structured Assessment for the Genetics of Alcoholism (SSAGA)68,69. Diagnoses of lifetime DSM-5 AUD were generated using SSAGA. The interview also retrospectively asked about the occurrence of alcohol-related life events, and the age at which the problem first occurred, from which a quantitative phenotype, the severity level of AUD, was computed. The severity level of AUD was indexed by the 36 alcohol-related life events (Supplementary Table 6) in the clinical course of the disorder, with life events given a severity weight of 1 for events 1-12; 2 for 13–24; and 3 for 25–3666,70. AUD severity was then calculated as the sum of the severity weights of the 36 life events67. The AUD severity phenotype is highly correlated with AUD diagnoses (Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient is 0.9, p value = 5e-287, Spearman Rho test, Supplementary Fig. 3)71. Additional details regarding participant recruitment and SSAGA are given in Supplementary Method’s sections S1 and S2.

The whole genome sequencing pipeline of the AI cohort was previously described72. In brief: blood derived DNA was sequenced using Illumina low-coverage whole genome sequencing. The pair-end sequencing was performed on HiSeq2000 sequencers (Illumina). About 80% of the samples had coverage between 3X and 12X. The qualities of the variant calling were confirmed using an Affymetrix Exome1A chip. Reads from whole genome sequencing were aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 human reference genome using BMA: and realigned near indels with GATK73. Variants were called using both GATK Unified Genotyper following the best practices for low-coverage samples and the LD-aware variant caller Thunder74,75. Several quality control procedures were applied which resulted in a total of 10,648,122 SNPs. More details regarding sample processing and quality control can be found in Supplementary Method’s section S3.

Core gene set

We began by identifying a set of genes that were either associated with AUD, alcohol dependence (AD), or were direct molecular targets of ethanol, which resulted in 506 genes (Supplementary Table 7). We refer to this set of genes as the core gene set. The sources of the core gene set are: 1) AUD associated genes in Diseases, Disgenet, and MalaCards databases76–78; 2) AD associated genes in Disgenet, KEGG Disease, and MalaCards databases79; 3) genes identified by the largest GWAS meta-analysis of problematic alcohol use2,80; 4) AUD genes previously identified in various analyses of the AI cohort57,67,81; and 5) molecular targets of ethanol56,82. The core gene set is described in further detail within the Supplementary Method’s section S4.

Core gene set expansion

STRING (StringDB) protein-protein interaction database and GeneHancer regulatory interaction database (the GRCh37/hg19 version) were used to expand the core gene set63,83. We refer to the resulting gene set as the expanded core set. StringDB was used to expand the core gene set in the following manner: for every gene in the core gene set, all of its neighbors that possessed overall score above 900, database score above 0.01, and experimental score above 0.01 were added to the expanded core set. These scores indicate high level of confidence that interaction exists and is supported by experimental and curated database evidence. For GeneHancer, any regulatory element that regulates a core gene, with a confidence score of more than 25, was added to the expanded core set. The final expanded core set contained 2285 genes, and 1451 regulatory elements. For the regulatory elements, the chromosomal loci were taken directly from GeneHancer. For genes, UCSC-hg19 genome assembly chromosomal loci were used. Two kilo byte (kb) windows were added around each gene, except for the major histocompatibility complex genes that have multiple alternative loci. The UCSC-hg19 genome assembly was obtained from UCSC genome browser table browser (Supplementary Data 1 and 2)84. We then selected all the SNPs within the AI dataset that belonged to genes and/or regulatory elements within the expanded core set and had minor allele frequency higher than 0.01, resulting in 476,850 SNPs.

Epistasis analysis

The SNP × SNP pairwise interaction analysis was performed using REMMA. REMMA is a linear mixed model tool to exhaustively search for pairwise interactions while accounting for both population stratification and individual relatedness by incorporating a genetic relationship matrix as the covariance matrix38,85. An exhaustive algorithm, albeit more computationally intensive, was chosen due to concerns about the performance of non-exhaustive algorithms86. We performed the epistasis analysis using five different REMMA configurations and then merged the results. Sex and age were included as covariates. We examined additional social economic variables as potential covariates. Educational attainment was the only SES factor significantly associated with AUD in this AI population (Table S4). Because educational attainment is a heritable trait with genetic overlap with AUD and may lie on shared or downstream causal pathways, it was not included as a covariate to avoid removing meaningful genetic signal and introducing bias. Further details are given in the Supplementary Method’s section S5.

Bi-clustering of SNP x SNP epistasis analysis results

Due to linkage disequilibrium (LD), the effects of SNPs located near each other are often correlated. Therefore, we employed a bi-clustering algorithm to group the interacting SNP pairs obtained from REMMA87. The algorithm clusters interacting SNP pairs into contiguous interacting SNP intervals that are enriched for interacting SNP pairs. For the bi-clustering algorithm, every SNP pair was considered as either interacting or non-interacting by thresholding on its REMMA p value. P value cutoff of <1e-8 (Wald Chi-Square test) was used to transform the REMMA results into a set of interacting SNP pairs. Note that the selection of the p value threshold impacts the bi-clustering results and their interpretations. To select SNP pairs for input into the bi-clustering step, we defined a p value threshold based on upper and lower bounds of the effective number of tests. The Bonferroni correction based on the number of independent SNPs provides an upper bound albeit overly conservative, as SNP × SNP tests are not independent, even after accounting for LD; this yielded the threshold of 4.4e-12 (0.05/(N2/2)) (Wald Chi-Square test). As a lower bound, assuming that all interacting SNP pairs sharing one SNP are fully dependent results in a significance threshold of 3.32e-7 (0.05/N) (Wald Chi-Square test). We therefore adopted an intermediate cutoff of p value < 1e-8 (Wald Chi-Square test) to balance stringency and sensitivity.

We performed bi-clustering algorithm on each chromosome pair individually to distribute the analysis. To illustrate the process, interactions can be represented as a matrix. SNPs from one chromosome are rows, while SNPs from the other chromosome are columns of an interaction matrix (Fig. 3). The values in the matrix are either ones (interacting SNP pairs) or zeros (no interaction). A sampling matrix of varying size, from 1 × 1 to the maximum of 60 × 60 is then traversed across the entirety of the interaction matrix. The maximum sampling matrix size contributes greatly toward algorithm runtime. Each instance of the sampling matrix is evaluated for interaction enrichment using hypergeometric distribution. P values from the hypergeometric one-tailed tests are reported. The maximum number of the hypergeometric tests performed for the entire analysis is bounded by the number of rows (A), in the interaction matrix, times the number of columns (B) times the maximum size of the sampling matrix. To compute the bi-clustering p value threshold using Bonferroni correction for the interacting intervals output by the algorithm, assume that A and B both equal the total number of SNPs. The significance threshold is then set to 1.22e-16 = 0.05/((A*B*602)/2), wherein A = B = 476,850. We also designated a suggestive significance threshold of 1.22e-15. A far less conservative threshold can be obtained by tracking the number of hypergeometric tests performed within the bi-clustering procedure, which has been optimized to minimize the number of unnecessary tests performed. The reason the number of actual tests is typically much smaller than the maximum number of tests is that the interaction matrix is extremely sparce. Note that while the resulting intervals are enriched for interacting SNPs, they also contain non-interacting SNPs. The interacting SNPs within each significant interacting interval were then mapped to genes and/or regulatory elements that they were located within. Thus, pairs of interacting intervals were translated into interacting gene and/or regulatory element pairs.

Analysis of interacting genes and regulatory elements

The bi-clustering results contained two types of significantly and suggestively significantly interacting pairs: gene x gene and gene x regulatory element. For each such pair, we looked for previous evidence of possible interaction using: StringDB, GeneHancer, mSigDB curated gene sets, and literature search. Details can be found in Supplementary Method’s sections S6 and S7. We also conducted two pathway and functional enrichment analyses on the significantly and suggestively significantly interacting pairs using FUMA GWAS41. We performed enrichment analyses of all the interacting genes using the expanded core gene set as the background. Additionally, we assembled all the protein coding genes regulated by the interacting regulatory elements with GeneHancer confidence score greater than 25 and then conducted enrichment analysis of these genes using the entire human gene set as the background.

Replication in all of us research database

Out of 8,973 American Indian or Alaska Native individuals in AoU, a total of 862 were diagnosed with AUD in electronic health records. Additionally, all 862 of these individuals had short read whole genome sequencing data. After removing related individuals, we generated a total of 744 cases and 4293 controls. We regressed out age, sex, and top 10 ancestry principal components from the phenotype. To select SNPs involved in the analysis, we took the hg19 interacting intervals identified in the AI cohort, lifted them over into hg38, and then mapped them onto genes and regulatory elements. For each gene, we then selected portions of that gene, surrounding the interacting intervals. Code used for this task can be found in our GitHub repository. For the regulatory elements, we simply selected the entire regulatory element, since they tend to be small in length. We then identified all the SNPs located within these boundaries, in the AoU Plink allele count/allele frequency (ACAF) callset, which resulted in a total of 209,236 SNPs. We conducted the epistasis analysis using Plink 1.9, with the following settings: snps-only, maf 0.01, and geno 0.5. We identified the lower and upper epistasis significance threshold bounds without accounting for LD: 2.39e-7 (t-test) and 2.28e-12 (t-test). We then used intermediate significance thresholds of <1e-8 (t-test) and <1e-9 (t-test) for the bi-clustering input. That is, these p value thresholds were used to signify whether an epistatic interaction reported by Plink was a significant interaction for use in bi-clustering algorithm. We thus performed bi-clustering twice, once with each input p value threshold. The significance threshold for the interacting intervals, output by bi-clustering algorithm, was 6.34e-16 = 0.05/(209,2362 * 602/2) (hypergeometric one-tailed test). The resulting interacting intervals, passing this threshold, were then mapped onto genes.

Statistics and reproducibility

The statistical tests performed are described throughout the text. Briefly, there are four main categories of statistical tests performed within this study: i) epistasis significance p values computed by REMMA and Plink, ii) hypergeometric one-tailed tests used to establish significant interacting intervals during bi-clustering, iii) pathway enrichment and tissue specificity significance analysis performed using FUMA GWAS GENE2FUNC as specified in Supplementary Materials sections S8 and S941, and iv) Spearman and Pearson correlation tests used to examine correlations between AUD severity, AUD diagnosis, and demographic variables of primary cohort. REMMA uses Wald Chi-square test to perform significance testing, with further details described in its publications38,85. Plink presumably uses a t-test for epistasis testing with quantitative trait88. Meanwhile FUMA GWAS GENE2FUNC relies on hypergeometric tests for enrichment analysis41. The hypergeometric one-tailed, Spearman (Rho test), and Pearson (R test) tests were computed using Python SciPy statistics module with default settings89. Bonferroni correction was used to account for multiple testing with REMMA and Plink. Benjamini-Hochberg multiple testing correction was employed with FUMA GWAS GENE2FUNC during enrichment analyses. The details of p value threshold used in bi-clustering are specified in the bi-clustering section of methods. Bonferroni correction was used to account for multiple hypergeometric testing during bi-clustering.

Data availability

The data that support the finding of this study are available by contacting the last author. The American Indian cohort genetic data contains personal data from vulnerable populations. It is covered by a certificate of confidentiality. The tribes have made it clear there is not to be any broad data sharing. The consent form had no provision for broad data sharing. Accordingly, data access will occur through collaborative agreements within the scope of approval by the Indian Health Council IRB. Data from the All of Us American Indian cohort are accessible through the All of Us Researcher Workbench. The cohort can be generated by selecting individuals that have short read whole genome sequencing data, are American Indian, and have alcoholism diagnosis in EHRs. The data underlying Figs. 1 and 2 can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Latest bi-clustering package can be found at [https://github.com/njdjyxz/geno-bicluster]. REMMA (version 2021.6.17) can be downloaded from Python pip package installer.
