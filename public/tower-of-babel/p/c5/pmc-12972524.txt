Heterogeneity in clinical appearance of chronic spontaneous urticaria: A cross-sectional analysis of a retrospective cohort☆
Authors: İlkim Deniz Toprak, Sule Celik Kamaci, Merve Hormet Igde, Pelin Korkmaz, Nevzat Kahveci, Deniz Eyice Karabacak, Osman Ozan Yegit, Derya Unal, Semra Demir, Aslı Gelincik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimThe study aimed to analyse the characteristics of subtypes of chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) and determine the factors that may influence treatment response.MethodsClinical and laboratory characteristics of CSU patients were compared between 3 groups: (1) those with isolated urticaria (CSUwoAE, n = 50, 27.93%), (2) those with both urticaria and angioedema (CSUwAE, n = 86, 48.04%), and (3) those with isolated angioedema, also referred to as chronic histaminergic angioedema (CHA, n = 43, 24.02%). The Visual Analogue Scale for the worst attack (VAS-WA), Visual Analogue Scale for angioedema control (VAS-AEC), angioedema control test (AECT), Urticaria Control Test (UCT) and Angioedema Quality of Life (AEQoL) were the assessment tools.ResultsA weak correlation was observed between disease onset age and BMI (r = 0.295, p < 0.001). The age of onset was higher in CHA than in CSUwAE (p < 0.001). Oropharyngeal angioedema was more frequent in CHA (p = 0.022), whereas eyelid angioedema occurred more often in CSUwAE (p = 0.001). CHA patients had more impaired AEQoL (p = 0.043) and poorer disease control per AECT and VAS-AEC (p < 0.001, p = 0.001, respectively). The rate of high-dose antihistamine response among patients with angioedema was lower in the CHA group (n = 1; 12.5%) compared with the CSUwAE group (n = 10; 66.7%; p = 0.027). Irrespective of CSU subtypes, omalizumab response varied by BMI in patients with angioedema (n = 51; p = 0.002), with the least response in obese patients (p = 0.033).ConclusionOur study observed that obesity appeared to be associated with a later age of CSU onset and with a lower likelihood of omalizumab responsiveness in patients with angioedema; however, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than suggesting any causal relationship. Additionally, CHA patients seem to have more severely impaired quality of life and a less controlled disease compared to CSUwAE.

Introduction

Urticaria is defined as a condition characterised by the development of pruritic wheals; angioedema (AE) may also accompany the disease in some cases.1 When it persists for more than 6 weeks without an identifiable trigger, it is classified as chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU).1 AE occurs in more than 40% of CSU cases,2,3 and approximately 10% of patients present with AE alone, without wheals.4,5

Patients with CSU often face significant challenges due to impaired quality of life (QoL), especially those with more severe disease activity and AE.2,6 Currently, urticaria control test (UCT) and AE control test (AECT), are recommended in clinical guidelines as tools to guide treatment decisions.1 The use of omalizumab and cyclosporine is indicated in cases where antihistamines are inadequate.1 The development of disease-modifying treatments offers a promising research avenue, extending beyond standard therapies.7 Identifying potential biomarkers that can predict disease progression at the onset is crucial, as this would allow for early determination of which patients could benefit from more advanced treatments. Therefore, efforts have been made to evaluate types of CSU.

The aetiology of CSU is either autoimmune, auto allergic or unknown and accordingly classified as type I – auto allergic or type IIb – autoimmune.1 Several studies have shown differences in laboratory values between the autoimmune and auto allergic CSU groups.1 Some research suggests that elevated anti-thyroid peroxidase (TPO) levels and low total Immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels may serve as useful diagnostic markers for autoimmune CSU8 and a higher anti-TPO/total IgE ratio may predict a poor response to omalizumab.9 Additionally, CSU patients can present either with urticaria, AE or both and all of which are considered within CSU according to current guidelines.1

On the other hand, our understanding of isolated recurrent AE, which is believed to be a component of CSU when it is responsive to antihistamines or omalizumab and also is referred to as chronic histaminergic AE (CHA), remains limited. In this context, some studies have indicated discrepancies between CSU subtypes. This has led to controversy regarding the classification of CHA as a separate medical condition.10 Further research is needed to better understand CHA and address these ongoing debates. Therefore, we aimed to analyse the characteristics of different CSU subtypes, identify differences in clinical and laboratory features, and determine the factors that may influence treatment efficacy.

Methods

Study design and data collection

This was a single-centre, record-based retrospective study, and all analyses were conducted using data obtained at the index visit. The study included 179 patients diagnosed with CSU according to the EAACI/GA2LEN/EuroGuiDerm/APAAACI Guideline, who were followed up at our outpatient clinic.1 Patients were excluded if they had undetermined rashes; AE attacks solely triggered by drugs, food, or venom; acute urticaria or a single AE attack; urticarial vasculitis; were younger than 18 years of age; or did not provide informed consent. In addition, patients with bradykinin-mediated AE were excluded. As suggested to ensure the accurate identification of CHA, the following steps were undertaken:•Medication review: Patients with a history of drug use associated with bradykinin-mediated AE (including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), neprilysin inhibitors, recombinant tissue plasminogen activators, or dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors) in whom attacks resolved within 6 months after withdrawal of the suspected offending agent were classified as drug-induced and excluded.11,12•Laboratory testing review: patients demonstrating reduced C4 and low levels or functional C1 inhibitor (INH) were excluded.13•Treatment response: patients whose AE attacks were unresponsive to standard therapies (antihistamines, corticosteroids, adrenaline, or omalizumab) and who required treatment with C1–INH concentrate, fresh frozen plasma, or icatibant were deemed ineligible.1•Genetic testing: carriers of pathogenic variants associated with hereditary AE with normal C1–INH were also excluded.13

The patients were divided into 3 groups based on the characteristics of their symptoms. Group 1 consisted of individuals presenting with isolated urticaria (CSUwoAE), group 2 included those with urticaria and AE (CSUwAE), and group 3 comprised patients with only CHA. CHA is defined as recurrent, localized swelling without wheals, occurring in the absence of abnormalities in C4, C1–INH level, or function, and showing responsiveness to antihistamines, corticosteroids, adrenaline and/or omalizumab.1

The evaluated clinical features included age at disease onset, aggravating factors and sites of involvement for AE, time for AE to reach its maximum severity and resolve, treatment patterns, comorbidities, smoking and alcohol habits, and family history. These variables were retrospectively extracted from patient records at the index visit. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated and classified into the following categories: underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), pre-obese (25–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥30 kg/m2).14

The laboratory tests included eosinophil, basophil, neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte and platelet counts in peripheral blood; anti-TPO, serum total IgE, CRP levels; and antinuclear antibody (ANA). Autologous serum skin testing was also recorded. Additionally, the neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), systemic immune inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), and anti-TPO/total IgE ratio were calculated. SII values were derived using the formula "platelet × NLR", and SIRI values were obtained using the formula "monocyte × NLR". 15, 16, 17

The number of AE attacks each patient experienced over the past 4 weeks was documented using attack diaries. The severity of the most intense attack in the previous month was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). The highest VAS for Worst Attack (VAS-WA) score recorded during that attack, ranging from 0 to 10, was used to quantify its severity. An increase in the VAS-WA score indicates greater attack severity.18, 19, 20

AE control was evaluated using the AECT over a four-week period and the VAS for AE Control (VAS-AEC). Urticaria control was assessed using the UCT. In the VAS-AEC, patients were instructed to select a point on an ordinal scale ranging from 0 to 10 for the past 4 weeks, where 0 indicated complete disease control and 10 indicated poor disease control.21 For the AECT, a cut-off score of <10 points was used to differentiate between insufficient disease control (minimum score: 0) and well-controlled disease (maximum score: 16). 22, 23, 24 An UCT score of ≥12 out of 16 was considered indicative of optimal disease control.21,25

The burden of AE on patients was assessed by AE Quality of Life (QoL) scale. The AE QoL instrument consists of 4 domains and 17 items, providing a total score ranging from 0 to 100, where a lower score reflects a better QoL. The total score was used to categorize the extent of QoL impairment into the following categories: no impairment (≤30.5), mild impairment (30.6–37.9), moderate impairment (38–50.5) and severe impairment (≥50.6).26

To minimize potential bias related to the timing of assessments and to ensure standardization across patients, a uniform evaluation schedule was applied. The omalizumab doses used by the patients included in the study were 150 mg, 300 mg, 450 mg, or 600 mg. The dosing intervals for patients receiving 150 mg, 300 mg, or 450 mg regimens were at least 4 weeks. In accordance with our clinical practice, patients on the 600 mg regimen received a total dose of 600 mg over 4 weeks, administered as 2 300 mg doses given at two-week intervals. Patient assessments were performed 2 weeks after the 300-mg dose administration. For all other patients, evaluations were performed 4 weeks after the last omalizumab dose. For patients not receiving omalizumab, assessments were administered during a 4-week period in which no changes in treatment dose or regimen had occurred. As treatment duration, dosing intervals, and dose adjustments could vary over time depending on individual clinical status, comparisons were made across treatment regimens based on administered dose, interval, and treatment duration. The omalizumab dose adjusted for body weight was calculated by dividing the administered during the last 4 weeks before the assessment by body weight (mg/kg). The dose per dosing interval (mg/day) was obtained by dividing the total dose by the dosing interval in days.

Comparative analyses were conducted cross-sectionally to examine the clinical and laboratory characteristics of patients across the CSUwoAE, CSUwAE, and CHA groups. Subsequently, the patients’ treatment patterns and responses to treatment were evaluated and compared between the groups. AECT and UCT indicating the disease control level were used to assess the treatment response.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated that the data followed an abnormal distribution. Non-parametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests) were used to compare continuous variables. The median and interquartile range (IQR: 25–75) were reported for continuous variables. The Pearson chi-square test was employed to compare categorical variables. Correlation analysis was performed using the Spearman test. Logistic regression analysis was applied to determine factors associated with omalizumab response. Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis. Missingness was observed only in laboratory variables, ranging from 11 to 23 patients out of 179 (6.1%–12.8%, depending on the specific variable). These patients were excluded listwise from the corresponding analyses.

We defined statistically significant differences as p values less than 0.05. GraphPad version 8.4.3 and SPSS version 23 was used for the figures.

Comparison of clinical characteristics among patients with AE

No significant difference was found in prodromal symptoms of AE between CHA and CSUwAE groups (p = 0.199). Stress was a more common trigger in CSUwAE than CHA (p = 0.009). The time to reach maximum AE severity was significantly longer in the CHA group [2 (0.5–4) vs 1 (0.3–2) hours, p = 0.036], while resolution time was similar (p = 0.35).

Assessment of disease control, and impact on QoL among patients with AE

Over the course of the preceding month, 44 patients in the CSUwAE group (51.16%) and 19 patients in the CHA group (44.18%) experienced at least 1 AE attack. Median monthly attack numbers were similar [2 (1–3) vs. 3 (1–6), p = 0.203].

The median (IQR) AECT score was significantly lower in the CHA group compared to the CSUwAE group [6 (4-8) vs 10 (7.25-11) with p = 0.001)]. The disease was uncontrolled in 47.7% of patients with CSUwAE and 84.2% of patients with CHA, a significant difference (p = 0.007). Furthermore, the VAS-AEC demonstrated more elevated scores in CHA than in CSUwAE (p = 0.001). The treatment characteristics of patients with AE receiving omalizumab were as follows: the median treatment duration was 39 months (22.75–76.25), the median dosing interval was 35 days (30–41.25), and the median omalizumab dose was 300 mg (300–300). The median omalizumab dose per kilogram of body weight in the last 4 weeks was 4.08 mg/kg (3.10–4.75). When analyzed across different disease phenotypes, there were no statistically significant differences in median omalizumab dose (p = 0.824), dosing interval (p = 0.126), treatment duration (p = 0.053), or dose per kilogram (p = 0.882). However, irrespective of CSU subtypes, among AE patients, omalizumab response differed significantly across BMI categories (p = 0.002), with the main difference between normal weight and obese patients (p = 0.033). Based on AECT scores, 51 patients were evaluated: response rates were 100% in normal weight (n = 16/16) and underweight (n = 1/1) patients, 88.2% in pre-obese (n = 15/17), and 52.9% in obese patients (n = 9/17) (Fig. 3). Impact of BMI on CSU

Age at disease onset, blood CRP and eosinophil levels differed significantly between BMI-based groups (p = 0.001, p < 0.001, and p = 0.022, respectively).

When pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction, only the difference between normal-weight and obese patients remained statistically significant (p < 0.001), with obese patients exhibiting an older disease onset compared with normal-weight individuals. The comparisons between underweight and pre-obese (p = 0.045) and underweight and obese patients (p = 0.009) did not meet the corrected threshold for significance. For CRP levels, significant differences were found between normal-weight and obese patients (p < 0.001) and between normal-weight and pre-obese patients (p = 0.007), both of which remained significant after adjustment. For eosinophil counts, only the normal-weight versus pre-obese comparison met the corrected threshold (p = 0.005), whereas the normal-weight versus obese comparison (p = 0.021) did not.

In the regression analysis comparing normal-weight and obese patients, CRP (p = 0.417) and eosinophil counts (p = 0.687) were not independently associated with BMI category, whereas age at disease onset remained significant (p = 0.002). No significant differences were observed between the BMI-based groups for other markers, including NLR, SII, SIRI, basophil count, anti-TPO, and the anti-TPO/total IgE ratio (p > 0.05 for all).

Discussion

Our retrospective cohort with cross-sectional analyses highlights important differences among CSU subtypes, especially in patients with isolated mast cell–mediated AE referred to as CHA. Our findings indicate that CHA patients tend to be older, have more severe attacks, experience greater impairment in QoL and show a poorer response to high dose antihistamines. Furthermore, obesity appeared to be associated with a lower likelihood of omalizumab response.

Our findings regarding later disease onset in CHA are consistent with those of Sabaté et al and Buttgereit et al,10,27 but differ from Láinez-Nuez et al, who reported no significant difference.28 Sabaté et al propose that CHA and CSU may represent distinct clinical entities with different age profiles.10 Alternatively, the absence of urticaria could be linked to age-related physiological changes, such as altered cutaneous blood flow. While urticaria affects the upper skin layers, AE involves deeper subcutaneous tissue, where blood flow is more pronounced. Increased capillary permeability and vasodilation play key roles in both conditions, and aging significantly impacts cutaneous circulation.29 Altered blood flow with age may lead to a weaker cytokine response and a diminished urticarial reaction. The advanced age of CHA patients may possibly reflect a physiological vascular phenomenon. The present study supports this hypothesis, as the CSUwAE group had the lowest onset age, suggesting stronger vascular reactivity in younger individuals. Indeed, studies with the potential to provide insight into the vascular hypothesis had already been conducted. In their comprehensive comparative study, Buttgereit et al. considered the possibility that vascular changes may influence the presentation of symptoms and signs associated with CSU.27 This was based on observations in CHA of a higher prevalence of tongue swelling, a tendency towards hypertension and obesity, and a tendency for AE to persist for longer periods. There is also an evidence that vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) levels are elevated in patients with CSU.30 Although the findings of our study did not show statistically significant difference between the groups with regard to tongue swelling, hypertension, obesity, and duration of AE, the older age at disease onset in CHA is in line with the hypothesis.

Differences in family history, attack sites, triggers and QoL scores suggest that CHA may represent a distinct clinical entity. In line with previous studies, our findings show a lower frequency of urticaria in family history among CHA patients, with no difference in AE history.10 Moreover, oropharyngeal attacks were more common in the CHA group, whereas eyelid involvement and stress as a trigger were more frequently observed in the CSUwAE group, consistent with earlier reports.10,27,28 Despite similar attack frequency and VAS-WA scores, CHA patients had poorer disease control and QoL, as shown by AECT, VAS-AEC, and AEQoL scores. Notably, this finding contrasts with the results of Rodríguez-Garijo et al, who reported significantly higher AEQoL scores in CSUwAE patients.31

Buttgereit et al reported a higher complete response rate in their CHA group,27 evaluating both up-dosed second-generation H1-antihistamines and omalizumab. In contrast, our study found no significant difference in the response to omalizumab for AE between the CHA and CSUwAE groups. The CHA group also showed lower response to high-dose antihistamines than CSUwAE. This indicates that in CHA, high-dose antihistamines might not offer additional benefit, suggesting distinct therapeutic needs for the 2 groups. Based on these results, we suggest that it may be appropriate in daily practice to initiate omalizumab therapy without delay if standard-dose antihistamines fail in CHA patients. The discrepancy between the 2 studies may be due to methodological differences. In our study, we assessed urticaria and AE symptoms separately using the UCT and AECT, respectively, and identified differences in AECT scores between the groups. In contrast, Buttgereit et al. based treatment response comparisons on physician global assessments. As noted in their study, the absence of AE-specific patient-reported outcome measures may have contributed to the differences observed.27 Nevertheless, both studies reported comparable responses to standard-dose antihistamines across the groups.

In recent years, several studies have explored the impact of BMI on CSU, yielding conflicting findings. Zbiciak-Nylec et al identified a significant correlation between higher BMI and older age at disease onset.32 Similarly, in the present study, a correlation between obesity and a later onset of CSU was identified. To further investigate this relationship, we analyzed intergroup differences in inflammatory biomarkers to assess the potential link between obesity and inflammation. Despite significant differences in age at disease onset, no variation was observed in NLR, SII, or SIRI values. This suggests that mechanisms beyond inflammation contribute to the obesity-CSU association. Liu et al demonstrated that white adipose tissue from obese individuals contained a higher prevalence of mast cells than that from lean donors.33 Additionally, a 2014 study reported a significant positive correlation between circulating tryptase levels and BMI,34 supporting the hypothesis that mast cells may mediate the link between obesity and CSU. The observed association between obesity and later-onset CSU suggests that obesity in older age may serve as a contributing factor in disease development.

On the other hand, Soria et al. suggested that obesity is not linked to severe CSU,35 while Savic et al observed a significant negative correlation between disease severity and obesity.36 Magen et al reported that omalizumab-resistant patients were more likely to be obese.37 In our study, a higher prevalence of obesity was observed among patients with lower AECT scores, suggesting a possible association between increased BMI and reduced treatment response. Although the omalizumab dose was not lower, dosing intervals were not longer, and treatment duration did not significantly differ in the obese group, patients in this category showed a tendency toward lower AECT scores, with approximately 94% lower odds of achieving response compared with the reference group. This odds ratio decreased and lost statistical significance when omalizumab dosing intervals were included in the analysis. However, in our study, in order to minimize bias related to omalizumab dosing intervals, the evaluation was performed 2–4 weeks after the omalizumab administration. Therefore, any apparent statistical differences arising from dosing intervals may not reflect true clinical variation. Thus, the association between obesity and reduced treatment response remains an important finding, indicating the need for multicenter studies including larger patient populations with comparable dosing intervals, doses, and treatment durations to further clarify this relationship.

On the other hand, the omalizumab dose per kilogram of body weight was lowest in the obese group. This finding suggests that the possible association between obesity and reduced treatment response may, at least in part, be influenced by pharmacokinetic factors. Given that omalizumab is administered as a fixed dose regardless of body weight in CSU, patients with higher BMI may achieve lower serum drug concentrations, resulting in reduced pharmacodynamic effects.

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study, several limitations must be considered. First, due to the retrospective design with cross-sectional analyses, the scales employed were not longitudinally repeated at specific intervals. Second, in order to assess the BMI of the patients, their weight and height were recorded on the day of study inclusion, with the assumption that there had been no change in their BMI since that time. However, patients were asked about any weight changes during the visits, and none of them reported any significant change. Third, baseline AECT scores were not available for the included patients; therefore, a comparison with baseline disease control could not be performed. Fourth, the multivariable analyses should be considered exploratory, as the relatively small number of events limits statistical power and may reduce the stability of the estimated associations. As a result, the observed relationship between BMI and response association should be interpreted with caution. Larger, adequately powered studies are needed to confirm these findings and to clarify the potential influence of BMI on disease characteristics. Lastly, another potential limitation is selection bias. As our institution is a tertiary referral center for CSU, patients with more severe or persistent disease may have been more likely to be referred and consequently included in our cohort. Therefore, the findings may not be fully generalizable to patients with milder forms of the disease.

In conclusion, our study highlights clinical differences between CHA and CSUwAE. CHA is characterized by a later age of disease onset, more frequent oropharyngeal attacks, a greater impact on QoL and a potentially a poor response to high dose antihistamines. In contrast, CSUwAE is marked by more frequent eyelid AE attacks and a higher incidence of stress-induced episodes. In CHA, initiating to omalizumab therapy without delay may be more appropriate when standard-dose antihistamine response is inadequate. Based on current evidence, the authors do not believe that CHA can yet be considered a distinct clinical entity separate from the CSU spectrum. However, we consider CHA to represent a clinically relevant phenotype within CSU that deserves further investigation due to its distinct clinical characteristics and impact on disease control.

Furthermore, in cases of AE, regardless of whether it is CHA or CSUwAE, BMI may be associated with variations in omalizumab treatment response and therefore should be taken into account in clinical evaluation. Lastly, the observed correlation between obesity and the onset of CSU in older age groups suggests that BMI may be a relevant clinical characteristic when evaluating patients with CSU, particularly older individuals, but its role should be interpreted as associative rather than contributory. Considering BMI in this context may help refine clinical assessment, although further studies are required to clarify the nature and direction of this relationship.
