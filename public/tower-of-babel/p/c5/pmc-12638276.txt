Interactions between the bone morphogenetic protein and the planar cell polarity pathways lead to distinctive ethanol‐induced facial defects
Authors: Raèden Gray, Anna Lloyd, C Ben Lovely
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD) describe a spectrum of ethanol‐induced developmental defects. Ethanol susceptibility is modulated by genetics, but the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. In all vertebrates, complex cellular events give rise to the body plan, including gastrulation and morphogenesis of the endoderm and cranial neural crest (CNC—gives rise to the facial skeleton). These events are crucial for establishing complex signaling interactions that drive embryo development, including the formation of the facial skeleton. In zebrafish, gastrulation occurs between 6 and 10 h postfertilization (hpf), while endoderm/CNC morphogenesis occurs between 10 and 24 hpf. In previous work, planar cell polarity (PCP) mutants are ethanol‐sensitive from 6 to 24 hpf (covering both gastrulation and endoderm/CNC morphogenesis), exhibiting multiple defects in the forming head. This raises the question of whether ethanol during both these time windows drives PCP‐ethanol defects. We hypothesize that PCP mutants are ethanol‐sensitive from 10 to 24 hpf, after gastrulation. We also hypothesize that bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling (ethanol‐sensitive 10–18 hpf) interacts with and sensitizes the PCP pathway to ethanol.MethodsWe treated PCP/BMP mutants with ethanol over various time windows between 6 and 30 hpf and combined morphometric and linear measurements to examine facial development.ResultsWe show that PCP mutants are largely ethanol‐sensitive from 10 to 24 hpf. We also show that BMP mutants sensitize PCP mutants to ethanol and lead to novel ethanol‐independent midline craniofacial defects. Our results suggest that the ethanol‐sensitive role of the PCP pathway occurs after gastrulation, during endoderm/CNC morphogenesis, and that the PCP and BMP pathways genetically interact during the morphogenetic events.ConclusionsUltimately, our work builds on a mechanistic paradigm of ethanol‐induced birth defects we have been developing, connecting the conceptual framework with concrete cellular events that could be ethanol‐sensitive beyond facial development.Keywords: craniofacial development, FASD, genetics, zebrafish

INTRODUCTION

Fetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD) describe a wide range of birth defects due to prenatal alcohol exposure (PAE) (Hoyme et al., 2016; Sokol et al., 2003). Although PAE is the most preventable cause of birth defects, FASD still impacts up to 5% of children in the United States, with estimates even higher in other regions across the globe (May et al., 2018; Popova et al., 2020). However, these are likely underestimates as up to 10% of women worldwide consume alcohol during pregnancy, though some estimates are higher, such as 23.3% of women in the Caribbean, for example (Landgraf et al., 2013; Lange et al., 2017; Popova et al., 2020). In addition, up to 50% of pregnancies in the United States are unplanned, and many pediatricians fail to recognize and diagnose FASD (Finer and Zolna, 2016; Rojmahamongkol et al., 2015). Overall, PAE is one, if not the leading, cause of birth defects.

A hallmark of FASD is craniofacial defects (Landgraf et al., 2013; May et al., 2009). Not only can craniofacial defects negatively impact the ability of children to eat and drink (Perkins et al., 1997), but children who have craniofacial defects, such as cleft lip/palate, face a significant amount of stigma and social rejection (Chung et al., 2019), increasing the disadvantages that the child will face. These factors combined can cost as much as a billion dollars annually (Swanson, 2023). While the developmental window of exposure and dosage of ethanol contribute to the etiology of FASD, human twin studies demonstrate that genetics also modulates the effects of prenatal ethanol exposure, where there is 100% concordance in FASD phenotypic outcomes in monozygotic twins over dizygotic twins (66%), full siblings (41%), and half siblings (22%) (Hemingway et al., 2019). However, the ethanol‐sensitive genetic loci that contribute to FASD susceptibility are still not well‐understood, highlighting the imperative of understanding the intersection between genetic and environmental factors driving FASD.

A key phenotype of FASD is jaw hypoplasia or reduction in jaw size. The jaw forms from the cranial neural crest (CNC) cells in the mandibular domain of the first pharyngeal arch (Chai et al., 2000), though this is highly dependent on the endoderm. Previous studies show that the endoderm acts as a signaling center through tissue–tissue interactions that provide extracellular cues to the CNC (Chai & Maxson Jr., 2006). In embryos that lack endoderm, the CNC fails to form the facial skeleton, with many genes involved in jaw development not active until after interaction with the endoderm (David et al., 2002; Trumpp et al., 1999). Multiple signaling pathways are critical for endoderm morphogenesis and jaw development, including fibroblast growth factor (Fgf) and bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) Signaling (Lovely et al., 2016). The BMP pathway is critical for endoderm morphogenesis, with loss of BMP signaling disrupting endoderm morphogenesis, leading to craniofacial malformations (Li et al., 2019; Lovely et al., 2016). We have recently shown that mutations in multiple genes of the BMP pathway, such as bmp4, sensitize embryos to ethanol‐induced defects in the anterior endoderm, leading to defects in both the jaw and palate (Lovely, C.B., 2025; Klem et al., 2025). However, ethanol does not disrupt BMP signaling directly, nor does it reduce expression of the downstream BMP target, nxk2.3 (Klem et al., 2025; Vo & Lovely, 2025). In addition, knockdown of nxk2.3 does not sensitize larvae to ethanol‐induced facial defects (Vo & Lovely, 2025). This suggests that ethanol is acting on additional loci, contributing to the facial defects in ethanol‐treated BMP mutants.

Previous work has shown that two components of the Planar Cell Polarity (PCP) pathway, vangl2 and gpc4, are ethanol‐sensitive, leading to disrupted axonal projections, synophthalmia, and facial defects, including those to the jaw. Work from Sidik et al. (2021) showed that vangl2 and gpc4 mutants are sensitive to ethanol from 6 to 24 h postfertilization (hpf), covering both gastrulation and endoderm morphogenesis and CNC migration. In zebrafish, gastrulation occurs from 6 to 10 hpf, while endoderm/CNC morphogenesis begins after 10 hpf, with endoderm/CNC morphogenesis largely complete upon CNC condensation in the arches (~36 hpf), though facial development does continue well past 36 hpf with the postmigratory CNC giving rise to the facial prominences and subsequent skeletal elements (Francis‐West et al., 2003). Vangl2 is a transmembrane protein whose phosphorylation is induced by Wnt ligands to promote PCP signaling and, in part, regulate E‐cadherin levels and distribution (Dush & Nascone‐Yoder, 2019; Yang et al., 2017). Gpc4 is a cell surface proteoglycan required for the transport of Wnt ligand, which is important in Wnt ligand transport from endoderm to other germ layers (Hu et al., 2021). Previous work shows that vangl2 and gpc4 genetically interact and play a key role in endoderm formation (Miles et al., 2017). Noticeably, there were ethanol‐induced jaw defects in both vangl2 and gpc4 mutants, suggesting that these ethanol‐sensitive PCP mutants may play a role in craniofacial development (Sidik et al., 2021; Swartz et al., 2014). However, the timing of ethanol sensitivity of these jaw defects was not further examined, raising the question of the timing of the ethanol‐sensitive role vangl2 and gpc4 play in facial development.

Here, we examine the role of vangl2 and gpc4, and their interaction with BMP signaling, postgastrulation, in jaw development, and what ethanol may be doing to disrupt that role. We show that vangl2 and gpc4 are sensitive to ethanol‐induced jaw defects starting at 10 hpf, after gastrulation, with vangl2; gpc4 double mutants being hypersensitized to ethanol. We go on to show that loss of bmp4 further sensitizes vangl2 or gpc4 mutants to ethanol‐induced craniofacial defects, resulting in novel ethanol‐induced midline facial defects not previously observed in PCP or BMP single mutants alone. Collectively, our data link perturbations in PCP and BMP signaling to ethanol susceptibility during jaw development, establishing a genetic pathway in gene–ethanol interactions for future studies in FASD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) care and use

All zebrafish were raised and cared for using established IACUC protocols approved by the University of Louisville (Westerfield, 2007). Adult fish were maintained at 28.5°C with a 14‐/10‐h light/dark cycle. The vangl2 m209 (Jessen et al., 2002), gpc4 fr6 (Topczewski et al., 2001), and bmp4 st72 (Stickney et al., 2007) zebrafish lines used were previously described.

Zebrafish staging and ethanol treatment

Embryos were collected, morphologically staged as previously described (Westerfield, 2007), sorted into sample groups, and reared at 28.5°C in embryo media (EM) to desired developmental time points. At 6, 8, 10, and 24 hpf, EM was changed to either fresh EM or EM containing 1% ethanol (v/v) depending on the time window being examined. At 10, 12, 18, 24, and 30 hpf, EM containing ethanol was washed out with three fresh changes of EM.

Alcian blue cartilage staining

Zebrafish larvae were fixed at 5 days postfertilization (dpf—single mutants) and 4 dpf (double mutants—due to increased die‐off at 5 dpf), and the facial cartilages were stained with alcian blue as previously described (Walker & Kimmel, 2007). Whole mount, ventral view, brightfield images of the viscerocranium were taken on an Olympus BX53 compound microscope.

Genotyping

The tails from 4 or 5 dpf old larvae were removed from our PCP and BMP larvae following Alcian Blue cartilage staining. The heads and tails of the larvae were separated. The heads remained in glycerol for storage for later imaging. Tails were lysed with 5 μL Proteinase K (10 mg/mL—Millipore Sigma) to extract gDNA. PCR was then performed on the gDNA for each sample using primers targeting genes vangl2, gpc4, and bmp4 to amplify regions containing the SNP of each gene. Restriction endonuclease‐based DNA digestion differentiates mutant from wild‐type alleles based on the SNP. The samples were then divided by genotype into pooled groups and were imaged for analysis.

Morphometric analyses

Morphometric analysis of Alcian‐stained larva was performed in TpsDig2 (https://sbmorphomectrics.org) and MorphoJ (Klingenberg, 2011). Landmarks were placed on the following joints: Meckel's cartilage midline joint, the joints between Meckel's and the palatoquadrate, the palatoquadrate and ceratohyal, and at the end of the hyomandibular cartilages. Linear measures were analyzed using TpsDig2. Principal component analysis (PCA), Procrustes ANOVA, and wireframe graphs of facial variation were generated using MorphoJ and are displayed in the figure legends of each PCA plot figure. Canonical variate analysis (CVA) was conducted to identify the difference between viscerocranium shape within treatment groups. Canonical variate (CV) scores were generated from the PCA data in MorphoJ (Thomas et al., 2023). The raw CV scores were then exported into Paleontological Statistics Software Package for Education and Data Analysis (PAST) analysis software (Hammer et al. 2001, version 4.03; [https://past.en.lo4d.com/windows]). Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) with Bonferroni correction was performed using the raw CV scores to identify significant differences in clustering among the treatment groups (Stevens, 2002; Warne, 2014).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Cartilage angles and linear measures of Alcian‐stained viscerocrania and lens‐to‐lens distances were analyzed with a two‐way ANOVA (type III) and Sidak's multiple comparison test in Graphpad Prism 10.4.1 (Graphpad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA).

RESULTS

Ethanol sensitizes vangl2 and gpc4 to ethanol‐induced jaw defects from 10 to 18 hpf

Previous work from Sidik et al. (2021) showed that when exposed to ethanol between 6 and 24 hpf, vangl2 and gpc4 zebrafish mutants exhibited a range of defects, including those to the facial skeleton. This time window encompasses a host of developmental processes, including gastrulation, endoderm morphogenesis, and CNC migration. Because the roles of the endoderm and CNC in jaw development occur after gastrulation, we hypothesize that vangl2 and gpc4 are required for jaw development after 10 hpf. To test this, we exposed our wild‐type and vangl2 or gpc4 mutant embryos to 1% ethanol from 10 to 24 hpf. We selected 1% ethanol (v/v) as it is the highest dose that does not cause craniofacial defects in wild‐type larvae, while higher doses have been shown to impact facial development (Everson et al., 2022; McCarthy et al., 2013; Swartz et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2014). 1% ethanol equilibrates to approximately 30% of the media within 5 min of exposure (Lovely et al., 2014), resulting in an embryonic ethanol concentration of 50 mM, roughly equivalent to a human Blood Alcohol Concentration of 0.23. While this is a binge dose, it is physiologically relevant to FASD, with humans readily surpassing this amount (Canfield et al., 2019; Ethen et al., 2009; Jones, 2008; Maier & West, 2001; Whaley et al., 2019).

We stained the facial cartilage with Alcian blue and used morphometric analysis to observe facial shape changes between our untreated and ethanol‐treated larvae. The benefit of utilizing morphometric analysis is that we can observe changes in facial shape, independent of general growth deficits. We generated PCA plots to visualize the variation between our experimental groups. Our PCA data show that while untreated vangl2 mutants are visually comparable to untreated and ethanol‐treated wild‐type siblings, they do cluster differently along PC1, shifting negatively along PC1 (Figure 1A–C,E). PC1, which accounts for 80% of all variation in the data, denotes a shortening and widening of the viscerocranium and a flattening of the angle of the ceratohyal (Ch) and Meckel's (Mc) cartilages (Figure 1E). When exposed to ethanol from 10 to 24 hpf, vangl2 mutants shift further negative along PC1 and have increased variation in jaw shape relative to untreated mutant and untreated and ethanol‐treated wild‐type siblings (Figure 1D compared to A–C, E). PC2, which represents only 4% of the variation in the data and denotes a subtle shape change in the position and angle of both the Mc and Ch cartilages, showed little spread observed in all groups along (Figure 1E, Procrustes ANOVA, F = 32.63, p < 0.0001). While the Procrustes ANOVA analyses revealed significant viscerocranial shape changes for both vangl2 and gpc4 mutant larvae, limitations in the software prevent pairwise comparisons. To make pairwise comparisons, we converted all PCA data to canonical variates and performed CVA combined with multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA). CVA/MANOVA of untreated and ethanol‐treated wild‐type and vangl2 mutant larvae showed there was a significant difference between untreated and ethanol‐treated mutants from 10 to 24 hpf (Figure 1F; Table S1, Wilks' Lambda = 0.1817, F = 24.18, p = 2.70e‐28). This suggests that while untreated vangl2 mutants have subtle facial shape changes compared to wild‐type siblings, ethanol exposure from 10 to 24 hpf (after gastrulation) leads to a significant change in facial shape.

Previous studies have shown that gpc4 mutants have craniofacial defects, including jaw hypoplasia and absent ethanol exposure (LeClair et al., 2009; Sisson et al., 2015). We observed similar facial defects in our morphometric approach, with untreated gpc4 mutants having shorter and wider viscerocranium and flattened Mc and Ch cartilages compared to wild‐type siblings (Figure 2A–C,E, PC1). Here, PC1 represents 86% of the variation in the data (Figure 2E, Procrustes ANOVA, F = 51.25, p < 0.0001). In contrast, ethanol‐treated gpc4 mutants showed little difference in facial shape compared to their untreated mutant siblings in the PCA (Figure 2D compared to C, E). However, we did observe a significant shift in facial shape between untreated and ethanol‐treated gpc4 mutants (Figure 2E,F, Table S2, Wilks' Lambda = 0.0072, F = 64.17, p = 8.84e‐35). While untreated mutants were relatively consistent along PC1, they had a greater spread over PC2 (representing 7% of the variation), which denotes a flattening of the Ch, leading to a greater distance between the midline joints of the Mc and the Ch (Figure 2E). On the other hand, ethanol‐treated gpc4 mutants showed greater spread along PC1, which includes an inversion of the Ch with the shortening and widening of the viscerocranium (Figure 2E). The penetrance of the Ch inversion was 33% in untreated mutants compared to 63% in ethanol‐treated mutants (Figure S1). This suggests that while much more subtle in their ethanol‐induced facial defects compared to vangl2 mutants, gpc4 mutants still display ethanol‐induced defects in viscerocranial shape when treated from 10 to 24 hpf.

To further confirm that both vangl2 and gpc4 are ethanol‐sensitive after gastrulation, during endoderm/CNC morphogenesis, we compared the previous phenotypic facial outcomes with the following additional ethanol exposure time windows: (1) 6–10 hpf gastrulation; (2) 10–18 hpf, when we have previously shown that BMP signaling is required for endoderm morphogenesis; and (3) 24–30 hpf, during CNC condensation into the pharyngeal arches. When exposed to ethanol from 10 to 18 hpf, vangl2 and gpc4 mutants had remarkably similar variation in viscerocranial shape and size compared to mutant larvae treated from 10 to 24 hpf (Figure 3A [Procrustes ANOVA, F = 16.75, p < 0.0001] and B [Procrustes ANOVA, F = 107.01, p < 0.0001]; Tables S3 [Wilks' Lambda = 0.1488, F = 12.09, p = 2.06e‐36] and 4 [Wilks' Lambda = 0.0213, F = 32.24, p = 1.09e‐83]). Interestingly, we observed subtle differences in viscerocranial morphology between our gpc4 mutant treatment groups. Here, exposure timing drove variation along PC1 (representing 87% of the variation in data; Figure 3B), shifting the mean shape further negative as ethanol duration increased.

To test that ethanol interacts with vangl2 and gpc4 during gastrulation or in the transition between gastrulation and endoderm/CNC morphogenesis, we treated our embryos with ethanol from either 6 to 10 hpf or 8 to 12 hpf and analyzed viscerocranial shape (Figures S2 and S3, respectively). When exposed to ethanol from 6 to 10 hpf, no significant difference was observed between untreated and ethanol‐treated gpc4 mutants (Table S5, Wilks' Lambda = 0.0142, F = 30.68, p = 1.05e‐73). However, untreated and ethanol‐treated vangl2 mutants were significantly different, though not to the extent when treated from 10 to 24 hpf (Table S6, p = 0.043 vs. p = 0.005, respectively, Wilks' Lambda = 0.0849, F = 8.74, p = 7.49e‐22). Neither mutant displayed any significant difference in viscerocranial morphology between untreated and ethanol‐treated mutants when exposed to ethanol from 8 to 12 hpf (Tables S7 [Wilks' Lambda = 0.134, F = 12.84, p = 1.40e‐38] and S8 [Wilks' Lambda = 0.0129, F = 21.29, p = 2.23e‐47]). This suggests that while gpc4 mutants are not ethanol‐sensitive earlier than 10 hpf, vangl2 mutants are.

To examine whether ethanol interacts with vangl2 and gpc4 after 24 hpf, when the CNC are condensing into the pharyngeal arches, we treated mutant larvae with ethanol from 24 to 30 hpf. We again did not observe any significant ethanol‐induced changes in viscerocranial shape in gpc4 mutants (Figure S4A; Table S9, Wilks' Lambda = 0.0549, F = 11.87, p = 5.17e‐30). However, similar to ethanol treatment from 6 to 10 hpf, there was a significant difference in vangl2 mutants when treated from 24 to 30 hpf compared to when treated from 10 to 24 hpf (Figure S4B; Table S10, p = 0.012 vs. p = 6.73e‐6, respectively, Wilks' Lambda = 0.0849, F = 8.74, p = 7.49e‐22). Overall, this suggests that the ethanol‐sensitive time window for gpc4 is 10 to 24 hpf during endoderm/CNC morphogenesis. However, the window of ethanol sensitivity in vangl2 mutants is broader, extending on either side of 10 and 24 hpf.

In addition to facial defects, ethanol‐treated vangl2 mutants also showed eye‐field defects, with the majority of ethanol‐treated vangl2 mutants displaying synophthalmia (eye‐field fusions) with a smaller minority showing full cyclopia (complete lens fusion; Sidik et al., 2021; Swartz et al., 2014). However, these studies exposed vangl2 mutants to ethanol from 6 to 24 hpf and showed that failure of eye‐field separation was driven in part by disrupted early eye‐field patterning before and during gastrulation (Sidik et al., 2021). In our exposure paradigm, we observed synophthalmia in 50% of ethanol‐treated vangl2 mutants but not in our gpc4 mutants (Figures 1D and 2D, Table 1). While phenotypically consistent with previous work (Swartz et al., 2014; Sidik et al., 2021), our exposure paradigm begins at 10 hpf, after gastrulation, suggesting that ethanol disrupts eye‐field separation independent of the early patterning events of eye‐field formation.

PCP mutants genetically interact during facial development

Previous work showed that vangl2 and gpc4 genetically interact during early endoderm development, leading to exacerbated endodermal defects over the single mutants (Miles et al., 2017). With this in mind, we wanted to establish whether vangl2 and gpc4 also genetically interact, further exacerbating ethanol‐induced facial defects. To test this, we generated vangl2 +/− ; gpc4 +/− double heterozygous carriers and crossed them to produce vangl2; gpc4 compound heterozygous (homozygous mutant for the named gene, heterozygous for the second gene) and double homozygous mutant larvae. Again, all larvae were treated with 1% ethanol (v/v) from 10 to 24 hpf as described above. We observed severe facial and eye‐field separation defects in ethanol‐treated compound heterozygous and homozygous double mutant larvae (Figure 4A–M, Table 1). However, due to the severe morphological changes in our mutant larvae, we could not perform our morphometric analyses of shape on compound heterozygotes and homozygous double mutants as we previously performed. To analyze the impact of ethanol on facial development, we quantified the length of the Mc + palatoquadrate (pq) and Ch cartilages, as well as the internal angle of the Ch cartilages (Figure 4N–P,S,T; Table S11). We also measured lens‐to‐lens distance for eye‐field separation (Figure 4M,R; Table S11). To account for variation in size due to ethanol exposure, we used overall head length in our linear measures (Figure 4Q; Figure S5; Table S12) and report our Mc + pq and Ch length measurements as Meckel's‐to‐Head length (Figure 4N; Table S11) and Ceratohyal‐to‐Head length (Figure 4O; Table S11) ratios. Looking at head length measures alone, ethanol did not impact head length in wild‐type and gpc4 mutant larvae (Figure S5; Table S12). While we did observe ethanol‐induced decreases in head length in vangl2 mutants, homozygous double mutant larvae were unaffected (Figure S5; Table S12).

The ethanol‐induced reduction in head length in larvae either heterozygous or homozygous mutant for vangl2 was expected, as the facial skeleton is significantly shorter and wider, resulting in a shorter head length (Figure 1E,F). However, even after accounting for head length, we still observed significant ethanol‐induced reductions in Mc + pq length in vangl2 single mutant larvae (Figure 4N; Table S11). Ethanol did not result in significant reductions in Mc + pq length in gpc4 mutants (Figure 4N; Table S11). In our homozygous double mutants, we did not observe ethanol‐induced significant changes in Mc + pq cartilage lengths. This was not surprising given that untreated double mutants already displayed severe craniofacial defects (Figure 4K). However, while Ch length was unaffected in all groups (Figure 4N,O; Table S12), ethanol did increase the size of the angle of Ch angle in our vangl2 single mutants and homozygous double mutants (Figure 4P; Table S11) compared to their untreated siblings, but not in gpc4 single mutants.

Unlike our vangl2 single mutants and homozygous double mutants, our compound heterozygous mutants mainly resemble their single mutant siblings when untreated (Figure 4G,I). However, when exposed to ethanol, many of our compound heterozygous larvae had exacerbated Mc + pq and Ch cartilage defects and eye‐field separation defects, comparable to the double mutant larvae (Figure 4H,J compared to K,L). We observed that Mc + pq length was significantly shorter in ethanol‐treated vangl2 compound heterozygous larvae (Figure 4N; Table S11). There was also a significant ethanol‐induced increase in Ch angle in vangl2 compound heterozygous larvae (Figure 4P; Table S12). As with gpc4 single mutants, there is no significant difference in Mc + pq length in gpc4 compound heterozygous larvae, although we did see a downward trend in that data (Figure 4N; Table S11). However, we did observe a significant increase in Ch angle in ethanol‐treated gpc4 compound heterozygous larvae (Figure 4P; Table S11), demonstrating that a flattening, or even an inversion, of the Ch occurs due to ethanol exposure. While we are not able to perform morphometric analysis on our compound heterozygous and homozygous double mutants, we did observe ethanol‐induced facial shape changes, reminiscent of our single mutants (Figures 1 and 2), suggesting the interaction between vangl2 and gpc4 is ethanol‐sensitive.

In addition to facial phenotypes, we observed increasing rates of ethanol‐induced synophthalmia (with or without partial lens fusion) and cyclopia (full lens fusion) in our compound heterozygous and homozygous double mutant larvae (Figure 4A–M, Table 1). As described above, we observed synophthalmia (without lens fusion) in ethanol‐treated vangl2 single mutants but not in gpc4 single mutants (Figure 1, Table 1). Synophthalmia was 100% penetrant in untreated homozygous double mutant larvae, 80% without lens fusion, and 20% with full cyclopia (Figure 4K, Table 1). Ethanol treatment increased synophthalmia with partial lens fusions from 0% to 64% (Table 1), though we did observe a decrease in full cyclopia from 20% to 9% (Table 1). This suggests that loss of gpc4 potentiates synophthalmia/lens fusions in vangl2 mutants, independent of ethanol, but ethanol exacerbates these defects. In both untreated gpc4 compound heterozygous larvae and vangl2 compound heterozygous larvae, 88% had complete eye‐field separation, while only 12% had synophthalmia without lens fusions (Table 1). However, ethanol treatment increased synophthalmia without lens fusions in gpc4 compound heterozygous larvae and vangl2 compound heterozygous larvae to 83% and 41%, respectively (Table 1). Strikingly, we observed 4% cyclopia in gpc4 compound heterozygous larvae but 0% in vangl2 compound heterozygous larvae. This suggests that while loss of a single wild‐type allele of vangl2 can sensitize gpc4 mutants to ethanol‐induced cyclopia, loss of a single gpc4 wild‐type allele does not sensitize vangl2 mutants to cyclopia.

To further quantify these eye defects, we measured the lens‐to‐lens distance in untreated and ethanol‐treated single, compound heterozygous, and homozygous double mutant larvae. Homozygous double mutant larvae did not display ethanol‐induced reductions in lens‐to‐lens distance, though we did see a downward trend (Figure 4M; Table S11). This is largely due to the severity of the ethanol‐induced phenotypes observed in untreated double mutants (Table 1). We did observe significant ethanol‐induced reductions in lens‐to‐lens distance in vangl2 single and compound heterozygous mutant larvae (Figure 4M; Table S11). We also observed significant ethanol‐induced reductions in lens‐to‐lens width in gpc4 compound heterozygous mutants but not in gpc4 single mutants. This suggests that loss of one copy of vangl2 sensitizes gpc4 mutants to ethanol‐induced synophthalmia. Taken together, our data show that, like our single mutants, our compound heterozygous and homozygous double mutants are ethanol‐sensitive after gastrulation and interact, increasing the penetrance and expressivity of ethanol‐induced facial defects.

Loss of BMP signaling leads to exacerbated craniofacial phenotypes in PCP mutants

We have previously shown that multiple members of the BMP pathway, including the ligand bmp4, are ethanol‐sensitive from 10 to 18 hpf, disrupting endoderm morphogenesis and resulting in defects to the viscerocranium (Klem et al., 2025). Based on phenotypic and exposure paradigm similarities, we predicted that the BMP and PCP interact and sensitize embryos to ethanol‐induced viscerocranial defects. To test this, we generated compound bmp4; vangl2 and bmp4; gpc4 double heterozygous crosses to analyze this potential interaction. Loss of one or both bmp4 wild‐type alleles further sensitizes vangl2 and gpc4 mutants to ethanol, increasing both the penetrance and expressivity of the previously observed phenotypes: jaw defects, palate defects (rodded ethmoid palate or jagged trabecula), and synophthalmia, which we collectively term “common phenotypes” (Figure 5A–F; Tables 2 and 3). We observed increases in synophthalmia and the severity of jaw hypoplasia in vangl2 mutant; bmp4 heterozygous larvae and homozygous double mutant larvae, but not in bmp4 mutant; vangl2 heterozygous larvae (Table 2; Figure S6). We observed similar increases in jaw hypoplasia and synophthalmia (which have not been observed in gpc4 single mutants) in gpc4 mutant; bmp4 heterozygous larvae, and homozygous double mutant larvae (Table 3; Figure S6). Strikingly, we also observed several new craniofacial defects not originally seen in ethanol‐treated BMP or PCP mutants alone, which we collectively describe as “uncommon phenotypes” (Tables 4 and 5; Figure S7). We observed ectopic cartilages (Figure 5G) and midline defects, which include asymmetry of the midline joint between the Ch and split, crooked, and/or rodded basihyal (Figure 5H–L). In our screen, these novel midline defects only marginally increased in ethanol, suggesting they may be a novel ethanol‐independent set of phenotypes in BMP/PCP interactions (Tables 4 and 5; Figure S7), though future morphometric work will be needed to fully analyze these midline defects. Taken together, our data show that disruptions to BMP signaling sensitize PCP mutants to ethanol, while generating novel midline defects not observed in BMP or PCP mutants. This suggests that BMP/PCP interactions may lie at the heart of ethanol‐sensitive endoderm morphogenesis, leading to facial defects.

DISCUSSION

FASD results in a wide range of structural malformations, including those to the facial skeleton (Hoyme et al., 2016; Sokol et al., 2003). Formation of the facial skeleton is a highly complex process requiring reciprocal signaling events between the endoderm and CNC. These signaling interactions rely greatly on the morphogenesis of each tissue (Lovely et al., 2016; Swartz et al., 2012). Previous work showed that vangl2 and gpc4 mutants are sensitive to ethanol‐induced facial defects and synophthalmia when treated from 6 to 24 hpf (Sidik et al., 2021), though the impact of the PCP–ethanol interactions during gastrulation or endoderm/CNC morphogenesis was not defined. Here, we observed that both vangl2 and gpc4 interact and are ethanol‐sensitive during endoderm/CNC morphogenesis, leading to facial defects and increasing severity in synophthalmia. However, the ethanol sensitivity of vangl2 mutants was more expansive, showing mild effects when exposed during gastrulation and after 24 hpf, while gpc4 mutants were only sensitive from 10 to 24 hpf. This suggests that while gpc4 mutants are ethanol‐sensitive from 10 to 24 hpf, the ethanol sensitivity of vangl2 mutants extends on either side of 10 to 24 hpf. We went on to show that loss of one copy of one gene leads to increased ethanol‐induced synophthalmia and facial defects in the other mutant, strongly supporting PCP–PCP and PCP–ethanol interactions disrupting developmental events occurring during endoderm/CNC morphogenesis. Future work varying exposure paradigms and broader mechanistic analyses at the tissue and cellular levels will be needed to fully address the impact of our PCP–ethanol interactions.

Surprisingly, our CVA/MANOVA analyses identified subtle, yet significant variation in facial shape in untreated vangl2 mutants compared to untreated and ethanol‐treated wild‐type siblings, though the shape change was not as pronounced as in ethanol‐treated vangl2 mutants. This suggests that loss of vangl2 increases facial variation, and ethanol exacerbates these impacts. While we did not observe similar differences between untreated and ethanol‐treated wild‐type siblings in our gpc4 mutant crosses, we did see significant variation in facial shape between untreated and ethanol‐treated gpc4 mutants that was not clear in our PCA analysis. While we do expect some variation between untreated and ethanol‐treated larvae, we were surprised by the differences observed in our morphometric analyses. Visually, we did not observe distinct variation in some of these samples that our analyses identified. For example, while vangl2 mutants were visually comparable to wild‐type siblings (untreated and ethanol‐treated) in facial morphology, both PCA and CVA/MANOVA analyses showed they were significantly different in facial shape. Taken together, our morphometric approaches and pairwise comparisons confirmed the complex relationships between genotype and treatment window.

Given that both vangl2 and gpc4 play roles in both endoderm morphogenesis and CNC development (Ling et al., 2017; Matthews et al., 2008; Miles et al., 2017; Sisson et al., 2015), both vangl2 and gpc4 may be playing roles in two different yet mechanistically linked developmental processes driving facial development. Both have been shown to regulate CNC development, with vangl2 mutants impacting CNC migration. However, this impact is mild and may not fully explain the ethanol‐induced facial defects in vangl2 mutants (Matthews et al., 2008). While previous work has shown that gpc4 is necessary for CNC development in part by regulating CNC polarity (Ling et al., 2017), gpc4 acts cell autonomously in the CNC, and loss of gpc4 does not appear to disrupt pharyngeal arch morphology at 36 hpf, suggesting it plays a role during CNC morphogenesis out of the pharyngeal arches to form the facial skeleton (Sisson et al., 2015). In addition to CNC development, vangl2 and gpc4 also regulate early endoderm development (Miles et al., 2017). Endoderm morphogenesis may be disrupted in vangl2 and gpc4 mutants, leading to defects in CNC development. This would be consistent with our previous results showing that BMP–ethanol interactions disrupt endoderm morphogenesis, leading to CNC defects (Klem et al., 2025; Vo & Lovely, 2025). While we have previously shown that BMP signaling is required in the endoderm for its morphogenesis (Lovely et al., 2016), we will need to test the cell autonomy of vangl2 and gpc4 to determine whether morphogenesis of the endoderm, CNC, or both is impacted in our ethanol‐treated mutants.

While tissue and cellular autonomy will give insight into the developmental impacts of PCP–ethanol interactions, it does not address the mode of action of ethanol in vangl2 or gpc4 mutants. Ethanol may be acting at the level of Vangl2 and/or Gpc4 protein. Expanding on this, Howard et al. (2011) defined the site of ethanol action on multiple proteins, such as Glycine Receptors, through distinct binding pockets (Howard et al., 2011). More recent work shows that the specific amino acid residue, tyrosine, is overrepresented in ethanol binding sites (Khrustalev et al., 2017). Currently, we know of no evidence that ethanol binds directly to Vangl2 or Gpc4 proteins, though additional biochemical techniques such as X‐ray crystallography and structural modeling will be needed to assess whether similar, potentially tyrosine‐rich, binding pockets exist on Vangl2 and Gpc4 and are accessible to ethanol. However, we can't rule out that ethanol may be synergizing with our PCP mutants via secondary targets. Ethanol may act on other components of the PCP pathway or downstream targets of the pathway, either at the gene or protein level. Ethanol could be acting on this yet undefined target of the PCP pathway, where loss of either vangl2 or gpc4 sensitizes embryos to ethanol action on a secondary target. One possibility is cell adhesion through E‐cadherin. Ethanol has been shown to disrupt E‐cadherin localization during epiboly, and PCP regulates E‐cadherin localization (Muñoz‐Soriano et al., 2012; Sarmah et al., 2013). Here, loss of either vangl2 or gpc4 would sensitize embryos to ethanol‐induced disruptions of E‐cadherin localization, disrupting tissue morphogenesis.

BMP–PCP interactions yield novel phenotypes

We have previously shown that multiple BMP mutants are ethanol‐sensitive from 10 to 18 hpf, leading to disrupted endoderm morphology (Klem et al., 2025). However, ethanol does not attenuate BMP signaling, or at least one of its downstream targets, nkx2.3, directly, but does increase CNC apoptosis in BMP mutants through a yet unknown mechanism (Klem et al., 2025; Vo & Lovely, 2025). BMP and PCP mutant larvae share a similar ethanol‐sensitive time window during endoderm/CNC morphogenesis (Klem et al., 2025). Ethanol‐treated PCP mutants phenocopy the ethanol‐induced shorter and wider facial shape observed in BMP mutants (Klem et al., 2025). Both BMP and PCP regulate endoderm development (Lovely et al., 2016; Miles et al., 2017). We observed that the loss of one or both bmp4 alleles further sensitizes PCP mutants to ethanol‐induced facial defects and synophthalmia, phenocopying vangl2; gpc4 double mutants. Surprisingly, we observed additional midline craniofacial defects in untreated larvae not seen in our BMP or PCP single/double mutants. This suggests that the PCP and BMP pathways may interact in two distinct ways: (1) an ethanol‐dependent exacerbation of facial defects and synophthalmia; (2) an ethanol‐independent generation of novel midline defects through yet unidentified mechanisms.

Given the complexity of facial development, several mechanisms may be driving these two distinct interactions. BMP may be upstream, regulating PCP component gene expression in the endoderm. This would lead to defects in endoderm morphogenesis, resulting in facial defects. However, this does not explain how we only see synophthalmia in PCP and BMP‐PCP mutants but not in BMP mutants alone. It also leaves the open question of how defects in endoderm morphogenesis give rise to defects in CNC development. It is possible that BMP drives ethanol‐induced defects in endoderm morphogenesis while PCP drives ethanol‐induced defects in the CNC. If this were true, we would predict that increased CNC apoptosis in ethanol‐treated PCP mutants would result in similar facial malformations as seen in ethanol‐treated BMP mutants (Klem et al., 2025). However, our morphometric and genetic interaction analyses argue against this as the PCP mutants do not fully recapitulate the ethanol‐induced facial phenotypes of BMP mutants. Building on this, the novel midline phenotypes in our PCP/BMP double mutants suggest a more complex relationship between BMP and PCP signaling. We previously showed that BMP signaling regulates the formation of lateral endoderm (Lovely et al., 2016), and PCP plays a key role in endoderm cell polarity (Miles et al., 2017). The presence of midline defects in our PCP/BMP double mutants suggests that medial‐lateral patterning may also be affected, leading to exacerbation of existing phenotypes and generation of the novel midline defects. Beyond the studies of cell autonomy described above, detailed analyses of BMP and PCP signaling dynamics and cell behavior will be needed to dissect the complex ethanol‐sensitive relationship between BMP and PCP signaling.

Combined, our results show that a complex relationship between genetics and ethanol exposure paradigms drives ethanol‐induced developmental defects. Given the genetic and developmental homology between zebrafish and humans, our work provides a powerful model to examine complex genetic signaling pathways and generate a deeper mechanistic understanding of ethanol interactions with these pathways on craniofacial development. Future studies to identify the mechanism on a cellular level will be needed to dissect the role of PCP and BMP during endoderm/CNC morphogenesis and the impact of ethanol on these events. Ultimately, this will continue to build on a conceptual framework and a mechanistic paradigm of ethanol‐induced birth defects we have been developing, connecting ethanol exposure with concrete cellular events that could be sensitive beyond facial development.
