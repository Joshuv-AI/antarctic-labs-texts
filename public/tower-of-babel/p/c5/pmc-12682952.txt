Management of an abnormal vertebral artery in aortic arch surgery: How we did it
Authors: Nicolas Nunez-Ordonez, Tomas Chalela, Carlos Villa-Hincapie, Carlos Obando, Nestor Sandoval, Jaime Camacho-Mackenzie
Published: 2025

Technique for abnormal left vertebral artery reimplantation in aortic arch surgery.

Central Message

An abnormal left vertebral artery arising directly from the aortic arch may be frequently found during aortic arch surgery. Careful preoperative planning including different approaches for reimplantation may be required to preserve adequate central nervous system protection.

The aberrant origin of the vertebral artery arising directly from the aortic arch (AA) is a relatively common variation of the AA branching pattern that has been reported in 2% to 6% of the population.1 This type of arch, which is usually asymptomatic, imposes an additional challenge for surgeons operating on the AA given that, depending on its size, preservation of this branch could be important for guaranteeing adequate central nervous system blood flow.

Here we present a case of a patient with a left vertebral artery (LVA) arising directly from the AA who underwent AA surgery. The technical considerations for protecting spinal flow intraoperatively are discussed.

Case Report

A 77-year-old female patient with history of hypertension, diabetes, and atrial fibrillation was referred to the aorta clinic because of an aneurysm of the AA and descending aorta (Figure 1). Besides the aortic aneurysm, an aberrant LVA arising directly from the arch was found on preoperative workup. Preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction was 55%. A 2-stage repair was planned that included a left subclavian-to-carotid bypass and an AA repair with a frozen elephant trunk (FET), with subsequent thoracic endovascular descending aorta repair (TEVAR).

Technique

With the aim of performing a zone 2 distal arch anastomosis, a left subclavian-to-carotid bypass was created first. The right axillary artery was cannulated via a side graft for anterior cerebral perfusion (ACP), and venous cannulation via right atrial appendage was performed.

The AA and supra-aortic trunks were dissected. A large LVA arising directly from the arch was evident. The temperature was lowered to 26 °C, after which ACP was started and the diseased aorta was resected, with careful preservation of the remaining supra-aortic trunks (innominate, left carotid, and left vertebral). The FET was completed by deploying the endovascular prothesis to the descending aorta, and the distal aortic anastomosis was performed in aortic zone 2, immediately starting lower body perfusion and rewarming through the side branch of the aortic prothesis, with placement of a distal arch clamp. Then the proximal aortic anastomosis was performed, followed by restoration of myocardial perfusion. The right innominate artery and left carotid anastomoses to the corresponding branches of the device were completed. Given the size of the vertebral artery, reimplantation was chosen to protect the patient from central nervous system (CNS) ischemia.

Because of the posterior position and retraction after arch resection, direct reimplantation was not possible, so the decision was made to perform an end-to-side anastomosis to the native ipsilateral internal carotid artery using a saphenous vein graft (Figure 2). The surgery was completed.

The patient's postoperative course was uneventful and the staged TEVAR was performed 10 days after the initial procedure, once adequate postoperative recovery was achieved. Given the extension of the endovascular repair beyond 15 cm, CNS protection was implemented with cerebrospinal fluid drainage and a mean arterial pressure target of 80 to 100 mm Hg. The patient was discharged to home. A follow-up computed tomography scan showed an adequate aortic repair with a patent left vertebral artery anastomosis (Figure 1, B). At the time of this report, she had been followed for 2.5 months, showing adequate recovery and no immediate nor delayed signs of spinal cord ischemia, and continued to be monitored at the aortic clinic.

Discussion

AA surgery remains a challenging intervention that frequently involves handling vital vascular structures, such as the carotid and subclavian arteries. Guaranteeing CNS perfusion during and after the procedure is a priority, with the aim of reducing the risk of neurologic complications that can have a significant impact on postoperative quality of life.

Given the significant morbidity burden that this condition can impose,2 careful preoperative planning is key when intervening patients with AA branching variations. In this case, a CT scan proved useful to guide intraoperative decisions.

The decision to ligate or reanastomose this branch should be individualized, given the lack of clear guidelines.1 Several previous publications have suggested that the importance of restoring flow through the LVA depends on the competence of the circle of Willis and the contralateral vertebral artery in sustaining blood flow.3,4 In the present case, we decided to restore flow based on intraoperative assessment of a 5- to 6-mm vessel. CNS protection is crucial in AA surgery; in this case, it included axillary cannulation for ACP, continuous near-infrared spectroscopy and bispectral index monitoring, and moderate hypothermia. The decision to revascularize the LVA was based on the presumed significance of this vessel in perfusion of the upper spinal cord and posterior fossa structures given its macroscopic size.

As a final remark, the orientation of the anastomosis is crucial for guaranteeing adequate blood flow to the distal artery specially by preventing flow-limiting rotations of the grafted vessel. In this case, the LVA was found in the posterior aspect of the AA, which made the reimplantation more technically challenging, and the operation was further complicated by a severe retraction of the stump after resection of the arch. The saphenous vein graft proved to be useful to extend the posteriorly lying LVA and allowed for easier anastomosis in an adequate position to the left carotid artery with the use of a side clamp with adequate mid-term patency.
