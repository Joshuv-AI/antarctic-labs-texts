Pre-treatment Tumor Volume Growth Rate and Baseline Volume Measured by an Artificial Intelligence Program Predict Overall Survival in Patients With Pleural Mesothelioma Undergoing Deferred Systemic Therapy
Authors: Illaa Smesseim, Valerio Pugliese, Jacobus Burgers, Kevin Groot Lipman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe optimal timing of systemic therapy in pleural mesothelioma remains debated, particularly for asymptomatic patients with a limited disease burden. Prior studies suggest that deferred treatment may not compromise overall survival (OS) in selected patients. Reliable prognostic markers to guide treatment timing are lacking. We investigated whether baseline tumor volume and pretreatment tumor growth rate, quantified using an artificial intelligence (AI)–based model, predict OS in patients deferring systemic therapy.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective study including patients with pleural mesothelioma diagnosed between 2010 and 2025 who deferred systemic therapy for more than or equal to 3 months and had at least two pretreatment computed tomography scans. Total tumor volume and tumor growth rate (mL/mo) were automatically measured using the AI-based ARTIMES model. Optimal cutoffs were determined using receiver operating characteristic analysis. OS was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier estimates and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsA total of 71 patients were included (median age 66.8 y; 80.3% male; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group 0–1). The median deferral time was 8.0 months. Optimal cutoffs were 56.5 mL for baseline tumor volume and 67.8 mL/mo for tumor growth rate. Patients with a low baseline tumor volume had a significantly longer median OS compared with those with a high volume (30.95 versus 16.89 mo; p = 0.0066). Similarly, a low tumor growth rate was associated with improved OS (29.40 versus 14.59 mo; p = 0.00057). In multivariable analysis, sarcomatoid histology remained independently associated with worse OS (hazard ratio = 3.28, 95% confidence interval: 1.38–7.75, p = 0.007).ConclusionsAI-derived baseline tumor volume and tumor growth rate are strong prognostic factors for OS in patients with pleural mesothelioma deferring systemic therapy. Patients with low baseline tumor volume and those with low tumor growth rate were significantly associated with a longer OS.Keywords: Pleural mesothelioma, Tumor volume, Artificial intelligence, Mesothelioma

Introduction

Pleural mesothelioma is a rare and aggressive malignancy that arises from the pleura.1 The clinical course of pleural mesothelioma is heterogeneous, with some patients experiencing an indolent disease trajectory, whereas others face a more aggressive one.2,3 Recent advances in systemic therapy, particularly after the publication of the CheckMate 743 trial, have introduced combination immunotherapy with nivolumab and ipilimumab as an important treatment option alongside conventional chemotherapy.4

The optimal timing of systemic therapy initiation remains uncertain. Although randomized data have revealed no clear survival benefit of immediate treatment compared with delayed treatment, retrospective studies have reported conflicting results, including associations between delayed treatment and improved survival.5, 6, 7 Pleural mesothelioma has limited treatment options, and the optimal timing for starting therapy remains debated. Clinicians especially question which patients benefit from delayed treatment and which require earlier intervention. Understanding the factors that influence disease progression and treatment response is therefore important to optimize treatment options and the timing of therapy initiation for patients with pleural mesothelioma.

An artificial intelligence (AI) model for automatic total tumor volume measurement, ARTIMES, was previously developed and validated for pleural mesothelioma.8 ARTIMES is a deep learning–based segmentation model trained on 1102 manually annotated computed tomography (CT) scans from patients with pleural mesothelioma across multiple international centers.8 The model automatically segments pleural tumor tissue on CT imaging and calculates the total tumor volume (TTV). This enables the automated quantification of tumor volume. In external validation across 18 independent hospitals, ARTIMES achieved a 95% DICE overlap with expert radiologist annotations and an R2 of 99% for TTV quantification.8 The AI-derived volumetric measurements formed the basis of novel volumetric response criteria and demonstrated improved prognostic performance compared with conventional mRECIST assessment.8 These volumetric measures may therefore serve as potential prognostic factors, helping to identify patients with different overall survival (OS) outcomes. We aimed to evaluate whether baseline tumor volume (in mL) and tumor growth rate (in mL/mo), measured using the AI-based model ARTIMES, are prognostic factors for OS.8 We focused on patients who deferred systemic therapy for at least 3 months. This interval was chosen based on standard follow-up schedules for time-to-event end points, which typically recommend disease assessment every 3 to 4 months after treatment.9

Methods

Study Population

We conducted a single-institution retrospective study and included all patients with pleural mesothelioma referred to our center between January 2010 and January 2025. We selected patients who met the following inclusion criteria: (1) histologically or cytologically confirmed pleural mesothelioma; (2) age above or equal to 18 years; (3) availability of at least two CT thorax scans made before the start of treatment with a slice thickness less than or equal to 3 mm and fully contained lungs in the imaging field; (4) deferred systemic treatment for at least 3 months after diagnosis, following a shared decision-making process between the patient and the treating physician; (5) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score of 0 to 1 at the time of the decision to defer treatment; and (6) received at least one line of systemic therapy after an initial period of treatment deferral.

Study End Points

The primary outcome was OS, defined as the time from diagnosis to death. Secondary outcomes included the prognostic value of baseline tumor volume and tumor growth rate for OS.

Data Collection

Baseline characteristics of included patients with pleural mesothelioma were collected at the date of the first clinic visit. These included age, sex, ECOG performance status, histology, smoking status, tumor side, pleurodesis, date of diagnosis, deferral time in months, and date of death or last follow-up.

Statistics

Continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR). Differences between groups were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test applied to the individual data points. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using Fisher’s exact test.

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to identify optimal cutoff values for baseline tumor volume and tumor growth rate per month in predicting 12-month mortality. Thresholds were selected using Youden’s index to maximize the sum of sensitivity and specificity.

Patients alive at last follow-up were censored. OS in patients categorized by slow versus fast tumor volume growth rate and by high versus low baseline tumor volume was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed with the log-rank test. Median survival time and IQR were calculated for each group.

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were performed for each potential prognostic factor, including age, sex, ECOG status, histology, pleurodesis (yes/no), tumor side, deferral time in months, baseline tumor volume (high/low), and tumor growth rate per month (high/low). Variables with p less than 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in a multivariable Cox model to identify independent prognostic factors for OS. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the Netherlands Cancer Institute (IRB number: IRBd22-361).

Tumor Volume and Growth Rate Measurement

Tumor volume was automatically quantified using ARTIMES.8 The model accepts thoracic CT scans reconstructed with a soft-tissue kernel, with or without intravenous contrast, and generates a binary segmentation of the total tumor burden. Total tumor volume was calculated by summing all voxels labeled as tumor and converting to milliliters based on voxel spacing. For each patient, the tumor growth rate was determined by dividing the change in total tumor volume between pretreatment CT examinations by the time interval between scans, expressed in milliliters per month (mL/mo).

Results

From a total of 113 patients with pleural mesothelioma who were referred to our hospital and had at least two scans before treatment, 71 patients were included in the study (Fig. 2). The median age was 66.8 (IQR: 62.1–73.4) years. Most were male (57/71, 80.3%) and had an ECOG performance status of 0 (61/71, 85.9%). The median deferral time was 8.0 (IQR: 4.0–12.5) months. Patients received a variety of systemic treatment regimens, either in clinical trials or in routine clinical care, including platinum-pemetrexed, platinum-gemcitabine, gemcitabine, vinorelbine, immune checkpoint inhibitors (nivolumab, nivolumab-ipilimumab, pembrolizumab, pembrolizumab-lenvatinib), vorinostat, oxaliplatin-gemcitabine, and MesoPher. These treatments were administered in heterogeneous sequences ranging from one to five consecutive lines of systemic therapy. The baseline characteristics are found in Table 1.

Receiver operating characteristic analysis was performed to identify optimal cutoff values for baseline tumor volume and tumor growth rate per month in predicting 12-month mortality. Baseline tumor volume had an optimal threshold of 56.5 mL, with a sensitivity of 84.6% and a specificity of 58.6%. The optimal cutoff for tumor growth rate per month was 67.8 mL, with a sensitivity of 76.9% and a specificity of 77.6%.

Baseline tumor volume (cutoff: 56.5 mL) did not differ significantly in histologic subtype distribution between patients with low (n = 36/71, 50.7%) and high (n = 35/71, 49.3%) baseline tumor volumes (Fisher’s exact test, p = 1.00). In the high-volume group, 25 of 35 (71.4%) had epithelioid histology, four of 35 (11.4%) had mixed histology, four of 35 (11.4%) had sarcomatoid histology, and two of 35 (5.7%) had unknown histology. In the low-volume group, 27 of 36 (75.0%) had epithelioid histology, five of 36 (13.9%) had mixed histology, four of 36 (11.1%) had sarcomatoid histology, and zero of 36 (0%) had unknown histology. There was no statistically significant difference in histologic subtype distribution between patients with low (n = 48/71, 67.6%) and high (n = 23/71, 32.4%) tumor growth rates (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.107). In the high-growth group, 15 of 23 (65.2%) had epithelioid histology, six of 23 (26.1%) had mixed histology, two of 23 (8.7%) had sarcomatoid histology, and zero of 23 (0%) had unknown histology. In the low-growth group, 37 of 48 (77.1%) had epithelioid histology, three of 48 (6.3%) had mixed histology, six of 48 (12.5%) had sarcomatoid histology, and two of 48 (4.2%) had unknown histology. Overall, histologic subtype was not significantly associated with either baseline tumor volume or tumor growth rate, suggesting that the prognostic value of these volumetric measures is unlikely to be confounded by histology.

Using these thresholds, Kaplan–Meier survival analyses demonstrated statistically significant differences in OS between groups. Patients with low baseline tumor volume had a median OS of 30.95 months (IQR: 19.19–57.92), compared with 16.89 months (IQR 9.40–31.67, p = 0.0066) for patients with high baseline volume (Fig. 3).

Kaplan–Meier curve comparing the overall survival of the low baseline tumor volume group versus high baseline tumor volume group.

Similarly, patients with a low tumor growth rate per month had a median OS of 29.40 (IQR: 17.35–57.46) months, whereas those with a high growth rate had a median survival of 14.59 months (IQR: 9.00–30.03, p = 0.00057; Fig. 4).

Kaplan–Meier curve comparing the overall survival of the low tumor growth rate per month group versus high tumor growth rate per month.

In univariate Cox proportional hazards models, histology, deferral time, pleurodesis status, classification of growth rate per month (high/low), and classification of baseline tumor volume (high/low) were significantly associated with OS. Specifically, sarcomatoid and biphasic histology had higher hazards compared with epithelioid histology (HR = 2.24, 95% CI: 1.07–4.69, p = 0.033). Longer deferral time was associated with a lower hazard (HR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–1.00, p = 0.013). Each additional month of deferral was associated with a 2% reduction in the hazard of death. Pleurodesis was associated with a lower hazard (HR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.24–0.88, p = 0.018), as was a low growth rate (HR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.23–0.68, p = 0.00083) and a low baseline volume (HR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.32–0.84, p = 0.0074) (Supplementary 1, Table 2).

In the multivariable Cox model including histology, deferral time in months, pleurodesis, growth rate, and baseline volume, sarcomatoid histology was independently associated with worse OS (HR = 3.28, 95% CI: 1.38–7.75, p = 0.007). The model revealed acceptable discriminative ability (concordance = 0.714) and was statistically significant overall (likelihood ratio test; p = 0.001; Supplementary 1, Table 2).

Discussion

In our study, baseline tumor volume and tumor growth rate per month were significant prognostic factors for OS in patients with pleural mesothelioma who deferred systemic therapy for at least 3 months. Patients with a lower baseline tumor volume (≤56.5 mL) had a median OS of 30.95 months compared with 16.89 months in patients with a higher baseline volume (HR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.32–0.84, p = 0.0074). Similarly, patients with slower tumor growth rates (≤67.8 mL/mo) had a median OS of 29.40 months compared with 14.59 months for those with faster growth (HR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.23–0.68, p = 0.00083). Although these findings do not directly answer whether therapy should be deferred, they suggest that careful monitoring of tumor burden and growth may help guide the timing of systemic treatment in clinical practice.

Our findings refine previous reports on the timing of systemic therapy in pleural mesothelioma.5, 6, 7 Prior studies have revealed conflicting results, with O’Brien et al.5 reporting no survival difference between early and delayed treatment, whereas Schmid et al.6 and a large population-based analysis reported improved survival with delayed treatment initiation.7 Our results are consistent with the latter observations and suggest that these findings may be influenced by underlying tumor biology. Specifically, patients with lower baseline tumor volume and slower tumor growth had markedly better OS, indicating that tumor burden and growth rate may contribute to patient selection in studies evaluating treatment timing. The use of an AI-based model for tumor volume assessment facilitates objective and reproducible quantification of tumor volume, making these measurements easily applicable in both research and clinical settings.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and single-center design introduces potential selection and information bias. Second, the sample size was relatively small (n = 71). Another limitation is that treatment decisions were influenced by the clinical judgment of the treating team, patient preference, and referral timing. Furthermore, our study focused only on patients who deferred systemic therapy. A key limitation of this approach is that patients who were initially managed with a deferred strategy but clinically deteriorated during follow-up and became too unwell to receive systemic therapy are not represented in this cohort. As a result, the study may underestimate the proportion of patients in whom a deferral strategy ultimately fails due to clinical decline. We cannot determine whether deferral affects outcomes compared with patients treated immediately or those who never received therapy. Future studies including these populations are needed to assess the safety of therapy deferral in larger cohorts of pleural mesothelioma patients. Finally, as the study focused on patients who deferred treatment for at least 3 months, the results do not apply to patients requiring immediate therapy or with more aggressive disease because of symptoms caused by pleural mesothelioma.

Despite these limitations, our results suggest that lower baseline tumor volume and slower tumor growth are associated with improved survival in patients deferring systemic therapy. This aligns with previous reports indicating a potential benefit of delayed treatment initiation in selected pleural mesothelioma populations. The use of ARTIMES allowed for precise, automated measurement of tumor volume, which could help in clinical decision-making.

Although our findings provide insight into prognostic factors in pleural mesothelioma, generalizability is limited to similar patient populations, primarily those with good performance status (ECOG 0–1) who can defer systemic therapy. The results may not be directly applicable to patients with symptomatic, fast-progressive disease or those who want to receive immediate treatment. Nevertheless, the demonstration that baseline tumor volume and growth rate are associated with survival may inform treatment planning and risk stratification in broader clinical practice. Future prospective studies in larger, multicenter cohorts are needed to validate these findings and refine recommendations regarding the optimal timing of therapy initiation.
