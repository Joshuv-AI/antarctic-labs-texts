A one-pot, enantioselective allylic oxidation of cycloolefins: a novel benzoic acid/TBHP system catalyzed by Cu-complexed SBA-15-immobilized chiral aniline oxazoline ligands
Authors: Shiva Majidian, Hamid Arvinnezhad, Saadi Samadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An innovative one-pot approach for efficient enantioselective allylic oxidation of cycloolefins has been developed using benzoic acid derivatives/tert-butyl hydroperoxide to synthesize enantioenriched allylic esters in good to high yields (up to 87%) and ees (up to 83%). This reaction is catalyzed by Cu(CH3CN)4PF6 coordinated to chiral aniline oxazoline ligands immobilized on SBA-15. The structure of the most efficient heterogenized ligand was characterized using FTIR, XRD, TGA, SEM, elemental mapping, and BET/BJH techniques. Reusability tests demonstrate that the catalytic activity remained effectively unchanged over five sequential runs, indicating the catalyst's excellent stability and potential for sustainable, green synthesis.

Introduction

Enantioselective allylic oxidation of olefins, known as the Kharasch–Sosnovsky reaction, is a powerful strategy in asymmetric synthesis for generating chiral allylic esters and alcohols. Chiral oxazoline-based ligands are among the most privileged chiral ligands and are widely employed in a broad range of asymmetric transformations, providing chiral products with high enantioselectivities. Because the chiral centers are located in the vicinity of the chelating nitrogen atoms, a well-defined chiral environment is created around the metal center, facilitating efficient chiral induction. Furthermore, these valuable ligands can typically be readily synthesized by reacting a carboxylic acid or a nitrile with a chiral β-amino alcohol, allowing their structures to be easily fine-tuned for specific transformations.3–15

SBA-15 (Santa Barbara Amorphous-15) is an ordered mesoporous silica material characterized by a 2D hexagonal framework, thick walls, a high specific surface area (>1000 m2 g−1), a large pore volume (1–2 cm3 g−1), excellent thermal and mechanical stability, and tunable pore diameters ranging from 5 to 15 nm.16 The primary advantages of SBA-15 over other mesoporous silicas (e.g., MCM-41) are its thicker walls and superior hydrothermal stability, which prevent structural collapse under harsh conditions.17 In contrast, porous carbon structures lack readily modifiable surface chemistry, and Metal–Organic Frameworks (MOFs), which offer great flexibility, typically exhibit poorer mechanical and thermal stability, particularly in acidic or basic media.18

Building upon the above-mentioned background, as well as our extensive experience in the allylic oxidation,9–15,19–26 particularly inspired by achieving excellent enantiomeric excess (ee) using heterogeneous chiral ligands,9,13,14,19,20,23,24 we aimed to immobilize chiral aniline oxazolines (An-Ox) on SBA-15 and evaluate the catalytic activity of their copper complexes in the enantioselective allylic C–H oxidation of simple cyclic and acyclic olefins. While presynthesized peroxybenzoate derivatives, typically prepared from substituted benzoic acids and tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP) in two tedious, time-consuming steps, are commonly employed as oxidants in this asymmetric transformation, the direct use of these substrates in a one-pot strategy for the reaction remains unexplored.

Experimental

General procedure for the synthesis of chiral aniline oxazoline ligands (An-Ox) 1a–d

Synthesis of chiral hydroxy amides 2a–d

In a 25 mL round-bottom flask containing Boc-4-aminobenzoic acid (Boc-4-Abz-OH) (1.19 g, 5 mmol), prepared from the reaction of 4-aminobenzoic acid with di-tert-butyl dicarbonate (Boc)2O,9,13 dissolved in dry CH2Cl2 (15 mL), oxalyl chloride (0.86 mL, 10 mmol) and DMF (3 drops) were added successively at 0 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere. The mixture was stirred for 4 h. The solvent was removed under reduced pressure, affording the corresponding acyl chloride as a light yellow solid in quantitative yield (1.27 g). This intermediate was then immediately dissolved in dry CH2Cl2 (15 mL), and added to a solution of the chiral aminoalcohol (5.1 mmol), obtained from the reduction of the corresponding chiral amino acids,27 and Et3N (0.77 mL, 5.5 mmol) in dry CH2Cl2 (15 mL) at 0 °C. The mixture was allowed to warm to ambient temperature and stirred overnight. After completion of the reaction (monitored by TLC), the mixture was washed with brine (15 mL), and the aqueous phase was extracted with EtOAc (3 × 5 mL). The combined organic extracts were dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, filtered, and the filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure. Flash chromatography (20–50%/EtOAc: n-Hexane) afforded the corresponding chiral hydroxy amides 2a–d as white solids in 90%, 91%, 89, and 90% for 2a, 2b, 2c, and 2d, respectively.10,12,13

Synthesis of N-protected aniline oxazoline ligands 3a–d (cyclization of hydroxy amides)

In a 50 mL flame-dried round-bottom flask under a nitrogen atmosphere, hydroxy amides 2a–d (3 mmol), and N,N-dimethyl-4-aminopyridine (DMAP) (0.019 g, 0.15 mmol) were dissolved in CH2Cl2 (10 mL). The mixture was cooled to 0 °C, after which Et3N (0.92 mL, 6.6 mmol), and a solution of p-toluenesulfonyl chloride (0.57 g, 3 mmol) in CH2Cl2 (5 mL) were added. The mixture was allowed to warm to room temperature and stirred for 12 h (monitored by TLC). It was then diluted with CH2Cl2 (10 mL) and washed with saturated NH4Cl solution (10 mL). The organic phase was separated, and the aqueous phase was extracted with CH2Cl2 (3 × 5 mL). The combined organic extracts were washed with saturated NaHCO3 solution, dried over Na2SO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure to yield light yellow oils. Purification of the crude products by column chromatography (25–75%/EtOAc: n-Hexane) afforded the chiral pure products 3a–d as light yellow oils in 95%, 88%, 84% and 97%, respectively.10,12,13

Synthesis of aniline oxazoline ligands 1a–d (deprotection of the N-protected aniline oxazoline ligands)

In a 10 mL round-bottom flask, N-protected aniline oxazoline ligand 3 (2.5 mmol) was added to a mixture of CH2Cl2 and TFA (7 mL, 6 : 1 v/v). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 3 h. Upon completion (monitored by TLC), water (8 mL) was added. The organic phase was separated, washed with aqueous NaHCO3 solution (8 mL, 5%), and dried over Na2SO4. The solvent was removed under reduced pressure to yield a crude yellow oil, which was then purified using column chromatography (30–40%/EtOAc: n-Hexane) to afford the ligands 1a–d as light yellow oils in 93%, 86%, 95% and 90%, respectively.10,12,13

General procedure for preparation of chiral heterogeneous ligand 4

Synthesis of SBA-15 and functionalized SBA-15 (SBA-Cl)

SBA-15 was prepared from Pluronic P123 (EO20PO70EO20) and tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS). The obtained SBA-15 was reacted with 3-chloropropyl trimethoxysilane (CPTMS) to yield the functionalized material (SBA-Cl), as described in our previous procedure (SI, page S3).14,28

General procedure for immobilizing the chiral An-Ox ligands (1a–d) on SBA-15

Under a nitrogen atmosphere, a 50 mL flame-dried round-bottom flask equipped with a condenser was charged with SBA-Cl (1 g) and dry toluene (20 mL). To this mixture, Et3N (4 mmol, 0.56 mL), and the chiral An-Ox ligand 1a–d (4 mmol) were added. The reaction mixture was heated under reflux for 24 hours. After cooling to room temperature, the solid was collected by filtration, washed with CH2Cl2/MeOH (3 × 20 mL), and Soxhlet-extracted with CH2Cl2/MeOH (50 mL, 1 : 1 v/v) for 24 h to remove any unreacted ligand. The resulting solid was dried at room temperature to yield the chiral heterogeneous ligands SBA-An-OX 4a–d. Considering that ligand 4a provided the best results, its structure was characterized by FT-IR, XRD, TGA, SEM, elemental mapping, and BET/BJH analyses.14

General procedure for one-pot enantioselective allylic oxidation

Under a nitrogen atmosphere, a 10 mL Schlenk tube was charged with dry CH3CN (2 mL), chiral heterogeneous ligand 4a (50 mg), and Cu(CH3CN)4PF6 (0.030 mmol, 11.16 mg), and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 h. 4-Nitrobenzoic acid 10a (1 mmol, 0.17 g), and the olefin 5–9 (2.5 mmol) were added, and stirring was continued for 20 min. TBHP (1.5 mmol, 0.14 mL) was subsequently added, and the reaction was stirred. Upon completion (monitored by TLC), the solvent was removed under reduced pressure, and the residue was extracted with EtOAc and 5% ammonia solution. The organic layer was washed twice with saturated aqueous NaHCO3 and dried over anhydrous MgSO4. After evaporation of the solvent, the chiral allylic esters were obtained.9,14,19,20,23,24

Result and discussion

Synthesis of chiral homogeneous An-Ox ligands and immobilization of them on SBA-15

The chiral heterogeneous ligands 4 were prepared by immobilizing chiral homogeneous precursors on SBA-15. Initially, chiral homogeneous ligands (An-Ox) 1a–d were prepared as described in our previous report.13 This involved converting Boc-4-aminobenzoic acid (Boc-4-Abz-OH), formed from the reaction between 4-aminobenzoic acid and (Boc)2O,9,13 to the acyl chloride using oxalyl chloride and a catalytic amount of DMF. Subsequent treatment of the resulting acyl chloride with chiral aminoalcohols provided chiral hydroxy amides 2a–d. Then, cyclization with p-toluenesulfonyl chloride in the presence of N,N-dimethyl-4-aminopyridine (DMAP) and Et3N afforded the corresponding N-protected aniline oxazoline ligands 3a–d. Finally, deprotection of 3a–d with trifluoroacetic acid provided the target chiral homogeneous ligands 1a–d in excellent yields (Scheme 1).

Synthesis of chiral homogeneous aniline oxazoline ligands (An-Ox) 1a–d.

The structure of the prepared ligands 1a–d were confirmed using 1HNMR and 13CNMR spectroscopy. For example, in the 1HNMR spectrum of ligand 1a (R = Ph), a broad singlet signal at δ = 4.01 ppm corresponds to the NH2 protons. The CH2–O and N–CH protons of the oxazoline ring appear as triplets at δ = 4.23, 4.75, and 5.34 ppm. The aromatic protons of the phenyl ring are observed at δ = 7.33 ppm as a singlet. The aniline protons appear as two doublets at δ = 6.68 and 7.85 ppm. In the 13CNMR spectrum, the imine carbon (O–CN) of the oxazoline ring and the C–NH2 carbon of the aniline moiety appear at δ = 166.5 and 149.8 ppm, respectively. Seven additional aromatic signals were observed in the range of δ = 113.8–142.8 ppm. The C–N and C–O carbons of the oxazoline ring appear at δ = 69.8 and 74.7 ppm.

In the next phase, the reaction of SBA-15 with 3-chloropropyl trimethoxysilane generated functionalized SBA-15 (SBA-Cl). This material then reacted with An-Ox ligands 1a–d in refluxing toluene to provide the chiral heterogeneous aniline oxazoline ligands (SBA-An-Ox) 4a–d (Scheme 2).14,28 The structures of SBA-15, SBA-Cl, and SBA-An-Ox-Ph 4a were characterized using various techniques, including FT-IR, XRD, TGA, SEM, elemental mapping, and BET/BJH.

Preparation of the chiral heterogeneous An-Ox ligands 4a–d.

In the FT-IR spectrum of SBA-15 (Fig. 1), a broad band at around 3400 cm−1 is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations of the adsorbed water and silanol groups. The corresponding O–H bending vibration is observed at around 1640 cm−1. Strong bands at 1084 and 800 cm−1 are characteristic of the Si–O–Si stretching vibrations of the silica framework. Immobilizing 3-chloropropyl trimethoxysilane (CPTMS) on SBA-15 was evidenced by the appearance of a weak aliphatic C–H stretching band and C–Cl vibrations at approximately 2946 and 780 cm−1, respectively. In the FT-IR of SBA-An-Ox 4a, bands at 1640 and 1508 cm−1 correspond to the imine (CN) and aromatic (CC) stretches of the ligand, respectively. Although the band at 1640 cm−1 partially overlaps with the O–H bending, its presence, along with the new aromatic CC band, supports the successful grafting of the homogeneous ligand 1a onto the functionalized support.14

FTIR spectra of the prepared mesoporous materials.

The mesostructured order of the synthesized materials (SBA-15, SBA-Cl, SBA-An-Ox-Ph 4a) was determined using powder low-angle X-ray diffraction (LAXRD) (Fig. 2). The presence of three characteristic peaks, one strong peak in the 2θ range of 0.76–0.87° corresponding to the (1 0 0) plane, and two smaller peaks at around 2θ = 1.4 and 1.6 corresponding to the (1 1 0) and (2 0 0) planes, respectively, was observed. These reflections confirm the p6mm hexagonal symmetry characteristic of mesoporous materials, indicating that the pristine SBA-15 framework remained intact during the immobilization process. The observed decrease in peak intensities from unfunctionalized SBA-15 to SBA-Cl and to chiral heterogeneous ligand 4a, as well as slight changes in the peak positions, suggest the successful immobilization, likely without disrupting the ordered pore structure.14

Powder LAXRD patterns of the prepared mesoporous materials.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) provided clear evidence for the stepwise functionalization of SBA-15 (Fig. 3). A slight mass loss of approximately 1–3% below 150 °C is attributed to the desorption of physically adsorbed water and residual solvents within the mesopores.

TGA thermograms of the prepared mesoporous materials.

The TGA thermogram of unmodified SBA-15 exhibits a gradual weight loss of approximately 5% up to 700 °C. This weight reduction is primarily attributed to the removal of water at lower temperatures and the progressive dehydroxylation of surface silanol groups (Si–OH) at higher temperatures.

In the CPTMS-functionalized material (SBA-Cl), the TGA curve shows an overall mass loss of around 11%. The additional ∼6% loss is attributed to the decomposition of the immobilized organic linker, confirming successful grafting. This curve exhibits a sharp decomposition step at temperatures above 350 °C, corresponding to the CPTMS moiety.

The TGA profile of SBA-An-Ox-Ph 4a displays a steeper mass-loss pattern, with a total loss of about 22%, representing an ∼11% increase relative to SBA-Cl. This increased mass loss and degradation pattern is attributed to the decomposition of the immobilized ligand. These TGA results, along with CHN elemental analysis, indicate that the ligand 1a loading on SBA-15 is approximately 0.47 mmol g−1.

Elemental mapping using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was employed to investigate the compositional features of the heterogeneous ligand 4a (Fig. 4). The images reveal a uniform distribution of Si, O, C, N, and Cl throughout the sample, demonstrating that the homogeneous ligand An-Ox 1a is uniformly anchored onto the mesoporous SBA-15 support. The presence of chlorine is attributed to residual unreacted chloro group from the SBA-Cl.

Elemental mapping images of heterogeneous ligand 4a.

Morphological feature of the heterogeneous ligand 4a was studied using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis (Fig. 5). The SEM micrographs show surface features consistent with parallel channels, and uniform particle size distribution.14

SEM micrograph of heterogeneous ligand 4a.

The textural properties of the mesoporous ligand 4a were evaluated by analyzing its N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm (Fig. 6). The isotherm corresponds to a type IV isotherm, which is a hallmark of mesoporous materials.17,29–32 A distinctive hysteresis loop is observed between approximately 0.6–0.85 p/p0, corresponding to nitrogen capillary condensation within the ordered mesopores. The loop resembles H1-type hysteresis, indicating uniform cylindrical mesopores and confirming that the structure is preserved after functionalization.

N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm and BJH pore size distribution of ligand 4a.

The BJH pore size distribution shows a sharp, narrow peak centered at ∼4 nm, consistent with uniform mesopore channels. The presence of a smaller pore size than pristine SBA-15 provides strong evidence for successful grafting of the oxazoline ligand within the mesochannels. The high peak intensity and the absence of significant broadening are attributed to the preservation of the mesostructured material's structural integrity (Fig. 6).17,30–32

Further structural features of the mesoporous materials were calculated from data obtained by BET, BJH, and XRD analyses.14,24 As shown in Table 1, the parameters derived from BET and BJH calculations (BET surface area, BET total pore volume, BJH pore volume, and mean pore diameter) decrease progressively from SBA-15 to SBA-Cl, and then to SBA-An-Ox-Ph (ligand 4a). In contrast, the wall thickness (calculated from the d100 spacing and the hexagonal unit cell parameter a0, determined from the (100) reflection) shows a corresponding increase. These trends validate the successful immobilization of the chiral homogeneous An-Ox ligand 1a onto SBA-15.

One-pot asymmetric allylic oxidation of cyclohexene in the presence of SBA-An-OX 4a–d

Initially, cyclohexene was subjected to enantioselective allylic oxidation in the presence of a complex of 0.03 mmol Cu(CH3CN)4PF6 and 20 mg ligand 4a at room temperature. Under these conditions, the desired chiral allylic ester 11a was obtained in 80% yield with 47% ee after 48 h (Table 2, entry 1).

To optimize the reaction conditions, evaluation of the ligand-to-copper ratio showed that using 50 mg of heterogeneous ligand 4a (∼0.024 mmol, based on TGA and CHN analysis) together with 0.03 mmol of Cu(CH3CN)4PF6 afforded the corresponding chiral allylic product in 95% yield and 65% ee within 24 h (Table 2, entry 4). It is reasonable to assume that a portion of the copper salt absorbs onto the SBA-15 surface and within its pores; therefore, the effective molar ratio of copper to ligand was approximately 1 : 1. Reducing the amount of ligand decreased both yield and ee and prolonged the reaction time, while increasing the ligand amount to 60 mg did not improve the results (Table 2, entries 1–5). A similar trend was observed when the amount of copper salt was decreased to 0.01 mmol. Although increasing the copper salt loading shortened the reaction time to 17 h, the yield decreased slightly to 92% and the ee to 59% (Table 2, entries 6–10).

Variation of the reaction temperature showed that the best results (79% yield and 77% ee) were achieved at 0 °C (Table 2, entry 12). At 10 °C, the chiral product was obtained in 84% yield and 68% ee (Table 2, entry 11). Lowering the temperature to −10 °C increased the ee value to 86%, but the yield dropped to 30% and the reaction time increased to 72 h (Table 2, entry 13). Further decreasing the temperature to −20 °C resulted in no detectable product even after 96 h (Table 2, entry 14).

Examination of other ligand derivatives (4b–d) revealed that the corresponding chiral allylic esters were obtained with lower ee's than with ligand 4a (Table 2, entry 12 vs. entries 15–17). While ligand 4b (R = benzyl) resulted in a slightly higher yield than 4a, ligands 4c (R = i-Pr) and 4d (R = i-Bu) provided lower yields.

In the final optimization step, various derivatives of benzoic acid 10a–k bearing electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs) and electron-releasing groups (ERGs) were examined (Table 2, entries 12, 18–26). The results demonstrated that regardless of the substitution position, as the strength of the EWG increased, the results generally improved (Table 2, entries 18–23). Conversely, benzoic acids substituted with ERGs such as p-Me 10j and p-OMe 10k derivatives, led to reduced results (Table 2, entries 25, 26). Notably, while the benzoic acid with the strongest EWG (3,5-di-NO2) 10b provided a slightly higher yield and a marginally shorter time compared to the p-nitrobenzoic acid 10a, its ee was not superior (Table 2, entry 12 vs. entry 18).

To examine the scope and generality of the reaction conditions, 5- to 8-membered cyclic olefins and the cyclic diene 1,5-cyclooctadiene (1,5-COD) were subjected to the optimized enantioselective allylic oxidation conditions (Table 3). The corresponding chiral allylic products were obtained in generally good to high ee values and yields. Cyclopentene, cycloheptene, and cyclooctene provided the desired products with lower enantioselectivities and yields compared to cyclohexene (Table 3, entries 2–4). Allylic oxidation of 1,5-COD resulted in the desired chiral allylic ester with the highest yield (87%) and ee (83%) in a shorter reaction time than all other substrates (Table 3, entry 5). These superior results may be attributed to the favorable accommodation of the 1,5-COD twist-boat conformation within the catalyst complex cavity.33–35 Examination of acyclic olefins obtained the corresponding achiral products with inferior yields.

Reusability of the chiral heterogeneous catalyst (4a-Cu)

The reusability of the chiral heterogeneous catalyst, the complex of 4a-Cu(CH3CN)4PF6, was investigated for the enantioselective allylic oxidation of cyclohexene under optimized conditions (Table 2, entry 12). Following reaction completion, the heterogeneous catalyst was recovered by filtration, washed with CH2Cl2, and dried at 50 °C. The catalyst demonstrated robust stability over five consecutive reuse cycles (Fig. 7). As shown in the diagram, after the 5th run, the catalyst efficiency decreased, resulting in lower yields and ee values, while the reaction required longer completion times. Deactivation of the catalyst, starting from the sixth reuse cycle, is likely due to pore blockage by residual solvents or starting materials, as well as copper leaching.

Proposed reaction mechanism

The mechanism commences with the coordination of Cu(CH3CN)4PF6 to the heterogeneous ligand, forming the chiral catalyst. According to the literature,9,36–45 the catalytic cycle initiates when TBHP coordinates to the copper(i) in the catalyst center through its oxygen atoms. Subsequent dissociation of the O–O bond oxidized Cu(i) into Cu(iii), generating hydroxyl and crypto tert-butoxy radicals, which are coordinated to the copper ion. Next, the olefin coordinates to copper via its double bond, followed by the abstraction of an allylic hydrogen by the hydroxyl group, leading to the elimination of water. In the subsequent step, the substituted benzoic acid enters the catalyst cycle; its acidic hydrogen atom is abstracted by the tert-butoxy group, releasing tert-butyl alcohol and forming the key reaction intermediate. Finally, the carboxylate intermediate attacks the si-face of the olefin through a pericyclic rearrangement-involving the migration of a π bond and a reductive-elimination process-regenerating the Cu(i) catalyst and producing the corresponding (S)-allylic ester. The catalytic cycle repeats upon the addition of TBHP (Scheme 3).

Conclusion

In summary, we have successfully developed an effective one-pot enantioselective allylic oxidation of cycloolefins. This strategy employs benzoic acid derivatives and tert-butyl hydroperoxide as efficient oxidants to obtain enantioenriched allylic products with good to high yields (up to 87%) and ee values (up to 83%) in the presence of Cu(CH3CN)4PF6 coordinated to SBA-15-immobilized chiral aniline oxazoline, as the catalyst. Characterization analyses confirmed the structure of the immobilized ligand. The catalyst's activity remained high over five reuse cycles, highlighting its robustness and potential as a sustainable, eco-friendly approach to asymmetric transformations. This procedure offers a valuable and sustainable protocol for the efficient synthesis of enantioenriched allylic esters.
