The Role of Nurse–Patient Mutuality on Self-Care Behaviors in Adults Living with Inflammatory Bowel Disease: A Multicenter Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Daniele Napolitano, Barbara Bassola, Maura Lusignani, Silvia Cilluffo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSelf-care is a core component of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) management and includes self-care maintenance (behaviors aimed at preserving health and treatment adherence), monitoring (attention to symptoms and bodily changes), and management (responses to symptom exacerbations). Nurse–patient mutuality, grounded in trust, reciprocity, and shared involvement in care, may support these behaviors, but its role in IBD remains underexplored. This study aimed to examine the association between nurse–patient mutuality and self-care behaviors in adults with IBD.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted across nine Italian IBD outpatient centers between April and June 2024. Adults with a confirmed diagnosis of Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis completed the Nurse–Patient Mutuality in Chronic Illness (NPM-CI) scale and the Self-Care of Chronic Illness Inventory (SC-CII). Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to examine the association between the three dimensions of mutuality and self-care maintenance, monitoring, and management, adjusting for age, sex, body mass index, disease type, education, and occupation.ResultsA total of 440 patients were included in the analysis; 52% were male, the mean age was 43 years (SD 16.4), 51% had Crohn’s disease, and 49% had ulcerative colitis. Among the dimensions of the NPM-CI scale, developing and going beyond was positively associated with self-care maintenance (β = .23, p = .003) and monitoring (β = .18, p = .019); deciding and sharing care was positively associated with self-care monitoring (β = .15, p = .022) and management (β = .28, p < .001); and being a point of reference was positively associated with self-care management (β = .25, p = .001). Crohn’s disease, compared with ulcerative colitis, was associated with better self-care maintenance (β = − .12, p = .012). The models explained 13% of the variance in self-care maintenance, 17% in self-care monitoring, and 26% in self-care management.ConclusionNurse–patient mutuality was significantly associated with self-care behaviors among patients with IBD, with distinct relational dimensions linked to distinct self-care domains. These findings support the relevance of strengthening therapeutic relationships to promote tailored self-care in IBD nursing practice.Keywords: Inflammatory bowel disease, Self-care, Chronic illness, Disease activity, Patient-reported outcomes

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a chronic, relapsing–remitting condition that imposes a substantial burden on individuals and healthcare systems [1]. Characterized by fluctuating disease activity, IBD affects approximately 0.5% of the population, with prevalence projected to approach 1% in Western countries by 2030 [2, 3]. Beyond gastrointestinal symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, and fatigue, IBD is associated with significant psychological distress, social limitations, and impaired quality of life [4, 5]. As a result, effective IBD management requires more than pharmacological treatment alone and increasingly relies on patient engagement, education, and adaptive health behaviors [6].

Within this context, the nurse–patient relationship is a fundamental pillar of effective IBD management [7]. As advanced practice professionals, IBD nurses play a central role in coordinating care, promoting adherence to treatment, providing disease-specific education, and ensuring continuity of care across fluctuating disease trajectories [8–11]. Owing to their sustained, close interactions with patients, nurses often serve as stable reference figures throughout the illness course, particularly during periods of uncertainty, symptom exacerbation, and therapeutic transitions.

In recent years, attention has increasingly focused on the concept of mutuality in the nurse–patient relationship. Mutuality is defined as a relational process grounded in reciprocity, trust, empathy, and collaboration, in which both nurse and patient actively contribute to the therapeutic relationship [12, 13]. Evidence suggests that mutuality may enhance communication, foster emotional security, and facilitate shared decision-making, ultimately improving care processes and outcomes [14, 15]. Despite its recognized relevance, mutuality has been only marginally explored within the context of IBD [16]. Given the chronic, unpredictable nature of IBD and the need for ongoing therapeutic interactions, this clinical setting appears particularly suitable for examining the development and potential impact of mutual nurse–patient relationships [17–19].

Grounded theory research has identified three core dimensions of mutuality: developing and going beyond, which describes the evolution from a purely clinical interaction to a personalized relationship; being a point of reference, reflecting the nurse’s role as a stable and trusted guide; and deciding and sharing care, which captures shared decision-making and collaborative care planning [20]. One key behavioral outcome that may be shaped by mutuality is self-care, broadly defined as the process by which individuals maintain health and respond to illness through intentional actions and decision-making [21].

According to the Middle-Range Theory of Self-Care of Chronic Illness, self-care comprises three interrelated components: maintenance, monitoring, and management, which are essential for achieving optimal health outcomes in chronic conditions [21]. In IBD, these processes involve treatment adherence, symptom recognition, and appropriate responses to exacerbations. However, compared with other chronic conditions, self-care in IBD remains relatively underexplored, and existing evidence suggests suboptimal performance, particularly in the management domain [21–23]. Taken together, these observations suggest that mutuality may represent an important relational mechanism supporting self-care in IBD.

On this basis, the present study investigated the relationship between nurse–patient mutuality and self-care behaviors in adults with IBD [24].

The aim of this study was to examine the association between nurse–patient mutuality and self-care behaviors in patients with IBD.

Methods

Design

This study employed a multicenter, cross-sectional, observational design, following the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational research [25]. This study was conducted within the framework of a research project supported by the N-ECCO Grant 2024 (European Crohn’s and Colitis Organization), which promotes nurse-led research in IBD.

Setting

Data were collected between April and June 2024 across nine Italian tertiary-level IBD centers Including the following: Fondazione Policlinico Universitario A. Gemelli IRCCS (Rome, coordinating center), ARNAS G. Brotzu (Cagliari), AOU “Renato Dulbecco” (Catanzaro), AOU Consorziale Policlinico di Bari (Bari), PTP Nuovo Regina Margherita (Rome), ASUFC – Azienda Sanitaria Universitaria Friuli Centrale (Udine), Ospedale del Mare, (Napoli), Santa Maria del Prato Hospital (Feltre), and Azienda Ospedaliera di Padova (Padua).

Participant Recruitment

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of CD or UC were consecutively recruited during routine outpatient visits, and all eligible patients attending the clinics during the study period were invited to participate. All participating centers operated within an established multidisciplinary IBD care model and had at least one dedicated IBD nurse formally integrated into the clinical pathway and routinely recognized by patients as a reference professional, ensuring a consistent and standardized approach to patient identification and enrollment across centers.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion criteria included fluency in Italian and a minimum disease duration of 12 months. Patients with severe concomitant chronic conditions, as defined by the Charlson Comorbidity Index or with diagnosed psychiatric disorders were excluded [26].

Study Procedures and Data Collection

Questionnaires were self-administered in private consultation rooms immediately following the outpatient visit. Trained research staff were available to clarify any doubts. Completion required approximately 15 min. Data were anonymized and entered into a centralized database.

Instruments

A comprehensive set of sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral variables was collected using a structured questionnaire to better understand the patient characteristics potentially associated with self-care behaviors. Sociodemographic variables included age, gender, marital status, educational attainment, and occupational status, which are commonly recognized as influencing health behaviors and access to care. Clinical data included the type of pathology (CD vs UC), disease duration, current pharmacological treatment (e.g., biologic therapy), history of prior surgical interventions, presence of perianal disease, and use of nutritional supplements.

Mutuality

Mutuality was assessed using the Italian version of the Nurse–Patient Mutuality in Chronic Illness (NPM-CI) scale [20]. Grounded in the theoretical framework of chronic illness care and informed by the conceptual framework of Nurse–Patient Mutuality in Chronic Illness, the scale was designed to capture patients’ perceptions of the quality of their relationship with nurses, emphasizing reciprocity, emotional connection, and collaborative involvement in care. The instrument comprises 20 items, grouped into three theoretically derived dimensions. The first dimension, developing and going beyond, includes items exploring the personal and emotional dimensions of the nurse–patient relationship, such as mutual respect and sharing personal experiences. The second dimension, being a point of reference, reflects the nurse’s role as a stable figure of support and reassurance in times of need. The third dimension, deciding and sharing care, addresses the extent to which patients feel involved in decisions regarding their treatment and perceive care planning as a shared process with their nurse. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“not at all true”) to 5 (“completely true”), with total scores spanning from 20 to 100. Higher scores denote a greater sense of mutuality within the nurse–patient relationship. The Italian version of the NPM-CI has previously been validated in patients with chronic illness [20].

Self-Care

Self-care was assessed using the Italian version of the Self-Care of Chronic Illness Inventory (SC-CII), adapted for patients with IBD [22]. This 18-item instrument is grounded in the Middle-Range Theory of Self-Care of Chronic Illness. It evaluates the patient’s ability to engage in self-care behaviors across three key domains: maintenance, monitoring, and management. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “never,” 5 = “always”), and subscale scores are standardized to 0–100 to facilitate interpretation. A threshold score of 70 or higher is generally considered indicative of adequate self-care in each domain [21]. The Italian version adapted for patients with IBD has previously been validated [22].

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sample characteristics, including means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationships among the three dimensions of mutuality and the three domains of self-care. Missing data were assessed at both the item and individual levels. To address the research question, multiple linear regression analyses were performed using SPSS version 29. Each dimension of mutuality (developing and going beyond, being a point of reference, and deciding and sharing care) was entered as an independent variable in separate models predicting self-care maintenance, monitoring, and management. All models were adjusted for age, gender, body mass index (BMI), disease type (CD vs UC), education, and occupation. Covariates were selected a priori based on their clinical and sociodemographic relevance and their potential association with self-care behaviors in chronic illness. Specifically, age and gender may influence health behaviors, disease type may reflect differences in symptom patterns and care needs, education and occupation may affect access to resources and engagement in care, and BMI was included as an indicator of general health status.

Results are reported as standardized regression coefficients (β) to indicate effect size, together with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p-values to reflect statistical significance and precision of the estimates. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Bias Control

To reduce potential sources of bias, all participating centers followed the same recruitment procedures and eligibility criteria. Consecutive enrollment was adopted to limit selection bias, and the regression models were adjusted for relevant sociodemographic and clinical covariates to reduce confounding.

Ethical Considerations.

The study received ethical approval from the Lazio 3 Territorial Ethics Committee (Protocol No. 0023486/23) and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06015789). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and anonymity and confidentiality were guaranteed.

Multivariate Linear Regressions

A multiple linear regression was carried out to examine whether the three dimensions of mutuality were associated with self-care maintenance. The model explained 13.2% of the variance in self-care maintenance and was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Developing and going beyond was positively associated with self-care maintenance (β = 0.23, 95% CI [0.4, 2.0], p = 0.003), while CD (vs UC) was associated with higher self-care maintenance (β = − 0.12, 95% CI [− 5.2, − 0.6], p = 0.012).

To examine associations with self-care monitoring, a regression model was fitted. The model explained 16.6% of the variance (p < 0.001). Developing and going beyond (β = 0.18, 95% CI [0.2, 2.4], p = 0.019) and deciding and sharing care (β = 0.15, 95% CI [0.1, 0.6], p = 0.022) were positively associated with self-care monitoring. None of the covariates were significantly associated.

Finally, a regression model was conducted for self-care management. The model explained 26% of the variance (p < 0.001). Being a point of reference (β = 0.25, 95% CI [0.3, 1.2], p = 0.001) and deciding and sharing care (β = 0.28, 95% CI [0.4, 0.9], p < 0.001) were positively associated with self-care management. None of the covariates were significantly associated (Table 3).

Discussion

This multicenter cross-sectional study investigated the association between nurse–patient mutuality and self-care behaviors in adults living with IBD. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to examine this association within the IBD context. Evidence from populations with multiple chronic conditions has shown that the three dimensions of mutuality play distinct roles in predicting self-care maintenance, monitoring, and management [27].

Using validated measures of mutuality and the three domains of self-care maintenance, monitoring, and management, we analyzed the data from 440 patients recruited across 9 Italian IBD centers. Multiple regression analyses showed that mutuality dimensions were differentially associated with the self-care domains: “developing and going beyond” was associated with self-care maintenance and monitoring, while “being a point of reference” and “deciding and sharing care” were associated with self-care management. These findings suggest that mutuality enhances patients’ ability to sustain health behaviors, attend to symptom changes, and respond appropriately [28].

Re-examining these associations from the perspective of mutuality dimensions provides deeper insight into how relational processes shape self-care in IBD. Developing and going beyond, defined by empathy, trust, reciprocity, and a willingness to move beyond traditional professional boundaries [14, 29, 30], appears to lay the foundation for effective self-care maintenance [21]by fostering open communication, treatment adherence, and lifestyle adjustments [29, 31]. This dimension also supports self-care monitoring, improving patients’ ability to interpret bodily changes and recognize subtle symptoms such as fatigue, pain, or alterations in bowel habits [32, 33]. When patients perceive the nurse–patient relationship as safe and collaborative, they may feel more confident in appraising symptoms and seeking clarification. Similar patterns have been observed in oncology and diabetes, where supportive therapeutic relationships enhance monitoring behaviors [34, 35]. Developing and going beyond has also been associated with better maintenance and management behaviors in chronic illness populations, aligning with our findings [27].

Deciding and sharing care, reflecting on shared decision-making and negotiating expectations and responsibilities, also plays a crucial role in self-care monitoring and management. In IBD, with unpredictable trajectories and complex therapeutic pathways, collaborative decision-making strengthens patients’ sense of agency and supports informed appraisal of symptom changes [18, 36]. Patients who perceive shared responsibility may more effectively monitor their condition and respond promptly to exacerbations. This dimension is especially relevant where organizational constraints limit opportunities for dialogue, making mutual understanding and shared goal-setting essential [18]. In IBD specifically, shared decision-making has been identified as a determinant of effective self-care behaviors [37]. Consistent with our results, studies on chronic illnesses found that decision-making and care sharing are associated with stronger self-care performance [27].

Finally, being a point of reference emerged as a significant predictor of self-care management. This dimension reflects the patient’s ability to recognize a trusted nurse as an accessible and stable source of clarification, reassurance, and guidance [22], which is particularly relevant during the periods of uncertainty or acute symptom worsening. A recognized reference nurse may support adherence and timely responses to clinical changes [35]. This aligns with evidence showing that IBD nurse specialists play a central role across disease phases, supporting continuity and coordination [7]. Notably, however, previous studies did not identify a significant association for this dimension, highlighting the novelty and relevance of our findings[23, 29, 38].

Overall, these results emphasize that mutuality is not a single relational construct but a set of complementary dimensions that support different aspects of self-care in IBD. By fostering trust and reciprocity (developing and going beyond), supporting collaborative decision-making (deciding and sharing care), and providing relational continuity (being a point of reference), nurses can promote sustained, informed, and adaptive self-care behaviors. Strengthening these relational processes may enhance patient engagement, improve disease management, and contribute to better long-term outcomes [21, 29, 37, 39].

In interpreting these findings, it is important to consider the role of the sociodemographic and clinical covariates included in the regression models. Across all three self-care domains, age, gender, BMI, education, and occupation were not significant predictors, suggesting limited influence when relational factors—particularly mutuality—are accounted for. This aligns with evidence underscoring the importance of relational and contextual dynamics over demographic factors in shaping self-care in chronic illness [20, 21]. Disease type was the only significant covariate: patients with CD demonstrated higher self-care maintenance than those with UC, consistent with literature describing stronger symptom vigilance and care continuity in CD [18]. However, disease type did not influence monitoring or management, which appears to rely more strongly on relational mechanisms such as mutual understanding, shared decision-making, and access to a reference nurse [7, 40].

Implications for Policy, Practice, and Research

In clinical practice, patients with IBD often face difficulties in maintaining self-care due to symptom variability, treatment complexity, psychological distress, and limited ongoing support [40]. Mutuality can help sustain engagement across these fluctuating conditions. Nurses should therefore prioritize relational and patient-centered frameworks that acknowledge evolving needs. Continuity of care and a sustained therapeutic relationship with a dedicated IBD nurse are essential organizational elements for helping patients recognize early clinical changes and adopt timely self-care strategies [7]. Given the dynamic nature of IBD, patients require support to adjust maintenance, monitoring, and management behaviors over time. Mutuality reinforces confidence in symptom interpretation, supports behavioral activation, and enables personalized adaptation of self-care strategies. Structured, mutuality-based nurse–patient interactions and phase-specific educational materials can further enhance patients’ ability to anticipate symptom changes and act appropriately [39]. From a policy standpoint, investing in nurse-led models of care grounded in mutuality should be considered a priority, as such models promote relational continuity, shared decision-making, proactive self-care support, and long-term disease control.

This study confirms evidence from chronic illness research, where mutuality dimensions show distinct associations with self-care [27]. Future research should examine how each dimension supports specific self-care processes, particularly how developing and going beyond contributes to symptom awareness, and how being a point of reference and deciding and sharing care facilitate effective management. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine how mutuality and self-care evolve over time and to guide interventions tailored to relational mechanisms in the nurse–patient interaction.

Strengths and Limitations of the Work

This was the first study to investigate nurse–patient mutuality among patients with IBD regarding self-care. The results suggest that the dimensions of the NPM-CI scale affect self-care differently. To control for selection bias and increase sample representativeness, we enrolled patients from several hospitals in outpatient settings.

This study has limitations. First, although the sample size was large, it was a convenience sample and representative of a single population and culture. Further studies on samples from different people and cultures would be appropriate to confirm these results. In addition, this study used a cross-sectional design. A longitudinal design would be appropriate to confirm the association between mutuality and increased self-care over time.

Conclusion

This multicenter study demonstrates that nurse–patient mutuality plays a meaningful role in supporting self‑care among individuals living with IBD. By evidencing a significant association between mutuality and all three components of self‑care, our findings highlight the importance of relational processes in helping patients maintain daily health behaviors, remain attentive to symptom changes, and respond effectively to exacerbations. These results underscore the potential value of incorporating mutuality‑informed approaches into IBD nursing practice to strengthen engagement and promote more adaptive disease management.

Future research should build on these findings by clarifying causal pathways and examining how mutuality can be integrated into targeted relational interventions. Longitudinal studies, in particular, are needed to understand how mutuality and self‑care evolve over time and to determine how best to support sustained self‑care behaviors in patients with IBD.
