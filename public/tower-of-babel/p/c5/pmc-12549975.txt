Niche-associated Type IV collagen promotes GLP-1/Notch receptor activation in the C. elegans germline
Authors: Pier-Olivier Martel, Rustelle Janse van Vuuren, Julia Degrémont, Sarah Turmel-Couture, Armi M Chaudhari, Eden Dologuele, Lucie Beaulieu, Alexandre Clouet, Patrick Narbonne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBasement membranes are thin and dense proteinaceous layers that surround tissues to maintain their structure. With age, chronic inflammation typically stiffens this basement membrane through a phenomenon called fibrosis, which is reflected by increasing levels of the basement membrane’s main structural component, type IV collagen (COL IV). Tissue fibrosis and elevated Notch signalling are two co-occurring hallmarks of many inflammation-linked diseases, including cancer, but their in vivo relationship has not been clearly defined. Here we show that EMB-9/COL IV accumulation around the C. elegans germline stem cell niche promotes GLP-1/Notch receptor activation in germline stem cells. Moreover, we find that contrasting with previous beliefs, reducing GLP-1/Notch activity in vivo leads to a generalized dramatic increase in EMB-9/COL IV levels, and thereby promotes fibrosis. The generalized fibrosis that comes along with inflammaging may therefore act as a root cause for cancer by promoting Notch signalling, and perhaps other ligand-receptor interactions.Subject terms: Adult stem cells, Cell signalling, Germline development

Introduction

Fibrosis increases with age and promotes cancer by stiffening basement membranes, altering several biophysical cues that impact on the phenotype, differentiation, and growth/proliferation of neighbouring cells1,2. Whether it is in skin, liver, kidney, lung or breast cancer, the fibrotic state of the tissue is often accompanied by increased Notch signalling3,4, an evolutionarily conserved pathway that plays an important role in development and homoeostasis5. Hence, Notch signalling inhibition has been investigated as a potential therapy for the treatment of fibrosis-related pathologies3,6,7. However, such strategies have led to conflicting results and have overall failed to meet expectations8–11, potentially due to the use of unspecific Notch receptor targeting drugs, such as gamma secretase inhibitors12,13, and/or in vitro models. We therefore aimed to better define the in vivo relationship between Notch signalling and fibrosis, two major actors in cancer, using the genetically tractable C. elegans model.

We show here that in this nematode’s distal germline, type IV collagen (COL IV) levels in the basement membrane surrounding the germline stem cell (GSC) niche, likely through increasing the gonad basement membrane’s stiffness, promote GLP-1/Notch receptor activation in adjacent GSCs. Across various mutations and conditions that vary in distal basement membrane COL IV levels, and using two different assays to directly measure GLP-1/Notch activity in vivo, our data strongly support a positive relationship between niche-associated basement membrane COL IV levels and Notch receptor activity. We further demonstrate that specifically reducing the secretion of COL IV by the niche significantly reduces GLP-1/Notch activation, establishing this relationship as causal. Furthermore, we unexpectedly find that in vivo, the loss of GLP-1/Notch activity leads to a generalised dramatic increase in COL IV levels throughout the animal. This effect occurs independently of GLP-1/Notch receptor activity and instead depends on the resulting consequences on germline development. These results therefore highlight how changes in the constitution of an organ’s basement membrane can influence Notch signalling within this organ in vivo.

Results

A basement membrane glycoprotein synergistically interacts with glp-1/Notch

In C. elegans, a Notch ligand, LAG-2, is expressed by the distal tip cells (DTCs) and binds to the Notch receptor GLP-1 at the surface of adjacent GSCs3,14. This releases the Notch intracellular domain (NICD), allowing its translocation to the nucleus where it promotes expression of the lst-1 and sygl-1 stemness genes, and acts as the niche signal allowing GSCs to proliferate15–18.

To identify new regulators of GSC proliferation, we used ethyl methane sulphonate (EMS) to randomly mutagenize a mutant strain carrying a conditional glp-1 gain-of-function (gf) allele, which causes the formation of proximal undifferentiated benign germline tumours under restrictive conditions19–21. These tumours arise because the glp-1(gf) allele prevents proper GSC differentiation, such that when these cells reach the proximal gonad, where another notch ligand (APX-1) is present, they begin to proliferate again22. We screened for glp-1(gf) enhancers that would cause the formation of germline tumours even under permissive conditions (see Methods for details), and isolated a novel allele of the mig-6L gene, coding for the long isoform of the C. elegans Papilin homologue (Fig. 1a–c, Supplementary Fig. 1a–c). We confirmed that this was the causal mutation by transformation-rescue (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 2a, b) while the mig-6L(qz2) allele was removed from the glp-1(gf) background and outcrossed 4 times prior to all subsequent analyses. Papilin is a highly conserved basement membrane protein that negatively regulates procollagen peptidases, thus acting to reduce collagen levels23. MIG-6L has mainly been implicated in the regulation of DTC migration, likely acting to soften the distal basement membrane and permit gonad elongation during development as the DTC is pushed by ongoing GSC proliferation24,25. As this new mig-6L(qz2) allele specifically affects its C-terminal protease and lacunin (PLAC) domain via a typical EMS-induced G/C to A/T transition that replaces the last cysteine within the highly conserved six-cysteine region by a tyrosine (Fig.1a, Supplementary Fig. 1a–c)26, its properties inform on the function of this poorly characterised domain.

To evaluate the effects of the qz2 mutation on MIG-6L expression and stability, we tagged endogenous mig-6L(WT) and mig-6L(qz2) with monomeric NeonGreen (mNG)27. MIG-6L(WT):mNG was present within the DTC and the gonadal basement membrane, consistent with its reported DTC-specific expression and secretion throughout gonad development (Supplementary Fig. 2c)24. In the mutant, DTC MIG-6L(QZ2):mNG levels were increased, but they were markedly reduced within the gonad’s basement membrane (Supplementary Fig. 2c–f). DTCs were also wider and shorter in mig-6L(qz2) mutants, but their volumes remained similar (Supplementary Fig. 2g–i). We infer from these data that MIG-6L’s PLAC domain likely promotes protein secretion, resulting in lower basement membrane MIG-6L concentrations in the qz2 mutant.

Single mig-6L(qz2) mutants did not develop any tumours (Fig. 1b, c) but had a mild phase III DTC migration defect, where the final elongation of the gonad arms was reduced by roughly 25% (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), and a slightly reduced brood size (Fig. 1d). In contrast, all three phases of DTC migration are severely impacted in mig-6L(ø) mutants, such that these animals are completely sterile24, confirming that mig-6L(qz2) is hypomorphic. The reduced brood size of mig-6L(qz2) animals was similar to that of glp-1(gf) single mutants grown under permissive conditions, yet the double glp-1(gf); mig-6L(qz2) laid even fewer eggs, the numbers being consistent with each mutation having an additive effect on egg production (Fig. 1d). There however was a synergistic effect between mig-6L(qz2) and glp-1(gf) on embryonic and larval lethality. Namely, the double mutants showed more than three times the embryonic lethality and more than twice the larval lethality than what would have been expected from additive effects (Fig. 1e, f). We conclude that mig-6L interacts synergistically with glp-1 during embryonic and larval development, in addition to within the context of GSC niche signalling, and acts to restrict glp-1/Notch signalling.

MIG-6L limits DTC-associated COL IV levels

Reduced function of MIG-6S, a widely-expressed shorter MIG-6 isoform that lacks the PLAC domain24,28, was reported to broadly increase COL IV levels across the animal23. We therefore asked whether mig-6L(qz2) affected deposition of COL IV in the gonad basement membrane, around the DTC. We used an established EMB-9::mCherry marker29 to visualise and quantify the COL IV α1 chain, and the Plag-2::GFP reporter30 to mark DTCs. We imaged distal gonads to create three-dimensional (3D) DTC models based on the green fluorescent signal (Fig. 2a). We did not detect any differences in Plag-2::GFP fluorescence intensities between control and mig-6L(qz2) mutant DTCs (Supplementary Fig. 3a), indicating that lag-2 expression was unaffected. Cropping out the volume corresponding to the GFP-labelled DTC from the red fluorescent channel allowed the quantification of COL IV levels within this DTC mask (thereon referred to as DTC-associated COL IV or DTC-AC) (Fig. 2a, b). Surprisingly, in control animals grown at 15 °C, we noticed that anterior DTC-AC was significantly higher than posterior DTC-AC (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Fig. 3b), despite no antero/posterior changes in DTC size and shape, or in MIG-6L::mNG levels (Supplementary Fig. 2d, g–i).

This method did however not distinguish COL IV located in the gonad basement membrane around the DTC from any unsecreted COL IV, which largely appeared as intracellular blobs (Fig.2a, Supplementary Fig. 3c). To evaluate distal basement membrane COL IV levels, we quantified COL IV levels within those intracellular blobs and subtracted their contribution to the DTC-AC mask (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). DTC-associated basement membrane COL IV levels followed DTC-AC, as there was still more COL IV in the anterior basement membrane than in the posterior within control hermaphrodites (Supplementary Fig. 3b, e).

To confirm this antero/posterior asymmetry in distal gonad basement membrane composition, we also measured DTC-associated NID-1/nidogen (DTC-AN), as this protein links the COL IV and laminin networks together, such that its level generally follows COL IV levels23,31,32. We used an endogenous NID-1::mNG tag23 together with a Plag-2::mCherry::PH33 marker to label DTCs (Fig. 2c). While there were no obvious intracellular NID-1::mNG (Supplementary Fig. 3c), DTC-AN was higher in the anterior gonad arm of control hermaphrodites (Fig. 2d), as expected. We conclude that the anterior distal gonad basement membrane has higher COL IV and NID-1 contents than the posterior and is thus likely to be more rigid34.

Due to this antero/posterior distal gonad basement membrane asymmetry, we considered DTC position in all subsequent analyses. In mig-6L(qz2) mutants, we observed a posterior-specific increase in DTC-AC (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 3f), causing the loss of the antero/posterior DTC-associated basement membrane COL IV asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). We therefore conclude that MIG-6L negatively regulates DTC-AC levels, consistent with its proposed role as a negative regulator of basement membrane COL IV levels23,24.

Since DTC migration is reduced in mig-6L(qz2) mutants, we considered the possibility that increased DTC-AC could be linked to reduced gonad elongation. However, DTC migration was similarly reduced in both gonad arms in mig-6L(qz2) mutants (Supplementary Fig. 3g), but DTC-AC was only increased in the posterior gonad arm (Fig. 2b). We also quantified DTC-AC in lon-2(e678) mutants, which specifically have longer anterior distal gonad arms (Supplementary Fig. 3g), but again, found that DTC-AC levels varied independently of distal gonad elongation, as these mutants did not show any antero/posterior asymmetry in DTC-AC (Fig. 2b) or DTC-associated basement membrane COL IV levels (Supplementary Fig. 3h, i). Since the DTCs are shorter and wider in mig-6L(qz2) mutants, we also considered the possibility that a change in the DTC shape could affect DTC-AC, but ruled out this possibility since both DTCs are equally affected in the mutant (Supplementary Fig. 2g–i) while only the posterior DTC-AC is increased (Fig. 2b). We therefore conclude that MIG-6L is required for the regulation of DTC-AC levels, independently of DTC shape, migration or gonad elongation.

GLP-1/Notch activation varies together with DTC-AC levels

Since mig-6L(qz2) enhanced glp-1(gf) defects (Fig. 1b,e,f), and specifically increased posterior DTC-AC levels to remove the antero/posterior DTC-AC asymmetry (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 3b, d–f), we asked if mig-6L(qz2) also affected GLP-1/Notch receptor activation asymmetrically. To approximate GLP-1 activation, we marked all germ membranes with red fluorescence (Pmex-5::TagRFP-T::PH)35 and the GLP-1(NICD) with green fluorescence (GLP-1(NICD)::GFP) [Saravanapriah Nadarajan, David Greenstein and Tim Schedl, personal communication]. Using the red channel, we generated a 3D model of distal germ membranes. We used this mask to separate the fraction of GLP-1(NICD)::GFP localising to the germ membranes from that localising OFF from the cell membranes, either to the cytoplasm, nuclei or rachis, and thus presumably representing the cleaved or “activated” GLP-1(NICD) fraction (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Movie 1). Of note, we did not detect any differences between distal germline GLP-1(NICD)::GFP fluorescence intensities between control and mig-6L(qz2) animals (Supplementary Fig. 4a), indicating that GLP-1 levels were unaffected. We first compared the OFF-membrane fraction of GLP-1(NICD)::GFP in the anterior and posterior gonads of control hermaphrodites but found no significant differences (Fig.3b, Supplementary Fig. 4b). Yet, in mig-6L(qz2) mutants, the fraction of OFF-membrane GLP-1(NICD)::GFP was significantly and specifically higher in the posterior gonad when compared to controls (Fig. 3b), consistent with their posterior-specific increase in DTC-AC (Fig. 2b). To further test whether COL IV levels could influence GLP-1 activation, we used a TGF-ß pathway mutant to alter DTC-AC amounts. The lon-2(e678) mutant is known to upregulate TGF-ß signalling, leading to increased collagen synthesis36,37. As within mig-6L(qz2) mutants, we measured a significant COL IV increase, specifically associated with the posterior DTC of these lon-2(e678) mutants (Fig. 2b). Accordingly, the fraction of OFF-membrane GLP-1(NICD)::GFP was also specifically increased in the posterior gonad of lon-2(e678) mutants (Fig. 3b). These results raised the possibility that mig-6L could reduce posterior GLP-1 activation through reducing posterior DTC-AC.

To validate these GLP-1(NICD)::GFP results, we used a genetically-encoded in vivo sensor able to detect lateral Notch signalling activity (SALSA)38. This sensor combines GFP with a nuclear-localised RFP, the two fluorophores being separated by a Tobacco etch virus protease (TEVp) site. The sensor strain also carries a glp-1(ar648) switch, in which a TEV protease was added to the endogenous NICD. Upon Notch activation, the NICD::TEVp is released from the membrane, enters the nucleus, and can cleave the GFP from its nuclear RFP tether, allowing Notch activation to be quantified as a nuclear RFP/GFP ratio. A switchless strain serves to establish a baseline control, while an RFP/GFP ratio greater than 1 in the glp-1(ar648) strain indicates significant Notch receptor activity (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 4c)38. Using high-resolution confocal microscopy and 3D modelling, we generated microspheres overlapping with germ nuclei to obtain their RFP/GFP ratios (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Movie 2). Since GLP-1 activity gradually declines as a function of the distance from the DTC38,39, we focused on nuclei located within 5 cell diameters from the DTC, where activity is strongest (Fig. 3d). Interestingly, Notch receptor activation was significantly higher in the anterior GSCs of control hermaphrodites than in their posterior GSCs (Fig. 3e), thereby following the asymmetrical DTC-AC levels (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 3b). We note that our previous GLP-1(NICD) analysis failed to detect antero/posterior differences in Notch activation for control hermaphrodites (Supplementary Fig. 4b), but this is presumably due to the increased sensitivity of the SALSA versus the GLP-1(NICD)::GFP assay. We also used the SALSA assay to measure GLP-1 activity in the anterior and posterior gonad arms of mig-6L(qz2) mutants, and according to their similar DTC-AC levels (Supplementary Fig. 3f), the antero/posterior difference in GLP-1 activity was lost (Fig. 3f). Since mig-6L(qz2) specifically increased posterior DTC associated basement membrane COL IV and GLP-1 activity (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 3e), but still left posterior DTC unsecreted COL IV lower (Supplementary Fig. 3d), we conclude that distal basement membrane COL IV levels vary together with GLP-1 activity. These results therefore further strengthen a potential direct relationship between DTC-AC and most likely between distal basement membrane COL IV levels and GLP-1/Notch receptor activation.

Notch activation controls the size of the germ stem and progenitor pool40. To further validate the difference in Notch activity between the anterior and posterior gonad arms in control hermaphrodites, we measured their respective proliferative zone (PZ) size. Consistent with the observed variations in Notch activity, we found that control hermaphrodites have a significantly smaller posterior PZ size (Fig. 3g). In mig-6L(qz2) mutants, however, in which anterior and posterior DTC-AC and Notch activity were similar, both gonad arms had undistinguishable PZ sizes (Fig. 3h). We conclude that the PZ size, an established readout of Notch activity19,20,41, follows DTC-AC levels. Collectively, in wild-type and mig-6L(qz2) mutants, across two Notch activation assays (GLP-1(NICD)::GFP and SALSA) as well as the PZ size readout, our data strongly support a positive relationship between DTC-AC levels and GSC GLP-1/Notch receptor activation.

GLP-1/Notch activity inversely affects COL IV levels

Next, we wanted to use a temperature-sensitive daf-7(e1372) allele to downregulate TGF-ß signalling, and potentially alter COL IV levels33,37. This daf-7 mutation was, however, reported to reduce lag-2 expression by the DTC33, results that we reproduced by quantifying the Plag-2::GFP reporter intensity (Fig. 4a, b). These animals were upshifted to 25 °C to inactivate daf-7, and under these conditions, controls did not show any antero/posterior DTC-AC asymmetry (Fig. 4c). As it would not have been possible to distinguish the effects of changes in DTC-AC levels from those of a reduction in lag-2 expression, we did not measure GLP-1 activity in daf-7 mutants. We nevertheless measured their DTC-AC, and found that it was drastically higher in both the anterior and posterior gonad arms relative to control hermaphrodites (Fig. 4a, c). This unexpected result raised the possibility that reduced lag-2 expression, either directly or through the resulting decrease in GLP-1/Notch activity, led to an increase in DTC-AC.

To investigate whether Notch activity influences DTC-AC levels, we quantified them in both glp-1(lf) and glp-1(gf) mutants19,42. We found that a decrease in GLP-1/Notch activity caused a dramatic increase in both anterior and posterior DTC-AC levels (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Fig. 5a). Conversely, increasing GLP-1/Notch activity significantly reduced DTC-AC levels (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Fig. 5b). Interestingly, the negative effects Notch signalling had on COL IV levels were not restricted to the DTC area, but more broadly affected the specimen. Indeed, COL IV levels were significantly increased in the body wall muscles and distal gonad basement membranes of glp-1(lf) mutants, but decreased in the body wall muscles of glp-1(gf) mutants (Fig.4d, Supplementary Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Movie 3). These results show that glp-1/Notch signalling broadly restricts COL IV accumulation in C. elegans, likely through cell non-autonomous systemic effects.

Changes in GLP-1 receptor activity could influence COL IV levels directly. However, since glp-1(lf) mutants quickly lose their GSC pools to differentiation43, while glp-1(gf) mutants grow GSC tumours19, changes in GLP-1 activity could instead regulate COL IV levels indirectly, through an effect on germline identity/status. Consistent with this, COL IV genes are largely expressed by the animal’s body wall muscles44, while germline loss was reported to increase the expression of many collagen genes45. To discern between a direct or indirect effect, we measured DTC-AC levels in triple gld-3(lf) nos-3(lf); glp−1(lf) mutants that lacked glp-1 activity, but conserved a GSC pool and grew germline tumours due to the simultaneous lack of gld-3 and nos-3 function, two redundant glp-1 downstream inhibitory targets42,46. The absence of a functional GLP-1 receptor per se did not perturb DTC-AC levels as it remained undistinguishable across control, gld-3; nos-3 doubles and gld-3 nos-3; glp-1 triples (Fig. 4d, e). Similarly, body wall muscle and distal germline basement membrane COL IV levels were undistinguishable between gld-3 nos-3 doubles and gld-3 nos-3; glp-1(lf) triples (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). These results suggest that the animal’s COL IV levels, both globally and locally around the DTCs, may be systemically adjusted in response to germline states and do not directly depend on Notch receptor activation status.

DTC-associated COL IV promotes GLP-1/Notch receptor activation

As GLP-1/Notch receptor activation followed DTC-AC levels (Figs. 2, 3, Supplementary Fig. 3), we next aimed to establish whether DTC-AC promotes GLP-1/Notch receptor activation. As COL IV genes are expressed by the animal’s body wall muscles in addition to their DTCs44, to avoid any confounding effects that could result from systemic manipulations of COL IV levels, we used a strain that allows us to specifically interfere with DTC COL IV production by RNAi. In this strain, an rde-1(ne219) allele systemically prevents any RNAi responses47, but a transgene returns RDE-1 activity specifically to the DTCs and restores RNAi only in these cells48. We crossed into this background either the EMB-9::mCherry or SALSA transgenes to measure DTC-AC and GLP-1 activity, respectively, in animals subjected to DTC-specific COL IV depletion. As expected, animals subjected to DTC-specific emb-9(RNAi) had significantly reduced DTC-AC, unsecreted EMB-9::mCherry blobs, and DTC-associated basement membrane COL IV levels when compared to those raised on a control RNAi vector (Fig. 5a–d). GLP-1 activity and PZ size were also decreased in animals subjected to DTC-specific emb-9(RNAi) (Fig. 5e, f). These results demonstrate that specifically decreasing DTC-AC levels causes a reduction in GLP-1/Notch receptor activity in adjacent GSCs.

DTC-AC promotes Notch activation.

Discussion

Due to the broad and severe adverse effects associated with in vivo COL IV level manipulations49–51, the relationship between tissue fibrosis and Notch signalling has remained largely unclear. In vitro analyses that varied matrix stiffness, on the other hand, have led to conflicting results52,53. Notch signalling has been well studied in vivo in C. elegans in two main contexts: during vulva development54,55 and as the GSC niche signal56. Despite such extensive analyses, and apart from a pioneering screen that revealed an interaction between glp-1 and some extracellular matrix components57, it remained unclear how the extracellular matrix composition affected Notch signalling. It was, however, more recently reported that the SDN-1 syndecan, a somatic transmembrane proteoglycan, affected glp-1/Notch signalling, but regulation was found to occur at the level of receptor expression58.

This work therefore adds an additional layer of regulation for GLP-1/Notch activity, namely that the interaction between a Delta-like membrane-bound ligand and the Notch receptor at the surface of stem cells within an adjacent tissue is favoured by increasing levels of COL IV, and thus most likely the stiffness, of the surrounding basement membrane34. Namely, the efficiency of GLP-1/Notch receptor activation in GSCs decreases when DTC-AC levels are low and increases when DTC-AC levels are higher. Further dissecting DTC-AC into unsecreted COL IV and DTC-associated/distal basement membrane COL IV suggested that it was the distal basement membrane levels that were influencing GLP-1 activity. Indeed, DTC-associated basement membrane or distal basement membrane COL IV levels always varied together with GLP-1/Notch activity, while DTC unsecreted COL IV did not (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 3d,h). Considering that growing and proliferating GSCs generate a force that pushes on the DTC, something necessary for DTC migration during development25, that force must be squeezing the DTC against the gonad arm’s distal basement membrane. A change in that basement membrane’s rigidity caused by a variation in it’s COL IV contents could therefore modify the DTC-GSC interface, such as to influence the Delta-Notch interaction (Fig.6a–c), and thereby the PZ size. More work will however be required to test that hypothesis, but if true, it could imply a widely applicable principle.

Hypothetical model for how DTC-AC levels may influence GLP-1 activity.

a Cartoon representation of the distal part of a C. elegans gonad arm. Germline stem cells (GSCs) (orange) are actively growing and dividing, effectively pushing (orange arrows) the distal tip cell (DTC, green) against the distal basement membrane (red). The basement membrane resists deformation (red arrows) according to its stiffness, which is largely governed by its COL IV contents34. b A thin basement membrane is expected to be more easily deformed, allowing for some freeplay between the DTC and GSCs, despite the pressure generated by their growth and proliferation. This looser fit could reduce the interaction between LAG-2 and GLP-1. c A thicker basement membrane is expected to be less easily deformed, preventing the DTC to freely respond to the GSC growth and proliferation-generated pressure, which effectively squeezes it against the basement membrane. This may provide a tighter fit between the DTC and GSCs that could facilitate the interaction between LAG-2 and GLP-1.

When cells rapidly grow and proliferate within an organ surrounded by a basement membrane, that basement membrane’s rigidity could influence cell-cell signalling across its constituting tissues. For instance, a stiffer basement membrane is expected to increase the internal organ pressure and thereby promote cell-cell and intertissue interactions. In the C. elegans distal gonad, this would promote the Delta-Notch interaction, but in principle, it could apply to any other membrane-bound growth factors or signalling molecules. This phenomenon could therefore potentially explain why rapid proliferation within tumours is often accompanied and favoured by fibrosis59.

Our results were somewhat surprising since the bulk of the literature reasoned that basement membrane fibrosis may be a consequence of elevated Notch signalling, and hence focused on targeting this pathway to treat both cancer and fibrotic diseases3,8. In C. elegans, however, downregulation of GLP-1/Notch receptor activity increased COL IV basement membrane deposition, while upregulation had the opposite effect. Fibrosis is therefore not a consequence of high GLP-1/Notch activity. Fibrosis may instead result from the indirect consequences of reduced Notch signalling—in the C. elegans case, the loss of its GSCs to differentiation. If these properties are conserved, we speculate that in humans, as inflammaging favours fibrosis, fibrosis may in turn promote Notch hyperactivation.

It is therefore unclear whether the pharmacological inhibition of Notch signalling to treat cancer or fibrotic diseases will pan out as a successful strategy. Strengthening this view, Notch deficiency was shown to lead to hypertrophy and fibrosis in the mouse heart60. Developing strategies to modify basement membrane collagen levels, for example, by altering precise components of TGF-beta signalling, or collagenase activity59,61–63, could provide more efficient and specific therapeutic approaches.

Methods

C. elegans strains and maintenance

C. elegans were grown on standard nematode growth medium (NGM) seeded with OP50 E. coli bacteria64. Animals were grown at 15 °C, and when indicated, upshifted to 25 °C at the indicated stage and for the indicated period (see Figure legends). When animals were upshifted at the L2 stage, they were further resynchronised at L4, based on vulva development65. Supplementary Table 1 lists all strains used.

Cloning of the mig-6L(qz2) allele

A standard EMS forward genetic screen64 was performed using a fog-1(q785)/hT2 [bli-4(e937) let-?(q782) qIs48]; glp-1(ar202)gf strain. Briefly, after P0 mutagenesis, F2 L4s were upshifted to 25 °C and fog-1(q785); glp-1(ar202)gf homozygotes, which do not develop tumours66, were screened for germline tumour formation. Since these feminised tumorous animals were sterile, we downshifted their fog-1/hT2; glp-1(gf); qz2/+ double heterozygote siblings to 15 °C to recover new fertile homozygous qz2(−/−) animals in the next generation, through re-testing part of their progeny at 25 °C. We later removed fog-1 from the background as we realised qz2 enhanced glp-1(gf) independently from fog-1.

The qz2 allele was subsequently identified as a mig-6L allele by whole-genome sequencing and RNAi screening of candidate mutated genes for any that phenocopied the qz2 phenotype. Finally, molecular identity was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and transformation rescue experiments (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 2a, b).

Transgenics

For mig-6L(qz2) transformation rescue, the qzEx4[mig-6L(+); Pmyo-3::mCherry] extra-chromosomal array was generated by standard microinjections60 of a mixture of pZH117[mig-6L(+) genomic/cDNA minigene]24 (40 ng/μl), pKSII (100 ng/μl), and pCFJ104[Pmyo-3::mCherry]61 (40 ng/μL).

For CRISPR/Cas9-mediated endogenous mig-6L tagging, we used the self-excising cassette method as described67. However, to improve knock-in efficiency, animals were grown on cku-80(RNAi) prior to microinjections68. Supplementary Table 2 lists all plasmids and primers used.

Tumour formation assay

Larvae were grown at 15 °C, synchronised by picking mid-L4 stage individuals65 to a new plate for an additional 72 h, still at 15 °C, except for the glp-1(gf) mutants shown at the bottom of Fig. 1c, which were upshifted to 25 °C for 24 h. The presence of proximal germline tumours was scored using DIC and Ppie-1::mCherry::HIS-58 epifluorescence with a Plan-Apochromat 20x dry objective (NA 0.8) mounted on an inverted Zeiss Axio Observer.Z1.

Imaging

Animals of the indicated stages were paralysed by soaking in an 8 µL drop of a 0.1% tetramizole (Sigma, L9756) M9 solution on a glass coverslip that was then flipped onto a 3% agarose/M9 pad placed on a microscope slide. The gut and germline are intertwined such that when one gonad arm is on top of the gut, the other arm is usually underneath, resulting in fainter fluorescence signals. This most often prevents same-animal antero-posterior fluorescence intensity comparisons. Therefore, except on the rare occasions where both arms were in the same focal plane, only either the anterior or posterior gonad arm/DTC, whichever was on top of the gut, was imaged for each animal.

Whole-animal differential interference contrast (DIC) and or epifluorescence images (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 2a) were acquired every μm using a Plan-Apochromat 20x dry objective (NA 0.8) mounted on an inverted Zeiss Axio Observer.Z1 with an Axiocam 506 mono camera and Zeiss 38 HE GFP or 45 HQ Texas Red shift-free fluorescence filter sets. Images were stitched using the Zen 2.6 software, and using ImageJ, brightness was uniformly adjusted, and animals were straightened for ease of visualisation. All DIC images show single focal planes, and where applicable, fluorescent images also show the corresponding single focal plane.

For all high-resolution confocal acquisitions (Figs. 2a, c, 3a, d, 4a, d, 5a, Supplementary Figs. 2c, 3c), animals were prepared as above, but coverslips were sealed with VALAP (1:1:1 vaseline, lanolin, paraffin), and 0.35 μm z-step stacks were acquired with a Leica SP8 point scanning confocal microscope with a HC PL APO CS2 40x/1.30 numerical aperture oil objective with a zoom factor of 1 for full gonad images, or of 4 for DTC images.

For MIG-6L::mNG quantifications (Supplementary Fig. 2c–f), mNG was excited/collected at 488/488–552 nm with 3% laser intensity.

For NID-1::mNG quantification (Fig. 2c, d), NID-1::mNG was excited/collected at 488/540-549 nm with 1% laser intensity. The DTC mCherry::PH was excited/collected at 552/590–632 nm with 1.5% laser intensity, gain was adjusted each time to detect most of the signal, but avoid saturation, in order to model the DTC as accurately as possible.

For EMB-9::mCherry quantification (Figs. 2a, b, 4, 5a–d, Supplementary Figs. 3b-f,h,I, 5), mCherry was excited/collected at 552/590–632 nm with 0,6% laser intensity. The DTC GFP was excited/collected at 488/487–520 nm with a laser intensity that varied between 1.5% to detect most of the signal, but avoid saturation, in order to model the DTC as accurately as possible.

For Plag-2::GFP quantification (Fig.4b, Supplementary Fig. 3a), GFP was excited/collected at 488/487-520 nm with 0,4% laser intensity.

For the GLP-1(NICD) “cleaving” assay (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Fig. 4a, b), GLP-1(NICD)::GFP was excited/collected at 488/495-538 nm with 6% laser intensity. The PH::TagRFP-T marker was excited/collected at 552/590–632 nm with a laser intensity that varied between 2–5% to detect most of the signal, but avoid saturation, in order to model distal germ membranes as accurately as possible.

For the SALSA assay (Figs. 3d–f, 5e, Supplementary Fig. 4c), GFP and RFP were excited/collected at 488/495–539 nm and 552/590–632 nm, respectively, each with 1% laser intensity.

Distal gonad length measurements

Larvae were grown at 15 °C, and late L4s were picked to a new plate for 24 more hours at 15 °C before they were imaged. ImageJ was used to measure distal gonad length by drawing a segmented line along the centre of the distal half of the gonad arm, from the bend to the distal tip.

MIG-6L 3D modelling

The 3D structure of MIG-6L was modelled using the SWISS-MODEL server69,70, through the automated modelling pipeline that uses the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database and experimentally resolved structures in the Protein Data Bank. A mutant version of the protein, incorporating the C2159Y substitution, was also modelled using the same procedure. The resulting wild-type and mutant structures were superimposed in PyMOL using the align command to assess changes in overall conformation and the distance command for local interactions, including potential alterations in side-chain positioning and bonding patterns71.

MIG-6L::mNG quantification

For MIG-6L::mNG fluorescence quantifications, images were processed with Imaris as above, except that 3D objects were generated based on the green channel to include the whole gonad basement membrane. This object was manually refined using the edit tool to delete unwanted surfaces such as unspecific signal, unwanted structures, etc. The mNG intensity sum that corresponds to the total fluorescence intensity of the gonad basement membrane was then pulled from the statistics tab (Supplementary Fig. 2e).

A second 3D object was generated from the MIG-6L::mNG channel using the same images and methodology, but using different settings, this time to obtain a surface specifically matching the DTC. This new surface was selected, and the DTC MIG-6L::mNG intensity sum was pulled. The DTC MIG-6L::mNG intensity was then subtracted from the gonad arm’s MIG-6L::mNG intensity to obtain the secreted MIG-6L::mNG intensity. Secreted MIG-6L::mNG intensity was then divided by the total gonad arm’s MIG-6L::mNG intensity to obtain the fraction of secreted MIG-6L::mNG (Supplementary Fig. 2f).

For Supplementary Fig. 2d, another set of MIG-6L::mNG acquisitions, this time using a higher zoom factor of 4, was made to achieve a more detailed DTC modelisation using the methodology described below for DTC EMB-9::mCherry quantifications.

Brood size, embryonic and larval lethality

For fecundity, according to standard procedures72, late L4 larvae were singled at 15 °C. Animals were transferred to a new plate every 24 h, after which the number of eggs laid and hatched larvae was scored. The assay was stopped when an individual laid no eggs for 24 h. For embryonic lethality, the number of unhatched eggs after 24 h was recorded (re-checked after 48 h). For larval lethality, the number of dead or arrested larvae was scored 4 days later.

Plag-2::GFP, EMB-9::mCherry and NID-1::mNG fluorescence intensity quantifications

Confocal z-stacks (0.35 μm spacing) were transferred to Imaris 9.2.1, and based on the green channel (Plag-2::GFP), 3D surfaces were generated using identical settings. The 3D models were then individually thresholded to optimally match the DTC surfaces. All objects corresponding to the DTC and its cytonemes were grouped as one structure. For DTC-AC quantification, the GFP object was selected, and using the edit tool, a new EMB-9::mCherry channel corresponding to the DTC-AC was obtained, and its mean intensity was pulled from the statistics tab. For Plag-2::GFP quantification, the total intensity for the GFP channel was pulled.

For NID-1::mNG quantification, the same method was used, but this time DTC modelling used the red Plag-2::mCherry::PH channel to obtain a model that optimally matches the DTC surface, and to generate a new NID-1::mNG channel corresponding to the DTC-AN.

For unsecreted and DTC-associated basement membrane COL IV measurements, with the same images used for DTC-AC quantification, 3D models were generated based on the red EMB-9::mCherry marker using identical settings that allowed for consistent and specific labelling of the DTC intracellular punctuate signals that are referred to as blobs in the manuscript. All objects corresponding to blobs were grouped as one structure. This object was selected, and using the edit tool, two new EMB-9::mCherry channels were generated. A first channel corresponding to the RFP signal found only in the intracellular blobs and a second one corresponding to the leftover signal after the intracellular blobs were deleted, originating from the basement membrane associated with the DTC. Each mean intensities were pulled from its respective statistics tab.

DTC-associated basement membrane measurements could not be obtained using this method in circumstances where basement membrane COL IV levels were too high to allow a clear separation of basement membrane and intracellular contributions (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). In these instances, we instead evaluated distal basement membrane COL IV as follows. For average body wall muscle and distal gonad basement membrane EMB-9::mCherry quantification, based on the red channel, 3D objects were generated as described above to optimally fit the body wall muscle or gonad basement membranes. For body wall muscles, a ≈ 40 × 20 x 5 µm thick section was selected using the edit tool. For the gonad basement membrane, a ≈ 20 × 10 x 1 µm thick distal gonad basement membrane section, ≈20 µm away from the DTC cell body, was selected using the edit tool (Supplementary Movie 3). For both sections, the mean intensity for the EMB-9::mCherry channel was pulled from the statistics tab.

Notch signalling quantification using GLP-1(NICD)::GFP

For GLP-1(NICD)::GFP fluorescence quantifications, images were processed as above, except that a 3D object corresponding to the whole distal gonad was generated using the Pmex-5::PH::TagRFP-T signal. This object was manually refined using the edit tool to delete unwanted parts such as the proximal gonad, unspecific signals, etc (Supplementary Movie 1). Using this mask, we first measured the total GLP-1(NICD)::GFP signal contained within the distal gonad (GFP intensity sum), corresponding to the sum of all fluorescence intensity values for the voxels located within the distal gonad mask. A second 3D object was generated from this trimmed Pmex-5::PH::TagRFP-T distal gonad mask, this time using different threshold settings to obtain a new object specifically matching all distal germ membranes. The corresponding GLP-1(NICD)::GFP intensity sum represented the signal originating from all distal germ membranes. The sum of distal germ membrane GLP-1(NICD)::GFP fluorescence intensity was then subtracted from the sum of distal gonad GLP-1(NICD)::GFP to obtain OFF-membrane GLP-1(NICD)::GFP. OFF-membrane fluorescence intensity was then divided by the total distal gonad arm fluorescence intensity to obtain the fraction of OFF-membrane GLP-1(NICD)::GFP.

Notch signalling quantification using SALSA

For SALSA quantifications38, we imported images into Imaris 9.2.1 as above, and using the spots tool, generated 3.6 μm diameter spheres based on the red (mCherry::H2B) channel corresponding to individual germ nuclei, using the same batch settings for all images. The edit tool was used to refine the selection to only the spheres corresponding to germ nuclei located within 5 cell diameters of the DTC (Supplementary Movie 2). The intensity sums for both the red and green channels from these spheres were pulled to generate individual RFP/GFP ratios for each GSC nucleus. Ratios from single gonad arms were averaged and normalised to the corresponding switchless control averaged anterior and posterior ratios. The switchless control RFP/GFP ratios were normalised to 1, to correspond to the expected equimolar nuclear quantities of GFP and RFP38.

DTC width, length, and volume measurements

Using the Plag-2::GFP acquisitions, DTC width measurements were made in Imaris across the widest part of the DTC body, occurring before the first cytoneme. For DTC length, the distance between the distal tip of the DTC and the tip of its longest cytoneme was measured. For volume measurements, the DTC was selected and its volume was pulled from the statistic tab.

PZ size evaluation

Imaris files used for the SALSA assay were reused, and, based on the red (mCherry::H2B) channel, 2.6 μm diameter spheres, corresponding to germ nuclei, were generated using the spots tool using the same batch settings for every file. We found that using a diameter slightly smaller than the actual nuclei diameter improved nuclei detection efficiency. The edit tool was used to manually delete spheres that did not correspond to PZ nuclei. We defined the PZ as distal to the first row of cells, where at least two distinctive crescent-shaped germ nuclei were present73 and obtained the total number of PZ spheres.

RNAi

Mid-L4s were transferred to control (L4440) or emb-9 RNAi plates74 at 25 °C for 24 h and retransferred to a new RNAi plate at 25 °C. Their progeny were analysed at the stages indicated in the figure legends.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.2.0. For Fig. 1b, we used Fisher’s exact test to determine the significance of the association between two categorical variables. For each other dataset, normality was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variance was verified with the F test for pairwise comparisons and using the Brown-Forsythe test for datasets comprising more than two groups. Tests used are indicated in the figure legends and were chosen according to the following criteria. For pairwise comparisons with normal distributions and equal variances, we used the unpaired two-tailed t-test. For non-Gaussian distributions, the Mann-Whitney test was instead used. When the distribution was normal, but the variance was unequal, we used the Welch’s t-test. For multi-group comparisons that all had normal distributions and equal variances, we used the one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons. For non-Gaussian distributions, we used the Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s multiple comparisons. When the distribution was normal, but the variances were unequal, we used the Brown-Forsythe and Welch’s ANOVA with Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons test.
