Volatilomics analysis reveals marker compounds for assessing chicken freshness and detecting frozen-thawed Korean native chicken
Authors: Zubayed Ahamed, Jeong-Uk Eom, Han-Sul Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the impact of frozen storage on the volatile compound profile of Korean native chicken (KNC) breast and identified marker compounds for freshness assessment and detection of frozen–thawed meat. Fresh samples were analyzed 2 hours postmortem, while frozen-thawed samples were stored at -18 °C for 7 days, thawed at 4 °C for 16 hours, and analyzed for volatiles using HS-SPME-GC-MS. The analysis identified 38 volatile compounds, with aldehydes, being the most prevalent. Freezing significantly reduced pH, increased TBARS values, and altered the relative abundance of several aldehydes and alcohols (P < 0.05). Notably, frozen-thawed samples showed increased levels of aldehydes with 6-10 carbon atoms and compounds such as 1-octen-3-ol, 1-octanol, and 2-ethyl-1-hexanol, indicating lipid oxidation. PCA, and PLS-DA, effectively differentiated between fresh and frozen-thawed samples, with the first two components explaining 50 % of the total variance. The top five discriminatory compounds identified by the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores of over >1.2 included 2-heptanal, 4-pentylbenzaldehyde, (E)-2-octenal, 1-dodecanol, and 2-ethyl-1-hexanol. Among these, 4-pentylbenzaldehyde was positively associated with fresh chicken, while the others were linked to frozen-thawed samples. These findings indicate that freezing- and thawing-induced changes in volatile compounds can serve as reliable markers of freshness. Overall, this analytical approach shows strong potential for quality control and for distinguishing fresh from frozen-thawed KNC.

Introduction

Meat and meat products are excellent sources of proteins, fats, amino acids, minerals, vitamins, and various micronutrients, which make them an integral component of the human diet (Manessis et al., 2020). Global meat consumption has increased substantially in recent decades, with chicken meat emerging as a particularly dynamic segment. This growth is driven by its high protein content, lower saturated fat and cholesterol levels compared to red meat, and relatively affordable cost (Belova et al., 2012; Barido et al., 2022). Despite these advantages, chicken meat is highly susceptible to loss of freshness due to enzymatic and chemical reactions, as well as microbial contamination. This vulnerability arises from its relatively high final pH, abundant nutrients, and elevated water activity (aw), all of which create favorable conditions for oxidation and microbial degradation, particularly in the absence of proper storage conditions (Cantalejo et al., 2016).

In Korea, Korean native chicken (KNC) is highly valued for its characteristic flavor, firmer texture, and perceived health benefits compared with commercial broiler chickens (Jayasena et al., 2013). These quality attributes are closely associated with differences in muscle fiber composition, lipid content, and fatty acid profiles, including relatively higher proportions of unsaturated fatty acids. However, such compositional characteristics may also render KNC more susceptible to oxidative deterioration during storage, especially under frozen conditions, which could potentially lead to pronounced changes in volatile compounds and sensory properties (Choe et al., 2010). Despite the growing consumer demand and premium market value of KNC, scientific information regarding the effects of frozen storage on its volatile profile and freshness-related chemical markers remains limited.

Frozen storage at -18 °C is one of the most widely used methods for preserving meat over extended periods, as it minimizes quality loss during long-term storage (Soyer et al., 2010). Effective rapid freezing suppresses microbial growth and slows undesirable biochemical reactions. However, microbial growth and chemical reactions during processing inevitably result in some irreversible changes to the physicochemical and sensory properties of chicken meat. These include protein degradation, lipid oxidation, and muscle discoloration, which can impact the overall quality of the meat (Yu et al., 2025; Al-Dalali et al., 2022). The freezing process also disrupts cellular structures and damages muscle fibers (Shin et al., 2024). The ice crystals that form can negatively impact the texture, nutritional content, and flavor-related compounds of meat.

The primary factors contributing to the degradation of stored muscle meat are lipid and protein oxidation. These oxidative processes affect the abundant proteins and lipids in muscle cells, influencing the formation of volatile flavor compounds (Al-Dalali et al., 2022a). Additionally, during thawing, the melting of ice crystals triggers the release of proteases, lipases, and lysosomes from damaged cells, further exacerbating lipid oxidation and protein denaturation (Ballin and Lametsch, 2008; Barido et al., 2022). However, some retailers fraudulently mislabel their products, selling frozen-thawed meat as fresh meat to gain higher profits (Chen et al., 2017). Therefore, there is a need to establish a method to distinguish between fresh and frozen-thawed meat.

The effects of frozen storage on volatile compounds of meat have been explored by previous studies (Yu et al., 2025; Shin et al., 2024). Al-Dalali et al. (2022a) investigated the impact of frozen storage on lipid and protein oxidation, as well as the flavor profile of marinated raw beef. They identified six marker compounds, primarily aldehydes and alcohols. They also reported increases in thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) and carbonyl content, indicators of lipid and protein oxidation, respectively, over the storage period. Similarly, Shin et al. (2024) analyzed taste-related and volatile compounds in fresh and frozen-thawed chicken, finding that the total content of sweet and bitter amino acids, inosine monophosphate (IMP), ketones, and oxetane was a key factor distinguishing fresh from frozen-thawed chicken meat. He et al. (2021) successfully differentiated fresh from frozen-thawed beef using rapid evaporative ionization mass spectrometry. Their findings revealed changes in fatty acid and phospholipid composition and content, which can ultimately influence the formation of volatile flavor compounds, further distinguishing the two types of meat. He et al. (2022) investigated the effects of frozen storage (up to 90 days) on fatty acid composition and volatile profiles in mutton. Their results demonstrated significant alterations in volatile compounds throughout the storage period, indicating that frozen storage markedly influences the aroma characteristics of meat. Notably, specific changes in key flavour compounds enabled discrimination among different storage durations, suggesting the potential of volatile profiling as a tool for monitoring storage time and quality changes in frozen meat. Similarly, Al-Dalali et al., (2022b) examined the impact of frozen storage on the major classes of volatile compounds and elucidated their underlying formation mechanisms. Their findings highlighted that lipid oxidation and related biochemical pathways play a critical role in the generation and transformation of volatiles during frozen storage. Therefore, it is both relevant and practical to further investigate the effects of frozen–thaw cycles and to develop reliable analytical approaches for distinguishing fresh meat from frozen–thawed counterparts, thereby ensuring product authenticity and quality control.

Headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) combined with Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) is a widely recognized and effective technique for extracting and analyzing volatile compounds from both cooked and raw meat, as demonstrated by numerous studies (Ahamed et al., 2023; Moran et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2025). Several previous investigations have employed HS-SPME for volatile compound extraction, followed by GC–MS for compound separation and identification (Al-Dalali et al., 2022a; Barido et al., 2022; Qi et al., 2021; Shin et al., 2024). Alternative techniques volatile extraction include solvent-assisted flavour evaporation (SAFE), solid-phase extraction (SPE), and conventional solvent extraction (Pu et al., 2022, 2025). Among these techniques, solid-phase microextraction (SPME) is widely favored due to its simplicity, solvent-free nature, reusability, and effectiveness in extracting a broad range of compounds from various matrices, including food and beverages. Despite its extensive application, few studies have specifically utilized it to examine the effects of frozen storage on the volatile profile of chicken meat or to distinguish between fresh and frozen-thawed meat.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of frozen storage on lipid oxidation and volatile compound profiles of KNC breast using HS-SPME-GC-MS, and to identify key volatile markers capable of reliably discriminating fresh from frozen–thawed meat. The findings of this study are expected to contribute to the development of practical tools for authenticating freshness and controlling quality in high-value poultry products.

Materials and methods

Sampling

A total of 32 KNC breast samples were obtained from 16 chickens, with each chicken providing two breast portions (left and right). The chicken carcasses (average weight 1.5 ± 0.1 kg) were sourced from a licensed slaughterhouse in Jinju-si, Korea, as part of routine commercial processing. The carcasses were transported to the laboratory in an icebox within 15 min of purchase to maintain freshness. In the laboratory, breast portions were excised from the already-processed carcasses. To minimize biological variation between treatment groups, the left and right breast muscles from each individual chicken were randomly assigned to different experimental groups. Specifically, one breast portion from each chicken was analysed immediately as the fresh group, while the corresponding contralateral portion was allocated to the frozen–thawed group. Samples assigned to the frozen–thawed group were stored at −18 °C for 7 days and subsequently thawed under refrigeration at 4 °C for 16 h prior to analysis..

pH

For pH measurement, 3 g of minced sample was taken and mixed with 27 mL of distilled water, followed by homogenization for 30 s using a Polytron homogenizer (T25, Kinematica). The pH was measured at 20 °C using a pH meter (MP230, Mettler-Toledo). To minimize deviations caused by temperature fluctuations, the automatic temperature compensation function of the pH meter was activated, and a three-point calibration was performed prior to all measurements using standard buffer solutions at pH 4.00, 7.00, and 9.00. Each sample was measured four times at different positions, and the mean value was recorded. The standard deviation was calculated to verify the measurement error across analyses.

Color measurement

The samples were exposed to air for 10 min, and then their color was measured using a color meter (CR-400, Minolta, Tokyo, Japan) calibrated with a standard white plate (Y = 93.5, X = 0.3132, and y = 0.3198). The measurements were repeated six times in the central part of each sample. The measurement conditions were D65 illuminant, 2° standard observer, and 8-mm aperture. According to the Commission Internationale de l’Eclairage (CIE) system, color has been expressed in terms of CIE lightness (L*), CIE redness (a*), CIE yellowness (b*), chroma, and hue angle (h°). The chroma and h° were calculated as follows:Chroma=(a*2 + b*2) × 0.5h°=tan−1 (b*/ a*)where a* represents redness and b* represents yellowness.

TBARS

Lipid oxidation was determined using the TBARS (thiobarbituric acid reactive substances) method, based on the procedure of Bozkurt and Erkmen (2004), with slight modifications. Three grams of sample were mixed with 27 mL of 3.86 % perchloric acid solution and homogenized at 14,000 rpm for 30 sec using a homogenizer (T25, IKA, Staufen, Germany). The homogenate was left for 1 hour in a dark, cold environment to allow sufficient extraction of lipid oxidation products. The extracted mixture was then filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and only the filtrate was collected. Two milliliters of the filtrate were mixed with 2 mL of 20 mmol/L TBA solution and allowed to react at room temperature for approximately 15 hours. After the reaction, the absorbance of the MDA–TBA complex was measured at 531 nm using a spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA).

The final TBARS value was calculated as mg MDA/kg meat using the following conversion factor.TBARS (mg MDA/kg)=A531 × 5.58

Headspace solid-phase microextraction

The sample preparation followed a method with slight modification (Ahamed et al., 2023). Each sample, precisely 2.5 grams, was combined with 5 mL of a 25 % NaCl solution. This mixture underwent thorough homogenization for one minute using a homogenizer. Subsequently, the prepared samples were transferred into 20 mL glass vials (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA). An internal standard of 1 µL of 0.4 mg/mL 2-methyl-3-heptanone dissolved in hexane was added. The vials were tightly sealed using mininert valves. For extraction of volatile compounds, the DVB/CAR/PDMS- 50/30 µm (PAL Smart SPME Fiber) fiber was utilized. The vial containing the sample was initially heated for 15 min at 60 °C as an incubation period. Following this, the SPME fiber was exposed to the headspace of the vial for an additional 30 min under the same temperature conditions with continuous shaking. Upon completion of the absorption process, the SPME fiber was desorbed in the injection port of the GC-MS instrument for 6 min. Prior to usage, the fiber was conditioned at 270 °C for 30 min. Additionally, before each analysis, the fiber underwent exposure to the injection port of the GC for another 10 min to clean it.

Running condition on GC-MS

GC-MS analyses were performed utilizing an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph coupled with an Agilent 5973C quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). An autosampler (PAL, Agilent Technologies) was also employed in the analysis process. Helium (99.99 %) was employed as the carrier gas, maintaining a steady flow at a rate of 1 mL/min. The injection port was fitted with a liner (0.75 mm i.d, Agilent Technologies) specifically designed for SPME analysis, and maintained at a temperature of 250 °C. An HP-INNOWax capillary column (60 m × 0.32 mm, 0.25 µm film thickness, Agilent Technologies) was utilized for compound separation. Initially, the oven temperature was set at 40 °C and held for 3 min. Subsequently, there was a gradual temperature increase at a rate of 4 °C/min up to 120 °C, followed by another increase at a rate of 8 °C/min up to 220 °C. A rapid increase to 250 °C was then applied at a rate of 20 °C/min and maintained for 5 min. The interface temperature was set to 280 °C. The mass spectrometer operated in electron ionization mode, with the electron energy set to 70 electron volts (eV) and a scanning range spanning 50 to 450 mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). The ion source and quadrupole Analyzing Volatiles in Pure and Adulterated Cooked Meat Using SPME-GC-MS 937 temperatures were established at 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively. During injection, a pulsed splitless mode was employed throughout the experiment.

Identification of volatile compounds and their relative identification

The identification of volatile compounds was achieved by comparing their mass spectra with the NIST v.14 mass spectral library. A minimum match quality threshold of 80 % was applied to ensure accurate identification. Data acquisition and integration of target ion peak areas were performed using Agilent ChemStation software. To confirm compound identity, linear retention indices (LRI) were calculated based on a homologous series of even-numbered n-alkanes (C6–C40) analyzed under the same chromatographic conditions as the samples. Additional verification of retention times and tentative compound identification was conducted by analysing standard samples of selected detected compounds. The LRI was calculated following the method described by Pranata et al. (2021), with minor modifications. The relative concentrations of the identified compounds were determined by comparing the peak areas of the analytes to those of the internal standard. The concentrations were calculated according to the procedure established in our previous study and expressed as µg/kg (Ahamed et al., 2023).

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). For multivariate data analysis, MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (www.metaboanalyst.ca) was utilized. Data preprocessing included conversion to a CSV file, log transformation, and auto-scaling to ensure normalization. Peaks with more than 50 % missing values were excluded from further analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were applied as unsupervised and supervised methods, respectively, to differentiate between the two groups based on the relative concentrations of the compounds. The Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores were calculated to identify the key compounds contributing to the discrimination between fresh and frozen-thawed meat samples. Cross-validation and response permutation tests were conducted to assess and validate the robustness of the models. All data were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE), and statistical significance was determined at (P < 0.05) using the t-test.

Colour is another crucial quality parameter influencing consumer purchasing decisions and is highly affected by freeze-thawing (Barido et al., 2022). The color parameters include lightness (L*), redness (a*), yellowness (b*), chroma (C*), and hue angle (h). Freezing tends to reduce the lightness of meat, and frozen-thawed meat often appears grayer or browner due to myoglobin oxidation, which is accelerated by the formation of ice crystals. This process increases protein exposure to oxygen, promoting metmyoglobin formation (Jeong et al., 2011). Additionally, freezing and thawing contribute to a greater loss of NADH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide hydride). This is a key factor in the metmyoglobin reducing activity cycle, as ice crystal-induced mitochondrial damage disrupts cellular integrity, accelerating meat discoloration (Henriott et al., 2020). The L* and a* values differed significantly (P < 0.05) between fresh and frozen-thawed samples, while b*, C*, and h* values showed no significant differences. These findings align with previous research (Barido et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2016). Fresh chicken breast had a significantly higher L* value (64.66) compared to frozen-thawed samples (58.81).

A significant increase (P < 0.05) in TBARS values was observed in frozen-thawed samples, with fresh chicken showing a TBARS value of 0.51 mg MDA/kg, which increased to 0.73 mg MDA/kg after freeze-thawing. Frozen storage has a significant effect on lipid oxidation. TBARS values increase gradually during frozen-storage (Al-Dalali et al., 2022). This rise in TBARS values is attributed to the loss of muscle tissue integrity due to freezing and thawing. Lipid oxidation is closely associated with colour deterioration, and Jeong et al. (2011) reported that in beef, lipid oxidation is strongly linked to myoglobin oxidation and is influenced by freezing and thawing methods. Additionally, ice crystal formation during freezing damages cells, leading to the release of pro-oxidants that accelerate lipid oxidation (Lu et al., 2022).

A significant difference in the relative concentration of many volatiles was observed between the two groups (P < 0.05). The greater number of aldehydes found in this study would come from lipid and phospholipid oxidation, as it generates aldehydes and other carbonyl compounds (Barido et al., 2022; Ahamed et al., 2023). The reasons for the higher oxidation activity of phospholipids may be: (1) the difference in the composition of fatty acids, such as the higher proportion of polyunsaturated fatty acids in phospholipids to maintain the fluidity of membranes; (2) surface location at the tissue-air interface; and (3) proximity to the catalytic site of the oxidase. Soyer et al. (2010) evaluated the effect of frozen temperature and storage period on lipid and protein oxidation, concluding that freezing increased the volatiles containing carbonyl groups in chicken. All the straight-chain aldehydes identified in this study were generated by the oxidation and degradation of unsaturated fatty acids. Among the aldehydes, hexanal and nonanal were the most abundant compounds, and there was a significant increase in their concentration from 179.82 to 354.50 µg/kg for hexanal, and 175.35 to 255.94 µg/kg for nonanal after freeze-thawing. Previous research has also identified hexanal and nonanal as prevalent volatile compounds, which are often regarded as excellent predictors of lipid oxidation (Yin et al., 2021). These two compounds primarily originate from the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids, particularly linoleic acid and oleic acid. Shin et al. (2024) also observed increased levels of hexanal and nonanal, along with octanal, associated with freeze-thawing.

The concentration of aldehydes identified fluctuated between increase and decrease, with a significant increase in the concentration of hexanal, heptanal, octanal, nonanal, (E)-2-octenal, benzaldehyde, and 2,4-nonadienal (P < 0.05). A significant decrease in freeze-thawed breast has been observed in (E)-2-undecenal, tridecanal, tetradecanal, and pentadecanal (P < 0.05). Previous research has also observed a higher number of aldehydes compared to other chemical groups in meat, as meat contains fatty acids that undergo the lipolysis process. Their double bonds are highly vulnerable and easily undergo oxidation (Beldarrain et al., 2022; Qi et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2018). This oxidation further promotes the formation of aldehydes in meat. Many of the major aldehyde compounds are generated through the auto-oxidation of different unsaturated fatty acids, such as linoleic and linolenic acids, which are converted into hexanal during oxidation (Al-Dalali et al., 2022b). Variations in the degree of lipid oxidation result in corresponding changes in the concentrations of these aldehydes.

The lipid and protein oxidation pathways are interconnected and can interact with Maillard reaction pathways, significantly influencing the formation of volatile compounds. For example, benzaldehyde, a known Maillard reaction product, was found to double in freeze-thawed meat, coinciding with increased lipid and protein oxidation. The alcohols identified in this study included 1-pentanol, 1-octen-3-ol, 1-heptanol, 2-ethyl-1-hexanol, 1-octanol, 2-octen-1-ol, 1-tetradecanol, and 1-dodecanol, which are primarily derived from lipid oxidation as secondary products of aldehyde reduction. All detected alcohols either remained stable or showed no significant decrease after freeze-thawing. All alcohol abundance significantly increased in freeze-thawed breast meat except (Z)-2-octen-1-ol and 1-tetradecanol (P < 0.05). There was a marked increase in the concentration of 1-octen-3-ol, from 150 to 234 µg/kg, which imparts a mushroom-like odour, derived from the autooxidation of linoleic acid. Shin et al. (2024) observed an almost 10 times higher abundance of 1-octen-3-ol after 2 months of freezing. A recent study investigating volatile compounds as indicators of quality deterioration in frozen white and red meat reported that 2-ethyl-1-hexanol was positively correlated with frozen storage duration, showing increased concentrations over time (Yu et al., 2025). Many previous studies have focused on the identification of volatile compounds in frozen meat species such as beef, pork, duck, and chicken. However, the present study specifically investigates native chicken, as it differs from commercial breeds, and also aims to develop a method for discriminating between fresh and frozen–thawed samples.

In this study, 1-dodecanol was not detected in fresh chicken meat, indicating its potential use as a discriminative marker for distinguishing fresh samples from frozen–thawed counterparts. As a long-chain alcohol, 1-dodecanol is primarily formed as a secondary oxidation product of long-chain fatty acids. Limited lipid oxidation in fresh meat results in minimal formation of this compound, rendering it undetectable under the current analytical conditions. Culleré et al. (2013) suggested that 1-octen-3-ol could be a possible marker for the differentiation between fresh and frozen-thawed fish, and its increased concentration was a consequence of the freezing process. 1-octanol, which can be produced by fat oxidation, was found in higher amounts in freeze-thawed samples, and a similar result for this compound was observed by Sun et al. (2021) in beef meatballs. Sun et al. (2021) also identified hexanal and 1-octen-3-ol with a greater odour-active value and key aroma compounds in cooked beef meatballs during frozen storage.

Two ketone compounds have been identified, namely 3,5-octadien-2-one and 5-pentyl-2(5H)-furanone. A significant increase of 3,5-octadien-2-one was observed after freezing, which is produced by the oxidation of linoleic and linolenic acid. This is a potential indicator of lipid oxidation and was identified as a discriminator between fresh and fermented fish (Wang et al., 2022). Moreover, freezing can cause protein denaturation, which is responsible for the generation of carbonyl compounds (Lu et al., 2022). No significant changes were observed in the abundance of hydrocarbons between the two groups. Yu et al. (2025) identified a correlation between frozen storage and heptadecane in red meat but a negative correlation in white meat during long-term frozen storage. This is in accordance with our results, as long-term frozen storage was not performed, and no significant changes were observed in either of these two groups. Conversely, some volatiles, such as 2-pentylfuran, which contribute to the characteristic chicken-like odor, showed a slight decrease after freeze-thawing. This reduction can be attributed to further degradation of the compound during frozen storage, interactions with other meat components, or partial loss of volatiles during thawing (Barido et al., 2022).

A mixed trend was found for the volatile acids. There was a significant decrease in the content of nonanoic acid after freeze-thaw (P < 0.05), and a non-significant increase was observed for hexanoic and heptanoic acid. Overall, a different volatile composition has been observed between the two groups, which would further be applicable to distinguish between fresh and frozen-thawed chicken. Collectively, all of these changes highlight the significant impact of freeze-thaw cycles on the volatile profile of chicken meat, reflecting underlying biochemical processes including lipid oxidation and protein degradation.

PCA and PLS-DA were performed as part of the multivariate analysis using the relative concentrations of volatile compounds. PCA was employed as an unsupervised method to reduce data dimensionality and visualize groupings among samples based on their volatile composition (Pavlidis et al., 2019). PLS-DA, a supervised technique capable of both prediction and feature selection, was subsequently applied to enhance classification accuracy. Both PCA and PLS-DA score plots demonstrated a clear separation between the two groups. In PCA, the first principal component (PC1) explained 39.6 %, while the second component (PC2) accounted for 15.8 % of the total variance, with a cumulative contribution of 55.4 %. Similarly, in PLS-DA, the first two principal components explained 55 % of the variance, effectively clustering the samples into distinct groups.

To assess the reliability of the PLS-DA model, a validation process was conducted using 1,000 random permutations to evaluate group prediction accuracy. The permutation test yielded a p-value of 0.001, indicating statistically significant results. Additionally, cross-validation was performed to assess the model's reproducibility and predictive capability. The R² (model fitness) and Q² (predictive accuracy) values were 0.93 and 0.85, respectively, demonstrating a robust classification model (Zhang et al., 2020; Amalia et al., 2022).

To identify the key volatile compounds responsible for differentiating fresh and frozen-thawed meat, the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score was calculated, as shown in Fig. 2. The VIP score quantifies the contribution of each volatile compound to the classification in the PLS-DA model (Sujiwo et al., 2024). Volatiles with VIP scores above 1.0 are generally considered significant in distinguishing sample groups, with higher VIP values indicating a greater influence on differentiation (Tu et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2022). A total of 15 volatile compounds were identified, with 12 compounds exhibiting VIP values above 1.2. The key differentiating volatiles included 2-heptenal, 4-pentyl benzaldehyde, (E)-2-octenal, 1-dodecanol, 2-ethyl-1-hexanol, 1-tetradecanol, tridecanal, nonanoic acid, octanal, 1-heptanol, hexanal, and tetradecanal. Among these, 2-heptenal exhibited the highest VIP score (1.8), indicating its strong influence in distinguishing fresh and frozen-thawed samples.Fig. 2Top 15 compounds based on the VIP value.Fig 2 dummy alt text

Most of the identified compounds are products of lipid oxidation, consistent with the elevated TBARS values observed in frozen-thawed meat. The color scale in Fig. 2, ranging from blue to red, represents the relative frequency of important volatiles in each group. In the freeze-thawed group, the occurrence of 2-heptenal, (E)-2-octenal, 1-dodecanol, 2-ethyl-1-hexanol, octanal, 1-heptanol, and hexanal was significantly higher, indicating their potential as markers for frozen-thawed meat. Yu et al. (2025) identified 2-ethyl-1-hexanol and octanal as indicators of quality deterioration in frozen white meat, aligning with our findings. Additionally, an increased concentration of these compounds was observed with prolonged frozen storage.

Among the detected volatiles, hexanal was identified as a key indicator of frozen-thawed samples, consistent with previous research (Al-Dalali et al., 2022a; Yu et al., 2025). Furthermore, Yu et al. (2025) reported a strong correlation between frozen storage and elevated levels of 2-ethyl-1-hexanol, octanal, and tridecanal, which corresponds to our observations in frozen-thawed meat. For fresh meat, 4-pentyl benzaldehyde, 1-tetradecanol, tridecanal, nonanoic acid, and tetradecanal were identified as important based on their VIP values and color-coded frequency distributions.

Conclusions

This study highlights the potential of using SPME extraction for volatile compound analysis, combined with multivariate analysis, as an effective approach for distinguishing between fresh and frozen-thawed KNC meat. Both PCA and PLS-DA successfully classified the two groups based on their volatile profiles. The key volatile compounds responsible for differentiation were primarily aldehydes and alcohols, which are derived from lipid oxidation and degradation. Freeze-thaw cycles had a significant impact on colour, lipid oxidation, and the subsequent volatile profile of chicken breast meat. Notably, frozen-thawed samples exhibited increased concentrations of 2-heptenal, (E)-2-octenal, 1-dodecanol, and 2-ethyl-1-hexanol, indicating their potential as key markers for distinguishing between fresh and frozen-thawed meat. Volatile compound profiling could therefore be used as a tool for assessing meat freshness and detecting fraudulent labeling of frozen poultry meat. However, the volatile compound can be affected by so many external factors which sometime make the volatilomics technique complext to get consistent results. Therefore, future research should include diverse breeds, storage conditions, and independent validation sets. Integrating sensory analysis and quantitative profiling of key markers could strengthen the practical application of volatile profiling for detecting frozen-thawed poultry meat.
