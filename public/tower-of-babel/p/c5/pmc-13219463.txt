Effects of ground motion spectral characteristics on RC frame fragility considering liquefaction
Authors: Yang Fei, Long-wei Chen, Sheng-cong Gan, Lang-zhou Tang, Zhen Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Seismic liquefaction represents a persistent and complex engineering challenge in soil dynamics. Post-earthquake damage investigations have shown that damage to buildings caused by site liquefaction constitutes a significant proportion of all seismic damage. The spectral characteristics of ground motions exert a predominant effect on structural damage, and an important issue that needs to be addressed is accurately describing how these spectral characteristics influence the structural fragility. A simplified six-story reinforced concrete (RC) frame model was established. The ratio of peak ground displacement to peak ground acceleration (referred to as Rd2a) was adopted as an indicator of spectral characteristics of ground motions. The collected ground motions were classified into five groups based on their respective Rd2a values. Using the in-built Incremental Dynamic Analysis (IDA) kit on the OpenSees platform, fragility curves of the simplified RC building were calculated under different Rd2a values to further assess the effect of the ratio of the peak ground displacement to peak ground acceleration on structural fragility. Additionally, 22 real ground motion records from liquefied sites (hereafter referred to as liquefied ground motions) and a same number of records from non-liquefied sites were also analyzed. The results underscore the distinct vulnerability patterns of structures on liquefied ground, revealing that the unique spectral shifts inherent in liquefied motions significantly intensify structural fragility compared to non-liquefied conditions.

Introduction

Seismic liquefaction of sandy soils has long been a frontier topic in geo-dynamics1–3. In recent earthquakes, the damage caused by liquefaction remains significant, with a trend of becoming even more severe. Scholars have referred to seismic liquefaction as a ‘tumor’ in earthquake disaster prevention and mitigation efforts4. With the effective implementation of new theories and methods such as resilient city earthquake resistance concepts and disaster prevention and mitigation strategies, the number of casualties triggered by building collapses in strong earthquakes has decreased. However, a considerable proportion of infrastructure functional losses are still induced by site foundation and substructure damage. Therefore, seismic risk assessments of site failure remain a priority in both scientific research and engineering design, with seismic liquefaction being one of the most typical forms of site failure.

Previous research into liquefaction mainly focused on understanding the liquefaction mechanisms, identifying liquefaction susceptibility in engineering sites, and developing mitigation and prevention strategies. In contrast, there has been relatively less exploration of the impact of site liquefaction on the dynamic response of structures from a vibration perspective, largely due to the scarcity of field records at liquefied sites, which limits the feasibility of dynamic time-history analysis for structures. Current theoretical calculations, earthquake damage investigations, and analyses of recorded seismic data show that site liquefaction significantly diminishes the high-frequency components of horizontal ground motions while amplifying the low-frequency components. Liquefaction extends the characteristic period of the horizontal acceleration response spectrum by at least 0.1 s, with an average extension of 0.3 s. The period of 0.8 s marks the boundary between seismic reduction and amplification effects due to liquefaction; below this value, liquefaction affords some level of seismic reduction, while above it, it amplifies the seismic response.

Engineers responsible for investigations into historical earthquake damage have identified three primary ways in which site liquefaction affects structures: (1) The softening of site soils during liquefaction influences ground motions, with liquefied soil layers amplifying the low-frequency components and filtering the high-frequency components of seismic waves, which results in ground motions with larger displacement amplitudes; (2) The loss of bearing capacity in the foundation due to liquefaction; and (3) Permanent horizontal and vertical ground deformations (displacements) following liquefaction, which directly trigger horizontal displacement and vertical settlement of building foundations. Recent investigations into post-earthquake liquefaction damage have found that low-rise to mid-rise buildings with shallow foundations are most affected by site liquefaction5,6. High-rise structures are less frequently damaged, and liquefaction affects them mainly through the long-period components of ground motions, causing damage to both structural and non-structural components.

Recent research on liquefaction and structure interaction has yielded significant results. Current studies are increasingly focused on how to account for soil-structure interaction in design to enhance the stability and seismic resistance of buildings in liquefied conditions. Bird et al. investigated ways in which liquefaction-induced damage to buildings was incorporated into the estimation of seismic loss. By combining a risk model for soil liquefaction with seismic fragility, they proposed a comprehensive seismic loss assessment method to quantify the effects of liquefaction on buildings. Their study used probabilistic analysis and empirical models to simulate the extent of damage caused by liquefaction-induced ground deformation on various building types7. Bray and Dashti studied the interaction between foundation soils and building structures during liquefaction, with a particular focus on issues such as building settlement, tilt, and lateral displacement due to liquefaction. Their research explored the effects of various factors, including seismic intensity, thickness of liquefied soil layers, and building foundation types, on the motion of buildings to reveal the governing mechanisms of building motions under liquefied conditions8.

Tabatabaiefar and Clifton examined the effects of soil-structure interaction on the seismic design of unsupported building frames on soft soil foundations9. Through numerical simulations, they analyzed the lateral displacement and internal force distribution characteristics of unsupported frame structures on soft soil foundations. Qi et al.studied the effects of different foundation types on the seismic response of low-rise buildings within liquefied soil layers, studying the interaction mechanism between building foundations and liquefied soil under seismic loads10. By comparing various foundation systems such as shallow and pile foundations, the study evaluated their effectiveness in mitigating structural damage caused by liquefaction. Karafagka et al.11 utilized the stochastic finite element method and Monte Carlo simulations to construct fragility curves for non-ductile RC frame structures, considering liquefaction and soil-structure interaction. Alzabeebee and Forcellini investigated the seismic response of reinforced concrete (RC) structures on liquefied soil foundations, developing a numerical model that accounted for the complex dynamic behavior of liquefied soil and its interaction with the superstructure. They systematically explored the effects of liquefaction-induced seismic events on the overall stability and dynamic response of RC structures12.

Seismic fragility is a representation of the probability that a building will reach, or exceed, a given state of damage, reflecting the extent of damage to which a building is subject after a seismic event13–15. Currently, seismic fragility analysis is a well-established and practical technique with various methods available; however, existing research on fragility, both domestically and internationally, mainly focuses on the diversification of structural models and the refinement of components, with less emphasis on the influences of ground motion characteristics and site conditions. Spectral components are one of the three key elements of ground motion characteristics, and the structural vibration response differs significantly depending on the spectral components of the input ground motion. Therefore, considering the effects of spectral characteristics of ground motions on structural damage (or fragility) analysis can more accurately reveal the state of the damage to a structure. To address these spectral uncertainties more rigorously, recent studies have emphasized the implementation of the conditional mean spectrum (CMS) and conditional spectrum (CS) within seismic safety evaluations to maintain spectral consistency between input motions and site-specific hazard levels16.

From a physical mechanism perspective, the filtering and amplitude amplification (or reduction) effects of site soil layers on seismic wave propagation affect structural responses and the distribution of seismic damage. Thus, the impact of site soil layers on structural fragility can be indirectly reflected through the spectral characteristics of ground motions.

According to existing studies, mainstream methods for seismic fragility analysis can be classified based on the source of structural damage data into empirical statistical methods17 and numerical simulation methods18. Empirical statistical methods rely primarily on historical investigation data and records of earthquake damage, using statistical analysis to determine the probability of damage to different types of structures under varying intensities of ground motions. Numerical simulation methods (also referred to as theoretical analysis methods) use structural dynamic analysis tools to perform a series of non-linear dynamic analyses on structures, determining structural response and performance. On the basis of these simulation results, fragility curves are generated for different types of structures under varying intensities of ground motions. Since most regions, both domestically and internationally, lack sufficient earthquake observation data and structural damage data for statistical analysis, numerical simulation methods remain the dominant approach for structural fragility analysis. While recent progress in ground motion selection—such as the development of genetic algorithms (GA) capable of matching target distributions of generalized intensity measures —has significantly improved simulation precision, these advanced techniques are rarely employed to characterize the unique spectral shifts and energy redistribution inherent in liquefied ground. This notable gap suggests that the interplay between liquefaction-induced site effects and structural vulnerability is still not fully elucidated within modern selection frameworks19.

Researchers worldwide have achieved substantial progress in the field of seismic fragility analysis. Whiteman et al.20 statistically analyzed the post-earthquake damage conditions of 1,600 buildings, using a damage probability matrix to illustrate the seismic fragility of different structures. This marked the first time seismic damage data in civil engineering structures were statistically analyzed, and empirical seismic fragility curves were derived through regression analysis. Yasser and Mostafa21 adopted finite element software to study the dynamic responses of four- and eight-story RC frame structures, producing corresponding fragility curves. Tushar and Siddhartha22 proposed a new regression method to replace the traditional lognormal regression analysis, aiming to evaluate the fragility of structures at multiple states of damage while reducing the statistical workload.

Recent studies have highlighted the growing attention among domestic and international scholars to the influence of site soil conditions on structural fragility. Forcellini23 investigated the impact of liquefaction on the seismic performance of masonry-infilled RC structures, using numerical calculations to reveal the effects of liquefaction-induced lateral displacement and settlement on fragility curves, however, the calculations are of limited utility. Pitilakis and Petridis24 studied the effects of soil-structure interaction and site amplification on the fragility of existing RC frame structures, proposing the use of fragility modification factors to adjust seismic fragility curves. Site liquefaction, as a typical form of earthquake-induced site failure, can cause foundation failure and large surface displacements (deformations), significantly impacting structures built on such foundations25. Incorporating site liquefaction into structural damage prediction or fragility analysis remains a challenging research topic both domestically and internationally.

This study estimates the effects of spectral characteristics of ground motions on the seismic fragility of structures based on a simplified RC frame model. A set of 22 established liquefied site records from both domestic and international sources were collected and organized. The Incremental Dynamic Analysis (IDA) method was employed, and the ground motion time histories, including liquefied site records, were input to obtain fragility curves of the structure under different states of damage. The study analyzes the influence of spectral characteristics of input ground motions on structural fragility and compares the fragility of structures in liquefied and non-liquefied sites. The findings are expected to provide a reference for selecting ground motion inputs and predicting structural damage in fragility analysis. Modeling

Studying the influences of spectral characteristics of ground motions and liquefied ground motions on the dynamic response of structures involves various working conditions, making numerical simulation the preferred approach. A suitable simplified model enables more economical and efficient computations. Through simplified calculations and statistical analyses, the effects of different types of ground motion on structural seismic responses can be understood. OpenSees, an open-source finite element analysis software, is widely used in seismic engineering design for numerical simulations due to its advantages, such as open-source code, support for various material constitutive and element models, ease of secondary development, and compatibility with other software and platforms26.

This study is based on the OpenSees platform. The layout of the RC frame structure consists of two spans, each 7 m long, with a total length of 14 m. The structure is six-story high, with each story measuring 3.6 m in height, giving a total height of 21.6 m. The elevations of the model, as well as the beam and column cross-sections, are shown in Fig. 2. The dimensions of the beams and columns are 600 mm × 500 mm and 500 mm × 400 mm, respectively. In this model, the Concrete01 uniaxial compressive constitutive model from the Kent-Scott-Park model is selected to represent the concrete material27, and the hardening material model, which accounts for both isotropic hardening and linear kinematic behavior, is selected to represent the reinforcement material28. Input ground motions

Selection of non-liquefied ground motions

Selection of seismic inputs plays a crucial role in structural fragility analysis. With the development of strong-motion observation networks worldwide, earthquake records are no longer scarce. Japan’s K-net and KiK-net seismic observation networks record high-quality strong-motion data and make these data available for research, providing fundamental data for research into earthquake engineering. In this study, 1594 ground motion acceleration time histories were collected from the KiK-net seismic observation database managed by the National Research Institute for Earth Science and Disaster Resilience in Japan (https://www.kyoshin.bosai.go.jp/), with a peak ground acceleration (PGA) greater than 50 gal. The acceleration time histories underwent standard processing methods, including baseline correction and band-pass filtering between 0.1 and 25 Hz.

Figure 4 demonstrates the distribution of PGA in the processed acceleration data. As shown in Fig. 4, the PGA of the selected ground motion data ranges from 0 to 0.8 g (1 g = 10 m/s2), with the majority of data points below 0.2 g, accounting for 83.5% of the dataset.Fig. 4PGA-value distribution of the dataset.

Characteristic parameters of ground motion spectra

In engineering, PGA and peak ground displacement (PGD) are commonly taken as quantitative indicators of the intensity of ground motions due to their straightforward, convenient application31. However, the peak ratio, defined as Rd2a = PGD/PGA, can represent not only the intensity of ground motions but also, to some extent, the spectral characteristics of ground motions. First, the Rd2a values of the selected ground motion data were calculated, and Fig. 4 shows the number of ground motion records within each peak ratio range. The Rd2a values were found to range from 0 to 0.19, with the majority concentrated below 0.04, accounting for 98.6% of the data. It should be noted that in this calculation, the units of PGA and PGD are m/s2 and m, respectively, so the unit of the peak ratio is “s2” rather than being dimensionless.

To validate the correlation between Rd2a and the spectral characteristics of ground motions, the low-frequency component richness index RL proposed by Miyajima is utilized32. Currently, there is no unified standard regarding the number of ground motions required for fragility analysis in relevant national and international codes. China’s ‘Code for Seismic Design of Buildings’ (GB50011-2010)33 and Japan’s ‘Building Standard Law Enforcement Order’34 require a minimum of three ground motion records for analysis and verification. The U.S. standards FEMA 35635 and ATC-5836 also recommend selecting at least seven or eleven ground motion records to estimate the exceedance probability under scaled ground motions with various intensity metrics. Considering accuracy, computational efficiency, and sample size, the selected number of acceleration time histories as input fully meets the requirements for fragility analysis.

Selection of liquefied ground motions

For time-history numerical calculations, 11 sets of liquefied ground motion records were collected, with each set containing two horizontal acceleration time histories. The data were primarily derived from the 2011 Christchurch earthquake in New Zealand, the 1995 Kobe earthquake in Japan, and the 1987 Superstition Hills earthquake in the United States37. To address the limitation in data quantity, each horizontal time history was processed independently, resulting in a total of 22 acceleration time histories. Gravity and dynamic loading

The finite element analysis described herein primarily includes a gravity loading step and a dynamic excitation step. By implementing the gravity loading step, it is possible to ensure that the model is correctly constructed and to verify the stability of the model under its own weight. Building on this, the subsequent dynamic excitation step introduces ground motion inputs to simulate the response of the model to actual seismic conditions. This approach ensures the correct generation of the model and guarantees that the gravitational loads are continuously applied to the model.

Based on the above analysis, under the same intensity (PGA value) of seismic input, the level of structural damage at liquefied sites is significantly higher than at non-liquefied sites, primarily due to differences in the spectral components of the input ground motions. Compared to non-liquefied ground motions, liquefied ground motions are characterized by richer low-frequency content and higher amplitudes. The correlation between spectral characteristics of ground motions and structural damage is key to understanding structural damage and predicting seismic damage. Moreover, whether the spectral characteristics of ground motions at liquefied sites are unique compared to typical non-liquefied sites is also worth exploring.

Fragility curves

Method of fragility calculation

IDA is a commonly used method in the numerical simulation of structural dynamics, which includes the stages of model establishment, numerical calculations, and fragility analysis. This method involves inputting into the structural model a series of ground motions scaled according to seismic intensity indices, followed by static-dynamic time-history analysis through use of numerical analysis software to obtain the structural response under each seismic action. Based on the structural response outcomes and predefined damage standards, the level of structural damage is assessed, thus determining the probability of damage under various seismic intensities and generating the requisite seismic fragility curves.

In terms of structural fragility analysis, the method incorporates an analytical fragility function fitting approach based on dynamic structural analysis proposed by Baker40, along with a specifically developed structural fragility solver. This method uses results from finite element numerical dynamic analysis to fit the statistical characteristics of structural response to fragility utilizing a log-normal distribution function, thereby deriving a log-normally distributed fragility function which is subsequently plotted as a fragility curve. This method is known for its accuracy, robustness, flexibility, and convenience. The detailed steps therein include:

Scaling the ground motion time histories according to seismic intensity indices, inputting these scaled motions into the structure, and conducting non-linear time-history analysis to compute the maximum inter-story drift ratios corresponding to the input intensity indices;

Determining the state of structural damage according to the levels and quantitative indices specified in the ‘Code for Seismic Design of Buildings’ (GB 50011-2010), assessing the state of damage under various seismic intensities, and recording the frequency of earthquake motions causing different levels of damage;

Using the structural fragility solver developed by Baker and others, performing a log-normal regression on the ratio of the frequency of damage occurrences to the total frequency to obtain fragility curves for different levels of structural damage.

Seismic fragility analysis considering liquefaction

Liquefaction, as a typical form of site seismic damage, has historically caused significant damage to structures. From the physical nature of the way in which liquefaction affects ground motions, liquefaction notably amplifies the low-frequency components and reduces the high-frequency components of ground motions, resulting in large displacements at the ground surface. For simplification, this analysis does not consider the impact of ground displacement (deformation) on structural damage, focusing solely on the influence of ground motions on structures. As shown in Figs. 12 and 13, compared to other groups, ground motion inputs from Group V result in the highest probability of structural damage exceedance; however, when compared to liquefied ground motion inputs, the exceedance probability of structural damage under Group V is lower, especially at higher intensities of ground motions. Furthermore, the differences between the two increase with the rising level of structural damage. As shown in Fig. 13a, b, when structures are in minor or moderate states of damage, the difference between the fragility curves under ground motion from Group V and liquefied ground motion inputs is small. When structures are in a severe state of damage (Fig. 13c), if the intensity of ground motions is less than 0.1 m/s2, the fragility curve under ground motion inputs from Group V is slightly higher than that under liquefaction; if the intensity of ground motions exceeds 0.1 m/s2, the fragility curve under ground motion inputs from Group V is slightly lower than that under liquefaction. A similar situation occurs when structures are in a state of collapse, except that the crossover point corresponding to the intensity of input ground motions increases, i.e., when the intensity of ground motions exceeds 0.3 m/s2, the fragility curve under liquefaction is higher than that under ground motion inputs from Group V, and when less than 0.3 m/s2, the situation is reversed. This implies that at lower intensities of ground motions, for the structural model used in the present study, site liquefaction tends to reduce the probability of non-severe damage and collapse, acting to “protect” the structure. Ground motions with pronounced low‑frequency components (Group 5) typically exhibit reduced spectral accelerations around the short‑to‑medium period range. At low‑to‑moderate intensity levels, the seismic demand imposed by such records is therefore lower than that of the liquefaction‑affected records, which retain a broader frequency content. As a result, the exceedance probabilities of higher damage states are temporarily reduced. This phenomenon is particularly evident for structures with relatively short fundamental periods, and it diminishes as the damage state becomes more severe or the intensity level increases. The crossing point of the fragility curves shifts toward higher PGA for more severe damage states, indicating that the protective effect is gradually overcome by the cumulative inelastic deformation demands. However, it is primarily true for buildings with shorter natural periods, as noted during the 1975 Haicheng earthquake41, where villagers reported observations such as “wet shaking is not as severe as dry shaking”, “places where water and sand ejected caused fewer house collapses”, and “thankfully the earthquake shook water and sand to surface, or else the houses wouldn’t have been spared”.

Ground motion inputs have always been a primary concern in structural seismic analysis. Selecting reasonable and appropriate seismic inputs can lead to accurate assessment of the seismic performance of a structure. Although the analysis presented in this paper is based on a simplified model, it reveals that site liquefaction significantly influences structural fragility. The seismic fragility analysis was conducted on a simplified six‑story RC frame model; while this model captures the essential dynamic characteristics of low‑to‑mid‑rise RC buildings, Future research should therefore extend the proposed methodology to a wider range of structural systems and site conditions.

Furthermore, the spectral characteristics of ground motions are also critical factors in the analysis of seismic fragility; the higher the Rd2a value, the more severe the structural damage under the same intensity of ground motions. The results provide quantifiable indicators for assessing the effects of ground motions on structural fragility and can serve as references for selecting seismic inputs in structural fragility analysis.
