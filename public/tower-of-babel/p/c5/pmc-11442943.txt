Integrated analysis to identify biological features and molecular markers of poorly cohesive gastric carcinoma (PCC)
Authors: Yuan-jie Liu, Qian-wen Ye, Jie-pin Li, Le Bai, Wei Zhang, Shuang-shuang Wang, Xi Zou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs one of the two main histologic subtypes of gastric cancer (GC), diffuse-type gastric cancer (DGC) containing poorly cohesive gastric carcinoma (PCC) components has a worse prognosis and does not respond well to typical therapies. Despite the large number of studies revealing the complex pathogenic network of DGC, the molecular heterogeneity of DGC is still not fully understood. We obtained single-cell RNA-seq data and bulk data from the tumor immune single cell hub, the public gene expression omnibus, and the cancer genome atlas databases. A series of bioinformatics analyses were performed using R software. Immunofluorescence staining, hematoxylin and eosin staining, western blot, and functional experiments were used for experimental validation. Caudin-3, -4 and -7 were lowly expressed in DGC and their expression levels were further reduced in PCC. The PCC components were mainly located in the deeper layers of the DGC and had a high level of hypoxic Wnt/β-catenin signaling and stemness. We further identified Insulin Like Growth Factor Binding Protein 7 (IGFBP7) as a marker for PCC components in the deep layer. IGFBP7 is stimulated by hypoxia and promotes cancer cell invasiveness and reduced claudin expression. In addition, programmed death-1 ligand (PD-L1) was specifically expressed in the deep layer, reflecting deep layer-specific immunosuppression. The PCC components are predominantly situated in the deeper layers of DGC. Initial molecular characterization of these PCC components revealed distinct features, including low expression of claudin-3, -4, and -7, high expression of IGFBP7, and the presence of PD-L1. These molecular traits may partially account for the pronounced tumor heterogeneity observed in GC.Keywords: Poorly cohesive gastric carcinoma, Spatial heterogeneity, Tight junctions, Hypoxia, Cancer stem cellsSubject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Globally, gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the fifth most prevalent malignancy and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality1. The Lauren classification divides GC into two main subtypes: intestinal and diffuse2. With the increasing emphasis on H. pylori treatment and healthy dietary habits, the overall incidence of intestinal-type gastric cancer (IGC) has decreased worldwide3. However, the incidence of diffuse-type gastric cancer (DGC), especially cases containing poorly cohesive gastric carcinoma (PCC) components, has been steadily increasing4. According to the fifth edition of the WHO definition, PCC is defined as a tumor composed of isolated or small groups of tumor cells, including signet-ring cell carcinoma (SRCC)5. Although the rationale for including SRCC as a subtype of PCC is controversial, it is well established that PCC is inherently heterogeneous6. PCC is characterized by isolated tumor cells, diffuse distribution, lack of adhesion, strong invasiveness, and relatively poor survival7. The scarcity and inconsistency of studies on PCC make diagnosis and treatment difficult, highlighting the need for an in-depth understanding of the essential features of PCC.

Tight junctions (TJs) play a crucial role in maintaining the structural integrity of epithelial cell polarization8. The TJ structure consists of different junctional molecules, such as occludin and claudin9. The two proteins are critical transmembrane proteins involved in paracellular barrier function10,11. Any imbalance in the expression or localization of claudin or occludin can lead to dysfunctions in the normal physiology of epithelial cells, potentially resulting in various disorders, especially malignancies12. Mechanistically, disruption of TJs leads to the loss of epithelial cell polarity and a transition to mesenchymal phenotype13. Clinical evidence suggests that claudin-3, -4, and -7 are expressed at low levels in DGC and may work synergistically with E-cadherin to contribute to the phenotype of loose cohesion in DGC14–16. However, given the high molecular and histologic heterogeneity of DGC, the intricate mechanisms underlying TJs require further exploration.

Hypoxia is present in almost all solid tumors and is considered an important tumor hallmark17. On one hand, the level of hypoxia within a tumor is heterogeneous and fluctuates dramatically across different tumor regions18. On the other hand, the response of tumor cell populations to hypoxia may vary considerably due to their inherent heterogeneity19. Tumor stromal remodeling, non-functional abnormal vascular structures, and the high metabolic demands of tumor cells are important factors contributing to hypoxia20,21. It has been extensively shown that hypoxia induces epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby conferring greater invasiveness to tumor cells22. EMT is a key process in tumor progression and is positively correlated with the expansion of cancer stem cells (CSCs, a subpopulation of cancer cells with the capacity for self-renewal and differentiation)23.

The microenvironment in which CSCs reside (referred to as the “CSC niche”) is critical for maintaining their phenotype and function. CSCs have been found to preferentially survive in hypoxic regions of the tumor24. The hypoxic microenvironment increases the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), which further maintains and promotes the malignant phenotype of CSCs by regulating related genes and various signaling pathways25–27. Hypoxia is known to induce EMT in various tumor cell populations28,29. However, it is important to note that not all tumor cells respond to hypoxia in the same manner. According to Salnikov et al., hypoxia-induced EMT signaling can occur in multiple tumor cell populations, but only the stem-like cells acquire high migratory potential and thus may be responsible for invasion and metastasis30. This indicates that a hypoxic environment predominantly increases the migratory capacity of cells with elevated stem cell characteristics, rather than inducing EMT uniformly across all tumor cell types. Understanding these nuances is critical for developing targeted therapies for cancer.

In the current study, we utilized publicly available bulk and single-cell RNA-seq data. The spatial heterogeneity of DGC was explored using multiple bioinformatics approaches, revealing that PCC components are predominantly located in the deeper layers of the DGC. This may be closely associated with the inherent hypoxia present deep within the tumor. Further studies showed that the PCC components deep in the DGC possessed CSC features with highly activated Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Immunofluorescence (IF) and hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining provided clear evidence that the PCC component exhibited decreased expression of claudin-4 and was associated with other cancer-related signaling crosstalk. The integration of transcriptome analysis and experimental validation provides conclusive evidence for the oncogenic role of IGFBP7, which is associated with CSCs and immunosuppression. This study may provide insights into the heterogeneity of DGC.

Public data sources involved in this study

For bulk data, RNA-seq data (containing relevant clinical data) were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) for 375 GC samples and 32 matched normal control samples. Data were extracted and cleaned with R (version 4.3.1) and exported as a canonical expression matrix.

For single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data, GSE18390431, containing 14 IGC samples and 6 DGC samples, and GSE16729732, containing 5 deep-layer DGC samples, 5 superficial-layer DGC samples, and 5 normal control samples, were downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and Tumor immune single cell hub (TISCH) (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/), respectively.

Results

CLDN3/4/7 possess a similar expression pattern and are expressed at a lower level in DGC

First, to explore the heterogeneity within tight junction proteins, we clustered 24 related genes (23 claudin–encoding genes and 1 occludin-encoding gene) by analyzing the Spearman correlation between these genes. The results showed that these 24 genes could be visually classified into two clusters with distinct expression patterns. Specifically, CLDN3, CLDN4, CLDN7, CLDN12, and OCLN exhibited similar expression patterns (Fig. 1A). Previously, it was reported that in a similar manner as E-cadherin, decreased expression of claudin-4 was associated with disruption of glandular structure and a state of dedifferentiation33. Considering that TJs provide adhesion necessary to maintain cell–cell interactions34, this implies that claudin-4 may play a role in the diffuse phenotype of GC cells and loose binding. Hence, we further analyzed the differences in the expression of CLDN3, CLDN4, CLDN7, CLDN12, and OCLN genes between different histological types of GC cases in the TCGA-STAD cohort. The analysis revealed that these genes exhibited low expression in DGC compared to IGC (Fig. 1B, n = 226: 163 IGC cases and 63 DGC cases, P < 0.05). Cox regression analysis identified CLDN1 (Progression-Free Interval, PFI), CLDN5 (PFI), CLDN6 (Overall Survival, OS; PFI; and Disease-Specific Survival, DSS), CLDN9 (OS, DSS, and PFI), CLDN10 (DSS and PFI), and CLDN11 (OS, DSS, and PFI) as significant unfavorable factors for GC (Fig. 1C–E). To better understand the expression pattern of TJs in the TME, we utilized a GC single-cell RNA sequence dataset (GSE183904), which includes 6 DGC samples and 14 IGC samples. The single-cell RNA (scRNA) data processing procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1F and Figure S3, with a UMAP and a violin plot displaying the cell markers of each cell lineage. The identified cell types, based on the expression of known marker genes and “SingleR” results, varied depending on the tumor histological type (Fig. 1G). The relative abundance of individual cell types based on histological type categorized the cell types into DGC-enriched (B cells and endothelial cells) and DGC-depleted (T cells, epithelial cells, and fibroblasts). We performed clustering analysis on all epithelial cells, revealing four significant cell subpopulations (Fig. 1H). Notably, at the single-cell level, DGC-derived epithelial cells exhibited significantly low expression of CLDN3, CLDN4, and CLDN7 (Fig. 1I,J), further confirming our previous assumptions and existing knowledge. To decipher the molecular characteristics distinguishing IGC and DGC epithelium, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). The DGC epithelium showed principal enrichment in hypoxia and response to metal ions. In contrast, compared with IGC epithelium, multiple immune activity-related pathways such as inflammatory response, innate immune response, cytokine activity, regulation of lymphocyte activation, and lymphocyte-mediated immunity were down-regulated in DGC epithelium (Fig. 1K,L).

The above results suggest that DGC may be in an immunosuppressive state. A recent observational study on the gastric signet-ring cell carcinoma (GSRCC) microenvironment indicated that the GSRCC TME appears to be quiescent, with severely impaired T- and B-cell reactivity and mobilization difficulties35. Considering that DGC is histologically poorly differentiated, we analyzed CLDN3, CLDN4, CLDN7, CLDN12, and OCLN in conjunction with clinicopathological features. The expression of these genes was significantly lower in poorly-differentiated cases than in well-differentiated samples, as determined by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Fig. 1M). Furthermore, a trend was observed between the low expression of CLDN3, CLDN4, CLDN7, CLDN12, and OCLN and the T stage, although this association was not statistically significant (Fig. 1N). The differential expression pattern of CLDN4 was confirmed by IF staining on resection specimens of different histological types (Fig. 1O). More importantly, we observed an interesting regional expression pattern of CLDN4, with the lowest expression levels when tumor cells exhibited poorly cohesive features (defined as consisting of isolated individual tumor cells or small clusters of tumor cells, as determined by HE staining) (Fig. 1O). These data further imply an important association between decreased expression of CLDN4 and the disruption of intercellular adhesion in GC.

Deep layer-tumor tissue exhibits PCC features, further CLDN4 loss and stronger stemness

To better understand the histologic features of DGC, we performed HE staining on 30 cases of DGC diagnosed pathologically as containing PCC components. As shown in Fig. 2A, we found that the PCC components are mainly distributed in the deeper layers of the DGC, particularly in areas under the muscularis mucosa (blue boxes). To decipher this phenomenon, a dataset containing 5 DGC cases was included in this study. This dataset comprised 5 superficial/deep layer tumor specimens and 4 paired normal control specimens. For each cell, UMAP coordinates and annotation information (meta-file) were obtained from TISCH, as shown in Fig. 2B. We observed that the proportion of each cell type varied across samples, suggesting potential heterogeneity (Fig. 2C). It has been demonstrated that tumor cells enriched in the deeper layers display a strong invasive profile, with a significantly activated EMT program32.

These findings further imply a close association between PCC components and deep-layer tumors. By clustering all epithelial cells, five unique cell subpopulations with tissue specificity were identified (Fig. 2D). We then estimated the copy number variation (CNV) levels of all epithelial cells using CD8+ T cells and monocytes/macrophages as references. As shown in Fig. 2E,F, the most prominent CNV events were present in C1, suggesting a high degree of malignancy. Therefore, C1 was annotated as malignant cells, and the other clusters were annotated as non-malignant cells (Fig. 2G). Next, we performed clustering analysis of all malignant cells and revealed four prominent cell subgroups (malignant C1-C4) (Fig. 2H). Notably, the vast majority of cells in C2 originated from the superficial layers of the tumor, whereas the vast majority of cells in C3 originated from the deep layers (Fig. 2I). Given the poorly differentiated features of DGC, we analyzed the differentiation status of malignant cells using CytoTRACE. As shown in Fig. 2J,C2,C3 exhibited high CytoTRACE scores, indicating an undifferentiated state. Accordingly, we hypothesized that a deep layer-specific and a superficial layer-specific population of CSCs might exist separately in the DGC. More importantly, deep-specific CSCs may highly overlap with PCC cells. To test this speculation, we further analyzed the expression of TJ genes in different malignant cell clusters. As shown in Fig. 2K,L, the expression of CLDN3, CLDN4, and CLDN7 was further depleted in deep layer-specific CSCs (C3), particularly CLDN4 and CLDN7. A previous work by our team identified Zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1) as a PCC biomarker36. Consequently, we calculated the correlations among ZEB1, the stemness marker CD44, and CLDN3/4/7, revealing negative correlations (Fig. 2M and Figure S4; CD44-CLDN3, R = − 0.164; ZEB1-CLDN3, R = − 0.231; ZEB1-CLDN4, R = − 0.257; ZEB1-CLDN7, R = − 0.330). The expressions of ZEB1, CD44, and claudin-4 were subsequently analyzed by IF staining in DGC samples. We observed that ZEB1 and CD44 expressions were higher in the deep layers of DGC samples (PCC components), while the expression of claudin-4 remained low (Fig. 2N). In the superficial layer, CD44 was often co-expressed with claudin-4 (Fig. 2N). These findings suggest that deep- and superficial-layer CSCs may constitute distinct poorly differentiated populations. This difference is closely linked to the loss of CLDN3/4/7, and deep layer-specific CSCs may be potential PCC cells (ZEB1+).

Deep layer-specific CSCs are in a more severe hypoxic state than superficial layer-specific CSCs

To identify potential transitions among different clusters of malignant cells, we performed cell trajectory analysis using the “SCORPIUS” toolkit. The pseudotime trajectory axes obtained from “SCORPIUS” indicated that C4 can transdifferentiate into C1, and then into C2 and C3 (Fig. 3A). Dynamic pseudotemporal expression of representative genes also marked the progression from C4 to C1, and then into C2/3 (Fig. 3A and Figure S5). We noted that the expression of respiratory chain complex-related genes gradually decreased during this transdifferentiation process, suggesting that the differentiated ends (C2 and C3) were in a hypoxic state. Enrichment analysis further confirmed this result: C2 and C3 contained a high percentage of cells in a hypoxic state and simultaneously exhibited enhanced glycolysis (Fig. 3B,C). In physiologic or pathologic conditions, hypoxia leads to significant dysfunction of TJs function in epithelial cells37. Since C3 exhibited lower CLDN3/4/7 expression than C2, we further compared the hypoxia scores between the two. As shown in Fig. 3D, the hypoxia score of C3 was higher than that of C2. Additionally, activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling was observed in C3 (Fig. 3E). It is well known that Wnt/β-catenin signaling is involved in hypoxia-induced changes in cellular phenotype38. We hypothesized that more severe hypoxia and high levels of Wnt/β-catenin signaling may be responsible for the further decreased expression of CLDN3/4/7 in C3. To test this, we performed IF staining for hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF1A), β-catenin, and ZEB1 in DGC samples. The observation that ZEB1+ cells (PCC cells, also identified by HE staining) tended to be located near the muscularis mucosa of the tumor tissue and were accompanied by high expressions of β-catenin and HIF1A suggested that PCC cells in the deeper layers were in a highly hypoxic state (Fig. 3F,a sample with T1).

To further investigate the molecular differences among different malignant cell subpopulations, we obtained gene markers specific to each cluster. As shown in Fig. 3G, IGFBP7, CLU, RASA3, HMGCS2, and IGFBP2 were highly expressed in C3, while VSIG1, AKR1B10, SPINK13, TPM2, and NCOA7 were highly expressed in C2. Friends analysis suggested that CSTA, which encodes Cystatin A—a member of the cystatin superfamily—and IGFBP7, which encodes Insulin-Like Growth Factor Binding Protein 7—a member of the insulin-like growth factor-binding protein family—are likely the most representative genetic markers for the C2 and C3 clusters, respectively (Fig. 3H). Cox regression analysis revealed that both CSTA and IGFBP7 were significant detrimental factors for GC (Fig. 3I,J; CSTA: P = 0.017 for DSS and 0.01 for PFI; IGFBP7: P = 0.001 for OS, 0.013 for DSS, and 0.015 for PFI). Subsequently, we examined the response of IGFBP7 and CSTA to hypoxic stimuli (Fig. 3K). For this experiment, we selected HGC27 (derived from undifferentiated GC) and MKN45 (derived from signet-ring cell carcinoma). To simulate tumor hypoxic microenvironmental conditions, we used a hypoxia incubator (1% oxygen) to induce a hypoxic environment. We found that the expression of IGFBP7, CSTA, β-catenin, HIF1A, and CD44 gradually increased with the prolongation of hypoxia treatment. Notably, MKN45 appeared to be more sensitive to hypoxia, showing a more significant increase in protein expression (IGFBP7, β-catenin, HIF1A, and CD44).

IGFBP7 is involved in a broader range of signaling pathways compared to CSTA

Since IGFBP7 and CSTA each mark different CSC populations, we proceeded here to investigate their clinicopathological and functional differences. The expression levels of IGFBP7 and CSTA in malignant cells are illustrated in Fig. 4A,B. The UMAP plots indicate that IGFBP7 is up-regulated in cluster 3 (C3), and CSTA is up-regulated in cluster 2 (C2). To further confirm the spatial relationships among IGFBP7, CSTA, and claudin-4 expressed by GC cells, we performed combined IF and HE staining (Fig. 4C,D). Given that cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are key secretors of IGFBP739, we used EPCAM to label epithelial components and Fibroblast Activation Protein Alpha (FAP) as a marker to exclude fibroblasts. FAP is a serine protease predominantly expressed by cancer-associated fibroblasts, where it plays a crucial role in extracellular matrix remodeling, tumor growth, and metastasis40. The staining results revealed that CSTA was scarcely expressed in deep-layer epithelial regions, which were characterized by high IGFBP7 expression and low claudin-4 expression. Conversely, IGFBP7 was expressed at low levels in superficial-layer epithelial regions, where high levels of CSTA and claudin-4 were observed (Fig. 4E,F). Considering that the depth of tumor infiltration is clinically measured using T stage, we compared the relationship between IGFBP7 and CSTA expression and T stage. We found that the expression of IGFBP7 increased progressively with T stage, whereas no similar correlation was observed for CSTA (Fig. 4G). Additionally, both IGFBP7 and CSTA were correlated with the degree of tumor differentiation (Fig. 4H,I, high levels in G3), consistent with their roles as CSC markers. We generated corresponding Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves to characterize the association between these two genes and survival in GC patients. As shown in Fig. 4J,K, OS (P = 0.003) and PFI (P = 0.008) were lower in the high IGFBP7 expression group compared to the low IGFBP7 expression group, and PFI (P = 0.004) was lower in the high CSTA expression group compared to the low CSTA expression group. Given the importance of the immune microenvironment for tumor progression, we applied the single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) method to further measure the relationship between IGFBP7 and CSTA expression and immune components. Spearman correlation analysis showed that IGFBP7 exhibited a stronger correlation with various immune cells, including mast cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells (DCs), compared to CSTA (Fig. 4L,M). Furthermore, IGFBP7 was highly correlated with the ratios of immune and stromal components (StromalScore, R = 0.838; ESTIMATEScore, R = 0.665; ImmuneScore, R = 0.396), whereas the correlation between CSTA and these scores was much weaker (ImmuneScore, R = 0.378; ESTIMATEScore, R = 0.297; StromalScore, R = 0.150) (Fig. 4N,O). We performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, revealing that IGFBP7 may be involved in a broader range of signaling pathways than CSTA. Specifically, for GO, a high degree of correlation with cell-substrate junction assembly, extracellular matrix organization, regulation of the Wnt signaling pathway, and regulation of angiogenesis was observed for IGFBP7, which was not seen in CSTA-related terms (Fig. 4P–S). Additionally, we quantified the correlation between IGFBP7, CSTA, and cancer-related signaling using gene set variation analysis (GSVA). As shown in Fig. 4T,U, IGFBP7 showed a high positive correlation with mesenchymal signals, including EMT markers, extracellular matrix (ECM)-related genes, angiogenesis, and transforming growth factor-beta (TGFB). Importantly, IGFBP7 was negatively correlated with cellular responses to hypoxia (R = − 0.27), while CSTA did not show a significant correlation (R = 0.03), further confirming our earlier inferences.

Combining these results, we explored possible functional differences between IGFBP7 and CSTA through correlations in the bulk data (Fig. 4V,W). Both IGFBP7 and CSTA showed negative correlations with CLDN3/4/7 (IGFBP7-CLDN3, R = − 0.113; IGFBP7-CLDN4, R = − 0.162; IGFBP7-CLDN7, R = − 0.245; CSTA-CLDN3, R = − 0.260; CSTA-CLDN4, R = − 0.182; CSTA-CLDN7, R = − 0.165). Interestingly, there was a strong correlation (R = 0.768) between IGFBP7 and ZEB1, a marker for PCC cells, which was not present for CSTA (R = 0.055). Histopathologic sections confirmed a high percentage of PCC cells in the high IGFBP7 expression group, while the high CSTA expression group showed distinct glandular structures (Fig. 4X), further suggesting a correlation between IGFBP7 and PCC components.

IGFBP7 promotes cancer cell invasiveness and decreases the expression of claudin-3, -4, and -7

Given IGFBP7's strong oncogenic effect, we next focused on its biological function. In vitro, IGFBP7 was overexpressed in MKN45 and HGC27 cells. The wound healing and transwell assays showed that IGFBP7 overexpression promoted cell migration and invasion (Fig. 5A,B). Moreover, the colony-formation and sphere-formation assays indicated that tumor stemness was enhanced with IGFBP7 overexpression (Fig. 5C,D). The analysis revealed a close correlation between IGFBP7 expression and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, as well as tight junctions. As shown in Fig. 5E, western blotting demonstrated that IGFBP7 overexpression increased the expression of ZEB1 (a PCC marker), Wnt3/3a/1, β-catenin, and CD44, but decreased the expression of claudin-3, -4, and -7. In addition, immunofluorescence staining showed that knockdown of IGFBP7 (sh-IGFBP7) significantly restored the expression of CLDN3, CLDN4, and CLDN7 compared to non-targeting control (NC) groups (Figure S6).

We further investigated the effects of IGFBP7 overexpression on tumor in a xenograft model. To do so, IGFBP7-expressing or control cells were subcutaneously injected into BALB/c nude mice. We found that IGFBP7 overexpression markedly raised the volume and mass of tumors (Fig. 5F). More importantly, IHC staining confirmed the regulatory role of IGFBP7 in vivo (Fig. 5G). Signaling crosstalk among deep layer-specific subclusters of macrophages, fibroblasts, and tumor cells (malignant C3)

Tumor cells exert immunosuppressive effects to support tumor growth through crosstalk with their microenvironment41. A recent study showed that tumor-specific FAP+ fibroblasts and Secreted Phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1)+ macrophages are tightly localized and interact with each other in the TME, and this interaction leads to fibrosis and immunosuppression40. SPP1, also known as osteopontin, is a multifunctional cytokine involved in regulating immune responses, promoting tumorigenesis, and facilitating metastasis within the tumor microenvironment42. Consistent with existing findings, we found that the relative abundance of individual cell types according to tumor depth compartmentalized the cell types into deep layer-specific (endothelial cells, monocytes/macrophages, and fibroblasts) and superficial layer-specific (B cells, epithelial cells, and plasma cells) groups (Fig. 6A–C). The analysis process of the scRNA dataset GSE167297 is demonstrated in Fig. 6D–G as well as Figure S7A (macrophages) and Fig. 6H–K as well as Figure S7B (fibroblasts), with bubble and UMAP plots showing the gene markers of each subpopulation. Notably, SPP1+ macrophages and FAP+ fibroblasts were almost exclusively present in the deeper layers of the DGCs (Fig. 6G,K). Considering the presence of deep layer-specific subpopulations of malignant cells, we hypothesized that there might be an interaction related to tumorigenesis among these three cell types. Therefore, the “Commpath” package was used to identify cell–cell communication in the DGC microenvironment based on known ligand-receptor information. As shown in Fig. 6L,M, there was frequent communication among SPP1+ macrophages, FAP+ fibroblasts, and two malignant cell subpopulations of interest to us (malignant C2 and C3). There was no difference in the number of communications between deep layer-specific malignant cells (malignant C3) and superficial layer-specific malignant cells (malignant C2) with other cells (Figure S8A-B), but there was a difference in the intensity of communications (Fig. 6N,O). Further ligand-receptor analyses showed that C2 and C3 possessed similar upstream signals (data not shown), whereas the communication signals downstream of C2 and C3 were distinct (Fig. 6P,Q). Thus, a communication network downstream of malignant cells was established to compare the different signaling outputs of C2 and C3. The results showed that malignant C3 could signal through the receptor MARCO expressed in SPP1+ macrophages, thereby leading to enhanced transmembrane receptor signaling activity (Fig. 6R). MARCO is a macrophage receptor with a collagenous structure that is highly expressed in the TME and activates the hypoxia pathway43–45.

Next, we aimed to validate the regulatory effects of IGFBP7 on macrophages and fibroblasts through in vitro experiments. To this end, we established an in vitro co-culture model and prepared conditioned medium, as illustrated in Fig. 6S,T. As expected, IGFBP7 overexpression in cancer cells resulted in the up-regulation of SPP1 in macrophages (Fig. 6U). Additionally, the conditioned medium obtained from the co-culture of cancer cells overexpressing IGFBP7 with macrophages significantly promoted the expression of FAP in CAFs, accompanied by an increase in collagen secretion (COL1A1) by CAFs (Fig. 6V). These results suggest that IGFBP7 is essential for TME modulation.

To further assess the spatial organization of FAP+ fibroblasts and SPP1+ macrophages, we performed spatial transcriptomic analysis of tumor tissue sections from a GC model to assess the spatial expression distribution of SPP1, FAP, and IGFBP7. As shown in Fig. 6W, there was spatial overlapping of SPP1, FAP, and IGFBP7, indicating physical proximity among the three cell types, suggesting potential interaction. For experimental validation, we performed multiple fluorescence and HE staining in DGC paraffin sections. SPP1+ macrophages and FAP+ CAFs were shown to localize near IGFBP7+ GC cells in deep layer-cancer tissues (Fig. 6X,Y). In contrast, FAP and SPP1 were barely expressed around regions (EPCAM+) of high CSTA expression that labeled the superficial layer-specific malignant cell population, reflecting the spatial differences in signal exchange.

Potential bidirectional crosstalk between CLDN4 expression loss and PD-L1

As a T-cell inhibitory molecule, PD-L1 (encoded by the CD274 gene) is expressed by cancer cells and infiltrating immune cells and binds to the receptor PD-1 (encoded by the PDCD1 gene) on T-cells to down-regulate anti-tumor immune responses46. Multiple mechanisms are known to be involved in the regulation of PD-L1 expression, among which it is impressive that PD-L1 expression is induced during EMT and is always accompanied by low expression of claudins47,48. In addition, patients highly infiltrated with FAP+ fibroblasts and SPP1+ macrophages showed a lower response to immunotherapy40,49. This may be due to the fact that FAP- and SPP1-enriched patients have a TME feature of immune exclusion, a feature that restricts lymphocyte infiltration and thus prevents them from exerting tumor-killing effects50. In the context of the present study, this evidence seemed to point to the speculation that the deep layer-PCC component of DGCs may be highly immunosuppressive. The work of Jeong et al. showed that Treg cells with an exhausted phenotype were enriched in the deeper layers of the tumor, suggesting the immunosuppressive properties of TME in the deep invasive layer of DGCs32. We compared the expression levels of immune checkpoint molecules in different tumor infiltration layers and found that LAG3, CTLA4, HAVCR2, PDCD1LG2, and CD274 were enriched in the deep layers (Fig. 7A). Further analysis revealed that these molecules were also highly expressed in malignant C3 (Fig. 7B). To further confirm the spatial differences in immunosuppressive features, we performed IF staining for PD-L1 and claudin-4 in DGC samples. It was observed that the expression of claudin-4 gradually decreased with increasing tumor depth, whereas the expression of PD-L1 showed an opposite trend, being highly expressed in regions close to the serosal layer (Fig. 7C). These findings suggest that the tumor depth-based immune signature may involve complex biological mechanisms. Discussion

GC is a complex disease characterized by significant intratumor and intertumor heterogeneity51, which complicates the development of effective therapeutic strategies. Our study focused on DGC, particularly the PCC subtype, known for its aggressive nature and poor prognosis. This investigation sought to elucidate the molecular underpinnings that contribute to the distinct biological behavior of DGC, with a focus on the roles of tight junction proteins and the TME.

Our results revealed that CLDN3, CLDN4, and CLDN7 are expressed at lower levels in DGC compared to IGC. The loss of these tight junction proteins, particularly claudin-4, was strongly associated with the PCC phenotype, suggesting a link between reduced cell–cell adhesion and the aggressive, invasive characteristics of DGC. This finding aligns with previous studies indicating that decreased expression of claudins is a hallmark of poorly differentiated gastric cancers, where cellular cohesion is disrupted, facilitating invasion and metastasis52,53.

We also observed that PCC components were predominantly localized in the deeper layers of the tumor, an area enriched with CSCs characterized by a hypoxic microenvironment and enhanced Wnt/β-catenin signaling. This spatial distribution suggests a correlation between hypoxia, stemness, and the aggressive behavior of PCC cells. The high expression of ZEB1 and CD44 in these deep layers, coupled with low claudin-4 levels, further supports the hypothesis that the hypoxic TME in the deeper tumor regions promotes a more invasive phenotype.

Our study identified IGFBP7 as a marker for deep layer-specific CSCs and CSTA for superficial layer-specific CSCs. IGFBP7 was particularly notable for its strong association with hypoxia54, enhanced invasiveness39, and a broad range of pro-tumorigenic signaling pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin and extracellular matrix remodeling55,56. This marker’s correlation with poor prognosis57 underscores its potential as a therapeutic target, particularly in combating the refractory nature of DGC with PCC components.

The interplay between tumor cells and the TME is crucial in shaping the progression and therapeutic resistance of GC58. We demonstrated that IGFBP7 not only enhances the invasive capacity of cancer cells but also modulates the TME by promoting the infiltration of immunosuppressive SPP1+ macrophages and FAP+ fibroblasts. These cells, in turn, contribute to a desmoplastic and immunosuppressive environment40, particularly in the deeper tumor layers, which likely hampers the effectiveness of immunotherapies.

Furthermore, our findings suggest a potential bidirectional crosstalk between the loss of claudin-4 expression and increased PD-L1 expression in the deeper layers of DGC. The observed enrichment of immune checkpoint molecules, such as PD-L1, in these regions points to an immunosuppressive TME that may facilitate immune evasion by the tumor. This underscores the complexity of the immune landscape in DGC and highlights the need for strategies that can overcome these barriers to improve therapeutic outcomes.

In summary, our study provides valuable insights into the molecular and cellular mechanisms driving the aggressive behavior of DGC, particularly those with PCC characteristics. The identification of IGFBP7 as a key player in modulating both tumor cell invasiveness and the TME offers a potential target for therapeutic intervention. Future studies should focus on further elucidating the role of IGFBP7 in gastric cancer progression and exploring its potential as a biomarker for patient stratification and as a target for combination therapies.

Conclusion

The PCC components are predominantly located in the deeper layers of DGC. The molecular features of PCC components were initially revealed, including low expression of caudin-3, -4 and -7, high expression of IGFBP7, and expression of PD-L1. These features may partially explain the strong tumor heterogeneities of GC and aid pathologists in distinguishing PCC components more effectively.
