Development and validation of a prognostic model and scoring system for in-hospital mortality risk in neonates with heart failure within 28 days: a multicenter retrospective case-control study
Authors: Meng Wei, Xinru Liu, Gaofeng Sun, Xiaoting Zhang, Wanchunxue Jiang, Pengcheng Fan, Xin Du, Yanmei Lu, Baopeng Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeonatal heart failure is a severe condition with high mortality, posing a significant burden on families and healthcare systems. Early prediction of mortality risk is crucial for improving outcomes. To develop and validate a predictive model and scoring system for in-hospital mortality within 28 days in neonates with heart failure. This multicenter retrospective study included 579 neonates from the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (training and internal validation sets) and 118 from Beijing Anzhen Hospital (external validation set). Data included sociodemographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, medical history, medication use, laboratory results, and outcomes. Lasso was used for variable selection from a large set of candidates, followed by logistic regression on the selected variables to build the final model and scoring system. Lasso regression identified 20 key variables. This study found that fibrinogen < 2 g/L, poor postnatal response, and oliguria were associated with an increased risk of death in neonates with heart failure, while the use of digoxin, cedilanid, dopamine, and epinephrine reduced the risk of death. The model showed AUC values of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.82–0.91), 0.83 (95% CI: 0.77–0.90), and 0.85 (95% CI: 0.77–0.93) in the training, internal validation, and external validation sets, respectively. The scoring system effectively categorized patients into low, medium, and high-risk groups. Introduction

Neonatal heart failure—characterized by ventricular dysfunction that precipitates circulatory collapse and metabolic crisis—is a severe complication in critically ill neonates and the common end-stage trajectory of diverse underlying disorders, with onset markedly increasing mortality1. Its acute onset, rapid progression, and atypical symptoms often result in clinical oversight or misdiagnosis, which can lead to rapid deterioration and missed therapeutic opportunities. The incidence is approximately 4.5%, with early mortality rates ranging from 20% to 50%, varying by region2,3. This increasing prevalence imposes significant economic burdens due to prolonged hospitalizations and associated costs4,5.

The etiologies of neonatal acute heart failure are diverse, including congenital heart defects, cardiomyopathies, vascular malformations, and conduction disorders, all of which severely impact survival and long-term health6–8. Prematurity and extremely low birth weight independently increase mortality risk9, while in resource-limited settings, severe anemia and infections further exacerbate heart failure incidence10. Current treatment strategies, including pharmacotherapy and mechanical ventilation, remain suboptimal due to the lack of personalized approaches addressing specific underlying pathophysiologies5.

Advances in technology, particularly through the use of big data and clinical decision support systems, hold promise for enhancing early detection and intervention in neonatal heart failure11. Existing neonatal mortality-risk prediction tools (e.g., CRIB-II, SNAP-II)12are built around the principal drivers of neonatal death—prematurity, birth weight, and perinatal asphyxia—but incorporate markedly fewer variables reflecting the diagnosis or management of heart failure and are optimized chiefly for estimating death risk in preterm infants. Moreover, heart failure cases constitute only a minute fraction of their derivation cohorts, leaving these models with poor discriminative performance in this specific subgroup and unable to deliver the precision stratification required. Consequently, a mortality-prediction model that is both dedicated to neonates with heart failure and enriched with disease-specific predictors is currently lacking. Especially, systematic assessment of in-hospital mortality risk remains underdeveloped11–14. There is a critical need for validated predictive tools to guide clinical practice and inform risk stratification15. This multicenter retrospective study aims to develop a mortality-risk model and scoring system by analyzing electronic medical records from neonates with heart failure. By applying machine learning techniques to demographic, clinical, and laboratory data, we aim to identify key predictors of mortality through complex pattern recognition16. The resulting model will support clinical decision-making, improve management of high-risk patients, and enhance survival outcomes17.

Methodology

Study design and study participants

This multicenter retrospective case-control study was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocols were approved by the Ethics Committees of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (approval numbers: K202403-48 and K202403-48–2503 A-Y1) and Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Beijing, China (ethical approval number: 2025241x). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for obtaining informed consent from all participants and/or their legal guardians was waived by both ethics committees. We consecutively enrolled neonates ≤ 28 days of age who were admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between 1 January 2012 and 1 March 2025 and received a discharge diagnosis of heart failure; these infants formed the training and internal-validation set (initial n = 600). A contemporaneous group of 125 neonates with heart failure admitted to Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University, was selected for external validation. Due to critical missing data, 21 infants were excluded from the Xinjiang group and 8 from the Beijing group, resulting in final analytic samples of 579 and 118 neonates, respectively. The 118 neonates enrolled in the external validation cohort were all recruited from the Department of Neonatology and Neonatal Intensive Care Unit of Beijing Anzhen Hospital. This center is geographically distinct from the hospital housing the internal cohort, with marked heterogeneity in clinical practice protocols and disease spectrum distribution; additionally, there was no overlap between the cases from the two centers, thus fully meeting the methodological requirements for a valid external validation (i.e., geographic independence, clinical practice heterogeneity, and sample non-overlapping).

Patients from the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University were randomly assigned to a training set (n = 405) and an internal validation set (n = 174) at a 7:3 ratio. Neonates from Beijing Anzhen Hospital comprised the external validation set (n = 118). Figure S1 details the patient selection process. Given the study’s retrospective design, the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University waived the need for informed consent. Neonatal cases were identified using the principal diagnosis ICD-10 code (P29.0) in the hospital’s electronic medical record system.

Data collection

In this study, patient data were retrospectively collected from the electronic health record systems of participating hospitals. We collected baseline birth information (e.g., preterm birth, low birth weight) from the delivery records. Laboratory tests, imaging parameters, baseline vital signs, and clinically positive signs were extracted from the first measurement obtained after hospital admission. Medication data were gathered for drugs administered within the first 24 h of admission. Including: (1) Demographic Characteristics: age, sex, ethnicity. (2) Clinical Presentation and Signs: including heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, height, weight, presence or absence of diminished heart sounds, edema, cardiac murmurs, pulmonary crackles, dyspnea, hypoxia, poor postnatal response, and oliguria. (3) Past Medical History: including preterm birth, low birth weight, history of infection, and jaundice. (4) Medication Use: The following medications were involved, including inotropes such as milrinone, digoxin, cedilanid, dopamine, dobutamine, adrenaline, noradrenaline, and isoprenaline; diuretics like furosemide, spironolactone, hydrochlorothiazide; vasodilators including sodium nitroprusside and nitroglycerin; other medications such as corticosteroids and sedatives. (5) Laboratory Findings: Complete blood count: white blood cell count, red blood cell count (RBC), Red Blood Cell Distribution Width (RDW-CV), lymphocyte count, monocyte count, neutrophil count, eosinophil count, basophil count, hemoglobin, platelet count, platelet distribution width (PDW), and plateletcrit. Liver function biomarkers: alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), lactate dehydrogenase cholinesterase, direct bilirubin (DB), indirect bilirubin (IB), albumin, globulin (GLO), and total bile acids. Renal function biomarkers: creatinine, uric acid, urea, and cystatin C. Lipid profile: triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and apolipoprotein A. Electrolytes: potassium, sodium, chloride, calcium, phosphate, magnesium, and glucose. Coenzymes function: prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), international normalized ratio, fibrinogen, and prothrombin activity. Blood gas analysis (ABG): pH, partial pressure of oxygen, oxygen content, standard base excess (SBE), total hemoglobin concentration, Fraction of oxyhemoglobin, Fraction of deoxyhemoglobin, bicarbonate (HCO3−), and a/A PO2 Ratio. Cardiac function indicators: creatine kinase. Infection indicators: procalcitonin. Echocardiography: left atrial diameter, left ventricular end-diastolic dimension, left ventricular end-systolic dimension, left ventricular posterior wall thickness, right ventricular outflow tract, and ejection fraction. (6)Outcomes: Information on in-hospital mortality among neonatal patients was collected.

For each newborn, four indicators were assessed immediately at birth18: (1) term gestation? (2) clear amniotic fluid? (3) good muscle tone? (4) good cry or breathing? If one or more of the four items was answered “no”, the newborn was defined as having poor postnatal response, requiring immediate entry into the resuscitation protocol and initiation of initial resuscitation. In cases of meconium-stained amniotic fluid, the newborn’s vitality was evaluated to determine whether endotracheal intubation and meconium aspiration were indicated.

Missing data processing

In this study, we used a stratified approach to handle missing data. First, we excluded variables with a missing rate of > 30% from the data of the First Affiliated Hospital, removing five indicators (4.95% of the original variables). For the remaining 96 variables (missing rate 0%–29.5%), we used the K-nearest neighbors (KNN) imputation method to generate five complete datasets. The imputation model included all outcome variables and major covariates. The relative increase in variance (RIV) was calculated to evaluate the imputation rationality, with all variables having an RIV of < 0.3, indicating no significant bias was introduced. Subsequently, Lasso regression was used to select 20 key variables from the imputed dataset for model development and internal validation. The same variables were also selected from the Anzhen Hospital dataset for external validation after KNN imputation.

Study outcome events

The study endpoint was in-hospital all-cause mortality within 28 days among neonates with heart failure. All-cause mortality includes death from any cause during the hospital stay, such as cardiovascular events or respiratory infections.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The following R packages were utilized: glmnet (version 4.1.7), pROC (version 1.18.0), ggplot2 (version 3.3.6), rms (version 6.4.0), ResourceSelection (version 0.3-5), and rmda (version 1.6). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD if normally distributed or median (Q25, Q75) if skewed, with group comparisons made using the independent-samples t-test or Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, respectively. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies (n, %) and analyzed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

A total of 96 candidate variables related to neonatal heart failure were collected in this study. First, all 96 variables were incorporated into the Lasso regression model. The lambda (λ) parameter was tuned using ten-fold cross-validation to optimize variable selection based on the lambda.min criterion, ultimately identifying core variables that were significantly associated with the prognosis of neonatal heart failure. Subsequently, these core variables were assigned values and incorporated into univariate logistic regression analysis as categorical variables. This analysis was conducted to calculate the strength of association between each variable and the prognostic outcome, expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Those achieving P < 0.05 were advanced to multivariable logistic regression. Additionally, variables with a large generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF) were excluded to avoid multicollinearity. Finally, a prognostic model with independent predictors was constructed. Neonates from the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University were randomly assigned to a training set and an internal validation set in a 7:3 ratio. The model was developed using the training set, and internal validation was performed using the validation set. The performance of the model was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC).

Decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration curves were used to assess model performance and goodness-of-fit. A nomogram was developed to illustrate the relationships between predictive variables. External validation was conducted using data from Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University. According to the Framingham risk scoring criteria19and combined with the results of multiple logistic regression, we established a risk scoring table for in-hospital mortality in newborns with heart failure. Score-effect analysis was performed. All tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Variable selection via Lasso regression

After variable selection using Lasso regression, 20 key variables were retained from the original 96 for further analysis. These included RBC, RDW-CV, Basophil count, PDW, DB, GLO, HDL-c, ALP, APTT, Fibrinogen, blood gas analysis SBE, blood gas analysis HCO3−, Edema, Poor postnatal response, Oliguria, Digoxin, Cedilanid, Dopamine, Adrenaline, and HR. The trajectory of variable coefficient changes and the selection process in Lasso regression are shown in Fig. S2.

Comparison of variables between internal training and validation and external validation sets

Variable comparison between our hospital set and the external validation set revealed significant differences across multiple parameters (Table 1). Specifically, our hospital set exhibited higher mean values for RDW-CV, Basophil count, GLO, ALP, APTT, fibrinogen, and heart rate, as well as higher proportions of patients with poor postnatal response and those receiving Digoxin, Cedilanid, Dopamine, and Adrenaline therapy (all P < 0.05). Conversely, lower mean values were observed in our hospital set for RBC, PDW, DB, HDL-c, ABG-SBE, and ABG-HCO3−, alongside a lower proportion of patients with oliguria (all P < 0.05). Notably, no significant difference in all-cause mortality was found between the two groups.

Comparison of the training and validation sets of neonates with heart failure

Neonates with heart failure from the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University were randomly assigned to a training set (n = 405) and an internal validation set (n = 174) at a 7:3 ratio. No significant differences were observed between the training and validation sets in terms of RBC, RDW-CV, Basophil count, PDW, DB, GLO, HDL-c, ALP, APTT, fibrinogen, ABG-SBE, ABG-HCO3−, edema, poor postnatal response, oliguria, digoxin, Cedilanid, dopamine, adrenaline, and HR. There were 114 deaths and 465 survivals during hospitalization, yielding a mortality rate of 19.69%. The mortality rates in the training and validation sets were 19.51% and 20.11%, respectively. After dummy variable assignment, no differences were found between the sets, confirming proper random allocation (all P > 0.05). Details of the dummy variable assignments are in Table S1; additional information is in Tables S2 and S3.

Characteristics comparison between death and survived groups in training set of neonates with heart failure

In the training set, there were 326 patients in the survival group and 79 in the death group. The death group had lower average levels of RBC, Fibrinogen, ABG-HCO3−, and HR, but higher RDW-CV and ABG-SBE levels compared to the survival group. The death group also had a higher proportion of poor postnatal response and oliguria. Conversely, the survival group had a higher percentage of Digoxin, Cedilanid, Dopamine, and Adrenaline use. Detailed comparisons of baseline data between the two groups are in Table S4. After dummy variable assignment, the death group had a higher percentage of patients with Fibrinogen < 2 g/L. Results of univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses in the training set

Univariate logistic regression analysis of neonates with heart failure showed that fibrinogen < 2 g/L (OR = 1.69), poor postnatal response (OR = 3.05), and oliguria (OR = 3.49) were potential risk factors, while digoxin use (OR = 0.12), cedilanid use (OR = 0.22), dopamine use (OR = 0.18), and adrenaline use (OR = 0.41) were potential protective factors. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that fibrinogen < 2 g/L (OR = 2.28), poor postnatal response (OR = 2.95), and oliguria (OR = 4.18) increased the risk of death, while digoxin use (OR = 0.18), cedilanid use (OR = 0.42), dopamine use (OR = 0.20), and adrenaline use (OR = 0.33) reduced the risk of death. The remaining 13 variables were associated with the prognosis of neonatal heart failure in univariate logistic regression analysis, but they lacked independent predictive significance after adjustment for confounding factors in the multivariable logistic regression model. Discussion

In a multicenter study, we developed a risk-stratification tool for neonatal heart failure using seven variables measurable within 24 h of hospitalization. The model identifies hypofibrinogenemia, oliguria, and poor postnatal response as key mortality predictors, with cardiotonic/vasoactive agents associated with survival benefits. The risk score stratifies infants into low-, medium-, and high-risk groups, guiding clinical management. With an AUC of 0.83–0.85, the tool complements clinical judgment, especially in resource-constrained settings, potentially improving equity in critical care.

Early high-risk identification and targeted interventions

Hypofibrinogenemia (< 2 g/L) is a strong independent predictor of in-hospital mortality in neonatal heart failure (aOR 2.28. This likely reflects endothelial injury, impaired hepatic function, and inflammation-driven depletion20–23, which drive microthrombosis and hemodynamic collapse. Notably, hypofibrinogenemia can also serve as a key biomarker for disseminated intravascular coagulation or overt liver dysfunction, both of which are well-established poor prognostic indicators in critically ill neonates. Our lower threshold (< 2 g/L) identifies a high-risk subset with severe coagulopathy and perfusion deficits, compared to prior studies linking fibrinogen < 3.6 g/L to higher mortality24–26. We recommend protocolized fibrinogen monitoring at admission and targeted supplementation for active bleeding or invasive procedures, consistent with trials showing mortality reduction27.

Poor postnatal response independently predicted mortality (aOR 2.95), characterized by respiratory depression (62%), hypotonia (58%), and low 5-min Apgar scores (≤ 5 in 41%). This phenotype is strongly associated with prematurity (63.9%), low birth weight (56.1%), and asphyxia (40.9%), which exacerbate cardiac failure through myocardial ischemia, calcium dysregulation, and metabolic acidosis28. Prior studies have shown a 2.8-fold mortality increase with Apgar scores < 729. For these infants, we recommend time-sensitive interventions: (1) therapeutic hypothermia for eligible HIE cases; (2) early echocardiography-guided inotropes (e.g., milrinone); and (3) correcting acidosis with targeted ventilation/bicarbonate, prioritizing myocardial oxygen delivery.

Oliguria was the strongest predictor of mortality (aOR 4.18), indicating decompensated cardiorenal syndrome. Reduced cardiac output (< 50 mL/kg/min) causes renal hypoperfusion, activating the RAAS and leading to fluid retention that increases cardiac preload and causes pulmonary edema30. Neonatal tubular immaturity, especially impaired potassium excretion, exacerbates this, with 28% of our cohort developing hyperkalemia (> 6.0 mmol/L), which can cause lethal arrhythmias31We recommend protocolized urine output monitoring (> 1 mL/kg/hr) and diuretic intervention (furosemide 1–2 mg/kg IV) if output is < 0.5 mL/kg/hr for four hours32. Echocardiography is crucial to differentiate cardiac from renal causes.

Our risk-stratification algorithm prioritizes urgent cardiac ICU transfer, fibrinogen replacement, and echocardiography-guided milrinone for neonates with oliguria, hypofibrinogenemia, and poor postnatal response. In resource-limited settings, empirical furosemide and selective fibrinogen testing are emphasized. Targeting modifiable pathways within six hours of admission, this approach aims to reduce mortality.

Optimizing pharmacotherapy in neonatal heart failure

We found that four types of drugs were associated with reduced mortality in neonates with heart failure during the treatment process, yet the clinical application scenarios for each drug were distinct. Cedilanid (lanatoside C) is valuable for acute stabilization, offering rapid positive inotropic effects and hemodynamic support during emergent deterioration. Its cost-effectiveness and flexible dosing make it suitable for resource-constrained environments. Digoxin is essential for chronic management but requires strict monitoring of serum concentrations and electrocardiographic surveillance due to its narrow therapeutic window. Dopamine provides multidimensional utility: at lower doses (1–3 µg/kg/min), it enhances renal perfusion by 30–40%, mitigating cardiorenal syndrome, while moderate dosing (5–10 µg/kg/min) offers inotropic support for hemodynamically unstable neonates, particularly preterm infants33. For refractory circulatory collapse, epinephrine is the definitive rescue therapy, providing immediate blood pressure stabilization and increased cardiac output through α1-mediated vasoconstriction and β1-adrenergic activity. However, it requires careful titration to avoid increasing myocardial oxygen demand and post-resuscitation dysfunction.

We propose a risk-adapted pharmacotherapy framework guided by three principles: early combination therapy (glycosides with dopamine) for high-risk neonates, protocolized hemodynamic monitoring during catecholamine infusions, and immediate antidote availability (e.g., digoxin immune Fab). Integrated with our mortality prediction model, this approach tailors therapy to risk level. High-risk neonates (score ≥ 2) receive preemptive cedilanid/dopamine and fibrinogen correction, while digoxin is reserved for stabilized medium-risk infants.

Although we found that the administration of digoxin, cedilanid, dopamine, and adrenaline was associated with a reduced risk of in-hospital mortality in neonates with heart failure, it must be emphasized that this association may be related to the disease severity and survival time of infants receiving these drugs, rather than the direct protective effect of the drugs themselves. The assignment of scores to dopamine use (-2 points) and adrenaline use (-1 points) in the scoring system of this study is by no means intended to imply that these two classes of drugs exert a direct protective effect, nor does it suggest that all neonates with heart failure should receive such treatments. For neonates with heart failure who present with hemodynamic instability and have been clinically evaluated to have a reversible pathological state, timely administration of dopamine or adrenaline can help stabilize circulation and improve short-term survival outcomes. However, given the current lack of comprehensive treatment regimens for neonatal heart failure, the conclusions of this study have certain significance. Initiating the above treatment regimens according to clinical needs may yield clinical benefits for patients.

Future directions

This study advances neonatal heart failure management via risk stratification, optimized pharmacotherapy, and multidisciplinary integration. Immediate implementation includes embedding tools in electronic health records and activating rapid response teams. Future work involves whole-exome sequencing, non-invasive urine output monitoring, and validation trials. These steps will enable precision neonatology.

Strengths and limitations

As the first clinical prediction model for neonatal heart failure mortality with external validation, this study shows superior performance across different settings. However, limitations include the retrospective design, which limits causal inference, and the modest sample size that restricts subgroup analyses. Key indicators like serial electrocardiograms and brain natriuretic peptide levels were unavailable, limiting their inclusion. Yet, the model’s accuracy without these biomarkers makes it useful in resource-limited settings. The model is currently applicable for the preliminary screening of high-risk neonates rather than the exclusion of mortality risk among neonates across the entire risk spectrum. Meanwhile, discrepancies in medication utilization rates between the two centers have partially compromised the predictive performance of the model. For future research, we recommend enrolling larger multicenter cohorts and adopting a center-specific stratified assignment approach to optimize the scoring rules. Alternatively, a dynamic scoring model can be constructed by integrating machine learning algorithms, which enables automatic adjustment of variable scores based on the medication profiles and etiological distributions of different centers. This will help balance the clinical convenience of the scoring table and the accuracy of cross-center prediction.

Conclusion

This study establishes a validated prediction model for in-hospital mortality in neonatal heart failure, identifying fibrinogen < 2 g/L, poor postnatal response, and oliguria as key risk factors inversely associated with survival, while cardioactive medications (digoxin, cedilanid, dopamine, adrenaline) correlate with reduced mortality. The integer-based risk score stratifies neonates into low- (0–4% mortality), medium- (7–37%), and high-risk (53–65%) tiers with robust discrimination (AUC 0.83–0.85). Implementation enables early targeted interventions: high-risk infants (score ≥ 2) warrant urgent ICU transfer and fibrinogen supplementation, while medium-risk neonates require intensified diuresis monitoring. This framework provides a pragmatic pathway for precision resuscitation in resource-limited settings lacking advanced diagnostics, pending prospective validation in diverse populations.
