Caspase-4 transgenic mice exhibit cytoplasmic TDP-43 accumulation and age-dependent neuropathology
Authors: Qingqing Jia, Longhong Zhu, Dandan Li, Zhenchun Nan, Junqi Hou, Yue Zhao, Gaolu Zhu, Kaili Ou, Mingwei Guo, Huajie Dui, Xiangyi Liu, Xiao-Xin Yan, Su Yang, Li’an Huang, Dongsheng Fan, Shihua Li, Xiao-Jiang Li, Peng Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTAR DNA-binding protein (TDP-43) is a multifunctional protein that binds DNA and RNA within the nucleus. In neurodegenerative diseases like Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), TDP-43 is mislocalized to the cytoplasm, forming inclusions. Current TDP-43 transgenic mouse models generally fail to exhibit significant cytoplasmic accumulation and loss of nuclear TDP-43, which hampers the investigation of cytoplasmic TDP-43 pathology. We previously discovered that primate-specific caspase-4 (CASP4) can cleave TDP-43, producing truncated fragments that are mislocalized to the cytoplasm. Here we show that a transgenic mouse model that expresses human CASP4 and recapitulates the cytoplasmic mislocalization of endogenous TDP-43 and motor dysfunction in an age-dependent manner. Moreover, CASP4 mice exhibited gene expression changes and neuropathology similar to patients with sporadic ALS. Inhibition of CASP4 by its antisense oligonucleotide ameliorated TDP-43 pathology and subsequent neurotoxicity in CASP4 mice. Thus, CASP4 mice present a valuable animal model for exploring endogenous TDP-43-mediated pathogenesis and therapeutics.Subject terms: Molecular neuroscience, Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

Introduction

TAR DNA/RNA binding protein (TARDBP, TDP-43) was initially identified in the postmortem motor cortex and spinal cord samples from Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) and Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration (FTLD) patients1. Subsequently, it was also detected in individuals with various other neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Lewy body disease (LBD), and Huntington’s disease (HD)2–4. Over 50 mutations in the TARDBP gene linked to ALS have been discovered, providing additional evidence for the central role of TDP-43 in the development of ALS5,6. TDP-43 contains two conserved RNA recognition motifs, RRM1 and RRM2, which are crucial for its nucleic acid-binding capability. These motifs enable TDP-43 to regulate various functions related to gene transcription and RNA processing within the nucleus7,8. When TDP-43 is shuttled between the nucleus and cytoplasm, cytoplasmic cleavage of TDP-43 can generate a truncated TDP-43 that lacks the nuclear localization signal (NLS) and remains in the cytoplasm. Typical TDP-43 pathology is characterized by the abnormal formation of TDP-43 cytosolic aggregations concurrent with the loss of nuclear TDP-43, which led to the hypotheses that both gain-of-function of cytoplasmic TDP-43 and loss-of-function of nuclear TDP-43 contribute to the pathogenic outcomes9,10.

Current studies mainly focus on the impact of loss of normal TDP-43 or overexpression of mutant TDP-43 in cellular and rodent models, but the influences of the cytoplasmic accumulation of endogenous TDP-43 remain to be fully understood. This is partly due to the fact that the current mouse models, though valuable for understanding certain pathological events and mechanisms, are unable to fully recapitulate pathological changes observed in human brains. For instance, ALS patient brains exhibit clear cytoplasmic TDP-43 inclusions1,11, but in most transgenic rodent models expressing either wild-type or mutant human TDP-43, human TDP-43 is predominantly present in the mouse nucleus6,12–16 or displays minimal levels in the cytoplasm15,17,18. However, in the monkey19–21 or pig22 brains expressing mutant TDP-43, its cytoplasmic localization and C-terminal truncation products could be observed, resembling the characteristics seen in patient brains. Our prior study revealed that the primate-specific caspase-4, but not the mouse homolog caspase-11, could remove the NLS-containing N-terminal domain in TDP-43, leading to the accumulation of fragmented TDP-43 in the cytoplasm19.

For a more thorough comprehension of the implications of cytoplasmic TDP-43 accumulation alongside nuclear TDP-43 loss, it is essential to establish a mouse model that manifests both cytoplasmic TDP-43 aggregation and nuclear TDP-43 reduction. In this study, we have successfully developed a mouse model expressing a single copy of the human caspase-4 gene (CASP4), capable of recapitulating the cleavage and translocation of endogenous TDP-43 in the prefrontal cortex—a region vulnerable in human patients. Furthermore, CASP4 mice displayed characteristic neuropathological features such as neuronal impairment and reactive gliosis, alongside age-related motor dysfunction and muscle atrophy. Additionally, the transcriptomic changes observed in CASP4 mice partially overlapped with those in sporadic ALS patients and were notably distinct from those in age-matched control mice. Importantly, the inhibition of caspase-4 through its specific antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) mitigated TDP-43 pathology and neurotoxicity in CASP4 mice. Hence, CASP4 mice offer a valuable animal model for studying the age-dependent neuropathology and pathogenic mechanisms stemming from both the cytoplasmic aggregation and nuclear depletion of endogenous TDP-43, as well as for identifying new therapeutic targets.

Results

Generation of CASP4 transgenic mouse model

To generate a transgenic mouse model expressing human caspase-4, the human caspase-4 cDNA, which encodes a Flag tag at the C-terminus and is expressed under the control of CAG promoter, was knocked into the mouse ROSA26 locus via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated DNA targeting. To achieve cell type-specific restriction of caspase-4 expression, the transgenic human caspase-4 construct is flanked by loxP sites so that its expression can be turned on by Cre expression. (Supplementary Fig. 1a). The transgene DNAs were injected into the fertilized mouse eggs to generate the F0 founders that were identified by DNA sequencing analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1b). The founders were mated with wild type mice to generate caspase-4flox/+ F1 animals, which were confirmed by Southern blotting and then bred with Nestin-Cre mice to generate the caspase-4flox/+ with Nestin-Cre positive mice (CASP4 mice), and with Nestin-Cre negative mice (caspase-4flox/+) that served as control (Ctrl) mice (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

Western blotting analyses with anti-CASP4 or anti-Flag antibodies confirmed the expression of caspase-4, accompanied by increased truncated TDP-43 (35 kDa), in the prefrontal cortex of CASP4 mice at 3 months of age when compared with its littermates of other genotypes (Fig. 1a, b). Immunostaining with anti-Flag provided clear evidence that the transgenic caspase-4 was widely expressed in different mouse brain regions (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Western blotting confirmed the widespread distribution of transgenic caspase-4 in different brain regions of CASP4 mice but not in the control littermate mouse brain (Fig. 1c). Consistently, caspase-4 cleaved the mouse endogenous TDP-43 in different brain regions, resulting in TDP-43 fragments of 35 kDa and 25 kDa, with 35 kDa fragment being more stable than 25 kDa fragment. Consistently, the increased amounts of truncated TDP-43 were dependent on caspase-4 activation, which is reflected by its proteolysis, in the mouse prefrontal cortex and cerebellum (Fig. 1c–e).

To determine the subcellular localization of cleaved TDP-43, we performed immunohistochemistry staining of the brains of CASP4 mice at 6-7 months of age using the C-terminal TDP-43 antibodies. The result revealed an obvious increase of cytoplasmic TDP-43, accompanied by the nuclear loss of TDP-43 in the prefrontal cortex region of CASP4 mice as compared with control mice (Fig. 1f). Double immunostaining clearly showed that cells expressing transgenic caspase-4 exhibited the cytoplasmic TDP-43 accompanied by the loss of nuclear TDP-43 in the prefrontal cortex (Fig. 1g) and spinal cord (Supplementary Fig. 2). Quantification of cells exhibiting cytoplasmic TDP-43 indicated that CASP4 mice contained a greater number of such cells as compared with the control mouse brains (Fig. 1h). These results demonstrate that human caspase-4 could also cleave endogenous mouse TDP-43 and generate truncated mouse TDP-43 that is able to accumulate in the cytoplasm, an important pathologic feature seen in the patient brains.

Age-dependent phenotypes of CASP4 mice

To assess whether CASP4 mice exhibit the ALS-like phenotypes, we performed a series of motor function tests on CASP4 and control mice at different ages (3-, 6-, and 12-months). We found that starting at 6 months of age, CASP4 mice crossed the beam more slowly than the age-matched control mice, and this motor deficit became more severe at 12 months of age (Fig. 2a, b). Consistently, CASP4 mice also showed decreased performance on the rotarod test, starting from 6 months and progressing to 12 months (Fig. 2c). The deteriorated open field exploratory behavior of CASP4 mice was observed at 12 months of age (Fig. 2d) and confirmed by quantification of the total distance traveled and travel speed (Fig. 2e, f). Since muscle atrophy is an obvious phenotype of ALS patients23, we accessed the EMG recording of biceps after stimulation of the sciatic nerve. The results showed a significant reduction in amplitude and prolonged latency in CASP4 mice at 12 months of age as compared to the age-matched control mice (Fig. 2g–i). We also analyzed the hind limb muscles of the 12-month-old CASP4 mice and observed the obvious skeletal muscle atrophy, as evidenced by the decreased muscle weight and volume (Fig. 2j–m). As a consequence, the grip strength of CASP4 mice also declined in an age-dependent manner (Fig. 2n).

We also examined the gait performance of CASP4 mice using the CatWalk instrument, which automatically recorded each paw print contact in the footprint and time view separately (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Through a visual analysis of the valid labeled paws, the 12-month-old CASP4 mice exhibited a smaller stride length, longer stand time, and increased swing time than the control mice (Supplementary Fig. 3b), though there was no difference between the 3-month-old CASP4 and control mice. Furthermore, the three-dimensional graphs boxed by the dots (Supplementary Fig. 3c) revealed that the maximum intensity or contact area and print area of all paws were reduced in CASP4 mice at 12 months of age, when compared with the age-matched control mice (Supplementary Fig. 3d). Although the CASP4 mice could live up to 12 months with no significant impact on body weight (Fig. 2o), the progressive death was observed starting at 12 months of age (Fig. 2p). Taken together, CASP4 mice develop age-dependent impairments in motor function and other phenotypes.

Age-dependent CASP4 activation and cytoplasmic accumulation of TDP-43

Given that caspase-4 activation is induced by cellular stress, aging, and other pathological insults24–26 and that TDP-43-associated neuropathology is age-dependent27–29, we investigated whether aging could enhance the activation of caspase-4 and the subsequent cytoplasmic accumulation of TDP-43 in CASP4 mice. Western blotting results revealed that caspase-4 cleavage or activition was increased in the aged CASP4 mice (12 months), accompanied by increased production of truncated TDP-43, as compared with young (3 and 6 months) CASP4 mice (Fig. 3a). Truncated TDP-43 fragments in the cytoplasm might exhibit lower stability compared to full-length TDP-43 due to their susceptibility to aggregation or degradation, making them harder to detect by Western blotting. We therefore performed immunofluorescent staining using antibodies targeting the C-terminus of TDP-43 to assess the cytoplasmic distribution of TDP-43. CASP4 mouse brains displayed a significant increase of cytoplasmic TDP-43 and a decrease of nuclear TDP-43 in the prefrontal cortex region in an age-dependent manner (Fig. 3b). However, quantifying the total TDP-43 in the prefrontal cortex did not reveal any obvious differences between control and CASP4 mice (Fig. 3c). The ratios of cleaved caspase-4 to full-length caspase-4 or cleaved TDP-43 to full-length TDP-43 from Western blotting analysis (Fig. 3d), along with the assessment of cytoplasmic TDP-43 positive cells in immunofluorescent staining results (Fig. 3e), confirmed that aging enhances caspase-4 activation and the production of C-terminal truncated TDP-43 in CASP4 mice.

Given the presence of insoluble aggregates of TDP-43 and phosphorylated TDP-43 in the brains of ALS patients, we also found that the insoluble and phosphorylated TDP-43 were markedly increased in the prefrontal cortex of 12-month-old CASP4 mice (Fig. 3f, g). Immunostaining using anti-pTDP-43 validated that the prefrontal cortex of a 12-month-old CASP4 mouse displayed a more prominent staining of phosphorylated TDP-43 compared to a 6-month-old CASP4 mouse (Fig. 3h).

Since the primate brains express endogenous caspase-4, we also investigated whether aging could enhance caspase-4 activity in the monkey brains. We first validated the expression of caspase-4 in neuronal and glial cells using double immunofluorescent staining (Fig. 4a). The Western blot analysis of neuron-enriched gray matter and glia-enriched white matter distinctly indicated that while neuronal caspase-4 was expressed at a lower level compared to glial caspase-4, it exhibited a tendency to be more activated and correlated with increased levels of cleaved TDP-43 in the aged monkeys (Fig. 4b). This was further supported by the quantification of the ratio of cleaved TDP-43 to intact TDP-43 (Fig. 4c). Immunofluorescent staining further verified that the prefrontal cortex of an old (29 years) monkey showed more abundant cytoplasmic TDP-43 than that of a young (11 years) monkey (Fig. 4d), and the percentage of cells with cytoplasmic TDP-43 (Fig. 4e) also suggested that aging increases the cytoplasmic accumulation of TDP-43.

Our early studies using Western blotting revealed that caspase-4 is upregulated in ALS patient brains19. In the current studies, we were able to acquire fixed postmortem brain tissues from three sporadic ALS (sALS) patients and three normal individuals (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Immunostaining of these brain tissues also revealed the increased levels of caspase-4 and cytoplasmic TDP-43 in sALS patient brains (Fig. 4f, g and Supplementary Fig. 4b). Similar to the monkey brains, caspase-4 is expressed in neurons in the gray matter and glial cells in the white matter of the brains from sALS patients (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Notably, these results are consistent with the findings that CASP4 is up-regulated in ALS30,31 and activated by ER stress19,32–34.

Age-dependent neurodegeneration in CASP4 mice

To explore whether CASP4 mice can recapitulate age-dependent neurodegeneration seen in TDP-43 associated pathological conditions, we examined symptomatic CASP4 mice at different ages. Western blotting analysis of the prefrontal cortex of 3-, 6- and 12-month-old CASP4 mice showed a significant reduction of neuronal marker proteins NeuN and MAP2 in 12-month-old CASP4 mice (Fig. 5a, b). The neurodegeneration was also evidenced by a reduction in the number of ChAT positive cells (Fig. 5c, e) and we also found significant neuronal degeneration in the spinal cord anterior horn (Fig. 5d, f). Furthermore, Golgi staining showed a significant reduction in the number of neurons (Fig. 5g, h) and decreased neuronal cell number (Supplementary Fig. 5a) in 12-month-old CASP4 mice. Nissl staining further validated the decreased number of neuronal cell bodies in the prefrontal cortex of CASP4 mice (Supplementary Fig. 5b), while TUNEL staining demonstrated an increase in neuronal apoptosis in the prefrontal cortex of CASP4 mice (Supplementary Fig. 5c). It is known that reactive gliosis with increased GFAP35 and Iba136,37 the astrocytic and microglial markers, are additional pathological events in ALS patients. Immunostaining analysis also showed that there was an increase of GFAP (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 6a, b) as well as the microglial marker Iba1 (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 6c, d) in the 12-month-old CASP4 mouse prefrontal cortex. The neurodegeneration in the prefrontal cortex of 12-month-old CASP4 mice was confirmed by electron microscopy (EM), revealing dark and characteristic degeneration profiles of neurons, swollen astrocytes, and the presence of neighboring microglia alongside motor neurons, as well as the axon degeneration (Fig. 5i, j). Consistently, Western blotting analysis also showed a reduction of several myelin structural proteins (MOG, MBP, and CNP) in 12-month-old CASP4 mice (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). Given that CASP4 mice displayed muscle atrophy, a characteristic ALS phenotype (Fig. 2), this was likely due to the impaired nerve signal transmission caused by axonal degeneration. To test this, we analyzed neuromuscular junctions (NMJs) using anti-AChR to mark postsynaptic sites, anti-NF for motor axons, and anti-synaptophysin for presynaptic nerve terminal, and observed the severe morphological disruptions in the NMJs of CASP4 mice compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 6g). Quantification of the innervation rate of NMJs in the gastrocnemius and femoral muscle confirmed denervation, with 25% showing full denervation and 28% exhibiting partial denervation of NMJs, in 12-month-old CASP4 mice (Supplementary Fig. 6h).

Gene expression alterations in CASP4 mice

The cytoplasmic aggregation of TDP-43, coupled with its nuclear clearance, impacts its role in gene transcription regulation8,38,39 and various cellular functions7,11. Consequently, we conducted bulk RNA sequencing on prefrontal cortex tissues from CASP4 mice and control littermates. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation between genotypes along the first principal component (PC1) (Supplementary Fig. 7a). To assess the transcriptional features relevance of our model, we compared these results to an existing dataset from the prefrontal cortex of sporadic ALS (sALS) patients and neurologically normal controls (GEO: GSE67196)40; relevant clinical and neuropathological information is provided (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Scatter plot analysis identified 592 up-regulated and 1,040 down-regulated genes in sALS patients, compared to 1,410 up-regulated and 1,965 down-regulated genes in CASP4 mice (Fig. 6a). The more pronounced differential expression in the mice is likely attributable to their uniform genetic background, age, and environment, which minimizes confounding variables and more clearly reveals the effect of abnormal TDP-43 distribution on the transcriptome. We also conducted a two-way comparison between CASP4 and sALS patients, and observed the partial differential expressed genes (DEGs) in both datasets were either upregulated or downregulated in the same way (Supplementary Fig. 7c). However, this CASP4 mouse model cannot exhibit the entire changed genes as ALS patients, because of the genomic difference between humans and mice, for instance, the numerous alternative splicing patterns exhibited non-conservation across species41–43.

Next, we identified the DEGs by comparing sALS patients to healthy controls and separately by comparing CASP4 transgenic mice to their controls. A Venn diagram analysis revealed an intersection of 183 common genes between these two DEG lists (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Hierarchical clustering of these DEGs demonstrated a comparable transcriptomic profile between the prefrontal cortex of 12-month-old CASP4 mice and sALS patients, with numerous genes showing similar expression patterns (Fig. 6b). We also analyzed an independent cohort from the NYGC, comprising 108 individuals (25 healthy controls and 83 sALS patients), and found a similar DEG profile (Supplementary Fig. 7e). KEGG enrichment analysis of the common DEGs showed significant enrichment in pathways related to energy metabolism imbalance (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation, PPAR signaling pathway, thermogenesis, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis) and cell death (e.g., ferroptosis, necroptosis, and the p53 signaling pathway) (Fig. 6c). Consistently, GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment for similar functions, including ribosome assembly and structural activity, in both sALS patients and CASP4 mice (Supplementary Fig. 7f).

To functionally characterize the transcriptional changes in the prefrontal cortex driven by caspase-4, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on DEGs from 12-month-old CASP4 mice compared to controls. The analysis revealed significant enrichment in three major categories: (1) pathways crucial for neural health and synaptic function, including neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, cholinergic synapse, and axon guidance; (2) cell death pathways, such as apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis; and (3) immune and inflammatory response pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, Toll-like receptor signaling, and NF-kappa B signaling (Supplementary Fig. 8a).

To further investigate the molecular alterations associated with the age-dependent progression of endogenous TDP-43 pathology, we conducted bulk RNA sequencing on prefrontal cortex tissues of 3-, 6-, and 12-month-old CASP4 mice as well as their corresponding control littermates. PCA revealed a clear separation of 12-month-old CASP4 mice from all other groups, including 3-month-old and 6-month-old mice of both genotypes and even 12-month-old control mice (Supplementary Fig. 8b). The volcano plot illustrates the varying numbers of up-regulated and down-regulated genes in the distinct comparisons among 3-, 6-, and 12-month-old CASP4 mice (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 8c). Interestingly, during aging, 264 DEGs were commonly altered in both CASP4 and control mice, while 872 DEGs were specifically altered in the prefrontal cortex of CASP4 mice (Fig. 6e). To investigate the systemic transcriptional impact of caspase-4 and aging, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) on samples from 3-, 6-, and 12-month-old mice. Hierarchical clustering of 23,965 genes based on topological overlap identified 14 distinct co-expression modules (Fig. 6f).

We calculated a module eigengene (ME) to represent the aggregate expression profile of each co-expression module and correlated each ME with key sample traits (genotype and age) using Pearson correlation (Fig. 6f). Four modules (turquoise, magenta, purple, and grey) demonstrating a significant correlation with either CASP4 genotype or aging (absolute correlation >0.5, P value < 0.01) were selected for further analysis. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed the biological functions associated with these modules (Fig. 6g). The turquoise module, for instance, was significantly enriched for pathways including axon guidance, MAPK signaling, and glutamatergic synapse, as well as processes such as ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis and autophagy. Notably, the expression of the turquoise module—enriched for these neuronal and proteostasis functions—exhibited a significant negative correlation specifically with aged CASP4 mice, but not with aged control mice (Fig. 6f). This indicates a pronounced downregulation of genes critical for neuronal structure, function, and protein degradation in aged CASP4 mice. Conversely, the magenta module was enriched for terms associated with neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease) and ribosome-related functions (Fig. 6g).

Importantly, analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed region- and age-specific effects. The prefrontal cortex (PFC) of 12-month-old CASP4 mice contained significantly more DEGs than the PFC of younger CASP4 mice or than the hippocampus and striatum of aged mice (Supplementary Fig. 9a). This pronounced transcriptional change in the aged PFC correlates with its more severe cytoplasmic mislocalization of TDP-43 (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 9b).

As previously reported, TDP-43 suppresses cryptic exons during RNA splicing, and its loss leads to inclusion of these exons within susceptible genes44. We therefore analyzed the types, number, and false discovery rate (FDR) of alternative splicing (AS) events in CASP4 mice (Fig. 7a–c and Supplementary Fig. 8d), the results showed AS events increased with age in CASP4 mice, which was due to increased TDP-43 pathology. We assessed AS in known TDP-43 splicing targets, including Unc13a, Stmn2, and Poldip3 and others38,45–49. Specifically, we observed a 319-bp cryptic exon between canonical exons 6 and 7 in Unc13a (GRCm38, chr8:71662873–71662554) and exon 3 skipping in Poldip3 transcripts in CASP4 mice (Fig. 7d).

Using RT-semi-PCR, we validated the presence of the Unc13a cryptic exon and Poldip3 exon skipping in the cortex of aged CASP4 mice compared to controls (Fig. 7e). Consistent with increased cryptic exon inclusion and exon skipping, the levels of canonical Unc13a and Poldip3 transcripts were reduced in 12-month-old CASP4 mice (Fig. 7f). These findings indicate that specific TDP-43-dependent splicing events—such as those affecting Unc13a and Poldip3—are conserved across species, as previously shown in TDP-43-depleted mouse neural progenitor cells and human ALS/FTD brain samples50.

However, in CASP4 mice, we did not detect the same AS events at the Stmn2 and Kcnq2 loci that are observed in ALS patients (Supplementary Fig. 8e), this partial overlap is consistent with known species-specific differences in splicing regulation41–43. Notably, the TDP-43 binding motif “UG/AG” was enriched at AS sites in these altered genes of Unc13a, Poldip3, Sort1 and Elp1 (Fig. 7g). Furthermore, the transcriptional levels of these genes decreased with age (Fig. 7h), aligning with splicing changes reported by Ling et al.44 (Supplementary Tab. 1) and Fratta et al.51 (Supplementary Tab. 2).

It should be noted that the extent of cryptic splicing in CASP4 mice is not as pronounced as that induced by Cre-mediated TDP-43 depletion in mouse embryonic stem (mES) cells44. It is possible that the complete absence of TDP-43 in mES cells leads to more substantial cryptic splicing compared to the partial loss of TDP-43 in adult mouse brains. Nevertheless, aged CASP4 mice exhibit a transcriptional profile that partially converges with molecular features of ALS, indicating a unique aging-associated dysregulation of genes implicated in neurodegeneration. Using a photo-crosslinking assay in HEK293 cells to covalently capture putative CASP4 substrates, Zhou et al. identified several candidates—including TAF15, EIF5, EXOSC9, TRIT1, AKT1, NCK2, ARFGAP2, and PDIA4—that are cleaved by CASP4 in human cells52. To investigate whether transgenic human caspase-4 can also cleave these endogenous substrates in the mouse brain, we performed a series of Western blotting assays using commercially available antibodies. The results indicated that, among the substrates tested, NCK2, EIF5, PDIA4, and FNTB showed slightly increased cleavage products (Supplementary Fig. 10). These findings suggest that while caspase-4 activation can cleave substrates beyond TDP-43, the cleavage events depend on caspase levels or activation status, as well as species-dependent substrate specificity.

Suppressing caspase-4 ameliorated TDP-43 pathology in CASP4 mice

To directly test whether the overexpression of human caspase-4 is the causative factor for the observed TDP-43 pathology and neuronal loss in our CASP4 transgenic model, we synthesized the 2’OMe-modified ASOs to target human caspase-4, then delivered them into the prefrontal cortex of 6-month-old CASP4 mice using Alzet Pump infusion for 4 weeks. Meanwhile, the age-matched CASP4 mice were treated with a DEPC solution containing non-target ASO (NT) for the same duration. Immunofluorescence staining with the Flag and TDP-43 antibodies revealed that suppression of the caspase-4 transgene significantly reduced the mislocalization of endogenous TDP-43, compared to NT-treated controls (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that the use of caspase-4 ASOs efficiently inhibited the expression of transgenic caspase-4, leading to reduced production of the 35 kDa fragment of endogenous TDP-43, in comparison to the non-targeted mice (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c). Importantly, the decline in MAP2 and NeuN intensity observed in CASP4 mouse brains was effectively reversed upon caspase-4 suppression (Supplementary Fig. 11d, e). This ameliorative impact was corroborated by immunofluorescence staining, revealing increased levels of MAP2 and NeuN following treatment with caspase-4-suppressing ASOs (Supplementary Fig. 11f, g). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the pathological features in CASP4 mice rely on the continued expression of the human caspase-4 transgene. This genetic approach substantiates the central contribution of caspase-4 to TDP-43 pathology and neuronal impairment in this model.

Discussion

The cytoplasmic accumulation and aggregation of TDP-43 represent key pathological hallmarks in ALS and other neurodegenerative diseases1–4. Interestingly, only <5% of ALS patients harbor TARDBP mutations11, suggesting that cytoplasmic accumulation occurs independently of genetic mutation. While most mouse models expressing mutant TDP-43 display predominantly nuclear TDP-43 with minimal cytoplasmic localization6,12–18, transgenic pigs and non-human primates expressing mutant TDP-43 exhibit cytoplasmic TDP-43 accumulation similar to patient brains19–22. This indicates that significant cytoplasmic accumulation of TDP-43 in large animal brains may be species-dependent.

Using non-human primate models, we identified caspase-4 as a primate-specific enzyme that cleaves TDP-43, generating truncated fragments capable of translocating from the nucleus to the cytoplasm19. This finding aligns with recent studies showing that human caspase-4 has a broader substrate range than its murine ortholog caspase-1153,54. Given this species-specific difference, we generated CASP4 mice expressing human caspase-4 to test its role in promoting TDP-43 mislocalization.

The transgenic expression of caspase-4 in mouse brains produced truncated TDP-43 that translocated to the cytoplasm. This cytoplasmic TDP-43 accumulation reduced nuclear TDP-43 levels and altered gene transcription. Consequently, CASP4 mice developed progressive neuropathological and behavioral deficits. Crucially, inhibition of caspase-4 by ASOs alleviated both neuropathology and cytoplasmic TDP-43 accumulation.

The age-dependent phenotypes in CASP4 mice reflect the age-related progression of TDP-43-linked neurological disorders. Caspase-4 activation is known to be enhanced by oxidative stress and toxic aggregates25, factors that increase during aging24. Additionally, the efficiency of protein clearance systems, such as the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway and autophagy, declines with age55. These age-associated changes collectively contribute to the phenotypes observed in CASP4 mice.

The neuropathology of TDP-43 involves at least two mechanisms: the loss of its normal nuclear splicing and regulatory functions, and a concurrent gain of toxic function in the cytoplasm9,10. Although mouse and zebrafish models expressing NLS-deleted mutant TDP-43 also show cytoplasmic TDP-43 localization56,57, our CASP4 model is distinct. It recapitulates both nuclear loss and cytoplasmic accumulation of endogenous TDP-43, mirroring key pathological features seen in patient brains. This underscores the importance of caspase-4-mediated cleavage in driving TDP-43 mislocalization. Prior studies have emphasized the importance of TDP-43 levels in neuropathology, as nuclear TDP-43 is essential for gene transcription and RNA splicing6,7. Simply suppressing endogenous TDP-43 mimics nuclear loss but not cytoplasmic accumulation, whereas overexpression may induce artifactual pathologies not seen in human brains. The CASP4 mouse model is therefore a valuable tool for studying disease mechanisms arising from its altered subcellular localization.

However, several limitations should be considered when translating findings from this model to human disease. First, key TDP-43-mediated splicing events and downstream pathways may not be fully conserved between mice and humans42. For instance, cryptic splicing in CASP4 mice is less pronounced than in human cell models of complete TDP-43 depletion, such as Cre-mediated knockout in embryonic stem cells44 or in TDP-43-deficient neurons isolated from post-mortem FTLD/ALS tissue58. Second, nuclear TDP-43 regulates gene expression through multiple mechanisms, including RNA splicing, transcriptional modulation, RNA stability, transport, translation, and DNA repair59,60. Transcriptional and splicing alterations in disease likely result from the combined effects of diminished nuclear TDP-43 function and potential interference from cytoplasmic TDP-43 aggregates, which have themselves been shown to influence gene expression51,61–63.

Additionally, we cannot entirely exclude the potential pleiotropic effects of caspase-4. As an inflammatory caspase with a broad substrate range, it may cleave multiple targets beyond TDP-43. Because mice do not naturally express caspase-4, it remains plausible that the cleavage of other non-TDP-43 substrates in the brain may synergistically contribute to neuronal dysfunction alongside TDP-43 pathology. Nevertheless, the pronounced nuclear loss and cytoplasmic accumulation of TDP-43 in CASP4 mice validate TDP-43 as an important substrate of caspase-4. Future studies are warranted to fully delineate the distinct and overlapping roles of these potential substrates, making the CASP4 model a continuing platform for exploring the complex interplay between inflammatory proteases and neurodegeneration.

Methods

Human samples

Adults with ALS were enrolled at the Peking University Third Hospital and Xiangya Hospital of Central South University in China. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The human brain specimens were obtained from the postmortem brains from the Brain Bank of Xiangya School of Medicine through the willed body donation program, and assessed for optimal histological integrity following a standard protocol proposed by the China Human Brain Banking Consortium64,65. This study was also approved by the Ethics Committee of the Peking University Third Hospital (Approval No. IRB00006761-M20250174). The use of postmortem human brains was approved by Institutional Ethics Committees, in compliance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki). Information about the samples is shown in Supplementary Fig. 4a.

Animals and ethics statement

Mice were housed in the Division of Animal Resources at Jinan University on a 12-h light/dark cycle and had free access to water and normal rodent chow. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jinan University (Approval No. IACUC-20220905-07). The wild type (WT) cynomolgus monkeys (M. fascicularis) were housed in cages at Guangdong Landau Biotechnology Co. Ltd., which is an Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care-accredited facility. Monkeys were housed individually in stainless steel cages under a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 AM) at a controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and relative humidity (50–60%). Drinking water was provided ad libitum via automatic watering system. The commercial monkey diet (Ke-Ao, #HFZ-15kg, Beijing) was offered twice daily. Environmental enrichment (including hanging mirrors, chew toys, and foraging devices) was provided. Health status was monitored daily by veterinarians. All animal-related protocols were approved in advance by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangdong Landau Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Approval No: LDACU 20201216-02). This study was carried out in strict compliance with the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Institute of Laboratory Animal Science (est. 2006)” and “the Use of Nonhuman Primates in Research of the Institute of Laboratory Animal Science (est. 2006)” to ensure the safety of personnel and animal welfare. The monkeys used in the experiments were aged 8-11 years and 29-30 years.

Generation of Human caspase-4 transgenic mice

C57BL/6J-caspase-4-transgenic mice were created via CRISPR-Cas9 technology. The gRNA to mouse ROSA26 gene (gRNA matching forward strand of gene: CTCCAGTCTTTCTAGAAGATGGG), the donor vector containing “CAG promoter-loxP-PGK-Neo-6*SV40 pA-loxP-Kozak-Human CASP4 CDS-3× FLAG tag-rBG pA” cassette, and Cas9 mRNA were co-injected into fertilized mouse eggs to generate targeted conditional knock-in offspring. A founder mouse was backcrossed twice to C57BL/6 J, establishing the stock C57BL/6J-caspase-4 (here hcaspase-4flox/+). To assess the role of caspase-4 in the brain, hcaspase-4flox/+mice were crossed with Nestin-Cre mice to generate hcaspase-4flox/+ with Nestin-Cre positive mice (CASP4 mice), and with Nestin-Cre negative mice (hcaspase-4flox/+) that served as control mice. All F1 progeny were genotyped at birth and enrolled in the survival study. All the cohorts were randomly selected from age-matched litters. All animals were tested at 3, 6, and 12 months for rotarod, grip strength, balance beam, and electromyography at 12 months, with technicians blinded to genotypes.

Mouse genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from mouse tail samples for genotyping using PCR with specific primers to identify transgenic mice. The primers utilized as follows:

Caspase-4-F-1: TCTACCTCTTTCCTGGCAATGACTACA

Caspase-4-R-1: CTTTATTAGCCAGAAGTCAGATGC

Caspase-4-F-2: CACTTGCTCTCCCAAAGTCGCTC

Caspase-4-R-2: ATACTCCGAGGCGGATCACAA

Nestin-M-F: CCTTCCTGAAGCAGTAGAGCA

Nestin-M-R: GCCTTATTGTGGAAGGACTG

Nestin-W-F: TTGCTAAAGCGCTACATAGGA

Rotarod performance and open field testing

Motor coordination was evaluated using an accelerating rotarod system (Rotamex; Columbus Instruments). Mice were acclimated to the testing room for 60 min, and mice underwent three consecutive days of training (days 1-3). A formal test on day 4, mice were placed on a rotating rod at 4 r.p.m., accelerating up to 40 r.p.m. over a 300 sec period. Each animal completed three consecutive trials separated by 60 sec rest intervals. Fall latency was recorded for each trial. The mean latency to fall of all three trials was calculated and reported. An open-field test was also employed to detect the motor ability of mice. Mice were placed in the arena (50 × 50 × 50 cm) and allowed to move freely for 300 sec. The open field test was conducted when the mice reached 12 months of age. The total distances and mean speed in the zone were collected using EthoVision XT.

Balance Beam

A balance beam test was employed to investigate the balance ability of mice at 3, 6, and 12 months. Mice were placed on the balance beam (100 cm × 12 mm) and the time spent on the balance beam was recorded. A minimum of 8 mice were analyzed per group.

Grip strength measurement

The limb grip strength was assessed using a grip strength meter (Ugo Basile, Italy). Animals were placed on a grid attachment for the instrument and gently pulled back with steady force until all paws released the grid attachment. The peak tension of all four paws was recorded. The average strength of three consecutive trials was analyzed.

CatWalk gait analysis

Gait analysis was conducted using the CatWalk™ system from Noldus, The Netherlands. Mice walked along an enclosed walkway, and images of their footprints were captured for analysis. The criteria for data collection included completing one walk within a time frame of 0.5 to 5 sec and maintaining a walking speed variation of less than 60%.

Electromyogram (EMG) recording

Under propofol anesthesia, the sciatic nerve and biceps were exposed under a stereomicroscope. A recording electrode was carefully inserted into the biceps muscle (posterior third, 0.2 mm in depth), while a stimulation of 0.3 mA with a duration of 0.5 ms was applied to the sciatic nerve using a monopolar stimulation electrode. The EMG signals were captured using a multi-channel system (VikingQuest EMG/EP System, Nicolet, USA). Each animal underwent the recording process at least 3 times with a 3-min interval between repetitions, and the averaged result was reported.

Total RNA extraction and qPCR or RT-PCR

Frozen or fresh mouse brain tissue was processed using TRIzol (1 ml for 50-100 mg tissue) for purification before homogenization. RNA quantification was performed using a Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific), and 500 ng of RNA was used for cDNA synthesis with the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit. Quantitative PCR was conducted with 25 ng of cDNA input in a 20 µl reaction using 2× SYBR Green PCR Master Mix. The relative fold gene expression of samples was calculated using the 2∆∆Ct method for analysis, with GAPDH serving as a control for qPCR. Prior to analysis with GraphPad Prism 8, the PCR results were normalized by GAPDH levels. The qPCR primers for the target genes used in this study are as follows:

Unc13a-qF: ACGGTGCCCAAGAGAAGT

Unc13a-qR: AGCGGGATCCACACAGT

Poldip3-qF: TGAACTCACGAAAGCAGCAG

Poldip3-qR: AGCAGGATGGGCATGAAGT

RT-semi-PCR was performed using TaKaRa PCR Amplification Kit in 20 µl reaction volumes containing 12.5 ng of template cDNA, with the following cycling parameters: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 sec, 56 °C for 30 sec, 72 °C for 45 s; followed by a final extension: 72 °C for 2 min. The amplification products were visualized on a 1.5% TAE gel. The RT-semi-PCR primers for the target genes used in this study are as follows:

Unc13a-F: CATCCCTGAGGAGGAGGCA

Unc13a-R: TGCTGGGCACTGGCA

Poldip3-F: TGAACTCACGAAAGCAGCAG

Poldip3-R: CAAGGCTTTGGTTCGAACAG

RNA-seq analysis

Prefrontal cortex samples from both Ctrl and CASP4 mice were isolated for the bulk RNA-Seq analysis. Total RNA extraction was conducted using TRIzol Reagent. The cDNA library construction and sequencing procedures were performed by Shanghai Sangon Company. Sequencing was carried out using the Hieff NGS™ MaxUp Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina®, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. After cluster generation, sequencing was performed, generating 150 bp paired-end reads on the Illumina platform. FastQC was used to evaluate the quality of raw sequenced data. Trimmomatic was used to analyze the raw RNA-seq data and trim sequencing adapters. Subsequently, HISAT2 was employed to map the clean data to the mouse genome (GRCm38.89) sourced from the Ensembl website, generating sam files. SAMtools was then used to convert the sam files into bam files. StringTie was used for gene quantification and conversion into a read count matrix. The quality of the alignment results was assessed using RSeQC, which provides comprehensive statistical analyses for RNA-Seq data. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the R package DESeq2, with the read count matrix serving as the input file. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on a fold change > 1.5, and an adjusted P value < 0.05. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis for the differentially expressed genes within a group was conducted using the clusterProfiler. KEGG pathways with false discovery rate (qvalue) <0.05 were considered significantly altered. The alternative splicing (AS) event was analyzed to classify by ASprofier, based on the gene model predicted for each sample.

WGCNA analysis

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed on log2 (TPM + 1) transformed data. Low-expression genes were filtered to reduce noise. An unsigned network was constructed using a soft thresholding power of 13 to achieve a scale-free topology (R2 > 0.8). Modules were identified using average linkage hierarchical clustering of topological overlap matrices, with a minimum module size of 30 genes and a merge cut height of 0.25. Modules with eigengene correlations ≥0.75 were merged. Module eigengenes (MEs) were calculated and correlated with experimental traits; associations with a Pearson correlation P < 0.05 were deemed significant. Disease-associated modules were subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package.

Tissue collection

Mice were killed with Isoflurane and perfused with 15 ml ice-cold 0.9% NaCl. Mouse tissues, including the brain, spinal cord, and muscle tissue were collected. The cervical spinal cord was divided into two pieces of equal size. One piece of the spinal cord tissue and the left brain of the mouse were postfixed in freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, and the left leg muscle tissue of the mice was postfixed for 72 h. The other piece of the spinal cord tissue, the right brain and right leg muscle tissue, were quickly frozen in dry ice. Samples for immunofluorescence and Immunohistochemistry were immersed in 30% sucrose buffer for 72 h after their postfixation period, and they were sectioned into slices of the brain and spinal cords (20 µm thickness), or muscles (30 µm thickness) using a cryostat microtome (CM1950, Leica) at −20 °C and mounted on gelatin-coated slides. For electron microscopy, mice were killed with isoflurane, perfused with 15 ml of ice-cold 0.9% NaCl, and the prefrontal cortex tissue was fixed in freshly prepared fixative (2% PFA, 2% glutaraldehyde, 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.2) until processing.

Immunoblotting

Mouse prefrontal cortex was homogenized in ice-cold RIPA Buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and clarified extracts were harvested by sonication and centrifugation. Equal amount of protein was loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk, and then incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and immunoreactive signals were detected using the ECL Prime kit.

Immunofluorescence, immunohistochemistry staining and electron microscopy

For immunofluorescence staining, the slides were washed three times in wash buffer (1× PBS) and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS at room temperature for 1 h, followed by a 1-h blocking step with 3% BSA/PBS. Subsequent incubation with primary antibodies (in blocking solution of 3% BSA, 2% donkey serum, and 1× TBST) overnight at 4 °C. Samples were washed three times with wash buffer and incubated with Alexa 647/555/488-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in blocking solution for 1 h at room temperature, followed by DAPI nuclear dye for 10 min. Muscle slides were labeled with 555-α-bungarotoxin (1:500) for 2 h at room temperature. Imaging was conducted using Olympus confocal microscope equipped with a digital camera and FV3000 software.

For immunohistochemistry staining, the slides were washed three times in wash buffer (1× PBS) and 3% hydrogen peroxide was used to remove endogenous peroxidase in the tissue. Then, the tissue sections underwent antigen retrieval treatment with citric acid buffer (10 mM citric acid, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6.0) in a 95 °C water bath for 15 min and were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS at room temperature for 1 h. Following this, the tissue sections were blocked with 0.1% TritonX-100/2% normal goat serum and 3% BSA/1× PBS for 30 min. Incubation with the primary antibody (TDP-43, 1:2000) overnight at 4 °C. An HRP/DAB Detection IHC kit and DAB kit were used for immunodetection. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss microscope (AXIO Imager.A2) with a digital camera (Axiocam 506 color ZEISS) and ZEN 2.3 software. EM study of mice’s prefrontal cortex was performed by Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Axon and myelin fiber diameters were measured using ImageJ. The G-ratio was calculated as the ratio of the inner axon diameter (r) to the total outer fiber diameter (R), which includes the axon and myelin sheath.

TUNEL and Nissl staining

For TUNEL staining, the tissue sections were fixed with an appropriate fixative and then permeabilized using 0.3% Triton X-100 permeabilization buffer. Subsequently, the TUNEL reaction mixture was prepared according to the manufacturer’s protocol, involving the mixing of TdT enzyme, nucleotide mixture, and reaction buffer. This mixture was used to incubate the tissue sections in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 1 h. Following this, the TUNEL reaction was stopped, and the tissue sections were washed with PBS. Subsequently, they were incubated with DAPI nuclear dye. Images were acquired using an Olympus Confocal microscope equipped with a digital camera and FV3000 software.

For Nissl staining, the tissue sections were rinsed with deionized water and then incubated with Nissl staining solution at 37 °C for 15 min. The Nissl staining solution was then discarded, and the stained tissue sections were placed in 95% ethanol for ~5 sec for dehydration. Subsequently, the stained tissue sections were incubated in fresh 95% ethanol for another 2 min to remove all water content. Following this, the stained tissue sections underwent a transparency treatment with xylene for 5 min. Finally, images were captured using a Zeiss microscope (AXIO Imager.A2) with a digital camera (Axiocam 506 color ZEISS) and ZEN 2.3 software.

Golgi-Cox staining

Golgi-Cox staining was performed using the FD Rapid GolgiStain™ Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, deeply anesthetized mice were perfused, and the harvested brains were rinsed with Milli-Q water before immersion in freshly prepared A/B impregnation solution. The brains were impregnated in this solution at room temperature for 14 days, followed by transfer to solution C for an additional week of storage in the dark at room temperature. After fixation, the brains were gradually frozen in pre-cooled isopentane using dry ice. Coronal sections (100 μm thickness) were then cut on a cryostat (Leica CM1950) at −20 °C. Sections were stained as directed, and images were acquired using an Olympus Confocal microscope.

NMJ innervation evaluation

The innervation status of NMJs in gastrocnemius or femoral muscles was analyzed by Olympus confocal microscopy to determine the degree of colocalization of presynaptic axons (neurofilament-H/synaptophysin double labeling) and postsynaptic α-bungarotoxin (α-BTX, labeling AChRs). When the percentage of the presynaptic staining covering the α-BTX-positive area >50%, the NMJ was defined as innervated; when 20%–50%, it was considered partially denervated, and when <10%, it was considered completely denervated.

Surgical procedure

The ALZET Brain Infusion Kit is utilized for the prolonged administration of ASO drugs over 4 weeks at a constant rate of 0.11 μl/h. The ASOs used are at a concentration of 2OD/100 μl, specifically targeting caspase-4 with the sequence 5’-UGAUGTCAAAGTCAGCUCCA-3’, or a non-target control in DEPC vehicle. Following anesthesia, a small incision is made in the mouse skin between the scapulae to create a pocket by separating the connective tissue, where a small osmotic pump is positioned under the skin. The cannula is then inserted into the right prefrontal cortex of the mouse using consistent stereotaxic coordinates (bregma: AP: +2 mm, ML: +2 mm, DV: +2 mm). The skin incision is closed with sutures. The treatment is completed and the mice have fully recovered from the surgery. Pathological assessments are conducted one month after cannula implantation.

Statistics and reproducibility

For biochemical and histological investigations, a minimum of three mice were included in each group. For immunofluorescent analysis, we assessed the number of cells and signal intensity in about 10 random fields (40×) per sample across three experiments. Three independent experiments are carried out to generate the data depicted in the gel, blot, or micrograph figures used for quantification, with representative findings shown in the figures. Animal behavioral experiments, EMG recordings, and pathological assessments were conducted using properly matched cohorts (age and sex) from independent litters to account for biological variability. All attempts at replication were successful. Statistical significance is assessed using the two-tailed Student’s t-test for comparisons involving two groups or two-tailed multiple unpaired Student’s t-tests and adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm-Sidak method. For analyses involving multiple groups, one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test is employed to establish statistical significance. A P value below 0.05 is deemed statistically significant. The data are presented as the mean ± SEM. All computations are conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software.
