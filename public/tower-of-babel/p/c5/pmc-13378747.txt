Prevalence of HER2 overexpression in gastric adenocarcinoma and its clinicopathological correlation: a prospective study from Bir Hospital, Nepal
Authors: Bikash Chandra Sah, Jainendra Kumar Chaudhary, Sharad Pokhrel, Indra Kumar Jha, Niroj Banepali, Sundar Shrestha, Shree Krishna Shrestha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Gastric adenocarcinoma remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in Nepal. Trastuzumab, in the ToGA trial, has been shown to improve overall survival in HER2-overexpressing patients, with overexpression observed in approximately 22.1%. However, population-level data on HER2 status in Nepalese patients are conspicuously lacking, hindering informed clinical decision-making.Methods:This prospective observational study was conducted at Bir Hospital, Kathmandu, from January 2025 to January 2026. Fifty-eight patients with histologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma, including Siewert type III gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) tumors, were enrolled consecutively. HER2 expression was assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC). An IHC score of 3+ was classified as positive; scores of 0, 1+, and 2+ were designated as negative. Fluorescence in situ hybridization testing was not performed for IHC 2+ cases due to resource constraints. Clinicopathological associations were analyzed using SPSS version 26, with significance set at P < 0.05.Results:The cohort comprised 58 patients (mean age 61.2 ± 12.54 years; range 25–86), with a male predominance of 70.7%. Tumors were predominantly antro-pyloric (62.1%), poorly differentiated (67.2%), and locally advanced (T3/T4 in 91.4%; stage III/IV in 81.0%). HER2 overexpression (IHC 3+) was detected in six patients (10.3%). A statistically significant association was observed between HER2 positivity and advanced T stage (P-value < 0.05), with rates rising progressively from 0% in T2 to 30.8% in T4b tumors. Non-significant patterns favoring HER2 positivity were observed for proximal/GEJ location and male sex. No significant associations were identified with age, BMI, histological grade, distant metastasis, or overall disease stage (all P-values > 0.05).Conclusions:HER2 positivity (10.3%) in this Nepalese single-center series is lower than globally reported rates, likely reflecting the predominance of distal, poorly differentiated tumors in this cohort. Multicenter, larger-scale studies are warranted to validate these findings and refine HER2’s prognostic and predictive role in the Nepalese population.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC), mainly adenocarcinoma, is the fifth most common cancer worldwide[1]. In Nepal, it is the fourth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the fourth most common cause of cancer-related patient mortality[2]. GC is more common in males than in females[2]. GC mostly presents at an advanced stage at the time of diagnosis and has poor outcomes. The prognostic factors for GC include older age, larger tumor size, lymphovascular invasion, and lymph node metastasis[3]. Surgery is the mainstay of treatment, and a combination of chemotherapy regimens improves survival in advanced GC. However, advanced and inoperable disease demands palliative treatment[4]. Despite all treatment modalities, advanced GCs have a high recurrence rate of 30–40%[5]. The prognosis for most patients with GC is poor, with a 5-year survival rate for all patients being less than 30%[6].

Trastuzumab, a human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-targeted monoclonal antibody, has increased overall survival in advanced/metastatic GC in the TOGA trial, with 22.1% found to have a HER2-positive rate[7]. HER2 is a 185-kDa transmembrane protein of the epidermal growth factor receptor family, located on chromosome 17, and functions as a proto-oncogene[8]. Amplification of the HER2 gene and protein overexpression plays a crucial role in proliferation, apoptosis, adhesion, angiogenesis, and aggressiveness of tumors[8,9]. HER2 expression is found in many cancers, such as breast, colon, bladder, ovary, uterine, cervix, gastric, and gastroesophageal (GEJ) cancers[10]. HER2 overexpression in GC IHC was first described in 1986[11]. HER2 positivity in GC ranges from as low as 4% to as high as 53%[12–14] HER2-neu has also emerged as a prognostic factor for poor outcomes, worse survival, and more aggressive disease in GC[15].

The ToGA trial[7] was noteworthy both for its successful introduction of a new therapeutic agent for patients with advanced/metastatic GC and for its contribution to the standardization of HER2 testing (based on a study by Hofmann et al[16] in all stages of GC. However, there is a paucity of Nepalese data on HER2 overexpression in gastric adenocarcinoma; consequently, data on the prognostication of HER2 overexpression and the efficacy of trastuzumab in the Nepalese gastric adenocarcinoma population are also scarce. In view of the above, this study was conducted to evaluate the prevalence of HER2 in GC and to correlate HER2 overexpression with clinicopathological parameters in patients presenting at NAMS, Bir Hospital, Nepal.

Methods

This was a prospective observational cross-sectional study performed in the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology and Medical Oncology, Bir Hospital, National Academy of Medical Sciences (NAMS), Nepal. A total sampling technique was used to collect samples. A total of 58 cases of GC adenocarcinoma were diagnosed either on small biopsies (endoscopic/laparoscopic/laparotomy) or gastrectomy specimens over a span of 13 months, from January 2025 to January 2026, and were included in this study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of NAMS (Reference number: 1256/2081-82).

The study included individuals of any gender over 18 years of age with histologically confirmed locally advanced or metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma. Siewert III gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma was included as GC. Patients below 18 years of age, those with early GC, those who did not give consent, or those with non-traceable HER2 reports were excluded. The primary objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of HER2 overexpression in GC tumor samples. The secondary objective was to assess the association of HER2 status with clinicopathological parameters. Clinical data featuring the patient’s demographic profile, including age, sex, family history, body mass index (BMI), Eastern Cooperative Oncologic Group (ECOG) status, hemoglobin (g/dl), and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA, measured in ng/ml) levels, along with contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) scans of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, assessed the patient’s clinical TNM (depth of tumor penetration, nodal involvement, and features suggestive of metastatic disease) and were taken into consideration. Examination of whether the patient was resectable or metastatic was also conducted.

Pathological parameters, namely tumor location, Borrmann classification, histopathological grading of tumor differentiation, depth of tumor invasion (T stage), nodal involvement, perineural involvement, and lymphovascular involvement, were assessed. The AJCC 8th staging system was used for staging patients based on clinical staging or the surgical specimen histology report.

All patients’ specimens were sent for detailed histopathological examination and immunohistochemistry (IHC) in 10% neutral buffered formalin to the Department of Pathology. Their 10% neutral buffered formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were retrieved. Sections were made, and staining was done using a routine hematoxylin and eosin stain. Suitable cases were selected and presented for IHC for HER-2 receptors using anti-HER-2 antibodies.

HER2 overexpression was determined by an IHC test using an FDA-approved, standardized test kit (Hercep Test Kit™, DakoCytomation Denmark A/S™, Glostrup, Denmark). IHC staining was performed on a fully automated Xmatrx IHC staining machine using poly-L-lysine-coated slides prepared from FFPE tissues. Thin sections of 3–4 microns were mounted on slides. The slides were then placed in the fully automated Xmatrx IHC staining machine and heated at 800°C for 15–20 minutes. Dewaxing was performed in three cycles of 3 minutes each, followed by washing with IHC buffer solution. Antigen retrieval was carried out by applying the micro reagent EZAR 1 (HX0031) for HER2. The slides were heated at 800°C for 20–25 minutes and then washed with IHC buffer. A peroxide block (HX0026) was applied, and the slides were incubated for approximately 10 minutes. A power block (HX0083) was then applied, and the slides were incubated for another 10 minutes. The antibody for HER2 was added, and the slides were heated and incubated at 370°C for 1 hour, followed by washing with IHC buffer and deionized water. The slides were then heated and incubated at 250°C for 40 minutes and washed again with IHC buffer and deionized water. Polymer HRP was added to the slides, which were heated at 300°C and incubated for 30 minutes. DAB working solution was applied, and the slides were incubated for 8–10 minutes, followed by washing with deionized water and IHC buffer. Counterstaining was performed using hematoxylin, and the slides were incubated for 5 minutes before being washed with deionized water and IHC buffer. Mounting was completed using DPX mountant. For the determination of HER2 expression, a semi-quantitative scoring criterion was applied to the sections, and the results were recorded as positive, negative, or equivocal according to the staining patterns(Table 1)[16]. Scores of 3+ were considered positive, while scores of 0 and 1+ were considered negative. HER2 IHC score 2+ was classified as equivocal and required further testing for gene amplification using the fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) method[16]. However, confirmation of cases reported as equivocal (HER2 IHC score 2+) by FISH could not be performed in our study due to financial constraints. Therefore, scores of 2+ were also included as negative for all practical purposes. Overall, HER2 overexpression in gastric and Siewert III GEJ adenocarcinoma was evaluated and further correlated with various clinicopathological features. The impact of trastuzumab on survival in HER2-positive cases was not studied. All these data were recorded in a preset proforma, MS Excel 365. Statistical analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) v.26 software through descriptive statistics. Descriptive analyses included the calculation of mean ± standard deviation, range (minimum and maximum) for continuous variables, and the presentation of patient count along with percentages for categorical variables. All variables were categorized into two groups: HER2 score negative and positive. The difference between the two subgroups was tested using the chi-square/Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the independent T-test for continuous variables. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while a P-value of ~ 0.07–0.08 was considered indicative of trend-level associations.

Results

This was a prospective study; a total of 63 GC patients were selected for this study; however, five patients were excluded (Lymphoma-2; IHC not tested in 3). So, 58 cases were evaluated in this study. The mean patient age was 61.21 ± 12.54 years, with a wide age range from 25 to 86 years. The study group composed of 70.7% and 29.3% male and female, respectively. Three patients (5.2%) had a positive family history of gastric adenocarcinoma. The mean BMI was 19.22 ± 2.97 kg/m2, with values ranging from 13.9 kg/m2 to 28.7 kg/m2, with 51.7% being underweight. Performance status of pt (ECOG): 8.6% (0), 37.9% (1), 31.0% (2), and 22.4% (3). Pathological tumor characterization of the study population was location of tumor: antral/pyloric (36, 62.1%), GEJ (12, 20.7%), cardia (6, 10.3%), and body (4, 6.9%). Tumor morphology (Borrmann): I (5.2%), II (60.3%), III (27.6%), and IV (6.9%). A total of 53.4% (31) cases underwent small tissue (endoscopic or laparoscopic/laparotomy) biopsies, followed by 46.5% (27) gastrectomy specimens for histopathological diagnosis. Of those who underwent surgical resection, 6 (22.2%) had perineural and lymphovascular invasion identified in each. Tumor gradings were poor (39/58, 67.2%), moderate (13, 22.4%), and well (6, 10.3%). T staging revealed predominantly advanced disease: T2 in 5 (8.6%), T3 in 27 (46.5%), T4a in 13 (22.4%), and T4b in 13 (22.4%). Lymph node staging showed N0 in 6 (10.3%), N1 in 6 (10.3%), N2 in 3 (5.2%), and N3 in 12 (20.7%), with positive clinical node based on CECT scan of abdomen and pelvis (cN+) in 31 (53.5%). AJCC 8th staging revealed stage IIa in 5 (8.6%), IIb in 6 (10.3%), III in 23 (39.6%), and IV in 24 (41.4%), confirming most cases as locally advanced or metastatic. Distant metastasis was present in 22 (37.1%). Nine cases were considered inoperable due to ECOG 3/4 (3), triple vessel disease (2), refused surgery (2), lost in follow-up (1), and death during neoadjuvant chemotherapy (1; Table 3).

HER2 status

According to Hofmann’s HER2 GC scoring criteria, strongly positive, i.e., 3+ (6 cases, 10.3%), 2+ or equivocal (8 cases, 13.8%), and 1+ and 0 (44 cases, 75.9%). In this study, HER2 expression was negative (scores 0, 1+, and 2+), which accounted for 89.7% (52 cases; Table 2). Clinicopathological association with HER2 overexpression

HER2-positive (score 3+) showed a statistically significant association with T staging (P = 0.012). HER2 positivity increased markedly with deeper tumor invasion: T2: 0% positive, T3: 3.7% positive, T4a: 7.7% positive, and T4b: 30.8% positive. Trend-level associations appeared for poorer ECOG performance status and clinically node-positive disease (cN+), where HER2-positive cases showed higher proportions with worse ECOG (e.g., 23.1% in ECOG 3) and cN+ (18.5%), proximal/GEJ tumor locations (17–25% positive vs. 5.6% in antral/pyloric), and slightly higher prevalence in males (12.2%) compared to females (5.9%). No statistically significant associations were identified between HER2 positivity and variables such as gender, age, BMI, family history, CEA levels, tumor location, Borrmann classification, tumor Discussion

In the current prospective study from a tertiary center in Kathmandu, Nepal, involving 58 patients with gastric adenocarcinoma, the HER2 overexpression positivity rate (IHC 3+) was 10.3%, which is comparable to rates in other low-resource or South Asian settings but lower than global benchmarks. For instance, the ToGA trial reported 22.1% positivity in screened advanced gastric and GEJ cancers across multinational cohorts, with subgroup variations (23.6% in Europeans and 23.9% in Asians), potentially due to differences in tumor biology, testing criteria, or population genetics[7]. HER2 overexpression rates indicate global averages vary widely, ranging from 4% to 53%[14,17]. Regional studies, like the Korean multicenter analysis[18] found 10.8%, the Indian cohort[6] 17.5%, and the Ugandan analysis[19] likely ~11.76%, which also reflects lower prevalence in African populations, where the HER2 overexpression included score 3+ and equivocal cases without further testing. Kattan et al[20], in a Lebanese multicenter study of 128 newly diagnosed GC patients, reported a 9.4% positivity rate, akin to our findings, possibly attributable to similar advanced-stage presentations and distal tumor predominance. These disparities may arise from sample sizes, ethnic diversities, or HER2 scoring (Hofmann criteria in our study grouping 0/1+/2+ as negative).

Clinicopathologically, our significant association with advanced T staging (P = 0.012, escalating from 0% in T2 to 30.8% in T4b) contrasts with the ToGA trial[7] and meta-analyses by Abrahao-Machado et al[14], which found no link to tumor depth, invasion, or nodal status, though occasional correlations with aggressive features exist. However, this aligns partially with some studies indicating HER2’s role in aggressive local features, such as in the Korean cohort[18], where positivity correlated with proximal/GEJ locations (similar to our trend of 17–25% in proximal/GEJ vs. 5.6% in antropyloric, P = 0.459). Akin to our study, the most common location of adenocarcinomas was in the distal stomach (73.08%). However, it was found that HER2 positivity was higher in tumors located in the proximal stomach, with no statistically significant correlation between tumor location and HER2 positivity[21]. Nadaf et al also divided their cases according to location as GEJ, proximal, and distal stomach, but HER2 positivity was more in tumors located in the GEJ[22].

Borderline trends in our data for poorer ECOG (P ≈ 0.07) and clinical node positive, i.e., cN+ (P ≈ 0.07), partially align with nodal involvement reports in some cohorts but not robustly in the ToGA trial (no nodal association)[7]. No ties to gender, age, BMI, family history, location, Borrmann type, grading, metastasis, invasions, or AJCC stage (P > 0.05) mirror the ToGA trial’s limited correlations, except for GEJ/proximal sites (32.2% vs. 21.4% distal), differing from our trend of higher positivity in proximal/GEJ (17–25% vs. 5.6% antral/pyloric, P = 0.459)[7]. Kattan et al[20] reported no significant associations with age, gender, location, grade, or stage, consistent with our null findings for most variables. Similar to our study, overexpression of HER2 was correlated with GC parameters like male sex, proximally located tumor, advanced stage, lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, and poorly differentiated tumor[23]. Thakur et al[6] link higher HER2 overexpression to males (OR 1.42).

There were several limitations to our study: firstly, it was conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample size. Secondly, HER2 score 2+ was considered negative without further FISH testing. These shortcomings may limit the generalizability of the findings, especially in subgroup analyses. The impact of Trastuzumab on survival was not studied. Larger Nepalese cohorts are needed to refine HER2’s role in prognostication and Trastuzumab selection.

Conclusion

The prevalence of HER2 positivity was 10.3%, which was lower than global estimates, likely reflecting the predominance of distal, poorly differentiated tumors and evaluating only score 3+ as HER2 positive. A potential association with advanced tumor stage was observed but requires validation in larger, multicenter studies.
