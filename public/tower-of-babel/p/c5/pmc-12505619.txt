A correlation study on triglyceride-glucose index and extensive neovascularization within carotid artery plaques
Authors: Sen Wang, Yiqun Lin, Shuai Zheng, Yuyang Gan, Ying Liu, Yukang Zhang, Wen He, Wei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the correlation between the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and intraplaque neovascularization (IPN), evaluate the predictive efficacy of the TyG index for massive IPN, and provide novel serological markers for the early clinical identification of vulnerable plaques.MethodsA total of 308 patients enrolled between May 2019 and December 2024 were included. IPN (0–3) was graded by Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) and dichotomized (Grade I: none/minimal neovascularization, Grade II: extensive neovascularization). The association between the TyG and Grade II and its predictive performance were evaluated.ResultsThe proportion of IPN Grade II significantly increased with ascending TyG quartiles (Q1: 37.66% vs. Q4: 85.71%, P < 0.001). After adjusting for confounders, the risk of Grade II in the Q4 group was 7.32 times higher than in the Q1 group (95% CI: 2.81–19.05). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed an exponential increase in risk when TyG > 8.28. Subgroup analysis demonstrated a stronger association between TyG and IPN Grade II in hypertensive populations. Furthermore, the TyG index yielded an area under the curve of 0.72, with a sensitivity of 81% and specificity of 60% at the optimal cutoff value of 8.645.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that the TyG index is significantly and independently associated with extensive IPN in carotid plaques. This finding suggests the TyG index has potential as a supplementary serological marker in the assessment of plaque vulnerability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01942-y.Keywords: Triglyceride-glucose index, Carotid atherosclerotic plaques, Intraplaque neovascularization, Contrast-enhanced ultrasound

Introduction

Ischemic cerebrovascular disease demonstrates high incidence and mortality rates. The formation of carotid atherosclerotic plaques (CAP) constitutes a primary risk factor for carotid stenosis and secondary ischemic cerebrovascular events [1]. Advancements in imaging technology coupled with population aging have substantially increased CAP detection rates. Critically, vulnerable plaques—characterized by inherent instability—represent the fundamental pathological substrate for triggering ischemic cardio-cerebrovascular events [2, 3]. Robust evidence confirms that intraplaque neovascularization (IPN) and intraplaque hemorrhage, established morphological hallmarks of vulnerable plaques, are demonstrably linked to elevated risk of ischemic cerebrovascular events [4, 5]. Notably, IPN presence exhibits a significant correlation with stroke recurrence [6, 7]. Consequently, early identification of IPN within CAP and accurate assessment of plaque vulnerability are imperative for implementing effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), a rapid and non-invasive emerging imaging modality, provides a robust method for detecting IPN by dynamically visualizing intraplaque microvasculature through microbubble contrast agents [8, 9]. Microbubble-based semi-quantitative analysis enables effective assessment of plaque vascularization, with increased enhancement intensity demonstrating positive correlation with plaque vulnerability risk [10, 11]. However, the current absence of clinically available biochemical markers directly correlated with IPN impedes precise stratification management of vulnerable plaques.

In recent years, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has emerged as a significant surrogate marker for assessing insulin resistance (IR) due to its simplicity and high efficacy. Calculated from fasting triglycerides (TG) and fasting blood glucose (FBG), the TyG index demonstrates strong correlation with both the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp technique, while exhibiting superior sensitivity (96.5%) and specificity (85.0%) [12, 13]. Furthermore, beyond reflecting dyslipidemia and chronic low-grade inflammation, the TyG index has been robustly associated with cardiovascular disease risk and all-cause mortality [14–16]. In recent years, to further enhance predictive performance, researchers have proposed multiple TyG-derived indices, including the TyG-BMI index (Triglyceride-glucose index × Body Mass Index)—integrating the obesity parameter (BMI) to capture the synergistic amplification of insulin resistance-induced vascular damage by obesity [15]—and the CTI index (C-reactive protein-Triglyceride-glucose Index)—incorporating the chronic inflammatory marker C-reactive protein (CRP) to quantify the synergistic pathogenetic mechanism between IR and inflammation [17]. Studies confirm that both derived indices exhibit significantly superior predictive efficacy for cardiovascular event risk compared to the baseline TyG index [15, 17]. Recent studies indicate associations between the TyG index and its derived indices with carotid intima-media thickness as well as plaque formation [18, 19]. However, its relationship with IPN within CAP remains unexplored.

Based on the above background, the present study aims to investigate the correlation between the TyG index and its derived indices with IPN within CAP. This investigation seeks to establish the TyG index as an accessible and reliable serological biomarker for identifying vulnerable plaques, ultimately enhancing risk stratification protocols and intervention strategies for ischemic cerebrovascular disease.

Methods

Study population

This study recruited 308 patients at Beijing Tiantan Hospital, affiliated with Capital Medical University, between May 2019 and December 2024 (Fig. 1). The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment (KT2022-015-04).

Flow chart of the study population enrollment

Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) Age ≥ 18 years. (2) Presence of ≥ 1 CAP with thickness > 1.5 mm confirmed by ultrasonography. (3) Hospitalized patients undergoing carotid CEUS. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Cases deemed ineligible for CEUS by sonographers or with incomplete CEUS datasets. (2) Plaques exhibiting extensive calcification preventing neovascularization visualization. (3) Patients lacking essential clinical parameters.

Standard ultrasound and CEUS examinations

Carotid standard ultrasonography and CEUS examinations were conducted post-admission. All scans utilized an Aplio 500 high-resolution ultrasound system (Canon Medical Systems) equipped with a 4–11 MHz linear array transducer. Patients were positioned supine with the head rotated contralaterally to optimize exposure of the target carotid artery. Dual-imaging mode acquisition enabled synchronous CEUS and grayscale visualization within identical scanning planes, facilitating real-time structural correlation and comparative plaque analysis.

During standard carotid ultrasound examinations, we measured the peak systolic velocity (PSV) distal to the stenosis site in the internal carotid artery and assessed the degree of stenosis: for plaques in the internal carotid artery, the diagnostic criteria issued by the Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound (SRU) in 2003 were applied; for plaques at other sites, the NASCET criteria were utilized, with classification based on the percentage reduction in luminal diameter [20, 21]. Stenosis severity was stratified into three grades: mild (< 50%, PSV < 125 cm/s), moderate (50–69%, PSV 125–230 cm/s), and severe (≥ 70%, PSV > 230 cm/s).

59 mg SonoVue® (Bracco Imaging SpA) was reconstituted in 5 mL saline and vigorously agitated. A 1.6 mL bolus was administered via antecubital vein injection, immediately followed by a 5 mL saline flush. The ultrasound internal timer was triggered upon injection to precisely document contrast arrival time and capture dynamic CEUS sequences. IPN was graded by analyzing intravascular microbubble kinetics within CAP (Fig. 2): IPN 0: No detectable microbubbles. IPN 1: Focal microbubbles restricted to plaque shoulders/adventitial border. IPN 2: Linear microbubble tracts penetrating plaque core. IPN 3: Diffuse microbubble distribution throughout plaque. For clinical stratification, IPN 0–3 were dichotomized: Grade I: IPN 0–1 (none/minimal neovascularization). Grade II: IPN 2–3 (extensive neovascularization) [22]. Grade II plaques (high-risk cohort) underwent intensified surveillance due to substantially elevated vulnerability. To ensure the reliability of IPN grading, all CEUS images were independently interpreted by two vascular sonographers blinded to clinical data; discordant results were resolved through consensus with a senior vascular specialist. Cohen’s kappa (κ) statistics were employed to evaluate inter-observer agreement for both the 4-category IPN classification system and the binary Grade system, demonstrating near-perfect agreement (4-category IPN: κ = 0.925; binary Grade: κ = 0.967) as detailed in Supplementary File S1.

Variables

This study incorporated the following covariates: Age, Sex, BMI, Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP), Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP), FBG, TG, Total Cholesterol (TC), High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (HDL-C), Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL-C), Glycated Hemoglobin (HbA1c), CRP, history of smoking and alcohol consumption, history of Hypertension (HP), history of Diabetes Mellitus (DM), history of Cardiovascular Disease (CVD), history of Stroke, history of dyslipidemia, degree of carotid artery stenosis as well as a record of all medications the patient was taking prior to admission (including Use of antilipemic agents, Use of antidiabetic agents, etc.).

The study outcome variables were the IPN 0–3 classification system and the dichotomized IPN grade variable (where IPN 0–1 were categorized as Grade I, defined as absence or minimal intraplaque neovascularization, and IPN 2–3 were categorized as Grade II, defined as extensive intraplaque neovascularization).

The dependent variables included the TyG index, TyG-BMI (Triglyceride-glucose index - Body Mass Index), and CTI (C-reactive protein - Triglyceride - Glucose Index), calculated as follows: (1) TyG = ln [TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2], (2) TyG-BMI = TyG × BMI, (3) CTI = 0.412 × ln(CRP) + TyG [15, 23].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26). Continuous variables were presented using the median and interquartile range [M (P25, P75)]. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage), with intergroup comparisons performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Patients were first stratified into quartile groups based on the TyG index. Based on CEUS results, IPN was classified as Grade I (none/minimal neovascularization) or Grade II (extensive neovascularization) to explore potential factors influencing substantial IPN. Subsequently, the non-linear relationship between the TyG index and IPN grade II was analyzed using restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves. Logistic regression models were then employed to calculate odds ratios (ORs), assessing the strength of association between the TyG index and substantial IPN.

Three logistic regression models were constructed: an unadjusted (crude) model, Model 2 (adjusted for age and sex), and Model 3 (further adjusted for BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, HP, DM, history of CVD, history of stroke, dyslipidemia, stenosis, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, use of antilipemic agents and use of antidiabetic agents). Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, HP, DM, history of CVD, stroke, dyslipidemia, stenosis, use of antilipemic agents and use of antidiabetic agents; the fully adjusted Model 3 results are presented stratified by these variables. The likelihood ratio test was used to evaluate interaction effects between the TyG index and subgroup variables. Finally, the predictive performance of TyG-related indices for substantial IPN was validated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Results

Basic characteristics of participants

This study ultimately included 308 patients with carotid artery plaques who had complete CEUS results and comprehensive clinical data. After stratification into TyG index quartiles (Q1-Q4), the baseline characteristics of each group are presented in Table 1. A significant graded increase in metabolic disorder parameters was observed with rising TyG levels. Among all participants, the TyG index showed significant positive correlations (all p < 0.05) with increasing levels of BMI, Glu, TG, TC, LDL-C, HbA1c, as well as the prevalence of HP, DM, history of stroke, and use of antidiabetic agents. Furthermore, HDL-C levels exhibited a significant inverse correlation with the TyG index, decreasing progressively across quartiles (p = 0.009).

Furthermore, a significant dose-response relationship was observed between the TyG index and both the severity of IPN and carotid stenosis. As the TyG index increased, IPN grading demonstrated stepwise progression, and carotid stenosis severity worsened progressively. In the TyG Q4 quartile, 85.71% of patients exhibited extensive IPN (Grade II) compared to 37.66% in Q1 (P < 0.001), while 70.13% had severe stenosis (≥ 70%) versus 41.56% in Q1 (P < 0.01). TyG-BMI and CTI values also demonstrated significant stepwise increases across the ascending quartiles (Table 1), confirming a graded association between TyG-related indices and IPN severity.

Analysis of baseline characteristics stratified by IPN Grade (Table 2) revealed that patients with IPN Grade II had significantly higher TyG index, TyG-BMI, and CTI levels compared to those with IPN Grade I (P < 0.01). Furthermore, Glu, TG, alcohol consumption, HP, DM, stenosis, history of stroke and use of antidiabetic agents were also significantly associated with IPN severity (P < 0.05).

Correlation strength between TyG index and IPN

Table 3 presents the ORs, 95% CIs, and P-values for the TyG index, TyG-BMI, and CTI across three logistic regression models: Model 1 (crude), Model 2 (adjusted for age and sex), and Model 3 (adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, HP, DM, history of CVD, history of stroke, dyslipidemia, stenosis, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, use of antilipemic agents and use of antidiabetic agents). The TyG index demonstrated a significant positive association with extensive IPN in both the crude and adjusted models. Specifically, in Model 3, each 1-unit increase in the TyG index was associated with a 3.43-fold increased risk of extensive IPN (95% CI: 1.92–6.11). Compared to the Q1, the Q4 group exhibited a 7.32-fold higher risk (95% CI: 2.81–19.05), with a significant dose-response trend (P for trend < 0.0001).

Predictive performance assessment

These findings robustly support the clinical advantage of the TyG index as an independent predictor for substantial IPN compared to its derivative indices.

Discussion

Through a systematic analysis of the clinical data and contrast-enhanced ultrasound findings of 308 patients with carotid artery plaques, this study for the first time reveals a significant association between the TyG index and IPN, providing a new serological tool for clinical identification of vulnerable plaques.

The core finding of this study is that there is a clear dose-response relationship between the TyG index and the severity of IPN. As shown in Table 1, with the elevation of the TyG quartile (Q1→Q4), the proportion of IPN Grade II (extensive neovascularization) shows a stepwise increase (37.66%→40.26%→68.83%→85.71%, P < 0.001). It is noteworthy that even after adjusting for a wide range of potential confounders—including the use of antilipemic agents and use of antidiabetic agents—the association between the TyG index and extensive IPN remained significant and robust. Specifically, each 1-unit increase in the TyG index was associated with a nearly 3-fold higher risk of IPN (OR = 3.43, 95% CI: 1.92–6.11), while participants in TyG Q4 exhibited a 7.32-fold increased risk compared to those in Q1 (95% CI: 2.81–19.05) (Table 3). The strength of this association is significantly better than that of its derived indices TyG-BMI (OR = 1.87 in Q4 group) and CTI (OR = 2.43 in Q4 group) (P < 0.05), highlighting the core predictive value of the basic TyG index. It is worth noting that the RCS analysis (Fig. 4) clearly reveals that when the TyG > 8.28, the risk of Massive IPN increases exponentially, while the optimal cutoff value of 8.645 determined by the ROC curve (sensitivity 81%, specificity 60%, AUC = 0.72) (Supplementary Table S3, Fig. 3) provides an operational threshold for clinical screening of high-risk populations.

The stratified analysis revealed significant population-specific differences in the TyG-IPN association, providing a direction for precision intervention. We found that the TyG-IPN association exhibited a synergistic effect in hypertensive patients. In hypertensive individuals, the efficacy of the TyG index in predicting massive IPN was significantly enhanced (OR = 5.36, 95% CI: 2.31–12.42), far higher than that in non-hypertensive individuals (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 0.37–4.37) (Table 4). This suggests high TyG and hypertension may synergistically promote vascular lesions. This finding indicates patients with hypertension and high TyG represent very-high-risk populations for IPN and plaque vulnerability, warranting aggressive primary prevention.

The association between the TyG index and IPN may be explained, at least in part, by IR-driven pathological processes. (1) Dysregulated pathological angiogenesis: IR is thought to upregulates VEGF expression through activating the HIF-1α pathway, potentially contributing to abnormal neovascularization within plaques [24–26]. The widespread presence of IPN in the high TyG group (IPN 3 accounted for 68.83% in Q4 group, Table 1) provides imaging evidence for this mechanism. (2) Accelerated lipid deposition: IR enhances the activity of hepatic enzyme HMG-CoA reductase, promoting cholesterol synthesis and plaque deposition [27, 28]. The significantly higher LDL-C level in the TyG Q4 group compared with the Q1 group (2.18 vs. 1.74 mmol/L, P = 0.001, Table 1) provides direct support for the lipid infiltration theory.

In this study, LDL-C levels increased significantly with ascending TyG quartiles (Q1: 1.74 mmol/L vs. Q4: 2.18 mmol/L, P = 0.001), suggesting its potential role in the TyG-IPN relationship. Mechanistically, LDL-C infiltrates the vascular wall through damaged endothelium and undergoes oxidative modification to form oxidized LDL (ox-LDL). Ox-LDL not only induces macrophage foam cell formation but also activates inflammatory signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB), promoting the release of pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF) that directly drive IPN development [29–31]. The insulin resistance reflected by TyG exacerbates atherogenesis by enhancing cholesterol synthesis and impairing LDL-C clearance, positioning LDL-C as a potential mediator of TyG-induced IPN. However, after adjusting for dyslipidemia in multivariable regression, the TyG-extensive IPN association remained significant (OR = 4.29, P < 0.0001), indicating that LDL-C is not the sole determinant. Rather, TyG and LDL-C likely act synergistically—TyG creates a permissive metabolic environment for LDL-C pathogenicity, while LDL-C accelerates IPN progression and plaque destabilization.

This study provides the first evidence of a significant independent association between the TyG index and extensive IPN. The observed performance characteristics (AUC 0.72, sensitivity 81%) suggest the TyG index may have potential utility in specific scenarios, such as: (1) As a potential pre-screening indicator prior to CEUS, leveraging its relatively high sensitivity to identify individuals who might benefit most from advanced imaging. Notably, TyG-BMI exhibited the highest sensitivity (94%) in this study despite lower specificity (25%) and AUC (0.59)—a valuable trade-off for rapid mass screening in resource-constrained settings. (2) Highlighting populations at potentially elevated risk (e.g., those in the highest TyG quartile, particularly with hypertension) who may warrant closer monitoring or consideration of more intensive risk factor management. (3) Providing a complementary perspective to existing lipid metrics, given the absence of established serological IPN markers. However, the moderate specificity (60%) and AUC, along with the cross-sectional design, necessitate prospective validation in diverse cohorts to confirm its actual clinical utility and refine optimal implementation strategies before clinical adoption can be considered.

It is noteworthy that the characterization of vulnerable plaques exhibits multimodal properties. This study focuses on IPN assessed by CEUS, which serves as a critical indicator of plaque neovascularization richness and leakage risk. Whereas intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH) detected by magnetic resonance T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) represents another key vulnerability marker, typically associated with rupture of intraplaque neovessels or erythrocyte extravasation [32–34]. IPN and IPH may represent distinct yet interrelated pathological stages in the development of plaque vulnerability. Future research integrating multimodal imaging (e.g., CEUS + MRI) may enable more comprehensive assessment of plaque vulnerability and its association with metabolic indices such as the TyG index.

Given that the TyG index serves as a high-efficacy surrogate for HOMA-IR, its predictive value may extend to coronary plaque vulnerability [12, 13]. First, key markers of coronary plaque instability—such as thin-cap fibroatheroma (TCFA) and intraplaque microvessels—have been validated to correlate closely with insulin resistance. An optical coherence tomography (OCT) study by Iguchi et al. demonstrated significantly higher TCFA prevalence (50% vs. 26%, P = 0.02) and increased microvessel density (54% vs. 28%, P = 0.02) in patients with elevated HOMA-IR (> 2.5) [35]. Plaque morphological evidence from cardiac MRI further supports this association: Matsumoto et al. identified high-intensity plaques (HIPs) on T1WI—indicating intraplaque hemorrhage/thrombosis—as an independent predictor of filter no-reflow (FNR) during percutaneous coronary intervention (OR = 30.6, P = 0.008) [35]. Emerging evidence confirms that the TyG index independently associates with vulnerable coronary plaques in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients, positioning it as a biomarker for reducing cardiovascular event rates in this population [36]. Future studies should integrate multimodal imaging (e.g., OCT for fibrous cap thickness, MRI for HIPs detection) to further validate TyG’s role in coronary risk stratification.

The novelty of this study lies in: being the first to extend the association of the TyG index to the plaque microvascularization level, validating the “metabolic-microvascular interplay” through CEUS imaging. This complements the previously established TyG-macroscopic plaque associations [37, 38]. This finding holds significant clinical implications: (1) The TyG index, derived solely from fasting TG and blood glucose without requiring additional calculation of BMI or CRP, better aligns with the need for practicality. (2) It clarifies the causal predominance: The low predictive efficacy of the TyG-BMI index suggests that obesity likely influences insulin resistance-related carotid plaque IPN primarily by exacerbating IR, rather than through an independent mechanism. Conversely, the limited improvement offered by the CTI indicates that inflammation is not a core mediating factor within the IR-IPN pathway.

This study has several inherent limitations. The core limitation of this study lies in its cross-sectional design. Since both the exposure factor (TyG index) and the outcome measure (IPN grade) were assessed at the same time point, we cannot establish the temporal sequence between them. Specifically, the available data cannot resolve: (1) whether elevated TyG precedes and promotes the development of extensive IPN; (2) whether pre-existing extensive IPN influences TyG levels; or (3) whether the association stems from shared underlying factors. Although we adjusted for numerous confounding factors, the possibility of residual confounding remains. Future prospective cohort studies incorporating serial TyG index measurements and CEUS evaluations are essential to delineate the temporal relationship and ascertain whether an elevated TyG index predicts the incidence or progression of IPN. Lifestyle indicators, such as dietary patterns and exercise intensity, were not included in the analysis, potentially introducing residual confounding due to these unmeasured factors. Furthermore, the optimal cutoff value for the TyG index in this study (8.645) was derived from a single-center Chinese population. It is noteworthy that differential thresholds may exist across distinct ethnic or geographic populations. For instance, a recent study in patients with chronic coronary syndromes demonstrated that a TyG index > 8.8 was an independent predictor of cardiovascular events [39]. Although this study reports a cutoff value close to ours, extrapolation of the current cutoff requires caution, and its generalizability should be validated in multi-ethnic cohorts. Subsequent research could integrate multi-omics approaches to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the TyG-IPN association and validate the cutoff’s generalizability in multi-ethnic populations.

Conclusions

This study provides the first evidence establishing the TyG index as a robust independent predictor of IPN within carotid artery plaques, with this association demonstrating particular significance in populations affected by hypertension. The risk stratification model developed based on the TyG index (optimal cutoff value: 8.645) offers a cost-effective and practical adjunctive tool for the clinical identification of vulnerable plaques. This advancement holds the potential to refine early intervention strategies for ischemic cerebrovascular disease, ultimately reducing the burden of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events. Future research, including multicenter prospective studies, is warranted to validate the association between the TyG index and vulnerable features across different vascular beds (e.g., coronary arteries), potentially integrating it with advanced imaging modalities like cardiac MRI and OCT.
