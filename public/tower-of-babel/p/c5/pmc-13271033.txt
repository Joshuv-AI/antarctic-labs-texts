Color Graphics: Can Everyone See Them?
Authors: Yutong Geng, Vivian Ortiz
Published: 2026

Nearly 1 in 12 men cannot distinguish red from green.1 Yet red-green fluorescence remains the default language of modern biology. Who, then, are we excluding from our data? For scientists, thinking of scientific methods is often as important as how results are presented. Graphics and tables need to be illustrative of the results but also need to be comprehensible for the public to enable dissemination and application of findings. As journals increasingly incorporate graphical abstracts2 and as omics studies become widely employed,3 we need to ask ourselves one important question: can everyone see our data? And this isn’t about access to journal articles—this is truly a topic for another day—but it is about our retina’s ability to build a color visual perception of what we have in front of us.

Color blindness is a condition that prevents an individual from discriminating between red and green colors; in rare circumstances, they may also have a hard time distinguishing blue. It results from a malfunction in one or more photoreceptors in the retina, the M and L cones.4 Its prevalence is highest in the Arabian Peninsula with 5.62%.5 It affects males predominantly, with up to 8% cases compared with 0.5% cases in females, associated with x-linked pattern of inheritance following defects in gene rearrangements.6 Contrary to a misconception, color blindness can also be acquired from conditions such as multiple sclerosis, diabetes mellitus, Parkinson’s disease, mercury toxicity, Alzheimer’s disease, glaucoma, toxicity from medications, and cataract formation.7 Although acquired color blindness may be progressive, may affect a single visual field, and may not be symmetrical, its prevalence may be higher than anticipated.

The implications become more concerning when we examine published literature. An analysis of images for scientific publications showed that 45% of cell biology and 21% of physiology publications contained images that were not accessible to people with color blindness.8 And in such cases, description in figure legends or annotations was only provided 18% to 47% of the time. Are we really then spreading our science? Part of this has to do with molecular labels inherently coming in red and green colors, instruments having a set preference to view results in these colors such as in confocal microscopy and flow cytometry, analysis software prompting researchers to use these colors in their data analysis, and an overall publishing culture that has continued to promote color images as a way to highlight scientific results. After all, variations in hue, brightness, and intensity in an image can show the natural appearance of an object and allow readers to visualize what researchers are trying to portray.

Strategies can be employed to increase access of scientific results to those with color blindness. Results can be presented in grayscale format to enable their visibility, where features can be enhanced by modifying the contrast. Much of the data output is already in grayscale, but grayscale visibility tests should be performed before an image is published to ensure that the desired features are distinguishable. Color Oracle (RRID:SCR_018400) is a simulation tool that can be used to test visibility for different types of color blindness. In addition, selecting the “simulate colorblindness” option in Fiji can allow images to be tested prior to publication.8

Images that require merging should also be avoided, as grayscale does not allow differentiation of the different channels used in merging, so we should instead display channels separately. Background color may also impact grayscale visibility, particularly with darker colors, for which inverted grayscale with a light background may be more appropriate in this setting. Cyan and magenta are alternative color palettes that can be used for people with different types of color blindness who not only are unable to distinguish between red and green, but also the rare form of color blindness who are unable to distinguish green and blue.8

Should authors be responsible for generating data that is also appropriate for colorblind people? Should the responsibility rest solely on the publishing company? In our opinion, responsibility lies with both sides. The publishing company can indeed make entire manuscripts grayscale and provide additional links for those with color blindness. But it cannot ensure that the published images indeed depict the results as intended by the authors. The onus also lies with the author in making appropriate palette choices on figures, avoiding merged images, and using simulation tests for color-appropriate images. Before submitting your next manuscript, run a colorblind simulation. Adjust one palette. Separate one merged image. Accessibility does not require reinvention, it requires intention. This ultimately helps to ensure our published work indeed reaches a wider population. Inaccessible visualization is not a technical oversight; it is a barrier to equitable science.
