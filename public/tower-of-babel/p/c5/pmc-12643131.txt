Expert consensus for difficult airway management in the absence of clear evidence using a Delphi method

ABSTRACT
The All India Difficult Airway Association developed clinical practice statements utilising the Delphi method among experts for specific interventions in the management of unanticipated difficult airways in adult, obstetric, and paediatric populations, as well as for the management of at-risk extubations, where existing evidence was either weak or absent. A Steering Committee consisting of nine airway experts and a Delphi methodologist convened a panel of 24 experts, from whom anonymous responses were collected via an online Delphi survey. Consensus was defined as at least 75% experts voting for a particular option in multiple-choice statements, and agreement (scores of 5–7) or disagreement (scores of 1–3) on a nominal 7-point Likert scale statement. The stability of responses between consecutive rounds was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test or Chi-square test, with a P value of greater than or equal to 0.05 indicating stability. Twenty-three experts completed four Delphi rounds conducted from 27 December 2024 to 25 January 2025. Of the 26 statements considered, 23 (88%) achieved both consensus and stability. Of note, the statement regarding the maximum number of attempts permitted for supraglottic airway insertion in adults to mitigate airway management-related complications did not achieve consensus among experts; however, the Steering Committee voted unanimously for a maximum of three attempts. From the 23 statements that achieved expert consensus and the statement that received the maximum vote during the adjudication process by the Steering Committee, 24 expert clinical statements were drafted. Future research is necessary to evaluate the impact of these clinical practice statements and to address the remaining uncertainties.

INTRODUCTION

Managing an unanticipated difficult airway is one of the most stressful situations for an airway operator and can become life-threatening if not managed properly. The fourth National Audit Project (NAP4) strongly recommended the adoption of airway algorithms and proposed that their utilisation might improve patient outcomes.[1]

In 2016, the All India Difficult Airway Association (AIDAA) committee of airway experts developed evidence-based national guidelines for the management of unanticipated difficult airway in adults, obstetrics, paediatrics, management of difficult extubation, and tracheal intubation in the ICU.[23456] The AIDAA 2016 guidelines did not include graded recommendations. In 2025, AIDAA has updated the adults, obstetrics, paediatrics, and extubation guidelines, providing recommendations and best practice statements which have been categorised according to the American Heart Association (AHA) Class of Recommendation and Level of Evidence to clinical strategies, interventions, treatments, or diagnostic testing in patient care.[7]

Providing guidance for interventions where the evidence is absent or weak (e.g., maximum number of attempts at tracheal intubation) is challenging. Nevertheless, such guidance is much required for clinicians when faced with an unanticipated difficult airway. Most international airway guidelines provide guidance based on expert consensus among the guideline committee members in this setting.[89] For the 2025 AIDAA guidelines, we conducted a Delphi process to synthesise national expert opinions for the management of unanticipated difficult airway where the evidence is either absent or weak. Multiple iterative survey rounds were conducted until stability (absence of variation in responses between two consecutive rounds) was achieved, maintaining anonymity of the experts until the end of the Delphi process, to avoid any bias or group pressure. The goal was to draft expert clinical practice statement based on the consensus achieved to incorporate into the airway guidelines, thereby providing guidance to airway operators when faced with an unanticipated difficult airway. In this article, we describe the Delphi process, the results of the Delphi surveys, and the expert clinical practice statements generated.

METHODS

Delphi process

In 2022, the AIDAA leadership appointed a Steering Committee consisting of nine airway experts (SMA, JVD, JRD, RG, PK, SNM, APS, SRS, and RV) who were involved in the development of the AIDAA 2016 guidelines to lead the development of the AIDAA 2025 adults, obstetrics, paediatrics, and extubation guidelines. This committee also served as the Steering Committee for the Delphi process. PN served as the Delphi methodologist. The Delphi process was initiated by the Steering Committee and the Delphi methodologist to achieve consensus among airway experts for the clinical research questions using Delphi methodology where the evidence was either absent or weak, for incorporation into the AIDAA 2025 guidelines. Since this was part of the methodology of the AIDAA 2025 guidelines, it was not registered as a study. All expenses required for the Delphi study, and technical support was provided by AIDAA.

For the 2025 AIDAA Guidelines, Patients or Population-Intervention/Comparison-Outcome (PICO) questions were formulated. A Librarian appointed by AIDAA conducted the literature search from January 2000 to December 2024 and prepared PRISMA flowcharts for each research question. The evidence was summarised and recommendations categorised according to the American Heart Association (AHA) Class of Recommendations and Level of Evidence to clinical strategies, interventions, treatments, or diagnostic testing in patient care.[7] Details of the literature search, evidence summary, and recommendations for each PICO question are available in the respective guidelines.[10111213] From this systematic search of published literature and published difficult airway guidelines, the Steering Committee identified interventions required for managing a difficult airway, for which evidence was weak or absent. These were used to draft statements for the first round of the Delphi process under five domains including adult, paediatric, paediatric rescue surgical airway, obstetrics, and extubation.

The Steering Committee recruited a panel of airway experts, prepared the statements for the first Delphi round, coordinated the iterative Delphi rounds, prepared the anonymised Delphi report, revised statements for subsequent Delphi rounds as necessary, and provided controlled feedback. To maintain objectivity and prevent bias, the Steering Committee refrained from participating in the voting process during the Delphi rounds.[141516]

Twenty-four airway experts who met the pre-defined inclusion criteria: (1) clinical expertise in the airway management, (2) teaching experience in airway management, and (3) research projects or publications in airway management were invited to participate in the Delphi project via email. Upon acceptance, the experts were included in the Delphi process. Participation in the Delphi process was voluntary and implied consent. The experts remained anonymous until the end of the Delphi process to avoid bias or group pressure. Patients and public were not included in this project due to the complexities involved in integrating their perspectives into the highly technical aspects of difficult airway management.

Conflicts of interest

Declaration of conflicts of interest was obtained from all members of the Steering Committee and each of the panellists prior to initiation of the Delphi process. Members having conflicts related to a specific topic were not involved in drafting or voting on the respective statements on the subject to avoid potential bias.

Delphi rounds

The Delphi surveys prepared using Google FormsTM included statements formatted as either multiple-choice questions or 7-point Likert scales. The survey was piloted among the Steering Committee to check for clarity of the statements and technical aspect of the survey. The surveys were shared with the experts via a unique online link. Experts were encouraged to provide feedback during each round through an open text field at the end of the survey. The questions were modified or deleted in the subsequent rounds based on the responses and feedback. Responses from the experts were anonymised. A cumulative report was shared with the experts following each Delphi round. Periodic reminders were sent during the Delphi rounds to minimise attrition. The rounds continued until a stable consensus was achieved for all statements.

Consensus and stability

Consensus was established when 75% or more of panellists selected the same option(s) in multiple-choice statements and indicated agreement (scores 5–7) or disagreement (scores 1–3) with a statement on the 7-point Likert scale. The stability (absence of variation in responses between two consecutive rounds) of responses was assessed from round 2 onwards, employing statistical tools such as the Kruskal–Wallis test for Likert-scale statements and Chi-squared tests for multiple-choice statements. When comparing statements between consecutive Delphi rounds, stability was defined as a P value of 0.05 or greater. Microsoft Excel (MS Office 2019, Microsoft Corp, WA, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The Delphi survey results were reported based on Accurate Consensus Reporting Document (ACCORD) Guidelines.[17]

Expert consensus statements

The Steering Committee drafted expert consensus statements from the survey statements that achieved consensus. In the event that a survey statement required for specific guidance in any of the guideline algorithms (e.g., maximum number of attempts at tracheal intubation) did not achieve consensus through the Delphi process, it was taken to the Steering Committee for adjudication through an anonymised voting process to make a decision using the majority vote, and thereafter included as an expert consensus statement. The results of the Delphi process and the expert consensus statements drafted were circulated among the experts before publication of the Delphi process to generate expert consensus for the AIDAA guidelines.

RESULTS

Four Delphi rounds evaluating 26 statements were conducted between 27 December 2024 and 25 January 2025. All 24 panellists accepted the invitation to participate in the Delphi process. However, only 23 participated in all the four rounds of Delphi surveys. Ten panellists were female (43%). All statements were stable, and 23/26 (88%) statements achieved consensus at the end of Delphi process. The steps of the Delphi process are detailed in Figure 1. The results of the Delphi process with the consensus and stability analysis of the statements in the Delphi surveys are presented in Table 1. The statement regarding the maximum number of attempts at supraglottic airway (SGA) insertion that should be permitted to limit airway management-related complications in adult patients did not achieve consensus among through the Delphi process. As per methodology, since this was required for inclusion in the adult algorithm, it was put for voting to the Steering Committee. The statement achieved 100% consensus for a maximum of three attempts. The 24 expert clinical practice statements drafted from the 23 statements that achieved consensus from the Delphi process and the statement that received the maximum vote during the adjudication process by the Steering Committee are listed in Table 2. DISCUSSION

Detailed discussion on each of the clinical practice statements is available in the respective AIDAA 2025 guideline they represent, which include the AIDAA 2025 guidelines for the management of unanticipated difficult airway in adults, obstetrics, paediatrics, and the management of at-risk extubations.[10111213]

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

The 24 expert clinical practice statements derived from the statements that achieved consensus provide guidance to airway operators for the management of an unanticipated difficult airway, where the evidence is either absent or weak. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that a robust Delphi methodology has been employed to achieve this. Blinding of the experts until the end of the Delphi rounds prevented any bias or group pressure. While we addressed several interventions for difficult airway management where evidence was either weak or absent, many areas of uncertainty still remain.

CONCLUSION

Using a Delphi method, 88% consensus was achieved among airway experts on interventions for difficult airway management, where the evidence is either absent or weak. Twenty-four expert clinical practice statements were derived from the statements that achieved consensus. These expert clinical practice statements provide guidance to airway operators for the management of an unanticipated difficult airway where the evidence is absent or weak. Future studies are needed to assess the effects of these clinical practice statements and to address the remaining uncertainties.
