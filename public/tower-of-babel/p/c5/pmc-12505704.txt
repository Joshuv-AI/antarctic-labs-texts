ICD-10 code validation for gastrointestinal bleeding in patients receiving oral anticoagulation: nationwide cohort study
Authors: Arnar Bragi Ingason, Jóhann Páll Hreinsson, Arnar Snær Ágústsson, Sigrún Helga Lund, Edward Rumba, Sigríður Kristjánsdóttir, Einar Stefán Björnsson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundICD-10 codes are commonly used to identify gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB) events in observational studies. However, most studies have neither used validated ICD-10 coding algorithms to identify GIB events nor manually confirmed the diagnosis of GIB, which raises concerns regarding the validity of their results. The aim of the study was to estimate the accuracy of ICD-10 codes for GIB in patients receiving oral anticoagulation and to develop new coding algorithm for identifying GIB events.MethodsUsing a national prescription database, all patients receiving oral anticoagulation in Iceland from 2014 to 2019 were identified. GIB events were identified using thorough search of ICD-10 codes from all five major hospitals in Iceland. To augment the code search, endoscopy results and death cause in the national death registry were reviewed. All diagnoses were manually confirmed via chart review and used as gold standard. The PPV was calculated for individual codes, and codes with PPV ≥ 75% were included in the ICD-10 coding algorithm.ResultsThe ICD-10 coding algorithm had 61.3% sensitivity, 99.6% specificity, 90.0% PPV, and 98.0% negative predictive value. GIB events identified using this ICD-10 coding algorithm were more commonly major bleeding events, and more commonly required hospitalization, anticoagulation reversal, anticoagulation discontinuation, and endoscopic intervention compared to GIB events that were missed.ConclusionsThe ICD-10 coding algorithm reported here had good accuracy in identifying true GIB events and the events that it identified were clinically more severe than those that it missed. The sensitivity of the ICD-10 coding algorithm was modest and can be improved by reviewing endoscopic results and death registries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04325-9.Keywords: ICD-10 code, Coding algorithm, Validation study, Gastrointestinal bleeding, Oral anticoagulant

Introduction

The standard practice for identifying gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB) events for patients receiving oral anticoagulants (OACs) has been to use International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes. Previous studies comparing GIB rates between OACs have rarely manually confirmed events identified using this method [1–7]. This is concerning as the positive predictive value (PPV) for using ICD-10 codes to identify GIB events in patients on OACs has been estimated to be only 55–75% [8, 9].

Additionally, more ICD-10 codes are used to code for GIB compared to bleeding from other locations [10]. Meanwhile, most large cohort studies use only a handful of ICD-10 codes to identify GIB events [1–4]. This raises questions about whether previous studies may have underestimated the incidence of GIB for patients receiving OACs. Supporting this, a nationwide study comparing GIB event rates between direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) found that by using a thorough “catch-all” method to identify GIB events (identifying events by using ICD-10 code search, reviewing endoscopic results, and death cause by the death registry), it was able to identify twice as many GIB events compared to conventional ICD-10 code search only [10]. Additionally, there is evidence that the ICD-10 codes used in previous studies are more likely to identify upper than lower GIB. For example, while upper and lower GIB rates have been found to similar in the general population [11, 12], a population-based study from the UK comparing GIB rates between OACs, found upper GIB rates to be sevenfold higher compared to lower GIB rates [3]. This means that the majority of lower GIB events in this cohort were likely missed. Similarly, two population-based studies from Denmark comparing GIB rates between warfarin and DOACs only used ICD-10 codes for peptic ulcer disease to identify GIB events [1, 2]. This is particularly concerning as the rates of upper and lower GIB have been found to differ significantly between different OACs [13], and the results of these studies may therefore be skewed.

Given this, there is a need for a standardized ICD-10 coding algorithm for GIB events. The aim of this study was to assess the accuracy of individual ICD-10 codes and to establish a new ICD-10 coding algorithm that is both sensitive and specific for GIB, irrespective of whether its origin is from the upper or lower GI tract.

Methods

This was an Icelandic nationwide study validating ICD-10 codes used for diagnosing GIB in patients on OACs.

Study design

The Icelandic oral anticoagulation database has been previously described in detail [10, 13]. Briefly, all patients receiving OACs (apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, rivaroxaban, or warfarin) in Iceland from 1 March 2014 to 28 February 2019 were identified using data from the Icelandic Medicine Registry. Next, using a comprehensive “catch-all” method, GIB events were identified by using a combination of ICD-10 codes (Suppl. Table 1), reviewing endoscopic results, and reviewing the death registry. All GIB events were manually confirmed via chart review. Data on GIB events were acquired from Landspitali University Hospital, the only tertiary hospital in Iceland, and the four regional hospitals in Iceland. Notably, data on all endoscopic procedures that the patients underwent at these five major hospitals during the study period were gathered using relevant NOMESCO Classification of Surgical Procedures codes (Suppl. Table 2). All these endoscopic procedures were manually reviewed to assess for missing diagnoses of GIB. ICD-10 codes queried and endoscopic results queried were limited to hospital encounters associated with emergency department visits or inpatient admissions.

All patients that received OACs and either had a confirmed GIB event or an ICD-10 code suggestive of GIB during the study period were included in the study. Patients were followed from the filling of their first OAC prescription until 28 February 2019 or earlier if treatment was discontinued or switched to another OAC.

GIB was defined as overt GIB (hematemesis, melena, or hematochezia) without signs of alternative source of bleeding (i.e., epistaxis or respiratory tract bleeding). Upper GIB was defined as hematemesis or confirmed upper GIB site on endoscopy. Lower GIB was defined as hematochezia or confirmed lower GIB site on endoscopy [14]. Patients with melena and uncertain bleeding site on endoscopy were classified as having GIB of unknown location [14]. All GIB events were manually confirmed independently by two authors (ABI and ASA). Discordant results were adjudicated by a third author (ESB). During the chart review, data was acquired on bleeding location, whether the patient underwent endoscopy, hemoglobin drop, transfusion requirement, use of reversal agent, and whether GIB was major or fatal. Major GIB was defined according to the International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) criteria as a bleeding leading to hemoglobin drop of 20 g/L or more, transfusion of 2 or more units or packed red blood cells, bleeding into a closed compartment (such as the retroperitoneum), or bleeding that was fatal.

Data on patients’ comorbidities were gathered using relevant ICD-10 codes from the five major hospitals included in the study (Suppl. Table 1). Patients’ overall comorbidity burden was assessed using the Charlson comorbidity index [14]. Data on concomitant drug use was gathered using relevant ATC codes from the Icelandic Medicine registry as previously described (Suppl. Table 3).

Statistical analysis

Welch’s t test was used to compare numerical variables. Pearson’s χ² was used to compare categorical variables if there were > 5 observations in each group. Otherwise, a Fisher’s exact test was used. Odds ratios were calculated using unconditional maximum likelihood estimation, and confidence intervals were calculated using normal approximation per Wald’s method. P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. PPV was calculated for individual ICD-10 codes. ICD-10 codes with PPV ≥ 75% were included in our final ICD-10 coding algorithm.

Results

In total, 14,611 patients received OACs during the study period. Of those, 745 patients were found to have GIB during this time period (Table 1). The mean age was 71.0 years and 6163 (42.2%) patients were female . There were 3,005 patients receiving apixaban, 1220 dabigatran, 153 edoxaban, 4515 rivaroxaban, and 5718 warfarin. Of all patients, 10,670 (73.0%) were receiving OAC for atrial fibrillation, 2813 (19.3%) for venous thromboembolism, and 1128 (7.7%) for other indications.

The following ICD-10 codes all had PPV ≥ 75% and were included in the final ICD-10 coding algorithm: K22.1 (Ulcer of esophagus), K22.3 (Gastro-esophageal laceration-hemorrhage syndrome), K25 (Gastric ulcer), K26 (Duodenal ulcer), K28 (Gastrojejunal ulcer), K29.0 (Acute gastritis), K55 (Vascular disorders of intestine), K62.5 (Hemorrhage of anus and rectum), K62.6 (Ulcer of anus and rectum), K63.3 (Ulcer of intestine), K92.0 (Hematemesis), K92.1 (Melena), K92.2 (Gastrointestinal hemorrhage, unspecified), and I85.0 (Esophageal varices) (Table 2). The final ICD-10 coding algorithm had 61.3% sensitivity, 99.6% specificity, 90.0% PPV, and 98.0% negative predictive value. The ICD-10 coding algorithm identified 64.9% of all upper GIB and 62.5% of all lower GIB events. The accuracy of the ICD-10 coding algorithm was similar for patients receiving warfarin and DOACs (Table 3). The accuracy was also similar for both males and females (Suppl. Table 4).

GIB events identified using this ICD-10 coding algorithm less commonly had an unknown location (9% vs. 17%, odds ratio [OR] 0.48, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.31–0.75, p = 0.002) compared to those whose GIB event was missed (Table 4). Otherwise, the proportion of patients with upper or lower GIB did not differ between the two groups. Patients whose GIB events were identified using the ICD-10 code were more likely to have complete blood count drawn upon presentation compared to those whose GIB events were missed. They were also more likely to have esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) performed as their sole endoscopic study (34% vs. 23%, OR 1.72, 95% CI 1.23–2.41, p = 0.002) and less commonly had both EGD and colonoscopy performed (31% vs. 45%, OR 0.55, 95% CI 0.40–0.74, p < 0.001). The proportion of patients that had no endoscopic studies performed during their work-up did not differ between the two groups (Table 4).

Patients whose GIB events were identified using the ICD-10 code were more likely to be treated with OAC reversal (31% vs. 11%, OR 3.48, 95% CI 2.30–5.26, p < 0.001), an endoscopic intervention (23% vs. 14%, OR 1.90, 95% CI 1.27–2.84, p = 0.002), and OAC discontinuation (82% vs. 72%, OR 1.79, 95% CI 1.26–2.55, p = 0.002) compared to those whose GIB events were missed (Table 4). Similarly, these patients more commonly had GIB that fulfilled the criteria for major bleeding (66% vs. 56%, OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.12–2.05, p = 0.009) and more commonly needed hospitalization (78% vs. 66%, OR 1.84, 95% CI 1.32–2.56, p < 0.001). However, their mean hospital stay was shorter than for patients whose GIB events were missed (6 ± 5 days vs. 9 ± 10 days, p = 0.004).

Discussion

In the current study, we present a validated ICD-10 coding algorithm with high specificity and PPV (99.6% and 90.0% respectively). Importantly, the ICD-10 coding algorithm was equally accurate in identifying both upper and lower GIB events. The ICD-10 coding algorithm only had a sensitivity of 61%, meaning that 39% of all GIB events were missed. However, the GIB events that were identified were more severe, with significantly higher proportion fulfilling the criteria for major bleeding or needing therapeutic intervention (such as endoscopic intervention or anticoagulation reversal), compared to those GIB events that were missed. Importantly, the ICD-10 coding algorithm had similar accuracy for identifying GIB events for patients receiving warfarin and DOACs.

Given this, the ICD-10 search described herein would be appropriate to use without manual chart review in large cohort studies, in which manual chart review is not feasible or not possible. However, for smaller studies with more limited statistical power or for studies that want to accurately estimate the true incidence of GIB in a population, it would be appropriate to review endoscopic procedure results and cause of death registries as well, as this greatly increases the number of GIB events captured.

The PPV for the current ICD-10 coding algorithm is higher (90%) than in previous studies which have ranged from 55% to 75% in patients receiving anticoagulation [8, 9]. Importantly, these studies have only included 23–190 true GIB events [8, 9]. Furthermore, there is a lack of high-quality studies validating ICD-10 codes for GIB in the general population, with previous studies being limited to single-center studies with limited number of GIB events [15, 16]. In comparison, the current study included 745 true GIB events from all the five major hospitals in Iceland, providing information from a nationwide study. Additionally, previous studies have selected their codes a priori and they have included fewer ICD-10 codes than the ICD-10 coding algorithm reported here. Given the lack of previous high-quality studies validating ICD-10 coding algorithms for GIB, it is perhaps unsurprising that previous large cohort studies comparing GIB rates between OACs have generally not referred to any validation study for the ICD-10 codes that they used to identify GIB events [3–7]. This is particularly concerning as these studies have generally not manually reviewed their GIB events [3–7], raising questions about the validity of their results. The exception are two population-based studies from Denmark that only used validated peptic ulcer disease codes to identify GIB events [1, 2]. However, as peptic ulcer disease only accounts for 12% of all GIB events in OAC users [13], this severely limited the number of GIB events identified in these two studies [1, 2]. Additionally, two studies from Iceland manually confirmed all GIB events [10, 13]. This is the gold standard but may not be technically feasible for all database studies. At minimum, the standard practice for observational studies should be to use validated coding algorithms to identify outcomes. It is our hope that the data presented herein may help guide ICD-10 code selection for identifying GIB events in future studies.

The relatively poor sensitivity of the GIB coding algorithm was not unexpected given the many ICD-10 codes that can be used to code for GIB and may be due to a combination of factors. First, patients may have received nonspecific ICD-10 codes, such as iron-deficiency anemia or diverticular disease. Second, patients may have had GIB during their admission without it being their main complaint, and in that case the patient may only have been assigned ICD-10 codes related to their main complaint. Supporting this, the GIB events that the algorithm missed were not as severe as the events that it identified.

The study has several strengths. It is nationwide, including all the major hospitals in Iceland and uses a “catch-all” method to identify GIB events, with manual review of all events. Additionally, OAC use was identified using a national drug prescription database, that includes data on all outpatient drug prescriptions in the country. Therefore, the study essentially includes all clinically relevant GIB events for patients on OAC over a 5-year period.

The study also has several limitations. First, although the study included data from all the major hospitals in Iceland, the Icelandic population is relatively homogenous and the results may not be generalizable to other more heterogenous populations. Second, although the study included 745 true GIB events, it may not have sufficient statistical power to estimate the accuracy of some of the less commonly used ICD-10 codes. For example, the code for gastrojejunal ulcer, K28, was only used once in our cohort. Third, the ICD-10 coding algorithm described herein was validated in OAC users and may not be applicable to other populations.

Conclusions

In summary, we report an ICD-10 coding algorithm with high specificity and PPV in a population of OAC users. Importantly, the accuracy was consistent among both warfarin and DOACs users, and similar for both upper and lower GIB events. The sensitivity of the ICD-10 coding algorithm was modest and can be improved by reviewing endoscopic results and/or death registries.
