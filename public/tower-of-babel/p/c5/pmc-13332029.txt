Neoadjuvant tislelizumab (anti-PD-1 antibody) plus chemotherapy in patients with advanced epithelial ovarian cancer: the exploratory NAIVE trial
Authors: Zhigang Zhang, Jing Fei, Sangting Lao, Rui Liu, Xiaoqing Zhu, Lingyun Zhai, Da Huang, Boya Deng, Junna Wang, Huibin Fang, Weijiang Zhang, Xiaoming Xu, Liangji Lu, Ying Zhu, Jianwei Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe clinical efficacy and immune modulation of neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy for epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) remain uncertain. To clarify these effects, the NAIVE trial (NCT04815408), a prospective phase II study, evaluated the efficacy of neoadjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy with tislelizumab (NACI) in comparison with chemotherapy alone (NAC) in patients with FIGO IIIC–IV EOC. The primary endpoint was the 1-year progression-free survival (PFS) rate; the secondary endpoints included PFS, R0 resection, clinical and pathological responses, and safety. Between April 2021 and July 2024, 25 patients were included in the final analysis. After a median follow-up period of 30.7 months, NACI was associated with numerically prolonged progression-free survival (27.2 vs. 21.8 months; HR = 0.44, 95% CI 0.15–1.26; P = 0.127), with higher 1-year (92.3% vs. 83.3%) and 2-year (62.3% vs. 31.8%) PFS rates than NAC. NACI also yielded superior tumor responses, including higher ORR (69.2% vs. 58.3%), more R0 resections, and increased CRS3 rates. No unexpected safety signals emerged, and immune-related adverse events were manageable. Immune profiling techniques, such as single-cell RNA sequencing and CyTOF, identified distinct signatures. NACI responders demonstrated heightened CD8+ T-cell-mediated toxicity and metabolic fitness, whereas nonresponders exhibited exhaustion phenotypes. Furthermore, the response correlated with enrichment of the CXCL13+Th1–GC B-cell–tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) axis, accompanied by increased TLS activity and differentiation to IgG-producing plasma cells. Overall, NACI yielded PFS trends with acceptable safety, but without statistical significance. The mechanistic analyses highlight the role of the CXCL13+Th1–GC B-cell–TLS axis as a potential biomarker and driver of therapeutic response.Subject terms: Gynaecological cancer, Translational research, Gynaecological cancer, Translational immunology

Introduction

Epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) ranks as the most fatal gynecologic malignancy and remains the fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women. Owing to the absence of effective early detection strategies, the prevalence of advanced stage (FIGO III–IV) disease at diagnosis is nearly 70%, contributing to a poor 5-year overall survival rate of less than 45%.1,2 For patients presenting with extensive tumor load or deemed unsuitable for surgery, platinum-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by interval debulking surgery (IDS) has been established as a standard option alongside primary debulking surgery (PDS).3 However, some clinical trials and meta-analyses have found that neoadjuvant chemotherapy plus IDS results in either poorer or non-inferior overall survival compared to the use of PDS, highlighting the urgent need for the development of novel strategies to improve durable disease control.4 The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) directed against the PD-1/PD-L1 axis has profoundly impacted therapeutic strategies across a wide spectrum of solid malignancies.5,6 Nevertheless, their efficacy in unselected EOC remains modest, with objective response rates (ORRs) of less than 15% in trials such as KEYNOTE-100 and JAVELIN Ovarian 200.7,8 These results underscore the need for rational combination strategies and biomarker-guided patient selection.

Emerging evidence suggests there is a compelling biological rationale for combining ICIs with chemotherapy in the neoadjuvant setting.9 Platinum-based chemotherapy can induce immunogenic cell death, increase tumor antigen release, upregulate MHC class I expression, and promote tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte recruitment. Concurrent administration of ICIs may exploit this transient window of heightened tumor immunogenicity and reprogram the tumor microenvironment (TME) toward an immune-activated phenotype.10,11 Clinical trials in other solid tumors have demonstrated that compared with adjuvant administration, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy can elicit more profound and durable systemic antitumor immunity.12,13 However, in EOC, the clinical activity, safety profile, and potential translational implications of neoadjuvant anti-PD-1 antibody-based combination therapy remain insufficiently characterized.

In EOC, early-phase studies, including the NINJA trial,14 have explored chemoimmunotherapy combinations, but the survival benefits remain inconclusive, and the immunologic mechanisms underlying response or resistance are poorly characterized. In particular, it remains unclear whether the integration of PD-1 blockade into standard NACT can improve tumor response and surgical outcomes in patients with advanced EOC, and which patient populations are most likely to benefit from such an approach. Tislelizumab, a humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody, was designed to bind to PD-1 with high affinity and specific binding. Its design aimed to minimize binding to the Fcγ (Fcγ) receptor on macrophages.15 Given its promising antitumor effects, tislelizumab has been approved for use in several solid tumors.16 Up to now, there is a lack of prospective evidence evaluating the feasibility and efficacy of combining tislelizumab with platinum-based chemotherapy in the neoadjuvant setting for EOC. Addressing these questions is critical for optimizing treatment strategies and advancing precision immunotherapy in ovarian cancer.

Hence, we designed this prospective randomized trial, the NAIVE study (NCT04815408),17 to assess the clinical efficacy and safety of platinum-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with the PD-1 inhibitor tislelizumab (NACI) versus chemotherapy alone (NAC) in patients with advanced-stage EOC. Additionally, the potential translational implications of this combination strategy were explored. The findings of this study provide preliminary clinical evidence supporting the feasibility of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in EOC and offer insights into its potential role in reshaping the treatment paradigm for advanced disease.

Results

Patient recruitment and characteristics

From April 2021 through July 2024, a total of 71 patients diagnosed with FIGO IIIC–IV stage ovarian cancer were assessed for eligibility. Forty-five patients were excluded from the study. Of these, 21 had a Suidan CT score of <3 and/or a Fagotti laparoscopic score of <8. Five patients were over the age of 75, and three had a pathological diagnosis of mucinous carcinoma. Three met the exclusion criteria, and thirteen individuals withdrew consent. Finally, twenty-six patients were ultimately enrolled. Patients randomized to receive platinum-based chemotherapy and tislelizumab (NACI group) received three 21-day cycles of 200 mg of tislelizumab on day 1, 260 mg/m2 of nab-paclitaxel on day 1 and an AUC = 5 of carboplatin on day 1, and those in the platinum-based chemotherapy group (NAC group) received three 21-day cycles of 260 mg/m2 of nab-paclitaxel on day 1 and an AUC = 5 of carboplatin on day 1 (Fig. 1a). All patients commenced treatment, although one patient in the NAC arm discontinued due to treatment refusal, leaving 12 patients in the NAC group and 13 in the NACI group for subsequent analysis (Fig. 1b). The baseline demographic and clinicopathological features of the enrolled patients are delineated in Table 1. All patients underwent baseline pathological diagnosis by laparoscopy, cytology of ascites or pleural fluid, or puncture biopsy. Pathology revealed that 23 (92%) of the patients had high-grade serous ovarian cancer, while the remaining two patients were diagnosed with endometrioid adenocarcinoma and clear cell carcinoma. Furthermore, 9 (36%) patients had FIGO stage IIIC disease, while 16 (67%) had FIGO stage IV disease. In addition, germline and/or somatic BRCA1/2 mutations were detected in 5 patients (20%), and 12 (48%) patients were found to have homologous recombination deficiency (HRD). After IDS, all patients received nab-paclitaxel and carboplatin adjuvant chemotherapy in combination with bevacizumab. Maintenance treatment with PARPi ± bevacizumab or observation was determined based on the patients’ BRCA1/2 and HRD status (see Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1a).

Efficacy

The median follow-up from randomization at the initial database cutoff for the primary endpoint analysis (June 2025) was 30.67 months (range 20.67–36.5 months). The median progression-free survival (PFS) in the NAC arm was 21.77 months (95% CI: 12.17–29.33 months), whereas in the NACI arm, the median PFS was 27.17 months. The hazard ratio (HR) for disease progression or death was 0.439 (95% CI, 0.153–1.262), with no statistically significant difference between groups (P = 0.1268) (Fig. 1c). The 1-year PFS rates were 92.31 ± 7.39% in the NACI group and 83.33 ± 10.76% in the NAC group. Furthermore, the two-year PFS rates were 62.31 ± 15.16% in the NACI group and 31.75 ± 14.93% in the NAC group. Five deaths (two in NACI, three in NAC) occurred (Supplementary Fig. 1a). As the follow-up duration has not yet reached the threshold specified in the protocol, overall survival (OS) will be reported in a future publication. The exploratory subgroup analysis based on patient and disease characteristics (concerning the most clinically relevant subgroups) is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1b. Across all subgroups, NACI exhibited modest, nonsignificant improvements across most subgroups, whereas a significant benefit emerged exclusively in patients receiving PARP inhibitor maintenance (HR = 0.20, P = 0.025).

Regarding the secondary endpoint, the objective response rate (ORR) was 69.23% (9/13) in the NACI group and 58.33% (7/12) in the NAC group. Notably, NACI achieved complete response (CR) in 7.69% of patients, while no CR was observed in the NAC group (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1c). For the Gynecologic Cancer InterGroup (GCIG) CA125 response analysis, CR and partial response (PR) were achieved by all patients in both groups (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Surgical outcomes demonstrated an enhancement in resectability, with the degree of residual disease post-IDS categorized as follows: complete (R0, absence of macroscopic residual disease), optimal (R1, diameter ≤ 1 cm), or suboptimal (R2, diameter > 1 cm). In the NACI group, 84.62% (11/13) of patients underwent R0 cytoreductive surgery, and 30.77% (4/13) of patients achieved a chemotherapy response score (CRS) of 3. In the NAC group, 41.67% (5/12) of the patients underwent R0, and 25% (3/12) of the patients achieved CRS 3 (Fig. 1e, f).

Safety

The safety summary for the neoadjuvant phase is presented in Table 2. Adverse events (AEs) of grade ≥3, including hematological toxicity and peripheral neuropathy, were documented in both arms and exhibited no significant difference in frequency between the NACI and NAC arms. The most prevalent (≥50%) AEs in any group were anemia, nausea, fatigue, and peripheral neuropathy. In the course of the neoadjuvant treatment, no cases of AEs necessitating discontinuation of treatment, reduction of dosage, or fatality were observed. In the NACI group, 30.77% of patients reported experiencing immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Specific irAEs were reported in four patients, all of whom were graded 1–2, including two cases of hypothyroidism that resolved after treatment with thyroxine tablets, one case of rash, and one case of immune-related pneumonia treated with glucocorticoids. Importantly, the events in question had no impact on the subsequent treatment of the patients.

Tumor microenvironment after neoadjuvant therapy

Dynamic changes in the TME before and after treatment are vital indicators for evaluating the effectiveness of tumor immunotherapy. Therefore, we analyzed a total of 3 pretreatment biopsy samples, 6 post-NACI samples, and 3 post-NAC surgery samples from 10 EOC patients using scRNA-seq and 9 pretreatment biopsy, 4 post-NACI, and 3 post-NAC surgery samples from 12 EOC patients using CyTOF (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 2). Following a rigorous quality filtration process, a total of 94,882 cells were identified, including 23,366 from the pretreatment group, 44,953 from the post-NACI group, and 26,563 from the post-NAC group. The total comprised 23,388 epithelial cells, 41,557 immune cells (including T cells, B cells, neutrophils, and macrophages), and 28,492 stromal cells (fibroblasts and endothelial cells) (Fig. 2b). Neoadjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy with or without tislelizumab had a remodeling effect on the TME, which manifested mainly as increases in T-cell and B-cell numbers (Fig. 2c), as observed by CyTOF analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). Patients with EOC who underwent NACI treatment were classified as well responders (NACI-WR), defined as patients with a CRS of 3, and poor responders (NACI-PR), defined as patients with a CRS of 1–2. An enrichment of B cells and fibroblasts in NACI-WR patients and myeloid cells in NACI-PR patients after NACI was observed by scRNA-seq and CyTOF analysis (Fig. 2d–f and Supplementary Fig. 2d).

Dynamic changes in CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells

We constructed a high-resolution map of CD8+ T cells in different treatment groups and different immune therapy response groups. Although the number of CD8+ T cells increased to varying degrees after treatment, it decreased in patients with NACI-WR (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 3a). Subsequently, we examined the expression of genes associated with cytotoxicity, exhaustion, and proliferation in CD8+ T cells to elucidate the pivotal alterations that are concomitant with the response to NACI. In the NACI-WR group, CD8+ T cells displayed an enrichment of transcripts linked to cytotoxic activity, including granzymes (GZMB, GZMK, GZMA), Fas ligand (FASLG), and perforin-1 (PRF1), suggesting robust effector functionality. In contrast, CD8+ T cells from the NACI-PR group exhibited upregulation of multiple coinhibitory receptors (PDCD1, TIGIT, HAVCR2, and LAG3) in conjunction with exhaustion-related genes (TOX, BATF, ENTPD1, and PRDM1), indicative of a transcriptional profile that is characteristic of T-cell exhaustion (Fig. 3b). To investigate the impact of treatment on the global CD8+ T-cell transcriptome, GSEA was employed. The trends in the distribution of genes associated with the cytotoxic, exhausted, and proliferative signatures were correlated with NAC and NACI treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3b), and the cytotoxic signature was more pronounced in the NACI-WR group than in the NACI-PR group. Conversely, the exhausted and proliferative signatures were observed to be more enriched in the NACI-PR group than in the NACI-WR group (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, CD8+ T cells in the NACI-WR group presented increased levels of mRNA transcription, translation, and protein synthesis, whereas in the NACI-PR group, CD8+ T cells presented increased levels of DNA damage and other features (Fig. 3d). We next centered on the CD8+ T clusters, which were subsequently classified into the following categories: intraepithelial lymphocyte (IEL), effector memory (Tem), and two tissue-resident memory (TRM) subsets (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). In terms of quantity, the number of mitotic CD8+ TRM cells increased slightly in the NACI-WR group, while no significant differences were detected in the other subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 3e).

Furthermore, CyTOF analysis indicated a lower number of CD4+ T cells in the NACI-WR group than in the NACI-PR group (Fig. 3e). GO biological processes revealed that CD4+ T cells in the NACI-WR group were more inclined toward the recruitment and chemotaxis of T/B cells (Fig. 3f). Among the CD4+ T clusters (Fig. 3g, h), NACI-WR samples showed a pronounced reduction in the CCR8⁺CD4⁺ regulatory T-cell (Treg) population, alongside an expansion of CXCL13⁺CD4⁺ Th1 cells (Fig. 3i). To further characterize the CD4+T-cell subset and evaluate the cell trajectory and lineage, pseudotime analysis was performed using Monocle3. When the start time was placed centrally within the CXCL13+CD4+ Th1 cells population, three distinct trajectory branches were observed (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g). CellChat analysis revealed that CXCL13+CD4+ Th1 cells interacted with almost all types of cells (Fig. 3j).

B cells and plasma cells predict response to NACI

CyTOF analysis revealed that B cells and plasma cells increased in the NACI-WR group (Fig. 4a). B cells in the NACI-WR group demonstrated a more pronounced activated functional state (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Current studies have underscored the significance of B cells and tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) in modulating the response to immunotherapy. The utilization of TLSs hallmark genes led to the identification of a significant augmentation in TLSs activity subsequent to NACI treatment in EOC, particularly within the NACI-WR group (Fig. 4b). This observation was confirmed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 4c). Moreover, correlations between TLSs activity and the number of CXCL13+CD4+ Th1 cells were validated through correlation analysis (Fig. 4d).

The subsequent objective was to ascertain which B-cell subsets are instrumental in NACI efficacy and TLSs activity in EOC. Subcategorizing the B cells in our EOC data identified naive B, GC B-C1, GC B-C2, IgA+, IgG+ and IgM+ plasma subsets (Fig. 4e). Notably, except for naive B cells, the abundance of other subtypes of B cells and plasma cells was elevated in the NACI-WR group (Fig. 4f). Moreover, germinal center B (GC B) cells exhibited elevated levels of TLSs activity (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Among B-cell subsets, GC B cells demonstrated the highest predicted interactive potential with CXCL13+CD4+ Th1 cells (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 4d).

Interestingly, it was predicted that GC B-cell-derived APOE would have a strong potential to activate CXCL13+CD4+ Th1 cells by targeting T-cell activation/expansion/effector-related genes (e.g., CXCL10, F3, IGFBP2, IL7, SQLE, and LCN2) (Fig. 4h). Pseudotime analysis revealed that in all four groups (Pre, Post-NAC, NACI-PR, and NACI-WR), B-cell developmental trajectories consistently exhibited a branched structure (branches 1 and 2) from GC B cells toward plasma cells. In the NACI-WR group, plasma cells, especially IgG-type plasma cells, were significantly enriched in the trajectory distribution, indicating that in ovarian cancer highly responsive to immunotherapy, B cells differentiate into plasma cells and that there is an active humoral immune response (Fig. 4i).

Distribution of macrophages and nonimmune cells

Macrophages (Mφ) play a pivotal role in the TME, contributing to tumor immune evasion through various mechanisms. They have the ability to target key receptors, transcription factors, and secreted proteins that are crucial for both downstream and upstream signaling pathways in the TME. The phenotypic diversity of Mφ in solid tumors necessitates a comprehensive exploration of distinct subpopulations. The analysis of scRNA-seq data led to the identification of seven distinct subclusters of Mφ, namely, SLC16A10+Mφ, PLIN2+Mφ, C1QB+Mφ, FCN1+Mφ, NFKB1+Mφ, RNASEK+Mφ, and MZB1+Mφ (Fig. 5a, b). Notably, NFKB1+ Mφ was enriched in NACI-PR compared to NACI-WR (Fig. 5c). Additionally, gene signature analysis revealed that NFKB1+Mφ presented relatively high expression of IL-1A/B and PTGS2 and moderate expression of HLA molecules (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Subsequently, we performed a clustering analysis of endothelial cells (ECs) using a UMAP algorithm (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 5c). The quantity of fibroblasts in the NACI-PR and NACI-WR groups was not significantly different (Supplementary Fig. 5d). In addition, fibroblast clusters, including mCAFs, apCAFs, tCAFs, iCAFs, and CCL2+ fibroblasts, were observed (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 5e). Discussion

In this randomized phase II study, the addition of tislelizumab to platinum-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACI) for patients with advanced-stage EOC was associated with a numerical extension in median PFS and a higher 1- and 2-year PFS rate than PFS rates compared to patients who received NAC. Nevertheless, the observed difference did not attain statistical significance. Furthermore, no unexpected safety signals were identified with this combination therapy, and it was generally well tolerated. It is noteworthy that multiomics profiling revealed distinct remodeling patterns in the TME in response to NACI, which were closely correlated with treatment efficacy. These findings provide clinical evidence supporting the feasibility of ICI-based strategies in EOC and yield mechanistic insights into the immune dynamics underlying treatment response. Regarding survival outcomes, the median PFS was shown to be numerically prolonged by 5.4 months in the NACI group (27.17 months vs. 21.77 months in the NAC group). Historically, standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by IDS has yielded modest PFS outcomes: the median PFS ranged from approximately 12 months in the CHORUS and EORTC-55971 trials to 15 months in the SCORPION trial, underscoring the inherent limitations of chemotherapy alone in delaying disease progression.18–20 In the NAC group in our study, the PFS was marginally longer than that reported in traditional studies. This phenomenon may be attributable to the recent incorporation of maintenance treatment with drugs such as PARPis. In the PAOLA-1 study, the median PFS of the entire population receiving maintenance treatment was 22.9 months (95% CI 21.9–27.0),21 which is analogous to that of the NAC group. Notably, patients receiving PARP inhibitor maintenance derived significant benefit from NACI, suggesting a potential synergistic interaction between immunotherapy, chemotherapy-induced immunogenicity, and PARP inhibition. These preliminary signals need larger studies to validate and elucidate the underlying immunologic interactions.

Notably, the doubling of the 2-year PFS rate with NACI was 62.31% vs. 31.75% with NAC. In the Neo PembrOV trial,22 patients were administered carboplatin, paclitaxel, and pembrolizumab neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, which resulted in a 5% increase in the 2-year PFS rate. The single-arm phase II KGOG3046/TRU-D trial reported a 2-year PFS rate of 45% with durvalumab plus tremelimumab in combination with chemotherapy,23 outcomes that surpassed those observed in historical chemotherapy controls. Despite their modest sample sizes and the single-arm nature of the studies, the observed tendency toward PFS extension lends support to the notion that the incorporation of an ICI into platinum-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens holds promise in terms of clinical benefits, a phenomenon that is indicative of the “long tail” survival effect, a hallmark of ICI treatment.24 The findings of this study suggest that for a specific population of ovarian cancer patients, precise neoadjuvant immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy may offer survival benefits. Furthermore, the immunomodulatory effects of tislelizumab may enable more durable disease control beyond initial cytoreduction, potentially through the establishment of antitumor immunological memory. Importantly, the addition of tislelizumab did not compromise the feasibility or safety of subsequent IDS. The ORR rates were comparable between the two groups (69.2% in the NACI arm vs. 58.3% in the NAC arm; P > 0.05), and the spectrum and severity of grade ≥3 hematological toxicity and peripheral neuropathy were similar. irAEs occurred in 30.77% of patients in the NACI group, all of which were manageable and consistent with the known safety profile of PD-1 inhibitors.

The in-depth immune profiling provided by the present study offers a unique opportunity to gain a more profound understanding of the manner in which NACI influences the TME, with the aim of promoting antitumor immunity. Furthermore, the identification of potential predictive biomarkers is a significant outcome of the present study. scRNA-seq and CyTOF analyses revealed that NACI induced a broad expansion of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Studies on the functional status of CD8+ T cells have demonstrated the efficacy of immunotherapy.25,26 The most striking difference between NACI good responders (NACI-WR, CRS 3) and poor responders (NACI-PR, CRS 1–2) was the functional state of CD8+ T cells. Tumors from NACI-WR patients exhibited CD8+ T cells with high levels of cytotoxic effector molecules (e.g., GZMB, GZMK, PRF1, and IFNG) and displayed transcriptomic signatures indicative of active protein synthesis and metabolic fitness. Conversely, NACI-PR tumors exhibited a distinct profile of CD8+ T cells, marked by elevated expression of exhaustion markers (e.g., PDCD1, LAG3, TIGIT, and TOX) and signatures associated with the DNA damage response. This stark dichotomy suggests that the efficacy of NACI critically depends on its ability to reverse CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and unleash its cytotoxic potential within the TME. Mitochondria play a vital role in the regulation of CD8+ T-cell function. Recent findings have indicated that mitochondrial dysfunction can have a detrimental effect on CD8+ T-cell activity, contributing to immune exhaustion and impairing antitumor immunity.27 The increase in proliferating CD8+ TRM-mitotic cells in WRs further supports enhanced local immune activation. CD4+ T cells play key roles in both the priming and effector phases of the antitumor immune response and are attributed to immunotherapy efficacy.28 In the NACI group, the functional polarization of the CD4+ compartment differed between the response groups. NACI-WR tumors contained fewer CCR8+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) and more CXCL13+ Th1 cells, with the latter demonstrating extensive cellular crosstalk within the TME. This phenotype mirrors the “CXCL13–B cells axis” observed in ICI-sensitive malignancies, where CXCL13+ CD4+ T cells coordinate tertiary lymphoid structure (TLSs) formation and enhance local humoral immunity.29,30 Our findings that TLSs activity was elevated in the NACI-WR, closely correlated with CXCL13+ Th1-cell abundance, and was accompanied by GC B cell expansion suggest that the CD4–B–TLSs triad is a central determinant of therapeutic benefit in ovarian cancer immunotherapy. B-cell dynamics further reinforced this paradigm: the enrichment of GC B cells and differentiated IgG+ plasma cells in NACI-WR, along with active humoral immune gene signatures, aligns with recently reported findings in melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer, wherein TLS-resident B cells contribute to durable ICI responses.31,32 The predicted interactions between GC B-cell-derived APOE and CXCL13+ Th1 cells via T-cell activation-associated ligands provide a potential mechanistic link between B-cell maturation and effector T-cell activation. This interdependency may represent a promising biomarker and therapeutic target for enhancing NACI efficacy.33 Conversely, myeloid and stromal compartments in the NACI-PR group exhibited features consistent with those of immunosuppressive remodeling. The enrichment of NFKB1+ Mφ, along with elevated expression of IL-1A/B and PTGS2, suggests a proinflammatory yet protumorigenic myeloid phenotype, which has previously been implicated in resistance to ICIs via the recruitment of immunosuppressive neutrophils and fibroblasts.34,35 The expansion of specific cancer-associated fibroblast subsets in poor responders may further contribute to immune exclusion by modulating chemokine gradients and extracellular matrix stiffness.36 These findings highlight the multifactorial nature of immunotherapy resistance, wherein stromal and myeloid barriers may counteract lymphoid activation.

The present study is subject to several limitations. As a phase II trial, the findings are exploratory and not widely generalizable. The relatively single-center, small cohort size (26 patients enrolled, 25 undergoing surgery) restricted statistical power, particularly since the prespecified target sample size was not achieved. The incorporation of predictive biomarkers into patient screening was not a feature of the study, thus precluding the potential for biomarker-guided stratification, which could have refined therapeutic benefit. Furthermore, although factors such as homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) are closely related to the biological characteristics and treatment responsiveness of ovarian cancer, these associations were not fully evaluated due to the limitations of sample size in the group analysis. Notwithstanding the valuable mechanistic insights provided by the application of a multiomics approach, the limited number of paired samples from before and after treatment constrained the depth of the analyses. Collectively, these preliminary results from the NAIVE study highlight the necessity for larger, biomarker-stratified phase III trials integrating multiomics profiling to validate and extend the current findings.

In conclusion, the addition of tislelizumab to neoadjuvant chemotherapy represents an emerging therapeutic strategy for advanced EOC. Numerically, it prolonged PFS with a manageable safety profile. Our comprehensive immune profiling revealed that NACI induces distinct immune remodeling in the ovarian cancer TME that is correlated with treatment efficacy. The CXCL13+Th1–GC B-cell–TLS circuit is a pivotal axis associated with response, whereas myeloid and fibroblast subsets enrichment characterize resistance. These mechanistic insights pave the way for rationally designed combination strategies to enhance immunotherapy efficacy in EOC.

Materials and methods

Study design

The NAIVE study was an investigator-initiated, prospective, single-center, open-label, randomized controlled trial conducted in China (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04815408).17 The protocol received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval No. 2024-0374). All study procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before initiation of neoadjuvant therapy through direct, in-person communication, during which the study objectives, design, procedures, potential risks, and benefits were fully explained. Patients were also informed of their right to withdraw consent at any time, including after the early response assessment.

Participants

Eligible participants were women aged 18–75 years with histologically confirmed advanced epithelial ovarian cancer, defined as FIGO stage IIIc disease with a Suidan CT score ≥3 or Fagotti laparoscopic score ≥8, or FIGO stage IV disease. Additional key inclusion criteria were an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1 and no prior exposure to immunotherapy for malignancies. Patients were excluded if they had received immunosuppressive treatment, had immunodeficiency, HIV infection, hepatitis B or C infection, active autoimmune disease, or other concurrent malignancies.

Treatments

Patients who had enrolled in the study were randomized to receive chemotherapy alone (NAC group) or combined with tislelizumab (NACI group). Before IDS, patients in the NAC group underwent three cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, comprising nab-paclitaxel at a dosage of 260 mg/m² and carboplatin administered at an area under the curve (AUC) of 5 every three weeks. Patients allocated to the NACI arm received tislelizumab at a dose of 200 mg every three weeks concurrently with neoadjuvant chemotherapy. All patients in both groups received 3–5 cycles of nab-paclitaxel, carboplatin, and bevacizumab after IDS. The administration of maintenance therapy was executed according to the guidelines established by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). Maintenance therapy with PARPis (including olaparib and niraparib) was initiated 4–6 weeks after the completion of adjuvant chemotherapy.

Study assessments

The primary endpoints of the 1 and 2-year PFS rates and PFS were assessed using the Kaplan–Meier method. Laparoscopy, puncture biopsy, and cytology of the ascites pleural fluid were performed by histopathology to confirm the pretreatment diagnosis. Pathologic response was analyzed in post-IDS surgical specimens according to the criteria for CRS and was reviewed by a dedicated pathologist for standardized reporting.37 The CRS 3 classification was determined as a complete or near-complete response, characterized by the absence of residual tumor or the presence of minimal, irregularly scattered tumor foci measuring up to 2 mm in size. These tumor foci were observed as individual cells, cell groups, or nodules. The predominant finding was fibroinflammatory changes associated with regression, and in rare instances, there was an absence or minimal residual tumor, with no associated inflammatory response. The presence or absence of residual tumor in post-IDS surgical specimens was documented, with particular attention to microscopic residual tumor.

Tumors were evaluated using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V1.1, with cross-sectional imaging (CT/MRI scan) conducted at baseline, within seven days prior to IDS, at the conclusion of adjuvant therapy, every 6 months during the initial year of maintenance therapy, and at the time of suspected disease progression. The ORR was defined in accordance with the percentage of patients demonstrating the best overall clinical complete or partial response, as per RECIST V1.1. Three weeks after undergoing three cycles of NACT, patients underwent IDS. The assessment of adverse events (AEs) was conducted at the conclusion of each treatment cycle in adherence to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 4.03. An autonomous data monitoring committee conducted periodic reviews of the safety data during the course of the study.

Sample collection

Tumor specimens were obtained from primary lesions of ovarian cancer at pretreatment biopsies and IDS. To explore predictive biomarkers and immunologic mechanisms, integrated multiomics analyses were performed. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and mass cytometry (CyTOF) were applied to characterize the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and delineate immune subsets associated with the response.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), median, minimum, and maximum values. Categorical and ordinal variables are reported as frequencies, percentages, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Within-patient comparisons of continuous variables were made using paired t tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, while between-group comparisons were made using independent t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, and ordinal variables were evaluated with nonparametric tests such as Kruskal–Wallis. Repeated measurements were assessed using mixed-effects models or repeated-measures ANOVA. The missing data were addressed via available-case analysis. The analyses were performed utilizing SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R software (v4.2.2), with a two-sided P ≤ 0.05 designated as the threshold for statistical significance.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing and annotation

Raw scRNA-seq data were processed using Cell Ranger (10× Genomics) for alignment to the GRCh38 reference genome and generation of the Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) matrix. Subsequent to the generation of the UMI matrix, doublet scores for each cell were predicted with Scrublet. A cutoff of 0.25 was then employed to filter out doublets. Downstream analyses were performed using the Python toolkit Scanpy. In addition, high-quality cells were retained based on the following criteria: 1000–25,000 UMIs, 200–7000 detected genes, and mitochondrial gene content less than 15%. Subsequent to the implementation of quality control measures, the raw count matrix underwent normalization through the utilization of the normalize data function, employing LogNormalize as the designated normalization method. Thereafter, the matrix was scaled by a factor of 10,000 and underwent a logarithmic transformation. The selection of 2000 highly variable genes was conducted through the implementation of the Find Variable Features procedure. The count matrix was normalized using LogNormalize, scaled by a factor of 10,000, and log-transformed. Highly variable genes (n = 2000) were identified via the Find Variable Features procedure, and the effects of mitochondrial gene percentage and total UMI counts were regressed out during scaling. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted, and the top 30 PCs were used to construct nearest-neighbor graphs with the Find Neighbors function. Clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm with a resolution of 0.5, and dimensionality reduction for visualization was achieved with UMAP.

CyTOF analysis of immune cells

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from freshly isolated tissues. Briefly, the tissues were washed with culture medium, minced into approximately 1 mm³ fragments, and enzymatically dissociated using a human/mouse tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec) in a shaking incubator at 37 °C for 1 h. The resulting cell suspensions were filtered through a 70 μm strainer, centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and resuspended in cell staining buffer (CSB) for counting. Commercially sourced purified antibodies were metal-tagged using the MaxPAR Antibody Labeling Kit (Fluidigm). All the conjugated antibodies were titrated to determine the optimal staining concentrations. For CyTOF staining, the cells were first incubated with 250 nM cisplatin (Fluidigm) for 5 min on ice to label dead cells. After Fc receptor blockade, surface staining was performed with antibody cocktails for 30 min on ice. Cells were subsequently washed, fixed overnight in Maxpar Fix and Perm Buffer containing 191/193Ir DNA intercalator (Fluidigm), permeabilized, and stained intracellularly. Following washing, the cells were resuspended in deionized water with 20% EQ calibration beads (Fluidigm) and analyzed on a Helios mass cytometer (Fluidigm). The raw data were debarcoded using unique mass-tag barcodes and normalized across batches with bead-based normalization. Dead cells, debris, and doublets were excluded by manual gating in FlowJo. Live single cells were clustered via the X-shift algorithm, and cell subsets were annotated on the basis of marker expression patterns visualized on heatmaps. t-SNE was used for dimensionality reduction. Population frequencies were compared using t-tests.
