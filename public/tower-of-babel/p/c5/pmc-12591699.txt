Hysterectomy Status and Outcomes in Patients With Grade 1, Stage IA Endometrioid Adenocarcinoma
Authors: Vasanti Jhaveri, Larissa Meyer, Lu Chen, Koji Matsuo, Roshni P. Romero, Victor Khor, Yvonne G. Lin, Jason D. Wright, Sarah P. Huepenbecker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVE:To compare clinicodemographic factors and outcomes of patients with grade 1, stage IA endometrioid adenocarcinoma who did and did not undergo hysterectomy.METHODS:This is a retrospective cohort study using data from the SEER (Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results) registry program, which collates population-based cancer registry information from 26.5% of the U.S. population. The SEER database identified patients aged 18 years or older with grade 1, stage IA endometroid adenocarcinoma from 2010 to 2020. The primary outcome was prevalence of patients who did and did not undergo hysterectomy. Secondary outcomes included endometrial and cardiovascular-specific death and all-cause mortality by surgical status and age. Descriptive and comparative statistics between the hysterectomy and nonhysterectomy cohorts were completed. Survival between surgical cohorts was compared using Fine-Gray models for cause-specific death (endometrial cancer, cardiovascular) and Cox models for all-cause mortality.RESULTS:Of 27,331 patients, 26,984 (98.7%) had hysterectomy and 347 (1.3%) did not. Most patients were between age 50 and 69 years (67.2%) and were non-Hispanic White (66.9%). Patients without hysterectomy were more likely to be younger than age 50 (54.8% vs 16.8%, P<.001), 80 years or older (7.2% vs 2.6%, P<.001), Hispanic (24.8% vs 15.6%, P<.001), and non-Hispanic Black (13.3% vs 5.7%, P<.001). The nonhysterectomy cohort had a higher cumulative incidence of death attributable to endometrial cancer (4.2% vs 1.2%), cardiovascular disease (7.8% vs 2.1%), and all-cause (23% vs 8.2%) mortality compared with the hysterectomy cohort.CONCLUSION:Patients from racial and ethnic minority groups and with extremes of age were less likely to undergo hysterectomy. Deaths resulting from endometrial cancer and cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality were higher in patients who did not undergo hysterectomy.

The recommendation for patients with presumed early-stage and early-grade endometrial cancer at presentation is to undergo hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and lymph node evaluation, which is associated with a 5-year survival rate of 95%.1

There is a subset of patients with uterine-confined endometrial cancer who may not undergo hysterectomy for their primary treatment. This group may include patients of reproductive age who desire fertility preservation or patients who are medically inoperable because of extensive comorbidities.2,3 There are specific criteria for patients considering fertility-sparing treatment, including the presence of a well-differentiated (grade 1) endometroid adenocarcinoma on dilatation and curettage, disease limited to the endometrium on imaging, absence of suspicious or metastatic disease, and no contraindications to medical therapy, including pregnancy and patient counseling.1 Younger patients are frequently managed medically with hormone treatment with a recommendation for surgical management once childbearing is complete.1,4 Primary treatment consists of continuous progestin-based therapy, including megestrol, medroxyprogesterone, and levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine device with consideration of dual progestin therapy.1

Although we acknowledge that a cohort of patients who do not undergo surgical management for uterine-confined endometrial cancer exists, population-level data on the epidemiology and outcomes of these patients in the United States are unknown. Previously published population-level data on early-stage endometrioid adenocarcinoma are limited to patients who underwent hysterectomy with ovarian conservation before age 50 years or had supracervical hysterectomy.5,6 Thus, a more comprehensive characterization of nonsurgical management among patients with early-stage, early-grade cancer is needed.

Our primary objective was to compare clinicodemographic factors and outcomes of patients with grade 1, stage IA endometrioid adenocarcinoma between those who did and those who did not undergo hysterectomy using the nationwide SEER (Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results) data. Our secondary objectives were to determine endometrial cancer–specific survival, cardiovascular disease (CVD) death, and all-cause mortality by surgical status and age.

METHODS

This is a retrospective cohort study using data from the SEER 17 registry program, which collates population-based cancer registry information from 26.5% of the U.S. population.7 The SEER program, developed by the National Cancer Institute, routinely collects and publishes data for cancer cases, including patient demographics; characteristics of the primary tumor such as histology, grade, and stage at initial diagnosis; and treatment course. Because SEER is a publicly available database with deidentified patient information, IRB approval was not required.

Using SEER*Stat, we identified patients aged 18 years or older with primary grade 1, stage IA endometrioid endometrial adenocarcinoma from 2010 to 2020. Staging for cases diagnosed between 2010 and 2015 was defined through American Joint Committee on Cancer 7th staging schema. Cases diagnosed between 2016 and 2017 were staged according to TNM Classification of Malignant Tumors, 7th Edition.8 Those diagnosed in 2018 or later were staged according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th staging schema. Both the American Joint Committee on Cancer 7th and 8th editions use the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics 2008 endometrial cancer surgical staging system. For patients who underwent hysterectomy, stage and histology were confirmed on pathology per the SEER summary stage manual. For nonsurgical staging of uterine cancer, the relevant codes are found through the SEER summary stage manual, which has specific codes depending on the extent of the cancer based on imaging and clinical findings.9 Because the nonhysterectomy cohort was not surgically staged, cancer was assumed to be early stage on the basis of the clinical and imaging findings that were collected and recorded in the SEER database according to this summary stage manual. Patients younger than age 18 years at the time of diagnosis, who had a history of other primary cancers, or who had radiation or chemotherapy were excluded. Cancer cases that were reported through death certificate or autopsy; cases that had a missing stage, tumor grade, or surgical status; or patients whose diagnosis was not confirmed with tissue diagnosis were excluded (Appendix 1, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E402). Patients who underwent primary radiation were excluded from this patient cohort because treatment with radiation would be the primary treatment of choice for patients with early-stage endometrial cancer who were medically inoperable.1 By excluding patients who were medically unfit for surgery, we were able to focus on a specific patient cohort assumed to be fit for a surgical intervention.

Patients were classified as having hysterectomy or not having hysterectomy according to the SEER Program Coding and Staging Manual and surgical coding. Patients who had hysterectomy with or without removal of adnexal structures were included in the hysterectomy group. Patients who did not undergo surgery or had an alternative surgery (ie, local tumor destruction by biopsy, polypectomy, or myomectomy) were included in the nonhysterectomy group. The route of hysterectomy (ie, minimally invasive and open) was not available in SEER.

Baseline patient characteristic variables collected included age at the time of diagnosis (18–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and 80 years or older) and race and ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native, non-Hispanic Asian or Pacific Islander, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic unknown race, and non-Hispanic White). Race was examined because differences in cancer incidence, treatment patterns, and survival across racial groups exist. Evaluating these associations may help identify disparities and improve the generalizability of our findings. In subgroup analyses, patients were stratified by an age cutoff of 50 years as a proxy for menopausal status. Race and ethnicity are identified and coded in the SEER database primarily according to a patient's self-declared identification; if this information was not available, race and ethnicity were based on documentation in the medical record. Year of diagnosis was stratified in 3- or 4-year intervals. Time-dependent county attributes, including rural compared with urban status and median income of the county, were provided by SEER and based on U.S. Census data and 5-year estimates from the American Community Surveys.10 Tumor size was categorized in 2-cm increments.

The primary outcome of this study was to determine the prevalence of hysterectomy as the primary treatment among patients with grade 1, stage IA endometrioid adenocarcinoma from 2010 to 2020. Demographics, clinical characteristics, socioeconomic factors, and age were assessed according to hysterectomy or nonhysterectomy cohort. For patients who did not have hysterectomy, the reason for foregoing surgical management was summarized with the use of SEER coding and categorization. For patients who did have hysterectomy, the distribution of time from diagnosis to surgery date was analyzed. We also compared cancer-specific survival, deaths attributable to CVD, and all-cause mortality by hysterectomy status. Cardiovascular disease mortality was specifically examined as a secondary outcome in this study because CVD is the most common noncancer cause of mortality in patients with endometrial cancer.11 Mortality data in SEER are reported from the National Center for Health Statistics, which collects data from all state vital health reporting systems, and records detailed cause of deaths from death certificates.12 Cause of death was determined by the SEER cause-specific death classification variable.

We conducted descriptive analyses on patients' baseline characteristics by surgery status by summarizing frequencies and percentages for each variable. To assess the statistical significance of differences between patients with and those without hysterectomy, P values were calculated with the χ2 test or the Fisher exact test when more than 20% of cells have an expected count less than 5. Prevalence of hysterectomy was calculated as the proportion of those with surgery of all patients, first overall and then by age and race and ethnicity. The Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model was used to estimate the cumulative incidence of endometrial cancer and CVD deaths while better accounting for competing risks of death, and the Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate all-cause mortality.13 Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were calculated to compare individuals with and without a prior hysterectomy. Cause-specific hazards for cancer and CVD death were estimated with Fine–Gray models to account for competing risks. For all-cause mortality, a Cox proportional hazards model was used. All models were adjusted for the baseline characteristics detailed in Table 1. A small proportion of individuals with missing covariates were excluded from the multivariate regression model analysis. Small numbers in the nonhysterectomy cohort precluded statistical analysis for survival outcomes and analysis based on stratification by age and race.

RESULTS

A total of 27,331 of patients with grade 1, stage 1A endometrioid adenocarcinoma were identified for this study who met criteria for analysis (S1), including 26,984 patients (98.7%) who had hysterectomy and 347patients (1.3%) who did not have hysterectomy (Table 1). Most patients were between age 50 and 69 years (67.2%), were Non-Hispanic White (66.9%), lived in a county with a median income above $75,000 (45.5%), and lived in an urban area (88.9%). Patients who did undergo hysterectomy and those who did not have hysterectomy were significantly different on the distributions of age, race and ethnicity, year of diagnosis, and median county income (all P<.05). Specifically, patients without hysterectomy were more likely to be younger than age 50 years (54.8% vs 16.8%), age 80 years or older (7.2% vs 2.6%), all-race Hispanic (24.8% vs 15.6%), and non-Hispanic Black (13.3% vs 5.7%) but were less likely to be diagnosed in the most recent period of 2018–2020 (19.0% vs 37.6%) or living in counties with the highest income category above $75,000 (36.0% vs 45.6%). There was no difference between the hysterectomy and nonhysterectomy cohorts based on tumor size or rural–urban residency (Table 1).

Among patients younger than age 35 years, 17–33% did not undergo hysterectomy for endometrial cancer compared with 6.2% of those 35–39 years, 1–2% of those 40–84 years, and 6.7% of those aged 85 years (Appendix 2, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E402, and Fig. 1). When further stratified by race and age, non-Hispanic White patients aged 18–49 years (2.8%) were less likely to be in the nonhysterectomy cohort compared with Hispanic (4.9%), non-Hispanic Asian or Pacific Islander (4.0%), and non-Hispanic Black (8.2%) patients (P<.001) (Fig. 2). In the cohort of patients who did not undergo hysterectomy, the most likely reason was that it was not recommended by the clinician (42.1%). The other reasons included that an alternative surgery was performed (35.4%) or the surgery was recommended but the patient refused (13.0%). Less common reasons were that surgery was recommended but it was unknown whether it was performed (4.9%), surgery was recommended but was not performed for an unknown reason (2.9%), and surgery was not recommended because of a contraindication (1.7%). Among those who had alternative surgical procedures, the most frequent surgical procedures included polypectomy (37%), excisional biopsy (37%), and local tumor excision not otherwise specified (17%) (Appendix 3, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E402).

Among the cohort of patients who did have hysterectomy, 74.3% of patients underwent hysterectomy within 1 month after diagnosis compared with 3.1% of patients who had a delay exceeding 4 months. When categorized by age, patients younger than age 50 years and 50 years or older had a median time to hysterectomy of 1 month (Appendix 4, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E402).

Over a median follow-up of 4 years, there was a low incidence of death attributable to endometrial cancer in the study population, with a total of 208 deaths representing 0.8% (95% CI, 0.7–0.9%) of the total cohort. Despite an overall low incidence, the cumulative incidence of endometrial cancer death (accounting for competing risk of other deaths) in the hysterectomy cohort was relatively lower at 0.4% (95% CI, 0.3–0.5%), 0.8% (95% CI, 0.7–1.0%), and 1.2% (95% CI, 1.1–1.4%) at years 3, 5 and 8, respectively, compared with 3.0% (95% CI, 1.5–5.4%), 3.0% (95% CI, 1.5–5.4%), and 4.2% (95% CI, 2.2–7.3%) in the nonhysterectomy cohort (Fig. 3A), with an adjusted HR of 4.1 (95% CI, 2.3–7.4). There were a total of 325 CVD deaths representing 1.2% (95% CI, 1.1–1.3%) of the overall cohort. The cumulative incidence of CVD death in the hysterectomy cohort was 0.5% (95% CI, 0.4–0.6%), 1.0% (95% CI, 0.9–1.2%), and 2.1% (95% CI, 1.9–2.4%) at years 3, 5, and 8, respectively, compared with 4.8% (95% CI, 2.8–7.6%), 6.2% (95% CI, 3.8–9.4%), and 7.8% (95% CI, 4.7–12%) in the nonhysterectomy cohort (Fig. 3B), with an adjusted HR of 5.6 (95% CI, 3.4–9.4). Lastly, all-cause mortality over the 4-year median follow-up was 1,294 deaths, representing 4.7% (95% CI, 4.5– 5.0%) of the overall cohort. The all-cause mortality in the hysterectomy cohort was 2.1% (95% CI, 1.9–2.3%), 4.3% (95% CI, 4.0–4.6%), and 8.2% (95% CI, 7.7–8.7%) at years 3, 5, and 8, respectively, compared with 13.0% (95% CI, 9.4–17%), 17.0% (95% CI, 13.0–21.0%), and 23.0% (95% CI, 17.0– 29.0%) in the nonhysterectomy cohort (Fig. 4), with an adjusted HR of 5.0 (95% CI, 3.8–6.6).

When we examined causes of death and overall mortality in patients younger than age 50 years, the proportion of all deaths in the hysterectomy cohort was lower compared with the nonhysterectomy cohort (n=67 [1.5%] vs n=8 [4.2%]). Deaths attributable to endometrial cancer accounted for a smaller proportion of all deaths in the hysterectomy cohort compared with the nonhysterectomy cohort (n=16 [23.9%] vs n=4 [50.0%]), and the proportion of deaths attributable to CVD was higher in the hysterectomy cohort compared with the nonhysterectomy cohort (n=18 [26.9%] vs n=1 [12.5%]).

For patients aged 50 years or older, the proportion of all deaths was also lower in the hysterectomy cohort compared with the nonhysterectomy cohort (n=1,167 [5.2%] vs n=52 [33%]). The relative proportion for endometrial cancer deaths was higher in the hysterectomy compared with the nonhysterectomy cohort (n=181 [15.5%] vs n=7 [13.5%]), but CVD mortality was lower (n=286 [24.5%] vs n=20 [38.5%]) (Appendix 5, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E402).

DISCUSSION

We identified a small cohort of patients with low-grade, stage IA endometrioid adenocarcinoma who did not undergo hysterectomy. These patients are more likely to be from a minority racial and ethnic group and at the extremes of age. Mortality attributable to endometrial cancer, CVD mortality, and all-cause mortality were higher in patients who did not undergo hysterectomy.

A prior SEER study reporting the characteristics and survival outcome of patients with early-stage endometrial cancer found that a supracervical (compared with total) hysterectomy was an independent prognostic factor for decreased endometrial cancer–specific survival.6 Another population analysis in patients younger than age 50 years with stage I endometrioid adenocarcinoma found that ovarian conservation did not adversely affect survival.5 Both studies focused on specific surgical procedures for low-grade endometrial adenocarcinoma management. We aimed to capture patients with early-stage endometrial cancer who were medically fit to undergo hysterectomy by excluding those who underwent primary radiation. We focused on an assumed medically fit patient population to better characterize our results, supported by the finding that only 1.7% of our nonsurgical cohort had a documented medical contraindication to surgery.

We found that underrepresented racial and ethnic groups, including non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and non-Hispanic Asian/Pacific Islander patients, were less likely to undergo surgical management. It has been recognized that Black and Hispanic patients are less likely to receive guideline-concordant care, including hysterectomy, and definitive surgical treatment compared with White and Asian/Pacific Islander patients.14,15 Our study found that Asian/Pacific Islander patients may be less likely to undergo a standard-of-care hysterectomy. Although prior studies have demonstrated differences in stage of endometrial cancer at diagnosis among Asian/Pacific Islander patients, the disparities we found in our cohort of patients with early-stage early-grade disease are not explained by stage of disease, for which the standard of care is clearly defined.16 Our study supports previously published data on racial disparities observed with endometrial cancer, including that young Black and Hispanic patients with endometrial cancer are more likely than their White counterparts to forego hysterectomy or to wait more than 4 months after diagnosis for the hysterectomy.

This study highlights the need to better understand the oncofertility implications of uterine-preservation treatment options for young patients with endometrial cancer. We specifically studied premenopausal individuals for whom hysterectomy might be deferred to preserve fertility. Previous publications have shown that conservative treatment options for select patients are safe and feasible options with close monitoring and until family planning is completed.2,17 Previously, Wright et al5 studied the effect of progesterone therapy on all-cause mortality in young patients and found that patients treated with hormonal therapy were 92% more likely to die compared with patients who underwent primary hysterectomy.18 Our study supports previously published work that relative morality is higher in patients foregoing hysterectomy and remains evident in patients younger than age 50 years. In this cohort of patients, deaths resulting from endometrial cancer doubled (50%) compared with those who underwent hysterectomy (24%). Unfortunately, the proportion of patients who experience a live birth after this diagnosis is relatively small, with a prior study demonstrating a live birth rate of 11.6% among patients treated with progesterone therapy alone.19 These findings emphasize the importance of timely surgical management once family planning is complete and underscores the need for comprehensive counseling.

Our study has multiple strengths. It offers contemporary insights into surgical trends for grade 1, stage IA endometrial adenocarcinoma. It identifies populations less likely to undergo surgery and provides U.S. population–level estimates on endometrial, CVD, and all-cause mortality. Using the SEER database, an extensive national cancer registry, we were able to examine rare outcomes in a large cohort over an extended follow-up period.

However, the study has limitations. First, SEER does not capture data on reasons for omitting hysterectomy, patient comorbidities, or overall health status. This limitation hinders our ability to fully understand clinical decision making and to interpret the observed differences in survival outcomes by surgery status. Although we adjusted for available covariates, the HRs, despite being statistically significant, remain vulnerable to uncontrolled confounding by these unmeasured factors, precluding causal inference. Second, the findings apply only to the U.S. population. Third, because of the small number of patients in the nonhysterectomy group, only 1.3% of the cohort, we could not perform multivariate analyses for survival outcomes or stratify results by both age and race. Fourth, we excluded patients undergoing primary radiation because we assumed that these patients were not candidates for surgery according to National Comprehensive Cancer Network guideline recommendations, although this may have biased our study population. In addition, although nonsurgical patients were assumed to have stage IA disease based on clinical evaluation and imaging, some may have had higher-stage disease, affecting survival.

In conclusion, our study identified a cohort of patients with early-stage, low-grade endometrial cancer who did not undergo hysterectomy, placing them at increased risk of worse endometrial, cardiovascular, and all-cause mortality. Further research is needed to explore interventions that can mitigate oncologic and nononcologic risks in these vulnerable patient populations to ensure equitable and optimal clinical outcomes.
