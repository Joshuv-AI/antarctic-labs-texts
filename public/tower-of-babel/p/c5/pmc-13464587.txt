Direct Percutaneous Retrograde Thoracic Duct Access for Embolization Using a Modified Sheath Technique for the Treatment of Chyloptysis and Plastic Bronchitis
Authors: Yee Ming Chong, John Chung Yan Chan, Kelvin Kin Ho Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thoracic duct embolization is an established minimally invasive treatment for thoracic lymphatic disorders refractory to conservative therapy. We report the case of a 58-year-old man with chyloptysis and plastic bronchitis who presented with persistent respiratory symptoms despite conservative management. Intranodal lymphangiography demonstrated opacification of the thoracic duct. However, antegrade access via the cisterna chyli was not feasible because of its challenging location, and retrograde transvenous access was unsuccessful because of ductal tortuosity. Ultrasound demonstrated a dilated upper thoracic duct in the left lower neck, which was accessed percutaneously using a micropuncture set adapted as a modified sheath, followed by successful navigation of the microcatheter into the cisterna chyli. Successful embolization was performed using a mixture of n-butyl cyanoacrylate and ethiodized oil, resulting in immediate symptomatic improvement and resolution of the patient’s supplemental oxygen requirement. This case demonstrates that direct percutaneous retrograde thoracic duct access using a modified sheath technique is a feasible alternative when conventional approaches are technically challenging.

Introduction

Chyloptysis and plastic bronchitis are thoracic lymphatic disorders. Chyloptysis, a rare condition, is characterized by expectoration of chyle and is likely secondary to an underlying lymphatic abnormality with reflux of chyle into the airways [1]. It can also result from reflux of chylothorax with a concomitant bronchopleural fistula [2]. Plastic bronchitis is characterized by the formation of casts in the airways from leaked chyle and can be secondary to chyloptysis [3]. Traditionally, these conditions are managed with conservative treatment, such as a low-fat diet and pleural drainage, as well as surgical treatment, such as thoracic duct ligation, if conservative treatment fails [4]. Interventional radiologic treatment with thoracic duct embolization has reemerged as a less invasive option for thoracic lymphatic disorders refractory to conservative treatment [4]. It is traditionally performed by antegrade access to the thoracic duct via the cisterna chyli through a percutaneous transabdominal approach or by retrograde venous access through the lymphovenous junction at the left subclavian vein. Direct percutaneous access to the thoracic duct has also been reported, with comparable results. We report a case of thoracic duct embolization via ultrasound-guided direct puncture of the dilated upper thoracic duct using a modified sheath technique.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old man with a history of stage 4 diffuse large B-cell lymphoma initially presented with a mesenteric mass four years before the index episode, with disease progression despite multiple lines of chemotherapy. His lymphoma eventually went into remission following CAR-T therapy one year before the index episode. He had not received radiotherapy to the chest.

During the index episode, he presented with a persistent cough and desaturation requiring supplemental oxygen. The patient was initially admitted for shortness of breath and treated empirically for pneumonia and concurrent heart failure. His symptoms persisted despite optimal medical treatment, with chest radiography demonstrating persistent right lower zone consolidation. The patient also reported expectoration of thick material. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed and yielded fibrinous material that was negative for infection or neoplasm. The chyle test was positive in the BAL specimen. A clinical diagnosis of chyloptysis and plastic bronchitis was established.

Intravenous contrast-enhanced CT of the thorax showed no central venous obstruction or mediastinal lymphadenopathy. After multidisciplinary team discussion, the patient was scheduled to undergo thoracic duct embolization for symptomatic relief.

A bilateral inguinal intranodal lymphangiogram was performed by accessing bilateral inguinal lymph nodes with a 25-G spinal needle under sonographic guidance, followed by sequential injection of 5 mL of ethiodized oil (Lipiodol, Guerbet, Villepinte, France) and 5 mL of 0.9% normal saline on both sides. Opacification of the cisterna chyli (Figure 1), thoracic duct, and lymphovenous junction (Figure 2) was achieved.

The cone-beam CT also demonstrated the cisterna chyli, which was small (3 mm in diameter) and positioned posterolateral to the abdominal aorta (Figure 3). Transabdominal access for antegrade thoracic duct embolization was therefore considered challenging.

Transvenous retrograde access to the lymphovenous junction from the left basilic vein was initially attempted. Cannulation of the lymphovenous junction was achieved after a prolonged attempt, with no stenosis of the lymphovenous junction demonstrated; however, the tortuous upper thoracic duct precluded stable access and cannulation with a microcatheter system (Figure 4).

Screening sonography of the left neck demonstrated a dilated tubular structure in the left lower neck communicating with the left subclavian vein (Figure 5), with no signal on color Doppler examination (Figure 6), corresponding to the dilated upper thoracic duct and lymphovenous junction visualized on conventional lymphangiography, respectively.

The decision was made to directly access this dilated portion of the thoracic duct. Under sonographic guidance, a 4 Fr micropuncture mini access kit (Merit Medical Systems, South Jordan, UT, USA) was inserted into the dilated thoracic duct using the Seldinger technique (Figure 7), acting as a modified sheath (Figure 8) to accommodate the microcatheter-microguidewire system. This approach was adapted from neonatal interventional radiology experience using the micropuncture set as a modified 3 Fr sheath to accommodate the microcatheter system [5]. A 2.4 Fr Maestro catheter was then inserted through this modified sheath with various microguidewires, including the V18 ControlWire (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) and Glidewire Advantage (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan). The cisterna chyli was eventually accessed with the microcatheter system, with confirmation by injection of water-soluble contrast (Figure 9). A total of 1.8 mL of a 1:1 mixture of n-butyl cyanoacrylate (Histoacryl, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) and ethiodized oil (Lipiodol, Guerbet) was injected through the microcatheter for definitive embolization. The lower portion of the thoracic duct and the cisterna chyli were successfully embolized (Figure 10).

The patient reported immediate improvement in shortness of breath and no longer required supplemental oxygen. He was discharged the next day after the procedure with complete resolution of symptoms.

Discussion

Chyloptysis and plastic bronchitis are pulmonary lymphatic disorders caused by abnormal lymphatic flow. Chyloptysis involves abnormal pulmonary lymphatic flow into the bronchial airways [3]. Plastic bronchitis is a rare condition in which protein-rich casts form in the bronchi and can cause cough, wheezing, dyspnea, or respiratory failure.

Establishing the diagnosis requires input from multiple perspectives, including clinical and laboratory evaluation to exclude other causes, such as asthma and infections, including allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis. Bronchoscopy is helpful in identifying and removing the bronchial casts, as well as excluding other differential diagnoses. Imaging can be challenging in these conditions and may be nonspecific because of the overlapping radiologic features of these conditions and their differential diagnoses. An increased incidence of ground-glass opacities has been reported in patients with lymphatic plastic bronchitis compared with patients with non-lymphatic plastic bronchitis [3]. Treatment combines airway clearance, bronchoscopy, and lymphatic embolization or surgical correction.

The cause of the thoracic duct dilatation is not well elucidated in our patient, but we propose that it may have been related to chemotherapy, which has also been reported in the literature as a cause of lymphatic disorders with negative lymphangiography [6].

Access to the thoracic duct for embolization is achieved by first mapping the lymphatic system and then selecting a route to the duct based on anatomy and operator experience. The most common modern approach is bilateral inguinal intranodal lymphangiography using contrast such as ethiodized oil or water-soluble contrast, which is simpler and faster than traditional pedal lymphangiography and is associated with fewer complications [7].

Direct percutaneous transabdominal access can be performed with a fine Chiba needle under fluoroscopy, followed by wire and microcatheter advancement into the thoracic duct without a vascular sheath after visualization of the cisterna chyli [8]. Transabdominal access necessarily traverses intra-abdominal structures, such as the liver, small intestine, and pancreas. An excellent safety profile has been reported in a retrospective review of patients undergoing transabdominal access to the cisterna chyli for thoracic duct intervention, with a complication rate of less than 3%, provided that the aorta and large intestine are avoided [8].

Alternative routes, apart from direct antegrade access, include retrograde cervical access to the lymphovenous junction either by transvenous catheterization from the left venous system [9] or by direct access to the upper thoracic duct [10,11].

However, there is a wide spectrum of variation in the anatomy and morphology of the terminal thoracic duct and the location of the lymphovenous junction [12-14]. The lymphovenous junction can be located at the bilateral venous angles, the left internal jugular vein, the left subclavian vein, or the left external jugular vein [12,13]. The thoracic duct can extend superiorly above the clavicle before terminating at the venous angle and may terminate in the venous system as a single duct, two ducts, or three terminal branches with variable branching and reanastomosing patterns [14].

Retrograde transvenous cannulation of the terminal thoracic duct requires careful planning based on lymphangiographic findings and may sometimes be technically challenging or even impossible, as the caliber of the terminal thoracic duct may be smaller in cases with multiple branches than in those with a single duct. Direct percutaneous access to the distal portion of the thoracic duct can be helpful in these cases by accessing a relatively large-caliber segment that can accommodate the microcatheter system for embolization. Previous literature has also reported that combining percutaneous access with retrograde transvenous access significantly improves the technical success rate as a bailout strategy [15].

As illustrated in our case, direct access to the thoracic duct provides a shorter access route and bypasses the tortuous thoracic duct, potentially providing greater stability than transvenous access. This is consistent with previous literature reporting improved overall technical success in thoracic duct cannulation [15]. Our case also demonstrated that the use of a modified sheath for percutaneous retrograde access to the thoracic duct, which has been inconsistently reported in the previous literature [10,11,15], provided additional stability and allowed navigation through the tortuous thoracic duct.

Conclusions

Chyloptysis and plastic bronchitis are thoracic lymphatic disorders caused by abnormal lymphatic flow through the thoracic duct. Clinical presentation is variable, ranging from incidental findings in asymptomatic individuals to clinically significant respiratory compromise, as illustrated in our case. Thoracic duct embolization can be considered in these patients. When traditional antegrade access to the cisterna chyli or retrograde transvenous access to the thoracic duct is not technically feasible, direct percutaneous retrograde thoracic duct access can be performed using a micropuncture set as a modified sheath to stabilize access. We report a case of chyloptysis and plastic bronchitis successfully treated with direct percutaneous retrograde thoracic duct access using a micropuncture set as a modified sheath, followed by embolization, demonstrating that this technique may be a safe and effective alternative to conventional approaches for accessing the thoracic lymphatic system.
