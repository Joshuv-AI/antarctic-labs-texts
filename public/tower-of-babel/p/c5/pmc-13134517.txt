Targeting Inflammation and Immune Regulation in Chronic Inflammation Associated Cancers
Authors: Lawan Rabiu, Mengzhu Chinn, Feng Chen, Yerkezhan Yerkinkazhina, Yuan Tian, Wei‐Guo Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic inflammation is a fundamental driver of cancer development, linking persistent immune activation to genomic instability, microenvironmental remodeling, and malignant transformation. In inflammation‐associated malignancies such as colitis‐associated cancer (CAC) and metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease–related hepatocellular carcinoma (MAFLD‐HCC), sustained inflammatory signaling integrates tissue injury with oncogenic pathways to promote tumor initiation and progression. Importantly, these cancers arise through prolonged preneoplastic stages, during which dysregulated immune and inflammatory responses not only drive malignant transformation but also create opportunities for cancer prevention and early disease interception. CAC and MAFLD‐HCC share convergent mechanisms, including IL‐6/STAT3 and NF‐κB activation, cytokine‐driven survival signaling, and cooperation with genetic alterations in APC, KRAS, and TP53. A critical but often underappreciated dimension of this process is the failure of endogenous immunoregulatory systems that normally restrain excessive inflammation. Among these, the negative regulator of immune cells TIPE2 plays an important role in limiting inflammatory signaling, and its reduced activity contributes to the persistence of a tumor‐promoting inflammatory microenvironment that supports both disease initiation and progression. This review synthesizes the shared inflammatory, immunologic, and microenvironmental mechanisms underpinning CAC and MAFLD‐HCC, with a particular emphasis on how impaired immune regulation drives the transition from chronic inflammation to cancer. We further highlight therapeutic and preventive strategies targeting inflammation‐driven pathways, underscoring the dual relevance of immune modulation for cancer prevention in chronic inflammatory disease and for the treatment of established malignancy.Keywords: cancer, chronic inflammation, immune regulation, inflammation associated cancers, tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Chronic inflammation is a well‐established driver of cancer initiation, promotion, and progression, acting through sustained immune activation, molecular reprogramming, and microenvironmental remodeling. Persistent inflammatory responses generate a tumor‐promoting milieu enriched with cytokines, reactive oxygen species, and growth factors that induce genomic instability, disrupt tissue homeostasis, and facilitate malignant transformation [1]. As a result, inflammation‐associated cancers (IACs) account for a substantial proportion of global cancer morbidity and mortality, emphasizing the clinical importance of understanding inflammation‐driven tumorigenesis [2].

Cancer development in chronically inflamed tissues follows a multistep process marked by the progressive accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations that transform normal cells into dysplastic lesions and ultimately invasive malignancies [3]. Central inflammatory mediators, including NF‐κB, STAT3, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, sustain proliferative signaling, suppress apoptosis, and reshape the tissue microenvironment, thereby creating conditions that favor tumor initiation and progression [4].

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents a prototypical example of inflammation‐driven malignancy and remains a leading cause of cancer‐related death worldwide. CRC is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer globally [5]. The risk is markedly elevated in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease, where persistent mucosal inflammation promotes colitis‐associated cancer (CAC) development [6, 7, 8]. Beyond the intestine, chronic inflammation similarly drives carcinogenesis in other organs. Metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) is a major precursor of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), with sustained hepatic inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune dysregulation contributing to tumor progression through mechanisms paralleling those observed in CAC [9, 10]. These shared features highlight chronic inflammation as a unifying pathogenic driver across diverse malignancies.

Despite advances in defining inflammatory signaling pathways, the immune regulatory mechanisms controlling the transition from chronic inflammation to cancer remain incompletely understood. Among these regulatory mechanisms, TNFAIP8‐like 2 (TIPE2) has emerged as an important negative regulator of immune and inflammatory signaling. TIPE2 restrains excessive activation of pathways such as NF‐κB, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK, thereby maintaining tissue homeostasis during chronic inflammatory stress [11]. Dysregulated or reduced TIPE2 expression has been linked to heightened inflammatory responses and increased susceptibility to inflammation‐associated cancers [12], making it a relevant molecule for understanding the transition from chronic inflammation to tumorigenesis. Increasing attention is therefore focused on regulatory networks that restrain excessive inflammation and maintain tissue homeostasis, as their dysregulation may critically influence inflammation‐driven tumorigenesis and therapeutic responsiveness [12]. Importantly, IACs such as CAC and MAFLD‐HCC arise through a prolonged preneoplastic phase characterized by chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation. In this context, therapeutic strategies targeting inflammatory and immune pathways are highly relevant not only for the treatment of established malignancy but also for cancer prevention and early disease interception.

Inflammatory Pathogenesis of Chronic‐IACs

Chronic inflammation associated cancers arise from the coordinated effects of persistent immune‐mediated injury, dysregulated signaling pathways, and the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations. In tissues such as the colon and liver, long‐standing inflammatory insults activate core oncogenic pathways, including NF‐κB, STAT3, and PI3K/Akt, which promote sustained proliferation, inhibit apoptosis, and remodel the local microenvironment [13, 14]. Similar pathway activation is observed in CAC and metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease–related hepatocellular carcinoma (MAFLD‐HCC), highlighting a convergent molecular basis for inflammation‐driven tumorigenesis across organs [15].

Colitis‐associated cancers arise through an inflammation‐driven dysplasia–carcinoma sequence, which is biologically and temporally distinct from the APC‐initiated adenoma–carcinoma pathway that characterizes sporadic colorectal cancer.

A defining feature of these malignancies is the inflammation–dysplasia–carcinoma sequence, which is biologically and temporally distinct from the adenoma–carcinoma pathway that characterizes sporadic cancer [16, 17]. Continuous exposure to inflammatory cytokines and growth factors drives persistent immune cell infiltration and repeated cycles of tissue damage and repair [18]. This inflammatory milieu promotes excessive production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, resulting in oxidative DNA damage, chromosomal instability, and mutations in critical oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes [19, 20]. Over time, these cumulative insults facilitate the progression from chronically inflamed tissue to dysplasia and invasive carcinoma.

Together, these interconnected mechanisms highlight the central role of chronic inflammation in shaping the molecular landscape of tumor initiation and progression. Understanding how these pathways operate in parallel across CAC and MAFLD‐HCC is critical for identifying shared therapeutic vulnerabilities and developing targeted interventions for inflammation‐driven malignancies.

Signaling Pathways

Chronic inflammation drives tumorigenesis through the sustained production of inflammatory mediators, including cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. Among these, IL‐6–STAT3 and NF‐κB signaling pathways play central roles in IACs by promoting epithelial cell survival, proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis [20] (Figure 1 and Graphical abstract).

Chronic inflammation activates nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB), interleukin‐6‐signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (IL6‐STAT3), phosphoinositide 3‐kinase/Akt (PI3K/Akt), and wingless/β‐catenin (Wnt/β‐catenin) signaling, promoting immune dysregulation through myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), and tumor‐associated macrophages. This persistent inflammatory and immunosuppressive environment drives carcinogenesis.

IL6‐STAT3 Signaling Pathway

IL‐6 is a pro‐inflammatory cytokine that promotes the survival and proliferation of colonic epithelial cells. It activates the Stat3 signaling pathway (a crucial cellular communication route that regulates gene expression in response to various cytokines and growth factors, playing significant roles in cell growth, survival, and immune responses.), which in turn upregulates the expression of antiapoptotic and proliferative genes. Elevated levels of IL‐6 and Stat3 activity have been observed in CAC tissues compared to normal colonic mucosa, as well as in MAFLD‐HCC tissues [21] Metabolic stress, lipotoxicity, and hepatocellular injury activate Kupffer cells, hepatic stellate cells, and infiltrating immune cells, leading to increased secretion of IL‐6 [22, 23]. Upon binding to its receptor complex (IL‐6R/gp130), IL‐6 activates Janus kinases (JAKs), resulting in STAT3 phosphorylation, dimerization, and nuclear translocation [22]. Activated STAT3 induces transcriptional programs that promote hepatocyte survival and proliferation through upregulation of oncogenic and anti‐apoptotic genes, including Cyclin D1, c‐Myc, Bcl‐xL, and surviving [15]. Sustained STAT3 activation also contributes to immunosuppression via increased expression of cytokines such as IL‐10 and TGF‐β, while driving epigenetic reprogramming, mitochondrial dysfunction, and impaired apoptosis, thereby reinforcing hepatocarcinogenesis [24]. Importantly, IL‐6–STAT3 signaling integrates metabolic dysregulation and inflammation through crosstalk with insulin resistance, altered lipid metabolism, and gut–liver axis dysfunction, where microbial translocation activates TLR4 signaling to further amplify IL‐6 production and sustain chronic inflammatory loops promoting MAFLD progression to HCC [25].

NF‐κB Signaling Pathway

The nuclear factor κB (NF‐κB) pathway is a central regulator of inflammation and immune responses and is persistently activated in IACs [26]. Under inflammatory stimulation, degradation of IκB proteins permits NF‐κB nuclear translocation, where it induces genes involved in cell proliferation, survival, and inflammation [27]. Chronic NF‐κB activation drives the sustained production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐6), chemokines (e.g., CXCL1, CCL2), and anti‐apoptotic factors such as Bcl‐2 and Bcl‐xL, creating a pro‐tumorigenic microenvironment that promotes angiogenesis and immune cell infiltration [28].

In CAC, continuous inflammatory cues from activated macrophages, dendritic cells, and damaged epithelial cells maintain NF‐κB activation in the colonic mucosa, leading to amplified cytokine production and mucosal inflammation [29]. Importantly, epithelial NF‐κB signaling enhances the survival of genetically damaged cells by suppressing apoptosis, facilitating the accumulation of oncogenic mutations. Consistently, epithelial‐specific inhibition of NF‐κB markedly reduces tumor incidence in CAC models, underscoring its causal role in inflammation‐driven tumorigenesis [29].

A similar mechanism operates in MAFLD‐HCC, where metabolic stress, hepatocyte lipotoxicity, and gut‐derived endotoxins activate TLR4‐dependent NF‐κB signaling in hepatocytes and Kupffer cells [25]. Persistent NF‐κB activation sustains hepatic inflammation through IL‐6 and TNF‐α production, promotes compensatory hepatocyte proliferation, and protects stressed cells from apoptosis, thereby favoring mutagenesis and malignant transformation [30]. NF‐κB further cooperates with JAK/STAT, MAPK, and metabolic pathways to drive fibrosis, clonal expansion, and HCC development, while its suppression attenuates MAFLD severity and tumor formation in experimental models [25].

PI3K/Akt Signaling Pathway

The phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K)/Akt signaling pathway is a central regulator of cell growth, survival, and metabolism and is frequently dysregulated in CAC, where it contributes to tumor initiation and progression [14, 31]. Activation of PI3K by growth factors and inflammatory cytokines leads to the generation of phosphatidylinositol‐3,4,5‐trisphosphate (PIP3), which recruits and activates Akt at the plasma membrane [32]. PIP3 functions as a membrane‐anchored signaling scaffold that recruits Akt and other pleckstrin‐homology containing proteins, enabling their phosphorylation and activation in response to inflammatory and growth factor cues.

Once activated, Akt phosphorylates multiple downstream targets that collectively promote cell survival and metabolic adaptation. Akt activates mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), thereby enhancing protein synthesis, cell growth, and proliferation in MAFLD‐HCC [31]. Concurrently, Akt inhibits glycogen synthase kinase‐3 (GSK‐3), leading to increased cell survival, stabilization of β‐catenin, and enhanced proliferative signaling [33]. Akt‐mediated phosphorylation of FOXO transcription factors prevents their nuclear localization and suppresses the expression of proapoptotic genes, further promoting cell survival [34]. In addition, Akt directly inhibits apoptosis by phosphorylating Bcl‐2–associated death promoter (BAD) and inactivating other proapoptotic regulators [32].

Akt signaling also interferes with tumor suppressor pathways. Phosphorylation of murine double minute 2 (MDM2) enhances p53 (a well‐established key tumor suppressor) degradation, weakening DNA damage–induced apoptosis and facilitating survival of genetically unstable cells [35]. Moreover, Akt promotes cell cycle progression by inhibiting cyclin‐dependent kinase inhibitors and stabilizing the oncoprotein c‐Myc, thereby supporting sustained cellular growth and metabolic reprogramming [36].

Collectively, aberrant PI3K/Akt signaling establishes a pro‐tumorigenic environment characterized by enhanced survival, uncontrolled proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and resistance to apoptosis. These integrated effects enable cancer cells to persist under inflammatory and metabolic stress, evade cell death, and drive the progression of colitis‐associated cancer [28, 37].

Wnt/β‐Catenin Signaling

In CAC, frequent mutations occur in p53, APC, and KRAS (), leading to impaired apoptosis, dysregulated Wnt/β‐catenin signaling, and enhanced MAPK/ERK‐driven proliferation [4, 37, 38]. p53 mutations are strongly associated with progression from dysplasia to carcinoma and confer resistance to cell death [39]. While APC loss promotes nuclear β‐catenin accumulation and proliferative gene transcription [40]. Loss of APC disrupts the β‐catenin destruction complex, preventing its phosphorylation and proteasomal degradation, which allows β‐catenin to accumulate in the nucleus and drive transcription of proliferative and anti‐apoptotic genes. KRAS mutations further enhance tumor aggressiveness through sustained oncogenic signaling [41].

Similar dysregulation of Wnt/β‐catenin signaling is observed in MAFLD‐associated HCC, where reduced APC expression via promoter hypermethylation or post‐transcriptional suppression contributes to β‐catenin stabilization despite lower APC mutation rates [42]. Chronic lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory cytokines promote β‐catenin–driven transcriptional programs that support hepatocyte proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and apoptosis resistance, facilitating clonal expansion and tumor initiation [43]. Notably, aberrant β‐catenin activation represents one of the most common oncogenic events in MAFLD‐HCC.

Together, these signaling pathways form an interconnected inflammatory network whose magnitude and duration are shaped by regulatory molecules such as TIPE2, thereby determining whether chronic inflammation resolves or progresses toward inflammation‐associated carcinogenesis.

TIPE2 As a Negative Regulator of Inflammatory Signaling

TIPE2 is an essential immunoregulatory molecule that maintains immune homeostasis by restraining excessive inflammatory signaling. Mechanistically, TIPE2 inhibits multiple oncogenic pathways including NF‐κB, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK by limiting upstream receptor activation and dampening the transcription of pro‐inflammatory and anti‐apoptotic genes [11]. In the context of CAC and MAFLD‐HCC, reduced TIPE2 expression has been associated with heightened cytokine production, increased immune cell infiltration, and sustained activation of inflammatory circuits that promote epithelial or hepatocellular transformation [12]. Experimental models demonstrate that TIPE2 deficiency exacerbates mucosal and hepatic inflammation, accelerates compensatory proliferation, and enhances susceptibility to inflammation‐associated tumorigenesis [12, 44]. Thus, TIPE2 functions as a key negative regulator that shapes the magnitude and duration of inflammatory signaling, and its loss contributes to the persistence of a tumor‐promoting microenvironment in IACs.

Organ Crosstalk‐ Accelerated Inflammation

The gut microbiota and the enteric nervous system (ENS) play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of chronic IACs. Dysbiosis, or an imbalance in the gut microbiota, and interactions between the ENS and immune system can influence the progression of chronic inflammation to cancer.

Gut Microbiota

Gut microbiota composition is markedly altered in IBD and CAC, with dysbiosis characterized by reduced beneficial bacteria and enrichment of pathogenic species [45]. Loss of SCFA‐producing bacteria, such as butyrate producers, impairs intestinal barrier integrity and anti‐inflammatory signaling (Figure 2), increasing permeability and chronic inflammation [46]. SCFA depletion impairs epithelial tight‐junction integrity and reduces mucosal energy supply, weakening the barrier and allowing microbial products such as LPS to translocate across the epithelium and perpetuate chronic inflammation. In this context, Gram‐negative bacteria, both commensal and pathogenic, release lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which engages TLR4 and related receptors to activate NF‐κB–driven inflammatory pathways [47], amplify cytokine production, and promote epithelial proliferation and survival, thereby fostering a microenvironment permissive for DNA damage, dysplasia, and carcinogenesis in the chronically inflamed colon (Figure 2) [48]. Rather than acting as an isolated carcinogen, LPS functions as a potent inflammatory stimulus whose tumor‐promoting effects emerge specifically in the setting of chronic dysbiosis and barrier disruption [49]. In MAFLD‐HCC, gut dysbiosis enhances hepatic inflammation through increased microbial translocation of LPS and other microbial products, disrupted SCFA and bile acid metabolism, and pro‐carcinogenic metabolites that drive metabolic dysfunction, hepatocyte injury, immune remodeling, and tumor progression [50].

Enteric Nervous System

The enteric nervous system (ENS), often termed the “second brain,” comprises neurons and glial cells that regulate gastrointestinal function and interact closely with immune cells and the gut microbiota [51]. Through neuropeptides and neurotransmitters (Figure 2), the ENS modulates inflammation, with substance P promoting pro‐inflammatory cytokine production and acetylcholine exerting anti‐inflammatory effects [51]. Additionally, gut–brain axis signaling links psychological stress to gut homeostasis. Chronic stress activates the HPA axis, leading to sustained cortisol elevation. While cortisol acutely exerts potent immunosuppressive and anti‐inflammatory effects, chronic exposure can lead to glucocorticoid resistance in immune cells, a dysregulated immune response, and ultimately the exacerbation of intestinal inflammation, thereby promoting cancer progression [52, 53].

TIPE2 also influences this immune landscape by restraining excessive activation of myeloid and lymphoid populations. Reduced TIPE2 expression, reported in chronic inflammatory states, may further skew the microenvironment toward sustained immune activation and immunosuppression [11], thereby facilitating inflammation‐associated carcinogenesis.

Myeloid‐Derived Suppressor Cells (MDSCs)

MDSCs are a heterogeneous immunosuppressive population that expands during chronic inflammation and cancer, where they inhibit T‐cell responses and promote tumor progression [58]. In CAC, persistent inflammatory signaling in the colonic mucosa drives MDSC recruitment and activation through cytokines and chemokines such as IL‐6 and TNF‐α [59]. MDSCs suppress antitumor immunity through several mechanisms, including iNOS overexpression, which increases nitric oxide production and consequently inhibits T‐cell proliferation and cytotoxic function, as well as ARG‐1 activity (ARG‐1 depletes extracellular L‐arginine, an amino acid required for T‐cell receptor ζ‐chain expression), reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and enhancement of regulatory T‐cell responses [58]. Clinical samples and mouse models demonstrate a strong association between MDSC accumulation and CAC progression, highlighting their role in the transition from colitis to carcinoma [60, 61].

Inflammatory mediators further amplify MDSC expansion. Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) promotes MDSC recruitment and induces S100A8/A9 expression, which establishes a positive feedback loop sustaining myeloid accumulation [62]. Increased ROS, VEGF, and TNF‐α within the inflammatory microenvironment enhance MDSC survival, angiogenesis, and immunosuppressive activity [63].

A similar immunopathological paradigm is observed in MAFLD‐associated hepatocellular carcinoma (MAFLD‐HCC). Chronic hepatic inflammation driven by lipotoxicity and oxidative stress promotes robust MDSC accumulation, leading to suppression of T‐cell–mediated antitumor immunity [64]. IL‐6 and TNF‐α are key drivers of MDSC recruitment in MAFLD‐HCC, paralleling mechanisms observed in CAC [65]. Spatial and single‐cell analyses reveal close interactions between MDSCs, tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs), and exhausted T cells, identifying myeloid immunosuppression as a dominant feature of the MAFLD‐HCC tumor microenvironment [66]. Emerging evidence further implicates gut–liver axis dysfunction and altered microbial signaling in regulating MDSC‐driven immune escape and reduced immunotherapy efficacy [67].

Regulatory T Cells (Tregs)

Tregs are essential for maintaining immune tolerance but acquire a tumor‐promoting role in chronic inflammation–associated cancers [68]. In CAC, persistent inflammatory stimuli drive enhanced Treg recruitment and functional stabilization within the tumor microenvironment, where they suppress antitumor immunity [68]. Tregs inhibit effector T‐cell responses through IL‐10 and TGF‐β secretion, suppression of dendritic cell activation, and inhibition of cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes, while also promoting the expansion of other immunosuppressive populations such as MDSCs and TAMs [69]. Pro‐inflammatory cytokines abundant in inflamed colonic tissue, particularly IL‐6 and TNF‐α, further enhance Treg accumulation and suppressive capacity, facilitating immune evasion and tumor progression [70]. Given its role in restraining inflammatory signaling, reduced TIPE2 activity may contribute to the cytokine‐rich environment that supports Treg recruitment and functional stabilization in chronic inflammation.

A comparable immunosuppressive role for Tregs is observed in MAFLD‐HCC. Spatial proteomic and single‐cell analyses reveal enrichment of Tregs in close association with exhausted CD8+ T cells and suppressive myeloid populations, implicating Tregs as key mediators of local immune suppression and resistance to immunotherapy [66]. Chronic hepatic inflammation, metabolic stress, and cytokines such as IL‐6 and TGF‐β promote Treg recruitment, metabolic adaptation, and functional stability in the liver, reinforcing suppression of antitumor T‐cell responses [71]. Consistently, experimental depletion or disruption of Tregs reduces tumor burden in MAFLD‐HCC models, indicating an active contribution to hepatocarcinogenesis [72].

Notably, Tregs in CAC also display functional plasticity; under sustained inflammatory conditions, they may acquire pro‐inflammatory features, including IL‐17 production, thereby perpetuating chronic inflammation while maintaining immunosuppressive activity [73]. Collectively, these findings identify Tregs as central regulators of the immunosuppressive microenvironment shared by CAC and MAFLD‐HCC. Therapeutic strategies targeting Tregs through selective depletion or modulation of Treg‐specific cytokine and metabolic pathways hold promise but require careful balancing to avoid exacerbating inflammation or autoimmunity.

Macrophages

Macrophages are highly plastic immune cells that play a central role in shaping the tumor microenvironment in IACs, including CAC and MAFLD‐HCC [74]. Their functional states are broadly categorized as pro‐inflammatory M1 and immunosuppressive M2 phenotypes, although polarization is dynamic and context dependent. In CAC, early inflammatory stages are dominated by M1 macrophages induced by IFN‐γ and LPS, which produce TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐12, ROS, and nitric oxide, thereby sustaining chronic inflammation, inducing epithelial DNA damage, and promoting tumor initiation [75].

As CAC progresses, the tumor microenvironment favors a shift toward M2 macrophage polarization driven by anti‐inflammatory cytokines, hypoxia, and metabolic cues [76]. M2 macrophages secrete TGF‐β, VEGF, and matrix metalloproteinases, promoting angiogenesis, tissue remodeling, tumor invasion, and immune suppression [77]. They also recruit regulatory T cells and other suppressive immune populations, further dampening antitumor immunity and facilitating disease progression [78]. This transition from M1‐ to M2‐dominant macrophage populations marks a critical switch from inflammation‐driven tissue injury to tumor‐supportive immune suppression in CAC [77]. TIPE2 is known to limit macrophage activation by suppressing NF‐κB and PI3K/Akt signaling; thus, reduced TIPE2 expression in chronic inflammation may facilitate the shift toward tumor‐promoting macrophage phenotypes [79].

Macrophages play a similarly pivotal role in MAFLD‐associated hepatocellular carcinoma. In the inflamed liver, Kupffer cells and infiltrating monocyte‐derived macrophages respond to lipotoxicity, gut‐derived endotoxins, and damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) (which are endogenous molecules released from stressed or dying cells (hepatocytes, in this regard) that activate innate immune receptors) by producing IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α, and CCL2, thereby amplifying hepatocellular stress and compensatory proliferation [23, 80]. During progression to MAFLD‐HCC, macrophages acquire TAM‐like properties, enhancing angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune evasion through VEGF, MMPs, IL‐10, TGF‐β, and PD‐L1 expression [81]. Activation of NF‐κB, STAT3, and NLRP3 inflammasome signaling within macrophages further accelerates tumor development by sustaining chronic inflammation and promoting immune escape [82].

Taken together, macrophages serve as key drivers of IACs across tissues, linking chronic inflammatory injury to immune suppression and tumor progression in both CAC and MAFLD‐HCC. Their central role and phenotypic plasticity make them attractive therapeutic targets in inflammation‐driven cancers.

Collectively, these immune alterations represent early events in inflammation‐driven tumorigenesis, highlighting immune regulation as a critical target for cancer prevention during the presymptomatic stages of CAC and MAFLD‐HCC.

Prevention and Therapeutic Implications in Inflammation‐Associated Cancers

Understanding the mechanisms underlying IACs provides opportunities for both cancer prevention and cancer treatment, which represent biologically and clinically distinct objectives. Cancer prevention focuses on presymptomatic stages, aiming to control chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and tissue injury before malignant transformation occurs. In contrast, cancer treatment targets clinically established disease, where tumor‐intrinsic alterations and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment dominate.

In this section, we distinguish between these two contexts while highlighting areas of overlap. Therapeutic strategies discussed below are therefore considered in terms of their preventive potential during chronic inflammatory disease as well as their therapeutic application in established CAC and MAFLD‐HCC (Table 2 and Figure 4).

Immune‐Based Strategies: Implications for Prevention and Treatment

Immunotherapies are actively being explored for IACs, including CAC and MAFLD‐HCC. In the treatment setting, immune checkpoint inhibitors such as anti‐PD‐1 and anti‐CTLA‐4 antibodies aim to restore antitumor T‐cell activity by blocking inhibitory signaling pathways and are currently under clinical evaluation in CAC [83]. CAR‐T cell therapy, which engineers patient T cells to recognize tumor antigens, is also under investigation, although its efficacy remains limited by antigen selection and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [84] (Table 2). Similar immune checkpoint‐based approaches in MAFLD‐HCC, particularly PD‐1 blockade, have shown encouraging clinical activity [85]. Therapeutic cancer vaccines targeting tumor‐associated antigens are likewise being developed to enhance immune recognition and antitumor responses in established malignancies [86].

From a prevention perspective, these immune‐based strategies underscore the importance of the immune landscape established during chronic inflammatory disease. Persistent immune exhaustion, regulatory T‐cell expansion, and myeloid‐cell mediated suppression during preneoplastic stages may shape subsequent responsiveness to immunotherapy. Thus, early modulation of immune dysfunction during chronic colitis or MAFLD/MASH may not only delay malignant transformation but also improve the efficacy of later immunotherapeutic interventions. As with other immune‐based approaches, immune‐related adverse events remain a consideration, with severity depending on the therapeutic modality and clinical context.

Microbiota‐Targeted Interventions: Preventive Modulation and Therapeutic Potential

Modulation of the gut microbiota represents a promising strategy that bridges cancer prevention and treatment in IACs. In the preventive setting, probiotics and prebiotics are being evaluated for their ability to restore microbial balance, reduce chronic inflammation, and limit the pro‐tumorigenic environment associated with CAC [87]. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is under investigation in colitis, aiming to reestablish a healthy microbiome and attenuate persistent inflammatory signaling that promotes colorectal tumorigenesis [88].

In established disease, microbiota‐targeted approaches may also complement conventional therapies by modulating immune responses and metabolic signaling within the tumor microenvironment. In MAFLD‐HCC, probiotics, prebiotics, and FMT have been shown to improve inflammatory and metabolic features and reduce hepatocarcinogenesis in MASH models [89]. Together, these findings highlight microbiome‐driven interventions as shared strategies with both preventive relevance during chronic inflammatory disease and therapeutic potential in CAC and MAFLD‐HCC.

Epigenetic Modulation

Epigenetic dysregulation contributes to both inflammation‐driven tumor initiation and tumor progression in IACs. Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors can reverse aberrant chromatin remodeling, reactivate tumor suppressor genes, and induce apoptosis in cancer cells, supporting their use in the treatment of established malignancies, particularly in combination with other therapies [90]. However, their clinical application has been limited by significant toxicities, including hematologic and gastrointestinal adverse effects arising from broad, non‐selective HDAC inhibition [91]. Ongoing efforts to mitigate these limitations include the development of isoform‐selective HDAC inhibitors, optimized dosing schedules, and targeted delivery strategies [92, 93].

From a preventive standpoint, epigenetic modulators may be particularly valuable during chronic inflammatory disease, where early epigenetic alterations accumulate prior to overt malignancy. DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitors aim to restore silenced tumor suppressor genes by correcting abnormal DNA methylation patterns [94], although toxicity including myelosuppression and infection risk remains a challenge [95]. Advances such as next‐generation DNMT inhibitors, metronomic dosing, and nanoparticle‐based delivery systems may improve their suitability for long‐term intervention [96]. In MAFLD‐HCC, both HDAC and DNA methylation inhibitors have demonstrated efficacy in MASH models by suppressing tumor cell proliferation and reactivating protective pathways [97, 98, 99], supporting further evaluation of epigenetic modulation across both preventive and therapeutic contexts.

Gene‐Based Interventions: Precision Prevention and Treatment

Gene‐based strategies offer precision approaches for both preventing malignant transformation and treating established IACs. In CAC, restoring tumor‐suppressive pathways through enhancement of TIPE2 expression in the colonic mucosa using viral or non‐viral delivery systems may reinstate immune‐regulatory and antitumor functions during chronic inflammation [100]. CRISPR/Cas9‐based gene editing technologies further provide the potential to precisely correct genetic alterations that drive inflammation, immune imbalance, and tumor progression [101].

In MAFLD‐HCC, similar approaches aimed at restoring TIPE2 expression or function have been shown to reestablish immune homeostasis and suppress tumorigenesis in MASH‐associated liver cancer [12] (Table 2). While gene therapy faces translational challenges, including delivery efficiency and safety, these findings highlight gene‐based interventions as promising tools for early precision prevention as well as targeted treatment across CAC and MAFLD‐HCC.

Targeting Inflammatory Signaling Pathways: Interrupting Disease Progression

Persistent activation of inflammatory signaling pathways is a central driver of chronic tissue injury, immune dysregulation, and malignant transformation in IACs [102]. Key pathways, including NF‐κB, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, and JAK/STAT, integrate inflammatory and metabolic cues to sustain pro‐inflammatory cytokine production, promote tumor‐supportive signaling, and drive tumor initiation and progression in CAC and MAFLD‐HCC [15, 31, 103].

In the treatment setting, pharmacological inhibition of NF‐κB signaling attenuates pro‐inflammatory cytokine expression, reduces immune‐mediated tissue damage, and limits tumor‐promoting inflammation in experimental models of colitis and liver disease [104], including DSS‐induced colitis, where NF‐κB blockade alleviated intestinal inflammation and improved epithelial barrier function [105]. Targeting the PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis suppresses aberrant cell survival and metabolic reprogramming while modulating immune‐cell function within the tumor microenvironment [106]. Inhibition of JAK/STAT signaling, particularly IL‐6–STAT3‐dependent pathways, further reduces inflammatory amplification, immune suppression, and epithelial or hepatocellular proliferation [21, 107].

Importantly, these pathway‐targeted strategies also hold preventive relevance, as early intervention may promote mucosal and hepatic healing, restore immune homeostasis, and interrupt the progression from chronic inflammation to malignancy.

Future Direction

Future research on IACs should prioritize several key areas to improve therapeutic outcomes. Greater emphasis is needed on elucidating the molecular and immunological mechanisms that link chronic inflammation to tumor initiation and progression, including pathways regulating immune activation, epithelial stress responses, apoptosis, and autophagy within the tumor microenvironment. In parallel, extensive investigation of gut microbiota immune system interactions is essential, as their bidirectional crosstalk critically shapes inflammatory tone and tumorigenesis and may enable the development of microbiota‐based therapeutic strategies. The identification of reliable biomarkers for early detection, disease monitoring, and risk stratification, particularly in high‐risk populations such as patients with chronic colitis, remains a major unmet need. Finally, rational combination therapies that concurrently target inflammation, immune evasion, and tumor progression hold promise for achieving synergistic efficacy and overcoming therapeutic resistance.

Conclusion

Chronic inflammation associated cancers pose a significant global health burden, driven by persistent inflammatory signaling, tissue injury, and immune dysregulation that promote genomic instability and malignant transformation across multiple organs, including the colon and liver. Despite organ specific contexts, cancers such as CAC and MAFLD‐HCC share common pathogenic mechanisms, highlighting inflammation as a unifying driver of tumor initiation and progression. TIPE2 is a regulator of immune homeostasis, influencing pathways such as NF‐κB, STAT3, and PI3K/Akt and shaping the inflammatory tumor microenvironment. Its dysregulation in diseases like IBD and MASH links chronic inflammation to cancer susceptibility. In parallel, the microbiota immune axis adds further complexity, as microbial alterations modulate inflammation, immune responses, and epithelial transformation. Addressing remaining knowledge gaps and integrating immunomodulatory, anti‐inflammatory, and microbiota‐targeted strategies within precision medicine frameworks will be critical for preventing and treating inflammation‐driven malignancies.
