Integrating urine metabolomic biomarkers and machine learning algorithms to predict preeclampsia
Authors: Qingshan Chen, Yong Qian, Mengjiao Feng, Hai Zhang, Hongjuan Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreeclampsia (PE) remains a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality. This study aimed to identify urinary metabolites as potential biomarkers for predicting PE by integrating metabolomic profiling with machine learning algorithms.MethodsThe untargeted metabolomics of urine samples were performed in three cohorts: healthy pregnant women, gestational hypertension (GH), and PE patients. Differentially expressed metabolites were identified using multivariate statistical analyses. Subsequently, a predictive model for preeclampsia was developed and validated through four machine learning algorithms.ResultsMetabolomic profiling identified 55 significantly dysregulated metabolites in PE compared to controls, while 22 metabolic signatures were observed between the GH and PE cohorts. Pathway analysis revealed vitamin B6 metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and histidine metabolism as core dysregulated pathways in PE pathogenesis. Next, LASSO regression selected four predictive metabolites: estriol-17 glucuronide, diethylphosphate, 4-deoxythreonic acid, and taurine. Notably, estriol-17 glucuronide demonstrated superior predictive accuracy compared to other metabolites. Furthermore, a machine learning model incorporating these four metabolic biomarkers was constructed for PE prediction. The XGBoost model showed significantly better prediction efficacy (94% accuracy, AUC = 0.976) compared to other machine learning models. In addition, estriol-17-glucuronide was significantly positively correlated with blood pressure.ConclusionsThis study identified four important urinary biomarkers and constructed an XGBoost-based predictive model for PE early detection. These findings provide a noninvasive approach for early PE screening in clinic and insights into its metabolic pathophysiology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03337-1.Keywords: Preeclampsia, Metabolomics, Machine learning, Predictive biomarkers, Gestational hypertension

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE), a major hypertensive disorder of pregnancy, affects approximately 2–8% of pregnancies worldwide and is a leading cause of maternal and perinatal mortality [1]. Clinical epidemiological studies report that 20–30% of women with gestational hypertension (GH) ultimately progress to PE [2]. Furthermore, PE significantly increases maternal risks, including placental abruption, hepatic or renal failure, and coagulopathies. According to World Health Organization statistics, PE and associated complications account for over 50,000 maternal deaths annually [3, 4]. Current diagnostic criteria primarily rely on new-onset hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation; however, approximately 15% of PE cases lack proteinuria [5]. Consequently, these conventional diagnostic methods remain inadequate for early prediction and fail to identify high-risk PE patients, highlighting the urgent need for novel biomarkers for both early detection and dynamic risk evaluation.

The pathogenesis of PE is complex and involves multiple mechanisms, including vascular dysfunction, inflammatory responses, and metabolic dysregulation [6]. Metabolomics reveals the physiological and pathological states of cells under specific conditions by profiling and quantifying small molecule metabolites in biological samples. Recent studies have revealed significant metabolic dysregulation in the blood, placenta, and gut microbiota of PE patients, characterized by dysregulated lipid metabolism, disrupted steroid hormone synthesis, and reduced short-chain fatty acids levels [7–9]. Although most studies have focused on plasma and placental tissues, urine as a non-invasive and easily accessible biological sample remains relatively understudied. Urinary metabolites not only directly reflect renal and systemic metabolic homeostasis but may also serve as earlier biomarkers for PE progression. Previous studies have identified several metabolic biomarkers associated with PE, but evidence is limited to small-scale cohorts [10, 11]. In addition, there is a lack of metabolomic biomarkers and understanding of the progression from GH to PE.

In this study, untargeted metabolomic profiling analysis of urine samples was performed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometry (UHPLC-QE-Orbitrap-MS). Urinary metabolomic profiles of GH and PE patients revealed four key metabolites significantly associated with PE, including estriol-17-glucuronide, diethylphosphate, 4-deoxythreonic acid, and taurine. Subsequent pathway analysis found vitamin B6 metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and histidine metabolism as the most relevant pathways in PE pathogenesis. Furthermore, a predictive model based on machine learning was developed and validated, achieving 94.3% accuracy for early PE detection. Collectively, these findings provide potential diagnostic biomarkers and a prediction model for the early detection of PE, while also elucidating novel pathogenic mechanisms that may guide precision therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Between May 2022 and January 2024, a cohort consisting of 104 healthy pregnant women (control group), 80 patients diagnosed with GH, and 102 patients diagnosed with PE was recruited for this study. The control group was defined as women with normal blood pressure and an uncomplicated single pregnancy, and who experienced preterm delivery were excluded. The diagnosis of PE adhered to the guidelines of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG): new-onset hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg) accompanied by proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation. In patients without proteinuria, PE was confirmed by concurrent organ dysfunction: thrombocytopenia, hepatic impairment, renal insufficiency, pulmonary edema, or new-onset neurological symptoms. This study was conducted with approval from the Ethics Committee of Shanghai First Maternity and Infant Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent for the collection and analysis of urine samples.

Sample collection and preparation

All urine samples were obtained from pregnant women admitted to the hospital before delivery. Samples were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C immediately after collection. For analytical procedures, samples were retrieved directly from – 80 ℃ storage tubes and thawed at 4 °C, strictly avoiding freeze–thaw cycles. Aliquots of 150 μL urine were mixed with cold acetonitrile (400 μL) in centrifuge tubes for protein precipitation. L-2-chlorophenylalanine (5 μg/mL) was added as an internal standard. After vortexing for 5 min, the mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 10 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to new tubes, dried under nitrogen gas, and stored at − 20 °C. Residues were reconstituted in 150 μL of mobile phase until analysis. Quality control (QC) samples, consisting of pooled aliquots of 5 μL from all samples, were prepared to monitor system stability.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis for urine samples

Untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed on an Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) coupled to a Q Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved using an ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) with maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase comprised solvent A (0.05% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.05% formic acid in acetonitrile). A linear gradient elution program was implemented as follows: 0–2 min (5% B), 2–3 min (5–20% B), 3–12 min (20–30% B), 12–21 min (30–74% B), 21–23 min (74–100% B), and 23–32 min (100–5% B), at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The autosampler temperature was maintained at 10 °C with a 5 μL injection volume. Mass spectrometry parameters were optimized as follows: capillary temperature 350 °C, spray voltage 3.5 kV (positive ion mode) and 3.0 kV (negative ion mode), full scan range 80–1200 m/z, and mass resolution 70,000. Instrument calibration and data acquisition were controlled by Xcalibur 3.0 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To ensure system equilibration, six QC samples were analyzed before batch runs, followed by intermittent QC injections every six samples.

Raw data processing and metabolite identification

Initially, the data were converted from vendor-specific formats to mzXML using ProteoWizard (version 3.0.20091). Subsequent peak processing was conducted in R (version 4.2.1) utilizing the XCMS package (version 3.18.1). This workflow included retention time alignment, feature detection, noise filtering, and cross-sample peak matching [12]. For the identification of differential metabolites, orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed using SIMCA-P (v14.1, Umetrics), with model validity confirmed by permutation testing (200 iterations). OPLS-DA is a supervised multivariate statistical method primarily used to isolate differences between groups and filter out orthogonal noise unrelated to classification [13, 14]. Significantly differential metabolites were identified based on these criteria: variable importance projection (VIP) > 1.0, P < 0.05, and Log2(fold change (FC)) ≥ 1.0 or ≤ 1.0. Metabolite annotation was performed through integrated analysis of MS1 accurate mass data and MS/MS fragmentation patterns, complemented by database matching against Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database [15]. Additional verification was conducted through retention time alignment with authentic standards and literature-reported fragmentation patterns. Finally, metabolic pathway analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 and the KEGG database.

Machine learning models

Overall, 80% of the datasets were used for training and validation, and 20% of the datasets were used for testing. In addition, tenfold cross-validation was performed on the training models to prevent overfitting. All datasets were preprocessed by scaling and centering before machine learning. The predictive model for PE was developed utilizing several machine learning algorithms, including logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and support vector machine (SVM).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with the IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 and R software (version 4.2.0). Continuous data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis among three groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The correlation between key metabolites and clinical characteristics was analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. P < 0.05 was defined statistical significance.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study participants

This study enrolled 104 healthy pregnant women, 80 GH patients, and 102 PE patients. As shown in Table 1, there were no significant differences in maternal age among the three groups, with mean ages ranging from 30.88 to 31.62 years. Both systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were significantly higher in the GH and PE groups compared to the control group (P < 0.001). The PE group had significantly higher serum creatinine (53.89 ± 10.61 μmol/L) and uric acid levels (403.14 ± 105.99 μmol/L) than both the control and GH groups. Notably, infants in the PE group had significantly lower birth weights (2973.53 ± 630.15 g) compared to those in both the GH group (3274.38 ± 381.29 g; P < 0.01) and the control group (3430.53 ± 321.03 g; P < 0.001), accompanied by lower Apgar scores. Furthermore, the cesarean delivery rate was substantially higher in the PE group (70%) than in both the GH (39%) and control groups (43%). No significant differences were observed among the groups in alanine transaminase (ALT) levels, aspartate transaminase (AST) levels, platelet counts, or the distribution of neonatal gender.

Differences in urinary metabolic profiles among healthy pregnant women, gestational hypertension and preeclampsia

The OPLS-DA analysis revealed distinct urinary metabolic profiles among healthy pregnant women, GH patients, and PE patients in both positive and negative ionization modes (Fig. 1A, B). Permutation testing validated the OPLS-DA analysis, as demonstrated by negative Q2 intercepts and significantly reduced predictive capability in permuted models, thereby excluding overfitting concerns. Subsequently, the OPLS-DA scores plot demonstrated a clear separation between control and GH patients (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, a total of 65 differential metabolites were identified between control and GH patients, with 36 metabolites were upregulated and 29 metabolites were downregulated (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Table S1). Similarly, OPLS-DA scores plot also showed a separation between control and PE patients (Fig. 1E). Compared with healthy pregnant women, 55 differentially expressed metabolites were detected in the patients with PE, with 25 upregulated and 30 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 1F; Supplementary Table S2). Notably, a total of 30 significantly different metabolites were commonly identified in both the GH and PE groups. These significantly differentially metabolites primarily included fatty acid metabolites, steroid hormones and their metabolites, amino acid metabolites and vitamin-related metabolites.

Distinct metabolic profiles between gestational hypertension and preeclampsia

Preeclampsia is a more severe and progressive stage of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. In our study, OPLS-DA analysis also revealed significant urinary metabolic profile distinctions between the PE and GH groups (Fig. 2A). Moreover, a total of 22 differentially expressed metabolites were identified in patients with PE compared to GH patients, comprising 18 upregulated and 4 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S3). Notably, three significantly differential metabolites (1-methylhistidine, palmitic amide, and pantothenic acid) were found across control, GH, and PE groups (Fig. 2C and Figure S1). Specifically, compared to the GH group, the relative abundance of 1-methylhistidine and palmitic amide increased significantly in PE group, while the relative abundance of pantothenic acid decreased significantly (Fig. 2D).

Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis in gestational hypertension and preeclampsia patients

Our metabolic pathway analysis of differential metabolites revealed characteristic metabolic pathway changes in GH and PE patients. Compared with healthy pregnant women, the significantly changed metabolic pathways in the GH group included vitamin B6 metabolism and histidine metabolism (Fig. 3A). PE patients exhibited more extensive metabolic pathway disorders, and significantly enriched metabolic pathways included steroid hormone biosynthesis, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, and vitamin B6 metabolism (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, compared with the GH group, we found that histidine metabolism and sphingolipid metabolism were significantly changed in the PE group (Fig. 3C). These results collectively revealed the metabolic characteristics of the development of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy and elucidate the key roles of vitamin B6 metabolism and histidine metabolism in PE patients.

Development of a machine learning model for PE prediction

Initially, Lasso regression analysis was employed to screen potential metabolites predictive of PE, and four metabolites were identified, namely estriol-17 glucuronide, diethylphosphate, 4-deoxythreonic acid, and taurine (Fig. 4A, B). Among these four metabolites, the prediction performance of single metabolite analysis showed that estriol-17 glucuronide exhibited the strongest predictive capability, with an AUC of 0.937 (Fig. 4C). Subsequently, machine learning models were constructed using these four metabolites, including LR, RF, XGBoost, and SVM models. Of these models, the XGBoost model demonstrated superior performance, achieving an accuracy of 0.94 (95% CI 86.8%-98.2%) and an AUC of 0.976, along with exceptionally high sensitivity and specificity (Fig. 4D, and Supplementary Table S4). Comparative analysis further indicated that Logistic Regression ranked second (AUC = 0.972), followed by SVM (AUC = 0.971) and the RF model (AUC = 0.968). Furthermore, the validation dataset confirmed the robustness of the XGBoost model (Fig. 4E). Feature importance analysis suggested estriol-17-glucuronide as the primary contributor to model predictions (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these findings indicate that the XGBoost model based on the four urine metabolites has high clinical application potential in the prediction of PE.

Correlations between key metabolites and clinical characteristics in PE

The relative abundance of key metabolites was initially analyzed. The results showed that estriol-17-glucuronide, diethylphosphate, and 4-deoxythreonic acid were significantly elevated in both GH and PE groups, whereas taurine was markedly reduced (Fig. 5A). Correlation analysis subsequently revealed the relationship between these key metabolites and clinical characteristics (Fig. 5B). Specifically, estriol-17-glucuronide, diethylphosphate, and 4-deoxythreonic acid were positively correlated with SBP and DBP. It is worth noting that taurine was negatively correlated with blood pressure, uric acid, and creatinine, suggesting its protective role in metabolic regulation. In addition, diethylphosphate and 4-deoxythreonic acid exhibited weak positive correlations with creatinine and uric acid. These results indicate that a close association between the identified key metabolites and the clinical phenotypes of PE.

Discussion

Preeclampsia remains a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality, yet early diagnostic biomarkers are lacking. While previous studies have focused on plasma-based biomarkers (such as the sFlt-1/PLGF ratio and miRNAs) or placental tissue, our study aims to identify and validate urinary metabolites for PE prediction. This study employed non-targeted metabolomics and machine learning algorithms to screen for urinary biomarkers and to construct a non-invasive early prediction model for PE. Significant alterations in the urinary metabolic profile were observed in PE patients compared to healthy pregnant women and GH patients. A key four-metabolite signature was identified, consisting of estriol-17-glucuronide, diethyl phosphate, 4-deoxythreonic acid, and taurine. Based on these biomarkers, the XGBoost model demonstrated excellent predictive performance for PE, providing novel biomarkers and method for non-invasive early diagnosis.

Several studies have reported that GH and PE patients exhibit characteristic metabolic disorders, primarily involving elevated levels of lipid metabolites and decreased levels of amino acid metabolites such as taurine and arginine [16, 17]. These alterations drive disease progression via oxidative stress and vascular endothelial injury. In this study, estriol-17-glucuronide was the most important feature variable in the predictive model, and its level was significantly positively correlated with blood pressure. Estrogen plays an important role in maintaining placental angiogenesis and maternal cardiovascular adaptation. Its metabolic disturbance may directly or indirectly contribute to endothelial dysfunction and systemic inflammatory responses, which are the core pathological links of PE. Estriol, the predominant estrogen during pregnancy, serves as an important indicator of placental function [18]. Estriol-17-glucuronide is a conjugated form of estriol, and the elevated level of estriol-17-glucuronide in urine may reflect a placental stress response and reduced maternal clearance capacity, implicating it is closely related to the progression of PE. Conversely, taurine levels were significantly reduced in PE patients and negatively correlated with blood pressure, serum uric acid, and creatinine, suggesting a protective function. Taurine, an amino acid with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, is critical for fetal organ development and placental function [19]. Previous studies have shown that taurine deficiency may exacerbate oxidative stress and endothelial cell injury, which is a key pathogenic mechanism of PE [20]. Animal studies further support that perinatal taurine supplementation helps regulate arterial blood pressure [21, 22].

4-Deoxythreonic acid, a vitamin C degradation product under oxidative stress, is considered a marker of increased systemic oxidative burden [23]. Since PE pathogenesis is closely linked to oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, the increase in 4-deoxythreonic acid levels further confirms the significance of oxidative stress in PE. Diethyl phosphate, a common organophosphate pesticide metabolite, suggests a potential link between environmental exposures and PE risk. Previous studies have indicated that prenatal exposure to certain environmental endocrine disruptors may impair placental development and function, potentially increasing the risk of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [24, 25]. We acknowledge that future experimental studies are crucial to elucidate the specific role of diethyl phosphate in PE pathogenesis.

Placental ischemia-hypoxia can lead to the release of anti-angiogenic factors (such as sFlt-1), causing vasoconstriction, endothelial injury, and an increase in systemic blood pressure [26]. Concurrently, inflammatory factors further promote oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, accelerating metabolic imbalance. In this study, pathway enrichment analysis revealed disturbances in several metabolic pathways in PE patients, with vitamin B6 metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and histidine metabolism representing core disordered pathways. Vitamin B6, a coenzyme for numerous enzymatic reactions, may promote endothelial dysfunction via impaired amino acid metabolism when dysregulated [27]. Abnormal steroid hormone synthesis directly impacts placental function, while histidine metabolism disturbances are increasingly recognized as novel markers of oxidative stress and inflammation. Collectively, these findings elucidate the complex metabolic pathophysiology of PE and provide new insights into its pathogenesis.

Currently, biomarkers and models for PE primarily include the sFlt-1/PLGF ratio, cfDNA methylation signatures, miRNAs, and blood metabolic markers [28]. Additionally, machine learning models and clinical scoring systems based on clinical indicators, biomarkers, multi-omics data, and imaging metrics have been developed [29, 30] ; however, limitations persist, including insufficient biomarker specificity, sensitivity and inadequate external model validation. By integrating multiple biomarkers using machine learning, this study significantly improved predictive efficacy. Our XGBoost model demonstrated superior accuracy and robustness compared to traditional methods. Crucially, unlike invasive plasma or tissue samples, our method offers advantages of non-invasiveness and ease of collection, providing high clinical applicability potential, especially for dynamic monitoring during pregnancy.

This study has several limitations. First, the samples in this study were primarily sourced from single center, which may limit its predictive ability across different ethnicities or populations, necessitating further validation with multi-center cohorts. Second, the development of PE involves dynamic changes in metabolites during pregnancy, future work will need to integrate gestational age specific metabolites to achieve stratified risk prediction. Third, since the urine samples were collected at the time before delivery, it remains unclear that the identified metabolic changes are a cause or a consequence of the clinical syndrome. Therefore, longitudinal studies with serial sample collection are needed to confirm their predictive value for PE. Finally, although estriol-17-glucuronide was identified as a key metabolite, its molecular mechanism in placental dysfunction is not yet clear. In summary, this study provides new evidence supporting the clinical utility of urinary metabolites for PE detection, but clinical translation requires advancement through multi-center validation and mechanistic research.

Conclusion

This study identified a signature composed of four urinary metabolites and developed an XGBoost machine learning prediction model, providing novel tools for non-invasive early PE diagnosis. These results not only help to identify high-risk populations for PE, but also provide new insights into PE pathogenesis.
