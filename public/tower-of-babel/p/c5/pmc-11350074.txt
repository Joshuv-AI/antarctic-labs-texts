RAM1 orchestrates arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis in non-legumes
Authors: Chandan Kumar Gautam, Prema Mutyala, Debatosh Das
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This article comments on: Ho-Plágaro T, Tamayo-Navarrete MI, Ćavar Zeljković S, Tarkowski P, García-Garrido JM. 2024. A dual regulatory role of the arbuscular mycorrhizal master regulator RAM1 in tomato. Journal of Experimental Botany 75, 5021–5036. https://doi.org/10.1093/jxb/erae210

Arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbiosis is a tightly regulated process reported in the majority of land plants. While the molecular pathways governing the autoregulation of mycorrhization (AOM) have been thoroughly explored in legumes such as Medicago, soybean, and Lotus, the understanding of this pathway in non-legumes remains limited. In their study, Ho-Plágaro et al. (2024) elucidate how the RAM1 transcription factor (TF) facilitates AM symbiosis and potentially enacts negative regulation of mycorrhization in tomato. They suggest that this negative regulation might occur through the inhibition of strigolactone biosynthesis, thereby indicating the potential role of RAM1 in AOM.

Plants’ association with the soil microbiome is regulated by complex crosstalks and processes, including the exchange of chemical signals, hormonal signaling, transcriptional reprogramming, and morphological changes in the host (Wang et al., 2017). AM symbiosis, which is preferred under phosphate starvation, is one of the most widely reported plant–microbial associations in nature occurring in the majority of land plants. The initial stage in AM symbiosis involves the exchange of chemical signals, starting with the phosphate starvation-induced release of strigolactones by the host plant which stimulates the germination of fungal spores (Wang et al., 2017). The germinated spores form hyphae which branch extensively and attach to the root surface as a hyphopodium which triggers a series of downstream pathways leading to the final accommodation of the AM fungi inside the cortical cells. The release of chitinaceous molecules (Myc-LCOs) by AM fungi, which are detected by specific LysM receptor-like kinases (LysM-RLKs) located in the plasma membrane of the host plant cell, is essential for the onset of molecular signaling leading to host colonization (Ho-Plágaro and García-Garrido, 2022). It has been shown that several TFs including CCaMK, CYCLOPS, GRAS, DELLA, MIG1, SCL3, and RAM1 influence the transcriptional regulatory network which facilitates the hosting and development of arbuscules in the host plant (Ho-Plágaro et al., 2019).

RAM1 transcription factor: conserved regulator of arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis signaling across plant species

Among several TFs involved in AM symbiosis, RAM1, which belongs to the GRAS domain-containing family, has been reported to be pivotal in AM symbiosis signaling, with conserved function across both legumes [Medicago (Gobbato et al., 2012) and Lotus (Park et al., 2015)] and non-legumes [Petunia (Rich et al., 2017) and Brachypodium (Müller et al., 2020)]. RAM1 is activated by DELLA complexed with CCaMK and CYCLOPS, which are triggered upon the perception of fungal Myc-factors by LysM-RLKs (Pimprikar et al., 2016). RAM1 affects several other GRAS domain-containing proteins and transcriptionally regulates AM pathway genes including those involved in nutrient exchange and arbuscular development. Previously characterized ram1 mutants showed reduced arbuscular branching and colonization across several plant species (Gobbato et al., 2012; Park et al., 2015; Rich et al., 2017; Müller et al., 2020). The tomato SlRAM1 (SlGRAS27) characterized in this study was initially reported as one of the GRAS tomato genes up-regulated upon mycorrhization (Ho-Plágaro et al., 2019). In this study, plants with SlRAM1-silenced roots colonized with the AM fungus Rhizophagus irregularis (Ri) showed a significant reduction in mycorrhization parameters including the percentage of total root length colonization and the prevalence of different types of arbuscules, as well as a decreased gene expression of the AM-associated phosphate transporter gene (SlPT4), suggesting SlGRAS27 as the putative RAM1 ortholog in tomato (Ho-Plágaro et al., 2024). Interestingly, upon overexpression of SlRAM1, the mycorrhization parameters showed significant reduction especially at later stages (82 d post-inoculation), which is contrary to previously reported studies in the non-legume Brachypodium (Müller et al., 2020). Furthermore, while the gene expression levels of AM marker genes such as CCD7, CYCLOPS, and CCaMK displayed a decreasing trend in the SlRAM1 composite plants, the fungal elongation factor 1-alpha (RiEF) did not exhibit a corresponding reduction, indicating a nearly static growth of the fungi. Global transcriptional analysis of non-inoculated SlRAM1 overexpression composite plants showed down-regulation of AM-induced genes and the co-regulation of 383 genes by AM and SlRAM1. Interestingly, there was a reduced expression of strigolactone biosynthesis genes such as CCD7, CCD8, and MAX1, which was also supported by reduced strigolactone content in the roots of SlRAM1 overexpression lines. Nevertheless, the expression also hinted at a positive regulation of mycorrhization, as reflected by the up-regulation of RAM2 and expansin/EXLB1. RAM2 encodes a glycerol-3-phosphate acyl transferase (GPAT) crucially involved in synthesizing cutin monomers necessary for the formation of hyphopodia in AM fungi (Loo et al., 2002). Inconsistent findings were also observed, including the lack of altered expression of key marker genes such as STR, FatM, and AMT2. These genes had been previously shown to be up-regulated by RAM1 overexpression in non-inoculated roots of other plant species. These discrepancies may stem from variations in experimental conditions or potential feedback regulation mechanisms (Park et al., 2015; Pimprikar et al., 2016; Müller et al., 2020).

Is RAM1–DELLA–GA at the heart of AOM in non-legumes?

The host plant receives nutrients, mainly Pi, and water from the fungal partner and in return provides photosynthetically fixed carbon compounds. To regulate the expenditure of resources and control the extent of colonization, host plants employ the AOM pathway. A well-studied receptor type in this pathway is the CLAVATA 1 (CLV1)-like LRR-RLK (leucine-rich repeat receptor-like kinase) receptor which has been studied across different legumes (sym29, sunn, nark, and har1) (Bashyal et al., 2024). Legumes possess the unique ability to symbiotically associate with rhizobia as well as AM, with the ability to control autoregulation of nodulation (AON) pathways in parallel. Interestingly, there are several shared components between the AON and AOM pathways. Non-legumes, which are devoid of AON pathways, have AOM-specific pathways that offer unique insights into mycorrhization events. From recent research on AOM in Medicago, we know that the CLV1-like receptor is a putative target for several CLE peptides, perceiving nutrients as well as symbiotic signals to regulate this AOM. However, this process remains poorly understood in non-legumes. The AOM pathway in non-legumes received attention with the first genetic evidence reporting the role of the LRR receptor-like protein (SlCLV-2) in AOM in tomato (Wang et al., 2018). Other studies also support the role of SlCLV2, along with the CLV1 ortholog (SlFAB) and the hydroxyproline O-arabinosyltransferase enzyme (FIN), in the negative regulation of AM colonization in tomato (Wang et al., 2021). Although SlCL2 was found to act in both roots and shoots, SlFAB acts locally in the roots, which contrasts with the reported action of CLV1 in shoots in Medicago (Müller et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021). Still, it is interesting to note that CLV1 shares similarity in both legumes and non-legumes when it comes to negative regulation of AM symbiosis, suggesting that CLV1 is a shared element across AOM and AON pathways (Müller et al., 2019, 2020; Wang et al., 2021). Müller et al. (2020) reported that ectopic expression of RAM1 in uninoculated Brachypodium down-regulated strigolactone biosynthesis pathway genes, which is similar to the effect of SlRAM1 overexpression in tomato, hinting at their possible native function in the regulation of strigolactone biosynthesis in these non-legumes. A similar up-regulation trend was observed for RAM2 in both studies (Müller et al., 2020; Ho-Plágaro et al., 2024). Furthermore, SlRAM1 overexpression led to up-regulation of the AM suppression peptide SlCLE11, which does not appear to require the two CLE receptors, SlFAB or SlCLV2 (Wulf et al., 2024; Ho-Plágaro et al., 2024). This implicates SlRAM1 as a potential missing component of the AOM pathway in non-legumes (Fig. 1). SlRAM1, either directly or in conjunction with other CLE/receptors, probably modulates AM symbiotic pathways and strigolactone biosynthesis (Fig. 1). Future studies should investigate whether these CLEs can directly bind to RAM1 and influence downstream signaling, or if they provide feedback to the known upstream DELLA–CYCLOPS complex (Pimprikar et al., 2016). Among the key hormonal regulators of AM symbiosis, auxins, strigolactones, and gibberellins (GAs), GA occupies a unique position as it has been reported to be both a positive and a negative regulator of AM symbiosis (Liao et al., 2018). DELLA, which acts as a central regulator in coordinating plant hormone signaling during AM symbiosis, is a known repressor of GA signaling. It binds to the GA receptors in the presence of bioactive GA and results in proteolytic degradation of DELLA, consequently fine-tuning the AM symbiosis (Floss et al., 2013). Although initially reported as having negative effects on AM symbiosis, findings in tomato (Martín‐Rodríguez et al., 2015) and Lotus japonicus (Takeda et al, 2015) suggest the role of GAs in modulating arbuscule formation in a dose-dependent manner (Liao et al., 2018). The AM-induced subtilisin-like serine protease1 (SbtM1) required for AM fungal colonization is repressed under low GA (Takeda et al., 2015). Intriguingly, SbtM1 and the AM symbiosis-specific phosphate transfer gene (PT4) are also transcriptionally influenced by RAM1 (Pimprikar et al., 2016). The change in levels of DELLA has been shown to influence RAM1 which is activated by DELLA complexed with CCaMK and CYCLOPS (Pimprikar et al., 2016), thereby establishing a potential feedback loop-based regulation of AM symbiosis with RAM1–DELLA–GA as key players (Fig. 1). Although speculating about the correct position of CLV1 in this loop remains challenging due to limited experimental evidence, it could potentially engage in AOM through regulation of strigolactone biosynthesis, as in legumes (Fig. 1). Further understanding of these interactions will provide valuable insights into the regulatory mechanisms governing AM symbiosis in non-legume plants such as tomato. Nevertheless, it is worth emphasizing that the regulation of AM symbiosis by GA can vary significantly among different host plants. For instance, in Eustoma grandiflorum, GA facilitates the development of Paris-type (intracellular hyphae and arbuscules) AM symbiosis, whereas, in the legume model plant L. japonicus, it inhibits the formation of Arum-type (intercellular hyphae and arbuscules) AM symbiosis (Tominaga et al, 2021). Similar to GAs, other hormones, genes, or gene products might demonstrate a dual role in regulating AM symbiosis. Furthermore, concerning genes, even though they are orthologous, their functions may not necessarily be identical across different species.

RAM1 as a potential central player in autoregulation of mycorrhization (AOM) in non-legumes. This diagram provides a detailed depiction of the molecular signaling pathways engaged during arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) symbiosis in plants. Strigolactone (SL), secreted by plant roots, activates the metabolism and spore germination of AM fungi, initiating symbiotic interactions. The signaling cascade begins with the activation of calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase (CCaMK) and its interaction with CYCLOPS, which are critical for downstream signaling events. CCaMK–CYCLOPS in complex with DELLA proteins led to the activation of RAM1, a key transcription factor specifically involved in transcriptional response to mycorrhization. RAM1 positively regulates the expression of genes such as SbtM1, which supports intraradical colonization, and RAM2 and PT4, which are essential for arbuscule development and maintenance. Interestingly, the plant hormone gibberellin (GA) plays a dual role in mycorrhization; while it inhibits arbuscule formation through the degradation of DELLA proteins, it also enhances the expression of SbtM1, facilitating intraradical colonization. This demonstrates the complex regulatory effects of GA within the symbiotic process. SL biosynthesis is negatively affected by the CLAVATA pathway in legumes, contributing to the autoregulation of mycorrhization. Recent findings by Ho-Plágaro et al. (2024) suggest that RAM1 may also impart negative feedback on AM symbiosis by down-regulating strigolactone biosynthesis suggesting its role in AOM.
