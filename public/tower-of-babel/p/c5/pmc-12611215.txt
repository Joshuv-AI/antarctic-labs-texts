Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2 Inhibitors-Induced Euglycemic Ketoacidosis Mimicking Myocardial Infarction in a Non-Diabetic Heart Failure Patient
Authors: KilYoon Pack, Kyung Eun Ha, Taeil Yang, Wook-Jin Chung
Published: 2025

INTRODUCTION

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) have become a cornerstone in the management of heart failure (HF) across the spectrum of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), increasingly used for their cardiovascular and renal benefits.12) However, SGLT2i may cause serious adverse events, including diabetic ketoacidosis and have also been reported to induce euglycemic ketoacidosis even in non-diabetic patients.3) As the incidence and risk factors of SGLT2i-induced euglycemic ketoacidosis in non-diabetic individuals are not well established, timely diagnosis remains challenging due to atypical presentations.4) Here, we present the case of a 40-year-old man with HF with reduced EF (HFrEF) who developed euglycemic ketoacidosis, initially presenting with the atypical symptoms of chest and back pain.

CASE

A 40-year-old male presented to the emergency department with severe chest and back pain. One hour prior to hospital admission, he developed sudden, crushing retrosternal chest discomfort radiating diffusely to the back, accompanied by diaphoresis, which persisted until presentation. His medical history included a left main coronary artery dissection secondary to cardiac contusion from a traffic accident three years prior, leading to HFrEF with an LVEF of 34%. He had been on dapagliflozin 5 mg once daily for the past two years, along with sacubitril/valsartan 25 mg twice daily, carvedilol 1.5625 mg twice daily, and ivabradine 5 mg twice daily, all of which he had been taking continuously as part of guideline-directed medical therapy for HF. For a month before admission, he experienced psychological stress related to personal issues, along with reduced appetite and intentional weight control. Despite the absence of interval changes on initial electrocardiography including persistent Q wave infarction in the anterior leads (Figure 1A), ongoing chest pain and a history of coronary stenting warranted urgent coronary angiography. We performed coronary angiography, which showed no significant stenosis compared with previous study (Figure 1B). Chest aortography and echocardiography revealed no evidence of aortic dissection or pericardial effusion (Figure 1C and D). Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated a mixed acid–base disorder, characterized by metabolic acidosis accompanied by marked respiratory alkalosis (pH 7.7 [ref. 7.35–7.45], PCO2 10 mmHg [ref. 35–45], HCO3 − 12.4 mEq/L [ref. 22–26], lactate 5.2 mmol/L [ref. 0.5–2.2]). Laboratory findings included elevated serum ketones (2,900 µmol/L [ref. 28–120]), near-normal serum glucose (135 mg/dL), HbA1c of 5.7%, and urinalysis indicating glycosuria (glucose 4+). These findings suggested a diagnosis of SGLT2i-induced euglycemic ketoacidosis. We postulated that hyperventilation from respiratory compensation led to respiratory muscle fatigue, contributing to the presenting chest and back pain. Following nutritional support and discontinuation of SGLT2i, his symptoms resolved, accompanied by improvement in metabolic parameters including a decrease in serum ketones to 258 µmol/L, correction of acidosis, and clearance of glycosuria (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

This case underscores the diagnostic challenge of euglycemic ketoacidosis presenting with acute chest and back pain, which may mimic myocardial infarction. Although euglycemic ketoacidosis in non-diabetic patients with HFrEF receiving SGLT2i has been reported, it typically presents with gastrointestinal symptoms.3) With the expanding use of SGLT2 inhibitors beyond diabetes into HF, clinicians should be aware that atypical manifestations—such as chest pain mimicking myocardial infarction, as seen in this case—may occur, highlighting the importance of careful differential diagnosis.

SGLT2i promote glycosuria and natriuresis by inhibiting renal glucose and sodium reabsorption, contributing to cardiovascular and renal benefits.1) However, by reducing glucose availability, stimulating glucagon release, and increasing ketogenesis, SGLT2i can precipitate ketoacidosis—particularly in the setting of fasting, stress, or acute illness.5) Euglycemic ketoacidosis may occur even in the absence of diabetes, as described in prior case reports.4) The condition most commonly presents with nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms, including nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, and may also be associated with malaise or dyspnea. Unlike prior reports, however, the predominant symptom in our case is severe, sharp chest and back pain, rather than gastrointestinal complaints. Although myocardial infarction is initially suspected due to the patient’s past medical history, coronary angiography shows no progression of coronary lesions compared to prior studies, supporting a non-ischemic cause of the presenting symptoms.

Although our patient differs in not having diabetes, comparable symptoms have been reported in diabetic patients. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain this association. Endothelial dysfunction leading to coronary vasospasm, stress hormone–mediated metabolic alterations, and increased free fatty acid release from adipose tissue may contribute to ischemia-like symptoms.6) Furthermore, recent reports have described life-threatening coronary vasospastic angina triggered by SGLT2 inhibitor–associated euglycemic ketoacidosis, potentially mediated by impaired acetylcholine metabolism and hyperventilation.7) In our patient, coronary angiography does not reveal angiographic evidence of vasospasm. However, given that vasospasm is a dynamic phenomenon that may not always be captured during angiography, a contribution of transient vasospasm, particularly in the setting of euglycemic ketoacidosis and hyperventilation, cannot be entirely excluded.8)

The elevated lactate level (5.2 mmol/L) in this case is higher than typically reported in euglycemic ketoacidosis. While respiratory muscle fatigue from compensatory hyperventilation may have contributed.8) additional factors such as reduced oral intake, dehydration, and psychological stress could have resulted in relative tissue hypoperfusion or impaired lactate clearance, thereby amplifying lactate levels.

To date, evidence regarding the relationship between HF and euglycemic ketoacidosis remains limited. A Korean multicenter study suggested that HF aggravation could precipitate SGLT2 inhibitor–associated ketoacidosis,9) whereas a pharmacovigilance analysis of the US FAERS database did not demonstrate a higher frequency of euglycemic ketoacidosis in patients with HF compared with those without.10) Collectively, these findings indicate that while HF itself may not independently increase the risk, acute decompensation and related clinical stressors may serve as important triggers in vulnerable patients. Further research is warranted to clarify this association.

Clinicians should maintain vigilance for euglycemic ketoacidosis in HFrEF patients on SGLT2i regardless of diabetes status, especially in the setting of reduced appetite, dehydration, or intercurrent illness. Regular monitoring of ketone levels and acid-base status, along with temporary discontinuation of SGLT2i when appropriate, may help prevent this complication. Further research is needed to better define the incidence, risk factors, and optimal management strategies for euglycemic ketoacidosis in this population.
