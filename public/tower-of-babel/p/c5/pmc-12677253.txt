Comparative Effectiveness of Acupuncture and Receptive Music Therapy for Sleep Disorders amongst Healthcare Workers with Occupational Noise Exposure: A Single-Centre Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: DingJun Zhou, JinDong Li, Juan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:To evaluate the therapeutic effectiveness of acupuncture, receptive music therapy and their combination for managing noise-induced sleep disorders amongst healthcare workers.Methods:This research represents a retrospective cohort analysis of 145 healthcare workers with noise-induced sleep disorders (January 2023–December 2024). The participants received conventional pharmacological treatment (n = 45), acupuncture monotherapy (n = 52) or combined acupuncture–music therapy (n = 48) for 8 weeks. Primary outcomes: Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), polysomnographic parameter. Secondary outcomes: salivary cortisol, the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale and the World Health Organization’s Quality of Life–BREF.Results:Combined therapy demonstrated superior outcomes, with PSQI scores decreasing from 9.47 ± 2.13 to 5.82 ± 1.65 (Δ = −3.65, P < 0.001) and sleep efficiency improving from 68.35 ± 8.42% to 84.73 ± 6.28% (P < 0.001). Acupuncture monotherapy achieved comparable results to pharmacotherapy at 8 weeks (PSQI: 7.92 ± 1.98 vs. 7.89 ± 2.03, P = 0.941), with superior sustainability at the 12-week follow-up (7.45 ± 1.89 vs. 8.12 ± 2.08, P < 0.05). Salivary cortisol levels were significantly lower in the combined therapy group (0.52 ± 0.11 µg/dL) than in the control group (0.78 ± 0.15 µg/dL, P < 0.001). Multivariate regression confirmed robustness after adjusting for confounders.Conclusion:Compared with monotherapy approaches, combined acupuncture–music therapy demonstrates superior efficacy for noise-induced sleep disorders, supporting the integration of multimodal approaches in occupational health management.

INTRODUCTION

Occupational noise exposure represents a pervasive environmental stressor in contemporary healthcare settings, with emergency departments, intensive care units and surgical suites routinely exceeding recommended acoustic thresholds.[12] The World Health Organization stipulates that healthcare environments should maintain noise levels below 35 dB(A) during the daytime and 30 dB(A)[3] during nighttime hours, but empirical measurements consistently document exposures in the range of 55–85 dB(A) in clinical areas. This acoustic pollution creates a cascade of physiological disruptions, with sleep architecture disturbances representing one of the most prevalent and debilitating consequences for healthcare personnel.[4]

The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying noise-induced sleep disorders involve complex interactions between auditory processing pathways and circadian regulatory systems.[5] Chronic noise exposure activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, elevating cortisol secretion and disrupting melatonin synthesis, thereby compromising sleep initiation and maintenance.[6] Furthermore, acoustic stimuli during sleep periods fragment normal sleep architecture, reducing slow-wave sleep duration and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep quality, ultimately resulting in nonrestorative sleep patterns.[7]

Traditional pharmacological treatments for sleep disorders, whilst efficacious in symptom management, often present limitations, including tolerance development, dependency risks and residual daytime sedation effects that compromise occupational performance.[8] Consequently, interest in complementary and alternative medicine approaches that offer holistic and noninvasive therapeutic modalities with minimal adverse effects has increased.[9]

Acupuncture, a cornerstone of traditional Chinese medicine, has demonstrated considerable promise in sleep disorder management via its regulatory effects on neurotransmitter systems and autonomic nervous system function.[10] Clinical evidence suggests that acupuncture treatments modulate the gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and serotonin pathways, promoting sleep initiation whilst enhancing sleep quality and duration.[11] Specific acupoint combinations, particularly Shenmen (HE7), Yintang (EX-HN3) and Baihui (GV20), have shown efficacy in addressing insomnia symptomatology via their calming and mind-settling properties.[12]

Music therapy represents another promising nonpharmacological treatment, leveraging the neuroplastic effects of musical stimuli on brain wave entrainment and emotional regulation.[13] Research has demonstrated that slow-tempo, low-frequency musical compositions can induce parasympathetic activation, reduce cortisol levels and synchronise brain waves to frequencies conducive to sleep onset.[14] The Mozart effect and other classical music compositions have been specifically validated for their sleep-promoting properties in clinical populations.[15]

Despite individual efficacy reports for acupuncture and music therapy in sleep disorder management, limited research has examined their combined therapeutic potential, particularly in the context of occupational noise-induced sleep disturbances amongst healthcare workers. To our knowledge, this represents the first comprehensive evaluation of combined acupuncture–music therapy protocols specifically targeting noise-induced sleep disorders in healthcare populations, addressing a critical gap in the occupational health literature. This population represents a unique clinical challenge, as they experience acute noise exposure during work periods and chronic sleep disruption patterns that compound over time.[16]

This retrospective analysis aimed to evaluate the comparative and synergistic effects of acupuncture and music therapy treatments on sleep disorders induced by occupational noise exposure amongst healthcare workers. We hypothesise that combined treatments demonstrate superior efficacy compared with individual treatments, providing a comprehensive therapeutic approach that addresses the physiological and psychological aspects of noise-induced sleep disturbances.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at tertiary hospitals between January 2023 and December 2024. Healthcare workers with documented noise-induced sleep disorders who had received acupuncture, combined acupuncture–music therapy or standard care were identified via electronic medical records and hospital treatment databases. The Institutional Review Board of Jingzhou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (202502003) approved the study protocol. All study participants signed informed consent forms.

Participant Selection and Classification

Initial screening identified 267 healthcare workers via systematic electronic health record review and occupational health surveillance databases. All individuals with documented insomnia were identified via our institution’s comprehensive healthcare worker wellness survey programme, which incorporates validated sleep quality assessment instruments. The following exclusion criteria were systematically applied: 52 participants were excluded due to pregnancy, lactation status or planning pregnancy within the study period; 39 participants, due to incomplete treatment records or missing follow-up data; 6 participants, for concurrent psychiatric medication use, potentially confounding sleep assessments; 9 participants, due to documented primary sleep disorders, unrelated to noise exposure and 16 participants, for treatment duration less than 8 weeks or documented noncompliance. After rigorous screening, 145 healthcare workers met the inclusion criteria and were retrospectively categorised into the following three treatment cohorts on the basis of documented therapeutic modalities: conventional pharmacological treatment (n = 45), acupuncture monotherapy (n = 52) and combined acupuncture–music therapy (n = 48).

The participants were retrospectively categorised into three groups on the basis of documented treatment modalities recorded in medical files: (1) the acupuncture group, which included patients who received acupuncture treatment alone; (2) the combined treatment group, which included patients who received acupuncture and music therapy and (3) the conventional pharmacological treatment group, which included patients who received standardised pharmacological treatment prescribed by hospital sleep medicine specialists. Group assignment was determined by reviewing comprehensive treatment records from the hospital’s integrative medicine department and sleep clinic databases. Treatment protocols were standardised across the study period according to established hospital clinical pathways. All treatments were documented according to institutional treatment guidelines, ensuring consistency in treatment delivery and outcome assessment procedures.

Participants

Healthcare workers from emergency departments, intensive care units and surgical suites with documented noise-induced sleep disorders were retrospectively identified via hospital electronic medical records and occupational health databases. The study population was derived from treatment logs of complementary therapy services provided by the hospital’s integrative medicine department.

The inclusion criteria for retrospective analysis were as follows: (1) aged 22–55 years; (2) registered nurses or physicians with ≥6 months of experience in high-noise clinical environments (LAeq ≥ 55 dB(A)) documented in hospital environmental monitoring records; (3) Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores of >5 recorded in medical files indicating clinically significant sleep disturbance; (4) documented patient-reported complaints that sleep disturbance began after noise exposure and improved during periods away from the noisy environment (e.g., vacations); (5) no history of sleep disorders prior to employment in high-noise areas; (6) complete treatment records with at least 8 weeks of documented treatment and (7) available pre- and posttreatment assessment data.

The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) pre-existing sleep disorders (sleep apnoea, restless leg syndrome, narcolepsy) documented in medical history prior to noise exposure; (2) psychiatric disorders requiring medication documented in pharmacy records; (3) pregnancy or lactation status during the treatment period; (4) concurrent use of sleep medications or psychoactive substances; (5) incomplete medical records or missing follow-up data; (6) treatment duration of <8 weeks or documented poor compliance; (7) acute upper respiratory infections, sinusitis or otitis media within the preceding week and (8) documented severe allergies to examination materials or environmental sensitivities.

Treatment Methods

Acupuncture Protocol

Licensed acupuncturists with ≥5 years of clinical experience performed standardised treatments via sterile and single-use stainless steel needles (0.25 × 25 mm2, Huatuo Brand, China). The acupoint prescription was systematically derived from traditional Chinese medicine theory and the following targeted ‘shen’ (mind–spirit) disturbance patterns: Shenmen (HE7), Yintang (EX-HN3), Baihui (GV20), Sishencong (EX-HN1), Anmian (EX-HN22) and Sanyinjiao (SP6).[17] Needles were inserted to standardised depths with manual manipulation to achieve ‘de qi’ sensation and then retained for 30 minutes with stimulation every 10 minutes. The treatment frequency was twice weekly for 8 weeks, totalling 16 sessions, which were consistently scheduled between 14:00 and 17:00 to optimise circadian effects.

Combined Acupuncture–Music Therapy Protocol

Participants received acupuncture treatment as described above and structured receptive music therapy. Certified music therapists delivered individualised sessions within the institution’s acoustic therapy suite. Musical selections comprised slow-tempo compositions (60–80 beats per minute), including Mozart’s Piano Sonata K448, Bach’s Air on G String and Debussy’s Clair de Lune, which were selected on the basis of validated sleep-promoting acoustic characteristics. High-fidelity headphones (Sennheiser HD280 Pro) were used in controlled acoustic environments in all sessions. The protocol included 45-minute sessions three times weekly, scheduled 30–60 minutes before the participants’ typical bedtime. The following structured home practice protocol ensured treatment adherence: (1) personalised USB devices containing prescribed compositions; (2) digital logs documenting listening session completion three times weekly; (3) weekly telephone follow-up by certified therapists addressing compliance barriers and (4) biweekly in-person sessions for data download from automated tracking applications. The compliance threshold was defined as ≥80% adherence to prescribed sessions.

Conventional Pharmacological Treatment

Participants in the conventional treatment group received standardised pharmacological treatments for 8 weeks as prescribed by hospital sleep medicine specialists. The treatment protocol included zolpidem tartrate (5–10 mg) or eszopiclone (1–3 mg) administered 30 minutes before bedtime, with dosages individually titrated on the basis of clinical response and tolerance. All participants also received standardised sleep hygiene education and follow-up consultations every 2 weeks. Medication compliance was monitored via prescription refill records and patient-reported adherence logs. The participants were advised to avoid alcohol consumption and maintain consistent sleep schedules throughout the treatment period.

Outcome Measures

Primary Outcomes

(1) PSQI: The validated Chinese version assesses seven sleep domains over 4-week periods: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, sleep medication usage and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored between 0 and 3, with global scores in the range of 0–21. Clinical cutoffs: ≤5 indicates good sleep quality, 6–10 suggests poor quality and >10 indicates severe disturbance. The Chinese validation demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.83) in healthcare populations.[18] Assessments were conducted at baseline, 8 weeks and 12 weeks by blinded research personnel. PSQI questionnaires were completed within 7 days before PSG recordings.

(2) Polysomnographic Parameters: Comprehensive overnight sleep studies utilised digital polysomnography systems (Compumedics Grael, Australia) with standard monitoring: Electroencephalogram (EEG) (10–20 electrode placement), Electrooculogram (EOG), electromyographyelectromyogram (EMG), Electrocardiogram (ECG) and respiratory parameters. All the recordings were manually scored by certified technologists according to the american academy of sleep medicine (AASM) criteria, with >90% interrater reliability. The primary variables included sleep onset latency, total sleep time, sleep efficiency, wake after sleep onset, rapid eye movement (REM) latency and sleep architecture percentages. polysomnography (PSG) recordings were performed within the same week as the PSQI measurements.

Secondary Outcomes

(1) Salivary Cortisol: Morning (07:00–08:00) and evening (23:00–24:00) samples were collected using specialised salivette tubes with 30-minute fasting periods. The samples were analysed using high-sensitivity ELISA (Salimetrics 1-3002, USA; detection range of 0.007−3.0 µg/dL) with <5% intra-assay variation.

(2) Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A): This 14-item clinician-administered scale assesses anxiety severity across psychological and somatic domains. The total scores were in the range of 0–56 and classified as follows: 0–7 = minimal anxiety, 8–14 = mild anxiety, 15–23 = moderate anxiety and ≥24 = severe anxiety. The interitem reliability coefficient was α = 0.85. Trained researchers conducted structured interviews via validated Chinese translations, with >0.90 interrater reliability maintained.[19]

(3) World Health Organization’s Quality of Life–BREF (WHOQOL–BREF) Scale: This 26-item instrument evaluates quality of life across four domains, namely, physical health (7 items), psychological well-being (6 items), social relationships (3 items) and the environment (8 items), in addition to two global items. The WHOQOL–BREF does not produce a single overall score; instead, it yields four domain scores.[20] Each domain score was calculated by taking the mean of items within the domain and multiplying by 4, resulting in domain scores in the range of 4–20. These raw scores were then transformed to a 0–100 scale via the following formula: (raw score − 4) × (100/16). Higher scores indicate better quality of life. The internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach’s α) ranged from 0.66 to 0.87 across the domains in our sample.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality assessment was performed via the Shapiro–Wilk test for all continuous variables. Normally distributed data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations, with between-group comparisons utilising one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc tests. For longitudinal data analysis, repeated-measures ANOVA was employed to assess group × time interactions. Within-group temporal changes were assessed via paired t tests. Multivariate linear regression models adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, professional experience, baseline noise exposure, baseline anxiety levels (HAM-A scores) and body mass index (BMI), were used. The statistical significance threshold was established at a two-tailed P-value of <0.05.

Anxiety and Quality of Life

Hamilton Anxiety scores decreased significantly in the treatment cohort, with the combined therapy group achieving the most substantial reduction (15.41 ± 3.38–7.23 ± 2.45, P < 0.001), representing a 53% improvement. The quality of life assessments demonstrated comprehensive enhancements across all the WHOQOL–BREF domains, with particularly pronounced improvements in the psychological and environmental subscales. Acupuncture monotherapy significantly reduced anxiety and improved quality of life, although the effects were less pronounced than those of combined therapy.

Treatment Adherence and Safety Profile

The treatment completion rates were 93.3% for conventional pharmacotherapy, 96.2% for acupuncture monotherapy and 91.7% for combined therapy. Adverse events were minimal across all treatment modalities. Mild needle site tenderness occurred in 9.6% of the acupuncture recipients, resolving within 24 hours without treatment. The pharmacotherapy recipients reported morning grogginess (24.4%) and taste alterations (13.3%). No serious adverse events were documented across any treatment modality [Table 5].

DISCUSSION

This retrospective analysis provides evidence for the therapeutic efficacy of acupuncture and music therapy treatments in ameliorating noise-induced sleep disorders amongst healthcare workers. The findings demonstrate that whilst individual treatments offer significant benefits, their combination produces additive effects that exceed those of single-modality treatments, supporting our primary hypothesis and extending the existing evidence base for complementary medicine approaches in occupational health management.

The enhanced performance of combined treatments can be understood via their complementary mechanisms of action addressing different aspects of sleep disturbance pathophysiology. Acupuncture treatments appear to primarily modulate the neurochemical pathways associated with sleep regulation, particularly those that increase GABAergic neurotransmission and reduce sympathetic nervous system hyperactivity.[21]

The therapeutic mechanisms of music therapy operate via different but complementary pathways, primarily involving brain wave entrainment and emotional regulation.[22] The slow-tempo and harmonically structured compositions used in our protocol likely facilitated cortical synchronisation to frequencies conducive to sleep onset whilst simultaneously reducing cortisol production via parasympathetic activation.[23] The significant reductions in salivary cortisol levels across the treatment groups, with the most pronounced effects in the combined treatment condition, support this mechanistic understanding and demonstrate the treatments’ capacity to address the psychological and physiological aspects of stress-related sleep disturbances.

Our findings align with and extend previous research in this domain. Recent meta-analyses of acupuncture for insomnia reported effect sizes in the range of 0.66–0.89 for PSQI improvements, whereas our acupuncture monotherapy group achieved a comparable effect size of 0.87. The combined treatment effect size of 1.73 substantially exceeds previously reported values for either modality alone. This enhanced efficacy may reflect synergistic neurobiological mechanisms, with acupuncture providing sustained neuromodulation, whereas music therapy offers immediate parasympathetic activation and conditioned relaxation responses. Notably, our results exceed those reported by Han et al.,[24] who reported that music therapy alone produced PSQI improvements of 3.2 points, whereas our combined therapy achieved a 3.65-point reduction.

The sustainability of the therapeutic effects observed at the 12-week follow-up represents a particularly noteworthy finding, distinguishing these treatments from many pharmacological approaches that often exhibit diminishing efficacy over time owing to tolerance development.[25] This durability may reflect the capacity of the treatments to induce neuroplastic changes and behavioural modifications that persist beyond the active treatment period. For acupuncture, repeated needle stimulation may produce lasting alterations in neural pathway connectivity, whereas music therapy may establish conditioned relaxation responses that participants can self-activate via learnt techniques.[26]

The differential impact on objective versus subjective sleep measures warrants careful interpretation. Whilst polysomnographic improvements were most pronounced in the combined treatment group, the subjective PSQI scores showed proportionally greater improvements across all treatment arms. This discordance may reflect the multidimensional nature of sleep quality perception, incorporating psychological factors beyond objective sleep architecture. The substantial improvements in anxiety scores and quality of life measures in the treatment groups suggest that addressing the psychological burden of sleep disturbance may be as important as improving objective sleep parameters.

The clinical significance of our findings extends beyond statistical measures to practical occupational health implications. The mean PSQI score reduction of 3.65 points in the combined treatment group substantially exceeded the minimum clinically important difference of 2.0 points, indicating meaningful improvements in sleep quality that are likely to translate to enhanced job performance, reduced medical errors and improved quality of life.[27] Furthermore, improvements in polysomnographic parameters, particularly sleep efficiency, which increases by more than 16.4%, represent clinically meaningful changes that correlate with improvements in subjective sleep quality and demonstrate objective validation of therapeutic effects.[28] Our findings align with and extend previous research examining complementary medicine approaches for sleep disorders. Systematic reviews have identified acupuncture as moderately effective for insomnia treatment, whereas meta-analyses of music therapy treatments have consistently demonstrated small-to-medium effect sizes for sleep quality improvements.[2429] However, our study represents one of the first comprehensive evaluations of combined acupuncture and music therapy protocols specifically targeting noise-induced sleep disorders in healthcare populations, addressing a significant gap in the occupational health literature. The retrospective design, whilst presenting certain methodological limitations, offers distinct advantages in evaluating real-world clinical effectiveness. In contrast to controlled trials that may not reflect routine clinical practice, our analysis captures the therapeutic outcomes achieved under standard care conditions, potentially providing more generalisable findings for clinical decision-making.[30] The use of propensity score matching and multivariable regression analyses helps address potential confounding factors inherent in observational studies, strengthening the validity of our conclusions. The economic implications of these findings merit consideration in healthcare policy contexts. Given the substantial costs associated with healthcare worker burnout, medical errors and turnover attributable to sleep disorders, the implementation of effective nonpharmacological treatments could yield significant healthcare system savings.[31] The relatively low cost and minimal side effect profile of acupuncture and music therapy make them attractive alternatives to pharmacological management, particularly for long-term treatment scenarios where drug tolerance and dependency concerns limit conventional approaches. However, several limitations must be acknowledged when these results are interpreted. First, the retrospective cohort design inherently precludes causal inference, and residual confounding may persist despite statistical adjustments. The absence of randomisation introduces selection bias, as treatment allocation may have been influenced by unmeasured patient preferences or provider characteristics. Second, the potential for information bias cannot be entirely eliminated despite statistical adjustments, particularly regarding self-reported outcomes and treatment adherence. Third, the study population comprised primarily female nurses from urban tertiary hospitals, potentially limiting generalizability to other healthcare settings or demographic groups. Fourth, the 8-week treatment duration, whilst producing significant effects, may not represent the optimal treatment duration for achieving maximal therapeutic benefits. Fifth, we did not control for all potential confounders, such as detailed psychological profiles, sleep hygiene practices, home environment factors or genetic predispositions to sleep disorders. Sixth, the lack of a dedicated music therapy-only group limits our ability to isolate the specific contribution of music therapy to the combined treatment effects. Finally, propensity score matching, whilst reducing observed confounding, cannot address unmeasured confounders that may influence treatment selection and outcomes.

Future research directions can explore dose–response relationships to optimise treatment protocols, investigate individual patient characteristics that predict treatment response and examine the comparative effectiveness of different acupuncture point combinations and music therapy genres. Prospective randomised controlled trials would provide higher-level evidence for these treatments, whereas long-term follow-up studies extending beyond 12 weeks would offer valuable insights into the durability of therapeutic effects and the potential need for maintenance treatments.

The integration of objective sleep monitoring technologies, such as wearable devices and home sleep testing, could increase the precision of outcome measurements whilst reducing research costs. Additionally, mechanistic studies utilising neuroimaging and advanced biomarker analyses would contribute to understanding the neurobiological foundations of observed therapeutic effects, potentially informing treatment optimisation strategies.

CONCLUSION

This retrospective analysis demonstrated the superior therapeutic efficacy of combined acupuncture–music therapy for noise-induced sleep disorders compared with monotherapy approaches. The significant improvements across subjective and objective sleep parameters, which were sustained at the 12-week follow-up with excellent safety profiles, support the integration of these modalities into occupational health programmes. Healthcare institutions should consider implementing these evidence-based and nonpharmacological treatments within comprehensive workplace wellness initiatives to address the burden of noise-related sleep disorders, potentially improving healthcare worker well-being and patient care quality.
