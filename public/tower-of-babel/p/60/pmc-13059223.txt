Comparison of two-lung ventilation with CO₂ artificial pneumothorax and one-lung ventilation during esophagectomy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chen Li, Jiang Shen, Linyi Yang, Chen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is limited research data on the effect of different ventilation modes and airway devices on postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) during esophagectomy. Our single-center retrospective study aimed to compare the incidence of PPCs between two-lung ventilation (TLV) with CO₂ artificial pneumothorax using a single-lumen tube (SLT) and one-lung ventilation (OLV) using either a double-lumen tube (DLT) or a bronchial blocker (BB).MethodsPatients undergoing either open or minimally invasive surgeries on esophageal cancer between January 1st, 2022, and December 31st, 2024, at a tertiary teaching hospital were enrolled in the study. All the eligible cases were grouped based on the ventilation modes: TLV with CO2 artificial pneumothorax using an SLT and OLV with a DLT or a BB. The primary outcome was PPCs, which were defined with clinical manifestations and imaging findings, including pneumonia, hemothorax, hydrothorax, pulmonary atelectasis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation. The secondary outcomes included pneumonia, in-hospital mortality, hospital length of stay (LOS), reoperation, anastomotic leak, infection, deep venous thrombus (DVT), chylothorax, and overall postoperative complications.ResultsA total of 455 patients were included, with 331 patients in the TLV cohort and 124 patients in the OLV cohort. According to the unadjusted and adjusted analyses, patients in the TLV cohort had a lower incidence of PPCs (31/331 vs. 20/124, 9.4% vs. 16.1%, unadjusted results: OR 0.5, 95% CI 0.3–0.9, P = 0.044; adjusted results: OR 0.3, 95% CI 0.2–0.7, P = 0.008) and pneumonia (27/331 vs. 18/124, 8.2% vs. 14.5%, unadjusted results: OR 0.5, 95% CI 0.3–0.9, P = 0.046; adjusted results: OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.2–0.9, P = 0.031) than patients in the OLV cohort. There were no significant differences between the cohorts in terms of LOS, and other postoperative complications. In the subgroup analysis, in which only minimal invasive esophagectomy was included, the incidence of PPCs and pneumonia was higher in OLV group than in TLV group (12/50 vs. 31/304, 24% vs. 10.2%, unadjusted results: OR 2.78, 95% CI 1.32–5.87, P = 0.007; adjusted results: OR 2.31, 95% CI 1.03–5.14, P = 0.041). No difference was found in pneumonia, in-hospital mortality, reoperation, anastomotic leak, infection, DVT, chylothorax, and overall postoperative complications in the MIE subgroup after adjustment.ConclusionPPCs represent one of the most severe adverse events following esophagectomy. The use of TLV with CO₂ artificial pneumothorax via an SLT was associated with a lower incidence of PPCs in these patients. These findings underscore the potential advantage of TLV with CO₂ insufflation in enhancing perioperative outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03726-x.Keywords: Postoperative pulmonary complications, Two-lung ventilation, CO2 artificial pneumothorax, One-lung ventilation, Esophagectomy

Introduction

Esophageal cancer ranked 11th in incidence and 7th in global mortality as of 2022, according to a report from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) [1]. It remains a significant global healthcare burden [2]. In China, esophageal cancer is among the top ten cancers in both sexes and ranks among the top five in disability-adjusted life years (DALY), based on global epidemiological data released in 2024 [3]. A comparative analysis of esophageal cancer data revealed a significant increase in esophageal cancer-related mortality and DALY both in China and worldwide [4].

Surgical resection remains the mainstay of curative treatment for esophageal cancer. Multimodal therapies, including neoadjuvant therapy and multidisciplinary approaches to care, including anesthesia management strategies, may influence the perioperative outcomes for patients with esophageal cancer [5, 6]. The surgery techniques evolved from open surgery and minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE) to robotic-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (RAMIE) [7, 8]. Various risk factors have been reported to be associated with postoperative complications following esophagectomy, most of which have focused on surgical factors [9, 10].

Respiratory complications are the most common and severe postoperative complications following esophagectomy and are the leading cause of surgery-related mortality [11, 12]. A Large amount of research has confirmed that minimally invasive esophagectomy could decrease the incidence of pulmonary complications [13]. Few studies have investigated the anesthesia management and PPCs during esophagectomy concerning ventilation modes and airway devices. With the development of CO2 artificial pneumothorax for MIE and RAMIE, various airway management strategies have been adopted, including traditional one-lung ventilation (OLV) with double-lumen tube (DLT) or bronchial blocker (BB) and two-lung ventilation (TLV) with CO2 artificial pneumothorax.

Over the past three years, open surgery has gradually been replaced by MIE and RAMIE for esophagectomy in our center [14, 15]. All esophagectomy procedures in our hospital were performed by a certain team of surgeons, ensuring comparability and reliability. It is unclear whether the perioperative anesthesia management, especially ventilation modes and the selection of airway devices, affects the outcomes of the esophagectomy. This single-center, retrospective study aimed to compare different airway tools and ventilation modes on PPCs following esophagectomy.

Materials and methods

Design and setting

This was an observational single-center retrospective cohort study conducted in an academic tertiary teaching hospital. The study was implemented on the grounds of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement of cohort studies. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Changzhou First People’s Hospital with a waiver of informed consent.

Study population and eligibility criteria

With ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Changzhou First People’s Hospital, data were collected from the electronic medical record system. Two trained data reviewers independently accessed the system and extracted the records of the patients. Discrepancies were resolved based on predefined definitions and protocols. Additional information on relevant comorbidities and medical histories was retrieved from the hospital’s electronic clinical system. Telephone interviews were conducted for confirmation of details if necessary. Consecutive surgical esophageal cancer patients from January 1st, 2022, to December 31st, 2024, were recruited. The inclusion criterion was patients with esophageal cancer undergoing esophagectomy. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) cancellation of the surgery for contraindications; (2) receiving only non-surgical treatment; (2) multiple surgeries during hospital stay; (3) receiving other surgeries within 3 months; (4) important baseline data missing; (5) refusal telephone interview for important information.

Clinical endpoints and definitions

The primary endpoint was postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs). PPCs were defined based on clinical manifestation and imaging findings, including pneumonia, hemothorax, hydrothorax, pulmonary atelectasis, acute respiratory distress syndromes (ARDS), and respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation. Pneumonia was defined according to investigator assessment when at least one of the following criteria was met: new and persistent or progressive and persistent radiographic infiltrate, and at least one of: fever (≥ 38.0 °C or ≥ 100.4 °F), leukopenia (≤ 4000 WBC/mm3), or leukocytosis (≥ 12,000 WBC/mm3). Additionally, at least two of the following were observed: new onset of purulent sputum or change in the character of sputum, or increased respiratory secretions, or increased suctioning requirements, new-onset or worsening cough, or dyspnea, or tachypnea, rales or bronchial breath sounds, worsening gas exchange [e.g., O2 desaturations (e.g., PaO2/FiO2 <240)], increased oxygen requirements, or increased ventilator demand. The secondary outcomes included pneumonia, in-hospital mortality, hospital length of stay (LOS), reoperation, anastomotic leak, infection, deep venous thrombus (DVT), chylothorax, and overall postoperative complications.

TLV was defined as the ventilation of both lungs using a single-lumen tube (SLT) with CO2 artificial pneumothorax. OLV was defined as ventilation of the independent lung using a DLT or a bronchial blocker (BB). MIE was defined as a combination of laparothoracoscopy during esophagectomy. Robot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (RAMIE), first introduced in 2003, was a modified minimally invasive Da Vinci operation compared to conventional minimally invasive esophagectomy [15, 16]. The choice of the ventilation modes and airway devices was made individually according to the surgical procedure, conditions of the patients and preference of the anesthesiologists under the negotiation of both surgeons and anesthesiologists. The main principle of the choice is to fulfill the requirements of exposure of the surgery and oxygenation to the patients.

Covariates

The following confounders with categorizations were included in the adjusted analysis model for primary and secondary outcomes: age (continuous), sex (male or female), ASA classification (I, II, III), comorbidities (COPD, hypertension and cardiac disease), FEV1/FVC % (< 70, ≥ 70), cT stage (0,1,2,3,4), cN stage (0,1,2,3), tumor location (upper/middle, distal, esophagogastric junction [EGJ]), surgical approach (open, HMIE, TMIE), RAMIE, surgical technique (Ivor Lewis, McKeown, transhiatal).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as the means (SD) or medians (IQR) for normally and non-normally distributed variables. Percentages were used to describe category variables. Continuous variables between cohorts were compared with the independent sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared with the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Factors with P < 0.1 in the univariate analysis and with clinical significance were entered as covariates into the multivariate logistic regression for the endpoints. Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.5. The significance level was defined as a P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline patient demographics in OLV and TLV cohorts

From January 1st, 2022, to December 31st, 2024, a total of 589 patients were included in the retrospective cohort analysis. One hundred thirty-four patients were excluded, meeting the exclusion criteria, leaving 455 patients eligible for final data analysis. The flowchart of patient enrollment is presented in Fig. 1. Baseline characteristics and perioperative data are shown in Table 1. There were 124 patients receiving OLV, and 331 received TLV during procedures. Age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, comorbidities (including COPD, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiac disease), pulmonary function parameters (including FVC, FEV1, FEV1/FVC), cT, cM, tumor type (adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), other), tumor location, neoadjuvant treatment, and anemia were balanced between the cohorts. More patients received total or hybrid minimally invasive procedures in the TLV cohort. McKeown procedure was more common in the TLV cohort, who underwent longer surgical procedures and received more intraoperative infusions (Table 1).

Endpoints between different modes of ventilation ( OLV vs. TLV) in all cases

Postoperative complications were compared between the two cohorts. Twenty-seven patients in the TLV cohort had pneumonia compared with 18 patients in the OLV cohort, and the incidence of pneumonia was significantly lower in the TLV cohort (27/331 vs. 18/124, unadjusted results: OR 0.5, 95% CI 0.3–0.9, P = 0.046; adjusted results: OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.2–0.9, p = 0.031). Moreover, 31 patients in the TLV cohort had PPCs, which was significantly lower than in the OLV cohort (31/331 vs. 20/124, 9.4% vs. 16.1%, unadjusted results: OR 0.5, 95% CI 0.3–0.9, P = 0.044; adjusted results: OR 0.3, 95% CI 0.2–0.7, P = 0.008). The occurrence rate of anastomotic leak was higher in the TLV cohort before adjusting for confounders but was not significant difference after adjustment (26/331 vs. 2/124, 7.9% vs. 1.6%, unadjusted results: OR 5.2, 95% CI 1.2–22.3, P = 0.026; adjusted results: OR 4.8, 95% CI 0.5–44.2, P = 0.17). In-hospital mortality was higher in the OLV cohort (3/124) compared to the TLV cohort (5/331), but no significant difference existed between them (1.5% vs. 2.4%, unadjusted results: OR 0.6, 95% CI 0.2–2.6, P = 0.52; adjusted results: OR 0.7, 95% CI 0.3–1.3, P = 0.66). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed in the incidence rate of reoperation, infection, DVT, and chylothorax between the TLV and OLV cohorts. The length of hospital stay was also similar between the two groups (16 (13,20) vs. 16(14,21), P = 0.14). The reoperation, infection, DVT, and chylothorax were similar between cohorts (Tables 2 and 3). Furthermore, the percentage of patients with composite complications was lower in the TLV cohort compared with the OLV cohort (Hazard ratio: 0.50, 95% CI 0.28–0.90, P = 0.0023) (Fig. 2). And the forest plot from multivariate logistic analysis showed that the surgery approach and ventilation modes (TLV vs. OLV) could be the most important factors associated with PPCs, and there was no statistical interaction between them (p = 0.65) (Fig. 3).

Baseline demographics stratified by tube types ( SLT, BB, and DLT)

Discussion

PPCs frequently occur during and after esophagectomy [16]. PPCs are serious postoperative complications leading to longer hospital stays, more healthcare burdens, and other in-hospital complications [17]. Esophagectomy techniques have evolved over the years, from open to minimally invasive surgery, from hybrid minimally invasive to totally minimally invasive, and recently to RAMIE [8, 18, 19]. Advances in anesthesia techniques and intubation skills have paralleled these surgical developments. Traditional open surgery requires OLV to create an optimal surgical field, typically using a DLT or a BB. However, with the introduction of artificial pneumothorax, it has become feasible to ventilate both lungs using an SLT. The potential impact of ventilation modes and airway devices on the perioperative incidence of PPCs highlights the need for anesthesiologists to optimize perioperative airway management. In this retrospective cohort study, we found that compared with OLV using BB or DLT, patients in the TLV cohort had a lower incidence of PPCs, both in all patients and the MIE subgroup. Furthermore, the BB cohort exhibited a higher incidence of PPCs and pneumonia compared with the SLT cohort. These findings suggest that two-lung ventilation using an SLT is safe and feasible and may be associated with a lower incidence of PPCs in patients undergoing esophagectomy.

The overall complication rate after esophagectomy at our institution was 19.3% (88/455), comparable to the 18.4% reported in a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials [20]. The most common complication in our study was PPCs, occurring in 11.2% (51/455) of patients, compared to 9.2% (78/850) in the systematic review. The second most common complication was anastomotic leak, with an incidence of 6.2% (28/455), higher than 3.3% in the meta-analysis. Findings from another systematic review reported a PPC incidence of 19.2% (114/576), which was higher than the rate observed in our single-center study. There is no doubt that PPCs are identified as the most frequent postoperative complication, a finding that aligns with all the studies [21]. Additionally, an updated review on perioperative management in esophagectomy also highlighted those respiratory complications, including pneumonia, as the most common postoperative complications, further supporting our conclusions [22].

Pathophysiological changes occur during one-lung ventilation. Ventilation/blood flow (V/Q) imbalance arises due to lung perfusion redistribution [23]. In the dependent lung, the increased ventilation burden could lead to hyperinflation. Furthermore, elevated alveolar pressure may compress capillaries, reducing blood perfusion and exacerbating V/Q imbalance. In the independent lung, blood perfusion without ventilation causes anatomical shunt, leading to intraoperative hypoxemia [24]. To compensate, hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) reduces the shunt effect, which can be impaired by anesthetics and cause pulmonary artery pressure to increase [25]. In addition, factors like barotrauma, oxygen toxicity, and inflammation may further damage the lungs, increasing the risk of PPCs [26]. CO2 artificial pneumothorax was introduced to video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and has become the mainstay for esophagectomy at our institution. Unlike OLV with DLT or BB, TLV during CO2 artificial pneumothorax is performed using an SLT [27]. TLV with CO2 artificial pneumothorax gained popularity with rapid development of minimally invasive procedures for thoracic surgery, especially after the introduction of RAMIE in our institution. TLV using a single lumen endotracheal tube with CO2 insufflation artificial pneumothorax was first introduced into thoracoscopy as early as 1993, and first introduced into esophagectomy in 2006. It has been applied in minimally invasive esophagectomy in many countries such as India, Italy, Japan, Korea and China [28]. However, concerns about CO2 artificial pneumothorax are CO2 accumulation, induced respiratory acidosis, increased arterial CO2 pressure, and decreased pH levels, which might contribute to hemodynamic changes [29]. Several studies have confirmed the feasibility and safety of artificial CO2 pneumothorax in minimally invasive esophagectomies [30, 31]. TLV becomes the priority choice for most of the minimally invasive esophagectomy after negotiation between surgeons and anesthesiologists in our institution. First, the abdomen and cervical procedure during esophagectomy can be done without CO2 pneumothorax, avoiding double lumen tubes or bronchial blockers. Second, if the pneumothorax is not sufficient to achieve perfect lung collapse to provide a good view for the surgeon, a bronchial blocker will be used through the single-lumen tube easily as a backup to see if a better view can be acquired. Third, if a patient had once received a tracheotomy or a suspicious difficult airway, a single-lumen tube will be preferred instead of a double-lumen tube. However, air leak is an important concern, especially in the context of TLV, where the right lung remains inflated at the initial stage of pneumothorax and may be at higher risk of intraoperative injury. In our institution, during thoracic procedures using TLV, pulmonary collapse is achieved with controlled CO₂ insufflation pressure, a lower tidal volume ventilation strategy, and the semi-prone position, all of which facilitate lung deflation (see supplemental Figs. 2&3). Moreover, the choice between TLV and OLV should be individualized, taking into account the surgical approach, patient characteristics, and communication between the surgical and anesthesiology teams. Besides, TLV was associated with a lower incidence of PPCs both in all patients and the MIE subgroup compared with OLV, which suggested a strong and robust relationship.

Several studies have compared the effects of TLV with CO2 artificial pneumothorax and OLV with DLT or BB. Miao Lin et al. investigated TLV with CO2 artificial pneumothorax versus OLV during the thoracic phase of minimally invasive esophagectomy and concluded that TLV with CO2 with artificial pneumothorax is a safe and feasible choice during MIE. It was found in their study that CO2 absorption-induced changes were transient and returned to normal levels after CO2 insufflation was discontinued [32]. A meta-analysis including four case-control studies concluded that a single-lumen endotracheal tube with CO2 inflation reduced pulmonary complications, which was aligned with our study [33]. However, it contained several biases, including inconsistent definitions of pulmonary complications and variability in CO2 inflation strategies, whereas our study maintained methodological consistency with one surgical team. Currently, there is no consensus on the incidence of pneumonia and PPCs with CO2 artificial pneumothorax. Li et al. compared early postoperative PPCs between TLV with CO2 artificial pneumothorax and OLV with bronchial blockade in patients undergoing minimally invasive esophagectomy and found no significant difference in early PPCs after MIE [34]. As it was also a retrospective study and the surgical confounders were not included, randomized clinical trials are warranted to provide stronger evidence.

Anastomotic leak is another serious complication after esophagectomy. Giulini et al. suggested that anastomotic leak and pneumonia were equally detrimental complications after esophagectomy, representing the most critical complications after the surgery [35]. There is no doubt that the most challenging aspect of thoracoscopic practice for esophagectomy is the reconstruction with the anastomosis. Some studies have reported the incidence of anastomotic leak after esophagectomy [36, 37]. Patton and colleagues found the lowest anastomotic leak in open esophagectomy [13]. However, Axtell et al. investigated the potential causes for anastomotic leak and found no direct association between MIE and an increased risk of anastomotic leak [38]. We found a higher incidence of anastomotic leak in the TLV group without statistical significance, which might result from the higher percentage of MIE in the group. However, the incidence of anastomotic leak was similar between the TLV and OLV cohorts in the analysis, in which only non-open surgery was included.

There is no consensus for the ventilation strategy for the thoracic procedure during esophagectomy. Protective ventilation strategies are suggested in the statements from the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons or the Society of Thoracic Surgeons. It is suggested that applying continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) to the non-ventilated lung during surgery prevents its complete collapse and has been shown to reduce the local inflammatory response [39]. It is similar in our TLV during esophagectomy in our institution and reduced local inflammatory response, which might lead to a reduction in the incidence of PPCs.

There are several limitations to the study. First, it was a single-center retrospective study, which inherently limits the generalizability of the findings. Although multivariate regression was used for adjustment, the potential influence of unmeasured confounding factors cannot be fully ruled out and should be referenced cautiously in a real scenario. The underpowered comparisons might fail to detect smaller but clinically relevant differences. Higher-level evidence calls for a larger sample of randomized clinical trials. Second, the air leak was not analyzed in our study. Theoretically, it is indeed a significant clinical concern. In our center, it is standard practice to disconnect the ventilator to maintain lung stillness during chest entry, followed immediately by single-lung ventilation or protective lung ventilation after entry. After chest tube placement, routine lung inflation is performed to check for air leaks. Unless patients have specific conditions such as abnormal adhesions, pulmonary injuries are rare in our center. Third, subgroup analysis indicated that the use of bronchial blockers was associated with a higher incidence of PPCs. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size.

Conclusion

To conclude, our findings suggest that the CO2 artificial pneumothorax during thoracoscopic esophagectomy using a single-lumen tube is safe and feasible. Compared with one-lung ventilation using a double-lumen tube or bronchial blockers, it was associated with a lower incidence of PPCs. No significant difference was observed in the incidence of anastomotic leak. However, the results need to be interpreted with caution, and prospective RCTs are warranted. These findings highlight the potential benefits of TLV with CO₂ insufflation in improving perioperative outcomes.
