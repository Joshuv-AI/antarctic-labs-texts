Development and validation of a prediction model for in-hospital mortality in patients with intra-abdominal sepsis: a dual-database study using MIMIC-IV and eICU databases
Authors: Jianjun Zhang, Yuhong Chen, Cong-cong Zhao, Jinrong Wang, Zhen-jie Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo develop and validate a predictive model for assessing in-hospital mortality in patients with intra-abdominal sepsis (IAS), a leading cause of sepsis.DesignSecondary analysis of two retrospective critical care databases.SettingData extracted from the Intensive Care Medicine Information Marketplace IV (MIMIC-IV) and the eICU Collaborative Research Database.ParticipantsPatients with IAS from MIMIC-IV (2008–2019; 1300 patients, 264 deaths) for model training and internal validation, and eICU (2014–2015; 149 patients, 33 deaths) for external validation.InterventionsClinical data were used for constructing a predictive model. Introduction

Sepsis remains a major global health challenge, characterised by dysregulated host response to infection and leading to life-threatening organ dysfunction. It is a leading cause of mortality in intensive care units (ICUs), particularly when associated with intra-abdominal infection (IAI), which accounts for a substantial proportion of sepsis cases.1 2 IAI is notably the most common cause of non-traumatic deaths in emergency surgical settings, with mortality rates ranging from 20% to 29%.3 4 intra-abdominal sepsis (IAS), sepsis originating from IAI, poses significant clinical challenges due to its heterogeneous aetiology, polymicrobial nature and rapid progression to multi-organ failure.5 6

Early source control and appropriate antimicrobial therapy are cornerstone management strategies for IAS.7 8 Delays in intervention are associated with increased mortality, underscoring the need for timely risk stratification.9 10 Although general prognostic scores such as Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, quick SOFA, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE II) score, and Predisposition, Insult/Infection, Response, and Organ dysfunction score are widely used in sepsis management, they are not specifically designed for IAS and may lack precision in this subpopulation.11,15 Several disease-specific models have been proposed, but many are limited by small sample sizes, lack of external validation or narrow focus on specific patient subgroups (eg, post-surgical or elderly patients).16,19 Consequently, there is an unmet need for a broadly applicable and validated prediction model for in-hospital mortality in patients with IAS.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate a pragmatic prediction model for in-hospital mortality among patients with IAS, using large-scale, multicentre data from the Intensive Care Medicine Information Marketplace IV (MIMIC-IV) and eICU databases.

Methods

Data source

The data for this study were extracted from MIMIC-IV (V.2.2) and eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD; V.2.0). MIMIC IV is a relational database containing patient data from the ICU at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts, USA, spanning from 2008 to 2019. This dataset was used for training and internal validation, with patients randomly divided into a training set (n=910, 70%) and an internal validation set (n=390, 30%) using a 7:3 ratio. The eICU-CRD is a multicentre database comprising more than 200,000 ICU admissions across 208 hospitals in the USA. It includes data from over 139,000 individual patients admitted between 2014 and 2015. This database served as an independent external validation set (n=149). Access to the databases was granted after completing the required ‘Protecting Human Research Participants’ training (certification ID: 58618095). The establishment of both the MIMIC-IV and eICU databases was approved by the institutional review boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Centre, with a waiver of informed consent due to the use of de-identified patient data. Our secondary analysis of these de-identified data did not require additional ethical approval from our institutional review board, as per the policy of the database providers and the guidelines of our institution.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. This is a secondary analysis of de-identified electronic health record data.

Study participants and outcomes

The study population was identified using a set of diagnostic codes for abdominal infections, which were adapted from Li et al.20 Since the MIMIC-IV database encompasses both ICD-9 and ICD-10 coding systems, we included patients bearing either the original ICD-9 codes from the prior study or their clinically equivalent ICD-10 counterparts. This mapping was performed based on clinical relevance (eg, ICD-9 code 5400 for acute appendicitis with generalised peritonitis was mapped to ICD-10 code K352). Sepsis was then defined according to the sepsis-3.0,21 which characterises sepsis as an infection accompanied by organ dysfunction, indicated by an increase of ≥2 points in the SOFA score.22 For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only data from the first admission was retained. Patients aged <18 years were excluded. In-hospital mortality was used as the outcome measure.

Variable extraction

Based on relevant references and clinical records, structured query language (SQL) in PostgreSQL (V.9.5) was used to extract the following data. Demographic characteristics: sex, age and ethnicity. Because there were many missing values of height, height and weight were excluded from body mass index calculations. Comorbidities: myocardial infarction (MI), congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, peptic ulcer disease, diabetes, renal disease, malignant cancer, metastatic solid tumour and Charlson Comorbidity Index. Based on relevant references and clinical records, SQL in PostgreSQL was used to extract the following data. All clinical variables were extracted from the first 24 hours following ICU admission. For each variable, the most abnormal value was selected to reflect the peak severity of illness (eg, the maximum value for lactate and creatinine, the minimum value for platelet count and mean arterial pressure (MAP)). Vital signs on day 1 of ICU admission: heart rate, MAP, respiratory rate, peak temperature, Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), Simplified APS II (SAPS II) and SOFA score. Laboratory test results on day 1 of ICU admission: white blood cell count, haemoglobin, platelets, sodium, potassium, calcium, blood glucose, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, total bilirubin (TBIL), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), pH, lactate, oxygen partial pressure (PO2), base excess and albumin. The ascitic fluid culture during the first ICU admission was considered bacteriological evidence. If the ascites culture was negative but the blood culture was positive for intestinal flora, it was classified as positive. The main surgical procedure during the first ICU admission was the source control procedure.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using RV.4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Free Statistics V. 1.9. Variables with more than 50% missing values were excluded. For the remaining variables, the missing rate was less than 30% per variable. Multiple imputation was performed using the mice package in R, with five replications and a chained equations approach. The first imputed dataset was used for all subsequent analyses. The same imputation procedure was applied separately to the training and external validation sets.

Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as means±SD and non-normally distributed continuous variables as medians with IQR. The categorical variables were represented as n (%) in each category. For continuous variables, the t-test was used to compare variables with a normal distribution, while the Mann-Whitney U-test was used to compare continuous variables with a non-normal distribution. The χ2 test with Yates' correction or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare proportions. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation was used to select potential prognostic factors. The optimal value of the tuning parameter (λ) was determined using the ‘one standard error (1-SE) rule’, which selects the most parsimonious model whose cross-validation error is within 1-SE of the minimum. This approach enhances model simplicity and generalisability. Variables with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ were retained for further modelling. A multivariable logistic regression model was then built using all potential prognostic factors, and the model was visualised using a nomogram. Model discrimination was evaluated using the C-index and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The DeLong test was used to statistically compare the AUCs of different models. The optimal probability cut-off value for the prediction model was determined by maximising the Youden Index (J=sensitivity+specificity−1), which identifies the threshold that best balances the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. Model calibration was evaluated using Brier scores and calibration plots. Model calibration was evaluated using Brier scores and calibration plots. The decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to assess the clinical applicability of the model, and the clinical impact curves (CIC) was used to further evaluate its usefulness. Internal validation was conducted using an enhanced bootstrap procedure. Specifically, regression models were fitted to 500 bootstrap replicates sampled with replacement from the training set. These replicates were used to compute the optimism-adjusted C-index and to derive bootstrap confidence intervals for the model performance metrics. For calibration plots, bias-corrected calibration curves were generated based on 200 bootstrap samples. To enhance the generalisability of the model, external validation was conducted using an independent dataset sourced from the eICU database.

Results

Model development and internal validation

The baseline characteristics of the 1300 patients from MIMIC-IV used for model development and internal validation are described in the Methods section (table 1). The potential prognostic factors were determined by LASSO regression analysis. When the optimal λ value was 0.031, lactate, age, APTT, BUN, Charlson Comorbidity Index, APSIII score, SAPS II score, TBIL and platelets were identified as predictors of in-hospital death (figure 2). Since clinical scores involve multiple clinical indicators, two models were developed: one including clinical scores (Model 1) and one excluding them (Model 2). There was a significant difference in the AUC of the two models (p<0.001; figure 3A). Model 2 was selected because it contained fewer clinical indicators, making it easier and quicker to use. Finally, six variables (lactate, age, APTT, BUN, TBIL and platelets) were included in the model and used to construct the nomogram (figure 3B).

The nomogram was assessed using the AUC, C-index and calibration curve. The AUC for predicting in-hospital mortality was 0.795 (95% CI 0.758 to 0.831; figure 4A). The difference in AUC between Model 1 (which included clinical scores) and Model 2 was statistically significant (p<0.001 by DeLong’s test). Despite the higher AUC of Model 1 (0.825, 95% CI 0.789 to 0.860), we selected the more parsimonious Model 2 for clinical practicality, as its performance remained excellent. At the optimal cut-off value determined by the Youden index (0.435), the sensitivity was 0.76 and the specificity was 0.67. This threshold value prioritises sensitivity to identify the majority of patients at high risk of mortality, which is crucial for a prognostic tool in critical care settings. The positive predictive value was 0.395, indicating that among those predicted to die, approximately 39.5% did not survive. The negative predictive value was 0.917, suggesting that among those predicted to survive, 91.7% did indeed survive. The false positive and false negative rates were 30.8% and 23.7%, respectively. These metrics further support the use of the model in risk stratification, particularly in ruling out low-risk patients. Detailed values are provided in online supplemental table S2. The C-index of the model was 0.795, indicating good discriminative capacity. Bootstrap validation was performed, yielding a corrected C-index of 0.767. The model showed excellent calibration, with a Brier score of 0.133 (figure 4B). The Brier score quantifies the mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (1 or 0 for death or survival), ranging from 0 for perfect prediction to 0.25 for a non-informative model that predicts a 50% event rate for all patients.23 24 A score of 0.133 indicates good predictive performance, substantially better than a non-informative model. Furthermore, the clinical usefulness of the nomogram was rigorously assessed using DCA and CIC (figure 4C–D). The DCA (figure 4C) demonstrates the net benefit of using the model to guide clinical decisions. The curve for our prediction nomogram (blue line) shows a consistently higher net benefit than the ‘treat all’ (grey line) and ‘treat none’ (black line) strategies across a wide range of threshold probabilities (from 4% to 99%). This indicates that basing decisions on the predictions of the model is clinically advantageous regardless of whether a clinician’s personal threshold for action is very low (favouring sensitivity) or very high (favouring specificity). The CIC (figure 4D) illustrates the potential clinical impact of using the model at 20% risk threshold. The red curve represents the number of patients per 100 who would be classified as ‘high-risk’ by the model. The blue curve shows the actual number of deaths (true positives) among those classified as high-risk. The close alignment between these curves indicates that the model efficiently identifies patients who are truly at high risk of mortality without overwhelming the system with false positives, thereby supporting its use for rational resource allocation in critical care.

External validation

In the external validation set, the nomogram maintained excellent performance. It demonstrated strong discriminative ability (figure 5A; AUC 0.846, 95% CI 0.772 to 0.919), good calibration between predicted and observed risk (figure 5B; Brier score 0.126), and compelling clinical outcomes. The DCA (figure 5C) indicated that the model provides a positive net benefit across a wide range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities. Furthermore, the CIC (figure 5D) demonstrated the effectiveness of the model in identifying high-risk patients.

Comparison with SOFA score

To further evaluate the clinical usefulness of our model, we compared its performance against the SOFA score, a widely used prognostic tool in sepsis. In the training cohort (MIMIC-IV, n=1300), our model demonstrated significantly superior discriminative ability (AUC 0.795, 95% CI 0.758 to 0.831) compared with the SOFA score (AUC 0.622, 95% CI 0.576 to 0.668) for predicting in-hospital mortality (online supplemental figure S2).

Discussion

IAS persists as a formidable clinical challenge in critical care practice, and existing generic sepsis prognostic tools (eg, SOFA and APACHE II) exhibit insufficient precision for this distinct subgroup. This dual-database study developed and validated a nomogram for predicting in-hospital mortality in IAS patients, incorporating six readily accessible parameters: lactate, age, APTT, BUN, TBIL and platelets. The model demonstrated robust performance (training cohort: AUC 0.795, 95% CI 0.758 to 0.831; external validation cohort: AUC 0.846, 95% CI 0.772 to 0.919) and favourable clinical outcomes, effectively addressing the unmet need for an IAS-specific risk stratification tool.

IAI is characterised by heterogeneous aetiology and polymicrobial features, and the high culture-negative rate and mixed infection rate of our cohort are consistent with the diagnostic challenges of abdominal infections.25 26 This underscores the advantage of our model, which focuses on host response rather than specific microbiological aetiology—avoiding limitations from unclear pathogen identification.

A key strength of our model is its parsimony: compared with complex scoring systems (eg, APACHE II), the six selected variables are routinely collected within hours of ICU admission, enabling rapid bedside assessment without delaying clinical decisions. The pathophysiological significance of these variables is compelling: Lactate reflects tissue hypoperfusion (a core feature of sepsis shock), age represents physiological reserve, APTT and platelets indicate coagulopathy and immunothrombosis, and BUN/TBIL reflect renal/hepatic dysfunction—collectively capturing the multi-organ derangement of severe IAS.

Compared with other studies, our model identified a parsimonious set of predictors reflective of organ dysfunction and physiological reserve. Pan et al27 developed a prognostic model with an AUC of 0.842, yet it depends on the APACHE II score—a tool that demands comprehensive data input and is not universally accessible at the bedside. In contrast, our model relies solely on routine laboratory and clinical parameters, facilitating timely risk assessment. Arvaniti et al28 emphasised age and illness severity as key predictors of IAS outcomes, which aligns with our inclusion of age as a core variable. Park et al.26 confirmed coagulation dysfunction as an independent predictor of mortality in IAI-related sepsis, supporting our integration of APTT and platelets. Ahmed et al11constructed a model for complicated IAI (optimism-adjusted c-statistic: 0.79) but included variables such as source control type—information not always available at initial patient assessment. The model of Xue et al5 (AUC 0.778) depended on the APACHE II score, limiting its practicality in resource-constrained settings. Li et al20 developed a high-precision nomogram (AUC 0.875) for older IAS patients, which integrated CT-derived skeletal muscle mass and geriatric syndromes; however, this requires specialised imaging and assessments. Our model, while not incorporating these factors, uses age as a proxy for physiological reserve and still achieves strong performance, rendering it more widely applicable across clinical settings. Tartaglia et al19highlighted baseline physiological reserve and frailty as key predictors in IAS patients requiring open abdomen management, which echoes our focus on integrating acute physiology (eg, lactate) and baseline patient status (age).

Notably, the proposed model outperforms the SOFA score—a cornerstone tool for evaluating organ dysfunction in sepsis.11 22 In the training cohort, the AUC (0.795, 95% CI 0.758 to 0.831) of our model was significantly higher than that of the SOFA score (0.622, 95% CI 0.576 to 0.668; DeLong’s test p<0.001). This superiority arises from two key factors: the SOFA score primarily quantifies acute organ dysfunction, whereas our model integrates age (to reflect long-term physiological reserve) and coagulation parameters (to capture immunothrombosis—a pathological process pivotal to abdominal sepsis).29,31 This holistic assessment makes our tool more specific to IAS, where both acute insult and baseline vulnerability drive patient outcomes. The clinical usefulness of the model is further supported by DCA, which demonstrated consistently higher net benefit than ‘treat all’ or ‘treat none’ strategies, and CIC, which confirmed efficient identification of true high-risk patients—validating its value for resource allocation and treatment decision-making in critical care.

This study has some limitations. First, although the MIMIC-IV cohort (n=1300) is sizeable, it is derived from a single centre; while external validation in the eICU cohort (n=149) showed good performance, the smaller sample size (and fewer outcome events, n=33) may limit the stability of performance estimates. Second, owing to the retrospective secondary analysis design, we were unable to extract data on the time interval from IAS diagnosis to infection source control; most patients without source control had pancreatitis, which may introduce selection bias.21 Fourth, case identification relied on ICD code mapping (adapted from Li et al20), which carries inherent misclassification risk associated with administrative coding. Fifth, patients aged <18 years were excluded, limiting the applicability to paediatric populations. Future prospective studies should incorporate multicentre data, source control timing, pathogen antimicrobial susceptibility profiles and paediatric patients to further optimise the model.

Conclusion

Based on the data from the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD databases, this study developed a prediction model for mortality in patients with IAI and sepsis. Lactate, age, APTT, BUN, TBIL and platelets are independent risk factors for in-hospital mortality in patients with IAS. The prediction scoring system composed of these factors could reliably predict the risk of in-hospital mortality in patients with IAS and provide a theoretical reference for clinicians to formulate individualised treatment strategies. However, external validation of this prediction scoring system is needed in multicentre studies to further evaluate its predictive performance.
