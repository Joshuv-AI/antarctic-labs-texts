Peculiarities of vaginal microbiota in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Xuan Qiu, Man Zhang, Lei Zhang, Hao Chen, Mujie Gao, Wenxiao Li, Zheng Yu, Zhiyong Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe changes in the vaginal microbiota and potential dysbiosis adjustment strategies in diabetic patients remain inconclusive. This study was designed to investigate the impact of Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) on the ecological dynamics of the vaginal microbiota in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women, with a focus on microbial community structure and functional homeostasis.MethodsVaginal secretion samples from 22 T2DM patients (DM group) and 23 healthy controls (CT group) under perimenopausal and postmenopausal conditions were analyzed via metagenomic sequencing. Alpha diversity (Observe, ACE, Shannon-Weaver, Gini-Simpson indices) and beta diversity (PCoA, NMDS) were assessed. Taxonomic profiling, LEfSe analysis, and co-occurrence network construction were performed to identify differential species and microbial interactions. Neutral community modeling evaluated stochastic vs. deterministic assembly processes.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in age (62.22 ± 5.74 vs. 58.23 ± 7.55, p = 0.052) or perimenopausal/ postmenopausal status (3/19 vs. 5/18, p = 0.748) between the DM and CT groups. The DM group exhibited significantly higher alpha diversity (p < 0.05) and distinct beta diversity clustering (p < 0.05), marked by reduced Lactobacillus relative abundance (28.7% in CT vs. 6.3% in DM) and increased abundance of opportunistic pathogenic genera (Klebsiella, Gardnerella, Staphylococcus). LEfSe identified Firmicutes as CT biomarkers, while the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes and Prevotella increased in DM group. Both fasting blood glucose and HbA1c levels significantly influenced the relative abundance of vaginal Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus gasseri, and Lactobacillus iners, showing a significant negative correlation. Co-occurrence networks revealed greater complexity and more integrated in the DM group (more triangles, lower modularity, higher node degrees, higher clustering coefficients, p < 0.0001). Neutral modeling indicated stochastic assembly (R² >0.5), with Lactobacillus species and opportunistic pathogens deviating from neutral predictions in DM.ConclusionUnder perimenopausal and postmenopausal conditions, T2DM disrupts vaginal microbiota homeostasis by diminishing protective Lactobacillus populations and promoting pathogen proliferation.Keywords: Diabetes, Vaginal microbiota, Perimenopausal, Postmenopausal, Metagenomic sequencing, Lactobacillus, Homeostasis

Background

Diabetes is a widespread metabolic disorder that affects various systems, including the cardiovascular, nervous, renal, and immune systems. In recent years, growing evidence has highlighted significant alterations in the microbiota of diabetic patients, including changes observed in both gut and vaginal microbiota [1, 2]. These shifts may be closely associated with the pathophysiological processes of diabetes and the development of its complications [2, 3]. Moreover, the severity of diabetes and its complications is associated with the degree of gut microbiota dysbiosis [1, 3]. Interventions targeting the gut microbiota may serve as a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of diabetes and its complications [1, 3].

The vaginal microbiota is a key element in maintaining the health of the female reproductive system, with Lactobacillus being the predominant genus [4]. Lactobacillus adheres to the vaginal epithelium and maintains a low pH environment by producing bacteriocins, hydrogen peroxide, and other metabolites, thereby inhibiting the growth of pathogenic microorganisms [5]. In women of reproductive age, the vaginal microbiome is typically dominated by one or more species of Lactobacillus, such as Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus paragasseri, and Lactobacillus iners [6]. These bacteria help maintain an acidic environment in the vagina by producing substances such as lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide, thereby inhibiting the growth of harmful microorganisms [7, 8]. Changes in the vaginal microbiota, such as a reduction in Lactobacillus abundance and an increase in facultative and anaerobic bacterial populations, may lead to bacterial vaginitis [9]. In menopausal and postmenopausal women, the vaginal microbiota undergoes ecological shifts, which are largely driven by declining estrogen levels, transitioning from a Lactobacillus-dominated community to a more diverse state [10]. In diabetic individuals, factors such as hyperglycemia, impaired glycogen availability, and use of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors can also disrupt the vaginal microbiota [2, 11], increasing the risk of infections like bacterial vaginosis and candidiasis [12, 13]. The alterations in the vaginal microbiota of diabetic patients may involve several key factors: (1) The hyperglycemic environment may suppress the growth of Lactobacillus, leading to an elevated vaginal pH and disrupting the microbial balance within the vagina [2]. (2) Dysbiosis can trigger local inflammation, further impairing immune function in diabetic patients [2, 4]. (3) Alterations in microbiota-derived metabolites in the female reproductive system, such as increased short-chain fatty acids associated with polymicrobial bacterial vaginosis may influence host metabolic and immune responses [14].

However, most current researches on the vaginal microbiota in diabetes focuses on the mechanisms and treatments of vaginal microbiota dysbiosis in women with gestational diabetes [15, 16]. Although several studies have investigated alterations in vaginal microbiota in general diabetic populations, most rely on microbiologic culture rather than metagenomic approaches for microbial characterization, and many conclusions are based on subgroup analyses [17, 18]. As a result, the existing evidence remains inconclusive—particularly regarding the impact of diabetes on vaginal microbiota in Chinese populations. Investigating the changes in the vaginal microbiota of diabetic patients not only aids in uncovering the potential connection between diabetes and female reproductive health, but also provides a theoretical foundation for developing microbiota-based intervention strategies, such as probiotics, prebiotics, or vaginal microbiota transplantation. These strategies could help improve the quality of life for diabetic patients and reduce their risk of vaginal infections.

Materials and methods

Study subjects and clinical data

The participants in this study were recruited from the Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the Ethics Committee of the local hospital (Approved number: KE2024-075-1, Date: July 15, 2024). The study included 22 patients with Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (DM group) and 23 healthy individuals (CT group). Each participant was individually informed about the nature, risks, and benefits of the study, completed a questionnaire, and signed an informed consent form. Additionally, complete clinical and pathological data were obtained from each participant. Inclusion criteria: (1) Female, aged between 18 and 80 years; (2) Diagnosed with T2DM based on the 1999 World Health Organization diagnostic criteria (for the DM group); (3) Resident of Hebei Province for more than 6 months (4) Perimenopausal or postmenopausal status [19]. To minimize potential confounding factors and ensure that the samples were not influenced by other variables, the exclusion criteria for participants were as follows: (1) History of infectious diseases within the past month prior to admission; (2) History of travel to other provinces within the past month prior to admission; (3) Use of probiotics, antibiotics, corticosteroids, or immunosuppressants within the past month prior to admission; (4) History of chronic inflammatory or autoimmune diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, etc.); (5) History of severe liver, kidney, or heart diseases (including heart failure and acute myocardial infarction), hematologic disorders, or malignant tumors; (6) Sexual intercourse, vaginal douching, or use of vaginal cleansing products within 48 h prior to sampling; (7) Pregnant or breastfeeding; (8) Current or recent (within the past month) use of SGLT2 inhibitors; (9) Use of hormone replacement therapy; (10) Unable to cooperate with the collection of vaginal swabs. The patient’s name, gender, age, body mass index (BMI), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels were retrieved and documented from their medical records.

Collection of vaginal secretion samples

Vaginal secretion samples were collected using sterile swabs. The participants were positioned on the examination table, and a speculum was inserted to allow for cervical observation. The sterile swab was carefully inserted into the vagina and rotated to ensure adequate secretion was obtained from the vaginal walls. The swab was then removed and placed into a 2 mL sterile tube (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) without any transport medium, which was transported to the laboratory. All samples were stored at -80 °C until sent for metagenomic sequencing. Strict precautions were taken during sample collection and handling to prevent contamination and preserve sample integrity.

Metagenomic sequencing of the vaginal microbiota, quality control, and species annotation

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from vaginal secretion samples using the E.Z.N.A. Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA). The bacterial DNA was eluted in elution buffer and stored at -20 °C. DNA concentration and purity were determined using a full-wavelength UV-Vis spectrophotometer (200–800 nm scan, triplicate readings); OD260, OD280 and OD230 were recorded, and DNA yield was calculated from OD260. Purity criteria were OD260/280 1.8–2.0 and OD260/230 ≥ 1.5, verified by 0.8% agarose-gel electrophoresis of both genomic DNA and PCR products to confirm integrity. Based on metagenomic library requirements (≥ 6 ng/µL, ≥ 600 ng total), only samples meeting both concentration ≥ 6 ng/µL and purity thresholds were advanced to library construction. PCR products were purified using the QIAquick 8 PCR Purification Kit, and electrophoresis products were recovered using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit. Metagenomic sequencing of all samples was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 PE250 platform (Shanghai Biozeron Biotech. Co., Ltd, China) with a sequencing depth of 10 GB per sample, and Illumina paired-end libraries were constructed. Raw data were subjected to initial quality control using Trimmomatic software, filtering out adapter sequences, N bases, and other low-quality data [20].

To ensure data accuracy, further quality control was performed on the raw sequences obtained from sequencing. This mainly involved the removal of contamination and human host sequences. First, Fastp (version 0.23.2) was used to trim low-quality reads, specifically those with an average quality score < 15 and a length < 15 bases [21]. FastUniq (version 1.1.0) was used to remove duplicate sequences, specifically addressing PCR duplicates or redundant sequences generated during PCR amplification in DNA sequencing data. Bowtie2 (version 2.5.1) was used to align the sequences with the human genome database (hg38), and human-derived sequences were filtered out [22]. After completing the quality control process, Kraken2 (version 2.1.2) was used with a standard database (kraken2_standard) to annotate the species of the genetic sequences, which resulted in the assignment of unique taxonomic identifiers (taxids) to each gene [23]. Subsequently, the names and taxonomic information of these taxids were translated into corresponding species information, from phylum to species. Only reads assigned to bacteria were retained for downstream analysis, as fungal and viral reads accounted for less than 0.1% of the classified sequences in most vaginal samples.

Data analysis and visualization

Data and visualization analyses in this study were conducted using R software (version 4.2.3). Microbial community composition analysis was performed using the microeco package (version 1.1.0) [24]. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney U or Wilcoxon test for non-normally distributed data. Comparisons of categorical variables were conducted using the Chi-square test with Continuity Correction. The relative abundance differences of microorganisms or functional genes in multiple groups were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with visualization conducted using ggboxplot from the ggpubr package. A p less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The MicrobiotaProcess package (version 1.12.4) was used to calculate microbial alpha diversity [25]. Alpha diversity, which assesses the diversity and evenness of microbial communities in a given habitat, was quantified using indices including the observed species index, ACE index, Shannon-Weaver diversity index, and Gini-Simpson index. The Bray-Curtis distance-based “vegdist” function was used for principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) to determine the beta diversity of microbial communities across different groups. ANOSIM was employed to test the significance of differences between two groups [26]. For non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS), the metaMDS function in the vegan package was used for ordination analysis [27]. Volcano plots were generated using the DESeq2 package to calculate and highlight species with differential abundance between two groups. The DESeqDataSetFromMatrix function was applied to obtain differential expression matrices and p matrices for different groups, with species showing a fold change greater than 2 and a p less than 0.05 considered differentially abundant species [28]. Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was used to identify species with significant abundance differences between groups (i.e., biomarkers), with a threshold set at p < 0.05 and LDA ≥ 3 [29]. A co-occurrence network was constructed based on the Spearman correlation matrix, with multiple p corrections performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Spearman correlation coefficients and adjusted p were set at 0.6 and 0.05, respectively, and visualization of the co-occurrence network was carried out in Gephi software. The neutral community model was used to simulate the assembly patterns of communities in different groups. The closer the R² value is to 1, the better the fit of the model. Points within the prediction interval represent communities that conform to the neutral community model [30].

In this study, the differences in microbial diversity between vaginal secretion samples from DM group and CT group were analyzed using metagenomic sequencing. For the calculated alpha diversity indices, the DM group exhibited significantly higher observe (p < 0.05), ACE (p < 0.01), Shannon-Weaver (p < 0.01) and Gini-Simpson (p < 0.05) indices compared to the CT group, suggesting that diabetes may increase the total microbial count, species richness, and diversity of the vaginal microbiota in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women (Fig. 1a). Subsequently, beta diversity between the two groups was compared using PCoA and NMDS. PCoA analysis revealed a clear separation in microbial community structure between the DM and CT groups (Fig. 1b), suggesting that diabetes may alter the composition of the vaginal microbiota in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women. NMDS analysis further confirmed this separation, showing significant differences in community composition (Stress = 0.131) (Fig. 1c), supporting the idea that diabetes may lead to changes in vaginal microbial community composition of perimenopausal and postmenopausal women. Lastly, a rank-sum test of Bray-Curtis distances was performed to compare the microbial Bray-Curtis distance rankings between the two groups (Fig. 1d). The results showed lower distance rankings in the CT group, indicating higher homogeneity of its vaginal microbiota, while the DM group displayed greater compositional variation. Significant differences in microbial community structure were found between the two groups (p = 0.004), further emphasizing the impact of diabetes on the vaginal microbiota composition. Therefore, diabetes seems to exert a significant impact on the composition, diversity, and evenness of the vaginal microbiota in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women, with notable alterations in community structure observed between the DM and CT groups.

The microbial community composition of the vaginal microbiota was compared between the DM and CT groups to explore the potential impact of diabetes on the structure of the vaginal microbiota in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women. At the phylum level, the results revealed that Proteobacteria and Firmicutes were the predominant phyla in both groups, with a significantly higher relative abundance of Proteobacteria in the DM group, while Firmicutes were more prevalent in the CT group (Fig. 2a). Additionally, a pie chart illustrating the microbial community composition at the genus level was presented (Fig. 2b). In the CT group, the genus Lactobacillus exhibited the highest relative abundance (28.7%), whereas in the DM group, the dominance of Lactobacillus was notably reduced, replaced by Klebsiella, which had the highest relative abundance at 15.5%, followed by Gardnerella (11.4%) and Staphylococcus (10%). Further analysis through stacked bar charts depicted the changes in the relative abundance of microbial genera in samples from both the DM and CT groups. It was observed that Klebsiella and Staphylococcus exhibited dominant relative abundances in almost all samples from the DM group, whereas Lactobacillus maintained a relatively high abundance in most samples from the CT group. These results suggest that diabetes may significantly influence the structure of the vaginal microbiota in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women, particularly at the genus level. Specifically, the abundance of Lactobacillus was suppressed in the DM group, while the relative abundances of potential pathogens such as Klebsiella, Gardnerella, and Staphylococcus were increased. These alterations in the microbial community may be closely related to the changes in the vaginal microenvironment induced by diabetes. We focused on the effects of FBG levels and HbA1c in diabetic patients on the top 20 most abundant bacterial species. The results revealed that both FBG and HbA1c levels significantly influenced the relative abundance of vaginal Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus gasseri, and Lactobacillus iners, showing a significant negative correlation. Specifically, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus crispatus significantly decreased with elevated FBG levels. Furthermore, as HbA1c levels increased, the relative abundances of all three Lactobacillus species exhibited significant reductions.

The results of the phylogenetic branch analysis revealed a significant increase in the relative abundance of Firmicutes and Bacilli in the CT group, while the abundance of other microorganisms was significantly higher in the DM group (Fig. 3a). The histogram of Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) scores revealed differential biomarkers between the two groups (LDA > 3, p < 0.05), which was consistent with the findings from the phylogenetic branch analysis. At the phylum level, the relative abundance of Firmicutes was significantly higher in the CT group, whereas Bacteroidetes showed a significant increase in the DM group (Fig. 3b). Additionally, the abundance of Prevotellaceae and Prevotella was significantly higher in the vaginal microbiota of DM compared to CT. The volcano plot further illustrated the differential microbiota at the species level. The top 14 species with the highest differential scores were labeled, indicating that three lactobacilli species—Limosilactobacillus reuteri, Lactobacillus paragasseri, and Lactobacillus crispatus showed a significant increase in abundance in the CT group, while Streptomyces lividans exhibited a significantly higher relative abundance in the DM group compared to the CT group (Fig. 3c). Random forest analysis identified the key differential biomarkers between the two groups. Among the top 100 species by abundance, 15 species that contributed the most to the inter-group differences were selected. Lactobacillus iners was identified as a core species contributing to the differences between the two groups, with lower abundance in the DM group than in the CT group. Meanwhile, the relative abundance of the other 14 species was higher in the DM group (Fig. 3d).

To reveal the relationships between microorganisms, the top 200 species by relative abundance were selected, and the vaginal microbiota networks of the DM and CT groups were analyzed (Fig. 4a). The results showed that the microbial network in the DM group was more complex. Significant differences were observed between the microbial networks of the DM and CT groups in several characteristics. Specifically, the number of triangles (representing mutual connections among three nodes) in the network of the DM group was significantly higher than that in the CT group (Fig. 4b), indicating that more complex interactions likely exist among microorganisms in the DM group. Furthermore, the modularity of the DM group’s network was slightly lower than that of the CT group (Fig. 4b), suggesting that the microbial network in the DM group is more integrated, with less distinct boundaries between modules. In terms of network node coefficients, the degree of nodes (i.e., the number of connections per node) was significantly higher in the DM group (Fig. 4b), indicating more frequent microbial interactions in this group. Additionally, the clustering coefficient of the DM group was higher than that of the CT group (Fig. 4b), emphasizing the more tightly connected network structure among microorganisms in the DM group. Subsequently, the core species maintaining the stability of the vaginal microbial network in both groups were identified. Microorganisms in both the DM and CT groups were classified into peripheral microorganisms and connector microorganisms. Connector microorganisms typically play a crucial role in maintaining the structure and function of the microbial network. In the DM group, specific microorganisms such as Lactobacillus gasseri, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella oxytoca played significant roles in the microbial network, whereas in the CT group, Lactobacillus gasseri and Enterococcus faecalis were key players (Fig. 4c). Changes in the abundance of species classified as connectors can lead to substantial disruptions to the vaginal microbiota. Elucidating the impact of diabetes on the vaginal microbial network structure and identifying core species under different conditions will help further explore how specific microorganisms influence vaginal health and the development of diseases.

To further investigate the driving factors behind the changes in vaginal microbiota, the Sloan neutral community model was employed to analyze the distribution characteristics of vaginal microbiota in different DM and CT groups, in terms of stochastic and directional processes. Microbial taxa outside the predicted range (represented by red and green points) indicate that their assembly follows a directional process, whereas those within the range follow a stochastic process. The neutral community model analysis for both DM and CT groups revealed that the R² values for both groups were greater than 0.5, indicating that stochastic processes play a significant role in shaping the vaginal microbiota structure (Fig. 5a). Among the 1226 species identified, 1144 species in the DM group exhibited random distribution, with 49 species below the predicted interval (DM low) (Fig. 5b). These species, characterized by relatively high abundance, primarily included Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Pasteurella multocida, Staphylococcus haemolyticus, and Ralstonia pickettii. 33 species exhibited relative abundance above the predicted interval (DM high), including species of the Prevotella and Lactobacillus, such as Lactobacillus gasseri, Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus jensenii, and Lactobacillus iners. In the CT group, 1169 species showed random distribution, with 39 species below the predicted interval (CT low), mainly including Staphylococcus, Bradyrhizobium, and Ralstonia, while 18 species were above the predicted interval (CT high) (Fig. 5b), including four lactobacilli: Lactobacillus iners, Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus gasseri, and Limosilactobacillus vaginalis. Additionally, Gardnerella vaginalis was also positioned above the predicted interval. Notably, Pseudomonas aeruginosa exhibited directional regulation in both healthy individuals and diabetic patients, with its relative abundance surpassing the predicted interval in the neutral community model (Fig. 5c). These species play unique roles or functions within the vaginal microbiota, particularly Lactobacillus species, which may exhibit specific responses to diabetic conditions.

Discussion

This study revealed that diabetes may exacerbate vaginal microbiota dysbiosis under perimenopausal and postmenopausal conditions. Compared to CT group, the DM group showed changes in microbiota diversity, composition, community differences, and microbial interactions. These changes were likely to disrupt the vaginal environment, aggravate the development and progression of vaginal diseases, and pose a risk to the reproductive health of diabetic patients in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women.

The human vaginal microbiota is a key determinant of vaginal health [31]. In gut health, greater microbial diversity typically signifies a healthier condition. However, in the vaginal microbiome, lower diversity is often associated with better health [32–34]. Alterations in the diversity of vaginal microbiota can increase the risk of bacterial vaginitis and, to some extent, elevate the risk of HIV infection [35, 36]. Additionally, a high-diversity microbial community is closely linked to elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the reproductive system [36].The increase of alpha diversity of the vaginal microbiota has also been linked to various obstetric and gynecological disorders [32–34]. In this study, alpha diversity of the vaginal microbiota in the DM group was greater, which may imply that diabetic condition provides a more favorable environment for the growth of a broader variety of vaginal microbiota species in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women. And the increase of alpha diversity of vaginal microbiota may be associated with vaginitis, which is a commonly encountered comorbid condition of diabetes [37]. Beta diversity is commonly used to assess compositional heterogeneity [38]. The findings of this study indicate DM group had greater compositional variation with higher beta diversity than the CT group.

The vaginal microbiota is primarily comprised of four major bacterial phyla: Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria, with Firmicutes representing the overwhelmingly dominant taxonomic group [39]. Current research suggests that Lactobacillus species, which belong to the Firmicutes phylum, typically make up more than 70% of the total microbial population in healthy women of reproductive age [40]. Disruption of vaginal microbiota homeostasis, clinically termed dysbiosis, is associated with increased susceptibility to bacterial vaginosis and other reproductive tract infections [41, 42]. Compared to the CT group, the DM group exhibited a decrease in the abundance of Firmicutes and an increase in the abundance of Proteobacteria. Meanwhile, the results of the phylogenetic branch analysis revealed a significant increase of Bacteroidetes in the DM group. In some gynecological diseases, such as HPV infection, an increase in the abundance of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria can also be detected [39].

Lactobacilli are the most prevalent bacterial groups found in healthy vaginal microbiota, contributing to the improvement and maintenance of a balanced vaginal microbial environment. In this study, the perimenopausal and postmenopausal women in the CT and DM groups exhibited low relative abundance of Lactobacillus, with a more pronounced reduction observed in the DM group. The relative abundance of the beneficial bacterial flora Limosilactobacillus reuteri, Lactobacillus paragasseri, and Lactobacillus crispatus in vagina was significantly higher in the CT group than in the DM group. Furthermore, the abundance of three Lactobacillus species exhibited a significant negative correlation with FBG and HbA1c levels. While, the relative abundance of Klebsiella, Gardnerella, and Staphylococcus increased in the DM group. The latter three bacteria are opportunistic pathogens associated with vaginitis. Otherwhile, the findings of this study indicate that the abundance of Prevotella in the vagina of patients in the DM group was significantly higher compared to those in the CT group. The presence of Prevotella in the vagina is correlated with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1α, IL-1β, and TNF-α, which can effectively trigger the inflammatory response [43]. Research has shown that Lactobacillus treatment is effective against Gardnerella infections in vagina, and results in a lower recurrence rate compared to metronidazole treatment [44]. Other studies have found that Lactobacillus strains from vaginal fluid of healthy women are effective in treating Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae infections [45]. Moreover, the administration of Limosilactobacillus reuteri in vagina significantly reduced the presence of Enterobacteriaceae and Staphylococcaceae in the vagina of mice, thereby promoting the balance and stability of the vaginal microbiota [46]. This also somewhat underscores the important role of Lactobacillus in suppressing vaginal infections. The reduced abundance of Limosilactobacillus reuteri, Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus paragasseri, and Lactobacillus iners in the vagina of perimenopausal and postmenopausal women with DM is likely to disrupt the homeostasis of the vaginal microbiota, thereby increasing the risk of vaginitis.

The stability of the vaginal microbiota is influenced not only by its composition and abundance but also by the interactions among its microbial members [47, 48]. In healthy women of reproductive age, the vaginal microbiota is typically dominated by one or more species of Lactobacillus, and it can be classified into five community state types (CSTs). CST I, II, III, and V are primarily dominated by Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus gasseri, Lactobacillus iners, and Lactobacillus jensenii [49]. The interactions among various species of Lactobacillus work together to maintain the stability of the vaginal microbiota and its pH, thereby supporting the overall health of the vagina [49]. Generally, the more complex and diverse the interactions within the vaginal microbiota, the lower its stability tends to be. Previous studies have shown that patients with polypoid lesions or stress urinary incontinence exhibited more complex network structures in vaginal microbiota compared to the control group [47, 50]. In addition to differences in species composition and abundance, the different network structures in DM and CT groups showed distinct community interactions. The DM group had a more complex and closely interconnected microbiome interaction compared to the CT group. Elucidating the impact of diabetes on the vaginal microbial network structure in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women and identifying core species under different conditions will help further explore how specific microorganisms influence vaginal health and the development of diseases.

Neutral community model is a stochastic, birth–death immigration process [51]. In this study, the R2 values of both groups were higher than 0.5, indicating that the distribution of the vaginal microbiota in both groups exhibited randomization. The R2 value of the DM group was 0.691, higher than the CT group’s value of 0.506, which somewhat suggested that the vaginal microbiota in the DM group exhibited a higher degree of randomization. This may be related to diabetes altering the dominant position of Lactobacillus species in the vaginal microbiota environment in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women. The results of this study indicate that the relative abundance of 33 species may be affected by diabetes, including Lactobacillus gasseri, Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus jensenii, and Lactobacillus iners. The CT group also had 18 species with abundances higher than the predicted range, including Lactobacillus iners, Lactobacillus crispatus, Lactobacillus gasseri, and Limosilactobacillus vaginalis, further suggesting that the abundance of Lactobacillus species is likely influenced by environmental factors and disease status under perimenopausal and postmenopausal conditions.

The perimenopausal and postmenopausal status can substantially alter vaginal microbiota homeostasis, partly due to decreased estrogen levels [52]. Women in these stages are also more susceptible to low-Lactobacillus vaginal microbial communities [53]. Since all participants in the CT group were perimenopausal or postmenopausal, the observed low abundance and reduced proportions of vaginal Lactobacillus species may be linked to natural fluctuations in vaginal microbiome composition influenced by multiple factors [54], with perimenopausal and postmenopausal status and age being influencing variables. Crucially, however, this study specifically characterizes the vaginal microbiota of perimenopausal and postmenopausal women with T2DM, and the findings likely represent a compound effect of estrogen deficiency and diabetes-related metabolic dysregulation.

This study has scope for improvement in several areas. First, the relatively small sample size may reduce the statistical power and generalizability of our findings, particularly given the inherent variability of the vaginal microbiota. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships between T2DM and observed microbial changes. Third, due to the inherent limitations of metagenomic sequencing technology, this study cannot directly elucidate the specific mechanisms by which T2DM affects vaginal microbiota homeostasis. Additionally, despite the absence of a statistically significant difference in menopausal status distribution between the DM and CT groups, we cannot fully disentangle the effects of T2DM from those of menopausal status and age. The near-significant age difference (p = 0.052) between groups further underscores the need for caution in interpretation. In future work, we plan to expand the cohort to enhance robustness, perform stratified analyses by age and menopausal status, undertake longitudinal studies to better establish causal relationships, clarify the independent contributions of T2DM to vaginal microbiota alterations, and investigate the underlying mechanisms involved.

Conclusions

In perimenopausal and postmenopausal women with T2DM, vaginal microbiota composition and abundance were significantly altered, exhibiting disrupted microbial equilibrium characterized by reduced Lactobacillus species and increased opportunistic pathogens. This dysbiotic profile is likely associated with menopausal status and diabetes-related dysregulation, and may contribute to increased susceptibility to vaginitis. Owing to the cross-sectional design and limited sample size of this study, a causal relationship between T2DM and vaginal microbiota alterations cannot be established, nor can the independent effects of T2DM be fully distinguished from age-related factors. Nevertheless, our findings provide valuable preliminary insights into the potential impact of T2DM on the ecological health of vaginal microbiota. Future research will involve expanded cohort studies to further elucidate the independent effects of T2DM on vaginal microbiota homeostasis and underlying mechanisms, ultimately contributing to the development of targeted strategies for preserving female reproductive tract health.
