Zotero as a teaching tool for independent study courses, honors contracts, and undergraduate research mentoring
Authors: Dyan E. Morgan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTManaging and evaluating the literature review process can be a time-consuming challenge when working one-on-one with students to teach scientific process skills, whether through an independent study, honors contract, or undergraduate research. In this article, I share my solution to address the pedagogical and organizational challenge posed by this type of teaching and mentoring: the citation management software Zotero used in conjunction with a template worksheet. Overall, this approach will save time, introduce students to a new multipurpose software tool, and lead to a set of reusable resources for future teaching.

INTRODUCTION

Deep, typically individualized, approaches to learning (e.g., independent studies, honors contracts, and undergraduate research) are beneficial to students as they are associated with higher grades and improvements in information retention and transfer (1). Most of these high-impact practices will introduce students to the scientific process via primary scientific literature. Reading primary scientific literature is associated with many positive benefits to students [reviewed in reference (2)] including improvements in the ability to develop research questions and hypotheses, interpret data, and draw conclusions [e.g., reference (3)] as well as improved content knowledge [e.g., reference (4)] among others. However, this type of teaching requires more course preparation time relative to higher enrollment courses [e.g., honors contracts (5, 6)]. Each student’s independent study, honors contract, or undergraduate research project may feel like a new course to prepare. While instructors generally find it valuable to introduce the research literature (7, 8), many instructors describe time constraints as a main reason they do not introduce primary scientific literature in courses (9, 10). As a result, the increased time requirement for independent studies, honors contracts, and undergraduate research could lead to fewer opportunities for undergraduate students to take part in these high-impact practices. When faced with this same faculty time vs. student opportunity challenge, I sought a technological solution to my problem of time and scale. I settled on Zotero, an open-source reference management software (11), used in conjunction with a template worksheet for students to complete for each research article. Zotero enables users to manage “libraries” of references (Fig. 1). Several of its features make it ideal for working with undergraduate students one-on-one. First, it is free of cost. Second, Zotero’s “group library” feature enables instructors to share a library of references with accompanying portable document format files and notes. Third, the attached notes provide a space for students to summarize their learning and an access point for the instructor to assess student learning. The approach described below is designed for instructors teaching independent studies or honors contracts (5, 6) and for mentors of undergraduate researchers.

PROCEDURE

Adaptation of templates

To provide a structured guide to the student’s reading of primary scientific literature, I adapted two templates for students to complete (Appendices 1 and 2). I started with the Figure Facts template (12), which incorporates many elements of the CREATE method (13) and then added components of other approaches to teaching primary scientific literature. First, I incorporated the steps of the CREATE method (13), especially the questions asking students to propose the next experiment, as suggested in the discussion of Round and Campbell (12). The CREATE method also asks students to read articles in a series to show the progression of the science, which I am often doing with students in these one-on-one teaching settings, so its approach fits well with this work. Additionally, I incorporated questions used by Janick-Bruckner (14), especially those asking students to consider why the study was done and whether there are alternative interpretations of the results. The templates (Appendix 1 and 2) include a requirement for the student to develop multiple-choice and discussion questions which can be used in current or future courses. This contribution to future students allowed my students to meet our University Honors Program’s requirement that each honors contract contributes to other students’ learning. Additionally, these student-developed questions provided me with further insight into the student’s level of understanding. Each instructor or mentor may customize their own template with additions or subtractions to the template. For example, some faculty may want to include or emphasize error analysis and could add that section while others may prefer to focus on experimental methods or future directions and could expand those sections of the template.

Implementation in one-on-one teaching or mentoring

Each semester, when I begin working with a student in a one-on-one teaching or mentoring setting, we start by discussing expectations regarding the amount and type of work, communication methods, and deadlines. For example, we decide how many research reviews or primary research articles they will read each week, how often we will meet and in what format, and how the student’s work will be assessed and graded. Depending on the nature of the work and independence level of the student, either the student, instructor, or both may be tasked with identifying the readings. Next, we set up Zotero. To start using Zotero, each student and instructor/mentor set up an account. Then, for each student, the instructor sets up a group library in Zotero. Zotero provides significant online help resources (15), and many university libraries provide training. I demonstrate to the student how to enter their template into the notes section for an article in Zotero. Each week, the students can fill in the template as a note for each paper and then hit the “sync” button. The instructor and student can either meet in person to discuss the notes or correspond via email depending on student and instructor preferences. In the case of undergraduate research projects especially, the mentor may ask the student to tag articles based on content or techniques to create a more easily searchable annotated resource.

Safety

There are no safety concerns as this activity is computer based.

CONCLUSION

I field tested this approach to teaching while working with students on independent studies, honors contracts, and undergraduate research projects in multiple formats over the course of eight semesters including in-person and both synchronously and asynchronously online.

Overall, these experiences were positive and time efficient for the students and instructor. While my university does not allow administrations of student evaluations of teaching in small courses, I can report that students provided consistently positive feedback about the process during each semester. For example, students frequently shared in our meetings that they liked using Zotero for our work, had not heard of it before, and had started using it for other classes. Some students reported teaching their friends to use Zotero. However, these students may have felt pressure to report positive outcomes.

Recently, I reached out to alumni who may feel slightly less pressure to provide solely positive feedback and asked them to share any thoughts about the process of using Zotero with the template during our work together. They reported a variety of positive impacts of using Zotero in conjunction with the template and did not report any negative feedback. The alumni replies fell into several categories. Some alumni were especially fond of technical features of Zotero:

“I really enjoyed the ‘tag’ feature of Zotero. It allowed me to organize my work so it was easily findable for myself and Dr. Morgan.”

“The ability to set up Group Libraries was, in my opinion, its best feature for this purpose. It made it incredibly easy for me to keep track of papers and resources we reviewed each week.”

“Another feature of Zotero that I found notable was content tagging and automated indexing. This saved time and enabled me to quickly locate specific topics or themes across my readings, often guiding our discussions.”

“Finally, with web-based, Windows, Android, macOS, iOS, and Linux-compatible apps, Zotero is remarkably accessible. Our shared library could easily be accessed at any time across all devices. The Zotero Connector, a tool that allows publications to be directly saved to the library from your browser, also contributed to its ease-of-use.”

“Some of the things I like best about Zotero are the abilities to share and view articles in a neat and concise folder on my laptop and the annotation features it provides. I also like that it opens each document as a separate tab so that I can switch back and forth when reading them.”

Others commented on how Zotero and the template positively affected the workflow:

“Zotero made collaborating with Dr. Morgan easy with the option to sync to a shared folder where we could both access our work. I never had to worry about uploading my projects to another platform which can be tedious when many require different formats (.pdf, .doc, etc.).”

“During the final semester of my undergraduate studies, I was fortunate to have the opportunity to complete an independent study under the guidance of Dr. Dyan Morgan. Each week, I read two articles and communicated my findings to Dr. Morgan. Central to this workflow was the use of the citation management tool, Zotero, as well as guided article templates. Despite never having used a citation management tool before, I found Zotero to be intuitive and user-friendly.”

“The ability to directly access PDFs within Zotero also streamlined my workflow and eliminated the hassle of searching through multiple folders or websites.”

Some students remarked on how Zotero allowed them to see their progress over time, an outcome I had not anticipated. It seems that both the students and I were monitoring their learning and growth:

“Since my reviews were all for the same project, I was able to tag them by date which helped me keep track of my progress each week.”

“Using the Notes feature, I was able to fill out an article template that helped me synthesize my findings, identify strengths and weaknesses of the study, and most importantly, identify other papers, concepts, and questions to explore next. These templates remain linked to the articles in our library, allowing me to revisit them and examine how my understanding has evolved over time.”

One alumna I contacted is currently in graduate school and provided feedback about her continued and expanded usage of Zotero:

“Even now, as a graduate student, Zotero remains my preferred citation manager. Though I did not make use of it at the time, one of my now-favorite features is integrating it with the AI citation-based literature mapping tool, Research Rabbit, to stay on top of new publications, organize literature reviews, and identify novel connections. How I use Zotero has evolved with my needs over time and remains a valuable tool that I’m grateful to have been exposed to early on in my academic career.”

Overall, I have been pleased that using Zotero with a template enables me to work with more students on honors contracts, independent studies, and undergraduate research projects than I initially thought I could accommodate. As a result, more students have been able to take part in these high-impact practices.
