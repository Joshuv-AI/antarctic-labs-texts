Medium-term outcomes of aortic valve–sparing procedure in trileaflet valve: Longitudinal analysis from the Heart Valve Society Registry
Authors: Matteo Pettinari, Peter Verbrugghe, Hans-Joachim Schäfers, Emmanuel Lansac, Ismail El-Hamamsy, Olivier Bouchot, Jolanda Kluin, Monica Contino, Jan Vojacek, José Aramendi, Igor Rudez, Gebrine El-Khoury, Laurent De Kerchove, Aortic Valve Research Network of the Heart Valve Society Aortic Registry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe aortic valve–sparing procedure has been performed for more than 2 decades and has proven durable results in several aortic and root phenotypes. Few studies have analyzed the impact of aortic valve regurgitation on the need for leaflet repair and outcomes after trileaflet aortic valve–sparing surgery. The aim of this study is to analyze in a large cohort the clinical and echocardiographic outcomes of trileaflet aortic valve–sparing surgery in patients with aortic root dilatation, focusing on the impact of preoperative aortic regurgitation and cusp repair.MethodsWe extracted the data from the Heart Valve Society Aortic Registry (previously also known as AVIATOR). We selected patients with trileaflet aortic valves undergoing operations for aortic root dilatation (root/ascending aorta ≥45 mm) by the valve-sparing procedure. The primary outcome was the incidence of moderate or more aortic insufficiency and progression level of aortic insufficiency of at least 1 grade at follow-up. A cusp repair score was created as the cumulative sum of all leaflet repair techniques used per patients.ResultsA total of 1116 patients were included in the analysis. Mean age of patients was 53 ± 15 years, and 84% were male. The observed incidence of moderate/severe aortic insufficiency at 1, 5, and 10 years was 0.7%, 3%, and 6.8%, respectively. Both moderate or more aortic insufficiency and progression of aortic insufficiency by 1° increase significantly with time. Preoperative aortic insufficiency and the need for additional clamping sessions were independent predictors of aortic insufficiency progression, whereas the cusp repair score increases the risk of aortic insufficiency of moderate or more.ConclusionsIn patients with trileaflet aortic valves undergoing sparing surgery, preoperative aortic insufficiency and the need of a more extensive cusp repair increase the long-term risk of aortic insufficiency. Time also impacts the recurrence of aortic insufficiency or its increase.Key Words: aortic root replacement, aortic valve repair, tricuspid valve

AI trend in function of preoperative AI, extra clamping session, and CRS.

Central Message

In the TAV, the degree of valve insufficiency and the need for additional clamping session increase the risk of valve dysfunction during the follow-up.

Perspective

Adequate patient selection and careful consideration in the decision to repair the aortic valve in patients with a high degree of preoperative aortic valve dysfunction could improve long-term outcomes.

Aortic valve–sparing root replacement (VSRR) demonstrated durable long-term results in patients with aortic root or ascending aorta aneurysm with dilated aortic sinus.1 As experience has improved in the last decades, indications for VSRR have extended to more complex conditions such as connective tissue disorder, aortic insufficiency (AI), bicuspid valve, and aortic dissection.2,3

VSRR is used less in case of AI combined with leaflet pathology because of the need for additional cusp repair, the lack of surgeons’ experience, and the risk of less optimal long-term durability.4 This situation is even more pronounced in trileaflet aortic valve (TAV) phenotypes, where the presence of multiple coaptation line makes the repair more challenging than the single coaptation line of the bicuspid valve.5 Tamer and colleagues6 demonstrated the negative impact of preoperative AI after the sparing procedure in a retrospective single-center analysis.

The present study analyzes the influence of preoperative AI and the need for aortic cusp repair on the long-term outcomes after VSRR in the TAV using the Heart Valve Society Aortic Valve Database.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study is a retrospective cohort analysis of a multicenter database and was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Cliniques Universitaire. Ethical approval was granted on January 9, 2024, under Approval Number IC2278.

Registry Design

The Heart Valve Society Aortic Valve Database aims at gathering and collecting information on aortic valve disease and ascending aorta aneurysm. It provides a unique database of 78 centers from 23 different countries, including 10,893 patients with AI and ascending aorta or root aneurysm undergoing surgical repair or replacement.7 The database has been open for patient enrollment since January 2013. Data for patients undergoing operation were collected retrospectively, and follow-up is continued prospectively according to database standards.8 Collected data include baseline patient characteristics such as risk factors for operative mortality (European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation), procedural information with detailed valve analysis and repair procedure, in-hospital outcomes, longitudinal measurement of echocardiography parameters, and valve-related events during at least yearly follow-up according to the guidelines of reporting mortality and morbidity after cardiac valve interventions.9 Collected outcome and definitions can be found at https://www.hvs.com.

Patient Selection

For the present study, all patients with a TAV and an aortic ascending or root diameter of 45 mm who underwent an aortic VSRR (including reimplantation and root remodeling) were identified within the registry. Patients treated for acute endocarditis and acute aortic dissection and with aortic leaflet calcification were excluded. Figure E1 shows the distribution per years of surgery, country, and center. Baseline characteristics, procedural data, and in-hospital and follow-up outcomes were analyzed. We conducted a subgroup analysis focusing on patients with no or trivial AI, aiming to explore the primary outcome in patients with no leaflet disease but isolated aneurysm pathology.

Cusp Repair Score

We created a cusp repair score (CRS) to quantify the complexity of leaflet repair. This score was calculated as follows: 1 point per repaired cusps (possible points 0, 1, 2, 3), 1 point for free margin plication or resuspensions with running sutures (0-1), 1 point for cusp resection/decalcification (0-1), and 2 points for patch repair (0-2). The possible score was 0, 2, up to 7. Free margin plication was performed to treat preexisting or induced cusp prolapse and optimize valve coaptation by increasing the effective height to 9 mm as recommended in the literature.10, 11, 12, 13 Resuspension with a running suture was also used to treat cusp prolapse but with the goal to close thin fenestration and reinforce the free margin. Cusp resection and decalcification include focal removal of cusp fibrosis or calcification. Patch repair was used to restore local cusp defect (large or ruptured fenestration, cusp perforation).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was moderate or more AI during the follow-up. The original 5-degree AI in the database (0 = no or trivial, 1 = mild, 2 = mild to moderate, 3 = moderate to severe, 4 = severe) was recoded in 3° mild (0 and 1), moderate (2 and 3), and severe (4) as ordinal variable. The progression of AI was defined as the increase of 1 degree of AI (on the ordinal 5 level scale) between the actual observation and the previous one. The secondary outcomes were overall survival (also compared with the age- and gender-matched European population), reoperation rate, incidence of cerebrovascular accident, and New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD or the median with interquartile range in case of normal or non-normal distribution (as assessed by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) and were compared between independent groups using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test or t test where appropriate. Categorical variables are shown as absolute number and percentages. Chi-square or Fisher exact test was applied to test for differences between groups. Generalized linear mixed models were used to evaluate the temporal trend for all the outcomes of interest.10, 11, 12 The ordinal version of the AI variable was modeled by a proportional odds ratio (OR) model. This extension of the logistic model serves to model the risk of higher-level response versus the lower.14

The Kaplan–Meier curves of the secondary outcomes were created (survival, reoperation rate, and NYHA class III or IV) and a Cox proportional hazards regression modeling identified the significant predictors for the outcomes of interest among the clinical and echocardiographic covariates available.

Missing data were imputed by multiple imputation using fully conditional specification15,16 (Figure E2). We assumed missing at random, but to confirm this assumption, we performed a sensitivity analysis under the missing not at random assumptions17 (Table E1).

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc) and RStudio: Integrated Development (R. RStudio). The Institutional Review Board or equivalent ethics committee of the Cliniques Universitaire Saint Luc approved the study protocol and publication of data. The patients provided informed written consent for the publication of the study data.

Results

Study Population

The study population comprised 1116 patients undergoing operations between 1995 and 2019, with a mean age of 53 ± 15 years and 84% male. The primary indication for surgery was aneurysm in 796 patients (71.5%), regurgitation in 186 patients (16.7%), and mixed pathology in 132 patients (11.8%). Patients with moderate or severe preoperative AI were older (57 ± 13 years and 55 ± 12 years vs 47 ± 16 years, P < .001) and were less likely to have connective tissue disorders (21% and 9.8% vs 43%, P < .001) (Table 1). Patients also had a higher chance of having an eccentric AI jet (41% and 53% vs 13%, P < .001), a larger aortic sinus (53 ± 7 and 55 ± 11 vs 50 ± 5, P < .001), and a larger ascending aorta diameter (46 ± 9 and 47 ± 12 vs 40 ± 8, P < .001) (Table 2).

The overall rate of cusp repair was 56%. A higher grade of AI required more cusp repair (grade mild: 44%; moderate: 60% and severe: 78%; P < .001) and had higher CRS (1.16 ± 1.42; 1.74 ± 1.58 and 2.40 ± 1.53; P < .001) (Table 3). The right coronary cusp was the most frequently repaired (49%) followed by the noncoronary cusp (29%) and left coronary cusp (22%). Free margin plication was performed in 55% of the patients and represented 98% of all repair techniques. Other repair techniques were less frequently used: running suture (polytetrafluorethylene [Gore-Tex] 7/0) (3.6%), cusp resection or decalcification (2.5%), and cusp repair with patch (2.2%) (Table 3). Overall, the rate of 1, 2, and 3 cusp repair was 25%, 20%, and 11%, respectively. The rate of 2 and especially 3 cusps repair increases with AI grade; whereas the rate of 1 cusp repair remained stable across AR grades.

Concerning the type of VSRR, patients undergoing reimplantation had a lower incidence of moderate preoperative AI (36.9% vs 52.8%, P < .001) but similar severe AI (13.4% vs 14%, P = .88) with respect to the patient undergoing remodeling with annuloplasty. In those with need of at least 1 leaflet repair (68.3% vs 49%, P < .001), the CRS (1.93 vs 1.37, P < .001) was higher in the remodeling plus annuloplasty group compared with the reimplantation group. The aortic sinus (52.4 vs 50.5 mm, P = .001) and ascending aorta (45.1 vs 42.7 mm, P = .008) were larger in the remodeling plus annuloplasty group, but the annulus diameter was similar (27.2 vs 27.3 mm, P = .9). The size of the graft used for the VSRR was smaller in the remodeling plus annuloplasty group compared to the reimplantation group (27.5 vs 30.7 mm, P < .001) (Table E2).

A total of 126 patients had no/trivial AI. Patients were younger (45 ± 17 vs 55 ± 14, P < .001) and less symptomatic (NYHA class III or IV = 0.8% vs 23%, P < .001), and had a higher probability of having connective tissue disorders (60% vs 23%, P < .001) (Table E3). They had a smaller sinus of Valsalva diameter (49 ± 4 vs 52 ± 8, P < .001) and ascending aorta (39 ± 8 vs 45 ± 10, P < .001) but similar aortic annulus (27 ± 3.9 vs 27.1 ± 4.2, P = .7) (Table E4). Among patients with no/trivial AI, 47 (37%) underwent of at least 1 repair of 1 of the cusps. The number of cusps repaired (1 cusp repaired 23% vs 26%, P = .4; 2 cusps repaired 8.7% vs 22%, P < .01; 3 cusps repaired 5.6% vs 11%, P = .04) and CRS (0.97 vs 1.71, P = .01) were significantly lower in the group with no/trivial AI (Table E5).

Primary Outcome

The observed incidence of moderate to severe AI at 1, 5, and 10 years postoperatively was 0.7%, 3%, and 6.8%, respectively (Figure 1). In a generalized linear mixed-effects model with time as a covariate, the odds of moderate to severe AI increased significantly over follow-up (OR per year, 1.12, 95% CI, 1.08-1.16; P < .001). Likewise, the odds of AI progression increased over time (OR per year, 1.06, 95% CI, 1.03-1.08; P < .001). Preoperative AI (OR moderate/severe AI, 1.44, CI, 1.32-1.57, P < .001; OR for AI progression, 1.33, CI, 1.15-1.58, P < .001) and the need for an additional clamping session (OR for moderate/severe AI, 1.43, CI, 1.01-2.02, P = .042; OR for AI progression, 1.38, CI, 1.03-1.84, P = .02) increase the risk of moderate or severe AI and its progression. Conversely, CRS (OR, 1.1, CI, 1.03-1.18, P = .007) only increases the risk of moderate and severe AI (Table 4). The type of VSRR (OR, 1.85, CI, 0.69-4.96, P = .221), the number of cusps repaired (OR, 0.48, CI, 0.13-1.77, P = .27), the use of a patch (OR, 0.91, CI, 0.32-2.57, P = .8), the use of running suture (OR, 1.54, CI, 0.92-2.55, P = .09), and resection or decalcification (OR, 1.37, CI, 0.62-3.04, P = .4) did not influence the incidence of AI moderate to severe or its progression. Secondary Outcome

Median follow-up was 3.3 years. Hospital mortality was 1.6%. Survival at 5 and 10 years was 93.1% and 76.5%, respectively. Figure 3 shows the Kaplan–Meier curve of the age- and gender-matched population. After 7 years, there is diversion of the 2 curves. The multivariable Cox regression analysis identified only age as a significant predictor (hazard ratio [HR], 1.154, CI, 0.8662-1.066, P < .001) of late survival.

Kaplan–Meier for the survival of the study population compared with the age- and gender-matched population.

During the follow-up, 43 patients underwent a reoperation. The reason for the reoperation was recurrent AI in 21 patients, endocarditis in 2 patients, thoracic aorta disease in 3 patients, stenosis in 2 patients, and reoperation unknown in 15 patients. Freedom from reintervention rate at 5 and 10 years was 93.8% and 89.9%, respectively. From the multivariate Cox analysis, left ventricle end-systolic diameter (HR, 1.044, CI, 1.014-1.074, P = .003) and the need for more than 1 clamping session (HR, 2.179, CI, 1.043-4.555, P = .03) significantly increased the risk of reintervention Figure 4. Freedom from NYHA class III or IV was 95.8% and 91.1% at 5 and 10 years, respectively. Preoperative atrial fibrillation (HR, 5.6, CI, 2.2-14.72, P < .001) and age (HR, 1.05, CI, 1.01-1.08, P = .008) were significant predictors of worse NYHA class during follow-up.

We registered a total of 26 thromboembolic events, 6 during the first hospitalization. Events were 10 transient ischemic attacks, 9 strokes, and 7 peripheral embolizations. Freedom from thromboembolic events at 5 and 10 years was 97.5% and 95.8%, respectively. During the first hospitalization, 16 patients required a pacemaker and 16 patients required a pacemaker during the follow-up. The 5- and 10-year freedom from pacemaker implantation was 96.4% and 94.8%, respectively.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the long-term results of VSRR in patients with aortic aneurysm and trileaflet anatomy. The study analyzed one of the largest multicentric cohort of patients followed prospectively in a unique international registry dedicated to aortic valve–sparing and repair surgery. The cohort represents the real-life activity of 35 centers including a mix of expert-high volume centers and smaller centers. Approximately 60% of the patients presented at least moderate AI or more, 30% had connective tissue disorders, and 7% underwent redo surgery.

The overall long-term survival and freedom from reoperation is in line with other reports in the literature.18, 19, 20 The primary outcomes analysis focusing on the valve function showed that postoperative AI worsened with increasing years of follow-up even in patients with normal valve function preoperatively. In general, preoperative AI, extensive cusp repair, and extra clamping sessions increase the risk of postoperative AI and its progression. In patients with normal preoperative valve function, extensive cusp repair increased the risk of postoperative AI progression.

The impact of preoperative AI on long-term results after VSRR is a key question thoroughly addressed by several authors in the literature.4,21 Effectively, with the broadening of VSRR indication to severe AI, the skill necessary to achieve optimal results became more clear. The final valve configuration (ie, effective height of 9 mm, coaptation height of at least 5 mm) is known to be essential to obtain a reduction or disappearing of AI and adequate long-term results.10,22,23 There is no doubt that cusp repair techniques such as plication or free margin suspension attenuate the risk in most of the patients,24,25 but if this result is not accomplished adequately (ie, effective height <9 mm, residual AI) the long-term durability could deteriorate.24 Likewise, the need for reclamping the aorta is a surrogate parameter of the necessary additive gesture on the valve to obtain an adequate immediate result.

In this study, as well as in most of the literature, there was no significant effect of preoperative AI on clinical relevant outcome parameters as survival or reoperation. However, at least 1 report from Escobar and colleagues25 demonstrates the significant impact of severe preoperative AI on the composite outcome of survival, reoperation, and thromboembolic events. To the contrary, the role of preoperative AI on postoperative moderate or severe AI and AI progression with time was demonstrated. In a multicentric study including 1015 patients from 3 German centers, Kari and colleagues26 analyzed the incidence of residual AI after VSRR and it progression over time. They found a trend of having more residual AI in case of preoperative AI and in larger aneurysm. Residual AI was present in 29% of the patients and progressed in 30% of these patients at a rate of 0.3 grades per patient-year within the first 5 years. Ouzounian and colleagues27 showed an increase of AI with time in a single-center longitudinal report of 333 patients after VSRR from 1334 echocardiograms available during the follow-up. The risk of AI progression with time is an important information for the surgeon and cardiologist in the lifetime management of these often young patients. Even in patients with preoperative normal valve function before surgery, long-term postoperative surveillance of AI is mandatory because AI can progress with time, particularly if they had extensive cusp repair.

The rate of cusp repair during VSRR varies in a wide range in the literature and depends on many factors such as patient selection (ie, TAV/BAV, preoperative AI), VSRR technique (ie, reimplantation, remodeling, graft sizing), and individual surgeon habits. In the literature, cusp repair is generally close to 90% or more in BAV and mainly involves the fused cusp, whereas in TAVs this number is lower, ranging from 30% to 90%.6,26,28, 29, 30 This study describes in details the rate, distribution, and techniques of cusp repair from low to high AI grade. Plication is by far the most frequent technique used, with the right cusp most frequently repaired cusp followed by the left and the non coronary cusp. The extent of cusp repair increases with AI grade.6,30

Several reports support the safety of cusp repair and the absence of negative impact on long-term results. These reports show that cusp repair can effectively restore the normal valve configuration making the result durable, especially if the cusp repair is guided by the intraoperative assessment and measurements (ie, effective height).4,18,30, 31, 32, 33, 34 It is known that intervention on multiple cusps or a more extensive repair might impact the efficacy.6 Therefore, in this study, we introduced the CRS score, a more specific parameter of cusp repair extensiveness and complexity. It gives not only the number of treated cusp but also the number of techniques used. Extensive or complex cusp repair, represented by a high CRS score, and additional clamping session were found to be independent predictors of postoperative AI and its progression. Obviously, both parameters reflect a certain degree of case complexity that may be due to high grade of preoperative AI, challenging valvular lesion or technical error leading to low valve coaptation. Our study confirms observations by Tamer and colleagues in a single center cohort of 303 reimplantation in TAV.6 In this study, cusp repair was not associated with higher reoperation rate or severe AI recurrence, but the percentage of recurrent AI moderate to severe increased progressively from 0 to 3 cusp repair. Another study from Settepani and colleagues35 reported a negative effect of the number of cusps repaired on the risk of reoperation. Effectively, in case of multiple cusps prolapse, as possible in a TAV, the correction of these defects could be more demanding because of the absence of reference nonprolapsing cusp. Moreover, multiple cusp plications or additive techniques such as resection, decalcification, and especially patch repair could lead to higher fibrotic reaction, restriction of the leaflet, and recurrence of AI.18,36,37

Study Limitations

First, the institutional participation in the Heart Valve Society Aortic Valve Database is voluntary and therefore depends principally on the referring center or physician. This introduces a bias in the possible patient's selection process depending only on the individual center. Second, the number of missing data during the follow-up is high. Approximately 50% of the echocardiography used for the analysis was obtained during the first year after surgery, and only 478 echocardiographic results were available between 5 and 10 years. Therefore, long-term results, over 10 years of follow-up, are needed to confirm our findings. However, we test the missingness with a sensitivity analysis, and the results obtained were tested.

Conclusions

In patients undergoing aortic VSRR with a TAV and aneurysm of the root, preoperative AI and the need for a more extensive cusp repair increase the risk of AI and its progression. However, survival, reoperation rate, and functional capacity are not affected by these factors.
