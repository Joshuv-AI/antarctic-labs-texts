Cervical esophagocolic anastomosis leakage caused by a tension pneumothorax after an esophagocoloplasty for caustic esophageal stricture in a child: a case report

Background

Caustic ingestion in children is a public health problem in low-income countries. This condition is mainly caused by domestic accidents caused by improper caustic agent packaging and storage. Lesions caused by accidental ingestion of caustics can affect functional and vital prognosis in 10% of patients [1]. Esophageal stricture is the most frequent late sequela, leading to chronic pain, dysphagia, and malnutrition. The colon graft is the most common organ used for esophageal reconstruction in patients with long or multiple caustic esophageal strictures. Its advantages include length, acid resistance [2], and excellent blood vessels [3]. The challenge of esophagocoloplasty is to ensure a good cervical anastomosis without any tension using a graft with sufficient length and blood supply [4]. Colonic interposition grafts are commonly placed in the posterior mediastinum and retrosternal space. The interposition of the colonic graft in the posterior mediastinum (orthotopic position) necessitates the ablation of the native esophagus and can result in additional morbidity, such as bleeding, mediastinitis, or pneumothorax development [5].

Cervical anastomotic leak is one of the most common complications associated with esophagoplasty with colon graft interposition. Several factors, such as inadequate lumen between the esophagus and the colon, acute ischemia of the anastomosis region, poor blood supply to the esophagus and the colon, mechanical compression of the cephalic portion of the graft, malnutrition, long-term corticosteroid therapy, or friable nature of the tissues, have been reported [6].

We report the case of a 5-year-old Black African male patient admitted for a nondilatable esophageal stricture due to caustic ingestion. Esophagectomy followed by esophagocoloplasty was performed using the transverse colon segment placed in the esophageal bed (posterior mediastinum). The postoperative course was characterized by an enterocutaneous fistula caused by a left-side tension pneumothorax. Thoracic drainage was performed, leading to early fistula healing. The 2-year follow-up showed no complications.

Case presentation

A 5-year-old Black African male patient with a 12-week history of accidental ingestion of caustic soda powder 12 weeks previously presented to the surgery department with dysphagia, retrosternal pain, and malnutrition for 14 days. On general examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable, with a blood pressure of 110/60 mmHg, a pulse rate of 90 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 25 breaths per minute, and a body temperature of 36.5 °C. Physical examination revealed pale conjunctiva, dehydration, and malnutrition (Fig. 1A). The ear, nose, and throat (ENT); thoracic; and abdominal examinations were normal. Blood tests showed a white blood cell count of 9300/mm3, hemoglobin of 6.0 g/dL, platelet count of 312,000/mm3, and a prothrombin time test of 11.1 s (international normalized ratio [INR] = 1.02). The blood biochemical tests were as follows: serum urea, 24 mg/dL; serum creatinine, 0.8 mg/dL; hyponatremia, 132 mmol/dL; hypokalemia, 2.9 mmol/dL; hypochloroemia, 89 mmol/dL; and C-reactive protein (CRP), 28 mg/dL.

A Image showing a 5-year-old malnourished and dehydrated patient. B Preoperative upper gastrointestinal barium swallow test revealing cervical esophageal stenosis (red arrow) and stricture of a long segment of the thoracic esophagus (blue arrow)

A preoperative upper gastrointestinal barium swallow test revealed cervical esophageal stenosis associated with a long thoracic esophagus stricture (Fig. 1B). Owing to the esophageal stricture length, endoscopic dilatation was not indicated in this patient. We initially performed feeding gastrostomy to correct the patient’s malnutrition and electrolyte imbalance; 2 months after performing it, the patient was admitted for esophageal replacement.

The patient was taken to the operating room and placed in a supine position with the head rotated to the right. The neck, chest, and abdominal sections were prepared. The intervention was performed under general anesthesia. A superior midline laparotomy incision and a left presternocleidomastoid cervicotomy were performed. Thoracotomy was not required; esophagectomy was performed using the transhiatal approach, and esocolic anastomosis was performed in the neck. A superior midline laparotomy was performed, and the colon’s posterior attachments were incised. The colon was completely dissected and mobilized from the ileocecum to the sigmoid colon. The transverse colon was chosen for interposition (Fig. 2A). The arterial vascularization of the colon was identified using transillumination, and the correct arterial outflow of the colon graft (middle colic artery) was determined. The venous outflow and length of the colon graft were also checked. The transverse colon was transected between the intestinal clamps (Fig. 2B). The cervical esophagus was approached via left presternocleidomastoid cervicotomy. The esophagus was mobilized using blunt dissection and encircled using a traction tape (Fig. 3A). The cervical esophagus was then divided. Esophagectomy was completed using a transhiatal approach (Fig. 3B, C). A left pleural injury was caused during the transhiatal esophageal dissection and went unnoticed. The transverse colon graft was carefully transposed through the esophageal bed (orthotopic position).

Performing the transhiatal esophagectomy. A Perioperative image showing transhiatal esophagectomy. B Perioperative image showing dissection of the cervical esophagus. C Specimen of esophagectomy

Cervical esophagocolic anastomosis was performed using a manual end-to-end interrupted stitch with polydioxanone surgical (PDS) 3–0 sutures. For cervical drainage, a corrugated sheet drain was used, and the cervical incision was closed. A gastrocolic anastomosis was then performed with a continuous end-to-side vicryl 3–0 stitch. Colocolic anastomosis was performed between the remaining colon segments, and the mesocolic defect was closed. The surgical procedure proceeded without any major peroperative complications. The operating time was approximately 4 hours and 30 minutes. The blood loss was approximately 100 mL. The postoperative course was characterized 3 days postoperatively by a cervical enterocutaneous fistula at the level of the corrugated sheet drain outlet. A postoperative upper gastrointestinal barium swallow revealed a cervical esophagocolic anastomotic leak associated with a tension pneumothorax in the left hemithorax, which required immediate attention (Fig. 4). The pneumothorax deviated to the right of all mediastinal organs, including the colonic graft. The deviation of the colon graft to the right caused cervical anastomosis stretching, which was responsible for the anastomotic leak. Thoracic drainage was performed on the left side of the patient (Fig. 5A). Cervical esophagocolic anastomotic leakage stopped 2 days after pneumothorax drainage, and the enterocutaneous fistula healed spontaneously early (Fig. 5B). This favorable evolution after pneumothorax drainage suggested that the fistula was caused by the deviation of the colon graft and cervical anastomosis stretching by the pneumothorax. The last barium swallowing test revealed proper caliber of the upper digestive tract and the absence of strictures and leaks. Oral nutrition was started postoperatively 18 days after the surgery, the thoracic drain was removed 24 days after the surgery, and the patient was discharged from the hospital 30 days postoperatively. The patient was followed up for 24 months with satisfactory oral nutrition without clinical dysphagia or radiological cervical anastomotic stenosis.

Discussion

Cervical esophagocolic anastomosis leakage and pneumothorax are two potential early complications of colonic transposition used to restore continuity of the upper digestive tract for the caustic-injured esophagus. These two complications can occur together or separately [7]. Pneumothorax caused by mediastinal pleura injury during transhiatal esophagectomy occurs in 13.09% of patients. It is mostly promptly recognized and managed by water-seal drainage [8]. An unnoticed pneumothorax can cause compression of the mediastinal organs and colonic graft and increase the risk of cervical leakage. The increased pressure around the anastomosis deteriorates the blood supply in that region, leading to mechanical local hypoxia and ischemia [9].

In this case reported, dissection of the native esophagus was performed without hemorrhagic complications or suspected pleural and pulmonary lesions. The colonic transplant was of good length and well vascularized. However, a left pleural wound had gone unnoticed and caused a compressive left pneumothorax postoperatively, leading to compression of the left lung on the mediastinum and pushing all the mediastinum organs toward the right. The deviation of the colon graft to the right and the cervical anastomosis stretching caused by the tension left-side pneumothorax can explain the mechanism underlying this cervical anastomotic leak. Pleural drainage allowed for rapid stoppage of the leak and rapid healing of the enterocutaneous fistula.

According to medical literature, colon interposition may be the best option to restore upper gastrointestinal tract continuity following esophageal caustic stricture in a child. It restores satisfactory oral food intake and a good quality of life. The advantages of a colon graft are the good blood supply, which guarantees the success of colon interposition, and the length of the graft to be pulled up to the neck, ensuring cervical anastomosis without tension. The normal location and functions of the stomach can be maintained, and good growth and long-term quality of life can be achieved after surgery [10]. The retrosternal and posterior mediastinal routes are the most commonly applied routes for colon interposition. The retrosternal route is longer and has a higher incidence of cervical anastomotic leakage (19–70%) than the posterior mediastinal route.

The biggest disadvantage of the retrosternal route is the potential risk of graft compression by the thoracic inlet, which leads to mechanical ischemia of the graft’s cervical portion, causing leakage or localized necrosis [11]. The orthotopic position in the posterior mediastinum has the advantage of being the shortest route and avoiding the two obligatory bends at the cervical level and at the abdomen reintegration. Furthermore, transplants placed in the bed of the native esophagus within the perioesophagitis were better contained and were significantly less redundant than those placed in the retrosternal position. This is particularly true for the colon, which has a strong tendency to become redundant when placed in the retrosternal space [12]. In addition, a burned esophagus presents a risk of malignant degeneration of 1.8–16% [13]. Therefore, the orthotopic route has become our first choice.

However, the posterior mediastinal route requires removal of the native esophagus. Ablation of the injured esophagus is certainly the most dangerous part of the procedure. The native esophagus was dissected and removed using a double cervical and transhiatal approach. Dissection of the middle esophagus is performed blindly using digitoclasis [14]. Dissection must be gentle and careful, especially in the case of perioesophageal and pleural synechiae, to avoid any significant bleeding and pleural lesions that will cause hemothorax or pneumothorax. However, starting the dissection at the hiatus in a healthy area and moving up into the mediastinum while remaining in contact with the esophagus appears to be easier [15]. Pneumothorax and hemothorax due to mediastinal pleura injury during transhiatal esophagectomy should be promptly recognized and drained by water-seal drainage [16].

Conclusion

Cervical esophagocolic anastomosis is a severe complication, and its management constitutes a challenge for surgeons. Several factors influence the occurrence of this complication. We share the experience of a case of cervical esophagocolic leakage caused by a tension pneumothorax and hope that it could be helpful in diagnosing and treating similar cases.
