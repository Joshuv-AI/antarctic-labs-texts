Diagnostic Accuracy of Estimated Fetal Weight Discordance in Predicting Birthweight Discordance in Monochorionic Twins: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jip A. Spekman, E. J. T. Joanne Verweij, Femke Slaghekke, Monique C. Haak, Chiara C. M. M. Lap, Derek P. de Winter, Jeanine M. M. van Klink, Enrico Lopriore, Sophie G. Groene
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of sonographic estimated fetal weight discordance (EFWD) ≥ 20% in predicting birthweight discordance (BWD) ≥ 20% (i.e., selective fetal growth restriction [sFGR]) in monochorionic (MC) twin pregnancies.MethodRetrospective cohort study including uncomplicated MC twin pairs (n = 213) and MC twins with sFGR (n = 134), with available ultrasound within 14 days of birth approximately 2002 and 2023 in our center. Within 14 and 7 days of birth, we calculated sensitivity and specificity. In a subgroup of twins with available ultrasound within 3 days of birth, the absolute error of BWD was calculated (BWD–EFWD).ResultsThe sensitivity and specificity of EFWD ≥ 20% within 14 days of birth in predicting BWD ≥ 20% were 84% (95% CI 76–89) and 85% (95% CI 79–89), respectively. Ultrasounds available within 7 days of birth (n = 192/347) showed comparable diagnostic accuracies (sensitivity 85% [95% CI 75–92], and specificity 81% [95% CI 73–88]). Among twins with available ultrasound within 3 days of birth (n = 90/347), the absolute error of BWD was −2.0% (SD 8.0), indicating a mean overestimation of BWD.ConclusionIn MC twins with sFGR, the accuracy of sonographic EFWD ≥ 20% in predicting BWD ≥ 20%, is relatively high. BWD is overestimated by an average of two percentage points within 3 days of birth.

Introduction

Monochorionic (MC) twins share a single placenta. Because of this shared placenta, these pregnancies are at high risk of complications [1]. One of the most prevalent complications is selective fetal growth restriction (sFGR) caused by unequal placental sharing, leading to an antenatal growth discordance with a prevalence of 10%–15% [2]. Postnatally, this manifests as a birthweight discordance (BWD). sFGR is associated with high rates of perinatal morbidity and mortality, partly due to (iatrogenic) preterm delivery as a result of fear of fetal demise in the smaller twin and the concomitant risk of brain injury or fetal demise in the larger twin [3]. While the optimal antenatal management strategy remains to be defined, accurate antenatal diagnosis is crucial to identify pregnancies that would benefit from increased surveillance and to determine the optimal timing of delivery.

A previous Delphi procedure identified an estimated fetal weight (EFW) of one of the fetuses < 3rd percentile as a solitary parameter for antenatal diagnosis of sFGR, or at least two out of the following: (1) EFW < 10th percentile in one twin, (2) EFW discordance (EFWD) ≥ 25%, (3) abdominal circumference (AC) < 10th percentile or (4) umbilical artery (UA) pulsatility index > 95th percentile [4]. However, these diagnostic criteria appear to be equally effective in predicting outcomes and a large BWD compared with solely using EFWD ≥ 20% [2]. Previous studies on the accuracy of EFWD in MC twin pregnancies show conflicting results with a high variability in sensitivities, large time intervals between ultrasound and birth, and lack of distinction in chorionicity [5, 6]. However, it is important to realize that even in singleton pregnancies there is an inherent error between the calculated EFW and the actual birthweight [7]. The use of the EFW of both fetuses in twin pregnancies to calculate EFWD may introduce an even greater margin of error, leading to an over‐ or underestimation of the actual intertwin growth discrepancy, which could adversely affect perinatal decision‐making.

This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of an EFWD ≥ 20% in predicting a BWD ≥ 20% (i.e., sFGR) in MC twin pregnancies, both uncomplicated and sFGR twins, within 14 and 7 days of birth. Additionally, we will evaluate the accuracy of sonographic EFW of both the smaller and larger twins within 3 days of birth to further evaluate the contribution of each individual twin to the error rate of EFWD in predicting BWD.

Methods

In this retrospective cohort study, we included all consecutive MC twin pregnancies with and without sFGR who received antenatal sonographic biometric measurements at our center, irrespective of place of birth, between May 27, 2002, and December 31, 2023. sFGR was defined as a BWD ≥ 20%, with BWD calculated as (birthweight larger twin—birthweight smaller twin)/birthweight larger twin × 100%. This definition serves as a postnatal expression of discordant growth in utero and was applied to all included twin pregnancies to classify them as sFGR. We excluded higher order multiple pregnancies, twin‐twin transfusion syndrome [8], twin anemia polycythemia sequence [9], monoamnionicity, twin reversed arterial perfusion, other congenital abnormalities and/or fetal death. Pregnancies lacking EFW measurements within 14 days of birth or those with unknown birthweights were excluded from the analysis. Because of the retrospective design of this study, a convenience sample was used. This study was reviewed by the Ethics Committee of the Leiden University Medical Center and confirmed that the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act did not apply (reference number G21.044).

The following baseline characteristics were collected from medical records: maternal age, gravidity, parity, UA Doppler flow pattern of the smaller twin (type I defined as positive end‐diastolic flow (pEDF), type II defined as persistent absent or reversed EDF (A/REDF), and type III defined as intermittent A/REDF (iA/REDF)) [10], days between last ultrasound with biometric measurements and birth, gestational age at birth, sex, delivery mode, BWD, birthweight and proportion of neonates born small for gestational age (SGA; birthweight < 10th percentile) [11], placental share and placental share discordance. As UA Doppler flows can change during pregnancy, the most prevalent flow pattern was chosen. All MC placentas are routinely injected with colored dye as part of standard care in our center, photographed and the images digitally stored for computer analysis using Image J version 1.57. Fetal territories were distinguished by the margins of the twin‐specific dyes and expressed as a percentage of the total placental surface [12].

Ultrasounds with biometric measurements were performed by obstetricians and sonographers with high expertise in MC twins, with a standard frequency of once every 2 weeks at our center for both uncomplicated MC twins and MC twins with sFGR. Ultrasound with biometric measurements of both fetuses closest to birth was selected. EFW was calculated using the Hadlock formula based on biometric measurements of head circumference (HC), AC, and femur length (FL), when all three measurements were available [13]. The EFWD was calculated as (EFW larger twin—EFW smaller twin)/EFW larger twin × 100%. An EFWD of ≥ 20% within 14 or 7 days before birth was considered discordant antenatal growth.

The primary outcome was the diagnostic accuracy of an EFWD ≥ 20% measured within 14 and 7 days of birth in predicting BWD ≥ 20% in MC twins. We estimated the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, positive likelihood ratio (i.e., true positives/false positives) and negative likelihood ratio (i.e., false negatives/true negatives) of an EFWD ≥ 20% for detecting a BWD ≥ 20%. Additionally, to further evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of an EFWD ≥ 20%, we constructed a confusion matrix comprising true positives, false positives and false negatives. We calculated the proportion of twins with an absolute under‐ or overestimation of BWD of a clinically relevant error of ≥ 10% within 14 days of birth using the absolute error of BWD, defined as (BWD—EFWD), subdivided into uncomplicated twins and sFGR twins. Furthermore, in twin pairs with available biometric measurements within 7 days of birth, the correlations between BWD and (1) EFWD and (2) a discordance in each of the separate biometric measurements (AC, HC, and FL; calculated as measurement larger twin—measurement smaller twin/measurement larger twin × 100%) were explored.

Secondary outcomes were analyzed in a subgroup of twins with biometric measurements available within 3 days of birth to minimize the confounding effect of the potential deflection in growth of the smaller twin during the last days of birth. In this subgroup, we analyzed the tendency to under‐ or overestimate BWD using the absolute error of BWD, subdivided into uncomplicated twins and sFGR twins. In addition, we visualized the distributions of the absolute error of BWD per gestational age at birth, subdivided into uncomplicated twins and sFGR twins. Furthermore, the mean estimation error of the EFW was calculated as (birthweight—EFW)/birthweight × 100%, and calculated separately for the smaller and larger twins, subdivided for uncomplicated twins and sFGR twins.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, United States). Data are presented as median (interquartile ranges [IQR]), mean (standard deviation [SD]), or n/N (%). To calculate sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, positive likelihood ratio and negative likelihood ratio, 2 × 2 tables and standard formulas for binominal proportions were used, including the Wilson's interval method or the 95% CI [14]. A Pearson correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the correlation between BWD and EFWD as well as discordances in individual biometric measurements within the Hadlock 3 formula (AC, HC, and FL). The confusion matrix outcomes were presented in a before‐after plot, which depicts the EFWD with corresponding BWD per twin pair, using GraphPad Prism version 5.00 for Windows (GraphPad software, San Diego, California, United States). Additionally, the absolute error of BWD per gestational age at birth was explored using a scatterplot. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Population

In total, 1633 MC twin pregnancies received antenatal care at our center. After applying the exclusion criteria, 347 pregnancies had an ultrasound with biometric measurements available within 14 days of birth. Of these twin pregnancies, 213/347 (61%) were uncomplicated and 134/347 (39%) were complicated by sFGR (Figure 1).

Uncomplicated twins were born at a median gestational age of 36.0 (IQR 33.6–36.4) weeks, while sFGR twins were born at a median gestational age of 32.7 (IQR 30.4–35.3) weeks (Table 1). Median time between the last ultrasound and birth was 7 (IQR 4–11) days for uncomplicated twins and 6 (IQR 2–9) days for sFGR twins. Ultrasound Within 14 days of Birth

The accuracy of sonographic EFWD ≥ 20% using the last ultrasound before birth in predicting a BWD ≥ 20% within 14 days of birth is depicted in Table 2 and Supporting Information S1: Table S2. Of the 134 twin pairs with a BWD ≥ 20%, 112 twin pairs also had an EFWD ≥ 20%, indicating a sensitivity of 84% (95% CI 76–89). Of the 213 pregnancies with a BWD < 20%, 33 had an EFWD ≥ 20%, indicating a specificity of 85% (95% CI 79–89). The positive predictive value of EFWD ≥ 20% was 77% (95% CI 69–84) and the negative predictive value was 89% (95% CI 84–93). The positive likelihood ratio was 5.4 and the negative likelihood ratio was 0.2. Ultrasound Within 7 days of Birth

Ultrasounds were available within 7 days of birth in 53% (112/213) of uncomplicated twin pairs and 60% (80/134) of sFGR twins (Table 2 and Supporting Information S1: Table S3). Out of 80 twin pairs with a BWD ≥ 20%, 68 pregnancies had an EFWD ≥ 20%, leading to a sensitivity of 85% (95% CI 75–92). Out of 112 twin pairs with a BWD < 20%, 21 pregnancies had an EFWD ≥ 20%, leading to a specificity of 81% (95% CI 73–88). The positive predictive value of an EFWD ≥ 20% was 76% (95% CI 66–84) and the negative predictive value was 88% (95% CI 80–94). The positive and negative likelihood ratios were 4.5 and 0.2, respectively. We found a positive correlation between EFWD and BWD (Pearson correlation coefficient 0.851, p < 0.001). In addition, we found a positive correlation between discordance in each of the separate biometric measurements and BWD (HC (n = 125), AC (n = 192) and FL (n = 141); Pearson correlation coefficient 0.647, 0.737, and 0.697, respectively; p < 0.0001).

Ultrasound Within 3 days of Birth

The subgroup of twins with available ultrasound within 3 days of birth consisted of 43 uncomplicated twin pairs and 47 twin pairs with sFGR. The mean absolute error of BWD was −2.0% (SD 8.1) for the uncomplicated twins and −2.0% (SD 8.0) for sFGR twins, denoting an overall overestimation of 2% points in both groups. The scatterplot in Figure 3 depicts the absolute error of BWD per gestational age at birth, subdivided into uncomplicated twins and sFGR twins. In the uncomplicated twins, the mean estimation error of EFW was 1.7% (SD 9.1) for the smaller twin and −1.1% (SD 9.6) for the larger twin, indicating an underestimation of actual birthweight in the smaller twin and an overestimation in the larger twin (Table 3). Similarly, the mean estimation error of EFW in the sFGR twins was 1.3% (SD 10.0) for the smaller twin and −2.3% (SD 8.2) for the larger twin.

Discussion

Main Findings

This study provides a comprehensive overview of the diagnostic accuracy of EFWD ≥ 20% using sonographic biometric measurements to predict BWD ≥ 20% (i.e., sFGR) in MC twin pregnancies. Ultrasound measurements within 14 and 7 days of birth demonstrated sensitivities of 84% and 85%, respectively, and specificity of 85% and 81%, respectively. BWD is overestimated by an average of 2% points within 3 days of birth for both uncomplicated and sFGR twins. This is primarily attributed to an overestimation of the larger twin, with a slight underestimation of the smaller twin.

Interpretation of the Results

Our diagnostic accuracy of EFWD ≥ 20% in predicting BWD ≥ 20% partly aligns with previous research, reporting varying sensitivities and specificities of 51%–79% and 86%–95%, respectively [5, 6, 15, 16, 17]. The lower sensitivity in other studies may be attributed to extended time intervals between ultrasound and birth (up to 28 days), lack of distinction in chorionicity, or a tendency to underestimate BWD, resulting in an increased prevalence of false negatives and lower sensitivity [5, 15]. However, there is no consensus in the literature whether EFWD over‐ or underestimates actual BWD in growth discordant twins [15, 18]. The higher sensitivity observed in our study (84%–85%) may be explained by the absolute overestimation of BWD of 2% points, leading to an increased likelihood of correctly identifying true positives. Additionally, sonographic assessments in our center were carried out by obstetricians and sonographers with expertise in MC twin pregnancies.

A study examining the performance of multiple EFW formulas in twins, including those specific to twins, found that formulas combining HC, AC and FL performed better in sonographic prediction of birthweight and BWD than those including only one or two of these measurements [16]. Consistent with this finding, we found that EFWD was the most reliable predictor of BWD when compared to discordances in separate biometric measurements (HC, AC and FL).

Clinical Implications

Although we demonstrated high sensitivities and specificities of EFWD ≥ 20% in predicting BWD ≥ 20%, in clinical practice, it is important to take into account the false positive and false negative rates of 16%. False positives (i.e., overestimation of EFWD) could potentially result in an earlier delivery, which may increase the risk of neonatal complications associated with prematurity. Conversely, false negatives (i.e., underestimation of EFWD) could lead to a minimized fetal surveillance and delayed delivery, which is associated with an increased risk of fetal mortality [19]. However, it remains unclear whether a high sensitivity, resulting in a higher proportion of twins monitored, outweighs the disadvantages of false positives.

No definitive conclusions can be drawn with regard to the influence of gestational age on absolute error of BWD as presented in Figure 3. There are numerous confounding factors which may influence the decision to prematurely terminate the pregnancy and subsequently lower the gestational age at birth, thereby precluding reliable statistical analysis in our current retrospective study design. These include the EFW of the smaller twin, the magnitude of measured EFWD, and the potential medical interference by the attending physician.

When comparing the individual EFWs, the overestimation of the larger twin was greater than the underestimation of the smaller twin, possibly because sonographers tend to focus more on the smaller twin. Additionally, in singleton pregnancies, FGR fetuses are known to be underestimated compared to appropriately‐grown or large for gestational age fetuses [20].

Compared with singleton pregnancies, sonographic biometric measurements in twin pregnancies come with different challenges. A recent study revealed that the accuracy of EFW measurements in twin pregnancies (n = 2154) was not affected by fetal sex, maternal body mass index or gestational diabetes [21]. Independent risk factors for inaccuracy of EFW consisted of an earlier gestational age at ultrasound, being the non‐presenting twin in the uterus and being the smaller twin. Notably, they found that the false positive rate of an BWD ≥ 20% was twice as high when the smaller twin was in the presenting position in the uterus compared to the larger twin (35% vs. 17%).

Research Implications

Prospective studies are needed to further evaluate the impact of maternal, obstetrical and twin‐specific factors on the accuracy of EFWD in twin pregnancies, such as the position of the fetuses in the uterus and amniotic fluid imbalances. A better understanding of the influence of these factors can help improve the accuracy of EFWD measurements in MC twin pregnancies, which can in turn improve antenatal management.

In case of retrospective studies on EFWD, it is important to consider that EFWD remains an estimate with a margin of error, whereas BWD is the true intertwin growth difference in utero. Therefore, we believe that the use of the postnatal criterium of a BWD ≥ 20% is a more accurate measure of whether sFGR was present in the retrospective study design, rather than solely using antenatal diagnostic criteria.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. Firstly, as a national referral center for complicated MC twin pregnancies, there may be selection toward more severe sFGR pregnancies, potentially resulting in a higher sensitivity. Secondly, the wide inclusion period (2002–2023) means that different ultrasound machines were used by multiple sonographers, which may have introduced unreported biases, including interobserver variability. Nevertheless, this may enhance the translatability of the findings into clinical practice. Moreover, it is important to note that our findings are based on the last available sonographic biometric measurements per twin pair. In clinical practice, however, biometric measurements are placed within the context of the antenatal growth curve of each fetus, thereby providing a more nuanced evaluation. However, as a national referral center for complicated MC twin pregnancies with a team of highly trained and experienced obstetricians and sonographers, we were able to provide a comprehensive overview of the diagnostic accuracy of BWD ≥ 20%, and quantify the over‐ and underestimation of both birthweight and BWD in MC twins within a short time interval of birth.

Conclusion

We found a relatively high diagnostic accuracy of sonographic EFWD ≥ 20% for the determination of BWD ≥ 20% (i.e., postnatal diagnosis of sFGR) in MC twin pregnancies, with a mean overestimation of BWD of 2% points. However, using EFWD ≥ 20% for diagnosing sFGR can lead to false positives and false negatives, both occurring in approximately 16% of MC twin pregnancies. Therefore, in clinical practice, we recommend combining EFWD with other antenatal criteria when measured at approximately 20% to facilitate adequate risk assessment and determining the timing of delivery. For (retrospective) research purposes, we recommend using the actual BWD as a true expression of the intertwin growth difference in utero.
