Successful mechanical thrombectomy of a large venous graft thrombus burden using a novel device in an unusual way: a case report

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMechanical thrombectomy (mTE) is an established standard of care for selected patients with acute ischaemic stroke. Introduction

Mechanical thrombectomy (mTE) is an established standard of care for selected patients with acute ischaemic stroke, as recommended by guidelines such as those from the AHA.1 The procedure is typically performed using a stent retriever—a self-expanding stent delivered via a microcatheter—which captures and removes the thrombus upon expansion. However, a recent study comparing thrombaspiration and stent retriever techniques for mTE found no significant differences in recanalization rates, need for bailout therapy, or favourable 90-day outcomes on the Modified Rankin Scale.2

In the cardiac equivalent of stroke—acute coronary syndrome (ACS)—guidelines do not recommend mTE. This is based on studies showing no mortality benefit of thrombaspiration, along with an increased risk of stroke.3,4 These studies primarily investigated aspiration catheters with two lumens: one for guidewire passage and a larger lumen for thrombus extraction from the culprit coronary artery. To our knowledge, no trials have assessed the use of stent retrievers in ACS.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 76-year-old male who presented to our emergency department with ACS. His cardiovascular risk factors included hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, and obesity. The patient had a history of coronary artery disease and had undergone coronary artery bypass grafting in 2013, receiving a left internal mammary artery graft to the left anterior descending artery, a right internal mammary artery T-graft to the circumflex artery (CX), and an aortocoronary venous graft to the posterior descending artery (PDA).

On physical examination, his blood pressure was elevated (155/90 mmHg), and he exhibited mild bradycardia (55 b.p.m.) with an oxygen saturation of 97%. Heart and lung auscultation revealed normal findings, and his electrocardiogram showed no abnormalities (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1). Laboratory tests indicated an elevated high-sensitivity troponin T (hs-TnT, 483.0 ng/L), while creatine kinase (CK) and CK-MB levels were within normal limits. Echocardiography demonstrated a normal left ventricular ejection fraction without regional wall motion abnormalities.

Given the typical symptoms and elevated troponin, an urgent coronary angiography was performed for suspected non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction. The angiogram revealed a chronic total occlusion of the CX graft and a subacute occlusion of the venous graft to the PDA (Figure 1). An initial attempt at percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of the native right coronary artery (RCA) was unsuccessful due to an inability to cross the severely calcified subtotal stenosis in the mid RCA with microcatheters or balloons. Consequently, we proceeded with PCI of the venous graft.

Coronary angiography showing the left coronary system with retrograde filling of the right coronary artery (A); a patent left internal mammary artery graft to the left anterior descending artery (B); a native right coronary artery with subtotal mid-segment stenosis, uncrossable with microcatheters (C); and a subacutely occluded aortocoronary venous graft to the posterior descending artery (D).

Due to a significant thrombus burden, only balloon angioplasty was performed, and conventional thrombus aspiration using an aspiration catheter (ASAP®, Aspiration Catheter & Kit 5.2F, Merit Medical Systems, South Jordan, UT, USA) was attempted but proved insufficient. Although perfusion was restored with TIMI-II flow, a large residual thrombus burden remained (Figure 2). Therefore, a bolus and continuous infusion of tirofiban were administered, and the patient was monitored in the intensive care unit.

Coronary angiography showing the aortocoronary venous graft to the posterior descending artery with well-demarcated large thrombus formations (→) following initial recanalization by balloon angioplasty and conventional thrombus aspiration (A) and on the following day after continuous tirofiban infusion (B).

The following day, repeat coronary angiography showed no significant reduction in thrombus burden despite tirofiban therapy. A second attempt with the ASAP thrombus aspiration catheter was unsuccessful. Consequently, we opted for a novel mTE device (enVast Coronary Thrombectomy System, Vesalio, USA). However, this system requires a 0.021″ microcatheter, which was not available in our inventory. Since we had previously used the ASAP catheter, which has a larger inner lumen, we repurposed it as a microcatheter. Using this setup, we performed two passes through the thrombus burden, successfully removing a large thrombus mass (Figures 3 and 4; Supplementary material online, Videos S1 and S2).

Coronary angiography showing the enVast stent retriever positioned in the distal segment of the venous graft (A) and the retrieved thrombi following the first (B) and second mechanical thrombectomy (C). Arrows indicate the radiopaque markers: distal tip with solid arrows, drop zone markers with dotted arrows, and proximal marker with dashed arrows.

Coronary angiography showing the enVast stent retriever (dotted arrows) and the ASAP catheter (solid arrow), which, due to its larger inner lumen, was repurposed as a microcatheter.

The angiogram after thrombus removal showed a proximal graft stenosis (∼70%), requiring implantation of a 4.0 × 38 mm drug-eluting stent. Post-dilation with a 4.0 × 12 mm non-compliant balloon achieved a good final angiographic result (Figure 5; Supplementary material online, Video S3).

Follow-up

The patient was continued on dual platelet treatment with 100 mg aspirin and 5 mg prasugrel per day, and his further clinical course was uneventful during the next 2 months.

Discussion

Patients with ACS and angiographic evidence of a large thrombus burden present a significant challenge in interventional cardiology. Peripheral embolization of thrombotic material is a major cause of the no-reflow phenomenon during acute myocardial infarction, which is associated with increased mortality and a higher incidence of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Large thrombus burdens and repeated balloon dilations are key predictors of no-reflow.

For many years, thrombus aspiration was routinely performed during acute interventions, particularly in ST-elevation myocardial infarctions, as a strategy to mitigate no-reflow. However, randomized trials in the mid-2010s demonstrated no clinical benefit and, importantly, an increased risk of stroke associated with thrombus aspiration.3,4 As a result, both European and American guideline recommendations were adjusted accordingly.

Nevertheless, many interventionalists report positive clinical experiences with thrombus aspiration, with only rare cases of stroke as a complication. Consequently, selective use of this technique persists in certain cases. The key to patient benefit lies in appropriate patient selection and proper execution of the procedure—factors that cannot always be fully captured in large randomized multicentre trials. While routine aspiration in all infarct patients is not advisable, there are specific scenarios where it remains a valuable tool.

Patient selection for thrombus extraction

It should be emphasized that the approach described in this case does not reflect the current standard of care and is not yet supported by scientific evidence; moreover, the enVast device itself is still under investigation in the ACS setting. Previous randomized trials have failed to demonstrate a clinical benefit of routine thrombectomy in acute myocardial infarction, likely due to the unselective application of the technique across broad patient populations. Our case underscores the importance of careful patient selection, as thrombectomy may provide significant benefit in highly selected scenarios. Specifically, vein grafts with a substantial thrombus burden pose a unique challenge: the absence of side branches facilitates the formation of long, extensive thrombi that, if left untreated, are prone to peripheral embolization, resulting in slow flow or complete no-reflow. Which patients, moreover, are most likely to benefit from thrombus extraction? Clinical experience suggests that aspiration is particularly useful in patients with large, localized, and fresh thrombus burdens where direct stent implantation is suboptimal due to lesion complexity or the risk of distal embolization. In our experience, thrombectomy in vein grafts with a substantial thrombus burden is best approached with an initial attempt using an aspiration catheter, with escalation to mTE if required; in native vessels, such an approach may be considered in the presence of at least TIMI thrombus grade III. Grade III is defined as definite thrombus with a greatest linear dimension exceeding half but less than two vessel diameters.5 However, for this specific scenario, conducting adequately powered prospective randomized trials is challenging, given the relatively low incidence and heterogeneity of such cases.

In recent years, novel mechanical aspiration systems have been developed, evaluated in small studies, and even incorporated into randomized prospective trials. Early experiences, including those with the enVast mTE device used in our case, suggest a potential resurgence of extraction-based techniques in interventional cardiology.

Key insights from our case

Our case highlights that stent retrievers can be effective in coronary interventions. Without the use of mTE, it seems unlikely that a durable perfusion of the venous bypass graft would have been restored. The most used microcatheters in interventional cardiology are not compatible to the enVast mTE device due to a too small inner lumen. An innovative adaptation of the technique—repurposing an aspiration catheter as a microcatheter due to the unavailability of a compatible device—proved highly effective. This approach not only facilitated successful clot retrieval but also provided an additional safety mechanism by allowing simultaneous aspiration during device withdrawal, reducing the risk of thrombus embolization. Moreover, this strategy enables a stepwise escalation: initial aspiration can be attempted as a first-line, less invasive, and resource-efficient option, with progression to the enVast mTE device if necessary.

Selection of the retrieval stent size is an important practical consideration. The enVast device is available in sizes from 4.0/30 mm to 6.0/35 mm, and sizing is guided by the vessel diameter and the length of the thrombotic segment. Matching the device to the approximate vessel size ensures effective thrombus engagement while maintaining procedural safety. In our case, angiography indicated that a 4.0 mm retrieval stent was suitable for the vein graft anatomy.

Clear contraindications for retrieval stent use have not been defined in the neurovascular literature, and no coronary-specific contraindications exist to date. However, by analogy with coronary aspiration thrombectomy studies (e.g. the TOTAL trial),3 an inability to safely and coaxially intubate the target vessel with a guiding catheter should be considered a practical contraindication, as procedural risks may outweigh potential benefits. This aspect becomes increasingly relevant as the technique transitions into broader coronary application.

Our modified approach used a single guidewire rather than the conventional dual-wire technique. Procedural safety can be further enhanced by selecting a guiding catheter that permits stable, deep seating in the proximal coronary artery during device extraction without increasing the risk of vessel dissection. After thrombectomy, thorough aspiration of the guiding catheter and repeated flushing of the Y-connector are essential to prevent embolization of retained thrombus fragments.

Overall procedural precautions align with standard principles of coronary mTE. Careful catheter manipulation under continuous angiographic guidance remains critical to minimize vessel injury or perforation. Given the limited clinical experience with coronary use of retrieval stents, markedly tortuous or sharply angulated segments may represent relative contraindications due to increased mechanical stress and navigation complexity. These considerations should guide patient and lesion selection for this evolving technique.

Furthermore, cerebral protection devices are not required when using the enVast mTE device. There is no evidence of clinical benefit in this setting; even in transcatheter aortic valve implantation, where such devices have been more extensively studied, results remain inconclusive. Consequently, cerebral protection is not recommended or routine practice during coronary mTE.

Intravascular imaging was not performed, as the thrombus burden and mechanism of stenosis were clearly identifiable on angiography, which is often sufficient in degenerative vein graft disease. A distal protection device was also not used because no relevant residual thrombus remained after thrombectomy and the risk of embolization during stenting was low. Current guidelines do not recommend routine distal protection in vein graft interventions, and its use has become uncommon in practice.

Conclusion

As mTE devices continue to evolve, their role in interventional cardiology may expand beyond traditional thrombus aspiration techniques. Our case demonstrates that innovative adaptations of existing devices can provide effective alternatives in challenging scenarios. Further research, including larger trials, is needed to define the optimal indications and refine the procedural techniques for mTE in coronary interventions.
