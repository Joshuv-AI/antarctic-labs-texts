Drive with me: Utilizing car-based mobile interviews to investigate place and health
Authors: Grace M. Savard, Mallory A.P. Sagehorn, Desiree L. Alvarez-McNelis, Michael H. Esposito, Jessica M. Finlay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While mobile “go-along” methods are increasingly used to understand and conceptualize the interconnections between health and place, most mobile interviews are conducted on foot. This paper investigates how mobile interviews within cars can advance knowledge of nuanced neighborhood navigation and local place-based features that influence health and well-being. The Neighborhoods and Health at All Ages Study interviewed sixty participants between August 2023 and March 2024. Living across the Minneapolis-St. Paul (MN) metropolitan area, participants were on average 42 years old (range: 23-75). 53% identified as female, 40% male, and 7% non-binary; 22% identified as Hispanic, 22% Asian, 18% non-Hispanic White, 17% Multiracial, 15% Black/African American, 3% American Indian/Alaska Native, and 3% Other. Among 56 participants who completed a mobile interview, nine (aged 23-69) chose to participate by car. The car-based sub-sample forms the primary focus and comparative participant group for this case study analysis. Thematic analysis identified how cars operate as catalysts for novel insights into participants’ built and social environments. Driving together yielded nuanced and broadened perspectives into local services, amenities, and hazards that shape health and well-being. Traveling by car was a highly accessible modality, thereby reducing barriers to participation and enabling environmental exploration amidst inclement weather and other socio-spatial risks. Illustrated through rich participant vignettes, this paper highlights how driving as a mobile interview modality can advance multifaceted, multi-scalar, and relational knowledge of social and behavioral pathways linking local environments and health. Novel findings can support critical methodological advancement and inform meaningful community-level policies and interventions.

Introduction

Health is deeply rooted in place. The built and social environments where we grow, learn, work, socialize, and play often have a far greater impact on health than genetics or access to healthcare (Bambra et al., 2019; Braveman & Gottlieb, 2014). While local environments and health are intricately connected through several well-established conceptual frameworks (e.g., Diez Roux, 2001; Diez Roux & Mair, 2010; National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities, 2025; Solar & Irwin, 2010), studies are often methodologically limited in how they explicitly capture and measure this complex relationship. Advancing knowledge in this area requires critical consideration of existing approaches to broaden and bridge established practices and develop new methodological applications.

Researchers increasingly use mobile methods to analyze nuanced experiences of self and place (Arntson & Yoon, 2023; Evans & Jones, 2011; Feldman et al., 2020; Finlay & Bowman, 2017; Versey, 2022; Xie et al., 2025). Most often walking interviews and other “go-along” modalities (Kusenbach, 2003), these approaches employ movement through space to elicit insights that might remain hidden in stationary (or seated) interviews (Brown & Durrheim, 2009; Carpiano, 2009). By situating conversations within participants’ everyday environments, mobile methods help researchers access embodied, sensory, and affective dimensions of place attachment and navigation. Mobile interviews—often paired with complementary traditional stationary interviews—are participant-guided tours through their homes, neighborhoods, and/or communities. Participants usually determine the route, duration, and mode of travel, while researchers listen, follow, and observe (Finlay & Bowman, 2017; Kusenbach, 2003).

To date, most mobile interviews have been conducted on foot (e.g., Butler & Derrett, 2014; Carder et al., 2023; Chaudhury et al., 2025; Finlay et al., 2015; Garcia et al., 2012; Holton & Riley, 2014). While walking interviews provide on-the-ground insights into participants’ surrounding environments, walking is not always feasible. Mobility challenges, low-density neighborhoods, suburban or rural landscapes, and safety or weather concerns can limit their practicality. In the United States, for example, only about 16% of adults reported walking for transportation in a one-week period (U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024), and about nine in ten households report access to at least one vehicle (Valentine, 2025). Car commuting is widespread, with over three-quarters of workers driving in personal vehicles to work (Schaeffer, 2024). Similar car-dominant commuter patterns exist in countries such as Canada (Statistics Canada, 2024), the United Kingdom (UK Department for Transport, 2025), China, Germany, Brazil, and South Korea (Armstrong, 2022), underscoring the relevance of car-based mobile methods in the U.S. and broader global contexts.

Recent studies have begun to integrate car-based research as a tool for learning about participants’ lived experiences and local environments. Lynch (2024) describes shared car journeys as producing distinctive rhythms of movement that invite spontaneous reflection and observation, thereby extending the logic of walking interviews to mobilities by car. Dahl and Tjora (2023) highlight the socio-material dynamics of “riding shotgun,” where seat position, visibility, and in-car technologies reshape researcher-participant relations and raise ethical and practical considerations. Yang and colleagues (2023) conceptualize the car as an “embodied habitation,” constituted through driving, gazing, listening, and communicating. They emphasize the multi-sensory ways people inhabit and experience car-based mobility. Collectively, this emerging body of literature suggests that car-based mobile interviews not only provide practical advantages for data collection but also present new methodological and conceptual possibilities for understanding how people encounter, interpret, and move through place.

This article builds on recent methodological advances to investigate how mobile interviews within cars can broaden knowledge of place-based navigation as well as local features that influence health and well-being. Illustrated through a case study analysis of car-based mobile interviews interwoven with rich participant narratives, maps, and comparisons with walking mobile interviews, we highlight the unique value of car-based mobile interviews in health research. We call attention to how car-based mobile interviews are positioned to capture important insights into participants’ broader local environments, often beyond the neighborhood scale. While neighborhoods are a critical component of the built and social environment, additional scales and spatial resolutions matter for health and well-being. Cars extend the capacity of mobile interviews to yield novel insights into key local and broader community-level features. Furthermore, offering car-based mobile interviews as an option for participants can importantly enhance accessibility of mobile methods research, thereby broadening participation to capture diverse perspectives from individuals with varying mobility needs in situ (Kusenbach, 2003). Finally, analyzing car-based mobile interviews alongside walking mobile interviews allowed our team to capture the everyday, complex, and multi-scalar relational patterns of mobility that often blend walking and driving. By critically assessing the benefits and drawbacks of car-based mobile interviewing, this article extends applied qualitative methods and demonstrates how this approach can generate novel, policy-relevant knowledge about the relationships between place and health.

Methods

The Neighborhoods and Health at All Ages Study was conducted in the Minneapolis-St. Paul (MN) metropolitan area between August 2023 and March 2024. Institutional Review Boards at the University of Colorado Boulder (#23-0225) and University of Minnesota (STUDY00019066) approved this study. The research team represented a range of interdisciplinary identities, positionalities, and training in public health, public policy, geography, sociology, and gerontology. We used purposive sampling (Campbell et al., 2020) to recruit a diverse sample of sixty participants across age, gender, race/ethnicity, geographies (Supplemental Fig. 1), and other identities. Further methodological details are available in Finlay et al. (2026). The research team obtained informed consent from all individuals at the beginning of each interview. All participants completed semi-structured stationary interviews and were invited to participate in a complementary mobile interview as well.

Participants chose the mobile interview route, duration, and whether they wanted to complete the mobile interview by foot or by car. Because two researchers were typically present during each interview, one researcher focused on asking questions and engaging with the participant while the other took photos of the surrounding environment and detailed handwritten notes. For car-based mobile interviews, the research team used either a researcher’s car or the participant’s car. During pre-interview communication and scheduling, researchers explained the premise of mobile interviews (i.e., a brief 15 to 20-min walk or drive around their local area) and prompted participants to consider in advance which sites they would like to tour. Researchers asked participants to think about local services, amenities, hazards, or features that they found particularly impactful (both positively and negatively).

Mobile interviews were designed to be complementary to stationary interviews, which were most often conducted immediately prior to the mobile interview and audio recorded. Mobile interviews were not audio recorded in an effort to minimize participant burden and encourage candid conversation in situ (Caronia, 2015; Lee, 2004; Marshall et al., 2021; Nordstrom, 2015; Rutakumwa et al., 2020). The research team collected mobile interview data using a variety of structured methods. In addition to taking notes and photos during interviews, researchers used a geospatial fitness tracking application to create a GPS route of the interview. Following each car-based mobile interview, researchers compiled a “mobile transcript”—a highly detailed, comprehensive written description of what researchers saw and experienced during the interview (Supplemental Table 1). Researchers also completed separate individual fieldnotes to capture subjective observations and preliminary analyses (Supplemental Table 2). GPS routes, photos, mobile transcripts, and fieldnotes were organized and stored alongside stationary interview materials in a secure online database.

All authors employed thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2021) to investigate the methodological opportunities, strengths, and limitations of car-based mobile interviews for place and health research. Following Braun and Clarke’s six phases, author GS (1) familiarized herself with the multimodal data by repeatedly reading mobile transcripts and fieldnotes and reviewing maps, photos, and stationary interview transcripts; (2) generated initial codes related to mobility, spatial experience, and health-related contexts; and (3) collated codes into candidate themes with JF. Following this, all authors (4) reviewed and refined themes and (5) defined and named themes to capture key dimensions of car-based mobile interview dynamics. Next, GS (6) produced an analytic narrative that integrated themes alongside annotated maps and interpretive insights, and all co-authors provided edits, additions, and revisions. Throughout the process, all authors engaged in iterative discussion, reflexive notetaking, and peer debriefing to enhance the credibility and trustworthiness of the analysis (Marshall et al., 2021).

Results

Participants were on average 42 years old (range: 23-75) and lived across the Minneapolis-St. Paul (MN) metropolitan area (Table 1; Supplemental Table 3). Just over half (53%) identified as female, 40% male, and 7% non-binary; 22% identified as Hispanic, 22% Asian, 18% non-Hispanic White, 17% Multiracial, 15% Black/African American, 3% American Indian/Alaska Native, and 3% Other. Among 60 total participants, 56 participants completed a mobile interview, and nine of these individuals (aged 23-69) chose to participate in a car-based mobile interview. Among these nine car-based mobile interviews, five participants drove their own car and researchers drove their car for four.

Thematic results highlight how car-based mobile interviews uniquely and meaningfully advance knowledge of place, health, and well-being. Cars offered a distinctive research space to learn and listen alongside participants. Operating as catalysts for novel insight, cars broadened understanding of participants’ built and social environments. Researchers and participants could drive to, visit, and observe key services, amenities, and hazards often inaccessible by walking during the allotted mobile interview time. Traveling by car was a highly accessible modality that reduced barriers to participation, accommodated participants with various mobility needs, and enabled neighborhood exploration amidst inclement weather. Moreover, because most participants navigated their local environments by driving and walking at various times, offering both modalities during this study and analyzing their respective merits more accurately reflected participants’ embodied and emplaced daily routines and experiences.

Driving as a catalyst for novel insights

Car-based mobile interviews generate unique insights through the flexibility of movement provided by vehicles. For example, two participants lived in the same university-adjacent Minneapolis neighborhood. Comparing their mobile interviews illustrates how driving as a modality brings otherwise overlooked spatial experiences and environmental influences into data collection for analytical consideration. Fernando—a 29-year-old Hispanic man and graduate student—completed a walking mobile interview (1.85 miles, 25 minutes), while Arjun—a 24-year-old Asian man and also a graduate student—completed a car-based mobile interview (2.97 miles, 15 minutes). As illustrated in Fig. 1, although Fernando and Arjun lived in the same neighborhood, the insights from each interview were distinct from one another and highly dependent on the mobility selected.

During Arjun’s stationary interview, we learned that he was a frequent public transit user who relies on local buses and the metro light rail to reach campus and other locations around his neighborhood. Arjun wanted to show our team his preferred transit routes and commuting patterns directly, and the car-based mobile interview allowed him to do so. Arjun sat in the passenger seat while DAM drove, and GS took photos and notes from the back seat. In our 15-minute drive together he pointed out his sequence of bus stops as well as the metro stop he frequents. Walking to see all of Arjun’s key commuting landmarks would have taken over an hour and would not have been feasible within the time constraints of the mobile interview. In contrast, Fernando walked us past key proximally located services and amenities, such as a bench he sits on during walks and the movie theater he frequents weekly. He also guided researchers down the nearby river boulevard, noting how he enjoys ambient contact with others who were also outdoors. These precise details about how Fernando interacts with his local environment emerged organically while walking, whereas driving likely would have elicited different, potentially broader geographic insights.

There were a variety of practical factors to consider between walking and car-based mobile interviews. In Fernando’s case, though we saw less geographically in a longer timeframe, we observed the specific and intricate ways that he engages with his surroundings. Fernando’s walking mobile interview yielded deeply contextual insights into his neighborhood perspectives and direct interactions with those he encounters. Alternatively, with Arjun we saw more geographically, but at a lower resolution. While a walking mobile interview may have provided more spatially specific insights, driving together allowed Arjun to show us how the expansive network of transit routes, local landmarks, and amenities, services, and hazards around him impacts his daily life and well-being.

Participants often spontaneously remembered local environmental features they wanted to show our team members during mobile interviews. Significant unexpected route changes were typically not possible during walking interviews due to time constraints, but major rerouting by car could be facilitated promptly. Leveraging the car’s mobility, we could quickly adjust destinations based on participant guidance. This was particularly helpful when participants had not thought about specific stops in advance of the interview. For instance, during Arjun’s interview, we drove to his university and were heading back to his apartment when he suddenly suggested detouring to a local bridge. With minimal explanation from Arjun, DAM made a few turns, and we arrived at the bridge’s entryway. There, Arjun shared valuable insights about one of his favorite stress-relieving strategies: walking on—and occasionally venturing beyond—this bridge (Fig. 2). Car-based mobile interviews supported this spontaneous remembering (Robin et al., 2019), allowing us to capture rich perspectives that may have surfaced only through serendipitous travel via car.

In addition to the mobile capacities of cars, when we (the researchers) rode in participants’ cars, the vehicles themselves served as extensions of the participant’s home environment. Cars are a medium through which people live their lives, and the objects and artifacts inside participants’ cars offered key novel insights into how people organized their lives to support their health and well-being. For example, photos of loved ones or fliers for local events revealed where and with whom participants spent their time. Observing the car’s interior arrangement and participants’ belongings within the car helped us ask more informed questions during the car-based mobile interview.

On a chilly winter morning, authors DAM and GS conducted a car-based mobile interview with Ella, a 57-year-old Black woman living in a North Minneapolis neighborhood. Pressed for time and facing cold weather, Ella opted to drive us in her van for the mobile interview. Ella uses her van to access many services and amenities, and being invited into her van allowed our team to observe a deeply personal mobile space that Ella occupies often. It gave us the opportunity to glimpse a highly relevant modality through which Ella interacts with her local environment. Importantly, as we climbed into the back seat, we navigated around a large scooter (Fig. 3)—a detail that Ella had only briefly alluded to during her stationary interview. Riding in Ella’s vehicle prompted us to more deeply consider how her scooter use shapes her movements, embodied experiences, and advocacy efforts around the neighborhood. This fundamentally expanded our understanding of Ella’s navigational strategies and helped us better frame our research and analytical lens to consider how factors in the built environment, like paved sidewalks and safe crosswalks, might support her mobility and well-being.

Observing community services, amenities, and hazards beyond walking distance

Car-based mobile interviews enabled participants and researchers to visit health-impacting local sites inaccessible on foot within the time constraints of the mobile interview. This approach was particularly useful to explore suburban communities where services, amenities, and other community places were often miles apart from one another and not within easy walking distance.

Mary—a 65-year-old White woman—had lived in the same innerring suburb of Minneapolis throughout her life. During the stationary interview, we quickly learned that most of the services and amenities Mary accessed were not within walking distance of her home. As such, she typically drove herself to these locations. Given driving was her primary form of transportation, Mary chose to participate in a car-based mobile interview and drove two members of our research team around in her own car. While Mary’s car-based mobile interview lasted nearly an hour (significantly longer than most of other mobile interviews for this study), researchers were able to view, observe, and visit the sites that impact Mary’s life, health, and well-being.

Throughout the car-based mobile interview, Mary pointed out key areas that were personally meaningful to her (Fig. 4). Filled with moments of nostalgia and stories of lived experiences, the interview became a mobile re-telling of her personal history and relationship with particular beloved and significant spaces over the decades of her life. We started at a local plant store, a site that supports her cognitive, creative, and social health through her master gardening work. We drove past a few shopping areas, her current home as well as a few former homes, recreational areas, and through George Floyd Square. She was deeply knowledgeable about local landmarks and keen to share memories of growing up in her suburb and the broader Twin Cities metropolitan area. Across participants, car-based mobile interviews like Mary’s typically revealed a wider array of health-impacting sites and experiences than walking interviews alone and helped researchers better understand how a geographically dispersed network of everyday sites connects to health and well-being.

In contrast with car-based mobile interviews, we found that walking interviews with those living in first-ring suburbs often produced limited insights into the broad array of services and amenities used by participants, largely because these sites were located outside of participants’ proximal neighborhoods. For example, Mark—a 33-year-old male dentist—lived in a calm, quiet neighborhood within a southern Minneapolis suburb. During the walking interview, Mark explained that the scarcity of noise in the area around his home was essential to managing his stress and sleeping well. He appreciated the low levels of pedestrian and car traffic, safe atmosphere, and lack of crime in his neighborhood (Fig. 5). He also verbalized that he drives to most of the services and amenities he uses. While walking enabled us to feel, see, and appreciate the atmosphere of his neighborhood, we could not directly see the places outside his immediate neighborhood that he drives to and frequents in his daily life including a brewery, his workspace, and grocery stores.

Enhancing accessibility and accommodating participants’ needs

Car-based mobile interviews were importantly more accessible and accommodating for some participants, particularly when walking was not a feasible option due to prior injuries, illnesses, or disabilities. For Diane, a 69-year-old multiracial woman living in a western suburb of Minneapolis, walking was not feasible due to prior injuries. Rather than skipping the mobile interview altogether, having a car-based option allowed our research team to listen and observe the many ways Diane engages with her surrounding environment (Fig. 6).

For this interview, DAM drove, Diane sat in the passenger seat, and GS sat in the back seat capturing photographs and taking notes. Diane guided researchers along the frontage roads near her home, noting how useful they were for accessing nearby shops and restaurants. We stopped by a local cultural center where she often parks to look out over a scenic bike trail. She also pointed out a nearby gym she occasionally visits. A memorable moment with Diane was stopping at a small pond near a retail store, where she likes to park her car and watch the ducks and geese. She later took us to the parking lot of a nearby community recreation center, where she enjoys sitting within her car taking in the lush treescape and occasionally people watching. These moments at the pond and parking lot exemplified that Diane occasionally interacts with local sites within her car exclusively, and the car-based mobile interview provided a unique gateway into learning about how Diane experiences and views her community around her. Furthermore, Diane often organizes her outings to visit multiple services and amenities located near one another, thereby clustering her errands to save time and gas. The car-based mobile interview provided our team with opportunities to learn more about her strategies of when, where, and why she drove to certain sites. Ultimately, the car-based mobile interview allowed us to glimpse places that provided a sense of tranquility for Diane, while doing so in a way that was less physically taxing for her body. Since Diane always accessed these sites by car, the car-based mobile interview allowed us to travel through her community and witness key health-impacting sites via a modality which more closely mirrored her daily routine.

Cars also enabled neighborhood exploration during inclement or prohibitive weather. For this study, we conducted interviews between August and March to capture participants’ experiences throughout the changing seasons. Weather often shaped how individuals navigated places and recreational opportunities. Many participants were not interested in walking outside while it was raining, snowing, or cold, and instead chose to participate in a car-based mobile interview.

Pari, a 56-year-old Asian woman living in a South Minneapolis neighborhood, was eager to show DAM landmarks around her area. However, due to the frigid temperature and icy roads (Fig. 7), she opted to drive DAM around her community in her car instead of walking. Furthermore, she felt that walking the entire route would be “long and unpleasant.” Over a seven-mile drive lasting about 30 minutes, Pari pointed out walking trails she uses, shops and restaurants she frequents, and even a pile of community mulch provided by the city. She casually shared stories about accidentally wandering onto a private golf course, her annoyance with an unsightly storage building, and her appreciation for an old grain mill that reminds her of her love for agriculture and organic farming. Despite the uncomfortable weather, the car-based mobile interview allowed us to learn how local services, amenities, and sometimes overlooked community sites impact her daily life and well-being.

When poor weather made outdoor interviewing difficult, participants who lacked stable housing or the ability to invite researchers indoors often turned to cars—typically the researcher’s vehicle—as a practical and accommodating alternative. In these times, cars provided shelter from the weather and served as a reliable, accessible, safe, and private setting for interviews. For example, we interviewed Abigail, a 23-year-old Black woman living in a North Minneapolis neighborhood, on a bitterly cold December morning. The interview started sitting around a picnic table exposed to the elements (Fig. 8). A few minutes into the interview, Abigail—not wearing a winter coat—shared that she was struggling to focus because of the freezing temperatures. DAM spontaneously suggested moving into her car for the remainder of the stationary interview, and Abigail quickly and enthusiastically agreed. After moving to DAM’s car, we learned that Abigail was living in a shelter temporarily and balancing multiple jobs while remaining a committed college student. She explained that she did not have a private space where she could host the interview indoors, and as we would later see in her car-based mobile interview, there were no community sites available in the area. After finishing the stationary interview within the car, we drove around the neighborhood, and Abigail pointed out key features such as the local corner, her typical walking route, and the bus stop she uses frequently. Notably, the neighborhood lacked coffee shops, restaurants, libraries, and the other kinds of public spaces where most stationary interviews took place.

During car-based mobile interviews, cars helped cultivate an enhanced sense of comfort in environments that otherwise felt uneasy or risky to participants. Driving as a modality was particularly useful when participants felt unsafe navigating neighborhoods on foot, as the durability of cars seemed to create a protective barrier between the participants and researchers and the surrounding environment. This observation was particularly prominent in our conversation with Gamila, a 39-year-old Black woman living in a North Minneapolis neighborhood. During her preceding stationary interview, Gamila shared that she did not feel safe walking in certain areas near her home. She explained that her uneasiness stemmed from witnessing people experiencing homelessness shouting at pedestrians and from an incident when her car window was hit by a bullet while she and her children were in the car. She called the police, but they failed to arrive. Gamila shared that her family was fearful to leave the house for about a month following this traumatic experience. At the time of our interview, Gamila and her family intentionally arranged their schedules to be home by early evening and preferred to drive rather than walk whenever possible.

Though not verbalized explicitly, Gamila’s safety concerns about walking likely contributed to her decision to participate in a car-based mobile interview. Gamila asked to be driven in a researcher’s car, providing directional insight along the way. DAM drove while Gamila sat in the passenger seat, and GS sat in the back seat, taking notes and photographs as we moved around her neighborhood (Fig. 9). As we drove, Gamila took us to nearby services her family used, including her community library, a local park where her kids ride scooters, the preschool her children attend, a teen recreation and educational center, and an adult education hub. Within the walls of the car, Gamila appeared to feel quite safe, even directing us to drive down streets that she verbalized were dangerous for pedestrians. Our team observed the car to operate as a structural shield, thereby protecting Gamila and our team from the hazards she had perceived or experienced in her local environment. Based on our conversations together, the car seemed to cultivate a sense of security, and it is likely that the Gamila was comfortable pointing out local hazards—as well as amenities and services—because we were traveling via car rather than via foot.

Discussion

Car-based mobile interviews are a unique and often-overlooked qualitative methodology to illuminate the fluid and dynamic interconnections between place and health. Scholars of non-representational theory have emphasized the need to capture the active processes and practices of everyday life, arguing that people and places rarely exist passively, statically, and in isolation from one another (Andrews, 2018; Andrews et al., 2014; Simpson, 2017; Thrift, 2007). Cars offer a unique research modality to broaden our knowledge of these relationships, and recent work has introduced cars as distinct socio-material and sensory spaces that mediate how people experience and interpret their environments (Dahl & Tjora, 2023; Lynch, 2024; Yang et al., 2023). Building on this emerging scholarship and extending early studies on walking interviews and other “go-along” methods (Evans & Jones, 2011; Finlay & Bowman, 2017; Kusenbach, 2003), our findings demonstrate how car-based mobile interviews broaden researchers’ capacity to glean insights on key features of participants’ lifeworlds (Husserl, 1970; Kraus, 2015)—the everyday interactions, encounters, and affective geographies that shape health and well-being. We further emphasize how the car’s structural and spatial affordances enhance accessibility and inclusivity, accommodating a wider range of participant needs and mobility contexts than walking interviews alone.

In alignment with prior literature combining stationary and mobile approaches (e.g., Carder et al., 2023; Carpiano, 2009; Evans & Jones, 2011; Finlay & Bowman, 2017), stationary interviews and car-based mobile interviews were a complementary pairing. The semi-structured stationary interview—typically conducted first—established a contextual framework for the car-based mobile interview and cued researchers to which local environmental features may be most important for each participant. Furthermore, the stationary interview helped build trust and rapport, important components of safe and productive qualitative fieldwork (Ahmed, 2024; Ellard-Gray et al., 2015; Schmid et al., 2024). Subsequent car-based mobile interviews then elicited situated reflections and observations about participants’ neighborhoods and communities, illustrating how mobility shapes the spatial and affective dimensions of local health environments.

Walking and car-based mobile interviews differed substantially in pace, modes of sensory engagement, spatial reach, and opportunities for social interaction. Prior scholarship on go-along approaches has highlighted how modes of movement shape what becomes perceptible and narratable in place-based research (Jessiman et al., 2023; Veitch et al., 2022). As discussed by Lynch (2024) and further supported by our study findings, traveling via foot meant moving slower through time and space which enabled fine-grained, ground-level interactions between participants and their built environments. Alternatively, traveling quicker by car during car-based mobile interviews provided broader-scale insights into spatial connectivity and movement patterns. While walking interviews offered in-depth, highly detailed insights on smaller geographic areas, cars facilitated the tracing of expansive everyday routes such as transit journeys, shopping areas, and health-related destinations. Often, car-based mobile interviews extended the analytic reach of mobile methods beyond neighborhood boundaries. During car-based mobile interviews, the social engagement with one’s surroundings was typically less direct and in-depth than walking mobile interviews, which are often described as ‘inherently sociable’ (Lee & Ingold, 2006, p. 69) Cars create a boundary between participants and their surrounding environments, resulting in a “bubble effect” that may impact the researcher’s capacity to observe participants’ embodied interactions in situ (Kusenbach, 2003). Yet, traveling by car is a major component of navigating time and space, and car-based mobile interviews can reveal unique insights about how participants interact with the local sites, services, amenities, and hazards around them.

Our findings align with prior ethnographic and social care research identifying cars as therapeutic and reflexive spaces (Dawson, 2017; Ferguson, 2009, 2016). The car’s enclosed, forward-facing configuration often fostered calmness and candor. This produces what Laurier et al. (2008) describe as relaxed and casual dialogue. Several participants appeared more at ease during car-based mobile interviews than in stationary interviews, suggesting that shared mobility within vehicles can reconfigure social distance, ease pressure, and facilitate conversation about sensitive or personal topics related to place and health. The seating and positionality of the researcher(s) and participant within the car further shaped conversational dynamics. Consistent with Dahl and Tjora (2023), front-seat positioning supported reciprocity and participant leadership. Positioning participants as drivers or front-seat passengers reinforced their role as guides through their own geographies, while the researchers’ seating supported observation and note-taking with minimal disruption. Our team made the intentional choice to refrain from audio recording mobile interviews. Instead, we collected data via mobile transcripts, detailed post-interview fieldnotes, photos, handwritten real-time observations, and GPS routes. Though the decision to not record audio may have reduced the precision of data capture, we feel that the absence of the recorder helped reduce participant burden and cultivate a comfortable, candid, and easygoing atmosphere which more closely mimicked participants’ routines and behaviors in situ (Caronia, 2015; Lee, 2004; Marshall et al., 2021; Nordstrom, 2015; Rutakumwa et al., 2020).

Car-based mobile interviews raise logistical and ethical considerations distinct from other qualitative approaches (Supplemental Table 4). Driving together can be vulnerable, intimate, and risky, particularly in studies like ours when researchers and participants are meeting for the first time at the time of the interview. Questions of control, safety, and comfort are central: who drives (participant or researcher), whose vehicle is used, and how these decisions are made shape relational dynamics and warrant deliberate planning and reflexivity (Dahl & Tjora, 2023; Lynch, 2024). Practical challenges, including fuel costs, vehicle access, and environmental conditions may also constrain feasibility. Nevertheless, these constraints must be considered alongside the adaptable nature of car-based methods, which can enhance accessibility for participants with various mobility needs and preferences and allow for environmental exploration amidst inclement weather. As explored by Grove (2021) and further supported by this study’s findings, cars can also serve as an alternative modality for those who feel unsafe walking or prefer to avoid interacting with those around them. Future research should explore how such methods can advance understanding of spatial inequities in health and continue refining best practices for car-based mobile interviewing.

Ultimately, these insights demonstrate that car-based mobile interviews can substantially enrich qualitative inquiry into place and health. Building on prior work studying relational geographies of identity, disability, and health (Hall & Wilton, 2017; Imrie, 2004; Imrie & Edwards, 2007; Malpas, 2012; Massey, 2005; Yeung, 2024) this paper offers new methodological insights to advance how researchers learn, observe, and engage with participants with different mobility needs and embodied/emplaced routines. Additionally, this study expands on existing geonarrative literature to capture dynamic, temporal, and multi-scalar assemblances of impactful local services, amenities, and hazards (Bell et al., 2015, 2017; Curtis et al., 2019; Palis, 2022). By bridging methodological and practical innovation, this approach offers a means to access broader spatial scales of lived experience while maintaining attention to the sensory, affective, and social textures of everyday life. As car-based methods gain traction across disciplines, continued methodological advancement and ethical guidance are essential to ensure rigor, safety, and inclusivity in future applications.

Strengths and limitations

A primary strength of this study is the robust sample of participants representing a variety of identities across age, gender, sexual orientation, and race/ethnicity who engaged in both walking and car-based mobile interviews. Diverse participation enhanced our understanding of how individuals may select and experience mobile methods and allowed for a deeply contextualized analysis of the value and limitations of car-based mobile interviews.

Car-based mobile interviews are particularly well suited to automotive-centric contexts such as the United States (Pucher & Lefèvre, 1996), where private vehicles structure daily mobility, extend individuals’ routine activity spaces well beyond their residential neighborhoods, and shape how people experience place in motion (Kent, 2015). In such contexts, the car often functions as a primary socio-material environment through which people encounter services, amenities, risks, and social encounters (Dawson, 2017; Lynch, 2024). Interviewing participants within this space can therefore offer a more naturalistic window into everyday spatial practices, capturing relational, temporal, and multi-scalar dimensions of place that may be less visible in stationary or walking-based interviews (Laviolette & Argounova-Low, 2021; Thrift, 2004).

At the same time, car-based mobile interviews are not a neutral methodological tool. Their strengths are inseparable from the structural conditions of car dependence, which reflect broader political, economic, and planning decisions that unevenly privilege those with access to private vehicles while marginalizing others. Conducting interviews in cars may inadvertently normalize automobility as the default mode of spatial engagement, potentially obscuring sensory and place-based experiences that are highly localized, embodied, or shaped by non-automotive forms of mobility (Kusenbach, 2003; Lee & Ingold, 2006). This method may also be less appropriate in dense urban contexts or regions where daily life is organized primarily around walking, cycling, or public transit, and where cars function more episodically than extensions of everyday social space.

Ultimately, the level of methodological fit for car-based mobile interviews varies by research question. Car-based mobile interviews may be particularly effective for examining dispersed activity spaces, service access, and perceptions of safety or avoidance across wider geographies, but less well suited for capturing environmental exposures or social interactions that are theorized to operate at very fine spatial scales near the home (Tartia, 2018). Recognizing these contextual and theoretical tradeoffs is essential for aligning mobile interviewing methods with the spatial processes under investigation.

Finally, this study did not include participants living in rural areas, individuals with cognitive impairment, non-English speakers, or people under 18 years of age. All participants resided in the Minneapolis-St. Paul metropolitan area, which may limit transferability to other socio-geographic contexts. Moreover, participants self-selected their preferred mobile interview modality, with most choosing walking. These limitations suggest directions for future research to further test and refine car-based mobile interviews across varied populations, mobility regimes, and spatial contexts.

Conclusion

Cars assist us with navigating to, from, and around the places in which we live. As such, car-based mobile interviewing can meaningfully reveal how local environments shape everyday experiences, behaviors, and well-being. From infancy to older adulthood, built, natural, and social environments play a pivotal role in supporting—or limiting—our health and well-being. Extending car-based mobile interviews to different populations may require creativity, such as conducting interviews on school buses with children, accompanying parents or families on errands, or riding with non-car owners in rideshare vehicles.

Mobile methods research can further expand beyond walking and driving. Scholars have explored mobile methods via biking (McIlvenny, 2013), public transit (Fletcher, 2024), train journeys (Bissell, 2009), and ferry trips (Vannini, 2011). What remains most important is selectively and thoughtfully choosing relational methodologies that situate researchers “in the mobile habitats of their informants, thus facilitating access to their experiences and practices as they unfold in real time and space” (Kusenbach, 2003, p. 478).

This study demonstrates the strategic value of car-based mobile interviews for examining how local environmental features shape health through social and behavioral pathways. Cars enable observation and discussion across broader spatial scales, particularly when services, amenities, and hazards are geographically dispersed. Furthermore, cars enhance accessibility, broaden participation, and provide a mobile socio-emotional refuge during fieldwork. By extending mobile methods beyond traditional walking interviews, this approach contributes to more inclusive, contextually grounded, and equity-oriented qualitative research. Car-based mobile interviews generate actionable, place-based insights that can inform transportation, housing, urban planning, and public health practice and policymaking aimed at reducing socio-spatial inequalities in mobility, access, belonging, and health.
