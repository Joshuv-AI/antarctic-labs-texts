Delayed life-threatening aortic rupture after rib fractures: a case report
Authors: Li-Ning Tseng, Ying-Cheng Chen, Wei-Jung Chang, Ya-Fu Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Most of the documented cases result in prehospital mortality or death shortly after arrival because of massive hemothorax. Background

Rib fractures are the most common form of thoracic injury, occurring in up to 30% of patients with blunt chest trauma [1]. The extent of injury is closely related to the number of fractured ribs. Although the overall in-hospital mortality rate following rib fractures is approximately 10%, it increases substantially up to 40% in cases involving > 6 ribs [2].

Although rib fractures are common, the incidence of associated intrathoracic complications varies. Pneumonia may develop in 40.2% of patients with multiple rib fractures within the first week of hospitalization [3]. Hemothorax and pneumothorax are observed in 10%–37% of all rib fractures and approximately 81.4% of cases involving > 2 rib fractures, respectively [4]. In contrast, aortic injury resulting directly from rib fracture is exceedingly rare. Previous study reported traumatic rupture of the aorta is associated with a prehospital mortality rate of approximately 85%, with only 15% of patients surviving to receive medical care [5]. However, the mechanism of these patients typically results from rapid deceleration causing aortic transection whereas displaced rib fragments mostly cause aortic penetrating injuries directly. Historically, aortic penetrating injuries caused by displaced rib were managed with emergent rib resection because surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) was not routinely practiced.

In 2024, the World Society of Emergency Surgery, in collaboration with the Chest Wall Injury Society, published a position paper summarizing the current evidence on SSRF. This review incorporates findings from randomized controlled trials, prospective studies, and meta-analyses, outlining indications, timing, and operative considerations. Overall, the evidence supports the use of SSRF in selected patients, particularly those with flail chest, with reported benefits including improved respiratory outcomes, shorter duration of mechanical ventilation, and reduced intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay [6].

Here, we describe a rare case of aortic injury caused by multiple rib fractures and complicated by massive hemothorax. Following successful cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), the patient underwent urgent aortic repair and subsequent SSRF. This report highlights the rarity of rib fracture-induced aortic injury and underscores the evolving role of SSRF in managing complex thoracic trauma.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with multiple laceration wounds over the extremities and shortness of breath following traffic accident. Her medical history included right breast cancer, which was treated by surgery and targeted therapy.

A timeline of the patient’s clinical course is shown in Figure S1. Upon arrival at the emergency department, her vital signs showed normal heart rate (74 beats/min), blood pressure of 131/106 mmHg, body temperature of 34.8℃, and oxygen saturation of 92%. She claimed that she was unconscious after the accident. Focused assessment with sonography for trauma revealed left upper quadrant fluid accumulation. Whole-body computed tomography (CT) showed left 3rd to 12th rib fractures with hemothorax and flail chest (Fig. 1A). Spleen laceration, liver laceration, and hemoperitoneum were also noted (Fig. 1B). No peri-aortic hematoma, subtle vascular abnormalities, or retrospective signs suspicious for impending vascular injury were present. The posterior 6th and 7th ribs were displaced at a posterior site 2 cm within the transverse process with inversion toward the descending aorta (Fig. 2). Left chest tube insertion was performed, and 300 mL of dark blood was drained. She was sent to the operating room 1 h after arrival, an emergent exploratory laparotomy revealed massive bleeding from the ruptured spleen and short gastric vessel, and splenectomy was performed. She was transferred to the ICU with a stable condition 3 h after arrival.

However, a sudden massive amount of blood was drained from the left chest tube, and in-hospital cardiac arrest was noted 8 h after ICU admission. After CPR for 10 min and clamping the chest tube with 6-unit whole blood transfusion, spontaneous circulation returned. She was sent for emergent thoracotomy with continuous blood transfusion immediately. Posterior-lateral thoracotomy was performed from the 5th intercostal space, and a 6-mm descending aortic laceration with diffuse intramural hematoma was found after suctioning 4,000 mL of hemothorax. The aortic laceration was compressed by a finger 30 min after operative room arrival. Unfortunately, cardiac arrest occurred, and direct open-chest cardiac massage was performed for 30 s, with 12-unit blood transfusion from a large bore catheter. After her vital signs became stable, her proximal aorta was clamped, and the aortic adventitia layer was opened due to the presence of diffuse aortic hematoma (Fig. 3a). The aortic intima layer was sutured with 5 − 0 Prolene (Prolene™, Ethicon Inc.), and the adventitia layer was covered with bovine pericardial patches (Fig. 3b). The aortic surface was covered with absorbable hemostatic gauze after the aortic laceration was repaired. The whole aortic repair procedure took 40 min.

The 6th and 7th rib fracture segments were just near the aortic laceration place and highly suspicious the cause of aortic rupture. To prevent further complications caused by rib fractures, the 6th and 7th rib fractures were stabilized with plates and screws (MatrixRIB™ system) (Fig. 4). Two 28-Fr chest tubes were placed after the surgery. The patient was cared for in the ICU uneventfully and was extubated on postoperative day (POD) 12. The chest tubes were removed on PODs 13 and 14. Internal fixation of her right proximal humerus fracture was arranged after ventilator weaning. She was discharged on POD 19 without bleeding or other complications.

Discussion

Aortic injury secondary to posterior rib fractures is exceedingly rare but nearly invariably fatal, most often resulting in massive hemothorax before any opportunity for intervention. Bruno and Batchelor initially described this phenomenon as a delayed aortic rupture caused by a displaced rib fragment, noting its rarity and devastating course [7]. More recent studies continue to report only sporadic cases, most of which are identified post-mortem because of rapid exsanguination and failed resuscitation efforts [8]. The 2021 European Resuscitation Council Guidelines further emphasized that survival in such cases depends on immediate thoracotomy and vascular control, although even timely diagnosis remains a clinical challenge [9].

The mechanism underlying delayed aortic rupture in this setting is likely multifactorial. Ongoing respiratory motion can lead to gradual displacement of fractured ribs, particularly in the presence of a flail segment, resulting in repeated contact with surrounding structures. In addition, necessary patient repositioning during resuscitation, imaging, or operative management may further accentuate rib movement. Over time, persistent contact between sharp posterior rib fragments and the descending aorta can weaken the aortic wall and may eventually result in delayed aortic rupture.

The most important determinant of survival in the present case was rapid hemorrhage control through emergency aortic repair. Once the source of bleeding was identified, immediate digital compression and proximal aortic clamping enabled temporary control of exsanguination. The aortic repair technique is vital for this patient due to the presence of diffuse aortic hematoma. We have to open the aortic adventitia layer with proximal aorta clamping to repair the intima layer within minutes. Direct suture of adventitia layer was not feasible and patch reinforcement was applied for restored vascular integrity.

SSRF has evolved considerably over the past century, transitioning from rudimentary fixation techniques to the development of anatomically contoured plating systems and minimally invasive techniques, which facilitate safe and effective stabilization, even in posterior fractures near the transverse process [10]. In patients with blunt thoracic aortic injury requiring endovascular or open repair, subsequent SSRF has been reported to be feasible, with potential benefits in improving chest wall stability and facilitating respiratory recovery [11]. Posterior rib fractures located within 2–3 cm of the transverse process pose a technical challenge owing to limited exposure, limited landing zones for screw insertion, difficult angles, and potential neurovascular risks. To address these hurdles, Bartscherer et al. described the use of costotransverse screws as a viable solution for securing proximal fractures that threaten the aorta [12]. Furthermore, modern intrathoracic approaches and dedicated instrumentation now permit secure fixation in these anatomically constrained areas.

The optimal timing for SSRF has become increasingly defined, with current evidence supporting early intervention for eligible patients who experienced trauma, ideally within 48–72 h following admission [13]. Once stable hemodynamics is achieved, early SSRF offers significant clinical advantages, such as shorter duration of mechanical ventilation, low pneumonia rates, fewer ICU days, and overall decreased morbidity [6]. Delayed fixation, particularly beyond 72 h, is associated with higher risks of respiratory complications, higher analgesic demands, and longer hospital stays. In patients undergoing emergent aortic repair because of penetrating rib fragments, early chest wall stabilization remains feasible and safe, provided that the vascular status is secured and bleeding is controlled. A multicenter cohort study showed that early SSRF decreased ventilator days from 9 to 6 and halved unplanned intubation rates (6.2% vs. 12%) [14]. These findings support early SSRF following hemodynamic stabilization, even in cases with prior aortic repair. This approach not only restores thoracic mechanics and reduces pain but also prevents long-term complications, particularly in posterior rib fractures with associated vascular injury.

For similar injuries, posterior rib fragments pointing toward the aorta should be treated as a direct vascular threat. Clinicians must identify high-risk CT features, such as peri-aortic fat stranding, aortic contour irregularity, or extreme proximity of the rib to the aorta, even without an overt hematoma. Even in the absence of definite vascular injury, these findings should prompt early consultation with cardiothoracic or vascular surgeons because delayed aortic rupture may occur after coughing, patient repositioning, positive-pressure ventilation, or chest wall motion. Repeat contrast-enhanced CT should be considered whenever clinical deterioration occurs or fracture displacement appears to progress. Following hemodynamic stabilization, early SSRF may be considered in carefully selected patients to eliminate repetitive mechanical irritation of the aortic wall and prevent delayed vascular injury.

Conclusions

Survival after delayed aortic injury secondary to rib fractures is exceedingly rare. In the present case, the favorable outcome was most likely related to early recognition, prompt hemorrhage control, aggressive transfusion resuscitation, and coordinated multidisciplinary trauma care. Following emergent vascular repair and hemodynamic stabilization, SSRF was feasible to address persistent chest wall instability. This case highlights that early recognition of high-risk CT patterns should prompt preemptive SSRF before fatally delayed vascular injuries occur.
