The diabetes exposome: interplay of environmental and genetic determinants in diabetes
Authors: Bushra Zia, Juma AlKaabi, Adnan Agha, Charu Sharma, Bassam R Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes mellitus arises from complex interactions between biological susceptibility and diverse environmental influences that extend beyond traditional “genes versus lifestyle” models. The exposome is defined as the aggregate of non-genetic environmental exposures and their biological consequences across the life course. It provides a systems-level framework to evaluate this interaction, spanning chemical, physical, social, and behavioral domains while integrating them with internal molecular responses to decode how environments shape metabolic health. Emerging assessment strategies, including exposome-wide association studies (ExWAS), high-resolution mass spectrometry–based biomonitoring, geospatial and remote-sensing platforms, and wearable exposure sensors, enable characterization of multi-exposure profiles rather than single agents in isolation. Within exposome domains, air pollution, endocrine-disrupting chemicals, food-borne contaminants, exposome characteristics of the built environment, and chronic psychosocial stress have each been shown to contribute to the development and progression of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. These diverse exposures often share common pathogenic mechanisms of chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative and nitrosative stress, alterations in the gut microbiome, and epigenetic changes that ultimately lead to glucose dysregulation. Recent progress in metabolomics, lipidomics, and epigenomics is elucidating the “internal exposome,” providing molecular fingerprints that encode prior exposure and detect early metabolic disruption. This integrated approach argues for a paradigm adjustment in diabetes prevention from behavior-centered strategies to those that also target upstream environmental determinants. Exposomic information can improve risk prediction, inform precision public health and medical practice, and inform policy regarding air pollution, chemical use, urban planning, and food systems. This review summarizes the current state of knowledge of the exposome in diabetes, describing its conceptual underpinnings, major tools of assessment, major epidemiologic findings, and biological mechanisms, and identifying the key challenges and opportunities for exposomics to inform effective approaches to diabetes prevention and planetary health.Keywords: Exposome, Diabetes mellitus, Environmental exposures, Lifecourse epidemiology, Exposome-wide association study (ExWAS), Insulin resistance

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a growing global health problem that is expected to affect nearly 783 million people by 2045 [1]. Despite the comprehensive understanding of insulin signaling pathways, increasingly precise mapping of genetic risk loci, and the translation of lifestyle-based recommendations into public health policy, these advances have contributed little to combating the rising global trend of the disease. There is a significant gap between the development of pathophysiological models and their successful application to population-level prevention. The current genetic/lifestyle model of disease, which focuses on susceptibility genes, diet, and physical inactivity, explains only a proportion of disease variability and fails to account for the major features of the disease distribution, including the geographic and temporal clustering of Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) in the absence of infectious causes, the rapid increase of Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) in populations with unaltered genetic structure, and the persistence of socioeconomic inequalities after adjustment for individual behavior patterns [2]. Thus, there is a need for an exposome-based approach to diabetes etiology for a better understanding of the disease.

The exposome: concept and relevance to diabetes

The exposome, first articulated in 2005 by Christopher Wild, refers to the totality of environmental exposures (chemical, biological, physical, social, and psychosocial) and their associated biological responses from conception across the life course [3, 4]. Recent studies have shown that for personalized medicine to be effective, there is a need to integrate the exposome (environmental, lifestyle, and social exposures) with genomic and multi-omics data [5]. The Human Exposome Project collects extensive lifetime exposure data which are managed as per participants’ privacy and advances environmental justice due to exposomethics roadmap [6]. In earlier literature, for ease of description, the exposome was classified into three broader interacting domains: the general external environment (climate, socioeconomic context), the specific external environment (air pollution, diet, infection), and the internal environment (metabolism, inflammation, gut microbiota) [3, 7]. However, in this narrative review, we have classified the diabetes exposomes into two domains, external and internal, which are further sub-divided into categories. Diabetes is a particularly informative test case for studying exposomes as it has marked social and environmental gradients, showing much higher prevalence of the disorder in poorer, more marginalized populations, and people living in polluted/deprived areas [8]. Data from critical developmental windows (e.g., in utero or early childhood) indicate that maternal and early-life environments contribute to diabetes risk, which is exacerbated by cumulative exposure to environmental risk factors during these stages [9–11]. Additionally, the three key pathophysiological processes leading to diabetes (insulin resistance, β cell dysfunction, and autoimmunity) are particularly sensitive to many different environmental factors, including chemical toxicants and psychosocial stressors.

This review presents a critical evaluation of the published evidence on the diabetes exposome and examines both T1D and T2D through the exposome framework. T1D is addressed first due to the distinct role of infectious and autoimmune triggers in its pathogenesis, followed by a more extensive coverage of T2D given the larger body of exposome-specific literature for this disease type. Parallel with T2D are drawn to illustrate how distinct exposome domains converge on shared autoimmune and metabolic pathways. Specifically, we aim to: (1) organize the heterogeneous array of external and internal exposures into a coherent, life-course diabetes exposome framework; (2) integrate epidemiological evidence with mechanistic insights from multi-omics; and (3) delineate implications for precision prevention, clinical care, and public health policy. We propose that diabetes risk and progression reflect sustained interactions between susceptible biology and an unequal, structurally patterned life-course exposome.

Methods and conceptual foundations

Literature identification

We performed extensive literature search across different platforms including: PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus for exposome–diabetes studies. For this narrative review, the primary searches included publications from 2005 to January 2026, with seminal prior works included where they provided foundational evidence base for specific domains. Evidence from consortia, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and studies were assessed using combinations of terms such as “exposome”, “type 1 diabetes” “genetic susceptibility”, “environmental factors” “infectious exposome”, “type 2 diabetes,” “Metabolome”, “lipidome”, “immune” and domain-specific keywords (e.g., “epigenomic,” “endocrine and metabolism disrupters”, “dietary patterns”, “pollution”, “dysbiosis”, “chemical exposure”).

Operationalising the diabetes exposome

The diabetes exposome is viewed as an integrated structure encompassing both external and internal domains where in environmental encounters and biological responses occur across a lifetime to influence disease risk [8]. The former consists of the exposures that come from outside of a person; and latter consists of the biological response of the person to those external influences [12]. In this review both domains were further subdivided to enable a clearer organization of the literature relevant to diabetes. The external domain is classified into five categories and internal domain into three categories based upon the exposure origin and interaction with individual. While external exposome captures the sources of exposure, the internal exposome represents the biological responses of those exposures including the molecular and physiological alterations that may exert effects on diabetes risk. Therefore, internal domain is further categorized into three categories based upon the genetic susceptibility, epigenome, and immune-inflammatory networks which constitute the biological embedding of the exposome as they provide mechanistic insight into disease pathogenesis and identify potential targets for prevention and intervention of diabetes.

In the external domain, the first category includes physical environment which consists of pollutants in air including fine PM₂.₅, nitrogen dioxide (NO2), ozone, noise pollution, geography, climate and temperature. The second category includes early life exposure (including in utero and early childhood) which also has detrimental consequences in the pathology and progression of the disease over the course of the lifespan of an individual [13]. The third category includes chemical exposures and exposomes with in the food system. This includes exposure to toxic metals including arsenic and cadmium, persistent organic pollutants, pesticides, endocrine and metabolism disruptors like bisphenols and phthalates which are present in consumer products, packaging and industrial materials [14].The component of food systems include dietary patterns: consumption of ultra-processed foods, and marketing of healthy and unhealthy foods [15]. The fourth category includes social environment which is determined by neighbourhood, access to resources, and exposure to stressors [16]. Lastly, the fifth category is infectious exposome, which includes identifiable viral agents (e.g., enteroviruses) as well as medical interventions [17–19].

In the internal domain, the first category deals with genetic susceptibility as the genome establishes the baseline biological context within which all environmental exposures operate. Critically, genetic susceptibility alone is insufficient for disease onset; rather, the genome determines individuals of higher polygenic risk who may experience greater metabolic disruption from pollutants, chemicals, or adverse dietary patterns. The second category is the epigenome which deals with DNA methylation, non-coding RNAs, and histone modifications that convert ecological signals into stable yet potentially reversible changes in gene expression. The plasticity of the epigenome offers translational potential for exposure monitoring and intervention. The third category in the internal domain is the immune and inflammatory network as chronic low-grade inflammation, represents a central mechanism through which diverse external exposures converge on metabolic dysfunction [20]. The pro-inflammatory cytokines embracing tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) are elevated in response to external stressors. The use of antibiotics upon exposure to infections can also affect immune system functions [21]. The profiles of our immune and inflammatory system, such as circulating cytokines, chemokines, autoantibodies and immune cell subsets, connect the external environment to the chronic low-graded inflammation associated with T2D and autoimmunity pathologies associated with T1D [22].

Characterization of the diabetes exposome

The diabetes exposome incorporates lifelong environmental exposures that can influence their susceptibility to disease. Characterizing these complex concepts requires integrating cohort studies, biomonitoring, and advanced analytical methodologies. Birth cohorts and repeat measures from adult cohorts (examples include the HELIX and INMA cohorts) provide the core infrastructure for life-course analysis [23]. The strength of these cohort-based studies enables investigation in critical windows of development however the limitations include the longer duration of follow-up (across decades) required for diabetes outcomes. Different methods like geographical location and remote sensing (temperature, green space, humidity, pollution etc.), area topology, biomonitoring of environmental chemical, and surveys to assess health and psychosocial stress have been used to characterize exposomes [24, 25]. The strength of these studies includes spatial and temporal coverage to evaluate the correlation of geographical factors with development of diabetes. However, the limitations include inability to capture indoor exposures and variability in exposure on an individual basis. Moreover, biomonitoring provides insights into variability in absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion across the population however the limitations include short half-lives for many chemicals and the need for specialized analytical chemistry. Other analytical approaches including Exposome-Wide Association Studies (ExWAS) adapt the genome-wide approach to test hundreds of exposures simultaneously [26]. Multi-pollutant models including elastic net, Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) attempt to disentangle effects of correlated mixtures [27]. Mediation analysis formalizes the pathway from external exposure to change in internal exposome subsequently resulting in diabetes thereby providing mechanistic support, researchers are increasingly employing Mendelian Randomization (MR) [28].

Components of T1D exposome

T1D being a multifactorial autoimmune disease is differentiated by polyuria, polydipsia and polyphagia along with ketoacidosis. Several studies have reported that population genetics along with environmental, lifestyle, and social determinants cause disease pathogenesis [29, 30]. Early-life infections, dietary patterns, obesity exposure to pollutants have been associated with disease progression [31]. Evidence syntheses explicitly adopting an exposome framework remain scarce; a dedicated scoping-review protocol on the T1D exposome has only recently been registered, and no completed scoping review is yet available Although these findings are convincing, still the role of the exposome in T1D remains insufficiently explored and fragmented. A more comprehensive understanding is crucial for identifying new strategies for diabetes prevention and management. The exposome provides a useful way of categorising any such factors against the development of T1D through immune system dysfunction with either starting point or genetic predisposition effect, as well as continued developing through the autoimmunisation, as well as impact on the time from autoimmune disease to clinical disease as well as variability of disease types.

External domain of T1D

Physical environment

The amount of data available for the relationship between T1D and exposure to environmental chemicals is much less than the amount available for T2D, but it is increasing. For example, some studies have reported a connection between air pollution (specifically PM₂.₅ and NO₂) and an increased incidence of T1D at earlier ages when combined with other factors [32, 33]. This may be due to environmental toxins causing oxidative stress throughout the body, which then triggers inflammation. This can lead to autoimmune activation happening much sooner than it would normally occur. While studies regarding pesticides and persistent organic pollutants regarding the T1D incidence are mixed, they do offer some evidence. For example, exposure to some chlorinated pesticides has also been associated with an increased risk of T1D in adults and adolescents [34, 35].

Additionally, geographic patterns in T1D incidence suggest a role for physical environmental factors as the incidence generally increases with distance from the equator, forming the basis of the “vitamin D hypothesis.” Lower ultraviolet B radiation at higher latitudes leads to reduced endogenous synthesis of vitamin D, a potent immunomodulator that promotes regulatory T-cell function and immune tolerance [36, 37]. Seasonal patterns in birth and diagnosis also implicate seasonal variations in sunlight/vitamin D, viral infections, or temperature. Urban versus rural differences, with higher incidence often reported in urban areas, may reflect a confluence of factors including differing infectious exposures, microbiome profiles, air pollution levels, and lifestyles [38].

Individuals living with T1D face a pronounced vulnerability within the broader context of geography- and climate-related threats, noting that people with T1D are particularly susceptible to heatwaves and extreme weather because of impaired thermoregulation, autonomic dysfunction, comorbid kidney and cardiovascular disease, and the practical challenges of insulin storage and glucose monitoring in unstable environments. This intersection of intrinsic pathophysiology and external environmental stress creates a unique and heightened risk profile, positioning T1D management as a critical component of climate adaptation and public health resilience planning [39]. From an exposome perspective, it is implied that climatic factors (ambient temperature, heatwaves, cold spells) and co-occurring stressors such as air pollution form part of the external environmental burden that can destabilise glycaemic control and increase acute events in individuals already living with T1D, with risk patterns varying by climate zone and urban versus rural settings [39, 40]. Early-life context is increasingly important in this framework: complementary evidence from cohort studies suggests that maternal and early-childhood exposure to ambient air pollutants and meteorological extremes may contribute to the incidence of paediatric T1D, positioning geography- and climate-linked exposures across gestation and early childhood as critical components of the T1D-relevant exposome as summarized in Fig. 1 [41].

Geography and climate likely function as composite exposure settings in which inherited susceptibility intersects with region-specific infectious, nutritional, ultraviolet, and built-environment factors. However, formal tests of HLA- or polygenic-risk-modified effects within these domains remain limited. At present, geography is best interpreted as an integrated exposomic context whose biological meaning will be clarified only when detailed exposure measurement is combined with genetic stratification in early-life cohorts [42].

Early-life exposure

Early nutrition and gut microbiota also play a crucial role in development of T1D. It can be alleviated through long-term breast-feeding, avoidance of early complementary foods and cow milk (before 12 months). These detrimental feeding habits create a gut microbiota dysbiotic state contributing to T1D onset [18, 43, 44]. Nutrition during infancy is one factor that contributes to determining the risk for developing T1D through its interaction with the developing immune system and developing gut microbiota. Breastfeeding has been linked to a slight lowering of the chances of developing T1D possibly because it passes on protective immune factors as well as developing a healthy gut microbiota. The timing of when complex dietary antigens such as gluten and cow’s milk protein are introduced into the diet is being researched as there is evidence that both introducing them too soon (within the first few months) and too late (after ~ 6 months) may increase the likelihood of developing T1D in at-risk individuals, suggesting that there may be an optimal “window” during which to introduce these antigens to create immune tolerance. The gut microbiome is instrumental in linking diet and immunity together as summarized in Fig. 1.

Children who develop T1D generally have dysbiosis (an imbalance of bacteria) prior to developing antibodies to T1D. This dysbiosis manifests itself as lower diversity of gut flora, a shortage of butyrate-producing bacteria (which are anti-inflammatory), and an overabundance of pro-inflammatory microorganisms. An extensive multi-omics and pre-clinical study demonstrated the role of microbial function and metabolic dysbiosis in T1D, highlighting the role of specific gut bacteria and metabolites as novel therapeutic targets [45]. Disruptive factors affecting the establishment of a healthy gut microbiome during infancy, such as having been delivered via caesarean section or having received antibiotics during infancy, are associated with increased T1D risk [46].

The prospective cohort study, T1D Prediction and Prevention Project (DIPP) showed the early introduction (3–4 months) of fruit, berries and roots was associated with a higher risk to develop β cells autoimmunity [47]. Studies have shown that feeding patterns promote a more diverse, Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFA) producing microbiota and reduced bacteroides-dominant dysbiosis, thereby supporting epithelial barrier integrity and regulatory immune networks implicated in protection from islet autoimmunity [48].

In TEDDY, association between the food introduction and islet autoimmunity varied according to HLA genotype. This infers that similar feeding exposure may confer different risk depending on inherited immune susceptibility [49]. Beyond HLA-stratified analyses, larger cohort studies on nutrition-microbiome-genome interaction in T1D remain limited. Therefore, an integrated early-life cohorts along with dietary assessment, microbiome and virome profiling, and genetic risk stratification is the need of the hour [42].

Chemical exposome

There are also growing concerns about how modern chemicals, such as bisphenols and phthalates, could disrupt how the body’s immune system functions, given their known immunomodulatory and potential adjuvant properties. There is a need to perform larger, prospective exposome-scale studies regarding T1D’s relationship with exposure to chemical mixtures like those that have been conducted about T2D. An epidemiological and experimental evidence report on environmental chemical exposures in relation to T1D suggested that any diabetogenic effects of chemicals are likely to be complex and modified by dose, timing, nutritional status, and interindividual differences in metabolism. Although the evidence remains preliminary, it underscores the importance of further investigation of environmental chemicals as potentially modifiable risk factors for T1D prevention [50].

Compared with infectious or nutritional exposures, evidence that specific germline variants modify the diabetogenic effects of chemical or physical exposures in T1D remains sparse. This is a notable gap, because HLA-mediated immune activation, xenobiotic metabolism, antiviral defense, and oxidative-stress pathways could plausibly influence susceptibility to endocrine-disrupting chemicals, metals, or other inflammatory exposures. Future studies should test these interactions explicitly rather than treating genetic susceptibility and chemical exposure as parallel but separate pathways.

Social exposome

Notably, while the social environment represents a well-established category in T2D exposomic research, its role in T1D remains largely unexplored with minimal evidence currently available.

Infectious exposome

Viral infections, particularly enteroviruses (e.g., Coxsackievirus B), is the one of the most well-studied components of the T1D infectious exposome. Prospective cohort studies like TEDDY have provided robust evidence that enterovirus infections, especially when recurring, are associated with the initiation and progression of islet autoimmunity in genetically at-risk children. The virome studies in T1D cohorts (TEDDY, DIPP, DIABIMMUNE) using conventional NGS generally found no clear association between the virome and IA/T1D but were limited by poor sensitivity for eukaryotic viruses [51]. A larger TEDDY stool study that combined NGS with virus culture reported a significant association between consecutive EV-B shedding and increased IA risk, though this approach was biased toward culturable EVs. Target-enriched sequencing (VirCapSeq-VERT) improved vertebrate virus detection and agreed well with qPCR yet showed no simple link between overall EV positivity and IA, instead revealing higher gut abundance of several EV-A genotypes in at-risk children [52]. Viral infections are consistently framed as a major, dynamic component of the external exposome that can initiate or amplify T1D in genetically susceptible individuals. Afolorunsho et al. emphasise persistent, low-grade enteroviral infection, with viral RNA and capsid protein detectable in pancreatic and intestinal tissue, as a key environmental driver that sustains islet-directed autoimmunity via molecular mimicry, epitope spreading, and chronic type I interferon–mediated inflammation, while relegating pure “bystander” activation to a minor role [53]. Jeremiah et al. extend this paradigm to a broader panel of “diabetogenic” viruses, including coxsackie B virus, cytomegalovirus, hepatitis C virus and SARS-CoV-2, highlighting direct β-cell tropism and cytolysis, virus-induced immune dysregulation, and interferon-dominated inflammation with extra-pancreatic effects on liver, adipose tissue and the renin–angiotensin system that worsen insulin resistance; SARS-CoV-2 is presented as a real-time stress test of this infection-diabetes model, capable of causing transient and sometimes persistent dysglycaemia, albeit often by unmasking latent metabolic vulnerability rather than creating de novo disease [54]. Rajsfus et al. further consolidate evidence that a wide spectrum of acute, recurrent and chronic viral infections including enteroviruses, CMV, HCV, HIV, SARS-CoV-2, act as infectious environmental triggers layered on top of HLA/non-HLA risk alleles and other lifestyle exposures, converging on shared pathways of β-cell stress, immune activation and systemic metabolic disruption [55]. Interpreted through an exposome lens, these viral exposures operate as time-varying, interacting components of the life-course exposome, rather than isolated “hits,” and the reviews collectively call for longitudinal, multi-omic and virome-integrated studies, as well as exposome-informed biomarker strategies and combined antiviral–immunomodulatory interventions, to more precisely define and therapeutically target the virus–exposome–genome interface in T1D [31, 53–55].

Possible routes include direct infection and lytic damage of β-cells, and also indirect routes such as molecular mimicry whereby viral proteins are similar to β-cell antigens (such as GAD65) and bystander activation of autoreactive T-cells in an inflamed microenvironment of islets as summarized in Fig. 1. However, because there are so many viruses present, other viruses may play a role (rotavirus, cytomegalovirus) indicating that the events of infection of other viruses provides a greater role of viral interactions in shaping immune development and responsiveness. In contrast, the “hygiene hypothesis” states that being exposed to a diverse array of microorganisms during early life may create a predisposition to a pro-allergic/pro-autoimmune phenotype. Support of this hypothesis comes from data collected from ecological studies that show a lower incidence of T1D in children raised on traditional farms or children who attend day-care in their early years, both exposure to a more diverse microflora. Overall, inconsistent results across T1D virome studies likely reflect differences in study design and methods, possible “hit-and-run” viral effects, and limited perinatal sampling, gaps now being targeted by newer ENDIA and DIPP-novum virome investigations.

Conclusively, in TEDDY and related cohorts, the timing and immunologic consequences of viral or respiratory exposures vary across HLA-defined risk groups and other immune-regulatory loci. Prospective virome analyses in genetically at-risk children have helped refine the temporal link between viral exposures and islet autoimmunity [56], while TEDDY data further show that genetic and environmental factors jointly shape autoantibody risk in early life [57]. Gestational respiratory infections have also been reported to interact with offspring HLA and CTLA4 to modify incident beta-cell autoantibodies [58]. These data support an explicitly genome-exposome model for T1D in which infection helps translate inherited susceptibility into immune activation rather than acting as a uniform trigger.

Internal domain of T1D

Genetic susceptibility

HLA

Genetic susceptibility to T1D is dominated by variation within the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region on chromosome 6p21, particularly HLA class II DR and DQ loci, which together explain the largest proportion of heritable risk. HLA class II molecules govern antigen presentation to CD4⁺ T cells, and certain allelic combinations favour the presentation of β-cell antigens in a manner that promotes loss of tolerance and islet-directed autoimmunity. More than 90% of children with T1D carry at least one of the high-risk haplotypes DRB103:01–DQA105:01–DQB102:01 (DR3–DQ2) or DRB104:xx–DQA103:01–DQB103:02 (DR4–DQ8), and the DR3–DQ2/DR4–DQ8 diplotype confers the highest known genetic risk, with up to an approximately 45-fold increase in risk relative to neutral or protective genotypes [59, 60]. These strong associations have underpinned mechanistic studies of T-cell–mediated β-cell autoimmunity and are integral to risk-stratification strategies in birth cohorts and prevention trials [61].

CTSH and other non-HLA loci

Genome wide association studies (GWAS) have identified other susceptibility loci of more modest effect including cathepsin H (CTSH) which has emerged as a notable non-HLA locus. Multi-omics analyses showed the altered expression of CTSH in pancreatic acinar cells along with involvement in antiviral and inflammatory pathways. Preclinical studies have reported that CTSH amplifies antiviral responses and pro-inflammatory cytokine production within the exocrine pancreas, creating an inflammatory microenvironment that promotes β-cell injury and T1D progression [62]. Other non-HLA loci, INS and GLIS3 gene further influence immune tolerance and β-cell survival, underscoring the polygenic nature of T1D [63].

Gene-environment interaction

Together, HLA and non-HLA variants create a polygenic background that influences immune regulation and β-cell function. While polygenic risk scores can identify individuals at particularly high genetic risk, many of these individuals never progress to clinical disease.

These observations highlight a key principle where genetic susceptibility alone does not determine disease. Instead, genetic risk must intersect with environmental exposures, captured within the exposome, to trigger islet autoimmunity, progressive β-cell loss, and the eventual clinical onset of T1D.

T1D exposome and genome-exposome interface

Taken together, the T1D exposome is best understood as an early-life, immune-centered genome-exposome system. HLA and non-HLA variants establish a background of susceptibility, but progression to islet autoimmunity and clinical disease appears to depend on how that background intersects with infectious exposures, early nutrition, microbiome development, chemical agents, and geographical or climatic context. Among these domains, infectious and virome-related signals currently have the strongest prospective evidence and have benefited most from repeated sampling and multi-omic integration in TEDDY and related cohorts [42, 57, 58].

Accordingly, the T1D exposome should be described as less mature methodologically rather than less relevant biologically. Priorities for the field now include virome-integrated ExWAS/EWAS, early-life exposome cohorts in genetically at-risk children, and joint modeling of HLA/non-HLA susceptibility with microbiome, metabolome, transcriptome, epigenome, and immune phenotyping. Such studies are needed to identify causal windows, distinguish exposure-responsive biomarkers from secondary correlates, and prioritize intervention targets before irreversible β-cell loss occurs [42, 57, 58]. By contrast, mixture-based analyses, exposome-wide designs, and integrated internal-omics studies remain far less developed in T1D than in T2D.

Components of Type 2 diabetes exposome

External domain of T2D

Physical environment

Environment represents a complex dimension of the T2D exposome, encompassing both harmful exposures and health-promoting resources that are inequitably distributed across populations. Pollution is major factor representing T2D exposome involving both the harmful exposure and health promoting resources which are unevenly disseminated across different sectors. The European Research Consortium have developed an approach to study the life-course exposome which includes environmental, psychological and cardio-metabolic risk factors (obesity, T2D and heart diseases) [64]. Long-term exposure to PM₂.₅ and NO₂ has been consistently associated with higher incidence, prevalence, and mortality of T2D as is summarized in Table 1. The underlying mechanisms are multifactorial since the inhaled particulates may trigger the systemic oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation via activating the NF-κB signalling and the NLRP3 inflammasome pathways as summarized in Fig. 2 [65]. This will lead to impaired insulin signalling subsequently leading to damage to the pancreatic β-cells. These metabolic effects are more pronounced in obese and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, indicating a strong correlation between biological vulnerability and social adversity.

Additionally, noise pollution contributes as a persistent stressor, leading to disturbed sleep patterns and persistently activating the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system. This activation has been reported to elevate cortisol and catecholamines leading to insulin resistance, increased gluconeogenesis, and visceral obesity [66]. Thermal stress is also important, as extreme heat is linked to dysregulated glycemic control, likely via dehydration, heightened inflammation, and heat strain. Conversely, dense, mixed-use, well-connected neighborhoods are associated with greater physical activity and lower T2D incidence, highlighting the role of urban design in shaping metabolic health [67]. Access to natural environments can mitigate these hazards as the green and blue spaces are linked to lower T2D risk by promoting physical activity, supporting psychological restoration, reducing air pollution, and potentially enhancing environmental biodiversity. Overall, the urban exposome encompasses both metabolic risks and protective resources. The socioeconomic disadvantaged communities often experience “double jeopardy” of higher pollutant, noise burdens and reduced access to walkable infrastructure.

Evidence that inherited susceptibility modifies the diabetogenic effects of air pollution is emerging but not yet fully consistent. One study reported that the association between air pollution and T2D was stronger at higher levels of genetic risk, with particularly suggestive signals for insulin-resistance-related loci [68]. Other large cohort analyses have shown stronger joint associations of air pollution and genetic predisposition with incident T2D, whereas UK Biobank work found no clear multiplicative interaction with a standard T2D genetic risk score [69, 70]. Taken together, these findings suggest that pollution-related effect modification may be pathway-specific and sensitive to exposure definition, ancestry composition, and the way genetic risk is modeled.

Early life exposure

The uterine environment serves a critical developmental exposome that determines metabolic health with T2D in pregnant women being a crucial example. Substantial evidence indicates the independent effect of maternal hyperglycemia in utero where in an offspring exposed to a diabetic intrauterine environment is at least thrice more likely to develop diabetes and obesity than their own sibling born before the mother developed diabetes [87–89].

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) also alters the intrauterine microbial exposome subsequently leading to elevated lipopolysaccharides and increased translocation of microbes across the placenta [90]. This results in gut dysbiosis, increased firmicutes/bacteroidetes ratio and priming of intestinal immune responses in the growing fetus resulting in low-grade inflammation and exposing the offspring to a higher chance of developing diabetes later in life.

Conclusively, metabolic and microbial factors play a critical role in intrauterine environment demonstrating that exposure to maternal diabetes itself, independent of genetic susceptibility, may result in adverse metabolic and immune responses. This underscores the critical role of optimizing maternal health not only for immediate perinatal outcomes but also for potentially curbing the intergenerational cycle of diabetes and obesity [87].

The unequal distribution of the exposome manifests in stark epidemiological disparities across racial, ethnic, and socio-economic lines, demonstrating that structural inequalities are fundamental biological determinants of metabolic health. To move beyond theoretical models, the exposome framework must be anchored in concrete demographic realities.

Chemical exposome and food systems

Arsenic (inorganic) is known to disrupt insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1) signaling and lead to T2D from drinking water containing levels of Arsenic that are even moderate [91]. Cadmium (food, tobacco) is a ubiquitous contaminant that accumulates within the kidney and pancreas and produces oxidative stress as well as impacts β-cell secretory function through direct impairment [92]. Persistent organic pollutants (POPs) such as dioxins, PCBs, etc. have long-term implications for diabetes due to their ability to persist in the environment and accumulate in the body with epidemiologic studies of high-risk populations demonstrating their association with disease likely resulting from activation of AhR and subsequent induction of mitochondrial dysfunction [93].

Bisphenols, for instance, act as potent metabolism disruptors by mimicking estrogenic and thyroid hormone activity, thereby altering adipogenesis and promoting a pro-inflammatory state [94]. Phthalates, commonly found in plastics and personal care items, disrupt PPARγ signaling, a master regulator of adipocyte differentiation and glucose homeostasis, and are epidemiologically associated with insulin resistance [95]. Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), known as “forever chemicals,” are associated with dyslipidemia, weight gain, and impaired glucose tolerance, potentially by interfering with mitochondrial bioenergetics and lipid metabolism. Prenatal exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals can induce epigenetic developmental metabolic reprogramming [96]. Modern metabolism-disrupting chemicals (MDCs) exhibit non-monotonic acting in complex mixtures and challenges conventional toxicological paradigms. From a risk-assessment perspective, No Observed Adverse Effect Levels (NOAEL)-based approaches should be used as adverse effects may occur at low doses that are not predicted from high-dose testing as the conventional threshold-based methods may underestimate real-world risk for MDCs and POPs. Their actions converge on dysregulated nuclear receptor signaling, chronic low-grade inflammation, and gut microbiome perturbation, collectively imprinting long-term susceptibility to T2D as summarized in Table 2.

Components of internal exposome associated with diabetes

Emerging evidence suggests that chemical diabetogenic effects may depend on inherited metabolic vulnerability. In a recent gene-environment interaction study, genetic predisposition to impaired β -cell function supported the association between serum pyrethroid levels and T2D risk [97]. This provides a proof-of-principle that MDCs may be especially harmful in individuals with limited β-cell reserve. However, comparable studies integrating broader MDCs with pathway-specific polygenic scores, rare variation, or fine-mapped causal loci remain scarce.

Conversely, apart from chemical exposome, food is one of the main exposure contributing to T2D, as it includes all nutritional, chemical, and physical structural ways that can influence disease risk. It has been shown that individuals consuming Western-type diets with a high volume of ultra-processed foods, especially sugar-containing beverages, have an increased risk for developing T2D as compared to individuals consuming Mediterranean-style diets [98, 99]. In addition to the composition of the diet, the environment in which we eat (the food environment) can also affect eating behavior and patterns by determining how economically and geographically available ultra-processed food is, how much it is marketed, and the degree to which health-promoting alternatives are available. Food also provides a major source of chemicals, including pesticides, food processing/safety/contaminating chemicals, and chemicals that enter food from packaging materials as summarized in Fig. 2. These chemical and nutritional components together influence our internal metabolome and are reflected in our body through changes in branched-chain and aromatic amino acids, acylcarnitines, and higher risk lipid markers like ceramides. This new category of exposure called the “eaten exposome” represents the interaction between diet, exposure, and metabolism by the gut microbiome as they relate to early metabolic biomarkers and mechanisms for linking food environments to the pathophysiology of T2D [100].

Compared with pollution and chemical exposures, robust evidence that common-variant polygenic scores materially modify the diabetogenic effects of dietary patterns or food environments is still limited. This is an important gap, because pathway-specific scores for β-cell dysfunction, adiposity biology, insulin resistance, and lipid handling may help identify which individuals are most vulnerable to ultra-processed diets or obesogenic food environments [101–103].

Social environment

The socio-economic environment constitutes an important factor of the T2D exposome, configuring both risk exposure and access to resources. These determinants become tangible representations of the lived experience through elements of neighbourhood disadvantage, differential access to care and to healthy resources, and repeated exposure to stressors such as job strain, financial insecurity, and discrimination and structural racism [16]. The marked increase in the T2D incidence especially in low socioeconomic population is majorly due to inaccessibility to the resources including affordable nutritious food, access to physical activity spaces, and high-quality healthcare. These drivers frequently coexist with environmental hazards such as air pollution and metabolism-disrupting chemicals, that accelerates metabolic deterioration in vulnerable populations [104, 105].

Evidence for polygenic or rare-variant modification of the effects of psychosocial adversity, neighborhood deprivation, or chronic stress on T2D risk remains sparse. Nevertheless, such interactions are biologically plausible because stress responses, inflammatory signaling, sleep disruption, and metabolic dysfunction which can converge with inherited susceptibility to insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction.

Infectious exposome

Unlike T1D, where enteroviruses and early life microbial exposure are well established triggers of islet autoimmunity, the infectious exposome has not been consistently implicated in the pathogenesis of T2D, reflecting a fundamental difference between the exposomes associated with the two types of diabetes.

Internal domain of T2D

Genetic susceptibility

The inherited genetic differences that make individuals more susceptible to T2D serve as the basis for the dynamic environmental factors (the external exposome) that will affect the risk of an individual developing T2D over their lifetimes. The heritable nature of T2D has been well-studied using several methodologies, including twin studies, family pedigrees, and population studies, and it has been demonstrated that genetic factors account for 25–72% of the risk of developing T2D on average [106]. The hereditary nature of T2D creates a dynamic environment in which a variety of genetic polymorphisms creates a diverse array of potential responses to the external exposome. The use of large genetic epidemiology studies like GWAS to systematically catalogue this genetic risk have identified over 500 distinct genetic variants that increase the risk of developing T2D and/or influence glycemic control; most of these variants are common, but their individual effects are modest [101]. The polygenic make-up of these genetic variants implicates many different biological pathways involved in regulating glucose levels including the development/function of pancreatic β-cells like TCF7L2, SLC30A8, action of insulin, biology of adipose tissue, and neuronal control of food intake and energy expenditure as summarized in Fig. 2 [107]. Although there has been a great deal of progress in understanding the genetics of T2D, there are still major gaps in scientific knowledge and research that highlight the need for the exposome model. For example, many common genetic variants (or alleles) associated with T2D that were identified through GWAS explain only a small percentage of the inherited risk of T2D, referred to as the “missing heritability” [107]. The existence of this “missing heritability” suggests that rare genetic variants, structural variation in the genome, complex relationships between genes (called epistasis), and the interaction of environment and genes over time are also contributing to T2D risk, but GWAS alone cannot capture these influences. However, missing heritability may also stem from the accumulation of weak effects contributed by numerous rare functional variants across the exome, a hypothesis aligned with the ‘omnigenic model’, which proposes that association signals propagate throughout the genome via interconnected gene regulatory networks, though this mechanism remains to be fully elucidated [108]. Furthermore, for most of the genetic regions that have been shown to be associated with T2D, researchers have yet to identify the specific gene or functional variant causing the genetic effect; due to the fact that the majority of genetic associations are in non-coding regulatory regions, the relationship between genetic variation and pathology is indirect. Finally, one of the major limitations of current research is that historically, the majority of individuals included in large-scale genetic studies represent people of European descent; this creates limitations in the usefulness of research results globally, can exacerbate existing health disparities by failing to include diverse populations, and can mask potential ancestry-based genetic risk factors and gene-environment interactions [109].

The genome should not be seen as fully causal by itself from an exposome standpoint. Rather, from an exposome perspective, the genome is a fundamental building block of the host’s internal biological environment, provided within that internal biological environment, the genome will influence how environmental exposures are received, processed, and how they impact an individual over the course of their life. Polygenic risk scores (PRS) indicate the general susceptibility of an individual due to the presence or absence of common risk alleles, thus providing the baseline incidence for a genetic background. The genetic background of an individual modifies the dose-response relationship to external exposures, with individuals who have a higher genetic burden of β-cell dysfunction exemplifying that chemical metabolism disruptor (such as some phthalates or other persistent organic pollutants) may induce failure of β-cells at lower doses than would occur in individuals with a lower genetic burden of β-cell dysfunction [110]. Genetic variations associated with insulin resistance serve to exaggerate the harmful health effects of consuming a diet high in ultra-processed foods or of chronic exposures to fine particulate matter (PM₂.₅). The link between genetic risk and environmental exposure is bidirectional; that is to say, while environmental exposures can alter the phenotype that expresses the genetic risk, genetic risk can also influence how we respond to and process environmental exposures. Nutritional intake, chemical exposures, and psychosocial stress can also produce changes in epigenetic mark (like methylation of genes (including PPARGC1A and IRS1)) that cause changes in the expression of genetic risk without changing the actual genetic sequence, thereby creating a record of the impact of the environmental exposures on the regulation of gene [111].

Researchers are now putting much more emphasis on defining the nature of Gene x environment interactions because they are hard to find accurately. The working theory indicates that the explosion of T2D cases is due, not to an increase in gene variants, but due to an historic genetic base colliding with an increasingly obesogenic environment and lifestyle. Additionally, genetics provides a strong means of making causal inferences in exposome research via MR [112]. Genetic variations act as proxy measures for modifiable exposures including using genetic variation in the GCKR gene to represent triglycerides or using genetic variation for BMI to provide evidence for potential direct relations between exposure and T2D. In order to have a complete picture of T2D causation, the genetic map needs to be integrated with both the outside and inside exposures. Conclusively, the genome sets the stage, and the exposome determines the future course: whether an individual genetic risk remains dormant or becomes activated into a trajectory towards metabolic dysfunction, insulin resistance, and clinical disease throughout their lifetime [113].

Epigenome

Epigenetic regulation via DNA methylation, non-coding RNAs, and histone modifications converts environmental exposures into stable yet reversible changes in gene expression. In T2D patients, these epigenetic alterations contribute to metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation and insulin resistance, with differential methylation of genes involved in insulin signaling and mitochondrial biogenesis linked to early-life exposures [114]. Exposures involve from in-utero conditions (e.g., maternal diabetes, undernutrition) to adult factors (e.g., chronic obesity, air pollution). Although the epigenetic landscape is not immutable; the plasticity and partial reversibility suggests that behavioral, nutritional, and targeted pharmacological interventions may remodel adverse epigenetic profiles. These mechanisms also provide evidence that the environment have long-term impacts on insulin signaling, inflammation and cellular function. This can modulate T2D risk as well as progression thereby underscoring the epigenome as a central mediator between the exposome and disease pathophysiology [115, 116].

DNA methylation changes associated with T2D or prediabetic states should not automatically be interpreted as purely environmental or purely causal. Some CpG signals appear to reflect exposure-responsive or downstream disease-associated methylation states, whereas others are partly genetically structured through methylation quantitative trait loci (meQTLs) that link germline variation to local or distal methylation differences [117]. Distinguishing these categories is mechanistically and translationally important: methylation marks that remain informative after accounting for genotype may serve as exposure-responsive biomarkers or intervention readouts, whereas CpGs tightly coupled to meQTLs can be used as causal anchors in mediation analyses and MR frameworks to prioritize regulatory loci for follow-up [117, 118].

Immune and inflammatory networks

In T2D, immune system continually interacts with exposomic factors (environmental triggers, metabolic disturbances), leading to metaflammation (chronic low-grade inflammation) [20]. The visceral fat is the main factor driving metaflammation as large adipocytes develop low oxygen levels and die (hypoxia and necrosis). This causes release damage-associated molecular patterns that attract and activate macrophages from their anti-inflammatory to pro-inflammatory state [20]. Further leading to release in many different cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) which are amplified by external exposures including air pollutants like PM₂.₅ and MDCs such as phthalates and bisphenols [119]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 activate serine kinases (e.g., JNK, IKKβ) impairing its tyrosine phosphorylation and thereby disrupting insulin signalling in liver, muscle, and adipose tissue. IL-1β further contributes to pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and apoptosis [120]. Ultra-processed foods rich in advanced glycation end-products can promote release of endotoxins by increasing gut permeability, lipopolysaccharide translocation and TLR4 activation fostering a systemic pro-inflammatory state. This cascade promotes endothelial dysfunction, creating a pro-atherogenic state leading to microvascular and macrovascular complications of diabetes.

Thus, the immune-inflammatory network underscores the critical role of exposome, where external chemical, dietary, and social stressors are biologically integrated into a persistent inflammatory state leading to systemic complications of T2D as tabulated in Table 2.

Regional heterogeneity and illustrative case study: the United Arab Emirates

Localized exposomic drivers significantly impact disease distribution in rapidly urbanizing environments such as the United Arab Emirates. The UAE is best treated as an illustrative case study of regional heterogeneity rather than as a separate local aside. Studies have shown that diabetes and prediabetes are very common in the UAE with risk being higher in some ethnic groups, especially UAE nationals and certain migrant populations. The main linked factors are older age, obesity, family history, and other metabolic risks, highlighting the need for targeted screening and prevention [127, 128]. This results in divergent prevalence rates and risk profiles between the indigenous Emirati population and diverse expatriate cohorts [127]. This exposome-wide study demonstrated that lifestyle factors accounted for most environmental associations with diabetes, while social and educational exposures emerged as potential causal determinants. For diabetes exposome research in the UAE, these findings highlight the need for a comprehensive approach that integrates lifestyle, sociodemographic, and region-specific environmental factors to better understand diabetes etiology in this population [129].

Challenges, limitations and equity considerations

Research on the exposome and diabetes faces three interconnected challenges: methodological limitations in exposure measurement, analytical difficulties in modeling complex mixtures, and equity concerns regarding disproportionate risk burden on disadvantaged populations.

Methodological limitations

The use of proxy exposure estimates is a basic constraint in exposome-diabetes research. Numerous studies use proxy estimates that do not account for indoor sources, occupational exposure, or individual time-activity patterns (such as simulated residential air pollution levels). This is important for diabetes research since incorrect exposure classification (such as underestimating one’s own exposure to air pollution) can mask actual diabetes risk variables or skew associations toward the null [130]. In addition, individuals encounter combinations of exposures rather than single exposure, which may lead to inaccurate conclusions.

Analytical challenges

The traditional statistical models fail to account for multicollinearity but should be considered in diabetes exposomes as the risk factors for T2D (diet, physical activity, air pollution, stress) are highly correlated in real-world settings. The use of analytical methods such as Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) and weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression are used to characterize additive and interactive effects of exposure mixtures [130], but significant challenges remain. The other rigorous methodological approaches such as MR, sibling-comparison designs, and natural experiments (e.g., policy changes, factory closures) are gaining momentum since observational studies are vulnerable to confounding and reverse causation [131]. MR uses genetic variants as proxies for exposure, sibling-comparison designs help account for shared familial factors, and natural experiments exploit external changes in exposure, such as policy interventions or factory closures, that are less dependent on individual characteristics. Together, these approaches help distinguish true causal effects from biased associations [132]. However, the validity of MR relies on key assumptions, including the absence of horizontal pleiotropy (where the genetic variant affects the outcome via pathways other than the exposure). This violation can introduce bias and remains the subject of ongoing methodological debate.

Equity considerations

An exposomic perspective is based on the fact that a combination of environmental and social risks disproportionately affects socioeconomically disadvantaged and marginalized groups. This is important for preventing diabetes because people from lower socioeconomic backgrounds have a clustered exposome that increases the risk of diabetes because they are frequently exposed to higher levels of air pollution, greater noise exposure, fewer food resources, and less access to green space. A major limitation of current research is the inadequate representation of these populations in exposomic studies. Both issues risk exacerbating existing health inequities rather than reducing the diabetes burden where it is greatest.

In summary, advancing exposome-diabetes research requires: (1) improving exposure assessment beyond proxy estimates, (2) applying mixture modeling approaches while acknowledging their assumptions and limitations and employing rigorous causal inference methods with transparent discussion of their validity conditions, and (3) ensuring equitable representation of disadvantaged populations in research. Without these considerations, results in incomplete and inequitable information being reported within the context of the growing diabetes epidemic.

Implications for prevention, clinical practice and policy

The diabetes research must transition fundamentally to exposome paradigm, rather than focusing solely on single exposures. The focus should be on life-course cohorts which follows range of individuals, external and internal exposures over the lifespan [5, 133]. The exposomic signatures or fingerprints derived both biologically and environmentally can identify these high-risk subgroups. Exposomic risk scores including genetic factors, BMI and geo-spatial exposure) indices should be integrated with internal biomarkers (metabolomic profiles and epigenetic markers) as this improves the identification of appropriate risk factors for an individual(s) thereby facilitating preventative interventions, which may include nutritional support during pregnancy; targeting the microbiome in early life; and targeted screening for individuals residing in high-exposure neighbourhoods. Multi-ancestry GWAS, fine-mapping, and functional genomics can help identify which exposure-sensitive pathways, such as beta-cell fragility, adiposity biology, insulin resistance, immune signaling, or xenobiotic responses, are most relevant in different populations and which environmental factors are most likely to be causal and actionable [102, 103, 134]. By advocating for changes to the existing policy and recommendations that aim at reducing pollution, removing chemicals that disrupt metabolic processes from the environment; promoting stricter marketing regulation for ultra-processed food, and urban designs that allow access to parks, healthcare providers, play a critical role at the broader level to support disease prevention. Translation of exposome science into prevention and regulation depends on robust, sustained long-term science–policy collaborations. International consortia (e.g., LongITools, the Human Exposome Project) are working together to harmonize protocols and bring together different types of data to create evidence that is useful for creating policies by incorporating cumulative and life-course-based data [64], while regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency and the European Food Safety Authority are beginning to embed mixture and co-exposure concepts into risk assessment. Strengthening these research–policy linkages is essential to convert advances in exposomics into more protective and equitable public health policies (Fig. 3).

Conclusions

The global increase in diabetes is a dramatic indication of the failure to apply the mechanistic understanding of diabetes to be an effective preventive measure for populations. The reason for the failure has been the reductive nature of separating “genetic” (i.e., nature) from “lifestyle” (i.e., nurture) which ignores the cumulative effects of all environmental exposures (both physical and social) through the life course. The exposome represents an integrated birth-through-death understanding of external and internal exposures to the body and the biological effects; by having a genetic and lifestyle factor considered together under the exposome, diabetes becomes redefined as the result of ongoing interaction between individual vulnerability and the combined environment.

Within the context of the exposome concept, multiple drivers of disease, including air pollution, dietary contaminants, food insecurity, psychosocial stressors, and urban form, are now viewed as interconnected components of a cumulative exposure environment rather than as separate risk factors. ExWAS facilitate examination of the effects of complex exposures and vulnerable developmental periods when exposure can cause epigenetic changes that influence future metabolic function. Chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and disruption of the gut microbiome provide signatures of an individual’s internal exposome, which is a molecular record of previous exposures. These signatures support the requirement for longitudinal, life-course studies of “risk phenotypes based on exposomics” through the use of sensor-based measurements and multi-omics. Clinically, there is a transition from providing individuals with advice on how to adapt to their toxic environments, to focusing on strategies that intentionally reduce an individual’s exposome according to their personalised exposome score. The exposome conceptual framework clearly exemplifies the intersection between the etiology of diabetes, the health of the planet and social inequities present in society today. Combustion of fossil fuels, industrial and agricultural chemicals and the structural inequalities that result from the above are all leading to the development of diabetes and an increased concentration of risk in the most disadvantaged populations. Consequently, in order to facilitate effective disease prevention, a focus on upstream policy interventions (i.e., climate change mitigation, strong regulation of chemicals, equitable planning of urban areas, food system reform) is required rather than exclusively using the downstream medical approach as summarized in figure 3. As such, diabetes changes from an individual clinical diagnosis to a socio-ecological outcome and the exposome is an integrative concept and operational framework designed to support reversing the diabetes epidemic and creating the conditions that will enable metabolic health to occur at the population level.

Future directions

A key next step is to translate exposome science into pragmatic clinical tools by focusing on limited panels of validated external variables and internal-exposome biomarkers rather than attempting to incorporate the full exposome into practice at once. Such hybrid models may identify individuals in an environmentally mediated high-risk or prediabetic state before overt hyperglycemia develops and could support targeted screening, follow-up, and exposure-informed prevention. Moreover, data from multi-ancestry GWAS, fine-mapping, molecular Quantitative Trait Loci (QTL) analyses, and functional genomics can be integrated with exposomics so that environmental and biological drivers can be studied across diverse populations. Multi-omic risk scores can be developed and linked to electronic health records for clinician interpretation. Explicit attention to data governance, privacy, representativeness across ancestry and exposure groups, and the risk of algorithmic bias could reproduce existing environmental inequities. Prospective validation and equitable implementation should therefore be treated as core translational requirements rather than optional downstream checks. A transformation of the exposome paradigm must move beyond merely describing these epidemiological associations to actively operationalizing this complex data at the point of care. While real-time data collection through wearable devices and geospatial sensors provides the necessary longitudinal exposure inputs, the immediate integration of comprehensive multi-omic, environmental, social data into routine clinical decision-making remains impractical given the current limitations in exposure measurement. To bridge this translational gap, future research must prioritize the development of targeted exposure surveillance systems that monitor well-characterized, high impact environmental risk factors (e.g., air pollution, proximity to green space dietary pattern) in geographically defined populations. Moreover, efforts should be focused on integrating limited set of validated environmental variables, particularly those with strongest and most reproducible epidemiological evidence in to existing clinical risk prediction models for diabetes like family history, BMI and HbA1c levels. Such models could be assisting clinicians and health-care providers in preparing a more well-informed diagnosis based on exposure-informed risk assessment. To achieve this, priority should be placed on use of large, geographically and socioeconomically diverse cohorts of individuals from before conception (in utero, birth, throughout childhood) until mid-life with deep molecular phenotyping to determine the critical window of susceptibility to diabetes-associated risk factors and to clarify the biological pathways of epigenetic programming. Only in this manner can targeted plans to reduce exposure to diabetes risk factors and improve environmental justice for the most burdened populations be formulated. Furthermore, at the policy level, it will be necessary to quantify co-benefits (in terms of prevention of diabetes) of climate change, environmental and chemical regulation and integrate exposomic evidence into development of regulatory and urban planning policies.
