Diversity, geographical distribution and environmental adaptations of snow molds
Authors: Tamotsu Hoshino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In regions of cryosphere, plants dormant under snow are attacked by snow mold pathogens, which include various fungal taxa (mainly oomycetes, ascomycetes and basidiomycetes). These fungi are well adapted to the environment with ambient temperatures fluctuating at around 0 °C. Their cold adaptation mechanisms differ at the phylum level. Oomycetous and basidiomycetous snow molds avoid freezing by intracellular host infection and ice-binding protein, respectively. Osmophily is another mechanism to cope with freezing for the ascomycete, Sclerotinia borealis. Thus diverse fungal taxa evolved snow molds to adapt to nival environments, illustrating adaptive radiations by developing cold tolerance and selecting host organisms.

1. Introduction: Fungi in the cryosphere

Approximately 80% of the biosphere are constantly or seasonally cold with temperatures below 5 °C. Such cold regions exist mostly in Polar regions, occupying 14% of the terrestrial biosphere (e.g., Gounot, 1999) and referred to as the cryosphere. The term cryosphere was proposed by the Polish scientist, A. B. Dobrowolski and collectively describes the portions of the Earth's surface where water exists as the frozen state-snow cover, glaciers, ice sheets and shelves, freshwater ice, sea ice, icebergs, permafrost, and ground ice (Barry & Gan, 2011). The cryosphere creates extreme environments seasonally in temperate and frigid zones and often disappears in summer.

A smaller number of fungal species were recorded from the cryosphere as compared to the temperate zone despite that major fungal taxa have already been found in the cryosphere (Hoshino et al., 2013b). Fungi normally have different cells in their life cycles; therefore, thermal dependence differs, according to their life cycle stages and is completely different from that of bacteria. Several cold-adapted fungi fit the concept of “psychrophile” proposed by Morita (1975) that has a wide growth temperature range from subzero temperatures to below 20 °C; however, Morita's definition was not applicable to some fungi, and another term “cryophilic fungi” was proposed for those that spend a certain life stage or whole life cycle (sexual and/or asexual stages) in the cryosphere (Hoshino & Matsumoto, 2012; Hoshino et al., 2013b). Abiotic and biotic environmental factors select physiological characteristics of the cryophilic fungi (Fig. 1: Hoshino & Matsumoto, 2013).

My co-authors and I have already described the diversity of the cryophilic fungal phytopathogens, i.e., snow molds and their environmental adaptations in this journal (Hoshino et al., 2009b) and other book chapters (Hoshino et al., 2013a, 2013b, 2013c). In this review, I refer to cryophic fungal phytopathogens as snow molds collectively and described their taxonomic progress and ecophysiological adaptation mechanisms to freezing elucidated since 2009 and added interesting facts that were not mentioned in the previous review but illustrated in the above book chapters.

2. Diversity of snow molds

2.1. Snow molds of agricultural crops

Snow molds attack dormant plants, such as forage crops, winter cereals, and conifer seedlings, at low temperatures under snow cover (Fig. 2: Hoshino et al., 2013c; Matsumoto & Hsiang, 2016). The first observations were probably made on rotted winter cereals under snow in Ichinoseki, Iwate and Kurihara, Miyagi, Japan in 1631 (Hoshino, 2018), and Eriksson (1879) published a scientific report on winter injury of winter crops in Sweden. These fungi can attack dormant plants at low temperatures under snow. Snow cover protects overwintering plants from freezing and maintains darkness, humidity, and low temperature. These environmental factors along with physiological deterioration of host plants characterize snow molds as opportunistic pathogens (Matsumoto & Hoshino, 2009). “Snow molds” or “snow mold fungi” is a generic name including diverse fungi in temperate to fridge zones belonging to various taxa i.e., oomycetes, ascomycetes, and basidiomycetes, but chytridiomycetes or traditional zygomycetes are not included. Molecular phylogenic analyses have dramatically changed taxonomic position and nomenclature of snow molds.

Snow cover affects the mode of epidemiology snow molds, and they are divided into two categories, i.e., facultative and obligate snow molds (Matsumoto, 1994). Sclerotinia borealis Bubák & Vleugel complex, Typhula incarnata Lasch and the T. ishikariensis S. Imai complex are obligate snow molds that prevail exclusively under snow and tolerant to cold with different mechanisms. Oomycetes (Globisporangium spp.) and ascomycete [e.g., Microdochium nivale (Fr.) Samuels & I.C. Hallett and S. nivalis I. Saito] are facultative snow molds and can develop without snow even in summer when it is cool and damp. They are moderately tolerant to cold. Though fungal pathogens are less frequently recorded from the cryosphere than those from the temperate zone, their diversity in adaptation strategy is considerably high and needs further investigations to reveal the complex ecosystem of the cryosphere.

2.2. Snow molds of conifer seedlings

Snow molds of conifer seedlings and acorns are mostly ascomycetes (Fig. 2: Hoshino et al., 2004, 2009b, 2013a) (important pathogens are shown in bold).

Allantophomopsis cytisporea (Fr.) Petr. (Sieber, 2019): This fungus is known as the causal agent of cranberry black rot on Vaccinium macrocarpon Aiton but has never been described as a snow mold. Allantophomopsis cytisporea is an endophyte in Calluna vulgaris (L.) Hull but seems able to kill leaflets and whole conifer shoots during winter. Caloscypha fulgens (Pers.) Boud. in Hokkaido [Ezo-raigan disease, non Agrocybe arvalis (Fr.) Heim & Romagn.], Japan (Ohmae et al., 2021), Chionobium takahashii Iwakiri & Hirooka [syn. Gremmenia abietis (Dearn.) Crous in Japan; Iwakiri et al., 2024], Ciboria pseudotuberosa (Cooke) Rehm (Ichihara et al., 2010; Matsumoto, 2009), Colletotrichum dematium (Pers.) Grove (Sahashi et al., 1995), Gremmenia abietis, G. infestans (P. Karst.) Crous (Crous et al., 2013a): Phylogenic analyses of ITS sequences of Phacidium infestans P. Karst. (syn: G. infestans) strains showed the presence of different clades (Zerouki et al., 2023), suggesting the polyphyly of the fungus. Mesophilic ascomycetes G. abietina var. abietina Dearn. and var. balsamea Petrini, L. E. Petrini, Lafl. & Ouell. cause scleroderris canker of conifers in summer (Lijia et al., 2010) and can grow at subzero temperatures, causing snow blight (Yokota et al., 1974). Winter temperatures influence infection rate; plants were more seriously diseased at -7 and -3 °C than at 0 °C (Petäistö & Laine, 1999). These findings suggest that vars of G. abietina are active in a wide temperature range from winter to summer in the cryosphere. Another ascomycete Microsphaeropsis sp., causing canker on Cornus controversa Hemsl. has similar life cycle on conifer (Akimoto, 1992).

Herpotrichia juniperi (Duby ex Rabenh.) Petr. (syn: Racodium therryanum Thüm.) in Asia (Cho et al., 2007; Iwakiri et al., 2021a; Sakamoto & Miyamoto, 2015). (2021b) developed microsatellite markers for population studies such as population structure, recombination and genetic diversity of R. therryanum. They observed insignificant linkage disequilibrium to imply genetic recombination. New record in Turkey (Oskay et al., 2011). Herpotrichia nigra Hartig, Neonectria candida (Ehrenb.) Rossman, L. Inval.]) in Volga-Ural, Russia (Hoshino et al., 2004).

2.3. Snow molds of snow algae (snow fungi)

Chytrids mostly with flagella attack snow algae on the surface of snow (Table 1; Fig. 2: Hoham & Duval, 2001; Sparrow, 1960). Their resting spores germinate in the soil under snow, and flagellated cells swim up the thaw water surrounding snow crystals until they reach the surface of snowpack (Hoham & Duval, 2001). The chytrids represent a dominant fungal group under the snow cover (Freeman et al., 2009; Schmidt et al., 2012) and are typical algal pathogens in aquatic ecosystems (Sparrow, 1960). With the onset of spring, melting snow adds nutrients to freshwater lakes, and the rise in temperature facilitates the rapid growth of algal phytoplankton. Soon after the algal bloom begins, the growth of parasitic zoosporic true fungi causes “chytrid epidemic” (Gleason et al., 2015; Sparrow, 1960). Chytrid epidemic may also occur on the snow based on the similar mechanism. These facts suggest that snow chytrids play a role in the carbon cycling within a bloom by parasitism and constitute a part of the snow food web (Naff et al., 2013). Ascomycete, Oospora nivalis [Nom. Inval.] coexisted with Chloromonas nivalis (Chodat) Hoham & Mullet but its pathogenicity was ambiguously described (Kol, 1939).

2.4. Interactions with other organisms under snow

Sclerotia of snow molds are liable to the attack of diverse mycoparasites. Matsumoto (1989) isolated 47 fungi from sclerotia of T. incarnata and T. ishikariensis to reveal mycoparasitism in eight fungi. They included traditional diverse fungal taxa, i.e., zygomycetes: Mortierella hyalina (Harz) W. Gams and Mucor hiemalis Wehmer, ascomycetes: Clonostachys rosea (Link) Schroers, Samuels, Seifert & W. Gams, Hormonema sp., Lecanicillium lecanii (Zimm.) Zare & W. Gams, Paraconiothyrium minitans (W. A. Campb.) Verkley, Trichoderma harzianum Rifai, and T. pseudokoningii Rifai. A basidiomycete, Cylindrobasidium parasiticum D. A. Reid is a mycoparasite of sclerotia of T. incarnata in Scotland (Woodbridge et al., 1989), and ascomycetes Episclerotium sclerotiorum (Rostr.) L. M. Kohn and E. sclerotipus (Boud.) L. M. Kohn also (Kohn & Nagasawa, 1984) attack the sclerotia of M. phacorrhiza and Sclerotinia spp. Saito et al. (2007) successfully cultured E. sclerotipus on the agar medium containing the hot water-extract of M. phacorrhiza sclerotia. However, when cultured on the dialysis membrane placed on the sclerotium-extract agar, the growth of E. sclerotipus was strongly impaired, suggesting the involvement of polymeric component from M. phacorrhiza sclerotia in the growth of the mycoparasite. Episclerotium sclerotipus grew in the range of 0-20 °C but failed to grow above 25 °C. Dual cultures of E. sclerotipus with M. phacorrhiza or four Typhula spp. on sclerotium-extract agar failed to reveal mycoparasitism.

Several microbes are antagonistic against snow molds (e.g., Torracchi et al., 2020). Trichoderma polysporum (Link) Rifai from Svalbard (Yamazaki et al., 2011) produced four compounds inhibiting mycelial growth of G. iwayamae (Kamo et al., 2016). Antagonistic fluorescent pseudomonads increased in Feb on grass leaves under snow (Matsumoto & Tajimi, 1987). Pseudomonas spp. from various soils in cold-climates inhibited mycelial growth of the T. ishikariensis complex (Hoshino et al., 2019). Paenibacillus macquariensis subsp. defensor inhibited the production of sclerotia by the T. ishikariensis complex (Hoshino et al., 2009a).

Three species of cold-adapted nematodes (Aphelenchoides saprophillus Fanklin, Paraphelenchus tritici Baranovskaya, and Aphelenchus avenae Bastian) fed on the mycelia of M. nivale in Russia (Tkachenko et al., 2015). Among these mycotrophic species, A. saprophillus most effectively reproduced in culture. These nematodes substantially reduced the degree of disease development and improved the growth of winter wheat in field trials.

Polar oomycetous snow mold, G. polare (Tojo, van West & Hoshino) H. D. T. Nguyen & C. F. J. Spies contains three different virus-like sequences (Sasaki et al., 2018). We determined the full-length genome sequence of one of them. The 5397 nt-length genome had two overlapped open reading frames consistent with a toti and toti-like viruses, that we named Pythium polare RNA virus 1 (PpRV1). Globisporangium polare has been isolated from two localities more than 100 km apart from each other in Svalbard, i.e., Longyearbyen (78°13’N, 15°38’E) and Ny-Ålesund (78°55’N, 11°55’E). Molecular phylogenetic analysis was performed using the nucleotide sequences of the coat protein gene of PpRV1 recovered from three strains from Longyearbyen and four strains from Ny-Ålesund. PpRV1 was subsequently divided into two groups based on the host localities with ca. 95% homology, which was contrasting to intra-locality homology of ca. 99% for both Longyearbyen and Ny-Ålesund strains. These results suggest that G. polare and PpRV1 evolved independently in each locality (Mochizuki, 2020).

The presence of a novel fungal structure has recently been found from myxomycetes under snow (Gmoshinskiy & Kireeva, 2023; Kuhnt, 2019; Yajima et al., 2013). Cyst-like fungal bodies were present in the sporophores of nivicolous myxomycetes Lamproderma sp. and L. echinosporum Meyl. Partial sequences of 18S rDNA gene from such Lamproderma sp. contained a novel sequence, suggesting that cyst-like fungal bodies belonged to the Rozellomycota (Corsaro et al., 2020; Yajima et al., 2013). Since Rozellomycota are phagotrophic parasites, the behavior of the cyst-like fungal bodies should be further studied to elucidate the host-parasite interactions under snow.

Sieber (2019) observed interactions of two tree snow molds, Allantophomopsis cytisporea and Herpotrichia pinetorum (Fuckel) G. Winter in the Alps and discussed the effect of climate change on their interactions with native plants. They have always been present in the ecosystem, causing damage to native tree species. However, the shortening of snow cover duration diminishes the length of time to spread under the snow, leading to a reduction of their mycelia. The reduction probably has more serious consequences for H. pinetorum than for A. cytisporea. Allantophomopsis cytisporea seems to present as an endophyte in the Ericaceae during spring and summer while H. pinetorum is dormant. The absence of a resting period and the use of C. vulgaris as a refuge could provide an advantage for A. cytisporea. The pink snow mold fungus, M. majus is the endophyte of grapevine and also the necrotrophic mycoparasite of Botrytis cinerea Pers. at 25 °C (Váczy et al., 2025).

A fungus, believed to be an ascomycetous snow mold, P. infestans extend web-like mycelia over the surface of snowbank during snowmelt (Hoham & Duval, 2001). Snow algae, Chloromonas cryophila Hoham & Mullet and C. pichinchae Wille are frequency found adhered to the surface of the fungal mycelia, but there is no evidence of symbiosis as is found in lichens. Although microscopic observations have not been made on the web-like fungus, the fungus and the algae may possibly exchange metabolites extracellularly.

3. Geographical distribution of snow molds

Simulated mean snowfall changes predicted a decrease by 30-45% in low latitude cryosphere such as Europe to Central Asia (Barry & Gan, 2011). Snowfall decrease should affect the biota of cryophilic fungi, and we need to elucidate their present distribution so that the effect of oncoming climatic change may be predicted on the mycobiota in the cryosphere.

3.1. Snow molds in Polar regions

Globisporangium iwayamae, one of the most prevalent oomycetous snow molds, has not been so far found in the Arctic or Antarctica; however a heterothallic species, G. polare was isolated from moribund moss tissues in both poles (Tojo et al., 2012). Symptoms on mosses caused by G. polare were the same, and isolates from both poles were sexually compatible. The fungus was highly homologous with G. iwayamae in ITS regions. Globisporangium sp. was also obtained from Entodontaceae moss growing on the ground surface along a road in Kuujjuarapik-Whapmagoostui, Quebec, Canada (Hoshino et al., 2021). This isolate was most closely related to G. okanoganense based on phylogenetic analyses using rDNA-ITS. Tojo et al. (2021) surveyed the species compound of Globisporangium spp. and their long-term isolation pattern in Sanionia moss in Ny-Ålesund, Svalbard from 2006 to 2018. Six species of Globisporangium spp. were isolated from moss colonies. The total isolation frequency of Globisporangium gradually decreased throughout the survey period. Isolation frequencies varied among the six species, and isolation frequencies of four of them were highest in 2006 and drastically reduced after 2016. The results suggested that Globisporangium inhabiting Sanionia moss in Ny-Ålesund has a unique composition of species and that most of the species reduced their population over the last decade.

In GBIF (Global Biodiversity Information Facility), DNA of M. nivale was detected in subarctic (Northern Norway and Iceland) and continental Antarctica (near Casey Station) (https://www.gbif.org/species/3464089). This fungus also existed in the subantarctic lakes of King George Is. (Gonçalves et al., 2012). Endophytic M. phragmitis Syd. & P. Syd. was also isolated from Antarctic hair grass Deschampsia antarctica É. Desv. along with M. nivale (Santiago et al., 2012). Two other fungi from moss of King George Is., i.e., M. lypodium (Jaklitsch, Siepe & Voglmayr) Hern.-Restr. & Crous and Herpotrichia sp. were highly homologous with snow molds, M. nivale and H. juniperi, respectively (Kim et al., 2025). However, there is no disease record of the above three species in Antarctica.

Sclerotium-forming snow molds, the S. borealis complex, T. incarnata and the T. ishikariensis complex are widely distributed in not only the cool temperate to frigid zones but also in Arctic regions (summarized by Hoshino et al., 2022; Saito et al., 2024) such as Alaska (Cao et al., 2025) and Yukon, Greenland, Finnmark (northern Norway), Iceland (S. borealis not found), Lapland (northern Finland and Sweden), Kola Peninsula in European Russia, Novaya Zemlya, Russia (only T. incarnata recorded: Shiryaev, 2006) and Svalbard. Records of T. incarnata are meager as compared to those of T. ishikariensis. The distribution patterns of the T. ishikariensis complex suggest that these fungi are highly adapted to the Arctic environment (Hoshino et al., 2023). However, S. borealis and T. ishikariensis were absent from Antarctica. Sclerotinia antarctica Gamundi & Spinedi existed on Antarctic hair grass in Antarctic peninsula. The fungus is morphological similar to S. borealis (Gamundi & Spinedi, 1987) and needs comparisons with S. borealis. We also found another fungal disease on the moss, Polytrichum juniperium Hedw. in King George Is., Antarctica (Yajima et al., 2017). The fungus had clamped hyphae but lacked sclerotia. Its DNA sequence of ITS region perfectly matched with Typhula sp. obtained from grass leaves in Macquarie Is. in the maritime Antarctica and was highly homologous with Typhula cf. subvariabilis from Iran (see below). These findings suggest that three genera of representative snow molds Globisporangium, Sclerotinia and Typhula exist in both Polar regions.

Some species are recognized as snow molds endemic to both Poles (Tojo & Newsham, 2012). Though usually regarded as saprobes in the temperate zone, an ascomycete Trichoderma polysporum caused asympomatic infections on the moss Sanionia uncinata (Hedw.) Loeske on Svalbard (Yamazaki et al. 2011). Basidiomycetous Microbotryum bistortarum (DC.) Vánky, the smut fungus, was also shown to be deleterious to the growth and survival of Alpine Bistort, Bistorta viviparum (L.) Delarbre under snow cover (Denchev et al., 2020; Tojo & Nishitani, 2005). The fungus also occurs in Svalbard (Tojo & Nishitani, 2005). The life cycle of M. bistortarum is similar to that of the scleroderris canker disease fungus G. abietina in the temperate cryosphere. Zygomycetous snow molds are not found in the Northern Hemisphere, but Rhizopus sp. was found to be pathogenic on the moss, Hennediella heimii (Hedw.) R. H. Zander at Cape Bird on Ross Island, Antarctica (Greenfield, 1983). Several ascomycetes such as Bryosphaeria megaspore Spooner, Epibryon chorisodontii Spooner, E. turfosorum (Mouton) Döbbeler, Mitt., Thyronectria antarctica var. hyperantarctica D. Hawksw., and an unidentified cleistothecial ascomycete were recorded from infected mosses (summarized by Tojo & Newsham, 2012). A smaller number of cryophilic fungi exist in Polar regions than in temperate regions (Hoshino et al., 2013b), and mycoflora in Polar regions are unique as compared to other regions of cryosphere in temperate and frigid zones. Therefore, there must be new niches for cryophilic fungal phytopathogens in Polar regions, and novel cryophilic fungi are likely to have evolved as phytopathogens to adapt to the local climate and vegetation.

3.2. Snow molds in low latitudes

Psychrophily delimits the distribution range of snow molds. Among psychrophilic snow molds (no mycelial growth above 20 °C), low-latitude records of the S. borealis complex were obtained from Altai republic in Russia, Xinjiang in China, and Kuzumaki (39°56’N, 141°28’E), Iwate, Japan (Hoshino et al., 2009b; Nakajima, 1998; Ozawa & Sato, 1986). Botrytis cirsii-spinosissimi (Senn-Irlet) Baral (Index Fungorum no. 454, 2020; Senn-Irlet & Peter, 2016) were found in Cirsium spinosissimum (L.) Scop. in Uri (46°47’N, 8°37’E), Alps. This fungus is phylogeneticaly close to S. borealis in the Scletotiniaceae. Southern geographical distribution limits of the T. ishikariensis complex are European Alps (Franchi & Marchetti, 2021; Hoshino et al., 2022) and T. canadensis in Mt. Gozaisho (35°01’N, 136°25’E), Mie, Japan (Hoshino et al., 2009b). Probably, the distribution of T. canadensis in Mt. Gozaisho was an artifact incited by transplanting of grass sods from Nagano, the mountainous region on Japanese main island.

Psychrotrophic snow molds (mycelia can grow above 20 °C) are more widely distributed than psychrophilic snow molds. Sclerotinia trifoliorum and seed-borne M. nivale are most widely spread (Matsumoto & Hsiang, 2016). Oomycetous snow molds, G. okanoganense and G. kandovanense were isolated from northern Iran (Bouket et al., 2015; Mostowfizageh-Ghalamfarsa & Banihashemi, 2005). Records of ascomycetous snow molds include S. nivalis, Zhushan (32°13’N, 110°13’E), Hubei, China (Fan et al., 2012) and H. juniperi in Taurus Mountains (37°N, 33°E) of southern Turkey (Oskay et al., 2011). For basidiomycetous snow molds: M. phacorrhiza in Ardabil (38°15’N, 48°17’E), northern Iran (Hoshino et al., 2007) and in Himachal Pradesh and Kullu, India (Khurana, 1980), T. incarnata in Huesca (42°10’N, 0°10’W), Spain (Ribes, 2010), Avigliana (45°5’N, 7°24’E), Italy (Titone et al., 2003), Hakkâri (37°′N22’, 43°72’E) and Antalya (36°53′N, 30°42′E), southern Turkey (Kesici et al., 2022; Ünal, 2023) and T. variabilis in areas with rare snow cover such as Pico Is. (38°27’N, 28°20’W), the Azores (Bensaude, 1926). These findings suggested that potential growth temperatures of cryophilic pathogens were one of the factors to delimit their distributions.

3.3. Effects of climate change on snow mold flora

Different taxa of snow molds have different strategies to adapt diverse environments under snow cover (Hoshino et al., 2013c). For example, S. borealis and T. ishikariensis have different reproduction strategies (e.g., Matsumoto & Hsiang, 2016); while S. borealis is air-borne, dispersing ascospores, T. isihikariensis is typically soil-borne to spread almost exclusively by mycelial extension in winter and possibly by sclerotial dissemination in summer. Its small, uniform sclerotia are characteristic of soilborne pathogens. Basidiospores are not regarded as effective propagule in T. ishikariensis (Matsumoto & Hsiang, 2016). The S. borealis complex are distributed in areas with severe winters characterized by deep soil frost before snow cover (e.g., Hoshino et al., 2010; Tomiyama, 1955). Among the four localities in Svalbard, S. borealis var. borealis and T. hyperborea were found exclusively in Barentsburg (78°04’N, 14°13’E), the warmest settlement in 1999 and 2000 (Hoshino et al., 2003). Later in 2007, my collaborator, Prof. Dr. Tojo later collected S. borealis var. borealis in Longyearbyen and Ny-Ålesund, Svalbard (Hoshino et al., 2011; Saito et al., 2024). Recent climate change probably favors the extension of its distribution in Svalbard.

In the lower latitude snowy region, Hokkaido, the northmost island of Japan, the recent tendency towards global warming has certainly changed snow mold flora. Tomiyama (1955) separated Hokkaido into the Sclerotinia and Typhula areas. Sclerotinia borealis var. borealis is an epidemic snow mold and occurs irregularly in eastern Hokkaido in years with deep soil frost and thin snow cover (Sclerotinia area, Fig. 3: Matsumoto & Hoshino, 2013). The winter of 1974-1975 favored the outbreak of S. borealis var. borealis in eastern Hokkaido by the late onset of snow cover, predisposing hosts to the attack of the pathogen and by deep snow fall late in the winter which delayed thawing. This snow mold has seldom occurred since then in eastern Hokkaido, mainly due to mild winters, characterized by the early onset of persistent snow cover. Recent climate change has reduced soil frost in the Sclerotinia area in eastern Hokkaido (Hirota et al., 2006). On the other hand, Typhula spp. occur in western Hokkaido characterized by unfrozen soil and thick snow cover (Typhula area). Subsequent studies revealed that T. ishikariensis var. isihkariensis, attacking both di- and monocots was the main snow mold fungus in western Hokkaido and that T. canadensis, pathogenic exclusively on monocots was prevalent in less snowy areas, including eastern Hokkaido. The outbreak of T. ishikariensis var. ishikariensis in eastern Hokkaido in 1999 made us realize that T. ishikariensis var. ishikariensis was replacing T. canadensis and S. borealis var. borealis. In conclusion the distinction between Sclerotinia and Typhula ares is not absolute and changeable according to winter climate.

Climatic change also affects the occurrence of Typhula spp. Typhula ishikariensis var. ishikariensis and T. incarnata were dominant snow molds in Naganuma, western Hokkaido before 1990 (Fig. 4). The former requires ca. 120 d of snow cover, and the latter ca. 60 d (Matsumoto & Hsiang, 2016). Annual snow cover days in Naganuma during 1974-1987 with an exception of 1976 exceeded 120 d and reduced, fluctuating between 105 and 120 d in 1988-1995. Typhula ishikariensis var. ishikariensis was not observed since 1988, but T. incarnata still remained. These findings indicated that global warming was ongoing to change winter climate, which consequently affected the occurrence of snow molds.

Shimoda et al. (2023) reported that snow compaction (commonly known as “Yuki-Fumi”) reduced snow mold incidence in winter wheat fields. Snow mold disease is considered to occur when the soil temperature under snow remains constant at around 0 °C. “Yuki-fumi” shortened the period of the optimal temperature range and consequently snow mold was reduced. This research result is useful for stabilizing wheat production as an environmentally friendly disease control measure that does not rely on chemical pesticides.

4. Ecophysiological and biochemical characteristics of snow molds

Snow molds are taxonomically diverse and can grow at subzero temperatures and tolerate freeze-thaw event, using different strategies. Many studies have been carried out on snow molds from plant protection view point. New findings regarding their ecophysiological and biochemical characteristics have been reported during the past decade by a few researchers, including the author's group. In this chapter, I show various strategies of snow molds against freezing.

4.1. Genomic overview of snow molds

A genome linkage map of T. ishikariensis var. ishikariensis was constructed by Chang and Jung (2008). Genome sequences were obtained from three ascomycetous snow molds, M. nivale F_00608 (Kazan Scientific Center of Russian Academy of Sciences) (Tsers et al., 2023), P. infestans DSM 5139 (Zerouki et al., 2023) and S. borealis var. borealis VKM-F4128 (Mardanov et al., 2014a, 2014b). The transcriptome analysis combined with extracellular enzymatic assays was carried out to assess the effect of host plant metabolites on the M. nivale phenotype (Tsers et al., 2023). The results indicated the crucial role of host plant lipid destruction and fungal lipid metabolism modulation in plant-M. nivale interactions.

Genome-mining of P. infestans (Zerouki et al., 2023) revealed numerous putative biosynthetic gene clusters that coded virulence factors and fungal toxins. Pisatin demethylase was indicative of the potential of this fungus to detoxify the terpenoid phytoalexins produced by its host as a defense mechanism. Proteomic analysis (Zerouki et al., 2023) revealed the potential survival strategies under the snow, which included the production of ice-binding proteins (IBPs), trehalose synthesis enzymes, desaturases, proteins related to elongation of very long-chain fatty acids, and stress protein responses. Genomic and metabolomic analyses of P. infestans (Zerouki et al., 2023) showed its metabolic processes involved in secretome production to adapt freezing temperatures through the production of osmoprotectants and cryoprotectants.

Badet et al. (2015) made genomic comparisons between S. borealis var. borealis and related mesophic species of S. sclerotiorum (Lib.) de Bary and B. cinerea. Enrichment in Thr, depletion in Glu and Lys, and low disorder frequency in hot loops were significantly associated with S. borealis var. borealis proteins as compared to the related fungi. Specific signatures of amino acid usage in S. borealis var. borealis were consistent with previous observations on psychrophilic prokaryotes. Hot loops, corresponding to protein regions did not predict a helix or strand secondary structure with a high degree of flexibility. Therefore, low disorder frequency in hot loops contradicted with their protein functions of psychrophily. Sclerotinia borealis var. borealis, S. sclerotiorum and B. cinerea notably had similar G+C contents, and consequently the G+C bias did not seem to affect the differential usage of amino acids. Plant cell wall-degrading enzymes, carbohydrate binding domain containing proteins and ion binding proteins play important roles in the cryophilic lifecycle of S. borealis var. borealis.

4.2. Ice-binding proteins (IBPs) of snow molds

Various snow molds other than oomycetes are freeze tolerant (Hoshino et al., 2009b, 2010), even though antifreeze activity was recognized in a non-snow mold oomycete from Antarctica (Xiao et al., 2010a). IBPs play a major role for them to prevail at subzero temperatures, inhibiting the growth of ice crystal by attaching to its surface: the phenomenon referred to as thermal hysteresis. Basidiomycetous snow molds produce extracellular IBPs by means of thermal hysteresis (Hoshino et al., 2009b): the phenomenon unique to basidiomycetous snow molds. IBPs have not been found in oomycetous or ascomycetous snow molds, as well as the phyla of traditional Zygomycota or Chytridiomycota (Arai et al., 2019).

Fungal IBP homologs are widely distributed in different kingdoms, which possibly occurred through horizontal gene transfer (HGT) between organisms (Białkowska et al., 2020). IBP genes of T. hyperborea are highly homologous to those coding unknown proteins from the mushroom, Lentinula edodes (Berk.) Pegler (Raymond & Janech, 2009). The deduced protein of Leucosporidium sp. Isolated from Arctic pond showed high sequence identity with other IBPs and hypothetical IBPs from fungi, diatoms, and bacteria, clustering with a class of ice-active proteins (Lee et al., 2010). The Antarctic, non-snow mold ascomycete, Antarctomyces psychrotrophicus Stchigel & Guarro has two isoforms of IBP, which have low sequence similarity to each other and are positioned separately in the phylogenetic tree. It is hence presumable that these two IBP isoforms were independently acquired from different bacteria through HGT, which implies that ascomycetes and bacteria frequently exchange their IBP genes (Arai et al., 2019). In addition, antifreeze activity was detected in culture filtrates of various fungal taxa and oomycete in Antarctica (Xiao et al., 2010a, 2010b).

IBPs of T. hyperborea (TisAFPs) adsorbed in both the basal and prism planes of hexagonal ice crystals (Cheng et al., 2016). IBP-bound ice crystals exhibited a dendritic structure in six directions and thermal hysteresis at high temperatures, i.e., approximately 2 °C, as was the case with insect hyperactive IBPs (Xiao et al., 2010b, 2014). Thus, IBPs secreted in the extracellular environment do not directly support mycelial growth at subzero temperatures. They probably attach to extracellular polysaccharides (EPS), covering the surface of mycelia, and consequently, the concentrated IBPs in EPS prohibit ice crystal growth near mycelia (Fig. 5: Hoshino et al., 2013a). The basidiomycetous yeast, Glaciozyma antarctica (Fell, Statzell, I. L. Hunter & Phaff) M. Groenew. & Q. M. Wang, produces frost-columnar colonies on agar plates frozen at -1 °C (Fujiu et al., 2021; Tsuji, 2023). Colonies were highly viscous when thawed, indicating that the yeast produced EPS in large quantity. Moreover, the yeast produced IBPs a little, inhibiting ice crystal growth. Extracellular IBPs were considered to accumulate in unfrozen water in the pores of the frost-columnar colony. Microdochium nivale does not produce IBPs but produces EPS such as cellulose and fructan (Hoshino et al., 2009b). These EPS of M. nivale are considered to bind plant antifungal peptides to nullify their activities (Hoshino et al., 2009b; Isobe et al., 2021).

Kondo et al. (2012) used X-ray crystallography to reveal the three-dimensional structure of TisAFP6 (one isoform of IBPs from T. hyperborea), which was homologous to that of an Arctic yeast Leucosporidium sp. (Lee et al. 2012). Both proved to have a β-helix structure, which was composed of stacked β-sheets. Khan et al. (2021) characterized isoforms TisAFP6, TisAFP7, and TisAFP8, which showed moderate, intermediate, and high thermal hysteresis values, respectively. The order was not correlated with the thermal stability. Genetically-modified bacterial IBPs was thermo-stable and remained active at different pH conditions (Nam et al., 2024). Marine bacterial IBP retained their secondary structures between pH 6 and pH 10 (Delesky et al., 2021). If these bacterial IBPs share similar biochemical characteristics with those of the T. ishikariensis complex with an optimum mycelial growth at pH 6 to 10 (Smith, 1987), the capacity of T. ishikariensis to produce various isoforms with different biochemical characteristics implies adaptions to diverse frozen environments.

4.3. Ecophysiological strategies of oomycetous snow molds

Oomyceteous snow molds, Globisporangium species, are less tolerant to low temperatures than other snow mold taxa (Murakami et al., 2015). However in Polar regions, Globisporangium species exist as pathogens on mosses (e.g., Tojo et al., 2012). Murakami et al. (2015) investigated the frost resistance of Globisporangium species from frigid (Hokkaido, Japan) and subantarctic regions. Free mycelia and hyphal swellings, i.e., structures for survival, lost viability after freeze-thaw cycles repeated three times; however, mycelia in host plants survived the treatment (Fig. 5).

Similar mechanisms may be found in fungi conserved in permafrost; the presence of natural cryoprotectants such as plant substrates or derivatives in permafrost and the ability of the fungi as phytopathogens to utilize the inherent mechanisms of protection of host plants (Ozerskaya et al., 2009). Cold-acclimated plants store cryoprotectants in their cytosol, and Globisporangium spp. in infected tissues, probably utilize host cryoprotectants to tolerate freezing. Stakhov et al. (2008) demonstrated that ancient seeds of higher plants constituted a specific habitat for microorganisms in frozen ground, which favored their survival for millennia.

4.4. Ecophysiological strategies of ascomycetous snow molds

Sclerotinia borealis is well adapted to severe winter climate characterized by deep soil frost and thin snow cover (e.g., Matsumoto & Hsiang, 2016). In most cases, epidemic of S. borealis starts from infection by airborne ascospores dispersed from apothecia. Under the arid environmental conditions in interior Alaska with rainfall less than 31.5 mm per annum, carpogenic sclerotium germination (germination to develop fruiting body) seldom occurs in S. borealis, and instead, the fungus infects through myceliogenic germination (McBeath, 2002).

Sclerotinia borealis, unlike basidiomycetous snow molds, does not produce extracellular cryoprotectants such as IBPs (e.g., Hoshino et al., 2009b) although IBP gene was present in the genome of S. borealis (Arai et al., 2019). The fungus has another physiologic strategy to adapt to the frozen environment, i.e., xerophily. Sclerotinia borealis var. borealis showed normal mycelial growth under the frozen condition, and mycelial growth rate on frozen plates at -1 °C was faster than that on unfrozen potato dextrose agar (PDA) at its optimal growth temperature range between 4-10 °C (Fig. 5: Hoshino et al., 2010). The S. borealis complex can grow at low water potentials on agar plates containing KCl as well as D-sorbitor. An increase in intracellular osmosis enhanced mycelial growth and shifted the optimal mycelial growth temperature from 10-15 °C to 4 °C (Fig. 6). However, mycelial growth of other snow molds such as S. nivalis and S. trifoliorum was inhibited at low water potential (Hoshino et al., 2009b, 2010). Therefore, osmophily of the S. borealis complex was probably a unique feature to adapt severe freezing (Hoshino et al., 2013a).

4.5. Interactions with environment in basidiomycetous snow molds

Typhulaceae is one of the cold-adapted fungal families in basidiomycetes, including snow molds. The representative genera, Typhula and Pistillaria are distinguished by the discontinuity between stems and hymenia in the former and the continuity in the latter (Fries, 1818). This taxonomic criterion is ambiguous, and consequently, the view of Karsten (1882) has been widely accepted: Typhula develops basidiocarps from sclerotia, while basidiocarps develop directly from substrata in Pistillaria. However, Corner (1970) observed basidiocarps of Pistillaria petasitis S. Imai developing from sclerotia in Hokkaido, Japan, while Hoshino et al. (2023) recognized that P. petasitis basidiocarps emerged directly from substrates on the ground in Hokkaido. An aberrant form of T. hyperborea was found in Upernavik, West Greenland. This specimen had a stem-like structure of the fungus on Poaceae plants, and sclerotia developed on its tip. Corner (1950) considered the stem base of Typhula sclerotioides basidiocarp as the origin of the sclerotium (Corner, 1950). When the cultures were kept at 4 °C for 2 y, sclerotia had stem-like structures with intercalary, small secondary sclerotia (Christen, 1979). In addition, Tkachenko (1995) reported another type of secondary sclerotia in T. ishikariensis var. ishikariensis on tulip bulbs in Russia. One to seven secondary sclerotia were found within the original sclerotia. I also observed this type of secondary sclerotia from T. ishikariensis complex (Hoshino, unpublished results). These observations pointed that Typhula spp. developed basidiocarps not only from sclerotia but also from mycelia, and these species formed basidiocarps multiple times, depending on their growth environments, which have not so far been elucidated, however (Hoshino et al. 2023).

We observed that T. hyperborea in Greenland had largest basidiospores among the fungi belonging to the T. ishikariensis complex (Fig. 7: Hoshino et al., 2023). Ascomycetous yeast Cyberlindnera jadinii (Sartory, R. Sartory, Weill & J. Mey.) Minter [syn. Candida utilis (Henneberg) Lodder & Kreger-van Rij] increased cell size in culture when incubated at low temperatures as compared to their optimal growth temperatures (Beales, 2006; Rose, 1968). Similar phenomena occur in various eukaryotic microorganisms (Rose, 1968). Favre (1955) measured the spores of 64 species each from alpine areas and lower altitudes; 47 had the same spore size in both regions, 16 had larger spores in the alpine areas, and one had smaller spores. Unfortunately, he did not specify species he used (Knudsen, 2006). Kauserud et al. (2011) suggested that significant relationships were observed between spore size and climate factors. They hypothesize that these relationships are ascribed to water balance optimization; water storage in spores is critical for successful germination in dry micro-environments prior to the fruiting and/or in continental climates.

Fig. 7 - Basidiospore dimensions of isolates belonging to the Typhula ishikariensis complex. White circles: T. ishikariensis var. ishikariensis, grey circles: T. canadensis, black circles: T. hyperborea from Norway, red circles: T. hyperborea from Greenland. (Modified from Hoshino et al., 2022).

Most of T. hyperborea strains showed irregular growth on PDA above 10 °C (Hoshino et al., 2023). However, these strains showed normal growth at the same temperatures on corn meal agar or PDA that contained free radical scavengers such as ascorbic acid or β-carotene. Typical strains of T. hyperborea are strongly virulent, which may help the fungus to obtain free radical scavengers from host plants. Several strains of T. hyperborea in West Greenland are weakly virulent (Hoshino et al., 2022) and grow normally at 10 °C (Hoshino et al., 2023). They also produce abundant aerial mycelium with a relatively less number of sclerotia.

The life cycle plasticity in species of Typhulaceae developed through the interactions between its ecophysiological potential such as mycelial growth temperature range and environmental conditions in their localities (Hoshino et al., 2023). Our observations suggested that the summer climatic conditions in the Arctic were conductive to the fruiting of Typhula directly from mycelia, like Pistillaria (Karsten, 1882); Arctic summer with low ambient temperature limits microbial diversity as compared to that in snowy temperate regions (Hoshino et al., 2013b). Competitors against T. hyperborea are probably fewer in the Arctic, and the fungus could grow saprophytically in summer when ambient temperature allows its mycelial growth. Other species of Typhula in the Arctic exhibit the growth habits relevant to the above phenomenon, i.e., isolates of Typhula sp. from subarctic Canada easily lost their sclerotium-forming ability on PDA plates at 10 °C (Hoshino et al., 2021), and the absence of sclerotia of T. cf. subvariabilis (identification inferred from ITS sequences) from diseased moss in Antarctica (Yajima et al., 2017). The latter fungus developed basidiocarps directly from the substrate. We also could not find sclerotia of Typhula sp. in Iran just after the snow melt. Further field observations revealed that the fungus did produce sclerotia ca. 2 wk to 1 mo after the snowmelt (Hoshino et al., 2007). These results supported that the above our hypothesis.

The short growth period for fungi in Polar regions selects those with short life cycles. Many rusts in the Arctic have shortened life cycles, only producing one kind of spore (micropuccinia) in contrast to three spore forms (eupuccinia) that occur more commonly in warmer areas (Knudsen, 2006; Lind, 1934). In the temperate zones in northern hemisphere, the habitat of snow mold gets increasingly severe with decreasing latitude to the south. Sclerotium size of T. canadensis was related with snow cover conditions in each locality (Matsumoto & Tajimi, 1990). Sclerotium size was correlated with the duration of snow cover, i.e., predictability of favorable conditions. Populations from localities with deep, persistent snow cover had large sclerotia (former T. ishikariensis biotype B in Japan) and variable in aggressiveness due presumably to its efficient sexual reproduction. Sclerotia became smaller with decreasing number of days with snow cover, and ultimately an ecotype (small sclerotium form of biotype B or former T. ishikariensis biotype C in Japan) with very small sclerotia evolved in localities with thin, ephemeral snow cover. The ecotype was invariably aggressive, practically asexual and soil-borne. These features facilitate the fungus to prevail on underground plant parts without biding its time (Honkura et al., 1986). Similar phenomena may be found in T. hyperborea in the Arctic and in Typhula sp. and P. petasitis in Hokkaido, Japan (Hoshino et al., 2023).

5. Evolution of snow molds

Fungal fossil diversity increased throughout the Paleozoic Era with all modern classes existed in the Pennsylvanian Epoch (320-286 Mya) (Taylor et al., 1994). Present climatic zones developed from the Cretaceous period (145-66 Mya), and then plants adapted in cold environment (Sakai, 1995). Cryophilic fungal saprophytes probably evolved from mesophilic saprophytes. In the meantime, some species of cryophilic fungi may have exploited moribund plant tissues and ultimately became pathogenic (Matsumoto, 1997). Globisporangium, Sclerotinia and Typhula are typical genera of snow molds, and species of mesophiles and psychrotolerants are greater in number than psychrophiles. Pathogenicity and cold-adaptation are essential factors for fungi to become snow molds, and these factors evolved simultaneously in Typhula (Hoshino et al., 2013c).

Hoshino et al. (2022) made a taxonomic revision of the T. ishikariensis complex based on morphology, mating compatibility, and molecular analyses. Typhula ishikariensis var. ishikariensis is widely distributed in the Northern Hemisphere, which may be ascribed to its wide host range to utilize both mono- and dicots (Matsumoto & Hsiang, 2016), as well as its strong competitiveness as a pathogen over other taxa in the complex (Matsumoto & Sato, 1983). Neither its ITS region nor mitochondrial genes could be related to geographical distribution. Therefore, it seems difficult to discuss on the locality of the origin. Typhula ishikariensis var. idahoensis exists exclusively in North America and does not mate with var. ishikariensis. The phylogenetic analyses revealed that the isolates of var. idahoensis constituted a separate cluster within a clade comprising of both varieties. These facts imply that var. ishikariensis evolved in North America from an ancestral form. Typhula canadensis mates with var. idahoensis but is separate phylogenetically. This species is present in North America, Norway, the Khanty-Mansi Autonomous Okrug, Irkutsk Oblast of Russia and the Far East but absent in Central Siberia and Europe (Fig. 8). These observations imply that var. idahoensis and T. canadensis, which are genetically compatible, are considered to have speciated in North America and that T. canadensis probably spread from North America to Eurasia via two routes, i.e., to the east (Scandinavia) and west (Far East).

Magenta: T. ishikariensis var. ishikariensis, yellow: var. idahoensis, green: T. canadensis, *: including th small sclerotiumal form (former var. canadensis and biotype C), blue: T. hyperborea, −: surveyed areas where the T. ishikariesnsis complex was not found, ?: the T. ishikariesnsis complex is considered to exist but details are unknown (Modified from Tkachenko, 2013 and Hoshino et al., 2022).

Typhula hyperborea exists in European Alps and Volga region in Russia where T. canadensis is absent (Fig. 8). Typhula hyperborea is also present in Kamchatka Peninsula of Russia but not in Sakhalin Is. or Japan (var. ishikariensis and T. canadensis were collected from these islands). I could not identify the origin of T. hyperborea. In Europe, this fungus had probably spread earlier than T. candensis but later in the Far East. Var. ishikariensis can infect both di- and monocots. Since T. canadensis and T. hyperborea exploit monocots, exclusively, they are considered to be obliged to compete with each other where they coexist. The rate of mycelial growth at 0 °C separates their distribution; while T. hyperborea grew at ca. 10 mm/wk on PDA (e.g., Hoshino et al., 2023), T. canadensis less than 5 mm/wk (e.g., Hoshino et al., 2004). These results suggest that T. hyperborea has selective advantage in the high latitude area such as the Arctic and T. canadensis in the low latitude area of less snowy localities. Consequently in such localities, T. canadensis developed an ecotype with small sclerotia that could complete its life cycle in the soil without snow cover (Honkura et al., 1986).

In Japan, T. ishikariensis var. ishikariensis and possibly, T. canadensis represent components of the boreal microflora that presumed to have passed over the land bridges during the Pleistocene (2 million to 10 thousand years ago: Fujii, 1990). Matsumoto et al. (2000) found a predominant mycelial compatibility group (super MCG) within isolates of T. ishikariensis var. ishikariensis. About 38 and 14% of isolates from eastern and central Hokkaido, respectively belonged to the super MCG, but no such isolates were obtained from southern Hokkaido or Honshu, the main island. Hoshino and Kiriaki (2003) also found isolates belonging to the super MCG in the remote islands of Rishiri and Rebun, as well as even in Sakhalin, Russia. These findings reinforce the hypothesis that T. ishikariensis var. ishikariensis reached to Japan from Sakhalin, Russia. Paleogeographical events show that there was a landbridge over Soya channel between Sakhalin and Hokkaido three times in the Pleistocene, 1.2 million (S1), 160,000 (S2), and 70-12,000 years ago and that islands of Hokkaido and Honshu were once connected 160,000 (T) years ago (Ono, 1990). Typhula ishikariensis var. ishikariensis and also T. canadensis are present in Honshu, and they are considered to have passed over landbridges S1 and T. Since the super MSG is absent from Hoshu, the super MCG may have reached Hokkaido via S2. An estimate based on the annual vegetative growth rate indicates that it takes 2 million y to travel 250 km from the northern tip of Hokkaido to Sapporo. Since sexual reproduction results in diversity in MCG, T. ishikariensis var. ishikariensis is most likely to spread exclusively through vegetative propagation such as mycelial extension and passive translocation of sclerotia.
