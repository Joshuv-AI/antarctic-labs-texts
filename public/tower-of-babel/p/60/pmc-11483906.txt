The Discussion of Ignition Criterion About the Mechanical Spark Ignition of Near-Limit Methane–Air Mixtures
Authors: Junrong Ma, Cheng Wang, Changsuo Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
An optimal ignition criterion must be selected for the numerical prediction of the ignition parameters for mechanical spark ignition of near-limit methane–air fuel mixtures based on fuel mixture equivalency ratio; wherein, the vast majority of ignition criteria can be used for stoichiometric fuel mixtures. However, the viability of each criterion must be individually determined for near-limit fuel mixtures. This study developed a high-resolution algorithm that can be used to model the mechanical spark ignition and combustion of methane–air fuel mixtures of varying equivalence ratios. Three ignition criteria, Van’t Hoff, reaction heat release rate, and −OH concentration, were evaluated at varying equivalence ratios based on their respective distributions at varying times. The widely used Van’t Hoff criterion cannot be used to predict near-limit fuel mixtures due to a high required ignition temperature and low rates of combustion. −OH concentration criterion is significantly affected by the rate of combustion and reaction temperature; therefore, it tends to erroneously overestimate ignition parameters for near-limit fuel mixtures. This study has found that reaction heat release rate criterion can be used at all equivalence ratios and faithfully produces ignition parameter trends for fuel mixture reaction schemes of varying simplifications. Reaction heat release rate is a valid ignition criterion because the location of peaks in reaction released heat at varying times corresponds to flame front location, which is its physical counterpart.

Introduction

In various industrial facilities, mechanically or frictionally produced sparks that have sufficient amounts of energy are explosion hazards in the presence of flammable gases as they may become sources of ignition. Sparks are actually small hot particles that are a product of high-velocity collisional or frictional contact between solid materials. Once sparks with sufficient energy contact flammable gases, energy from the sparks is transferred via heat conduction and radiation, which, upon meeting the varying environmental requirements, induces ignition.

As the heat transfer mechanisms of heated particle ignition are also conduction and radiation, both spark ignition and heated particle ignition can be assumed to be the same process. As such, the more thoroughly studied theories of heated particle ignition can be applied to the study of mechanical spark ignition. In reality, ignition occurs in an instant; therefore, a method of determining the exact instant of ignition, or ignition criteria, is needed to accurately provide ignition parameters. Experimentally, the ignition criterion is explicit as the instant that a flame appears is taken as the ignition time.1 Numerically, ignition occurs when the chemical reaction zone detaches from the surface of the particle and can only be illustrated by selecting and observing certain parameters.2 At the stoichiometric ratio, many parameters can be used as ignition criteria; however, at near-limit fuel mixtures, further validation studies must be conducted for each criterion to determine its respective validities. Therefore, the goal of this study was to identify suitable ignition criteria for the prediction of heated particle ignition of lowcombustion rate fuel mixtures.

The development and validation of numerical models for heated particle ignition rely on the accumulation of significant amounts of both stationary and moving heated particle ignition experimental data. Beyer et al.1 and Sliver3 found that ignition temperatures of both stationary and moving heated particles are inversely proportional to particle size; while ignition time is directly proportional to particle size. Beyrau et al.4,5 observed that particle chemical reactions that occur during the heating process significantly affect ignition temperature and time. Paterson6,7 contends that moving particles have a higher ignition temperature than stationary particles and found that ignition temperature increases with increasing particle velocity.

Numerically, the selection of the ignition criterion significantly affects the accuracy of the computationally determined ignition time and temperature. The widely utilized Van’t Hoff criterion8 is a hot surface ignition criterion that assumes that ignition occurs when the temperature of the fuel mixture exceeds that of the hot surface; however, it can also be used for the study of heated particle ignition as both have the same heat transfer mechanisms. Zhang et al.,9 Coronel et al.,10 and Roth et al.11 used Van’t Hoff criterion to model the heated particle–induced ignition of fuel mixtures at varying conditions. Zhang et al.9 simulated the ignition of hydrogen–air fuel mixtures using varying sizes of stationary heated coal particles. Coronel et al.10 modeled the ignition of ethane–air mixtures using moving inert heated particles to determine ignition location and time. Roth et al.11 modeled the ignition of hydrogen–air mixtures using mechanically produced sparks. The results of the abovementioned studies indicate that the Van’t Hoff criterion can accurately predict the ignition time, location, and temperature for heated particle ignition of a fuel mixture with a high rate of combustion heat release. However, the applicability of the Van’t Hoff criterion is yet to be validated for heated particle ignition of methane–air mixture that approaches the flammability limits.

This study elects to utilize the experimental data of the study conducted by Ma et al.12 to conduct a comprehensive numerical study of the ignition around stationary heated particles in methane–air mixtures; wherein, comparative analyses were conducted with respect to the applicability of Van’t Hoff criterion, heat release rate criterion, and −OH concentration criterion by using a wide range of equivalence ratios. The results of the comparative analyses were used to identify an optimal ignition criterion that can accurately predict ignition parameters for near-limit fuel mixtures.

To accurately predict ignition delay times, reduced mechanisms must consist of at least ten reactions.16 This study elects to use the detailed reaction model (DRM-19) that was developed by Kazakov and Frenklach,17 which consists of 19 species and 84 reactions. Though DRM-19 is a simplified reaction model, it is still sufficiently detailed and is able to predict the ignition time for methane with equivalence ratios within the flammability limits.18

The inert particle zone is assumed to have uniform temperature distribution and is devoid of chemical reactions; where the uniform distribution of temperature can be explained using the Biot number approach. Biot number is the ratio of particle-surrounding gas convective heat transfer to heat conduction within the particle. Biot number can be expressed as Bi = hLc/ks, where Lc is the characteristic length scale defined as Lc = V/A, where V and A are the volume and surface area of the sphere, respectively, and ks is the thermal conductivity of the material. When Bi < 0.1, particle heat conduction is faster than heat convection that transfers heat from the particle to the fuel mixture.19 Baehr et al.19 found that when Bi = 0.1, the temperature distribution within the particle has a less than 2% variation range. Given the particle sizes and temperatures considered in this study, the Biot number will invariably be less than 0.1. Therefore, the assumption of a uniform temperature distribution within the particle is sound.

The simulations, with particle diameters of 45 μm, boundary heat sources of 1.2 MW/m2, and initial conditions of P0 = 100 kPa and T0 = 300 K, were performed with initial and boundary conditions that mirrors those of the experimental condtions,12 and the origin of the domain is set at the center of the particle with the domain extending radially outward, as shown in Figure 1. To resolve the heat transfer near the particle, there should be at least 4 cells in the particle, and the size of the cell is 10 μm. To ensure that ignition is not affected by boundary effects, an appropriate gas zone length (r = 3 cm) was determined using trial and error. The outer boundary of the gas zone uses Neumann-type boundary conditions, which prevents both heat and compositional interaction between the gases within and outside the gas zone. The particle-gas boundary prevents compositional interaction, and temperature curve of the boundary follows the uniformly distributed particle temperature; while the temperature of the particle mimics that of the experimentally obtained fitted temperature history. In this paper, the temperature history is fitted from experimental data in Ma et al.12 by least-square method. The comparison between experimental data is shown in Figure 2.

Fitting results of the particle temperature.

The solutions of the governing equations of the numerical model were obtained using DNS solver; wherein the convection terms were resolved using fifth-order weighted essentially nonoscillatory (WENO) finite difference schemes20 and the nonlinear diffusion terms were discretized using the sixth-order standard central difference scheme. The WENO finite difference method is advantageous in that it can achieve a high-order accuracy in smooth regions and capture sharp discontinuities. The time integration was resolved using the third-order strong stability preserving the Runge–Kutta method.21 The implementation of the code has been validated in various studies comprising ignition time scales13 and flame propagation.22

Results and Discussion

Validation of the Van’t Hoff Criterion

The Van’t Hoff criterion assumes that at the moment of ignition, the rate of heat loss is equal to the rate of heat release via chemical reactions for the gas surrounding the particle. This section seeks to verify the applicability of the Van’t Hoff criterion when the combustion heat release of the fuel mixture is slow, that is, the near-limit fuel mixtures. All models have identical experimental particle temperature histories, and the equation of the fitted temperature histories for all models is Tp (t) = 300 + 2920t0.3715. This is a reasonable assumption since most of the explosive mixtures of practical interest contain relatively small amounts of fuel and, hence, their thermal conductivity is expected to be very similar to that of air.

The combustion intensity of fuel mixture depends on their relative concentrations. At the stoichiometric ratio (φ = 1.0), the rapid heat release of fuel mixture combustion is due to the complete consumption of reactants. Whereas, for rich fuel mixtures (φ>1.0), the slow heat release of fuel mixture combustion is due to a shortage in the concentration of the oxidizer. This study initially conducted two sets of numerical simulations (φ = 1.0 and φ = 1.55); wherein the temperature distributions with respect to time are shown in Figure 3a,b, respectively. The ignition time is defined as the moment when the slope of the temperature curve near the particle begins to appear positive, and the ignition temperature is the temperature calculated according to Tp (t)=300 + 2920t0.3715 at the time of ignition.

The results shown in Figure 3a indicate that, prior to ignition, the temperature of the fuel mixture surrounding the particle will gradually increase due to heat conduction from the particle. Thus, the temperature of the particle will invariably be greater than that of the surrounding fuel mixture. However, after ignition occurs, the temperature of the surrounding fuel mixture will exceed that of the particle due to initiation of the combustion reaction within the fuel mixture. A comparison of the particle and fuel mixture temperature histories in Figure 3b indicates that the temperature of the particle will be greater than that of the fuel mixture throughout the entirety of the experiment due to a higher ignition temperature and slower heat release of combustion within the fuel mixture. Therefore, the Van’t Hoff criterion cannot be accurately applied for the prediction of the ignition parameters of near-limit methane–air mixtures.

Validation of the Heat Release Rate Criterion

The limitation of the Van’t Hoff criterion with respect to near-limit mixtures necessitates the identification of alternative ignition criteria that can be used to predict the ignition of near-limit mixtures. In order to establish an ignition criterion that most realistically corresponds to that of the physical experiment, the determination of what constitutes ignition was analyzed; thereafter, its analog within the numerical simulation was determined. In the literature,4,5 and,11 the instance of ignition was assumed to be the initial appearance of a visible flame. In reality, visible flames occur in a thin region that corresponds to a rapid heat release from combustion reactions; at any given time, the chemical heat release rate, , value should peak at the location of the flame.

Validation of the −OH Criterion

The domain of the flammable gas zone in the numerical model can be further separated into three regions: the burned, chemical reaction, and unburned regions. −OH is a free radical that is both a reactant and a product in the combustion reaction. Therefore, the concentration of −OH varies within the three regions of the gas zone; wherein it is present in the burned and chemical reaction regions and is absent in the unburned region. This study’s −OH ignition criterion assigns the ignition time to the time that corresponds to a maximum −OH value that does not occur on the surface of the particle, which is similar to that of the criterion.

The distributions of −OH with respect to time for φ = 1.0 and φ=1.55 are shown in Figure 5a,b, respectively. Using the −OH criterion, the predicted ignition times and ignition temperatures are summarized in Table 3. For φ = 1.0, the predicted ignition time has an error of 5.36% (lower), while, for φ = 1.55, it overestimated the ignition time by 8.77%. The underestimation of the ignition time, as previously explained, is due to the simplification of the gas chemical reaction model and the omission of gas heat convection. The overestimation of ignition time by 8.77% for φ = 1.55 implies that the underlying cause significantly affects the prediction of rich fuel mixture ignition parameters by the −OH criterion as the −OH criterion normally characteristically underestimates ignition times. The abovementioned overestimation of ignition time is caused by a combination of slow combustion and low reactivity of the rich fuel mixture.

This study found that when the equivalence ratio of methane–air mixture is 0.5–1.5, the change in the concentration of −OH can be used as the ignition criterion to predict the ignition time better, which is similar to the results of previous studies on the ignition time of highly active gases using this criterion; for example, ethylene,23 iso-octane,24 nitromethane,25 etc. If the method of (26) is referred to and the formation rate of −OH is used as the ignition criterion, the calculated ignition time error of methane–air mixture near the lower flammability limit is lower, but for the case near the upper flammability limit, the ignition time will still be overestimated, and the error is larger. Therefore, the chemical reaction heat release rate is the most suitable ignition criterion for the near-limit methane–air mixture.

Figure 7 shows the evolution of key component concentrations of gas around particles before and after ignition under two equivalence ratios φ = 1.0 and φ = 1.55. For φ = 1.0, the ignition time is 96.6 ms. According to the evolution process of component concentration, the whole process of hot particles igniting combustible gas can be divided into the following three stages:Slow oxidation stage. In this stage, small amounts of reactants O2 and CH4 participate in the reaction to produce intermediate products CO and H2O. At this stage, the slow reaction rate and low reaction temperature lead to the formation of small amounts of CO2 and −OH radicals.Ignition stage. When the time approached about 96.6 ms, the chain reaction was rapidly activated, the concentration of highly active radical −OH increased sharply, the reactants O2 and CH4 were rapidly consumed, and the products such as H2O and CO2 were rapidly generated. In addition, the CO that accumulated in the slow oxidation stage is oxidized to CO2. At the same time, the system releases a lot of heat due to the rapid combustion reaction.Ignition completion stage. After ignition, the gas near the particles is transformed from the initial reactant to the main products such as H2O, CO2, and −OH, and the combustion reaction can self-propagate without an external energy supply.

Influence of Chemical Models on Ignition Time Based on the Criterion

Given that criterion is most optimal for the 84-step chemical model for both stoichiometric and rich fuel mixtures, this study elected to further investigate its applicability for an alternative chemical reaction model. Thus, a comparative analysis was conducted between the experimental results of Ma et al.,12 4-step chemical model developed by Jones and Lindstedt,27 and the previously utilized 84-step chemical models for methane–air fuel mixtures with varying equivalence ratios within the flammability limits. The results indicate that both 4-step and 84-step models can predict ignition time trends using criterion for equivalence ratios within the flammability limits, as shown in Figure 8. However, this study has found that predicted ignition times decrease with increasing simplicity of chemical reaction models due to omissions or simplifications of significant mechanisms of branching reactions such as chain initiation and some endothermic reactions.

Ignition times for the methane–air mixture calculated at different equivalence ratios using simplified and detailed chemical kinetic schemes.

In summary, the criterion can predict ignition parameters for fuel mixtures within flammability limits for varying chemical reaction models. Moreover, simplified chemical reaction models should be used when predicting trends of ignition parameters due to computational efficiency; while more complex chemical reaction models should be relied on if the goal is to determine accurate ignition parameters.

Conclusion

This study utilized the numerical simulation of the ignition of methane-air mixtures using heated inert particles to investigate the applicability of Van’t Hoff, heat release rate , and −OH ignition criteria for the prediction of ignition parameters. The conclusions of the research are as follows:All three criteria can be applied for the prediction of ignition parameters for methane–air mixtures near the stoichiometric ratio resulting from a fast reaction rate and low ignition temperature.
