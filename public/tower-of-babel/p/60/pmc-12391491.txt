Accuracy assessment of a cochlear implant imaging tool using clinical computed tomography images
Authors: Stephan Geiger, Matti Iso-Mustajärvi, Tim Nauwelaers, Petro Julkunen, Pia Linder, Aarno Dietz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn today’s clinical routine, imaging tools are increasingly used for cochlear implant surgical planning and assessment. However, low resolution and artifacts of computer tomography (CT) images often prevent a reliable assessment of the cochlea geometry, as well as introduced trauma from the surgery. This study evaluated the reliability of a cochlear implant imaging tool for evaluation of clinical CT scans. 20 sets of clinical pre- and postoperative CT images, implanted with Advanced Bionics SlimJ and Mid-Scala electrode arrays, were analyzed using fusion imaging, manual measurements, and the imaging tool. The compared outcomes included cochlea diameter and width, angular insertion depth, and electrode scalar location. Cochlea diameter (A) and width (B) demonstrated excellent and good inter-rater agreement between the imaging tool and manual measurements, respectively, while angular insertion depth and electrode scalar location showed excellent agreement. The imaging tool achieved an overall high accuracy of 96.9%, with a sensitivity of 83.0% and specificity of 98.3% for assessing the electrode scalar location. The imaging tool exhibited high accuracy and good to excellent agreement with other established assessment methods for clinical CTs. With its strong usability and fast evaluations, this evidence supports the tool’s reliable use in clinical practice.Subject terms: Software, Medical imaging

Introduction

Cochlear implants (CI) are a standard treatment option for profound to severe hearing loss. In recent years, the importance of electrode placement within the scala tympani (ST) as well as preserving intracochlear structures like the basilar membrane (BM) or osseous spiral lamina (OSL) during surgery have become more important as they can result in preservation of residual hearing and better postoperative hearing outcomes for CI recipients1–5.

Radiological imaging tools allow for an assessment of the electrode placement within the cochlea, with potential 3D reconstructions of the cochlea and electrode array. Reliable imaging tools are necessary for a characterization of electrode behaviour, trauma assessment as well as surgical technique for clinical and temporal bone studies. In addition, other objective measurements like the angular insertion depth (AID) of the electrode and proximity to the modiolus can be evaluated from the postoperative images. In the future, these factors may provide clinical insights to surgery, fitting, and postoperative quality control6–8. This emphasizes the need for imaging tools within the clinical routine to understand how well-placed electrodes are and ultimately achieve better patient outcomes.

Most introduced imaging tools are verified on temporal cadaver bones due to the fact that histological images or Micro-CT images, where delicate structures like the basilar membrane or osseous spiral lamina are visible and allow an accurate comparison to the imaging tool results9–16. While Micro-CT images or histological images allow a very precise validation of results, it should be noted that CT scans from cadaver temporal bones usually provide better contrast and resolution than clinical CT scans due to less bone material and soft tissues surrounding the cochlea. In addition, other effects like blurring, head movement, or artifacts can significantly worsen the quality of clinical CT scans, making it difficult for imaging tools to perform accurate evaluations17,18. Therefore, a validation of an imaging tool against clinical CTs taken from implanted CI users is essential to ensure reliable results, also for a clinic environment.

When imaging tools are assessed using clinical scans, this is usually done by comparing the results with manual assessments19–22. Manual assessment and categorization of electrode scalar position is a subjective evaluation and depends on the experience of the observer as well as CT quality. The fusion imaging (FI) technique is a proven method for evaluating electrode scalar location in clinical CTs22–26. This method fuses pre- and postoperative CT images and, if available, also MRI images and allows to reconstruct the electrode array. MRI images of the cochlea have the advantage to represent the delicate OSL and BM. However, MRI images can only be taken preoperatively, as metal artifacts of the electrode contacts make this modality unusable for postoperative assessment. Fusing these modalities has the advantage that the electrode location can then be precisely assessed relative to the delicate structures. Sipari et al.23 showed that the fusion imaging technique is a reliable way of evaluating electrode placement within the cochlea on clinical CTs.

The imaging tool assessed in this present study was previously tested on cadaveric temporal bones implanted with the Advanced Bionics HiFocus SlimJ or HiFocus Mid-scala electrodes by Geiger et al.12. In the temporal bone study, the imaging tool showed high sensitivity of 100% (detecting trauma correctly) and high specificity of 98% (detecting non-trauma correctly), with the limitation of only using six temporal bones and having better resolution and quality images than usually available in clinical routine. Using clinical CT images, Arends et al.19 demonstrated a high sensitivity of 97% and specificity of 96% of the electrode scalar location on 116 electrodes for the same imaging tool. However, this study assessed only pre-curved electrodes, and the results were compared to visual manual assessments, which are inherently subjective.

Here we present a consecutive study, evaluating the imaging tool on clinical CT images with various resolutions and contrasts, assessing pre-curved and straight electrode arrays, and validating the data using the fusion imaging technique.

Methods

Clinical imaging data

This study used 20 sets of clinical CT images collected between 2016 and 2023. All data sets were analyzed retrospectively. The study was conducted according to Finnish legislation, with the Kuopio University Hospital granting institutional approval (No. 5551883) and waiving the requirement for informed consent. The anonymity of participants was guaranteed. A CT scan was taken preoperatively before the cochlear implantation with a varying resolution shown in Table 1 and an average of 0.34 mm pixel spacing and 0.44 mm slice thickness. The high variation of preoperative CT scans can be explained, as the preoperative scans were taken by the referring central hospitals of the Kuopio University Hospital district. After each CI surgery, a postoperative cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan was taken. The postoperative scans had an average pixel spacing of 0.15 mm and 0.2 mm slice thickness. The postoperative scans showed a lower variance as all postoperative scans were taken first day postoperatively at the Kuopio University Hospital. The HiFocus™ MidScala (MS) was implanted in 13 image data sets, and the HiFocus™ SlimJ (SlimJ) was implanted in 7 data sets. Both electrode arrays are from Advanced Bionics LLC (Valencia, USA). All information about the 20 data sets can be found in Table 1.

Imaging tool

The research imaging tool used for this study was developed by Advanced Bionics (Valencia, USA). It generates a three-dimensional cochlea model including an estimation of the basilar membrane and osseous spiral lamina (BM/OSL). Because the BM/OSL are not visible on a clinical CT scan, the software uses a database of known high-resolution cochlea geometries and fits this onto the segmentation of the clinical CT scan. The users are required to manually mark 4 landmarks: ‘round window’, ‘inner wall at the round window’, ‘center of modiolar axis’, and ‘apical end’ on the CT image (Fig. 1). This enables to generate an individual 3D cochlea model including an estimate of the BM/OSL. The postoperative scan is registered on the preoperative scan automatically, then used to segment the single electrode array contacts and construct a 3D model of the electrode. With the 3D cochlea model as well as the 3D electrode model, the imaging tool is able to automatically calculate cochlea diameter (A) and width (B), the angular insertion depth of each contact as well as the electrode scalar location12. The imaging tool can be used with little anatomical knowledge and experience. The results are automatically extracted from the generated 3D models and presented to the user.

The tool requires the user to set four anatomical landmarks to generate the cochlea model. The first three points are set on the basal turn view (left) along a guiding line (yellow): the ‘round window’, ‘inner wall at the round window’, and ‘center of modiolar axis’. The fourth point is placed on the ‘apical end’ of the modiolar axis.

The imaging tool requires manual user input of the correct placement of landmarks. To ensure consistency and minimize variability, all landmarks were placed by a single experienced user following a standardized protocol. It should be noted that, although a slight variation of results might occur depending on the placement of landmarks, assessment of inter-user variability was not part of this study.

Cochlea dimensions and angular insertion depth

We used the cochlea diameter (A), width (B), as well as the angular insertion depth to compare the different models. Cochlea diameter, width, and angular insertion depth were measured manually and compared to automatic results from the imaging tool. For manual measurements, the software MIMICS (Ver. 23.0, Materialise NV, Leuven, Belgium) was used. Cochlea diameter (A) and cochlea width (B) were measured as defined by Escude et al.27. Cochlea diameter was measured from the round window through the modiolar axis to the opposite side of the cochlea. The cochlea width was measured from inner cochlea wall to wall, perpendicular to the length measurement, and through the modiolar axis. Postoperative CT scans were used to measure the cochlea diameter (A), width (B), and angular insertion depth, as their higher resolution allowed for more precise measurements.

The imaging tool is calculating the cochlea diameter by measuring in 3D from the center of the round window to the 180 degrees point of the cochlea wall. For the cochlea width, the distance between the 90 degrees and 270 degrees points is measured.

Angular insertion depth is measured by calculating the angle between the round window, the modiolar axis and the most apical contact. 360 degrees is added if more than one turn is covered by the electrode.

Analysis of electrode scalar location

The scalar location was analysed for each individual contact with the fusion imaging technique as well as with the imaging tool. For rating the scalar location, the radiologic grading scale introduced in the predecessor study by Geiger et al.12 was used. In this rating scale, an electrode scalar location (ESL) rating of 0 means that the electrode lies at the observed location within the scala tympani and thus is associated with ‘no trauma’. An ESL rating of 1 indicates an interaction between the electrode body and the basilar membrane or osseous spiral lamina (BM/OSL). This relates to a ‘potential trauma’ as this can either mean the electrode body lifts the BM/OSL or that it is already ruptured. An ESL 2 rating is applied when the electrode sits in the scala media or scala vestibuli (SM/SV), which relates to a translocation and indicates ‘trauma’.

The imaging software automatically gives the rating based on these criteria. ESL 0 is given if the electrode sits on the scala tympani side below the upper uncertainty range of the software-estimated BM/OSL. ESL 1 is given if the electrode body sits above the upper limit of the BM/OSL but less than 50% of the electrode are above the BM/OSL estimation. If more than 50% are above the BM/OSL, the software gives an ESL 2 rating. For a detailed description, please refer to Geiger et al.12.

For the analysis using fusion imaging, the software from BrainLab (iPlan Net 3.6.0 Build 77, BrainLab AG, Munich, Germany) was used. The fusion imaging technique does not give an automatic rating of the electrode scalar location. Using the reconstructed electrode and the fused image modalities, an experienced otosurgeon rated the location for each contact using the same trauma grading. Detailed descriptions of the image fusion technique to assess intracochlear trauma after cochlear implantation have been published23,26.

Statistics

For correlation of the categorial evaluation of electrode scalar locations between fusion imaging and the imaging tool, the Cohen’s Kappa value was calculated. For correlation of continuous measurements of cochlea diameter, width, and insertion angle, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) with 95% confidence interval was calculated with a two-way mixed model for consistency. For interpretation, the guideline introduced by Cicchetti et al.28 was used. There, values lower than 0.40 are considered poor agreement, between 0.40 and 0.59 as fair, 0.60 to 0.74 as good, and from 0.75 to 1.0 as excellent agreement. Statistical analysis was done using RStudio (RStudio Team (2020). RStudio: Integrated Development for R. RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA, http://www.rstudio.com).

Sensitivity and specificity of the imaging tool assessment were calculated assuming the fusion imaging technique results as the ground truth. The sensitivity was calculated by the number of correctly identified ESL ratings 1 and 2 divided by the true number of ESL ratings 1 & 2. This showed if ‘potential trauma’ or ‘trauma’ identified by the fusion imaging technique was also detected by the imaging tool. Specificity was calculated by dividing the number of correctly identified ESL ratings of 0 by the true number of ESL 0 ratings. This represents if ‘no trauma’ is correctly identified by the imaging tool. Overall accuracy was calculated by adding the number of correctly identified ESL ratings 1 and 2 and correctly identified ESL ratings of 0 and dividing it by the total number of ratings.

In addition, ICC, sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy were calculated for the two sub-groups of straight (SlimJ) and pre-curved (MS) electrode arrays.

Results

Cochlea dimensions and angular insertion depth

The mean cochlea diameter (A) was 9.27 ± 0.40 mm standard deviation (SD) for the manual measurement and 9.31 ± 0.32 mm for the imaging tool. The mean absolute deviation of the automatically generated length from manual measurement was 0.16 mm. The ICC value was calculated to 0.86 (0.680–0.942), which translates to an excellent interrater agreement. The mean cochlea width for manual measurement was 6.80 ± 0.27 mm, while the imaging tool measured 6.93 ± 0.24 mm. Mean absolute deviation was 0.22 mm. ICC for B was a good agreement with 0.672 (0.337–0.856). Mean angular insertion depth (AID) was 385 ± 43.1 degrees for the manual measurement and 378 ± 43.3 degrees for the imaging tool. Mean absolute deviation was 11.1 degrees. With an ICC of 0.964 (0.912–0.986), the two methods did show excellent interrater agreement. Looking at the two different electrode types, the mean AID for the straight electrode array (HiFocus SlimJ) was 412 ± 56.4 degrees for manual measurement and 404 ± 57.6 degrees for the imaging tool, while the mean AID for the pre-curved electrode array (HiFocus MidScala) measured 370 ± 23.2 degrees manually and 364 ± 23.5 degrees with the imaging tool. Mean absolute deviations for the SlimJ and MS electrodes were 10.0 degrees and 13.1 degrees between manual and imaging tool measurements, respectively. With an ICC of 0.972 (0.849–0.995) for SlimJ and an ICC of 0.902 (0.711–0.969) for MS, both electrode arrays did show an excellent interrater agreement between manual and imaging tool results.

Each dot represents an electrode contact, and the color of the dot indicates the different ratings (ESL 0 = green, ESL 1 = yellow, and ESL 2 = red). When the fusion imaging results are used as ground truth values, the software did achieve a sensitivity of 82.8% for detecting potential trauma and trauma correctly as trauma or potential trauma. With a specificity of 98.3% the software was able to correctly detect no-trauma as non-trauma contacts. Overall accuracy was calculated to be 96.9%.

A Cohen’s kappa value of 0.67 (0.54–0.79) showed a good agreement between fusion imaging evaluation and the imaging tool evaluation.

Looking at the sub-groups of straight (SlimJ) and pre-curved (MS) electrode arrays. The SlimJ sub-group did achieve a sensitivity of 77.8%, specificity of 97.9% and accuracy of 94.6%. The MS sub-group reached a sensitivity of 90.9%, specificity of 98.5% and accuracy of 98.1%.

Discussion

This study assessed the accuracy of an imaging tool used to evaluate clinical cochlear implantation CT data. Cochlea dimensions, along with angular insertion depth and electrode scalar location of the electrode array, were evaluated and compared between the automatic assessment of the imaging tool and results derived from fusion imaging and manual measurements. All comparisons showed a good or excellent agreement between the imaging tool and alternative assessment methods.

Cochlea dimensions

The cochlea dimensions showed an excellent agreement for the length and a good agreement for the width. On average, the imaging tool presented slightly higher values than the manual measurements. This may be attributed to the differences in how measurements are taken. Manual measurements were done on a cross-section plane of the basal turn. As the cochlea shape changes along its height, this cross-section does not always show the maximum dimensions. The imaging tool measures in a three-dimensional space and thus always measures the maximum dimensions of the cochlea. This could explain the slightly higher dimensions for this method. Arends et al.19 report for the same imaging tool a mean deviation to manual measurements of 0.65 mm for cochlea diameter (A) and 0.48 mm for cochlea width, whereas in this study, the mean deviations are noticeably smaller, with 0.16 mm for length and 0.22 mm for width. The reason for this could be that Arends et al. performed their manual measurements differently. They measured length from the basal end of the cochlea to the opposite side, without the requirement of crossing the modiolar axis, which may have resulted in higher values. Also, the width was measured without modiolar axis crossing as requirement. Wherein, in this study, the manual measurements as well as the imaging tool measurements follow the same definitions. In a study conducted by Rivas et al.29the cochlea diameter (A) was automatically calculated with an imaging tool and compared with manual measurements of two observers. Mean absolute deviations between the two observers and automatic measurements were 0.25 mm and 0.18 mm. With a mean absolute deviation of 0.16 mm in our study, the imaging tool calculation was slightly more accurate. In another study by Paouris et al.30using an imaging tool with the capability for automatic measurements, the tool measured an average cochlea diameter of 9.16 mm and cochlea width of 6.70 mm, while the mean for the two manual raters measured 8.99 mm and 6.76 mm for length and width, respectively. In our case the tool averages were 9.27 mm and 6.93 mm, and for manual measurements 9.31 mm and 6.80 mm for cochlear length and cochlear width, respectively. While averages do not have a meaningful statistical validity, they indicate that for our imaging tool, the average length values agreed better, while in the study by Paouris et al. the imaging tool showed better alignment of the automatic width values. For another cochlear implant-specific imaging tool, a study by Khurayzi et al.21 found a mean manual measurement of cochlea diameter (A) of 8.43 mm while the imaging tool measured a mean diameter of 8.44 mm. Cochlea width was not measured manually in this study. For the same imaging tool, a study by Alahmadi et al.22 found a mean difference of 0.04 mm for cochlea diameter (A) and a mean difference of 0.21 mm for cochlea width (B) between manual and imaging tool measurements. Similar to their study, our findings showed better agreement for cochlea diameter measurements compared to cochlea width. One explanation could be that the manual width (B) measurement gives more room for variation with the requirement of alignment to the modiolar axis, perpendicular to the cochlea diameter measurement. Another contributing factor could be that, as mentioned earlier, the imaging tool in our study calculates the cochlea width three-dimensionally, whereas manual measurements are done on the basal plane.

Angular insertion depth

For angular insertion measurements, the imaging tool demonstrated reliable results, with a mean absolute deviation of 11.1 degrees and an ICC rating showing excellent inter-rater agreement. It should be noted that measurements of pre-curved electrodes tend to have higher angular deviation as the apical contacts are closer to the modiolar axis. Arends et al.19 found in their study a mean absolute difference of 15.1 degrees for pre-curved electrodes, while in this study, pre-curved and straight electrodes were evaluated. If SlimJ (straight) and Mid-Scala (pre-curved) electrodes were assessed separately in our study, we get a mean absolute deviation of 10.0 degrees for Mid-Scala electrodes and 13.1 degrees for SlimJ electrodes. Inter-rater agreement was excellent for both sub-groups. Even though the sample size was low to consider them separately, we did not observe that the imaging tool used in this study would evaluate the pre-curved less accurately than straight electrode arrays.

Electrode scalar location

The electrode scalar location comparison between the imaging tool and the fusion imaging assessment showed good agreement. The tool showed an overall accuracy of 96.8%. With a sensitivity of 82.8% and a specificity of 98.3%, the imaging tool was more reliable in detecting contacts without trauma than those with trauma. The lower value for sensitivity came from five ‘potential trauma’ ratings by the FI method, while the imaging tool still rated as ‘no trauma’. All translocating electrodes were detected as translocating electrodes by the imaging tool. Looking at the sub-groups of straight and pre-curved electrodes, the pre-curved electrode arrays showed, with 90.9% a higher sensitivity than the straight electrode arrays with 77.8%. However, due to the low sample size and even lower occurrence of trauma, the sub-groups results should be interpreted with caution. Both sub-groups resulted in high specificity of 97.9% and 98.5% and accuracy of 94.6% and 98.1% for straight and pre-curved arrays, respectively.

In the study by Arends et al.19 a different criteria is used. Each contact was rated by experienced observers. If two or more contacts of an electrode array were rated as a translocation, the whole electrode array is rated as translocation. Otherwise, the electrode array is rated as a non-translocation. Using this type of criteria for our study, the sensitivity as well as the specificity would be 100%. This would also be most relevant for clinicians, as it is usually of interest whether trauma was introduced or not. But as the sample size of 20 was low for this, each individual contact was rated, which also gives the data points for evaluating the accuracy of the imaging tool.

Fusion imaging demonstrated good accuracy in temporal bone studies26,31 when comparing radiological and histological images. Sipari et al.31 reported in their study a sensitivity of 87.5% and specificity of 97.3% of the fusion imaging method compared to histological images. Similarly, Iso-Mustajärvi et al.26 received a significant intraclass correlation (r = 0.697) between FI and histology. These findings suggest that fusion imaging provides reliable results and confidence as a comparison tool. However, it should be noted that FI cannot always identify the electrode scalar location with complete certainty.

Another study where electrode scalar location was detected by an imaging tool and assessed on clinical CT images was reported by Andersen et al.20. In their study, they compared the automatic results to manual segmentations. They reported a 98.3% accuracy in identifying scalar location for each contact, distinguishing only between a scala vestibuli or scala tympani contact location. Applying the same definition to our study would result in an accuracy of 97.5%.

CT image quality

It is important that an imaging tool works reliably for a variety of CT image qualities. Resolution is the main factor for the success of imaging tools, while other factors like contrast, metal artifacts, or blurring can further complicate segmentation. Although the postoperative CT images in our study had mostly consistent resolution and were obtained from the same CT scanner, the preoperative images exhibited significant variation in resolution. These images were taken at different hospitals, using different CT scanners with varying settings. Preoperative images range from 0.11 mm to 0.49 mm in pixel spacing and from 0.15 mm to 1.00 mm for slice thickness. Too much difference in pre- and postoperative CT image resolution could potentially result in an error in registering pre- and postoperative images, which could not be observed in this study. The difference in resolution does not impact the outcomes, as the cochlea model is solely based on the preoperative CT while the electrode model is based on the postoperative CT. Other imaging studies that use clinical CT images for accuracy assessments typically employ images with high isotropic resolution of 0.08 mm from the same scanner20or 0.45 mm/0.5 mm isotropic resolution from the same scanner19.

New technologies such as the photon-counting detector computed tomography (PCD-CT) enable high-resolution images with excellent contrast, making fine structure more visible32–34. This will allow for precise image reconstructions in the future. However, these advanced scanners are not yet widely used, which highlights the importance of imaging tools to perform reliably across various imaging modalities.

Imaging tool usability

While delivering accurate results is crucial for an imaging tool, it is equally important for the tool to be user-friendly. The imaging tool used in this study only requires a few alignments and selection of 4 landmarks, while pre- and post-operative CT image registration, as well as the cochlea and electrode model, are done automatically. Further, all measurements are done fully automatically. Evaluating one imaging set took about 7–12 min.

Fusion imaging, on the other hand, requires manual steps to register the images. The reconstructions must be interpreted by the user, which requires a certain level of anatomical knowledge and experience.

A tool that requires minimal experience and enables a fast evaluation of post-operative results is essential for routine use in a wide range of clinical environments.

Conclusions

Building on the demonstrated efficacy for temporal bone CT scans12this study evaluated the reliability of the imaging tool on clinical CT scans. The results were compared to established assessment methods, such as manual measurements and fusion imaging. ICC and Cohen’s kappa values showed good to excellent agreement between the imaging tool and these established methods for cochlea diameter, width, and angular insertion depth. With high accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, the tool reliably detected the presence or absence of trauma after cochlear implantation. This initial evaluation indicates that the imaging tool performs reliably with CT scans acquired during clinical routine. Its application holds promise to add valuable insights for surgery, fitting, and postoperative quality-control towards improved patient outcomes in the future.
