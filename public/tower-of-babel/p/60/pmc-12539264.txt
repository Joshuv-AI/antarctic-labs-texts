KAZN methylation as a potential diagnostic and prognostic marker in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Songling Zhu, Yuting He, Wei Wang, Yanlei Jiao, Ziqiong Xu, Shaohui You, Chong Yu, Jiayi Ao, Mengran Long, Jihong Ren, Hongli Zhang, Shu-Lin Liu, Hongxia Bao, Shuang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is characterized by aggressive malignancy and poor prognosis often attributed to challenges in early diagnosis. Using machine learning on extensive public methylation databases (including TCGA and GEO), we developed a model that accurately distinguishes NSCLC from normal tissues (>94%), comparable to typical diagnostic methods. KAZN methylation patterns also proved informative for predicting patient survival. Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses provided support for a potential causal relationship between KAZN methylation and NSCLC development. In vitro experiments validated that KAZN expression is significantly lower in NSCLC tumor tissues and cell lines compared to normal controls. Demethylation treatment significantly elevated KAZN expression in lung adenocarcinoma cell lines. KAZN expression significantly affects the apoptosis and proliferation in NSCLC cell lines by potentially targeting USP2. Overall, aberrant methylation of KAZN plays an important role in NSCLC pathogenesis and KAZN methylation may serves as a useful biomarker for molecular characterization, diagnosis, and prognosis.Subject areas: cancer, epigenetics, machine learning

Introduction

Lung cancer is a deadly type of cancer with high incidence and mortality. According to GLOBOCAN 2022, lung cancer remained the leading cause of cancer death, accounting for about 1.8 million deaths in 2022.1 Lung cancers are traditionally classified into two main types: small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and NSCLC,2 and the latter one was further divided into three major histological subtypes including NSCLC adenocarcinoma (ACA), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and large-cell carcinoma (LCC).3 Among all lung cancers diagnosed, NSCLC accounts for approximately 85%. Diagnosed stage of the lung cancer is directly related to the clinical outcome of the patients. However, NSCLC patients are often diagnosed at later stages, which cause a very poor survival rate and increased symptom burden.4 Hence, identifying reliable diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers as well as therapeutic targets for NSCLC is crucial for improving patient clinical outcomes.

DNA methylation is a type of main epigenetic modifications that cause covalent attachment of a methyl group at C5 on cytosine residues of DNA molecule.5 This alteration can directly regulate the gene expression and usually lead to gene silencing.6 Extensive studies have confirmed that DNA methylation is strongly associated with the development and progression of various tumors.7 The DNA methylation profiles exhibit significant variation not only between different diseases but also across different stages of a single disease. These unique DNA methylation signatures therefore present a significant opportunity for early detection and intervention in lung cancer. Some previous studies have also identified methylation sites in certain genes as potential biomarkers for classification and diagnosis.8,9,10,11 However, these findings have yet to be translated into clinical applications.

Kazrin (KAZN), a highly conserved periplakin-interacting vertebrate protein, contributes to desmosome formation,12 cell adhesion, cytoskeletal organization, and epidermal differentiation.13 The human kazrin gene is located on human chromosome 1p36.21 and encodes at least seven isoforms.14,15 KAZN is localized in various cellular compartments, including plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and the nucleus.12 Depletion of KAZN will cause ectoderm blisters, and craniofacial development defects in Xenopus embryos.16,17 Previous studies indicated that, downregulation of KAZN leads to cell apoptosis in U373MG human astrocytoma cells, and overexpression promotes cell proliferation and epithelial-mesenchymal transition to contribute to malignancy in HeLa and C33A cells.18 Our previous study also revealed that higher expression of KAZN is related to a lower survival rate in ovarian cancer.19 However, a recent study by Suguru Kurokawa et al. found a negative correlation between KAZN expression levels and disease severity as cirrhosis progressed to hepatocellular carcinoma.20 Lisa M Sevilla et al. observed a decreased expression of differentiation markers and increased proliferation of human epidermal keratinocytes by knocking down of KAZN.13 To date, the methylation pattern and the expression of KAZN in lung cancer is not yet investigated.

In this study, we integrated genome-wide DNA methylation and gene expression data from TCGA and GEO to identify KAZN methylation sites with potential for early NSCLC diagnosis. Using a machine learning approach, we utilized methylation markers within the KAZN gene to accurately distinguish normal lung tissue from NSCLC. These methylation markers were then validated in two independent cohorts. Furthermore, KAZN methylation markers demonstrated significant predictive power for NSCLC patient survival. Finally, the protein expression patterns and tumor suppressive activity of the KAZN was further validated by our in vitro experiments. Overall, this study demonstrates that the distinct KAZN methylation patterns may serve as an effective diagnostic or prognostic biomarker for NSCLC and reflect their involvement in tumor development.

Results

KAZN is downregulated in NSCLC

We conducted a comprehensive analysis of KAZN gene expression across pan-cancer datasets using the TCGA platform. Of the 33 cancer types examined, 9 lacked corresponding normal tissue samples (Table S1). Among the remaining 24 cancer types, KAZN expression was significantly altered in 13 types of cancers (Figure 1A). Specifically, KAZN was downregulated in breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), head and neck SCC (HNSC), kidney chromophobe (KICH), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PCPG), and prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), while upregulated in cervical SCC and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) (Figure 1A).

To further validate the differential expression of KAZN in lung cancer, we analyzed TCGA-LUAD, TCGA-LUSC, and genotype-tissue expression (GTEx) datasets. Our findings revealed a marked downregulation of KAZN in LUAD, with a more pronounced difference when compared to LUSC (Figures 1B and 1C). Additionally, we assessed the prognostic value of KAZN expression using TCGA-LUAD, TCGA-LUSC, and CPTAC data. In LUAD, patients with high KAZN expression exhibited significantly better survival rates compared to those with low KAZN expression (Figure 1D). However, in LUSC, no statistically significant difference in survival rates was observed between the high and low expression groups (Figure 1E). Analysis of KAZN expression in relation to TNM stage revealed no statistically significant differences (Figure S1A), suggesting that KAZN’s prognostic value in LUAD might be independent of tumor stage.

KAZN methylation sites hold diagnostic value for NSCLC

Altered DNA methylation is often seen as an early marker of tumorigenesis.21 To further investigate the methylation profile of the KAZN gene in lung cancer, we analyzed 2249 lung cancer samples and matched normal tissues from the TCGA, CPTAC, and GEO databases, focusing on the methylation sites of the KAZN gene on Illumina 450K DNA methylation microarrays (Table S2). Based on probe annotations of the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip (450K), we identified 133 CpG sites within the KAZN gene, which are mainly distributed in the promoter and enhancer regions (Figures 2A and S1B). We then identified 105 common methylation sites across the datasets, and performed clustering analysis. The results revealed distinct methylation patterns between lung cancer and normal tissue groups, underscoring the potential diagnostic value of KAZN methylation sites (Figure 2B).

To assess the diagnostic potential of KAZN methylation sites, we developed a classifier using a convolutional neural network (CNN) model. A flowchart of the study design is presented in Figure 2C. The model was constructed with 1534 samples from TCGA and GEO, including 1083 lung cancer samples and 451 normal samples (Table S2). The dataset was divided into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%). Independent datasets from CPTAC and GSE75008 were used for validation (Table S2). The CPTAC dataset, consisting of 715 samples, achieved an overall accuracy of 94%, correctly classifying 298 of 320 cancer samples and 280 of 295 normal samples (Figure 2D). The GSE75008 dataset, comprising 80 samples, achieved an accuracy of 95%, with 40 normal samples correctly predicted and 36 out of 40 cancer samples accurately classified (Figure 2E).

Furthermore, model performance was evaluated using ROC curve analysis, yielding an AUC of 0.97 in the CPTAC dataset (Figure 2F) and an AUC of 0.96 in the GSE75008 dataset (Figure 2G), demonstrating the strong discriminative ability of the classifier. These findings suggest that KAZN methylation sites could serve as reliable diagnostic biomarkers for lung cancer, with the potential to improve early detection and patient outcomes.

KAZN CpG sites are associated with survival in NSCLC

To investigate the correlation between KAZN methylation status and patient survival, we conducted an association analysis between KAZN gene methylation sites and lung cancer survival. Our findings revealed that 20 methylation sites including cg16985952, cg01567509, cg18861119, cg08118599, cg05033389, cg17596058, cg04487600, cg12485744, cg21413173, cg16663033, cg06840246, cg08237695, cg08468082, cg24425531, cg23273221, cg12436612, cg13322252, cg16822407, cg16552271, and cg23238147 were significantly associated with lung cancer survival (Figure 3A). Notably, these methylation sites also exhibited differential methylation between lung cancer and normal tissue samples (Figure 3B). Furthermore, we examined the relationship between these methylation sites and clinical staging of lung cancer. Among these, cg16985952, cg08237695, cg08468082, cg23273221, cg17596058, cg12485744, cg13322252, and cg16552271 were strongly associated with survival outcomes (Figure 3C).

To explore the potential of KAZN gene methylation sites as predictive biomarkers for lung cancer patient survival, we employed a deep learning approach using the DeepHit model.22 The results indicated that the model’s predictive performance was acceptable (5-fold cross-validation, average C-index = 0.655), suggesting that methylation patterns at KAZN gene sites could be used to predict survival times in lung cancer patients, offering potential clinical utility in personalized medicine.

KAZN expression is significantly reduced in lung cancer cell lines and primary tumor cells

To further validate the expression of KAZN in NSCLC, we collected 5 pairs of LUAD and adjacent normal tissues. RT-qPCR, western blotting, and immunohistofluorescence were carried out to detect KAZN expression at both the protein and mRNA levels. Our results indicated that KAZN expression was significantly lower in lung cancer tissue. This finding is consistent with our previous bioinformatic analysis, further supporting the downregulation of KAZN in lung cancer (Figures 4A–4E). Besides tissue analysis, we also examined KAZN expression in vitro using a normal human bronchial epithelium cell line (BEAS-2B) and two NSCLC cell lines (A549 and NCI-H1299). Consistent with our findings in patient tissues, KAZN expression was significantly lower in both NSCLC cell lines compared to the normal bronchial epithelium cell line BEAS-2B (Figures 4F–4H). These in vitro experiments further confirm that KAZN expression is significantly decreased in NSCLC.

The methylation profile of KAZN is correlated with its gene expression pattern

To elucidate the impact of DNA methylation on KAZN expression in NSCLC, we investigated the correlation between differential methylation patterns and KAZN gene expression profiles in cancerous and normal tissues. We used both methylation and RNA sequencing data to predict the correlation between the β value of KAZN CpG sites and expression level of KAZN. Spearman’s correlation analysis of 120 CpG sites revealed 46 methylation sites were inversely correlated with KAZN expression levels, while 47 CpG sites showed a positive correlation (p < 0.05, Table S3; Figure 5A). DNA methylation can have both activating and repressive effects on gene expression depending on the genomic context. We first evaluated the in vitro effect of the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-azacytidine on KAZN methylation using public data from GEO (GSE145588) to validate the impact of methylation on KAZN expression. Our analysis revealed that 5-azacytidine significantly inhibited KAZN hypermethylation across six human lung cancer cell lines (A549, NCI-H1299, CL1-0, CL1-5, H1792, PC9), and this inhibition was most effective in the A549 cell line (Figures 5B and S2A). Next, we determined the IC50 values of the DNA methyltransferase inhibitors 5-azacytidine in NSCLC cell lines A549 and NCI-H1299 using MTT assays (Figures S2B–S2C). Based on these results, we next treated the cells with 5 μmol/L and 10 μmol/L of 5-azacytidine, respectively, and the expression of KAZN was subsequently analyzed by western blotting. Our results showed that treatment with DNA methyltransferase inhibitor could significantly increase KAZN expression in both cell lines tested (Figures 5C–5F), supporting the role of epigenetic silencing in KAZN suppression in NSCLC cells.

KAZN promotes cell apoptosis and inhibit cell proliferation in human NSCLC cells

To determine the biological function and molecular mechanism of KAZN in lung cancer, we investigated the effects of KAZN on cultured NSCLC cells. As a first step, we analyzed the expression levels of different KAZN transcripts in tumor and normal control tissues using RNA sequencing data from GEO dataset (SRP229747). As shown in Figure S3, there are four main transcripts for KAZN, of which ENST00000376030.7 (NM_201628.3, Kazrin E), ENST00000361144.9 (NM_001018000.4, Kazrin B), and ENST00000400797.3 (NM_001017999.3, Kazrin C) expression was significantly decreased in lung cancer. We picked kazrin B for further study. In silico analysis of the genomic KAZN sequence by methprimer23 predicted a large CpG island at the promoter region of kazrin B (Figure S4A). CpG islands are often targets for DNA methylation, which can influence gene expression. To investigate the effects of KAZN overexpression, we transiently transfected CDS sequence of kazrin B into the NSCLC cell lines A549 and NCI-H1299 (Figures S4B–S4E). The immunoblotting results showed that overexpressing KAZN in A549 and NCI-H1299 cells resulted in increased levels of cleaved caspase-3, a marker of apoptosis activation, suggesting a potential role for KAZN in promoting apoptosis in NSCLC cells (Figures 6A and 6C). Furthermore, KAZN overexpression significantly reduced the expression of PCNA, a key regulator of cell cycle progression (Figures 6B and 6D). DNA synthesis in cultured NSCLC cell lines was subsequently assessed via 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay. In line with the previous findings, KAZN overexpression significantly decreased the EdU-positive cell number compared with the control group (Figures 6E–6H). We then silenced KAZN by siRNA in A549 cells (Figure S4D). Following immunoblotting results showed that KAZN downregulation significantly downregulated the expression of cleaved caspase-3 and upregulated PCNA (Figures S4E and S4F). In summary, our data show that KAZN may suppress tumorigenesis in NSCLC by simultaneously promoting apoptosis and inhibiting cell proliferation.

USP2 is a potential target of KAZN

We further conducted a gene correlation analysis based on TCGA LUAD mRNA expression data (TPM values) to identify genes associated with KAZN expression. The results from this correlation analysis were then used to perform gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), which was employed to explore the biological processes and signaling pathways associated with genes correlated with KAZN. The GSEA results indicated that genes negatively correlated with KAZN are primarily enriched in pathways related to cell development and cancer progression (Figure S4G).

To investigate potential functional relationships, we predicted protein-protein interactions (PPIs) among genes correlated with KAZN in lung cancer. As shown in Figure S4H, 6 proteins including TXN2, TXN1, SMIM10L1, CXXC4, BEX1, and BEX2 are negatively correlated with KAZN, 5 proteins including USP2, PSMA1, ARDID5A LMO2, and LZTS1 are positively correlated with KAZN expression. To identify potential downstream targets of KAZN, we overexpressed KAZN in A549 cells and examined the expression of USP2, PSMA1, and LMO2. Our results showed that KAZN overexpression significantly increased USP2 at mRNA levels (Figures S4I–S4K). Further experiments confirmed that USP2 expression levels were significantly lower in LUAD tissues compared to adjacent normal controls (Figure 7A). Overexpression of KAZN significantly upregulated the expression of USP2 in A549 and NCI-H1299 cell lines (Figures 7B and 7C), while KAZN siRNA interference (Si 1309) significantly decreased USP2 expression in A549 cells (Figure 7D). Immunofluorescence staining of USP2 and KAZN revealed that these two proteins are colocalized in the cytoplasm of BEAS-2B cells (Figure 7E), further suggesting a potential interaction between the proteins. These results provide insights into the molecular mechanisms that KAZN may interact with USP2 to affect lung cancer pathogenesis and highlights potential targets for further investigation.

Mendelian Randomization analyses of KAZN gene methylation sites

To further investigate the relationship between KAZN methylation sites and lung cancer, we conducted a two-sample MR analysis. The analytical workflow is illustrated in Figure 7F. We applied stringent selection criteria to identify methylation sites for MR analyses. Using a threshold of p < 5e-8 and excluding single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in strong linkage disequilibrium, we identified KAZN gene methylation sites with at least three independent SNPs for subsequent analyses. All instrumental variables (SNPs) associated with these methylation sites had F-statistics greater than 10, indicating robust instruments for MR (Table S4).

In the MR analysis, methylation sites cg18861119, cg01208873, and cg19260606 yielded significant results using both the inverse variance weighted (IVW) and weighted median methods, with consistent effect estimates (Figure 7G; Table S5). The IVW p values were 0.0255, 0.0004, and 0.0034, respectively, while the Weighted Median p values were 0.0285, 0.0093, and 0.0401, respectively (Figure 7G; Table S5). Although the MR Egger method did not produce significant results, the effect directions remained consistent with those of the IVW method, reinforcing the potential causal association (Figure 7G; Table S5).

Of the three CpG sites, cg01208873 hypermethylation was negatively correlated with KAZN mRNA expression (Table S3; Figure 7H). Furthermore, cg01208873 demonstrated significantly higher methylation levels in LUAD compared to normal controls (Figure 7I). These findings suggest that cg01208873 methylation may play a role in LUAD development.

For methylation sites cg01567509 and cg24545979, the IVW method revealed a significant association between the CpG sites and the outcome (p = 0.0344 and 0.0371, respectively) (Figure 7G). The MR Egger and weighted median methods, though non-significant or borderline significant, maintained consistent effect directions with the IVW results. This alignment in effect direction across multiple methods supports the robustness of these findings. No evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was detected, as the MR Egger intercept test yielded p values greater than 0.05 for all sites (p = 0.6170, 0.6065, 0.4219, 0.5870, and 0.6892, respectively), indicating no significant pleiotropic effects (Table S6). Additionally, heterogeneity tests revealed no significant heterogeneity (p > 0.05), further supporting the reliability of our findings (Table S7).

Collectively, these results suggest a potential causal relationship between KAZN gene methylation and lung cancer. Methylation sites cg18861119, cg01208873, and cg19260606 were associated with an increased risk of lung cancer (OR > 1). Most importantly, hypermethylation of cg01208873 potentially contribute to NSCLC development by inhibiting the KAZN expression. Methylation sites cg01567509 and cg24545979 were associated with a decreased risk (OR < 1).

In the MR scatterplots (Figure 7J), the SNP effects on exposure (methylation) versus outcome (lung cancer) are displayed, with regression lines representing the IVW, MR Egger, and weighted median methods. The consistency in effect directions across these methods intuitively supports the quantitative results, further reinforcing the plausibility of a causal effect.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that methylation signatures of KAZN are valuable indicators for NSCLC diagnosis and prognosis. Furthermore, we validated the biological function of KAZN, revealing its capacity to modulate NSCLC cell proliferation and apoptosis through variations in its methylation status. MR analysis identified potential CpG sites associated with lung cancer risk. Our findings may contribute to improved NSCLC detection and provide potential therapeutic targets.

Our study investigates the potential of KAZN methylation sites as diagnostic biomarkers using a CNN model. The model was meticulously designed and trained on a well-balanced dataset consisting of 1534 samples. The dataset was divided into 70% for training and 30% for testing, ensuring a robust evaluation of the model’s performance. To prevent data leakage, best practices were adhered to, including the separation of training and testing data and independent validation with the CPTAC and GSE75008 datasets. This approach effectively mitigated the risk of overfitting and confirmed the model’s capacity to generalize to unseen data, as evidenced by the high accuracy and AUC values obtained. Despite these promising results, several potential issues warrant consideration. While the dataset’s size is relatively substantial, the sample size remains limited, which may impact the stability and accuracy of the results. Additionally, the model’s reliance on specific methylation sites derived from tissue samples may limit its applicability to other sample types or diverse populations. Future research should evaluate these methylation sites across different samples and populations to ensure their broad applicability. While these findings are promising, further clinical research is essential to assess the practical value of these biomarkers. Specifically, testing in real-world clinical settings is needed to determine their effectiveness for early detection and patient prognosis. Overall, our research provides compelling evidence supporting the potential of KAZN methylation sites as lung cancer diagnostic biomarkers. However, addressing the outlined issues is crucial for ensuring the reliability and practical application of these findings and translating them into clinical benefits.

The biological significance of DNA methylation in lung cancer has gained increasing recognition, prompting several studies to investigate its potential as a diagnostic tool for NSCLC. For instance, Akira Ooki et al. identified a methylation panel of 6 genes (CDO1, HOXA9, AJAP1, PTGDR, UNCX, and MARCH11) that achieved an accuracy of 93.0% in an independent cohort of stage IA primary NSCLC.24 Jeffrey A. Tsou et al. used four DNA methylation loci (CDKN2A EX2, CDX2, HOXA1, and OPCML) to distinguish LUAD from normal controls, achieving a sensitivity of 67%–86% and specificity of 74%–82%.25 Similarly, an optimized methylation status of a 5-gene panel (NEUROG2, NID2, RASSF1A, APC, and HOXC9) demonstrated high accuracy in detecting stage I NSCLC (91.26% sensitivity and 84.62% specificity),26 while Nan Shen et al. identified a prediction model based on four CpG loci across four genes, achieving 94.57% accuracy.27 In contrast to these multi-gene approaches, our study focused on CpG loci within a single gene, KAZN, achieving diagnostic accuracies of 94% and 95% in two independent cohorts. Our in vitro study validated KAZN as an important tumor suppressor gene in NSCLC. This single-gene CpG-based prediction model simplifies testing procedures, potentially increasing its clinical application potential.

KAZN is an evolutionarily conserved gene essential for vertebrate development; however, its function in cancer development and underlying molecular mechanisms remains to be fully elucidated. Our analysis of KAZN negatively correlated gene sets revealed a statistically significant over-representation of pathways related to the cell cycle, TP53 and TP73-mediated cell-cycle arrest, and cancer, pointing to a potential tumor suppressor role for KAZN in lung cancer. This role is further supported by a broader pan-cancer analysis demonstrating a striking tumor-specificity in KAZN expression (Figure 1A). These findings, stand in stark contrast to previous studies demonstrating tumor-promoting activities of KAZN in oral SCC and human cervical cancer cells.18,28 This apparent contradiction underscores the intricate regulation and functional diversity of KAZN, highlighting that its impact on cellular processes is highly context-dependent. Further comparison of the KAZN methylation profile in LUAD, KIRC, and STAD showed that the methylation of KAZN is site-specific across different cancer types, and LUAD and KIRC with downregulated KAZN expression have a more similar methylation profile to each other than to KAZN upregulated STAD (Figure S5). Future studies are crucial to unravel the complex interplay between KAZN and the tumor microenvironment across various cancer types.

KAZN exhibits complex transcriptional diversity, as demonstrated in our analysis of RNA sequencing data, which revealed nine distinct isoforms (Figure S3A). Differential expression analysis highlighted a significant downregulation of isoforms ENST00000376030.7, ENST00000361144.9, and ENST00000400797.3 in tumor tissues (Figure S3A). Overexpression of isoform ENST00000361144.9 significantly elevated apoptosis and inhibited proliferation in NSCLC cell lines. However, while isoform ENST00000400798.6 demonstrated a high overall expression level, it did not exhibit significant differential expression between tumor and normal tissues. These findings strongly suggest that the biological function of KAZN is intricately linked to the relative abundance of its different isoforms, with each isoform potentially exhibiting unique functional roles. These differences may arise from variations in protein-protein interactions, subcellular localization, or other regulatory mechanisms. The observed complexity of KAZN isoform expression and function in the context of tumor development aligns with previous studies demonstrating functional diversity among different isoforms.12,29,30,31,32 This diversity highlights the importance of considering isoform-specific functions, as alterations in isoform ratios are increasingly recognized as key drivers of cancer development and treatment resistance.33,34,35 Further investigation is warranted to fully elucidate the specific roles and regulatory mechanisms of each KAZN isoform in both normal physiology and tumorigenesis.

MR is a method that utilizes genetic variation to determine the causal effects of modifiable exposures on health outcomes.36,37 The MR analysis conducted in this study adhered to the core assumptions required to infer causal relationships. First, the instrumental variables (IVs) selected for each KAZN gene methylation site met the relevance criterion, as evidenced by their strong association with the exposures (methylation levels) and F-statistics greater than 10, indicating robust instruments and minimizing the likelihood of weak instrument bias. Second, the exclusion restriction assumption appears to hold, as none of the SNPs were significantly associated with lung cancer in the outcome dataset (p > 5e-8), supporting the postulation that the SNPs do not influence the outcome directly. Moreover, the MR Egger intercept tests did not show significant pleiotropy (p > 0.05), further suggesting that the IVs do not exert direct effects on the outcome independent of the exposure. Finally, the independence assumption was addressed by examining potential confounders using the LDtrait Tool, which revealed that none of the SNPs was significantly associated with other phenotypes that could act as confounders. This comprehensive validation strengthens the robustness of the causal inference from our MR analysis. Several studies have emphasized that when the effect direction is aligned across methods, especially in cases where MR Egger lacks significance but the IVW method is robust, it still supports the existence of a causal effect.29,38,39 Therefore, despite the lack of statistical significance in MR Egger, the results from IVW and the supportive role of the Weighted Median method strengthen the conclusion that methylation of specific KAZN gene CpG sites likely contributes to lung cancer risk. The findings from this MR study contribute to our understanding of the epigenetic regulation of lung cancer and highlight specific methylation sites that may serve as potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets. However, further experimental validation is necessary to confirm the observed association between these CpG sites, KAZN expression, and lung cancer development.

In summary, we identified a novel CpG methylation panel within the KAZN gene that demonstrates high sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis and prognosis of NSCLC. We further validated the tumor-suppressive role of KAZN in vitro, demonstrating its ability to inhibit proliferation and promote apoptosis. These findings suggest that the KAZN methylation profile could be utilized to identify NSCLC and predict patient survival. Furthermore, specific methylation sites within KAZN or the gene itself could serve as potential targets for cancer treatment. Further investigations are warranted to fully explore these promising clinical applications.

Limitations of the study

While this study demonstrates the diagnostic and prognostic value of KAZN methylation in NSCLC, several limitations should be noted. Although our combined dataset was substantial, the relatively small sample size of specific validation cohorts, such as GSE75008, may affect the generalizability and stability of our findings. Furthermore, while MR analysis provided supportive evidence for a causal relationship, additional experimental validation is necessary to definitively confirm the causal impact of specific KAZN methylation sites on NSCLC development and to elucidate the intricate interplay with other factors in real-world clinical settings. While our in vitro findings have established KAZN as a modulator of NSCLC cell proliferation and apoptosis, in vivo studies are still required to further confirm its biological relevance.

Experimental model and study participant details

Patient samples

This study involved the prospective collection of 10 pairs of lung tissues, including fresh lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) (n=5) and paired adjacent normal lung (n=5) tissues, from the second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Additionally, 5 pairs of paraffin-embedded, biopsy-proven lung adenocarcinoma tissue samples were sourced from Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital. The participants for the prospective fresh tissue collection ranged in age from 46 to 73 years, and both males and females were included. Due to the limited sample size, detailed analysis of the influence of sex and age on the study results was not performed; the potential impact of these factors warrants further investigation. Fresh tissue samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C until further analysis. All patients provided written informed consent prior to the study. The research protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University (Reference number: IRB2006724).

Method details

Gene expression and RNA-Seq analysis

Gene expression data were primarily obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project, encompassing 33 different types of cancer along with corresponding normal tissue samples. To account for batch effects, the R package SVA was employed, utilizing the ComBat function for correction. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package, with statistical significance determined by the Wilcoxon test. Additionally, RNA-Seq data for project SRP229747 were downloaded from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database. This dataset includes 51 lung cancer samples and 49 matched normal control tissues. The STAR aligner was used to map the sequencing reads to the reference genome, which was consistent with the GTEx project, utilizing Gencode v26 (GRCh38). Gene and transcript expression profiles were quantified using RSEM. The normalized read counts were subsequently used for downstream expression analyses.

Construction, training, prediction, and evaluation of the neural network model

The analytical workflow is illustrated in Figure 2B. Methylation data were sourced from the 450K array datasets available through TCGA and GEO. Shared methylation sites across the datasets were selected, followed by batch effect correction, normalization, differential methylation site analysis, and feature selection using the sklearn library. Data preprocessing included the imputation of missing values and scaling of features. Initially, the data were split into training and testing sets using an 70/30 ratio. Feature selection was performed using the SelectKBest method with ANOVA F-statistics (f_classif), retaining all relevant features. The selected features were then standardized using the StandardScaler. The input layer consists of reshaped data, transforming one-dimensional arrays into two-dimensional matrices.

The CNN architecture began with a 2D convolutional layer with 32 filters and a kernel size of 3 × 3, followed by a max-pooling layer with a pool size of 2 × 2. The output was then flattened, passed through a dense layer with 128 neurons and a ReLU activation function, followed by a dropout layer (50% dropout rate) to prevent overfitting. The final output layer used a sigmoid activation function for binary classification.

Model training was conducted using the binary cross-entropy loss function, optimized with the Adam optimizer, and the model's performance was tracked using accuracy as a metric. Training involved 100 epochs, with 20% of the training data reserved for validation. The trained model's performance was evaluated using independent validation sets from CPTAC and an additional GEO dataset. Predictions were assessed using a confusion matrix and ROC curve analysis, enabling a robust evaluation of the model's classification accuracy and diagnostic capability.

Overall survival calculation and survival analysis

The overall survival data were obtained from TCGA LUAD, LUSC, and CPTAC datasets. Overall survival (OS) was calculated as the number of months between the year of diagnosis and the year of death from any cause, the date of last follow-up, or a 5-year censored survival data point, whichever occurred first. To investigate the prognostic significance of mRNA expression levels or methylation status at specific methylation sites, samples were categorized into “high” or “low” expression/methylation groups based on median expression or methylation levels. The survival distributions between these subgroups were compared using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, with the log-rank test employed to assess statistical significance. A p-value of less than 0.05 in the log-rank test was considered indicative of a statistically significant difference in survival between the subgroups. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using the 'survival' and 'survminer' packages in R (Version: 4.3.3), which allowed for the visualization and interpretation of survival outcomes across different expression/methylation groups.

The DeepHit model, a deep learning-based approach for survival analysis was employed to assess the prognostic value of KAZN methylation sites in predicting survival. The model's predictive performance was quantified using the concordance index (C-index), a metric that evaluates the accuracy of risk predictions relative to observed survival outcomes. A higher C-index indicates better discriminatory ability of the model to distinguish between patients with different survival probabilities.

Enrichment and protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis

The gene expression data were first filtered to retain only protein-coding genes, with any duplicates removed to ensure data accuracy. After normalization of the expression matrix, a Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to identify genes associated with KAZN expression. Genes exhibiting both positive and negative correlations with KAZN were then extracted and ranked according to the absolute values of their correlation coefficients. The clusterProfiler package was utilized to conduct Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on the most strongly correlated genes, using gene sets from the MSigDB database. The results of the enrichment analysis were subsequently visualized with the enrichplot package.

Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) involving KAZN were investigated using data from UniProt and STRING databases. These interactions were mapped and visualized using Cytoscape, with proteins color-coded based on their correlation with KAZN expression in lung cancer. To validate these interactions and assess their biological relevance in the context of lung cancer, the online tool OncoDB was employed.

Mendelian randomization

Two sets of genetic instruments were employed to investigate the potential causal relationship between KAZN methylation sites and lung cancer. The exposure dataset was obtained from the Genetics of DNA Methylation Consortium (GoDMC), and included genetic variations associated with 420,509 methylation sites across 32,851 participants. The outcome dataset, ebi-a-GCST90018875, was downloaded from the IEU Open GWAS project, comprising a total of 492,803 European ancestry samples.

The selection criteria for genetic instruments were as follows: GWAS-associated P-value < 5 × 10-8, linkage disequilibrium (LD) r2 < 0.1, and physical distance between index variants < 1000,000 base pairs. The MR analysis was conducted using the R package TwoSampleMR. The primary methods applied included the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) random effects model, MR-Egger regression, and the weighted median method. Heterogeneity and pleiotropy were assessed using Cochran's Q test and the MR-Egger intercept, respectively, as implemented in the TwoSampleMR package.

Cell culture and treatment

Human lung adenocarcinoma cell lines A549 and NCI-H1299 were cultured in complete DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and the human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B were cultured in bronchial epithelial cell medium supplemented with bronchial epithelial cell growth supplement at 37°C in humidified air with 5% CO2.For plasmid transfections, A549 and NCI-H1299 cells were seeded into 6-well plate at a density of 2.5×105 cells per well one day before the experiment. Upon 70% cell confluence, the recombinant plasmids were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Kit in accordance with the operational instructions provided by the manufacturer, and the cells were collected for subsequent experiments after 48h of transfection. To inhibit methyltransferases, cells were first starved for 6 hours in serum-free culture medium and subsequently treated with either 5 μmol or 10 μmol of 5-Azacytidine for 72 hours, with the treatment solution being replaced every 24 hours.

Plasmid construction

The full-length of Kazrin B (Genbank: NM_001018000.4) cDNA fragment was synthesized (GENEWIZ, Suzhou, China) and cloned into a pcDNA3.1(+) vector.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells or fresh tissues with TRIzoL reagent. Quality assessment and quantification of the total RNA were performed using the NanoDrop. Complementary DNA was synthesized by the Revertra Ace qPCR RT kit. All qPCR analysis were performed using SYBR Green Realtime PCR Master Mix on a real-time PCR detection system. Quantification of mRNAs was normalized to the levels of GAPDH.

Tissue immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence staining on paraffin sections, slides were heated at 65°C for 2 h and submerged into xylene, followed by serial deparaffinization steps (2 × xylene, 100% ethanol, 80% ethanol, 50% ethanol; each 5 min).Antigen retrieval was done by microwave boiling in 10 mM citrate buffer for 10 min. Slides were permeabilized in 0.3% TritonX-100 at room temperature for 30 min, then blocked with 5% goat serum at 37°C for 2 h and incubated with primary antibodies in 1% BSA at 4°C overnight. On the next day, slides were washed with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 15 min. All images were acquired under a microscope. Image adjustment and analysis was performed with ImageJ software.

H&E staining

Paraffin tissue sections were roasted at 65°C for 2 h firstly. After paraffin sections were dewaxed and hydrated, the nucleus was stained with hematoxylin staining solution, and then cytoplasmic staining was carried out with eosin staining solution. After the slides were dehydrated, the sheets were preserved with a neutral balsam and the images were captured with an inverted microscope.

Protein extraction and western blotting analysis

All cells and tissues were lysed in Cell lysis buffer for Western and IP supplemented with 1% PMSF. The total protein concentration in the tissue homogenate was measured by the BCA protein assay kit. A total of 40μg protein from the samples was fractionated via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, then transferred to a nitrocellulose (NC) filter membrane. The NC membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat milk for 1h at room temperature, and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Next, the membranes were incubated in horseradish peroxidase-labeled secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Afterward, the previous primary antibody and secondary antibody were stripped from the NC membranes with stripping buffer for about 8 min at RT and the condition of stripping would be reexamined with enhanced chemiluminescence. After being re-blocked, the membrane was re-incubated with another primary antibody overnight at 4°C. The following steps were the same as described above. One NC membrane was used to detect at most three different proteins. Protein bands were developed through enhanced chemiluminescence, and grey value was quantified with ImageJ software.

MTT assay

A549 and NCI-H1299 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 7000 cells per well and incubated in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C overnight. Next, the cells were incubated in a medium containing 5% FBS and 0, 1, 2.5, 5, 10, 20 and 40 μmol/L 5-AzaC for 72 h, changing the solution every 24 h. Simultaneously, the IC50 of 5-AzaC was determined. Subsequently, 100 μL MTT (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well for 4 h incubation, and the supernatant was then removed, followed by the addition of 100 μL DMSO to terminate the reaction. The cells were then incubated for another 10 min at room temperature, and the optical density (OD) value of each well was detected at a wavelength of 492 nm using a microplate reader.

EdU proliferation assay

EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine) was detected by the BeyoClickTM EdU Cell Proliferation Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well. EdU was added into the transfected cell culture medium at the final concentration of 10 μM for 2 h, followed by 15 min of fixation and 15 min of permeabilization. After 30 min of incubation in 0.5 mL click reaction solution and 15 min of nuclear staining with 1 × hoechst, the images of the cells were then acquired under a microscope.

Immunofluorescence double staining and colocalization analysis

BEAS-2B cells were fixed with ice-cold methanol for 15 min, washed with PBS, and then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 30 min, followed by blocking with 5% BSA at room temperature for 30 min. After blocking, cells were incubated with USP2 mouse primary antibody at room temperature for 4 h, washed with PBS, and then incubated with KAZN rabbit primary antibody for another 4 h. Following primary antibody incubation, cells were sequentially incubated with Cy3-labeled Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) and FITC-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L), each at 37°C for 1 h. Finally, nuclei were stained with DAPI at room temperature for 15 min, washed with PBS, and colocalization analysis was performed under fluorescence microscopy.

siRNA transfection and knockdown

A549 cells at 80% confluence were transfected using the E-Poly™ Transfection Reagent. Reagent A and Reagent B were equilibrated to room temperature and vortexed. Transfection complexes were prepared by mixing 1 μg siRNA (NC or si-KAZN at 20 pmol/L concentration) with 50 μL Reagent A, followed by addition of 3 μL Reagent B and incubation at room temperature for 20 min. 50 μL of each complex was added to 6-well plates containing 1 mL complete medium, and cells were cultured for 48 h before RNA extraction.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Unless otherwise mentioned, significance tests were determined by the two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) derived from at least three independent experiments. R software or GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software were used for statistical analyses and data graphing.
