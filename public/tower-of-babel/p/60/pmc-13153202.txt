Contrasting spatial and temporal structuring of seawater and sediment bacterial communities in coastal environments
Authors: Stephanie N Vaughn, Jacqueline C Pavlovsky, Jordan A Heiman, Colin R Jackson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial communities in seawater and sediment are essential to biogeochemical processes in coastal systems, yet their spatial and temporal dynamics remain poorly understood, and few studies have examined how bacterial communities in these habitats co-vary. We characterized bacterial communities along the northern Gulf of Mexico coast using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing of > 800 seawater and sediment samples collected over 15 months from five beaches spanning 40 km. Seawater and sediment harbored distinct bacterial assemblages that exhibited contrasting patterns of variation. Seawater communities showed seasonal turnover and a temporal distance–decay relationship driven by temperature and salinity, with the most abundant populations showing species-specific relationships to environmental conditions. Sediment bacterial communities were more diverse and were more stable over time with greater spatial differentiation. These results show that seawater and sediment bacterial communities in nearshore systems are structured by distinct spatiotemporal processes and provide new insights into the dynamics of coastal microbiomes. Understanding how bacterial communities in nearshore ecosystems respond to spatiotemporal and environmental variation is essential for predicting ecosystem resilience and informing monitoring and management strategies in an understudied coastal system.Keywords: Coastal microbiome, marine bacteria, seawater, sediment, spatiotemporal structuring, environmental gradientsSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology, Ocean sciences

Introduction

Bacteria play critical roles in nutrient cycling and organic matter degradation in coastal environments1,2. However, bacterial community dynamics in coastal systems remain poorly resolved, as is the influence of environmental factors on these communities over different spatial and temporal scales. Spatiotemporal analysis of marine bacterial communities has focused on environmental gradients from freshwater to marine and coastal to offshore transitions3–5, or on long-term trends of bacterioplankton communities that respond to environmental conditions such as temperature, salinity, and nutrient concentrations3–6. However, studies that describe temporal and spatial patterns in both seawater and sediment bacterial communities are limited, especially in coastal systems. More specifically, little is known about bacterial community dynamics along the northern coast of the Gulf of Mexico, particularly the Mississippi coast. Increasingly, this region is influenced by freshwater inputs from rivers, bays, and flood defense systems7–10, underscoring the need to characterize baseline bacterial community structure and dynamics to better anticipate responses to ongoing and future environmental changes.

Coastal environments are characterized by interconnected yet ecologically distinct habitats, with seawater and sediment supporting diverse microbial assemblages1. Both habitats are important for coastal biogeochemical processes such as organic matter degradation, nutrient recycling, and sediment–water exchange1,2, yet few studies have directly compared the dynamics of seawater and sediment bacterial communities. The two habitats harbor distinct microbial communities11–13, with seawater communities typically dominated by Alphaproteobacteria, Bacteroidota, and Cyanobacteriota5,6,12 and sediment communities primarily composed of Gammaproteobacteria, Planctomycetes, and Bacteroidota13,14. Additionally, marine sediment bacterial communities typically are more diverse than those in seawater12,13,15. Seawater bacterial communities can show seasonal or annual cycles in their composition primarily correlated with changes in temperature and salinity2,6,16, while sediment bacterial communities often lack this cyclical pattern and may be more stable to temporal and environmental fluctuations13,15,17,18. How these potential differences in bacterial community dynamics vary between seawater and sediment communities in the same coastal system has implications for ecosystem processes, and for how coastal systems respond to environmental change.

In this study, we determined the importance of spatial and temporal variation in structuring bacterial communities in seawater and sediment along the Mississippi coast. We hypothesized that seawater bacterial communities would show more variation over time, a function of a more dynamic habitat than sediment, and be influenced by factors such as salinity that can show high variation in these systems. In contrast, we hypothesized that bacterial communities in sediment would show more spatial variation than those in seawater and be more influenced by environmental factors such as temperature that show longer term patterns. Through an extensive sampling campaign that collected > 800 samples that integrated long-term (15-month) and short-term (daily, weekly) temporal sampling with regional-scale (five beaches across almost 40 km) sampling for spatial patterns, we identify broader patterns of community turnover and stability in these coastal habitats. Our findings expand our current knowledge of microbial communities in coastal systems, while specifically highlighting bacterial communities in an understudied region.

Results

Seawater and sediment bacterial communities show distinct spatiotemporal patterns

Bacterial community composition varied significantly by habitat type (i.e., seawater or sediment; PERMANOVA; F = 1,478.5, R2 = 0.27, p < 0.001), date (F = 38.3, R2 = 0.19, p < 0.001) and beach (F = 66.5, R2 = 0.05, p < 0.001), with a three-way interaction of habitat x date x beach (F = 5.24, R2 = 0.10, p < 0.001; Fig. 1A). In seawater, the effect of date on bacterial community composition (F = 88.2, R2 = 0.64, p < 0.001) was much greater than the spatial effect of beach (F = 23.5, R2 = 0.04, p < 0.001), although both were significant, and there was a date x beach interaction (F = 8.67, R2 = 0.24, p < 0.001).

(B) Seawater and (C) sediment communities ordinated separately to show spatiotemporal patterns, with colors representing month and shapes representing beach, with beach numbers increasing from west to east. Vectors for temperature (Temp), salinity, dissolved oxygen (DO), and pH are shown on ordinations of seawater (B) and sediment (C), with length and direction of vector lines indicating the relative strength and direction of associations with bacterial communities.

Bacterial communities in seawater samples showed significant systematic dispersion by date (permutest ANOVA; F = 10.1, p < 0.001) and beach (F = 2.65, p < 0.05). When sampling date was grouped into meteorological seasons (i.e., Spring, Summer, Fall, Winter), significant seasonal variation in seawater bacterial communities was detected (F = 82.7, R2 = 0.35, p < 0.001) with a significant, but much weaker season x beach interaction (F = 3.12, R2 = 0.05, p < 0.001). There was also systematic dispersion of seawater bacterial communities by season (F = 27.1, p < 0.001). NMDS visualization further illustrated seasonally structured shifts in seawater bacterial community composition over the 15-month period (Fig. 1B).

Sediment bacterial communities also differed by date (F = 14.9, R2 = 0.27, p < 0.001) but the effect size of date was similar to the spatial effect of beach (F = 70.8, R2 = 0.20, p < 0.001). As with seawater, there was a date x beach interaction (F = 4.66, R2 = 0.34, p < 0.001), as well as significant systematic dispersion by date (F = 1.80, p < 0.01) and beach (F = 9.07, p < 0.001). While there was some similarity in community composition between sediment samples taken towards the beginning and end of the 15-month study, NMDS did not suggest a strong seasonal trend in sediment communities (Fig. 1C). This was confirmed when sample dates were grouped by season, as while there was a significant seasonal effect on sediment bacterial communities, this explained only a small amount of variation (F = 21.1, R2 = 0.11, p < 0.001), as did the season x beach interaction (F = 3.41, R2 = 0.07, p < 0.001).

Salinity and temperature exerted strong influences on seawater bacterial communities (envfit; R2 = 0.73 and R2 = 0.69, respectively, p < 0.001), with pH and DO having weaker, but significant, influences (R2 = 0.31 and R2 = 0.07, respectively, p < 0.001). Sediment communities were less influenced by environmental parameters, with temperature having the largest effect (R2 = 0.31, p < 0.001), followed by salinity and pH (both R2 = 0.14, p < 0.001) and DO (R2 = 0.08, p < 0.001).

Bacterial communities exhibit stronger temporal patterns in seawater than in sediment

Bacterial communities in seawater showed a temporal distance-decay relationship, with community dissimilarity increasing with days between samples (linear regression: y = 0.531 + 0.001days; Fig. 2A). This linear model explained 39% of the variation in community dissimilarity (R2 = 0.39, p < 0.001). The non-linear GAM explained much more variation in community dissimilarity (69%, EDF = 8.92, R2 = 0.69), with dissimilarity decreasing as temporal distance exceeded approximately 200 days (Fig. 2A). Sediment communities displayed a much weaker temporal distance-decay relationship (linear regression: y = 0.709 + 0.0003days), with days between sampling explaining just 7% (linear model: p < 0.001) of community dissimilarity or 13% with the non-linear GAM (EDF = 8.99, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B).

Higher bacterial richness in sediment contrasts with greater temporal variability in seawater

Bacterial species richness (Sobs) was higher in sediment (mean ± SD: 1,429 ± 284) than in seawater (647 ± 157) (ANOVA; F = 15,940, p < 0.001; Fig. 3A). In both habitats, species richness differed by date (ANOVA; seawater: F = 47.8, p < 0.001, sediment: F = 60.3, p < 0.001) and beach (seawater: F = 25.9, p < 0.001, sediment: F = 85.4, p < 0.001), with a date x beach interaction (seawater: F = 8.93, p < 0.001, sediment: F = 6.06, p < 0.001). Temporal variability explained more of the variation in bacterial species richness in seawater than in sediment, with GAMs indicating that days between sampling explained 32% of the variation in richness in seawater (EDF = 8.79, R²=0.32, p < 0.001) compared to 20% for sediment (EDF = 8.31, R²=0.20, p < 0.001). Linear regressions showed a decrease in richness over time for seawater (lm: y = 763-0.60d; R2 = 0.14, p < 0.001) and sediment (lm: y = 1575-0.75d; R2 = 0.07, p < 0.001). Mantel correlations further demonstrated that temporal variation was the dominant driver of seawater species richness (Pearson’s (r) = 0.30, p < 0.001), while spatial variation was insignificant (p > 0.05). In contrast, sediment richness showed weak, but significant, associations with temporal (r = 0.09, p < 0.01) and spatial (r = 0.08, p < 0.01) distance, and tended to fluctuate every few months rather than show a consistent temporal pattern (Fig. 3A). Species richness in seawater was negatively correlated with temperature (r=-0.35, p < 0.001) and pH (r=-0.31, p < 0.001) and not correlated with salinity or DO. Species richness in sediment was negatively correlated with pH (r=-0.29, p < 0.001), positively correlated with DO (r = 0.18, p < 0.001) and showed no correlations with temperature or salinity.

Bacterial species diversity (as determined by the Inverse Simpson’s index) followed similar patterns to species richness. Species diversity was higher in sediment (mean ± SD: 257.5 + 6.04) than in seawater (59.1 + 1.04) (F = 1,028, p < 0.001), and varied significantly by beach (seawater: F = 24.1, p < 0.001, sediment: F = 54.3, p < 0.001) and date (seawater: F = 19.7, p < 0.001, sediment: F = 47.8, p < 0.001) in both habitats (Fig. 3B). Days between sample collection explained 33% of the variation in species diversity of seawater (EDF = 4.22, R²=0.33, p < 0.001) and 16% of the variation in sediment (EDF = 7.76, R²=0.16, p < 0.001). Linear regressions showed that bacterial species diversity in seawater had a slight increase over time (lm: y = 65.8-0.03d; R2 = 0.02, p < 0.05) but did not vary significantly for sediment (p > 0.05). In contrast to species richness, spatial variation between beaches was more influential on species diversity in seawater (r = 0.19, p < 0.01) and sediment (r = 0.15, p < 0.01) than temporal variation between sample date (seawater: r=-0.12, p < 0.05; sediment: p > 0.05). Bacterial species diversity in seawater was negatively correlated with temperature (r=-0.56, p < 0.001) and pH (r=-0.44, p < 0.001), positively correlated with salinity (r = 0.22, p < 0.001), and not correlated with DO. In sediment, bacterial species diversity was positively correlated with temperature (r = 0.16, p < 0.01) and DO (r = 0.13, p < 0.01) and showed no correlations with salinity or pH.

ASVs show temporal patterns in seawater and spatial patterns in sediment

Common ASVs in seawater included ASV1 and ASV3 (both Cyanobium PCC-6307 sp.; Cyanobacteriota), detected in 76% and 75% of seawater samples, respectively. Both showed strong temporal patterns (MANOVA; F = 91.5, p < 0.001 and F = 64.1, p < 0.001; Fig. 4A), and weaker spatial patterns by beach sampled (F = 12.9, p < 0.001 and F = 9.52, p < 0.001). ASV1 was associated with warmer temperatures (r = 0.67, p < 0.001), higher DO (r = 0.15, p < 0.001), and lower salinity (r=-0.48, p < 0.001). ASV3 was also associated with higher temperatures (r = 0.38, p < 0.001) and DO (r = 0.23, p < 0.001) but correlated weakly with higher salinity (r = 0.11, p < 0.05). Two prominent ASVs in seawater were Candidatus Actinomarina sp. (Actinomycetota): ASV4 (68% samples) and ASV 10 (45% samples; Fig. 4A). The abundance of each varied temporally (F = 35.7, p < 0.001 and F = 161.7, p < 0.001, respectively) and spatially (F = 16.2, p < 0.001 and F = 6.90, p < 0.001). ASV4 was associated with warmer temperatures (r = 0.56, p < 0.001), higher DO (r = 0.14, p < 0.001), and lower salinity (r=-0.42, p < 0.001). ASV10 showed the opposite patterns, being associated with lower temperatures (r=-0.38, p < 0.001), lower DO (r=-0.12; p < 0.001), and higher salinity (r = 0.50, p < 0.001). Interestingly, the representation of ASV1 and ASV4 in seawater tracked each other, and their abundances were positively correlated (r = 0.58, p < 0.001). ASV3 and ASV10 also tracked each other but were less strongly correlated (r = 0.18, p < 0.001). ASV1 and ASV4 were more dominant in warmer, lower salinity periods from March–October 2024 (months 3–10), whereas ASV3 and ASV10 were more abundant as the seawater became cooler and of higher salinity from August–December 2024 (months 8–12; Fig. 4). ASV2 (hgcI clade; Actinomycetota) was found in 54% of seawater samples and was more abundant at the western-most beaches (beaches 6 and 7) and from February–July 2024 (months 2–7; Fig. 4A). There were significant differences in the abundance of ASV2 over time (F = 67.2, p < 0.001) and between beaches (F = 61.5, p < 0.001), correlating with lower salinity (r=-0.73, p < 0.001), warmer temperatures (r = 0.32, p < 0.001), higher DO (r = 0.23. p < 0.001), and higher pH (r = 0.24, p < 0.001). The abundance of ASV2 positively correlated with ASV1 (r = 0.61, p < 0.001) and ASV4 (r = 0.49, p < 0.001), and negatively with ASV10 (r=-0.52, p < 0.001).

Even the most common ASVs in sediment were detected in < 50% of samples. These included ASV13 (Flavobacteriaceae; Bacteroidota; 43% samples), ASV27 (Woeseia sp.; Gammaproteobacteria; 33% samples), ASV 24 (Arcticiflavibacter sp.; Bacteroidota; 29% samples), ASV28 (Muriicola sp.; Bacteroidota; 19% samples), and ASV47 (Algoriphagus sp.; Bacteroidota; 16% samples). Abundances of these ASVs varied by beach (ASV13: F = 71.1, ASV27: F = 91.2, ASV24: F = 64.2, ASV28: F = 24.3, ASV47: F = 8.32, p < 0.001 for all) and spatial patterns were generally stronger than temporal patterns, although those were also significant (ASV13: F = 18.8, ASV27: F = 8.0, ASV24: F = 40.7, ASV28: F = 34.3, ASV47: F = 3.13, p < 0.001 for all; Fig. 4B). Correlations of sediment ASVs to environmental conditions were weaker than those observed in seawater. ASV24 and ASV28 were more abundant at lower temperatures (r=-0.35 and − 0.46, p < 0.001) and pH (r=-0.22 and − 0.32, p < 0.001), and ASV24 was also associated with higher salinity (r = 0.19, p < 0.001). ASV47 was associated with warmer temperatures (r = 0.20, p < 0.001), lower salinity (r=-0.33, p < 0.001), and higher pH (r = 0.18, p < 0.001). ASV 13 correlated with lower salinity (r=-0.24, p < 0.001) and pH (r=-0.13, p < 0.01), whereas ASV 27 was associated with warmer temperatures (r = 0.10, p < 0.05) and lower DO (r=-0.24, p < 0.001). Abundances of these sediment ASVs were generally positively correlated (r = 0.11–0.40, p < 0.001–0.05), other than ASV 24 being negatively correlated with ASV 47 (r=-0.19, p < 0.001), and ASV 27 being negatively correlated with ASV 28 (r=-0.16, p < 0.01).

Discussion

Seawater and sediment harbored distinct bacterial assemblages and showed fundamentally different spatial and temporal dynamics, supporting our hypotheses. Temporal influences were pronounced for seawater communities, which exhibited a seasonal pattern driven largely by changes in salinity and temperature. In contrast, coastal sediment bacterial communities were stable over longer time periods and showed greater spatial heterogeneity between the five beaches sampled. Sediment communities had weaker relationships with environmental variables and less pronounced seasonal variation, with spatial variation more strongly influencing bacterial communities.

Seawater bacterial communities showed a temporal distance–decay relationship with community dissimilarity increasing as days between samples increased, but a strong non-linear model showed these differences declined after 6–8 months. This temporal trend suggests that nearshore seawater bacterial assemblages undergo recurring turnover, likely driven by changes in temperature and salinity that promote distinct community structure across seasons. Similar annual patterns have been observed in other marine and estuarine systems2,6,16, where microbial communities exhibit predictable seasonal succession linked to environmental variability. In contrast, sediment communities displayed much weaker temporal distance–decay relationships, indicating greater temporal stability and resistance to environmental fluctuations18. The higher bacterial richness of sediment may promote this stability, whereas the lower richness and stronger species-specific turnover of dominant ASVs in seawater leads to greater responses to changing conditions, and more pronounced temporal effects on community composition.

Sediment bacterial communities were largely influenced by distance between beaches, likely because of limited transport between our sample sites, which spanned almost 40 km of coastline. Even the most common ASVs in sediment were found in less than half of our samples, likely reflecting both spatial and temporal variation. Both factors were associated with the richness of sediment communities, albeit weakly, while richness of seawater bacterial communities was only related to time. Many of the ASVs in sediment were identified as Bacteroidota, particularly Flavobacteriaceae, which have been found at high abundances in nearshore sediments12,13,19. Functional redundancy within taxonomically similar groups, combined with higher overall richness, could promote compositional stability and resistance to environmental perturbation in nearshore sediment bacterial communities18. Together, these features may buffer sediment bacterial communities against environmental fluctuations, even as individual ASVs turn over.

Seawater ASVs showed strong temporal patterns, particularly seasonal shifts in different ASVs classified as Cyanobium and Candidatus Actinomarina. Similar patterns in the temporal dynamics of a phototroph and a heterotroph could indicate a trophic relationship, and such interactions occur in marine systems (e.g., between Rhodobacteraceae and photosynthetic bacteria)4,20, although the association here may be highly species-specific and based on prevailing conditions. Another temporal pattern saw higher abundances of the freshwater-associated hgcI clade during February–July 2024, a period when our study area received frequent precipitation (typically 4–6 mm d− 1; www.weather.gov), which may have facilitated the transport of hgcI into coastal waters. These temporal dynamics in the distribution of ASVs reinforce the idea that the coastal seawater bacterial community is structured by recurring environmental variation over time.

Our findings suggest that the ecological processes regulating nearshore bacterial communities differ fundamentally between seawater and sediment: seawater bacterial communities respond to temporal variation in environmental parameters, whereas sediment bacterial communities are buffered by higher richness and show more spatial variation. These findings improve our understanding of coastal ecosystems, which can help inform monitoring and management strategies and provide a foundation for predicting how nearshore microbial communities respond to environmental change.

Methods

Sample collection and processing

Five beaches were sampled along the Mississippi, USA, coast at monthly, weekly, and daily timescales over a 15-month (423-day) period, with a total of 27 collection dates. The five beaches span 37.9 km of the northern Gulf of Mexico coastline and are monitored by the Mississippi Department of Environmental Quality (MDEQ). Beaches are (west to east) numbered according to MDEQ designations: beach 6 (Pass Christian Central; 30.311611, -89.253221), beach 7 (Long Beach; 30.339825, -89.163475), beach 10 (East Courthouse; 30.377100, -89.043380), beach 12 (Biloxi West; 30.392525, -88.938699), and beach 13 (Biloxi East; 30.392764, -88.876812). Sampling on each date proceeded from west to east beginning at beach 6 around 11:00 and ending at beach 13 around 14:00. Monthly sampling occurred once a month from January 2024 to March 2025, weekly sampling was every seven days from 6/11/2024 to 7/30/2024, and daily sampling was every day from 6/25/2024 to 7/2/2024. For analyses examining broad seasonal patterns, sampling dates were grouped into meteorological seasons, with dates from March–May classified as spring, June–August as summer, September–November as fall, and December–February as winter.

On each date, three samples each of seawater and sediment were collected from the shoreline of each beach, with replicates collected < 1 m apart. This amounted to 15 samples of seawater and 15 samples of sediment on each date (810 samples total for the study) from the surf zone. Surface seawater was collected directly into sterile 500 ml Nalgene bottles and sediment was collected by scooping moist surface sand into sterile 50 ml centrifuge tubes. Sediments collected from all beaches consisted of damp, coarse sand characteristic of open Gulf coast shorelines. Across sites and sampling dates, gravimetric sediment moisture content averaged 16.9% and ash-free dry mass averaged 1.37% (determined by drying subsamples of all sediment samples at 70 °C for 48 h followed by combustion at 500 °C for 2 h) indicating low organic matter content consistent with coarse, well-drained sands. All sediment samples were collected from the surf zone, an environment subject to frequent but gentle inundation. Water temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen (DO), and salinity were measured at each site using handheld probes. Samples were kept on ice and transported to the laboratory for processing within 18 h.

For seawater, 200 ml of each sample was filtered through a sterile 0.2 μm nylon membrane filter (MilliporeSigma, Cork, Ireland), with one half of the filter transferred into a bead tube from PowerSoil Pro DNA extraction kits (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) for processing. For sediment, 0.5 g of each sample was placed into a bead tube for DNA extraction. Samples for DNA extraction were stored at -20 °C.

DNA extraction, amplification, and sequencing

DNA was extracted from sand and filters using PowerSoil Pro DNA extraction kits and a dual-index barcoding approach used for Illumina next-generation sequencing21, following established procedures22,23. A 250 bp part of the V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using two barcoded primers, 515F (5’-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3’) and 806R, (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’)21 and amplicons standardized (SequalPrep plates, Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY) and pooled. The library was spiked with 20% PhiX and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq at the UMMC Molecular and Genomics Core Facility. Raw sequence files (fastq) were processed using the 16 S rRNA pipeline in DADA2 v1.26.0, in R v4.2.2. and followed trimming, merging, and filtering protocols we established previously23,24. Sequences were classified using SILVA23 v138.1 (released August 2020). Sequences were clustered as amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and singletons removed. Eight sediment and two seawater samples had low sequencing depth and were excluded from further analyses. This left 403 seawater samples (mean ± SE number of reads: 108,438 ± 7,487; median 36,660) and 397 sediment samples (104,005 ± 7,752 reads; median 28,123) for analysis. For diversity analyses, samples were rarefied by subsampling each to the lowest sequence count (5,019) of any remaining sample, an approach that was taken to retain as many samples as possible while still maintaining adequate sequencing depth, as indicated by rarefaction curves and Good’s coverage scores (mean ± SE 93.0 ± 0.002%). The unfiltered dataset contained 120,282 ASVs, which was reduced to 33,946 ASVs for statistical analyses after rarefaction.

Statistical analyses

Good’s coverage was calculated using phyloseq_coverage in metagMisc24 to assess sequencing depth. Permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used to test for significance in Bray-Curtis community dissimilarity among samples based on habitat (seawater/sediment), date, and beach. Homogeneity of variance between date and beach of each habitat type was assessed using permutest and betadispr functions in ‘vegan’25. Differences in bacterial community composition were visualized using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) ordinations based on dissimilarity scores, overlaying correlations between environmental parameters and dissimilarity using envfit in ‘vegan’25. To evaluate temporal distance-decay relationships, we compared pairwise dissimilarity scores to the number of days separating all possible pairs of samples within each habitat. Linear models were used to assess whether dissimilarity increased with time between samples, indicating a temporal distance-decay relationship. Potential non-linear patterns in community turnover over time (i.e., cyclical patterns) were assessed by fitting a generalized additive model (GAM) using ‘mgcv’26 with a cubic regression spline with shrinkage to prevent overfitting. The degree of non-linearity was determined from the estimated degrees of freedom (EDF) for each GAM. We calculated species richness (species observed, Sobs) and species diversity (Inverse Simpson’s index) of seawater and sediment bacterial communities using estimate_richness in ‘phyloseq’27. ANOVA were used to assess differences in species richness/diversity by habitat, date, and beach, as were GAMs and linear models to assess the effect of time on species richness/diversity, and Mantel correlations (Pearson’s r) to assess both time and geographic distance. The most frequently detected ASVs above a per-sample abundance (> 1% of sequences within a given sample) were determined using ‘microbiome’28, and their relative abundance assessed in relation to temporal, spatial, and environmental patterns.
