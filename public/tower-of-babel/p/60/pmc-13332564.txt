Introduction

Tongue fasciculations, with electrophysiologically confirmed denervation, strongly support a diagnosis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) in bulbar presentations, although mimics have been reported [1]. While “tongue tremor” or rhythmic movements have been described in progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) [2, 3], genuine fasciculations with electrophysiologically confirmed denervation are not expected. We report a patient with autopsy‐proven PSP presenting with visible tongue fasciculations, needle electromyography (EMG)‐confirmed denervation, severe bulbar dysfunction, and other features fulfilling the Awaji criteria for possible ALS [4].

Case Report

A 73‐year‐old woman presented with progressive dysarthria and dysphagia for a year. She denied orthostatic dizziness or bowel/bladder dysfunction. Neurologic examination revealed mild nonfluent aphasia and executive dysfunction (Frontal Assessment Battery score 8/18), with preserved global cognition (Mini‐Mental State Examination score of 29/30). Her cranial nerve examination demonstrated mild bilateral tongue atrophy with nonrhythmic fasciculations. There was a snout reflex. Vertical saccades were minimally slowed, whereas ocular movements were not restricted. No muscle weakness or atrophy was found in the limbs. There was generalized hyperreflexia, with positive pectoralis major reflexes and Hoffmann signs. Sensory examination was normal. There were no cerebellar signs or parkinsonism. Motor and sensory nerve conduction studies of the upper and lower limbs were normal. Needle EMG of the tongue demonstrated spontaneous fibrillation potentials and high‐amplitude motor unit potentials (> 2 mV) with decreased recruitment, confirming active denervation and chronic re‐innervation. EMG of the upper and lower limb muscles and paraspinal muscles was normal. The brain MRI showed mild left‐predominant cortical atrophy. Atrophy of the midbrain tegmentum was subtle and equivocal (Figure 1). Dopamine transporter (DAT) SPECT was normal. Based on combined UMN and LMN signs, she was diagnosed with possible bulbar‐onset ALS [4] and was treated with riluzole. No fasciculations were observed outside the tongue throughout the course. During the second year, she experienced falls and balance impairment during turning, despite relatively preserved limb strength. The clinical course was rapid and aggressive. Her forced vital capacity declined from 89% to 48% within the first year, and continued to deteriorate to 23% over the following 2 years, accompanied by hypercapnia. Limb strength remained preserved until late in the course. She died of respiratory failure at age 76.

(B) Axial FLAIR MRI shows mild left‐predominant cortical atrophy (arrowheads). (C) Dopamine transporter SPECT demonstrated visually preserved striatal uptake with a “comma‐shaped” appearance.

Neuropathologic Findings

The brain weight was 985 g. Macroscopically, diffuse cortical atrophy with frontal accentuation was observed. The midbrain tegmentum showed moderate atrophy. Histologically, Gallyas‐Braak silver staining revealed globose‐type neurofibrillary tangles, tufted astrocytes, and coiled bodies in the basal ganglia and brainstem, satisfying PSP criteria [5] (Figure 2). No histopathological findings of ALS were seen. The precentral gyrus showed preserved lamination without Betz cell loss (Figure 2C). In the spinal cord, anterior horn cells were well‐preserved. Phospho‐TDP‐43 immunohistochemistry was negative throughout, and no Bunina bodies were found. Notably, the hypoglossal nucleus was structurally preserved without motor neuron loss or tau accumulation (Figure 2D). The tongue muscle showed mild fiber caliber reduction and fatty change. Biochemical analysis revealed a 33‐kDa C‐terminal tau band (4‐repeat tau), and immunoelectron microscopy demonstrated straight and ribbon‐like filaments, reinforcing the PSP diagnosis.

Neuropathologic findings showing PSP pathology without ALS‐type motor neuron degeneration. (A) Subthalamic nucleus, Gallyas‐Braak silver stain (original magnification ×400). Numerous argyrophilic structures are present, including globose neurofibrillary tangles (yellow arrow), tufted astrocytes (blue arrowheads), coiled bodies (white arrow), and argyrophilic threads (blue arrows), consistent with PSP pathology. (B) Superior temporal gyrus, Gallyas‐Braak silver stain (×200). The cortical laminar architecture is preserved without marked neuronal loss, but there are numerous argyrophilic deposits predominantly in tufted astrocytes (blue arrowheads), indicating widespread cortical tau pathology. (C) Precentral gyrus, hematoxylin–eosin stain (×200). The cortical layers are preserved, Betz cells (black arrows) are maintained, and neuronophagia is not evident, arguing against ALS‐type motor cortex degeneration. Immunohistochemistry for phospho–TDP‐43 was also negative (not shown). (D) Hypoglossal nucleus, hematoxylin–eosin stain (×200). Motor neurons (black arrowheads) are preserved without obvious degeneration. No tau‐positive inclusions were detected on phospho‐tau (AT8) immunostaining (not shown).

Discussion

We report a patient with autopsy‐proven PSP mimicking bulbar‐onset ALS with clinically evident tongue fasciculations and EMG‐confirmed lingual denervation. The patient's presentation—progressive bulbar palsy, tongue fasciculations, generalized hyperreflexia, and rapid respiratory failure—was highly characteristic of ALS. The lack of parkinsonism and minimal oculomotor signs and equivocal midbrain atrophy on MRI further obscured the correct diagnosis. Clinical overlap between ALS and PSP has been previously reported [6, 7], though pathological confirmation was not obtained in those cases.

PSP is not restricted to supranuclear and extrapyramidal structures but can involve LMNs and peripheral nerves. Limb EMG has shown chronic neurogenic changes in nearly half of PSP patients [8], and distal axonal or small‐fiber neuropathy has been reported more frequently than in Parkinson's disease [9, 10]. Jaw and tongue “tremor” have also been described in PSP [3]. A video case report illustrated “tongue tremor” as the presenting feature of PSP, with movements superficially resembling fasciculations [2]. Tongue fasciculations were previously reported in a patient with overt PSP features, but EMG of the tongue was not performed [11]. In contrast, our patient presented with only subtle fasciculations localized to the lateral tongue. Our case complements these reports by demonstrating electrophysiologic evidence of active denervation and chronic re‐innervation of the tongue, in autopsy‐proven PSP.

Neuropathological examination is morphology‐based and may not capture functional abnormalities at the cellular or axonal level; thus, the absence of overt structural changes does not exclude physiological dysfunction. The mechanism of lingual denervation remains uncertain. The hypoglossal nucleus was structurally preserved without tau deposition, contrasting with some PSP cases [12]. Given the preservation of the hypoglossal soma, we hypothesize that subclinical involvement of the extramedullary hypoglossal nerve or its Schwann cells, analogous to the distal axonopathy previously described in PSP [9, 10], may have contributed to the lingual denervation, although peripheral nerve pathology could not be evaluated. Absent TDP‐43 pathology argues against concomitant ALS, suggesting PSP tauopathy as the etiology. Differentiation was particularly challenging, as the patient exhibited multiple features highly suggestive of ALS. Although hyperreflexia is a known feature of PSP variants with corticospinal tract degeneration, such as PSP‐primary lateral sclerosis [13], the coexistence of tongue denervation and respiratory insufficiency created a clinical picture strongly suggestive of ALS with combined upper and lower motor neuron dysfunction. A normal DAT SPECT may have contributed to the initial diagnostic impression of ALS; however, normal findings can occur in atypical PSP variants [14]. Paradoxically, the tongue denervation that suggested PSP‐related neurodegeneration simultaneously precluded a clinical diagnosis of PSP under the MDS criteria [14].

Limitations include the single‐case design, absence of follow‐up EMG, unavailability of genetic testing and neurofilament biomarkers, and lack of ultrasound documentation. Nonetheless, this case highlights that PSP should be recognized as a potential ALS mimicker when tongue fasciculations occur. Even subtle or equivocal midbrain atrophy, when accompanied by minimal vertical saccade slowing or unexplained falls, should prompt consideration of PSP.
