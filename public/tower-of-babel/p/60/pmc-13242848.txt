Exercise Attitudes Mediate the Impact of Mental Health on Physical Activity Practices Among Chinese College Students: A Cross-Sectional Study in Chongqing
Authors: Wenhao Wang, Hui Yang, Xinhua Chen, Wenjun Ma, Yun Li, Kaiqi Zhang, Jiabao Yang, Qing Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study examined how exercise attitudes mediate the link between mental health and physical activity in Chongqing college students, using structural equation modeling to identify key factors.Patients and MethodsA cross-sectional survey recruited college students at Chongqing Medical University between October 29 and November 20, 2024. Demographic data and Mental Health, Attitude and Practice scores were collected using self-Administrated questionnaires.ResultsA total of 2113 valid responses were analyzed. Among the respondents, 1,288 (60.96%) were first-year students, and 1,337 (63.27%) reported having a regular exercise habit. The mean scores for mental health, attitude as well as practice were 1.54 ± 0.57 (possible range: 1–4.85; representing the mean score per item across 80 items, where scores <2 indicate no mental health issues), 21.43 ± 3.36 (possible range: 6–30) and 33.09 ± 6.16 (possible range: 9–45), respectively. Spearman Correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between attitude and practice (r=0.7195, P<0.001). Mediation analysis showed that the direct effect of mental health on both attitude (β = −0.38, P<0.001) and practice (β=−0.06, P=0.001), as well as of attitude on practice (β=0.88, P<0.001), furthermore, mental health indirectly affected practice through attitude (β=−0.34, P<0.001).ConclusionThe findings suggest that exercise attitudes mediate the mental health-physical activity link. Integrated campus strategies combining psychological support and attitude interventions are recommended.

Introduction

Physical activity is essential for both physical and psychological well-being. However, a significant number of Chinese college students face challenges in maintaining adequate levels of physical activity, with national data indicating that fewer than 30% meet the World Health Organization’s recommended standards for physical activity.1 This developmental stage, characterized by increased autonomy, coincides with elevated academic stress associated with China’s education system, complicating the adoption of healthy lifestyle behaviors.2

The COVID-19 pandemic further disrupted students’ physical routines. Preliminary data indicate that 68% of students utilized digital fitness platforms during lockdowns.3 Although there has been a 40% rebound in individual outdoor exercise since that time,4 psychological distress continues to affect over half of students in post-lockdown assessments.5 Socioeconomic disparities also play a role in these trends, with a 22% variance in physical activity recovery observed among different demographic groups.6 These complexities underscore the necessity for nuanced models that consider both behavioral and psychological factors in the post-pandemic context.7,8

College students, in particular, have exhibited evolving exercise preferences and heightened awareness of mental health issues.9–12 The Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) model remains pertinent within China’s collectivist culture, where group-based activities are prevalent on campuses.13 However, the attitudes of Chinese students toward exercise are more profoundly influenced by academic schedules compared to their Western counterparts.8 Additionally, the KAP framework may not fully encapsulate behavioral outcomes without accounting for mediating psychological factors. Despite the widespread application of the KAP model, there is a pressing need for more integrative approaches to explore how mental health status influences attitudes, which subsequently shape activity practices. To address this, our study extends the traditional KAP framework by integrating it into a Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) approach, facilitating the examination of both direct and mediated effects of mental health on physical activity behaviors through exercise-related attitudes. This KAP-SEM model acknowledges mental well-being not merely as a background variable but as a significant determinant that influences motivational and behavioral pathways.

Our approach is informed by three key gaps. First, there is a lack of culturally validated instruments to concurrently measure multidimensional mental health and physical activity behaviors—most existing studies rely on Western tools that lack cultural sensitivity.10 Second, changes in post-pandemic education policies, such as fitness-based academic assessments implemented in over 89% of universities, remain largely unexplored in behavioral research.9 Third, there is a critical need to incorporate Self-Determination Theory (SDT) into behavioral frameworks to reflect China’s motivational climate, where collective achievement and external expectations significantly impact exercise behaviors.14

Our sample is drawn from Chongqing Medical University, a representative institution in southwestern China with rigorous academic demands, thus providing valuable insights into the mental and behavioral health of medical students. Through this multidimensional and culturally grounded framework, we aim to inform interventions that operate at both individual and institutional levels within China’s higher education context. For full details on the study instruments, including the CAMHS and KAP questionnaire, and the sampling technique, see Materials and Methods.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional study recruited college students from Chongqing Medical University between October 29 and November 20, 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) participants aged 15 years or older; and (2) individuals actively enrolled in degree programs during the study period. The study obtained ethical approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University, with ethics number No. 2022(008). Enhanced protections were instituted for participants under 18 years of age, which included the necessity for parental or guardian consent.

Questionnaire and Quality Control

The questionnaire (electronic questionnaire) utilized in this study was meticulously developed in accordance with the “Physical activity guidelines for Chinese children and adolescents”,15 “COVID-19 pandemic-induced physical inactivity: the necessity of updating the Global Action Plan on Physical Activity”16 and other pertinent literatures.17–19 The initial draft of the questionnaire was reviewed and revised by 4 professors from Chongqing Medical University to ensure content validity. Subsequently, a pilot study was conducted, during which 50 participants completed the questionnaire. Of these, 48 responses were considered valid, resulting in a validity rate of 96%. The overall Cronbach’s α for the pilot study of the scale, excluding the Chinese Adult Mental Health Scale, was determined to be 0.925. Notably, the Cronbach’s α for the attitude dimension was 0.864, whereas the practice dimension exhibited a Cronbach’s α of 0.895. The final iteration of the questionnaire comprised four sections totaling 119 items, systematically organized as follows: 1) Demographics and Physical Activity Context (24 items). This section collected data regarding participants’ demographic characteristics (eg., age, gender, grade) and the conditions under which they engaged in physical activity (eg., frequency, duration, types). 2) Mental Health Assessment (80 items). Adapted from the Chinese Adult Mental Health Scale (CAMHS), this section assessed ten dimensions of mental health, each represented by an 8-item subscale rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = none, 5 = severe). Subscale scores were computed as the sum of the item scores divided by 8, while the overall mental health score was derived by dividing the total sum of all 80 items by 80. The criteria for interpretation were as follows: a. Subscale or Total Score < 2: No mental health issues. b. 2–2.99: Mild issues. c. 3–3.99: Moderate issues. d. 4–5: Severe issues. Subscales: 1. Interpersonal Sensitivity (Items 10, 14, 23, 31, 49, 53, 71, 79); 2. Low Stress Tolerance (Items 2, 17, 26, 40, 50, 62, 74, 77); 3. Poor Adaptability (Items 6, 18, 35, 38, 48, 61, 69, 80); 4. Psychological Imbalance (Items 8, 15, 24, 39, 42, 54, 63, 72); 5. Emotional Dysregulation (Items 1, 13, 22, 37, 45, 52, 57, 65); 6. Anxiety (Items 4, 20, 28, 34, 47, 58, 67, 70); 7. Depression (Items 7, 12, 21, 27, 33, 55, 59, 66); 8. Hostility (Items 3, 11, 30, 41, 44, 51, 76, 78); 9. Paranoia (Items 9, 16, 25, 32, 43, 56, 64, 73); 10. Somatization (Items 5, 19, 29, 36, 46, 60, 68, 75); 3) Attitudes Toward Physical Activity (6 items). This section evaluated perceptions regarding the benefits of physical activity (eg., academic performance, mental health, social interaction) and barriers (eg., academic pressure) utilizing a 5-point Likert agreement scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Items were reverse-scored where applicable (eg., barriers), with a total score range of 6–30. 4) Behavioral Practices and Policy Perceptions (9 items). This section assessed: a. Behavioral Intentions (3 items): Willingness to participate in school-organized activities, establish regular habits, and engage with peers; b. School Strategy Perceptions (1 multi-part item): Support for interventions (eg., diverse course offerings, facility improvements, fitness-based evaluations); c. Health Engagement and Policy Attitudes (2 items): Willingness to acquire health-related knowledge and opinions regarding mandatory physical activity policies. College students’ attitude, and practice scores as well as the total KAP score higher than 70% were considered as with positive attitude, and proactive practice, and good KAP, respectively.20

Data were collected through the Sojump, a web-based survey platform, and the electronic questionnaires were distributed to the students through the social media such as WeChat, which is widely used among college students in China. The college students filled out the questionnaires independently, and they were not compensated for their participation in the study. The completed questionnaires were collected by 3 research assistants by downloading the responses from the survey platform. The team ensured that the data collected were complete and accurate before proceeding with the data analysis. The questionnaires with entirely duplicated answers were excluded.

Sample Size Estimation

The sample size was estimated prior to data collection to ensure sufficient statistical power for reliable analysis. Given that the questionnaire contained 119 substantive items across the mental health, attitude, and practice dimensions, the commonly used guideline of 5 to 10 participants per item was applied.21 This approach suggested a total required sample size of 595 to 1190 participants to achieve stable estimates and support robust statistical analysis. To account for potential non-responses and incomplete submissions, an additional 20% buffer was incorporated into the initial target, leading to a planned recruitment of approximately 1488 participants.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Stata 17.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA) and Analysis of Moment Structures (AMOS) version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality and described as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on the distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). Continuous variables conformed to a normal distribution were compared using independent sample t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA), and those with skewed distribution were compared by the Wilcoxon-Mann–Whitney test or Kruskal–Wallis analysis of variance. Correlation analyses employed Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients based on data distribution. Correlation between MH-AP dimensions was interpreted as follows: weak correlation = 0.10 to 0.39, moderate correlation = 0.40 to 0.69, strong correlation = 0.70 to 0.89, and very strong correlation = 0.90 to 1.00.22 Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were conducted to explore associations between demographic variables and psychological health, positive attitudes, and proactive practices. A structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis was performed to observe the correlations among MH-AP. The hypotheses for the SEM were (1) MH directly influences attitude, (2) attitude directly influences practice, and (3) MH directly and indirectly influences practice. All statistical analysis were performed using a two-sided test. Two-sided P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

A total of 2,282 responses were initially gathered. After excluding 169 individuals who opted out of participation, 2,113 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis. Ultimately, 2,113 valid responses were examined. Among these participants, 1,288 (60.96%) were first-year students, and 1,460 (69.1%) identified as female, with a mean age of 18.99 ± 1.53 years. Furthermore, 1,814 (85.85%) reported being nearsighted, while 1,337 (63.27%) engaged in regular exercise, and 1,209 (57.22%) assessed their health as good. Additionally, 1,527 (72.27%) perceived their physical ability as average when compared to their peers, and 953 (45.1%) occasionally exercised for durations exceeding 30 minutes during their leisure time. In relation to factors influencing their physical activity, hobbies and interests emerged as the most frequently cited reasons (54.3%), followed by the time allocated to homework (27.34%). Regarding aerobic activities, walking was the most commonly reported (57.65%), succeeded by jogging (31.78%). The mean scores for attitude and practice were 21.43 ± 3.36 (possible range: 6–30) and 33.09 ± 6.16 (possible range: 9–45), respectively. Discussion

Despite high awareness of the benefits of exercise, actual participation remained suboptimal, indicating the need for interventions that address both psychological barriers and motivational deficits.

The SEM analysis confirmed attitude as a significant mediator between mental health and physical activity engagement. Although mental health had a small direct effect on behavior, its indirect impact through attitude was more substantial, highlighting the importance of attitude-shaping strategies. However, some previous studies have reported a direct positive effect of knowledge on physical activity, differing from our findings, which suggest that knowledge alone might not be enough to drive behavior without an accompanying shift in attitude.23 This discrepancy could be attributed to contextual differences, including variations in educational systems, health promotion strategies, and social norms related to exercise.24 Cultural factors shaped behavior patterns. Academic pressure and group-based participation played a more prominent role than individual motivation, reflecting the influence of collectivist values in Chinese campuses. The cultural emphasis on academic performance in China may partially explain why knowledge alone did not significantly predict physical activity levels. Unlike in Western settings, where structured physical education is integrated into academic programs, Chinese students often deprioritize exercise in favor of academic tasks, particularly in high-pressure environments. Recent university-level physical education reform policies in China, aimed at promoting student health and reducing academic burdens, have implications for student lifestyle behaviors, including physical activity engagement.25 However, research suggests that while reduced homework loads theoretically provide students with more time for exercise, behavioral changes remain dependent on institutional support and personal motivation. The substantial proportion of students citing academic pressure as a barrier reflects the unique challenges within the Chinese educational system, where intense academic competition may limit time for physical activities.

Gender differences in both cognition and practice were evident, with male students reporting higher scores in both domains. This pattern is consistent with broader research showing that men generally have higher self-efficacy regarding physical activity and are more likely to engage in structured exercise routines than women.26,27 However, our multivariate analysis did not find a statistically significant relationship, suggesting that gender disparities in exercise participation may be influenced by other factors such as social support, accessibility to facilities, and prior exposure to sports during early education.28,29

Anxiety, depression, and somatization were negatively associated with physical activity, emphasizing the need to tailor interventions based on specific psychological profiles. Unlike general mental health tools used in prior studies,30 the CAMHS enabled a multidimensional analysis. Notably, somatization showed the strongest negative association with physical activity participation, while hostility, counterintuitively, was positively associated with high-intensity activity, potentially reflecting stress-regulating behavior patterns. Students in the high-anxiety tertile exhibited 2.3-fold lower adherence to physical activity recommendations compared to their low-anxiety counterparts, underscoring the need for tailored interventions. This supports existing research linking mental health to physical activity, where individuals with higher stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms often exhibit lower levels of engagement in exercise due to motivational deficits, fatigue, and reduced perceived benefits of physical activity.31 Our structural equation model revealed that mental health had both a direct negative effect on physical activity practice and an indirect negative effect mediated through attitudes. This negative relationship aligns with the psychological barriers model proposed by Stults-Kolehmainen and Sinha,32 which suggests that psychological distress can inhibit physical activity through multiple pathways. First, individuals experiencing mental health challenges may experience fatigue, reduced motivation, and cognitive resources that directly impair exercise initiation and maintenance.33 Second, as demonstrated by our mediation findings, psychological distress may negatively influence attitudes toward physical activity by intensifying perceived barriers and diminishing perceived benefits.34 The stronger indirect effect compared to the direct effect suggests that psychological states primarily influence behavior by shaping perceptions and attitudes, rather than through direct behavioral inhibition. This finding extends previous work by Vancampfort et al,35 who documented similar mediation patterns in Western clinical populations, to a non-clinical Chinese student sample. The cultural context may explain the relatively smaller direct effect in our population, as collectivist social structures may provide scaffolding for behavior maintenance even when individual psychological resources are compromised. While some studies suggest that regular physical activity can improve mental health outcomes, our findings indicate that psychological barriers themselves may act as deterrents to participation, reinforcing the need for integrated interventions that address both mental health support and physical activity promotion simultaneously. While SDT has been widely applied in Western contexts, its relevance in Chinese educational settings remains underexplored. Recent studies indicate that Chinese students’ autonomy in exercise choices is often constrained by academic expectations and parental influence, potentially moderating the impact of self-determination factors on physical activity engagement. Research in the Chinese context suggests that perceived competence is strongly linked to structured extracurricular programs rather than individual motivation, highlighting the necessity for culturally tailored interventions.36 This aligns with SDT’s emphasis on competence as a key motivator for sustained behavior. Similarly, the finding that students who disliked PE classes were less likely to exercise independently suggests that early experiences affecting perceived competence and autonomy may have lasting impacts on physical activity engagement.37 The role of social support and peer influence in our results also reflects SDT’s relatedness component, particularly evident in the preference for group-based activities among Chinese students.

Despite SDT’s emphasis on autonomy, only 15% of students reported planning exercise independently, while 73% relied on institutional schedules, indicating constrained autonomy. Mediation analysis further revealed that collectivism orientation amplified the effect of relatedness while reducing autonomy’s impact, underscoring the cultural moderation of SDT constructs in China. These findings contrast with Western studies, where autonomy typically emerges as a stronger predictor of exercise adherence. For instance, Teixeira et al’s systematic review38 of 66 predominantly Western studies found autonomous motivation explained 36% variance in exercise behaviors, while Liu et al39 reported that among Chinese university students, social factors explained 45% of variance compared to 28% for autonomous motivation. Similarly, Standage et al40 observed that Western students prioritized individual achievement in physical activity settings. Self-perceived physical ability emerged as another significant predictor of exercise engagement. Students who perceived themselves as physically weaker compared to their peers were significantly less likely to participate in physical activity, reinforcing findings from prior research indicating that confidence in physical ability is a major determinant of exercise behavior.41 This suggests that fostering a sense of competence in students who feel physically inadequate may be crucial in increasing participation rates. Furthermore, students without prior exercise habits exhibited significantly lower levels of engagement, highlighting the importance of early exposure to structured physical activity. Previous studies have shown that individuals who establish exercise routines early in life are more likely to maintain them into adulthood, which underscores the necessity of promoting consistent exercise habits from an early age to prevent sedentary lifestyles in later years.42,43

Policy perceptions also played a role in shaping activity behaviors. Specifically, 89% of participants supported fitness-based academic evaluations, and perceived policy support moderated the relationship between facility availability and exercise engagement. These results suggest that institutional endorsement can enhance the impact of environmental enablers. Universities should integrate flexible, inclusive, and motivating programs into campus systems, with an emphasis on enjoyment and peer-led participation.

Besides, universities should integrate physical activity as a fundamental component of student well-being. Policies that include dedicated exercise breaks, flexible physical activity options, and improved recreational infrastructure on campuses could help normalize daily exercise as part of student life. Additionally, ensuring that physical activity resources are accessible to students from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds is essential, as financial constraints can limit participation in structured exercise programs. Governments and institutions should consider subsidizing gym memberships, providing free access to sports facilities, and incorporating exercise programs into student health services to reduce barriers to participation.10

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. While our cross-sectional design provided valuable insights, future longitudinal studies are needed to track how physical activity patterns evolve throughout the academic year and across different years of study. The self-reported nature of our data may be influenced by recall bias and social desirability; future studies could incorporate objective measures such as wearable activity trackers. While our sample from Chongqing provides important insights into a specific regional context, multi-center studies across different Chinese regions would help validate the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, comparative studies with international student populations could help isolate cultural factors from universal student experiences. While SEM provided valuable insights into key pathways, qualitative follow-up interviews would help clarify why 22% of students with high knowledge scores still held negative exercise attitudes. Furthermore, future assessment tools developed based on this research should be validated in geographically diverse university contexts to account for regional variations in physical activity opportunities and barriers. Importantly, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; the mediation pathways identified reflect associations consistent with the proposed model but cannot establish temporal ordering or directionality. Additionally, the high standardized path coefficient (β = 0.88) between attitude and practice warrants cautious interpretation, as it may reflect common method bias inherent in self-report studies; future studies incorporating objective behavioral measures would help validate this association.

Conclusion

Chinese college students in Chongqing demonstrated predominantly positive attitudes and perceptions regarding physical activity, with a substantial proportion engaging in regular exercise. Nevertheless, their exercise habits were significantly influenced by psychological and behavioral factors, including mental health, perceived physical capability, and prior experiences with physical education. Furthermore, attitude functioned as a critical mediator in the relationship between knowledge and engagement in physical activity. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions that not only enhance students’ knowledge and attitudes but also address psychological barriers and motivational factors to promote sustained participation in physical activity within this demographic. In conclusion, this study provides evidence that culturally informed, psychologically integrated approaches, combining mental health support with attitude-focused interventions, are essential for promoting sustained physical activity among Chinese college students, and these findings offer a replicable framework for campus health promotion in similar educational contexts.
