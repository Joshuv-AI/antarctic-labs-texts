Association of IgG N-Glycans With Adverse Outcomes in CKD
Authors: Elena Butz, Miriam IJ Bach, Fruzsina Kotsis, Inga Steinbrenner, Tamara Štambuk, Ivan Gudelj, Barbara Radovani Trbojević, Tea Pribić, Anika Mijakovac, Jennifer Nadal, Kai-Uwe Eckardt, Gordan Lauc, Anna Köttgen, Peggy Sekula, Ulla T Schultheiss, GCKD Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIgG N-glycosylation modulates inflammation and may influence chronic kidney disease (CKD). We investigated associations between IgG N-glycan patterns, kidney function, mortality, and kidney failure (KF) within the German CKD (GCKD) study.MethodsIgG N-glycan peaks were measured using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography in 4782 GCKD participants (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]: 30–60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 or overt albuminuria). We assessed associations with baseline kidney function, 6.5-year mortality, and KF using linear and Cox regression. We used bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) to explore causal relationships.ResultsThe cohort comprised 4782 participants (mean age: 60 years, 40% women) with a median eGFR of 46 ml/min per 1.73 m2, and autoimmune kidney disease in 22%. Higher agalactosylated IgG was associated with lower eGFR (−4%/SD; 97.9% confidence interval [CI]: −6% to −3%) and higher mortality (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.39; 97.9% CI: 1.19–1.62). Conversely, higher digalactosylated and sialylated IgG were associated with higher eGFR (+5%) and lower mortality (HR: 0.68 and 0.80, respectively). Associations with urinary albumin-to-creatine ratio [UACR] and KF varied by age, sex, and autoimmune status. Core fucosylation and bisecting N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) showed weaker associations, primarily with UACR. MR suggested bidirectional causal relationships between eGFR and specific N-glycan traits.ConclusionIgG N-glycan patterns reflect inflammatory aging and associate with kidney function, mortality, and KF in CKD. These findings identify IgG N-glycans as potential immunologic biomarkers for CKD. Further validation is required to confirm causal relevance.Keywords: CKD progression, IgG N-glycosylation, kidney function, mendelian randomization, mortality

CKD affects 10% to 13% of the global population, increasing cardiovascular risk, mortality, and health care burden.1,2 Despite its prevalence, no cure exists, and the mechanisms that cause and accelerate CKD remain incompletely understood. Mechanisms beyond genetic variation, such as epigenetic and posttranslational regulation, likely play a major role in CKD pathogenesis,3, 4, 5 because identified genetic loci explain only a fraction of CKD risk.6

Here, we investigate a specific posttranslational modification that may potentially accelerate CKD progression: N-glycosylation of IgG. During N-glycosylation, sugar chains (glycans) attach to the nitrogen atom of asparagine residues in proteins. In IgG, N-glycans mainly attach to a conserved site in the Fc domain. These sugars shape how strongly IgG binds activating Fcγ receptors,7, 8, 9 thereby regulating inflammatory processes. Inflammation exarcebates CKD in many etiologies through kidney fibrosis and nephron damage. Therefore, altered IgG N-glycosylation could contribute to disease onset or accelerate progression.10,11 So far, distinct IgG N-glycan patterns have been linked to the onset of diseases such as type 2 diabetes,12 cardiovascular disease,12,13 and hypertension.14 In addition, they have been associated with eGFR in a population-based cohort5 and with eGFR decline10,15 as well as prevalent and incident nepropathy16 in patient cohorts of diabetes mellitus.

To our knowledge, no study has explored the role of IgG N-glycosylation in kidney health among patients with established CKD of diverse etiologies. Furthermore, although both CKD progression and IgG N-glycosylation8 vary by sex,17, 18, 19 age,20 and autoimmune status,21 the extent to which these patterns are related remains uncertain. We aimed to extend previous diabetes-centered findings by investigating the relationship between IgG N-glycan traits and kidney health in the GCKD study, assessing cross-sectional associations with eGFR and UACR and prospective associations with incident KF and all-cause mortality. In addition, eGFR slopes were considered as an intermediate outcome estimated from follow-up data. Finally, we assessed the directionality of observed N-glycosylation–eGFR associations using MR.22

Methods

Study Population

The GCKD study is an ongoing prospective CKD cohort study that recruited 5217 participants between 2010 and 2012 across 9 regional centers and 169 nephrology practices in Germany (DRKS00003971). Key inclusion criteria were an eGFR of 30 to 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 or > 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 with excess albuminuria.23 Ethics approval was obtained from all centers, and written informed consent was provided by all participants.23,24

Baseline Variables and Measurements

Trained personnel systematically obtained demographic and clinical data, and collected biosamples according to predefined procedures. Autoimmune kidney disease (not including participants with self-reported rheumatoid arthritis) was defined from the leading kidney disease diagnosis recorded by the treating nephrologist at baseline (predefined list or free-text entry). The underlying CKD etiology was assigned by the treating nephrologist as a single primary cause based on all available clinical information, including medical history, laboratory findings, imaging, and kidney biopsy when available (∼26%).24 If multiple potential causes were present without a clearly dominant etiology, cases were categorized as “uncertain” (or equivalent category). Etiologies were subsequently grouped into broader categories (e.g., glomerular, diabetic, hypertensive/vascular, interstitial, systemic, and inherited causes) to enable analysis. Immune-mediated and systemic autoimmune glomerular diseases were classified as autoimmune kidney disease. Rheumatoid arthritis was assessed separately by self-report at baseline and was not included in this definition (details: Supplementary Method S1). All other project-related baseline variables are listed in Supplementary Method S1 as well. From samples shipped frozen to a central biobank, a standardized set of biomarkers was measured in a central certified laboratory using standardized protocols. From the obtained measures, eGFR using the creatinine-based CKD Epidemiology Collaboration estimation equation25 and UACR (mg/g) were calculated. Skewness in distribution led to log-transformation for eGFR, UACR, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, C-reactive protein.

N-glycomic analysis of available stored baseline plasma samples of GCKD study participants was performed at the GENOS Glycoscience Research Laboratory, Croatia. IgG N-glycans were measured using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography. Isolation, measurement, and labelling are described in detail in Supplementary Method S1.

A total of 24 N-glycan peaks, where each glycan peak represents a chromatographically separated group of structurally similiar N-linked sugar chains (glycans) attached to IgG antibodies, were measured per chromatogram. The amount of glycans in each peak was expressed as the percentage of the total integrated area across the chromatogram26 (Supplementary Table S1). In addition, 6 traits were derived from 24 N-glycan peaks using formulae provided by GENOS Glycoscience Research Laboratory (derived traits represent biologically meaningful summary measures of IgG glycosylation, computed as combinations of specific glycan peaks reflecting shared structural features; Supplementary Table S2).

Derived IgG N-glycosylation traits capture the proportions of glycans with specific structural features, providing biologically interpretable summaries of complex N-glycan profiles. For example, the glycan structures shown in Figure 1b to d would all contribute to the fucosylation trait (because they contain a core fucose residue), whereas the structure in Figure 1c would contribute to the sialylation trait (because it carries galactose modified by sialic acids). Derived traits were defined such that each measured N-glycan peak was assigned to either 1 or more N-glycosylation traits (Supplementary Figure S1). Each derived trait was then calculated as a sum of corresponding glycan peaks (Supplementary Table S2). The traits of a-, mono- and digalactosylation and sialylation were mutually exclusive. N-glycan measurements were quality-controlled and normalized (Supplementary Method S1).

Prospective Endpoints and eGFR Slope Calculation

In the GCKD study, participant-specific events are continuously adjudicated by trained physicians using hospital discharge letters, nephrologist outpatient records, and death certificates, following a standardized event adjudication catalogue.27 For this analysis, adjudicated events from the first 6.5 years of follow-up were available (data freeze on March 21, 2022). The endpoints of interest included mortality, defined as death from any cause; and incident KF, defined as the initiation of kidney replacement therapy (any form of dialysis and/or kidney transplantation) or death from foregoing dialysis. Estimation of eGFR slopes was carried out using linear mixed-effects models that incorporated a mean number of 9.6 creatinine measurements in 5214 GCKD study participants.28

Statistical Analyses

All the analyses were conducted on GCKD participants with no missing values in any of the requested variables (complete case cohort, N = 4782, 92%; Supplementary Figure S2). All analyses were performed in R29 and RStudio,30 using the survival package31 for prospective analyses, and the ggplot232 package for data visualization, or Stata/SE 15.1 (Copyright 1985-2017 StataCorp LLC).

Each regression analysis included 1 N-glycan peak or derived trait as the primary explanatory variable. A series of hierarchically adjusted models were fitted as follows: (i) an unadjusted model; (ii) a minimally adjusted model, including age and sex; and (iii) a fully adjusted model incorporating additional covariates selected based on an extensive literature review and biological plausibility (Supplementary Figure S2). The fully adjusted models included age, sex, log(eGFR) and/or log(UACR), log(body mass index), smoking, autoimmune kidney disease status, log(C-reactive protein), prevalent diabetes mellitus, prevalent cardiovascular disease, systolic blood pressure, blood pressure–lowering medication, and log(lipid levels) (Supplementary Method S1). If not stated otherwise, reported regression results from both main and stratified analyses referred to the extended model throughout the main manuscript.These models were designed to ensure that all reported associations were independent of known confounders, such as the physiological shifts in IgG N-glycosylation that occur with chronological aging.7

We evaluated the associations of N-glycan peaks and derived traits with log(eGFR), log(UACR), and eGFR slope using multivariable linear regression (Supplementary Method S2). Prospective associations with KF and mortality were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models, with competing-risk methods applied for KF (Supplementary Method S2). Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals (showing absence of major deviations; data not shown).

Throughout all regression analyses, a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold was employed to account for testing multiple N-glycan peaks, with statisticial significance defined as P < 0.05/24 = 0.0021 and corresponding 97.9% CI. To assess effect heterogeneity, we performed stratified analyses by age (< or ≥ the median of 63 years), sex, and autoimmune kidney disease. Additional stratified and sensitivity analyses are described in Supplementary Method S2.

To assess if N-glycan traits causally affect eGFR or vice versa, we conducted bidirectional 2-sample MR. We used eGFR summary statistics from Stanzick et al.33 and IgG N-glycosylation data from Frkatović-Hodžić et al.,34,35 including data shared directly by the authors. Using inverse-variance–weighted MR and MR-Egger methods, we evaluated causal directions in both scenarios as follows: N-glycan traits as the exposure (driving changes in eGFR) and eGFR as the exposure (driving changes in N-glycan traits). For the eGFR-to-glycan direction, we applied weighted median, weighted mean, and mode-based estimators to assess the robustness of our findings against violations of MR assumptions (Supplementary Method S3, Supplementary References).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

In the complete case cohort comprising 4782 participants with a high similarity to the overall cohort (N = 5217; Supplementary Table S3), the average age was 60 years (SD = 12), and 39.9% were women (Table 1). Consistent with the study eligibility requirements, the median UACR was 50.2 mg/g (interquartile range: 9.4-374), and the median eGFR was 46.2 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (interquartile range: 37.0–57.3). Autoimmune kidney diseases were present in 21.5% of participants, and younger participants as well as those with autoimmune kidney diseases had higher rates of proteinuria. The cohort presented with a diverse burden of comorbidities; hypertension was highly prevalent, affecting 96.2% of the population. Among the participants, 26.6% were treated with thiazide diuretics; 38.1% with loop diuretics; and 72.9% received renin-angiotensin system blockers, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers. Diabetes and cardiovascular disease were common, with prevalences of 35.3% and 30.3%, respectively. Participants had a median body mass index of 28.8 kg/m2, indicating a high burden of overweight. A total of 16.1% of participants reported being current smokers.

Because the cohort was analyzed by subgroups based on age (< or ≥ the median of 63 years), sex, and autoimmune kidney disease status, we examined the overlap between these characteristics. Glycosylation Profiles

Generally, IgG N-glycans can be categorized by the number of terminal galactose residues; agalactosylated glycans lack galactose (e.g., structures presented in Figure 1a and d), monogalactosylated glycans contain 1 galactose, and digalactosylated glycans contain 2 galactose residues (Figure 1b). They can be defined by additional structural features: sialylated glycans have a terminal sialic acid on galactose (Figure 1c); core fucosylated glycans carry a core α1,6-linked fucose on the innermost GlcNAc (Figure 1b–d), and glycans with a bisecting GlcNAc bear an extra GlcNAc on the core mannose (Figure 1d). In the GCKD study, an individual’s IgG N-glycome was on average composed of 37% agalactosylated, 34% monogalactosylated, 16% sialylated, and 12% digalactosylated IgG (Supplementary Table S4 and Figure S4). Core fucose was present on 96%, and 19% of N-glycans featured a bisecting GlcNAc structure. Whereas the definitions of derived traits are reflected in the pairwise correlations of the derived traits, glycan peaks show variable correlation patterns (range: −1 to 1; Supplementary Figure S5). In general, associations of individual N-glycan peaks were consistent with those observed for the derived traits to which they contributed (Supplementary Figure S6). Only for the derived traits of core fucosylation and bisecting GlcNAc did the contributing N-glycan peaks show associations in opposing directions for any outcome.

The outcomes were modeled as percent changes per SD (eGFR, UACR) or absolute differences per SD (eGFR slope), and time-to-event outcomes as HRs (overall mortality) and cause-specific HRs (KF). Bonferroni-adjusted 97.9% CIs accounted for 24 N-glycan peaks. Fully adjusted estimates were reported (a comprehensive overview is presented in Supplementary Figure S7), with results for unadjusted and minimally adjusted models available in Supplementary Tables S5, S7, S9, S11, and S13.

Cross-Sectional Associations With eGFR

For each SD in agalactosylated IgG, eGFR at baseline was approximately 4% lower (97.9% CI: −6% to −3%, Figure 2, Table 2, Supplementary Table S5). In contrast, higher levels of digalactosylated and sialylated IgG were associated with higher eGFR. Each SD in digalactosylated and sialylated IgG corresponded to higher eGFR (+5%/SD; 97.9% CI: 3%–7%) and (+5%/SD; 97.9% CI: 3%–6%, respectively). These associations were consistent across subgroups defined by age, sex, and autoimmune kidney disease status (Supplementary Table S6).

Cross-Sectional Associations With UACR

Each SD in monogalactosylated and digalactosylated IgG was associated with lower UACR (−18%/SD; 97.9% CI: −25% to −10%) and (−10%/SD; 97.9% CI: −19% to 0%), respectively; whereas IgG with bisecting GlcNAc were associated with higher UACR (+13%/SD; 97.9% CI: 3%–24%; Supplementary Table S7). Associations differed markedly by age and autoimmune kidney disease status. An inverse association of IgG galactosylation with UACR was evident in older participants and those without autoimmune kidney disease but was attenuated or reversed in younger participants and those with autoimmune kidney disease (Supplementary Table S8). Sialylated IgG, which showed no association overall, was associated with higher UACR in participants with autoimmune kidney disease (+40%/SD; 97.9% CI: 16%–70%) and in younger participants (+30%/SD; 97.9% CI: 13%–48%), but not in their counterparts.

eGFR Slope Associations

No IgG N-glycan traits were associated with eGFR slope overall (Supplementary Table S9). However, in women, agalactosylated and monogalactosylated IgG were nominally linked to higher (+0.12 ml/min per 1.73 m2/yr) and lower (−0.11 ml/min per 1.73 m2/yr) eGFR slopes, respectively; however, these associations were absent in men (Supplementary Table S10).

Prospective Association With Overall Mortality

Results observed for overall mortality largely resembled those for eGFR both in direction and in their consistency across subgroups. Higher levels of agalactosylated IgG were associated with higher mortality (+39%/SD; HR: 1.39; 97.9% CI: 1.19–1.62), whereas higher levels of digalactosylated and sialylated IgG were associated with lower mortality (−32%/SD; HR: 0.68; 97.9% CI: 0.56–0.82) and (−20%/SD; HR: 0.80; 97.9% CI: 0.68–0.94), respectively (Supplementary Table S11). In addition, higher levels of monogalactosylated IgG were associated with lower mortality (−21%/SD; HR: 0.79; 97.9% CI: 0.70–0.89). Associations were consistent across subgroups, except for monogalactosylation, where the effect direction differed by autoimmune kidney disease status (Supplementary Table S12).

Prospective Association With KF

None of the IgG N-glycan traits were associated with a hazard for KF in the overall cohort (Supplementary Table S13). However, several subgroup-specific patterns emerged, often in opposing directions. Digalactosylated IgG were associated with a higher risk of KF in younger participants (+26%/SD; HR: 1.26; 97.9% CI: 1.02–1.57), which was not observed in older participants (Supplementary Table S14). Similar nominal associations were observed in women, but not in men; and in participants with autoimmune kidney disease, but not in those without. Sialylated IgG showed a similar pattern. Conversely, agalactosylated IgG was nominally associated with a lower hazard of KF in women, in younger participants, and in those with autoimmune kidney disease, but not in their respective counterpart groups.

MR

We performed bidirectional MR to investigate potential causal relationships between eGFR and 7 IgG N-glycan traits. We only used variants available in both datasets (17–62 instruments for N-glycan traits, approximately 960 for eGFR; Supplementary Method S2, Supplementary Tables S15 and S16).

Glycan to eGFR Direction

Inverse-variance–weighted analysis suggestively linked higher levels of agalactosylated IgG to lower eGFR (P = 0.058) and higher monosialylated IgG to higher eGFR (P = 0.048; Supplementary Table S17). MR-Egger regression supported these findings and indicated no substantial pleiotropy.

eGFR to Glycan Direction

Inverse-variance–weighted and weighted median methods indicated that higher eGFR was associated with lower proportions of agalactosylated IgG (P < 0.05). Sensitivity analyses (Egger regression, weighted mode, and simple mode) supported this direction. A similar pattern appeared for digalactosylated IgG, though the simple mode estimate differed (Supplementary Table S17).

Discussion

In this CKD cohort of 4782 participants with diverse etiology, IgG N-glycans were differentially associated with kidney function, albuminuria, and mortality. Higher agalactosylated IgG were associated with lower eGFR and higher mortality, whereas digalactosylated and sialylated IgG were associated with higher eGFR and reduced mortality across subgroups. Context-specific associations were observed for UACR and incident KF, with agalactosylation, digalactosylation, and sialylation showing patterns that varied by age, sex, and autoimmune status. Core fucosylation and bisecting GlcNAc had weaker, primarily UACR-specific associations; and MR suggested potential bidirectional causal relationships between eGFR and select N-glycan traits. Importantly, these associations do not necessarily contradict one another. Instead, they suggest that IgG N-glycan patterns behave differently depending on the context. Their effects differ between systemic outcomes such as mortality and kidney-specific outcomes such as albuminuria or KF; age, sex, and autoimmune status further modify these relationships. Similar associations between IgG glycan traits and kidney disease outcomes have been reported in diabetic and autoimmune nephropathy, although the specific glycan patterns and their relationships with kidney outcomes appear to vary across disease settings.5,15,36

Findings Consistent With the Previous Literature

Our observation that agalactosylated IgG associated with lower eGFR and higher mortality aligns with established evidence linking agalactosylated IgG to systemic inflammation, cardiometabolic risk, and impaired kidney function, including findings from TwinsUK and diabetes cohorts.5,10,12,13,15 Consistent with this, studies in CKD and diabetes have shown that reduced galactosylation and sialylation are associated with lower eGFR and adverse renal outcomes.5,15 Conversely, greater digalactosylation and sialylation associated with higher eGFR and lower mortality in the GCKD study, consistent with studies linking more galactosylated and sialylated IgG to favorable immune profiles and reduced cardiovascular risk, and with mechanistic work showing Fc sialylation dampening effector activity.5,9,12,13 The average IgG N-glycan profile in the GCKD study reflected age-related patterns seen in other cohorts, showing relatively high levels of agalactosylation and low levels of diagalactosylated and sialylated N-glycans, consistent with the immune changes that occur with aging.8,37, 38, 39 Together, these findings reinforce that IgG N-glycosylation captures chronic inflammatory stress relevant to CKD and aging biology.

Findings Divergent From Previous Work and Internal Contrasts

In contrast to overall protective associations, higher digalactosylation and sialylation associated with higher UACR and higher hazard for KF in younger participants and those with autoimmune kidney disease, and with KF alone in women. This opposes studies reporting lower sialylation in lupus nephritis,9,40 and generally antiinflammatory activity of sialylated IgG. Previous studies in autoimmune kidney disease have linked reduced galactosylation and sialylation to more severe disease,36 indicating a pattern that differs from our subgroup findings. A plausible mechanistic interpretation is that galactosylation and sialylation may exert context-dependent effects. While promoting immune tolerance systemically, they may enhance complement activation via C1q binding in immune complex–mediated kidney disease.15,41 In such contexts, excess Fc sialylation may be maladaptive. When sialylated IgG binds less effectively to Fcγ receptors,42 immune complexes may be cleared less efficiently, allowing them to deposit in the glomeruli, where they contribute to mesangial and endothelial injury. Sialylated immune complexes may engage inhibitory sialic acid–binding Ig-like lectins (siglecs) on kidney myeloid cells,43 inducing phagocytic paralysis and delayed resolution, similar to Siglec-driven immune suppression in cancer.44,45 This duality highlights that IgG N-glycans can be beneficial in metabolic and inflammatory aging, but may be detrimental in autoimmunity-driven nephropathies, a pattern reported across the immune disorder literature.46 Consistent with this, kidney tissue N-glycan changes in lupus models show a shift toward less complex glycans during active disease.47

Sex-specific associations of IgG N-glycans with KF should be interpreted cautiously. Although estrogen can increase IgG galactosylation and sialylation,48 it is unclear whether shifts simply reflect hormonal variation or contribute directly to immune regulation.

Systemic Versus Kidney-Specific Information Content

IgG N-glycans can capture systemic health burden, evidenced by consistent mortality associations.12,13 However, divergent directions for KF versus mortality in subgroups suggest kidney-specific biology in select contexts, potentially reflecting enhanced immune complex deposition or complement activation in autoimmune or early-onset CKD.9,41 Thus, IgG N-glycans may represent dual-context biomarkers and may be strong indicators of systemic inflammatory status, but also sensitive to kidney-specific pathogenic pathways in immune-mediated disease states. This is supported by studies showing that circulating IgG glycosylation correlates with systemic inflammation, whereas kidney tissue N-glycome changes are associated with local disease severity in CKD.49 Taken together, IgG N-glycans appear to capture 2 partially distinct domains as follows: (i) systemic inflammatory and cardiometabolic health, reflected in consistent mortality associations; and (ii) kidney-specific immune mechanisms, particularly in autoimmune or early-onset CKD, reflected in albuminuria and KF patterns. Distinguishing these domains is essential to avoid oversimplified interpretation of directionality across outcomes.

Effect Direction

MR indicated potential bidirectional effects. Genetically higher levels of agalactosylated IgG associated with lower eGFR, whereas genetic determinants of lower eGFR associated with higher agalactosylation and lower digalactosylation.50, 51, 52 Though exploratory, these results give preliminary evidence that IgG N-glycans and kidney function might interact dynamically, consistent with emerging evidence for reciprocal immune-kidney feedback loops.53, 54, 55, 56 Similar bidirectional relationships between glycan traits and kidney function have been suggested in CKD and diabetes populations.5,15

Novelty Aspects

Our data link population-level IgG Fc N-glycosylation to clinically relevant kidney outcomes and align these epidemiologic findings with mechanistic pathways implicated in CKD. To our knowledge, the relationship between IgG N-glycosylation and kidney health had so far only been investigated for the outcome eGFR5 or within diabetes cohorts.10,15,16 Previous work in the TwinsUK study first associated lower galactosylation and sialylation with reduced eGFR5 and diabetes studies linked proinflammatory IgG N-glycomes to accelerated kidney decline.10,15 We extend these associations into hard kidney endpoints within a CKD cohort of diverse etiologies and show that IgG N-glycan derived traits do not uniformly display favorable or unfavorable associations. Instead, the same trait may be adverse for one outcome yet beneficial for another. Furthermore, our exploratory MR analysis suggested that IgG N-glycan traits and eGFR may be causally related. Although these findings are highly tentative, they are of interest because such relationships have been difficult to demonstrate in previous MR studies on IgG N-glycosylation.57 Together, convergent evidence across outcomes, mechanisms, and MR positions IgG N-glycosylation as a tractable pathway for risk stratification, and potentially, immunomodulatory intervention in CKD.

Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of our study is the use of a large, well-characterized cohort of participants with moderate CKD from the GCKD study, enabling robust findings. Detailed phenotyping, including standardized assessments of kidney function, albuminuria, comorbidities, and medication use, allowed for careful adjustment of confounders and stratified analyses. The combination of cross-sectional and prospective data provided a comprehensive view of IgG N-glycans’ relevance across different stages and outcomes of CKD. Using genetic data from 2 large public Genome-Wide Association Studies, we explored potential causal links between eGFR and IgG N-glycans and found preliminary evidence supporting this possibility. However, this analysis was exploratory and not intended as confirmatory. Futher limitations are that the absence of a healthy control group precludes conclusions about changes in N-glycan traits preceding CKD onset. Because all participants had established disease, we could not assess whether observed patterns reflect causal mechanisms or consequences of CKD. Moreover, the IgG N-glycan measurements were semiquantitative and only from a single point in time. Total IgG concentrations were not available, precluding assessment of their influence on glycosylation patterns. Although traits were analyzed as relative abundances, residual confounding by IgG levels cannot be excluded. The cross-sectional nature of key associations limits causal interpretation, and it remains unclear whether eGFR and UACR should be treated as confounders or mediators in prospective analyses, which may have led to overadjustment in these models.

In Summary

IgG N-glycan signatures reflect systemic inflammatory aging and relate to kidney function and survival in CKD, with differing associations in autoimmune kidney disase and younger individuals. Although our study is explorative, these findings highlight IgG N-glycans as a promising immunologic biomarker for CKD phenotyping and risk stratification, while emphasizing that the complex biology underlying these relationships needs to be validated in mechanistically defined subgroups and interventional studies. Future studies should focus on large, phenotypically homogeneous CKD populations with detailed immunologic and molecular characterization to disentangle disease-specific effects from systemic effects and clarify whether IgG N-glycosylation contributes causally to kidney injury or progression.
