Life cycle assessment of high value activated carbon production based on mass and functional performance metrics
Authors: Junaid Saleem, Zubair Khalid Baig Moghal, Furqan Tahir, Tareq Al-Ansari, Ahmed I. Osman, Gordon McKay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The production of activated carbon (AC) from biomass holds substantial environmental potential, but its impact varies widely depending on the synthesis methods employed. However, unreliable experimental data results in inconsistent life cycle assessments (LCA), often dependent on generic or highly localized information. Most available data focuses solely on production metrics, neglecting crucial performance-based indicators. This study conducts LCA for a conceptual AC production facility designed to produce 1 kg of AC per batch of coconut shell (CS), particularly examining potassium hydroxide (KOH) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) activation routes. Environmental impacts (EIs) are evaluated using two functional units—mass-based and adsorption-based—and span eighteen metrics, including six key ones: net energy, climate change (CC), ozone depletion, fine particulate matter formation, marine eutrophication, and metal depletion. CC (kg CO₂ eq.) for 1 kg of AC production is 1.255 for KOH and 1.209 for NaOH, while energy requirements (in MJ) are 28.314 for KOH and 27.063 for NaOH. Notably, the pyrolysis step emerges as the most energy-intensive and significant contributor to carbon emissions. Per the adsorption-based unit, the KOH-led pathway shows a higher adsorption capacity of 729 g/kg versus 662 g/kg for NaOH, requiring less AC per kg of dye adsorbed. Consequently, the KOH pathway achieves 5% greater energy efficiency and 6% lower carbon emissions than the NaOH pathway. Synthesized ACs outperform commercial AC in all metrics, especially in energy use and carbon emissions. The study proposes improvements, such as alternative drying methods, to mitigate EIs and emphasizes the need to consider both production efficiency and functional performance to guide sustainable AC production and application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-16300-1.

Introduction

Over the past few decades, activated carbon (AC) and biochar from biomass have become well-established technologies for thermochemical conversion in oxygen-limited environments1,2. Compared to conventional methods, biomass conversion provides a more sustainable alternative by consuming less energy and producing a smaller carbon footprint3. AC is widely used in water treatment to remove organic compounds, pollutants, dyes, chlorine, and impurities, as well as in supercapacitors, fuel cells, and batteries due to its high surface area and conductivity4,5.

Coconut shells (CS) AC stands out for its advantages over other biomass sources6–15. The coconut industry generates an abundant supply of shell and husk biomass, which can be converted into high-quality adsorption materials for various applications16. CS is particularly efficient at producing high surface area AC, making it ideal for wastewater treatment, drug delivery, energy storage devices, and adsorption processes17,18.

Despite its potential, the thermochemical conversion of CS requires a comprehensive environmental assessment to ensure sustainability. Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) is a systematic approach to evaluating the environmental impacts of products and systems, providing a strong foundation for impact assessment and policy development19. Given the pivotal role of biomass recycling, applying LCA is both relevant and advantageous in terms of circular bioeconomy.

Numerous studies have evaluated the environmental impacts (EIs) of CS recycling for AC production20–22. For instance, an analysis of CS-based AC production in Indonesia focused on energy sources and overall environmental performance. It was identified that using renewable energy sources, such as biomass, could reduce human toxicity by up to 60% and global warming by up to 80%23. Besides, the environmental performance of AC produced via steam activation, along with the resulting electrodes, was assessed. Emissions of 5.68 kg of CO2 and a cumulative energy demand of 34.4 MJ per kg of AC were reported, with AC production accounting for approximately 60% of the total EIs in supercapacitor electrode manufacturing24.

Moreover, a study highlights that understanding the physicochemical properties of CS biomass prior to thermochemical conversion is crucial for producing high-quality charcoal and optimizing conversion processes to reduce greenhouse gas emissions25. A comparison of the EIs of AC produced from CS and palm oil shells revealed that AC from CS has higher EIs, particularly regarding climate change, acidification/eutrophication, and fossil fuel consumption26. Furthermore, a comparative analysis of CS AC with other raw materials such as coal, wood, peat, and reactivated coal AC revealed that CS AC has the lowest EI across several categories27.

The EIs associated with the production and application of AC from CS are often underexplored. In particular, the choice of chemical activators, such as KOH and NaOH, plays a significant role in shaping the EIs of AC production. The increased carbon emissions and net energy consumption associated with KOH production stem from its energy-intensive manufacturing process. KOH is primarily produced through the electrolysis of potassium chloride (KCl), which demands significantly more energy than NaOH production. This is because KOH, which readily absorbs carbon dioxide and water, requires a more energy-heavy operational process. The electrolysis of KCl results in KOH, chlorine, and hydrogen, consuming more electricity than NaOH production, which uses the membrane electrolysis of sodium chloride (NaCl). The NaOH process efficiently separates hydrogen and NaOH from chlorine, minimizing the risk of hazardous by-products. Additionally, KOH production is more expensive due to the higher cost and lower natural abundance of KCl compared to NaCl, further contributing to its elevated energy input and carbon emissions.

Given these considerations, a comprehensive LCA can offer valuable insights into the environmental impacts of AC production, yet a significant gap remains in the literature—particularly in comparing different activation routes and incorporating performance-based metrics. Most existing studies use a single, mass-based functional unit (per kg of AC), which provides a baseline but overlooks the material’s functional performance in real applications. Adsorption capacity, a critical performance indicator, is better captured by an adsorption-based functional unit (per kg of dye adsorbed), offering a more application-specific perspective. Using both functional units enables a nuanced assessment of trade-offs: the mass-based unit facilitates cross-study comparisons, while the adsorption-based unit reflects environmental efficiency in practical use28. This dual approach ensures both production efficiency and functional performance are integrated into sustainability assessments.

Herein, we conduct an LCA of AC production using KOH and NaOH activation methods. EIs are assessed per kilogram of AC produced and per unit of dye adsorption capacity, focusing on six impact categories: net energy, climate change, ozone depletion, fine particulate matter, and metal depletion. Additional impact categories are provided in the supplementary file. We also compare these results with commercial AC and propose improvements, such as replacing the oven drying step with sunlight drying. This comprehensive approach not only enhances our understanding of the environmental implications of AC production but also informs more sustainable practices in its application. Such research is vital for guiding industry practices and policy-making towards more sustainable and environmentally friendly solutions.

Materials and methods

KOH, NaOH, and Gentian Violet (GV) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. CS were collected locally in Qatar.

The process starts with collecting CS, which are transported to the facility via a diesel-fueled truck. The shells are cut into 1–2 cm pieces and dried—either in a hot air oven or under sunlight (the latter reduces emissions but takes longer). Activation is carried out using 2.0 M KOH and NaOH solutions prepared with deionized water. The dried CS is soaked overnight in the basic solution, then rinsed with water to remove excess chemicals. After drying again in a hot air oven, the material undergoes pyrolysis in a furnace heated from 30 to 600 °C at 20 °C/min and held at 600 °C for 3 h, converting it into AC.

Activation of CS: The process flow diagram of the activation process is depicted in Fig. 1, and the specific details and remarks are presented in Table 1. Detailed procedures for the activation processes using KOH and NaOH are illustrated in Figs. S1 and S2, respectively (supplementary file). Initially, an overall impact assessment is undertaken for two different scenarios. This leads to a detailed attribution of percentage contributions from each stage to pinpoint trade-offs, identify the most significant impact areas, and unearth potential areas for improvement. Subsequently, these findings are compared with the EIs attributed to commercially available AC.

The neutralization step involves the removal of basic characteristics (KOH and NaOH) to achieve a neutral pH, accomplished by rinsing with water multiple times (three to five rinses). The activation process requires a combination of a base (KOH or NaOH) and water. Given that both the base and CS are solids, dilution is necessary. Therefore, we prepared a 2 M solution of the base using water. The activation process involves using both the base solution and water. Drying occurs in two stages: before activation and after the activation-neutralization step.

Dye Adsorption: 3.2 g/L of GV in water was used to assess the maximum adsorption capacity for the as-prepared AC.

LCA

‘LCA for Experts’ software was used with ‘GaBi’ database. The process was designed as per experimental work. The related modules and flows were collected from the database and applied in the software. The LCA method is structured into four key stages: (i) goal definition and scope delineation, where the aims of the analysis, the limits of the system under study, and the functional units are specified; (ii) compilation of a life-cycle inventory (LCI) to catalogue the system’s core inputs and outputs; (iii) execution of a life-cycle impact assessment that evaluates the potential environmental toll associated with the system via designated impact categories that may include issues like climate change, acid rain, and eutrophication; and (iv) result interpretation, where the findings from the LCI are considered alongside the guidelines of standards ISO 14040 and ISO 1404429–31.

Goal and Scope: This research aims to ascertain the EIs of different techniques used in the production of AC from CS. It encompasses the transportation of CS and all necessary inputs, as illustrated in Fig. 1, but omits the AC’s end-of-life phase. The LCI stage involves detailed cataloging and quantification of all the inputs and outputs. Data collection covers raw materials consumption, energy use, and emissions for each step of the activation process. In the LCI assessment stage, methods are applied to transform LCI information into quantifiable potential EIs associated with the product. This process entails the selection and categorization of impact areas, the application of characterization factors, as well as normalizing and weighing the various impact categories. For the assessment of environmental concerns such as climate change, human toxicity, and acidification in the production of AC, the study utilizes the ReCiPe midpoint approach. This widely recognized method in LCA quantitatively determines potential EI during the product’s life cycle. As a midpoint approach, it estimates impact scores at a juncture mid-way through the characterization to endpoint stages, addressing a spectrum of effects on the environment, health, and resources. Results and discussion

Fig. 2(a) XRD patterns, and (b) FTIR spectra for KOH and NaOH activated carbon.

(a) XRD patterns, and (b) FTIR spectra for KOH and NaOH activated carbon.

Figure 2a and b present the X-ray diffraction and FTIR patterns of the as-synthesized AC. The XRD spectra remains the same for both synthesis pathways. The spectra are characterized by broad diffraction bands rather than distinct sharp peaks, suggesting that the material is mainly amorphous, which has a significant impact on the performance of the AC in the adsorption tests32. Specifically, two diffraction bands are observed at 24° and 42°. The presence of these bands indicates the existence of graphite crystallites within the carbon scaffold of the AC.

Moreover, the functional groups identified through FTIR analysis enhance the material’s adsorption capabilities by offering active sites for chemical interactions. The peaks in the 3300 to 3500 cm−1 range are commonly observed in the spectra of activated carbons (ACs)33. These peaks represent the stretching vibrations of O–H bonds in hydroxyl groups and N-H bonds in alkoxy groups. Both activation methods show similar spectra, suggesting that the chemical structure remains largely unchanged by the activation process. Additionally, sharp peak shifts around 1620–1625 cm−1 indicate C =O stretching in aldehyde and ketone groups34,35, while the broader peaks at 1160–1170 cm−1 suggest the presence of amine groups34.

Scenario 1: eis as per mass-based functional unit

Table 3; Fig. 3 present the results for six key impact categories associated with 1 kg of AC production using KOH and NaOH pathways. Additional categories are available in the supplementary file (Fig. S3). The two most critical EI are climate change (CC) and net energy, which are 8% and 4.6% higher for the KOH pathway. Additionally, metal depletion is 100 times greater in the case of the KOH activation route.

CC and net energy

The increased levels of carbon emissions and net energy consumption linked to KOH production are due to its specific operational process. KOH is mainly produced through the electrolysis of potassium chloride (KCl), which requires more energy than NaOH production. This is because KOH, which absorbs carbon dioxide and water, necessitates more energy-intensive manufacturing processes.

The production of KOH involves KCl as the primary input, resulting in KOH, chlorine, and hydrogen. This process consumes more electricity and energy than NaOH production, which uses the chlorine-alkali electrolysis of sodium chloride (NaCl) via a membrane process. NaOH production effectively separates hydrogen and NaOH from chlorine, preventing the formation of hazardous compounds.

The higher costs of KOH production also contribute to its EI. KCl is more expensive than NaCl due to its more complex extraction and purification processes and lower natural abundance. These factors lead to higher energy and resource inputs for KOH production, increasing carbon emissions and energy consumption.

Metal depletion

KOH production typically results in more metal depletion than NaOH production due to differences in their electrolysis processes and the nature of their raw materials. For KOH, the electrolysis of KCl requires electrodes that can degrade under harsh conditions such as high temperatures and corrosive environments. This degradation leads to metal depletion in the electrodes, necessitating their replacement.

In contrast, NaOH production through the electrolysis of NaCl has seen advancements, including the use of more efficient membrane cell technologies. These technologies reduce metal depletion by minimizing direct contact between the electrolyte and electrodes, decreasing corrosion and metal depletion rates.

Moreover, NaCl’s greater abundance and lower cost compared to KCl allow for more optimized and less metal-intensive NaOH production processes. The higher cost and complexity of KCl extraction and purification also indirectly contributes to more metal-intensive KOH production methods. Hence, due to the specific requirements of the electrolysis process, the characteristics of the raw materials, and the technological differences in production methods, KOH production generally leads to more metal depletion than NaOH production.

Comparison with commercial AC

Our LCA results demonstrate that the synthesized ACs outperform the commercial AC across several metrics. Notably, the energy net and CC are considerably reduced for the synthesized ACs, as shown in Table 3.

The variation arises primarily from the distinct production routes used for the different ACs (e.g., NaOH-AC, KOH-AC, and commercial AC), which result in differing quantities and types of raw materials required to produce 1 kg of AC. Moreover, the background data for commercial AC is based on industrial averages from the GaBi database, which likely account for process optimizations, energy recovery, and emission control measures that are not implemented at the lab scale. These differences in production scale, process efficiency, and system boundary assumptions contribute to the observed differences in environmental impacts.

Furthermore, the other impact categories are normalized concerning KOH and compared in Fig. 4. Employing our pathways for the synthesis of ACs offers considerable advantages with respect to stratospheric ozone depletion and fine particulate matter formation. In short, our activation pathways help reduce emissions and net energy requirements.

Contribution analysis

Table 4; Figs. 5 and 6 depict the contribution analysis of the two activation pathways. The contribution analysis helps understand the stages that burden the environmental footprints, and it helps identify the room for improvements. It can be seen that the stages, such as transportation and shredding, account for the lowest EIs across both pathways.

The drying process is divided into two stages: the first occurs before activation with KOH or NaOH, and the second occurs after the activation-neutralization step. The second drying stage occurs once the pH is neutralized from the alkaline medium. Both stages are essential to prepare and neutralize the material. The drying was carried out in an oven using electricity generated from natural gas, as is the case in Qatar. Later, we will provide information regarding reducing EIs associated with oven drying by replacing it with drying under sunlight.

Activation with KOH is crucial in metal depletion, contributing 99% of the activation process. In the case of NaOH, this step is reduced to 26%.

Pyrolysis is the most dominant step in EIs assessment. It has the most profound effect on climate change and net energy. The pyrolysis step consumed 1.94 kWh of energy for the production of AC. The energy requirement remains the same for both activation pathways. During the pyrolysis of CS, several gases and by-products are emitted, which can impact the environment and contribute to climate change3,36,37: (a) Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs): These include a range of hydrocarbons that can be harmful to the environment and human health.

VOCs contribute to the formation of ground-level ozone and smog; (b) CO2: As a major greenhouse gas, CO2 is a significant contributor to climate change. Although pyrolysis is performed in the absence of oxygen, the process can still result in CO2 emissions from the breakdown of organic materials; (c) Methane (CH4): Methane is another potent greenhouse gas, much more effective at trapping heat in the atmosphere than CO2 over a short period. It can be released during the pyrolysis process if not properly managed; (d) Carbon Monoxide (CO): While not a direct greenhouse gas, CO can contribute to the formation of CO2 in the atmosphere. It is also toxic to humans and animals at high concentrations; (e) Particulate Matter (PM): Fine particles can be released during pyrolysis, contributing to air pollution and respiratory issues. PM can also have indirect climate effects by influencing cloud formation and atmospheric chemistry; and (f) Non-Condensable Gases: These include gases such as H2, CO, and CH4, which are not easily condensed into liquids. Some of these gases can be captured and used as energy sources, potentially offsetting some emissions.

The emissions from pyrolysis, mainly CO2 and CH4, directly affect climate change due to their greenhouse gas properties. Reducing these emissions or capturing them for beneficial use (e.g., energy recovery) can mitigate some of the EIs. Proper management and optimization of the pyrolysis process can help minimize harmful emissions and improve the overall sustainability of producing AC from CS.

Scenario 2: EI as per performance-based functional unit

Table 5 presents the dye adsorption capacities of AC prepared from CS using two different activation pathways. The KOH-activated AC exhibited a high adsorption capacity of 729 mg/g, while the NaOH-activated AC showed an adsorption capacity of 662 mg/g. These values are comparable to or even exceed most reported values in the literature for CS-based AC. For instance, previous studies have reported adsorption capacities of 42 mg/g for Rhodamine B22,38, and for Methylene Blue (MB), adsorption capacities of 278 mg/g39, 51 mg/g40, 321, mg/g41, 156 mg/g42, and 315 mg43 have been documented.

Figure 7 presents a comparative analysis of the EI assessments for the KOH and NaOH activation pathways, based on per kg of dye adsorbed. This figure highlights that the KOH pathway is more energy-efficient, requiring 38.84 MJ to adsorb one kg of dye, compared to the NaOH pathway, which requires 40.87 MJ. Similarly, the KOH pathway is associated with lower carbon dioxide emissions, generating 1.72 kg CO2 eq. for every kg of dye adsorbed, a reduction of 6% compared to the NaOH pathway’s 1.83 kg CO2 eq.

Conversion factors were applied to standardize measurements for adsorbing one kg of dye to compare the two pathways accurately. For the KOH pathway, the conversion factor was calculated to be 1.3717, based on the adsorption of 729 g of dye per kg of AC produced. Conversely, the NaOH pathway’s conversion factor was determined to be 1.5105, reflecting the adsorption of 662 g of dye per kg of AC. These factors demonstrate that the NaOH pathway requires more AC (1.5105 kg) to adsorb one kg of dye, compared to the KOH pathway’s requirement of 1.3717 kg, contributing to higher energy demands and carbon emissions.

However, KOH-activation led to higher metal depletion of 0.14029 kg Cu eq. Compared to NaOH activation, which is 0.0018 kg Cu eq., which was discussed in detail in the previous section. Both stratospheric ozone depletion and marine eutrophication values are similar for both activation pathways. Thus, while the KOH activation pathway demonstrates superior energy efficiency and lower carbon emissions in dye adsorption, it’s essential to consider these findings in the broader context of EI.

Scenario 3: potential improvements in eis

Scenario 3 explores potential improvements in EIs, mainly focusing on the drying phase of the activation process. Here, the idea is to consider using sunlight as an alternative to conventional oven drying methods.

Instead of relying solely on conventional oven drying methods, which typically consume significant amounts of energy, utilizing sunlight offers a more sustainable alternative. Sunlight is abundant, renewable, and freely available, making it an attractive option for drying processes. Sunlight drying can also be more cost-effective in certain regions, especially in areas with ample sunlight throughout the year.

Figures 8 and 9 compare the activation process when replacing oven drying with open drying under sunlight. These figures are presented using 1 kg of AC production as a basis. It was observed that sunlight utilization significantly affects net energy requirement by 7.5% and carbon emissions by 7.7% for the normalized KOH and NaOH activation pathway data.

Sensitivity analysis:

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the influence of varying the molar concentrations (1 M, 2 M, and 3 M) of KOH and NaOH on the EIs of AC production. As shown in Fig. 10, increasing the molarity of the activating agents leads to a noticeable rise in EIs across most impact categories, including net energy, CC, and metal depletion. This trend is primarily attributed to the higher chemical input and associated upstream processing burdens linked with more concentrated solutions. Notably, the shift from 1 M to 3 M concentrations results in a considerable increase in CC emissions and energy demand, indicating that the environmental cost of higher molarity is non-negligible. While higher concentrations may enhance activation efficacy and surface properties, the marginal gain in adsorption capacity must be weighed against the disproportionately higher environmental footprint. These findings emphasize the need for a balanced selection of activation conditions that optimize both performance and sustainability. Therefore, 2 M appears to offer a compromise between acceptable adsorption efficiency and environmental performance, as also reflected in the base case scenarios of this study.

Conclusion

This comprehensive study provides a detailed environmental impact (EI) assessment of coconut shell (CS) activated carbon (AC) production using potassium hydroxide (KOH) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) activation methods, with comparisons to commercial AC. Evaluations were conducted using two functional units—per kg of AC produced and per kg of dye adsorbed—offering a nuanced understanding of environmental implications. The KOH activation pathway achieved a higher adsorption capacity of 729 g/kg, while the NaOH pathway reached 662 g/kg, both exceeding many values reported in the literature. Synthesized ACs also significantly outperformed commercial AC in terms of energy consumption and carbon emissions. Additionally, the study explored potential improvements in EIs by replacing conventional oven drying with sunlight, resulting in a 7.5% reduction in net energy use and a 7.7% decrease in carbon emissions, thus highlighting the potential for further environmental gains. Overall, the findings contribute to understanding the EI associated with CS AC production, emphasize the importance of incorporating both production efficiency and functional performance in sustainability assessments, and offer a solid foundation for future efforts to enhance the environmental performance of AC production and application.
