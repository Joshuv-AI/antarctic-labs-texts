A unique case of IUD associated group A Streptococci pyogenic ascites
Authors: Smadar Goldfarb, Maya Korem, Limor Rubin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Group A Strep (GAS) infection associated with intrauterine devices (IUDs) is rare and only sporadically described following IUDs removal. We present a unique case of toxic shock syndrome presenting with pyogenic ascites following IUD removal in a 29 year old otherwise healthy woman. Upon admission, the patient primarily complained of abdominal pain and diarrhea and was found to have acute kidney injury. Despite empiric antibiotic therapy, her condition deteriorated, developing acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), worsening renal failure, ascites, and a desquamating rash on her palms. All cultures obtained from blood, urine, stool, and peritoneal fluid were negative. However, 16S rRNA PCR testing of the peritoneal fluid confirmed the diagnosis of GAS. Source control and full recovery were achieved following an exploratory laparotomy with abdominal washouts in addition to antibiotics administration. This report adds to the limited literature on GAS infections associated with IUDs and demonstrates the need for heightened clinical suspicion and advanced diagnostic techniques in similar scenarios.

Introduction

The use of intrauterine device (IUDs), also known as intrauterine contraceptive device or coil, are a common and safe form of long-acting reversible birth control. Serious complications are rare and include ectopic pregnancy and pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), occurring in less than 1 % of patients [1]. Mostly, complications associate with IUDs insertion, and only rarely with its removal [2]. IUDs placement is performed using sterile gloves and cleaning the cervix and adjacent vaginal fornices with an antiseptic solution. IUDs removal is a non-sterile procedure performed using forceps, pulling the IUD via its strings form the vagina [3]. In cases of PID, the removal of an IUD is not necessarily required since the outcomes following IUD removal or retaining are similar [4].

Bacteria associated with serious IUDs infections are mainly Staphylococcus and Streptococcus species [2]. Actinomyces associated infections are very rare, however commonly associated with long-term IUD use [5].

Group A Strep (GAS, Streptococcus pyogenes) infection is a major concern in obstetrics and gynecology; being the most fatal infectious etiology in pregnant and post-partum women [6]. Invasive GAS occurs annually in the United States at a rate of 0.06 per 1000 live births, with a maternal Case fatally rate of 3.5 % and up to 7.7 % in other developed countries [7]. However, fatal or severe GAS infections associated with IUDs are rare – with only scarce case reports 8, 9, 10. Approximately six percent of invasive GAS infections in adults develop into streptococcal toxic shock syndrome (TSS), a life-threatening complication with a mortality rate of up to 36 % [11].

Primary peritonitis due to GAS is a rare clinical entity, but several small case series have been reported in the literature. One notable series from Japan described 25 cases of primary GAS peritonitis between 2000 and 2017. In this cohort, 72 % of the patients were female, and 41 % had positive vaginal cultures for GAS. These findings led the authors to propose that, in females, the likely route of infection is ascending spread of GAS from the vagina and uterus to the peritoneal cavity [12]. A similar conclusion was reached by Tilanus et al., who reported on 10 cases of primary GAS peritonitis [13].

Below we present a unique case of GAS pyogenic infection following removal of an IUD, with associated toxic shock syndrome.

Case description

A previously healthy 29-year-old woman (G2P2AB1) presented to the emergency department (ED) with a five-day history of general weakness, chills, shortness of breath, abdominal pain, and watery diarrhea (3–4 stools/day). Her symptoms began approximately 24 h after an uncomplicated removal of an IUD (levonorgestrel-releasing T-shaped intrauterine device) that had been in place for 3.5 years. The patient reported being sexually active with a single male partner and denied engaging in oral sex. She elected to have her intrauterine device removed to attempt conception. The removal procedure was performed at an outpatient community clinic. She experienced mild discomfort during the procedure and did not recall any associated bleeding.

On admission, she exhibited tachycardia (133 bpm), a blood pressure of 117/62 mmHg, tachypnea (40 breaths/min), and an oxygen saturation of 89 % on room air, which improved to 92 % with nasal cannula (NC) oxygen. Physical examination revealed diffuse abdominal tenderness without peritonitis and minimal bilateral ronchi on lung auscultation. Vaginal examination was unremarkable, without discharge, bleeding, or pelvic tenderness suggestive of PID. Initial laboratory findings included acute renal failure (creatinine 390 µmol/L), metabolic acidosis with respiratory alkalosis and an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) of 40.7 mg/dL. Complete blood count (CBC) was within normal limits. Urinalysis showed no signs of infection and a fractional excretion of sodium (FENa) of 0.2 % suggested pre-renal failure. Initially, the patient was treated for presumed acute gastroenteritis with supportive fluid resuscitation and antibiotics (ceftriaxone 2 g/d and metronidazole 500 mg/tid). Acute kidney injury (AKI) was attributed to hypovolemia and possible sepsis. Over the next 24 h, her tachypnea persisted, and she became anuric despite adequate fluid resuscitation. Her white blood cell (WBC) count rose to 19.4 × 109/L, necessitating transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU).

Stool cultures for Shigella, Salmonella and Campylobacter species were negative, as were stool PCR for Clostridioides difficile toxin genes and urine PCR for Neisseria gonorrhea and Chlamydia trachomatis.

A chest X-ray revealed bilateral opacities and pleural effusion with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), requiring high-flow NC support for adequate oxygenation. Thoracocentesis yielded transudative fluid with negative Gram-stain and cultures. Over the subsequent four days, the patient showed improvement with antibiotic treatment and diuretics, requiring less oxygen support and demonstrating concurrent laboratory improvement, including a decrease in CRP (8 mg/dl) and creatinine levels, and an increase in urine output. Antibiotics were discontinued due to persistently negative cultures. However, three days later (day 8 of admission), the patient remained tachycardic and tachypneic. Physical examination revealed mild abdominal tenderness with shifting dullness, suggestive of moderate ascites, and a new desquamating rash on her palms. Given the cutaneous presentation and the temporal relationship to IUD removal, TSS became a likely diagnosis and treatment with piperacillin/tazobactam and clindamycin was initiated. A repeat vaginal examination remained normal, but a transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) revealed a significant amount of thick peritoneal fluid (Fig. 1B). Upon further questioning, the patient recalled that the IUD strings were not visible during removal, and a surgical clamp was required for extraction.

A peritoneal paracentesis was performed, yielding approximately 3 liters of thick, suppurative fluid (Fig. 1 C). The fluid analysis showed a WBC count of 114.5 × 109/L, with a neutrophil predominance, a serum-ascites albumin gradient (SAAG) of 1.1 g/dl, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) of 18,850 U/L, a total protein of 42 U/L and glucose < 0.3 mmol/L. Bacterial cultures and a sample for 16 s rRNA PCR were sent for analysis. Due to persistent tachycardia, tachypnea and a rising CRP level (17 mg/dl) and after consultation with gynecology and surgical specialists, an exploratory laparoscopy was performed to rule out organ perforation or other major abdominal pathology and to achieve source control via abdominal washout. The laparoscopy revealed a large amount of pus, fibrin and omental adhesions throughout the peritoneal cavity surrounding the abdominal and pelvic organs. Limited visualization due to the inflammatory process necessitated conversion to an exploratory laparotomy for thorough examination and repeated abdominal cavity washes, followed by the placement of four surgical drains. All intraoperative cultures were negative, and pathology samples were consistent with purulent fluid without other specific findings. On day 15 of admission, the 16 s ribosomal rRNA PCR of the peritoneal fluid returned positive for Streptococcus pyogenes, confirming the diagnosis of TSS.

Three days postoperatively, the patient was transferred from the ICU to the gynecological ward. She completed a 5-day course of intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam with clindamycin, followed by a 5-day course of ceftriaxone and metronidazole. Over the subsequent days, she gradually recovered and was discharged. At a three-month follow-up visit, the patient had a normal physical examination, and a TVUS showed no pathological findings or free abdominal or pelvic fluid collection.

Discussion

This case presents a rare instance of severe Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus - GAS) primary peritonitis complicated by TSS following IUD removal. The patient met the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) clinical criteria for TSS, including hypotension and evidence of multiple organ system involvement (renal and pulmonary failure) [14].

Early manifestations of TSS can be non-specific, mimicking influenza or gastrointestinal illness, often preceding the generalized erythematous rash and multi organ failure. 15, 16. Our patient initially presented with weakness and diarrhea, subsequently developing AKI and ARDS, highlighting this diagnostic challenge. The pathophysiology of TSS involves superantigens [17] - bacterial toxins/proteins that trigger a massive non-specific T cell activation, leading to a profound pro-inflammatory cytokines storm [17]. Treatment for TSS includes aggressive supportive care and close monitoring with antibiotic therapy, normally a combination of penicillin or aminopenicillin with Clindamycin that inhibits protein synthesis and reduces toxin production [18]. While adjunctive intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) has not consistently demonstrated a reduction in mortality or length of hospital stay in clinical trials 18, 19, other studies—including a systematic review and meta-analysis that combined data from several small non-randomized cohorts and one randomized trial—suggest that IVIG may offer benefit as an adjunctive treatment [20]. Given the high morbidity and mortality associated with STSS, the use of IVIG is supported by some experts as a rescue therapy in severe cases, due to the potential for high mortality [20].

Interestingly, our patient did not present with typical clinical features of PID and initial cultures from blood, pleural fluid and peritoneal fluid were negative. Although mild free fluid in the rectouterine pouch is commonly observed in PID, ascites is rare and should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses, including liver disease and malignancy. In the context of PID, ascites is typically associated with severe complications like tubo-ovarian abscess or Fitz-Hugh–Curtis syndrome [21]. However, as described above, primary peritonitis and ascites have been reported in small case series due to GAS infection, with or without associated toxic shock syndrome, and show a trend toward affecting females of reproductive age 12, 13.

A previously published case report by Cho et al. described a similar presentation of IUD-associated TSS in a 49 year old woman, including moderate ascites, ARDS, liver injury and renal failure. In that case, GAS was cultured from blood and the IUD, and despite intensive care, the patient succumbed to sepsis [8]. In a similar case reported by Sakaguchi et al., a 22-year-old woman was admitted to the hospital with abdominal pain and rapidly progressed to multi-organ failure, accompanied by ascites and bilateral empyema. GAS was isolated from her blood, pleural, and peritoneal fluids. Further analysis identified the strain as a rare emm103 clone expressing the speB superantigen. The clinical course was severe, necessitating a prolonged ICU stay of 21 days, extended invasive mechanical ventilation, and subsequent rehabilitation [22]. In contrast, our patient experienced a favorable outcome following aggressive fluid resuscitation, close monitoring, targeted antibiotic therapy and surgical intervention for source control. The identification GAS in the normally sterile peritoneal fluid, was only achieved through molecular 16S rRNA PCR, underscoring the importance of such diagnostics in atypical presentations.

We hypothesize that the IUD removal procedure caused minor trauma to the endometrium, potentially microperforation, allowing for the seeding of GAS into the abdominal cavity. This likely resulted in primary pyogenic GAS peritonitis complicated by TSS. While IUDs insertion and removal are generally considered safe and common procedures, this case highlights the potential for rare and severe infectious complications. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for sepsis following any intrauterine manipulation, even in the absence of typical PID signs, and initiate early antibiotic therapy when indicated. The utility of molecular diagnostics should be considered in cases with high clinical suspicion but negative conventional cultures.
