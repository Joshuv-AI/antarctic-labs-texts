Insight into the influence of various cultivation regions on the identification of metabolites from Capsella bursa-pastoris via a clustering algorithm
Authors: In Young Lee, Doo-Hee Lee, Min Ju Lee, Ju Hong Park, Nami Joo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study focused on the regional differentiation of metabolites of C. bursa-pastoris cultivated across Korea via UHPLC‒HRMS‒based untargeted metabolomics. Extensive screening was conducted on samples collected from five distinct sites in 20 cities, resulting in the identification of 311 primary and secondary metabolites. The samples were classified via six clustering techniques (K-means, agglomerative clustering, spectral clustering, Birch, mini-batch K-means, and bisecting K-means) via a clustering algorithm that is based on metabolite concentrations. Twenty key features that significantly influenced the clustering were extracted and validated. The classification results demonstrated a strong correlation with the geographical location of the cultivation site. C. bursa-pastoris from inland regions presented relatively high concentrations of sulfureous compounds, such as glucosinolic acid and isothiocyanate. The findings of this study provide valuable insights into the integration of machine learning techniques with untargeted metabolomics, facilitating the development of targeted phytochemical profiles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19391-y.Keywords: Capsella bursa-pastoris, Machine learning, UHPLC‒HRMS, Clustering, MetabolitesSubject terms: Plant molecular biology, Mass spectrometry

Introduction

Plant-derived medicinal substances are highly valued as primary sources of new biologically active compounds because they are readily available, cost-effective, and culturally significant1–4.Various plant organs, such as leaves, fruits, stems, seeds, and roots, contain significant amounts of bioactive compounds, such as phenols, flavonoids, and tannins, which exhibit antioxidant properties and inhibit excessive free radical production5,6. These phytochemicals, highlighted in epidemiological and clinical studies, are known for their protective and disease-preventing properties due to their antioxidant capabilities, leading to an increased focus on their characterization and quantification in recent years.

Capsella bursa-pastoris (L.) Medik. (C. bursa-pastoris), a weedy isoploid plant of the Brassicaceae family, is an annually distributed root vegetable crop worldwide7. It grows and produces seeds at low temperatures and exhibits excellent antioxidant capabilities8 because of its ability to adapt to cold9. C. bursa-pastoris contains numerous bioactive compounds, including phenols10 and polysaccharides11. The edible root of this plant has been utilized in traditional Korean medicine to treat edema and hypertension. Previous phytochemical studies have isolated flavonoids, terpenoids, and phenolic compounds from this plant and reported their antibacterial, antifungal, antitumor, and hepatic catalase activities. Additionally, the plant is used for culinary purposes; in Korea, it is consumed boiled or fried, whereas in Turkey, it is typically consumed raw or added to salads12,13. Owing to their pepper-like taste, leaves are known as “peppergrass”14, and the edible portion contains sulfur-based compounds that contribute to its distinctive aroma15. C. bursa-pastoris is extensively distributed across both northern and southern Hemispheres’ temperate zones16–18, and is naturally cultivated in open fields throughout Korea. The chemical composition of plants, including C. bursa-pastoris, is influenced by various factors, such as climate and the environment, which govern the synthesis of secondary metabolites19. Therefore, investigating the effects of these factors on metabolite accumulation in C. bursa-pastoris is crucial for enhancing the functionality of natural plant sources and advancing breeding efforts. However, owing to the paucity of related studies, this study evaluated C. bursa-pastoris samples from different regions via liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry (LC‒MS) to determine the composition of metabolites.

In the field of metabolomics analysis, LC‒MS is the predominant technique employed because of its ability to evaluate a wide array of metabolites. The method of untargeted profiling is particularly advantageous, as it does not require prior knowledge of the compounds involved, thus facilitating the identification of virtually all analyzable metabolites. This method is especially critical in the discovery phase, where identifying a broad range of relevant metabolites is essential for understanding biological processes. In untargeted UHPLC‒MS‒based metabolomics, high-resolution MS (HRMS) serves as the primary platform capable of providing precise metabolite masses, while tandem MS (MS2) is also utilized. Consequently, methodologies employing high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) are recognized as among the most reliable analytical platforms because of their comprehensive screening and retrospective analysis capabilities, as well as the high level of confidence associated with the identification process. Moreover, when integrated with chromatographic techniques, the validation of positive results within the HRMS workflow relies on several identification criteria, including retention time, mass accuracy, isotope analysis, and fragmentation patterns. This comprehensive approach significantly enhances the reliability of the identification process20. Therefore, this study investigated metabolite differences in C. bursa-pastoris from various Korean regions via UHPLC‒HRMS‒based untargeted metabolomics in both ion modes. To the best of our knowledge, a comprehensive investigation of the phytochemical profile of C. bursa-pastoris has not yet been reported. This study aimed to evaluate the composition of primary and secondary metabolites of C. bursa-pastoris collected at the same harvest stage from 20 different regions to determine its potential as a source of bioactive metabolites in the health functional food industry.

Furthermore, we analyzed the acquired data via multivariate analysis to explore the unique metabolites present in samples from different regions. Analyzing statistical data is an effective method for understanding the metabolic state and evaluating the quality of medicinal plants from various cultivation areas21,22. Moreover, known as data segmentation, cluster analysis aims to group a set of objects into clusters or subsets, ensuring that items within each cluster share more similarities with one another than those in other clusters do. Clustering is one of the most prominent tasks in the field of unsupervised machine learning23,24. The primary aim of unsupervised learning algorithms is to identify patterns and learn meaningful relationships within data, thereby revealing their underlying structure. Hence, we utilized a clustering algorithm to achieve high-precision discrimination and prediction between samples from different regions. This study aimed to develop a methodology to isolate biologically active compounds from C. bursa-pastoris extracts and to analyze the correlation between cultivation region and the quality of metabolites through the application of unsupervised learning clustering techniques. The results of this study provide valuable insights for applying current technological methods to obtain the desired phytochemical profiles.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Twenty regions in Korea (Seoul, Gyeonggi, Chungcheongbuk-do, Chungcheongnam-do, Gyeongsangbuk-do, Gyeongsangnam-do, Jeollabuk-do, and Jeollanam-do) were selected, and C. bursa-pastoris was collected from five districts within each region (Fig. S1). In South Korea, C. bursa-pastoris, which is grown primarily in the western and southern coastal regions, is renowned for its exceptional nutritional quality, which is attributed to the influence of yellow soil and maritime breezes. As a result, twelve coastal areas recognized for C. bursa-pastoris production were identified, along with eight inland regions where samples are also cultivated, to serve as sampling sites for this study. The average temperature and humidity, annual precipitation, and soil types of the sampling areas are presented in Table S1. C. bursa-pastoris from all regions were grown without fertilizer or protection and manually harvested between March 15 and March 30, 2024. After harvesting, the samples were dried for a minimum of 72 h via a freeze dryer (Ilshin Biobase, Dongducheon, Korea) and then ground with a cutter mill. The samples were stored at -80 °C in a freezer (New Brunswick Scientific Co., Buckinghamshire, UK) and prepared for analysis.

Untargeted metabolite profiling

The samples were extracted with 80% aqueous methanol. The extracts were then analyzed via a UHPLC‒Orbitrap-HRMS system for metabolite identification. Chromatographic separations were carried out via a Vanquish Flex binary UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) equipped with a CORTECS T3 column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 1.6 μm particle size, 120 Å pore size; Waters, Milford, USA). A gradient elution protocol was implemented, employing mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in LC/MS‒grade water) along with mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) in the following sequence: 3% B from 0 to 0.5 min; a transition from 3 to 15% B from 0.5 to 15.0 min; an increase to 95% B from 15.0 to 50.0 min; maintenance of 95% B from 50.0 to 55.0 min; a decrease from 95 to 5% B from 55.0 to 56.0 min; and a final phase with 5% B from 56.0 to 60.0 min. The injection volume for the samples was standardized at 1 μL, and the column oven temperature was consistently maintained at 45 °C, with a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min. A Q Exactive Plus Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) was utilized in both positive and negative ionization modes. Full-scan monitoring was conducted in conjunction with data-dependent MS2 (DDMS2) mode, with the mass range set at 100–1500 m/z. The resolution of the mass spectrometer was set to 70,000 for full scans and 17,500 for MS2 scans. The parameters for the heated electrospray ionization (H-ESI) source included a sheath gas flow rate of 45 arbitrary units (arb), an auxiliary gas flow rate of 10 arb, a sweep gas flow rate of 1 arb, a positive capillary voltage of 3.8 kV, a negative capillary voltage of 3.2 kV, an ion transfer tube and a capillary temperature of 350 °C. Data acquisition was facilitated via Trace Finder 4.1 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Nontargeted metabolite data were obtained via Compound Discoverer software version 3.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A comprehensive workflow for the Compound Discoverer analysis is presented in Table S2. The mean values of the data were calculated through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) from five separate determinations. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted with SIMCA, and heatmaps were generated via MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Sunburst distributions and stacked graphs were created with OriginPro® 2023 (OriginLab, USA).

Data clustering via unsupervised learning

Data preprocessing

Model generation and examination were conducted via scikit-learn, an open-source library in Python. For data preprocessing, the input data were initially processed with SimpleImputer to address missing values, and all numerical data were standardized through robust normalization. This approach was implemented to ensure equitable weighting of each dimension of the data. Dimensionality reduction techniques, including t-SNE, PCA, UMAP, ICA, and Isomap, were employed to transform the data into a three-dimensional space, facilitating effective visualization for clustering analysis.

Clustering algorithms

Six unsupervised clustering algorithms—K-means, agglomerative clustering, spectral clustering, balanced iterative reduction and clustering hierarchies (BIRCH), mini-batch K-means (MBKM), and bisecting K-means—were employed to identify hidden patterns within the metabolite profile data. Agglomerative clustering is a method that begins with each data point as an individual cluster and subsequently merges the two closest clusters until a specified termination condition is met, specifically until the desired number of clusters is achieved. The determination of the ‘closest’ cluster, or the method of measuring distance, is governed by three parameters: Ward, average, and complete linkage. BIRCH clustering incrementally constructs a clustering feature tree (CF-tree) using the data to establish a hierarchy, after which it applies the K-means algorithm or another clustering algorithm to cluster the leaf nodes of the CF-tree. This method is particularly advantageous because of its high performance and memory efficiency when handling large-scale datasets25. In contrast, spectral clustering is a technique based on graph theory that represents the similarities among data points as a graph and conducts clustering on the basis of the spectrum of this graph. This approach is especially effective in cases where the data distribution is nonlinear or where the shape of the clusters is irregular. Conversely, K-means clustering partitions a dataset into K clusters by assigning each data point to the nearest cluster center. Its simplicity makes it easy to understand and implement while also requiring relatively few computational resources. Bisecting K-means begins with the entire dataset as a single cluster and iteratively divides this cluster into two clusters via the K-means algorithm, ultimately resulting in the desired K clusters. Finally, MBKM is a modified version of the K-means algorithm designed to increase the efficiency of K-means clustering on large datasets. Unlike the basic K-means algorithm, which updates cluster centers via the entire dataset—an approach that can be computationally intensive and time-consuming—MBKM updates the cluster centers via the use of only random samples from the data.

Model construction and evaluation

Six clustering algorithms were applied, with the number of clusters set to various values ranging from 3 to 10. The optimal number of clusters was determined through performance evaluation. The clustering performance was assessed via the following metrics: the silhouette score measures how effectively each data point is clustered. The Calinski–Harabasz index (CH) is used to evaluate the separability between clusters and the cohesion within them. Additionally, the Davies–Bouldin index (DBI) assesses clustering quality by comparing the separability and cohesion of the clusters.

Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)

To assess and rank the significance of each variable and clarify its contribution to the predictions of the most effective model, we employed SHAP values, as described by Lundberg and Lee (2017)26. SHAP is a unified, model-agnostic explanatory framework grounded in cooperative game theory and Shapley values. It provides a consistent and theoretically sound method to quantify the contribution of each input variable to a given prediction27. After feature extraction, a supervised random forest (RF) classifier was trained using cluster labels derived from agglomerative clustering as pseudo-labels. To validate the model’s reliability, we performed five-fold stratified cross-validation, achieving high predictive performance. Based on this model, SHAP values were computed to determine the impact of each metabolite on the classification results. In SHAP analysis, the value for each input feature represents the average marginal contribution of that feature across all possible feature subsets. Results

Metabolite identification

Untargeted metabolites of C. bursa-pastoris extracts from different regions were analyzed via a UHPLC‒HRMS system. Distinctive fingerprints containing several diagnostic ions were observed in both the positive and negative ion spectra (Figs. S2, S3). The ability of the UHPLC‒HRMS system to generate chemical compositions rapidly has facilitated the identification of bioactive compounds, drugs, and other substances in various medicinal and horticultural plants.

Nontargeted profiling of metabolites from C. bursa-pastoris grown in various regions in Korea revealed 80 amino acids, 53 lipids, 21 nitrogen compounds, 10 organic acids, 6 organic compounds, 8 organic oxygen compounds, 94 phenol compounds, and 39 sulfur compounds (Fig. 1). Identification was achieved via reference standards, MS fragmentation, and literature data28–30. The mass spectrometric data for the compounds in the methanol extract of C. bursa-pastoris are presented in Table S3 according to their chemical families. In a previous study, 18 different amino acids, including glutamic acid, were identified in the 50% methanol extract of the aerial parts of the plant as primary metabolites31. Secondary metabolites have been studied more extensively, with seven phenolic compounds identified in an 80% methanol extract from the aerial parts of C. bursa-pastoris32. Additionally, five flavonoids, including quercetin-6-C-glucoside, were found in a 50% methanol extract31, whereas nine flavonoids, including quercetin and icaritin, were detected in an ethanol extract19. Furthermore, two flavonoids were identified in an ethyl acetate extract33. A comprehensive analysis revealed seven phenolic compounds and 16 flavonoids in an ethanol extract from the whole plant of C. bursa-pastoris10, along with 16 glucosinolates identified in a dimethyl sulfoxide extract34. Our study is significant because it provides an extensive investigation of the chemical composition of C. bursa-pastoris, identifying a total of 143 primary metabolites and 168 secondary metabolites, including those previously discovered in earlier studies.

Chemometrics of metabolites

We used the score plot of the PCA model for the initial classification of C. bursa-pastoris. This study analyzed the data structure and evaluated clustering via various techniques and dimensionality reduction methods to accurately identify relationships between samples on the basis of cultivation region. Techniques such as PCA and HCA were utilized to identify chemical similarities in samples, highlight distinctions, and identify chemical markers in the dataset. Metabolites identified at various stations in C. bursa-pastoris were assessed via chemometric analysis to determine their variations and correlations with phytochemicals. The PCA score plots accounted for 25.9% of the total variance (Fig. 2). The plots of positive and negative ionization clearly demonstrated separation between PC1 (13.0%) and PC2 (12.9%). Regional variations in the metabolite composition of C. bursa-pastoris were evident in the score plot (Fig. 2A), with most sample groups located in the 1st and 4th quadrants. However, groups J, L, and Q presented unique characteristics, showing no similarities with the other groups. The biplot graphical depiction of the PCA results provides a clear view of the marker distribution in C. bursa-pastoris across different regions (Fig. 2B). Flavones and flavonols, concentrated in the 1st and 2nd quadrants of the biplot, suggest associations with all groups except A and Q. Conversely, a unique distribution of glucosinolic acids and benzofurans in the 3rd quadrant indicates that they predominantly explain variations in group Q relative to other groups. Additionally, peptides represented in the third quadrant characterize groups J and L.

A hierarchical clustered heatmap was utilized to categorize the C. bursa‒pastoris samples by region on the basis of their metabolite concentration similarities (Fig. 2). The average concentration data from the five sampled prefectures in each region can be utilized to clearly identify trends in metabolite changes between the experimental groups. To identify nontargeted compounds contributing to the biological activity of regional C. bursa-pastoris extracts, a correlation analysis was conducted on all identified metabolites, including phenolic compounds, amino acids, and lipids. A comprehensive analysis of metabolite composition involved classifying amino acids, lipids, phenolic compounds, and other substances into their respective classes, followed by the validation of clustering among experimental groups. Notably, the amino acid composition indicated that the sample group could be divided into two clusters (I and II), with Cluster I showing lower concentrations of most amino acids, including proteinogenic amino acids and peptides, than Cluster II, where sample L was strongly associated with peptides (Fig. 2C). Additionally, lipid clusters were similarly divided into two groups, with tetraterpenes predominantly concentrated in Cluster I (Fig. 2D). Lipid Cluster I was associated with samples collected from adjacent coastal areas, such as E, F, I, and M, N, O, which exhibited similar patterns. Compared with the other material groups, the phenolic compounds presented a more complex composition (Fig. 2E). Cluster I, which encompassed the majority of the samples, was further divided into two subclusters. The first subcluster included samples M, N, and O from the southern coastal region, whereas the second subcluster included samples E, F, G, and I from the western coastal area, which revealed significant metabolite profile differences. Furthermore, inland-sourced samples D and K presented negative correlations with most phenolic compounds. Cluster II was characterized by the incorporation of only two inland samples, J and Q, which presented similar findings. The major flavonols, such as quercetin and kaempferol glycosides, were notably concentrated in sample J. In contrast, sample Q presented a distinctive profile characterized by elevated levels of specific phenolic compounds, including sinapic acid and m-coumaric acid, distinctly differentiating it from the other samples. The analysis of other compositional characteristics revealed two distinct clusters (Fig. 2F). Sample Q was particularly enriched in sulfur compounds and benzofurans, distinguishing it from the other samples. Specifically, Sample Q presented a significant concentration of sulfur compounds, including glucosinolic acid and isothiocyanic acid, which were present at low concentrations or entirely absent in the other samples. In cluster II, Samples E, F, and I presented similar compositional patterns, whereas the other samples each presented unique profiles.

Clustering of metabolites via unsupervised learning

Clustering is a fundamental aspect of data mining, involving the grouping of data into clusters on the basis of similarities in the features and characteristics of data points35,36. Recently, a variety of data clustering techniques have been developed to address different clustering challenges36,37. Clustering analysis techniques are generally classified into two principal categories: hierarchical clustering and partitional clustering38. This study investigates six unsupervised learning classifiers: agglomerative clustering, and BIRCH, which are classified under hierarchical clustering, and K-means, bisecting K-means clustering, spectral clustering, and MBKM, which are categorized as partitional clustering. While some degree of agreement was observed among certain clustering algorithms (e.g., Agglomerative and BIRCH), the grouping patterns were not entirely consistent across methods. For example, samples A and D were grouped together in Agglomerative and MiniBatch K-means, but separated in Spectral and Bisecting K-means (Table 1). This variation highlights the sensitivity of unsupervised clustering to algorithmic assumptions and parameterization39.

Analyzing a dataset necessitates an understanding of the number of groups present within the data objects40. Nonetheless, clustering is primarily an exploratory method, and importantly, there is no definitive or “correct” number of clusters41. The majority of clustering methodologies treat the number of clusters as a variable parameter that can be modified in accordance with the specific attributes of the dataset. Various algorithms are available for estimating the optimal number of clusters. The most commonly used algorithms include the silhouette score, CH, and DBI; consequently, this study also assessed the effectiveness of the clustering model utilizing these metrics. The silhouette score evaluates the quality of clustering for each data point. On the other hand, the CH and DBI are internal evaluation metrics that evaluate the degree of separation between clusters and the degree of cohesion within clusters to verify the effectiveness of the clustering process. The silhouette score ranges from –1 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating a more optimized number of clusters. Conversely, DBI approaches its minimum value of 0, which signifies higher clustering quality. Additionally, the CH increases as the clustering quality improves. To determine the optimal number of clusters (K), we evaluated clustering solutions for K = 3 to 10 using three internal validation metrics: silhouette score, CH, and DBI42–44. As shown in Fig. 3A, the silhouette score exhibited a clear elbow at K = 5, indicating a turning point beyond which increasing the number of clusters yielded diminishing improvements in cohesion and separation. The score peaked at K = 5 and then gradually declined, suggesting that additional clusters did not meaningfully enhance cluster quality. The DBI also showed a local minimum at K = 5, implying optimal compactness and separation of the clusters at that point. Although the CH score continued to increase with higher K values, this trend is known to occur even when cluster quality does not meaningfully improve, particularly in high-dimensional data42,44. Taken together, these metrics support K = 5 as the most appropriate solution, balancing performance across all three indices.

Representative agglomerative clustering analysis results of metabolites from Capsella bursa-pastoris across various regions: (A) silhouette score, Calinski–Harabasz score, and Davies–Bouldin index, (B) 3D plot depicting five clusters, (C) SHAP summary plot, (D) feature importance plot from random forest analysis.

Setting the number of clusters to at least 4 but less than 10 resulted in most classifiers grouping samples E, F, and I within the same cluster. Moreover, when all the algorithms were applied, samples H, M, N, and O were similarly clustered, confirming that samples from coastal areas have similar chemical properties. Conversely, samples from the western and southern coasts were distinctly different. Additionally, samples C, J, L, and A, D, collected from the continental zone, were grouped together, indicating differences in plant material composition between coastal and continental regions. Nevertheless, no significant variations were detected in sample compositions from different continental regions. These findings consistently indicate that the composition of functional substances in C. bursa-pastoris varies with environmental factors in inland and coastal areas. Based on this, we visualized the clustering results in three dimensions (Fig. 3B) and investigated the ecological relevance of the five resulting clusters.

To simplify the modeling process and enhance detection efficiency, we implemented feature importance analysis via the SHAP summary plot (Fig. 3C). This method uses an algorithmic model to identify critical features and discard data that minimally impact or detract from classification, which reduces the interference of redundant information and improves model accuracy. As a result, it not only reduces the computational load but also speeds up detection. The first 20 weighted features were extracted via the selected agglomerative model. To identify key metabolites that differentiate the five clusters, we trained a supervised random forest classifier using agglomerative clustering labels as pseudolabels. Stratified five-fold cross-validation yielded perfect performance (accuracy and F1 score = 1.00) using a pipeline that included robust normalization and median imputation (Fig. S4). The metabolites with the most substantial influence were cytosine, a nitrogen compound; L-pipecolic acid, an amino acid; and benzoic acid, a hydroxybenzoic acid. The substances affecting the clustering model predictions included 8 amino acids, 4 lipids, 4 phenols, 3 nitrogen compounds, and 1 sulfur compound. For clustering analysis, only selected feature bands were used, minimizing redundant information, saving computational time, and more effectively capturing key spectral features.

To further validate the cluster-level variation in metabolite expression, we visualized the relative abundances of the top 20 SHAP-identified metabolites using boxplots (Fig. S5). These plots revealed distinct distribution patterns across the five unsupervised clusters for most metabolites, with several compounds (e.g., Ala-Met, jaceosidin) showing marked differences in abundance between specific clusters. The consistency between statistical significance (ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD) and visual separability reinforces the interpretation that these metabolites contribute meaningfully to the biochemical stratification of Capsella bursa-pastoris according to geographical origin.

Additionally, the feature importance for all substances was validated via the random forest model (Fig. 3D). In this graph, the bar length indicates the importance of each feature, excluding substances without importance data. The primary metabolites that significantly influenced the model predictions were L-pipecolic acid, Gly-Pro, and cytosine, a pyrimidine base, which was consistent with the SHAP summary plot findings. Additionally, several secondary metabolites, including 2′-hydroxyflexixanthin (a tetraterpenoid) and phenolic compounds such as 2-(hexopyranosyloxy) benzoic acid, diosmetin 7-O-rutinoside, and chrysoeriol 7-O-glucoside, were also identified among the top features. Notably, 13 amino acid substances ranked among the top 30 (10%) features, highlighting that the type and concentration of amino acids significantly impact the metabolite composition variations of C. bursa-pastoris across different cultivation areas.

Discussion

Metabolomics of Capsella bursa-pastoris according to cultivation region

C. bursa-pastoris is widely distributed across various global regions and is cultivated throughout South Korea. This species is classified as a winter annual plant that is typical of temperate climates and serves as a root vegetable crop. This phenomenon represents a robust mechanism for cold adaptation, enabling plants to grow and produce seeds under low-temperature conditions9,45. Plants subjected to cold stress exhibit the upregulation of antioxidant gene synthesis de novo8. Additionally, low temperatures markedly affect plants by altering the protein-to-lipid ratio in the membrane system and altering fatty acid composition46,47. Prior studies have indicated that the size and shape of C. bursa-pastoris leaves often vary depending on the temperature and soil moisture of the cultivation area, affecting their functions and chemical compositions48–50. Notably, C. bursa-pastoris exhibits unique chemical complexity, making this diverse material a valuable resource for the discovery of bioactive compounds. Hence, evaluating C. bursa-pastoris from different regions in Korea, analyzing its composition, and exploring potential applications are essential. In this study, we compared the differences in the compositions of primary and secondary metabolites in C. bursa-pastoris extracts from 20 regions in Korea to investigate the variations in bioactive compounds based on the plant growth environment. LC‒MS analysis confirmed significant variations in the metabolite compositions of C. bursa-pastoris depending on the region of cultivation, even when they were grown and harvested concurrently.

The environmental conditions of the cultivation area significantly affect the composition of primary and secondary metabolites in plants. In the case of C. bursa-pastoris, the clustering patterns of metabolite composition and concentration were strongly correlated with the geographical location of the cultivation site. Regional differences among samples were identified primarily through PCA in both modes. The samples harvested from inland areas such as Seoul, Mungyeong, and Gochang were significantly different from the C. bursa-pastoris samples harvested in coastal cities. However, no significant differences were detected between samples grown on the west coast and those grown on the south coast in the score plot. Additionally, to examine the correlation between regional characteristics and metabolite composition, all metabolites were classified into classes such as amino acids, lipids, and phenolic compounds and then analyzed via a hierarchical clustering heatmap. Upon examining the correlation of each metabolite concentration with the sample group, C. bursa-pastoris from inland areas such as Yesan and Gochang were particularly distinct from other regions, containing high concentrations of sulfur compounds such as glucosinolic acid and isothiocyanic acid. Furthermore, samples harvested from Seosan and Taean on the west coast and Namhae on the south coast presented consistently similar characteristics. Six clustering algorithms were subsequently applied to identify the primary factors considered in the clustering of sample groups and to utilize them for model prediction. Three samples from the southern coastal region and four from the western coastal region were closely related when the number of common clusters was less than 4–10 in both positive and negative ion modes. Conversely, samples from inland regions such as Seoul, Chungju, Gochang, and Iksan were similar, but no commonality was identified on the basis of inland location. In summary, the composition and concentration of the samples growing near the western coastal region with high rainfall were markedly different from those of the samples growing in inland or mountainous regions. These geographical differences may be linked to the diversity of primary and secondary metabolites, possibly due to variations in rainfall, temperature, and soil characteristics.

Finally, the most prominent peaks among the 311 identified substances were assessed, major metabolites were selected, and the peak areas of the ion chromatograms were calculated to estimate their concentrations (Fig. 4). Four amino acids (L-isoleucine, L-phenylalanine, L-proline and L-tryptophan), one phosphoglycerate, two organic acids (maleic acid and malic acid), nine phenolic compounds (including one hydroxybenzoic acid, one hydroxycinnamic acid, three flavones, and four flavonols), and four sulfur compounds (two glucosinolic acids, one isothiocyanic acid, and one nitrile) were identified as key metabolites, with samples being compared primarily on the basis of the concentrations of these secondary metabolites. The composition of key metabolites revealed substantial variability across the majority of the samples. Notably, the abundance of sulfur compounds was significantly influenced by the specific cultivation area. Brassicaceae plants, known for their richness in glucosinolates, can be enzymatically hydrolyzed by myrosinase or converted into nitriles or isothiocyanates, depending on factors such as pH and temperature, and have sulfur compounds that are located primarily in the roots rather than the leaves34. The observed differences in the contents of sulfur compounds among the samples suggest potential variations in the proportions of the roots of the plants. Although all the samples were harvested simultaneously, the growth rates and ratios of roots to aerial parts varied with temperature, climate, and soil conditions in the cultivation area, leading to significant differences in specific sulfur compound concentrations. In samples obtained from coastal regions, the concentrations of 3-indolylmethyl glucosinolic acid and 9-methylsulfinylnonyl isothiocyanic acid were significantly lower than those observed in samples from inland areas (K, Q, R). When the nutrient content in the soil is sufficient, plant root growth is suppressed by carbohydrates, which increases the allocation of organic resources to aerial parts and reproductive structures51,52. This mechanism suggests that once nutrient levels in the roots reach an optimal threshold, a greater proportion of nutrients are redirected to the leaves and stems, thereby increasing their growth. Consequently, C. bursa-pastoris from coastal regions display more developed aerial parts than those from inland areas. It is hypothesized that inland samples develop more root hairs in response to relatively low nutrient concentrations53,54.

Total accumulation of key metabolites in Capsella bursa-pastoris from different regions.

For phenolic compounds, samples collected from coastal regions presented minimal regional variation, whereas those obtained from inland areas presented prominent regional differences. The cumulative graphs depicting the major phenolic compounds in geographically similar locations, such as the samples from Seosan and Taean (E, F, I) and those from the South Sea (M, N, O), were nearly identical, as anticipated. Previous studies have identified the methanol extract of C. bursa-pastoris as containing flavonoids, including apigenin, luteolin, quercetin, and kaempferol, as well as phenolic compounds10,19,31,33. The major compositions of the phenolic compounds observed in this study align with these findings. In contrast, among the proteinogenic amino acids, L-isoleucine and L-phenylalanine did not exhibit significant regional differences; however, L-proline concentrations were notably higher in inland areas than in coastal regions. Specifically, the southern coast displayed a marked difference from the inland areas, corroborating previous studies that indicated a lower proline content in plants grown without exposure to cold stress than in those that were exposed to cold stress55. Proline accumulation occurs in various plant species under diverse stress conditions, functioning as a hydroxyl radical scavenger and serving as a source of energy and nitrogen56. It is also recognized as a reliable indicator of plant stress tolerance. Furthermore, all amino acids serve as precursors in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. For example, glucosinolates can be derived from methionine, alanine, and branched-chain and aromatic amino acids57. Consequently, variations in amino acid composition among samples from different cultivation regions may influence the concentration of sulfur compounds, such as glucosinolates.

To verify the hypothesis that environmental factors influence the metabolites of Capsella bursa-pastoris, the effects of specific environmental variables on key metabolites were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation (Fig. S6). The results showed that temperature, humidity, and precipitation were strongly correlated with the accumulation patterns of distinct metabolite classes in Capsella bursa-pastoris.

Interestingly, sulfur-containing glucosinolates, such as 10-methylsulfinyl-decanenitrile and 9-methylsulfinyl isothiocyanate, exhibited negative correlations with temperature but positive correlations with humidity. This suggests that cooler temperatures may promote their biosynthesis, which could partially explain their elevated levels in inland populations. However, their positive association with humidity indicates that other environmental factors, such as atmospheric moisture or pathogen pressure common in coastal habitats, may also induce their accumulation. Therefore, the regional abundance of these sulfur compounds likely reflects a combined influence of multiple environmental variables, and their distribution cannot be attributed solely to root-related drought adaptation or a single abiotic factor. Flavonoid glycosides—particularly quercetin, kaempferol, and luteolin derivatives—were more abundant in coastal samples. Although their Spearman correlations with environmental variables were moderate, their levels increased under warmer and more humid conditions, suggesting inducibility by such abiotic stimuli and a role as photoprotective antioxidants58. In contrast, inland populations exhibited elevated levels of L-proline, an osmoprotectant amino acid, which showed weak negative correlations with temperature (ρ = –0.21) and precipitation (ρ = –0.34), consistent with stress-induced accumulation under drier, hotter inland conditions59. Notably, L-phenylalanine and L-proline were inversely correlated (ρ = –0.37), indicating possible competition or trade-offs in metabolic flux under abiotic stress. These findings correspond well with the regional metabolite distributions shown in Fig. 4 and provide a mechanistic basis for how C. bursa-pastoris adapts its metabolome in response to local ecological constraints.

The data presented in this study support the idea that cultivation conditions such as soil type, rainfall, temperature, and harvest time significantly influence the chemical composition of plant metabolites. Furthermore, the phytochemical profiles were correlated with the geographical location of the cultivation site, suggesting that a clustering method could be proposed for the classification of plant samples. Using phytochemical data from C. bursa-pastoris cultivated in various regions of Korea, a fundamental classification was achieved through statistical methods to identify similarities among the samples. However, the application of clustering algorithms enables more accurate model predictions by facilitating the extraction of metabolites with significant functions, followed by the clustering of the samples. Moreover, we propose that further studies utilizing quantitative data on key bioactive compounds through metabolomic profiling of C. bursa-pastoris may enhance these findings.

Conclusion

Metabolites derived from Capsella bursa-pastoris samples cultivated across 20 distinct regions in Korea were identified and utilized for quality assurance and classification purposes. UHPLC‒HRMS‒based untargeted metabolomics in both positive and negative ion modes enabled comprehensive metabolite screening. Metabolite identification was conducted through retention time, mass accuracy, isotope patterns, and MS/MS fragmentation analysis, resulting in the identification of 311 primary and secondary metabolites. The samples were classified using six unsupervised clustering algorithms based on their metabolite concentrations. The clustering results showed strong alignment with the geographical origins of the cultivation sites. Notably, C. bursa-pastoris grown in inland regions exhibited elevated levels of sulfur-containing compounds such as glucosinolic acid and isothiocyanic acid, distinguishing them from samples cultivated in other areas. Furthermore, samples from western and southern coastal regions shared consistent metabolic characteristics, reinforcing the influence of environmental factors such as precipitation, temperature, and soil properties on the chemical composition of C. bursa-pastoris. Unsupervised learning methods, particularly clustering algorithms, proved effective in classifying plant samples based on their phytochemical profiles and cultivation regions. These approaches offer robust and interpretable tools for uncovering structure and patterns in high-dimensional metabolomic datasets, complementing conventional statistical analyses. We hypothesize that incorporating additional metabolite variables in future models may further enhance the discriminatory power of such classification frameworks. Overall, our study provides valuable insights into how modern analytical techniques can be applied to achieve region-specific phytochemical profiling and support the traceability of bioactive plant materials.
