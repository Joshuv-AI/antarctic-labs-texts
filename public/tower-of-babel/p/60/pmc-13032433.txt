The effect of muscle examination findings on determining conservative treatment options for temporomandibular joint disorders
Authors: Tuğgen Özcivelek, Fulya Basmacı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTemporomandibular joint disorders are common in the community. Identifying the relationship between specific painful examination sites and the choice of conservative treatment methods may have predictive value in determining treatment guidelines. This study aims to investigate the possible effect of pain findings at specific examination sites on determining the selection of clinical decision rules.Materials and methodsData were retrospectively analyzed from 1020 patients who presented to a specialty clinic with complaints of pain, movement restrictions, or joint sounds in the temporomandibular joint, temple, and facial regions. Examination findings from 12 palpation sites and administered conservative treatment methods were extracted from diagnostic and treatment records. The binary logistic regression model using a backward stepwise method was constructed to evaluate the contribution of each pain site to each treatment choice. Canonical correlation analysis, based on the optimal scaling approach examined the relationships between two sets of dichotomous variables.ResultsStrong correlation was not found between the distribution of painful palpation sites and the selection of conservative treatments sufficient to establish clinical treatment guidelines. Pain at certain examination sites significantly influenced the cold-hot application, interocclusal splint, and pharmacological treatment recommendations (p < 0.05). The odds of receiving these treatments varied concerning specific pain sites.ConclusionsWhile palpation findings are crucial for diagnosis, they do not reliably guide the selection of conservative treatment methods.Clinical RelevanceAlthough essential for diagnosis, palpation-induced pain findings are not predictive in determining treatment methods. Further studies are needed to elucidate evidence-based clinical decision-making mechanisms for temporomandibular joint disorders.Keywords: Temporomandibular joint disorders, Physical examination, Conservative treatment, Treatment protocols, Treatment guidelines, Clinical decision-making

Introduction

Temporomandibular joint disorders (TMDs) are a group of disorders that affect the masticatory muscles, facial structures, and temporomandibular joints (TMJs), with an estimated prevalence of approximately 10% in the population over the age of 18 [1]. Despite this high percentage, only 5% of TMD patients seek treatment options [2], which might be related to the occasionally asymptomatic nature of the disease [1]. Helkimo [3], Greene and Marbach [4], Rugh and Soleberg [5], Carlsson [6] and LeResche [1] defined TMD, and in 1992 Dworkin and LeResche introduced the Research Diagnostic Criteria for TMD (RDC TMD) protocol for standardization of examination and diagnosis [7]. It was revised in 2014 as the DC/TMD examination protocol and diagnostic criteria, to improve specificity and sensitivity [8].

However, these clinical assessments and diagnostic methods do not directly contribute to the decision of clinical therapeutic approaches [1, 9]. The diversity of etiological factors which may include trauma, sensitization of nociceptive pathways, anatomic, physiologic, structural, postural, psychosocial, and genetic factors [10–14] requires a variety of conservative and/or invasive treatment options provided for the patients [15]. Conservative treatments include patient education, behavioral changes aimed to eliminate the etiological factor, creating cognitive awareness by progressive relaxation techniques, psychological counseling, pharmacological management with muscle relaxants and/or anti-inflammatory drugs, and physical therapy methods. Physical therapy options may involve exercise programs, muscle trigger point injections, cold/heat applications, and low-level laser therapy [16]. Additionally, intraoral appliance therapy is commonly employed. Invasive procedures such as arthrocentesis, arthroscopy, or open joint surgery may be considered for cases unresponsive to conservative approaches [2, 17–19]. The initial treatment for TMD recommended by the American Association for Dental Research guidelines is to explain the condition to the patient and proceed with reversible conservative treatment modalities [20]. In clinical practice, treatment plans are individualized for each patient considering etiological factors, and different treatment methods can be combined to optimize outcomes.

Researchers have sought to identify clinical situations that could contribute to the development of a decision tree, attempting to specify initial conservative treatment options that are effective in treating TMD and to develop TMD patient management strategies and practice guidelines [16, 20, 21]. Lövgren et al. attempted to differentiate patients who needed further TMD evaluation and treatment, with the 3Q/TMD questionnaire, which screened for jaw pain, pain during function, and catching and/or locking more than once in the week prior to the examination [22]. However, no significant relationship was observed between gender, age, the fee system to which the individual was assigned to, and prescribed TMD treatments. Although affirmative answers to the 3Q/TMD were related to TMD treatment, most of screen-positive individuals did not receive further assessment or treatment according to dental records [23]. Similarly, Nasstrom et al. reported no decisive correlation between 3Q/TMD responses and the treatment modalities applied [21].

The impact of examination findings that might have on determining the treatment protocol needs to be further investigated to provide insight into the treatment of patients with TMD. Therefore, in the current study, performed or recommended TMD treatments were investigated retrospectively to evaluate if muscle examination findings could predict the choice of conservative therapeutic options. This cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the effects of palpation-induced pain at each examination site and the distribution of simultaneously painful multiple sites on the same side of the face, on the choice of treatments given in patients presenting with TMD. The null hypothesis assumed that twelve examined individual pain sites, ipsilateral temporal and masseter muscles being painful together, or at least three simultaneously painful examination sites on the same side of the face do not have a decisive influence on the treatments given.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval for this study was obtained from the Health Sciences University Noninterventional Research Ethics Committee on 26.11.2019 with reference number 19/391. The protocol conformed to the criteria of the Helsinki Declaration.

Study subjects and data collection

In this study, data from 1020 patients who presented to a TMD specialty clinic between October 2017 and October 2018 with complaints of pain in TMJ, temporal, and facial region, movement restriction, or joint sounds were evaluated. The anatomical sites were examined with digital pressure mentioned as in RDC/TMD criteria by a single examiner who practiced the video-based examination training videos recommended by the international consortium [24–26]. Data on examination findings included palpation-induced pain in the bilateral temporal (T), masseter (M), lateral pterygoid (LP), sternocleidomastoid (SCM), posterior cervical (PC) muscles, and TMJs [7, 26]. Data on administered treatment methods such as Jacob’s progressive relaxation technique (JPRT), patient education (PE), cold/hot pack compress application (C/HP), jaw mobility exercises (EX), interocclusal splint (IOS), and pharmacologic treatment (PT) (muscle relaxant and/or anti-inflammatory medication) were present in the surveyed diagnosis and treatment forms. In this series, patients were advised to follow a daily JPRT exercise video until they reached a level of relaxation where they could practice the technique without the video. The demeanor that the patients should pay attention to was included in the patient education forms, such as consuming a soft diet, avoiding hard foods, chewing gum, and parafunctional behaviors such as holding a pen between the teeth and nail-biting. In addition, patients were advised to stand in a neutral postural position and relax their muscles when they noticed bruxism or clenching, and, not to lean on their jaws which would cause the jaw to be directed or stressed. These are consistent with the self-care practices recommended in the literature for initial treatment [18]. The treatment options applied to patients varied depending on the diagnosis and etiologic factors detected in the history and examination of the patient.

Patients who had pain in at least one of the examined areas on palpation were included in the study group. Patients with incompletely filled examination forms and patients without palpation-induced pain were not included to 1020 patients in the study. Exclusion criteria were the patients younger than 18 years of age (n = 49), and the patients who were diagnosed with trigeminal neuralgia, atypical facial pain, burning mouth syndrome, migraine, fibromyalgia, atypical odontalgia, neurological or neuropathic pain (n = 23) [27]. Seventy-two patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded from the study. A total of 948 patients meeting these criteria were included in the study.

Statistical analysis

The relationships between the painful examination sites and the treatment options of 948 patients were statistically analyzed. The relationship was assessed for each examination site (TMJ, LP, T, M, SCM, PC) separately and for the two groups. Simultaneously painful temporal and masseter muscles on the same side of the face (T + M) and any three painful examination sites on the same side of the face (MYO) were grouped for analysis.

Painful sites were defined as independent variables, whereas administered treatments served as dependent variables. A binary logistic regression model with a backward stepwise method was constructed to evaluate the contribution of each pain site or group to prescribed treatments. The effect of a one-unit change in a painful site on the probability of a treatment being administered was examined while controlling for all significant independent variables in the model. Variables found significant in the univariate analysis were entered into multivariate models, and after applying backward stepwise selection, only the variables remaining significant in the multivariate analysis were reported.

In addition, canonical correlation analysis based on the optimal scaling approach (SPSS Version 22.0, IBM Corp., NY) was performed to assess the multivariate dependence between the two sets of dichotomous variables. This method was chosen to provide an overarching view of the overall association pattern between the groups, complementing the logistic regression results that focused on the strength and significance of individual associations.

The data were summarized as the mean±standard deviation (median, range) and frequency (percentage) for scale, and categorical variables, respectively. The similarities of the variable sets were furtherly evaluated by the simple correspondence analysis constructing a correspondence table. The “ca.” R package [28] was used to calculate the mass of each point (x1000), the inertia of the point, the contribution of the point to the inertia of dimension (crt), and the contribution of dimension to the inertia of the point (cor) values. The maximum number of dimensions was set to 5, determined as one less than the number of variables in the smaller variable set.

The statistical significance level was accepted as p < 0.05 for all analyses. These comprehensive methods sought for a detailed information about the association between painful examination findings and the choice of conservative treatment modalities.

The effect of each painful examination site on the given treatments was examined. Relationships between the painful sites and the JPRT, PE, and EX treatments were not significant (p > 0.05). The presence of pain in the examined areas had a statistically significant effect on the C/HP, IOS, and PT recommendations (p < 0.05).

Table 3 shows the probability of an event occurring based on a one-unit change in an independent variable while controlling for the effects of all other independent variables in the model. The effect of the presence of pain at the LP, M, and SCM sites on the recommendation of C/HP was statistically significant (pLP<0.001, pM<0.001, pSCM=0.003). When controlling for all other independent variables in the model, the odds of receiving C/HP were 2.70 times greater for individuals with pain at the LP site compared to those without pain at the LP site. Similarly, those reporting pain at the M and SCM sites were 2.57 and 1.99 times more likely to receive C/HP, respectively. The effect of simultaneous pain in at least three sites on the same side of the face on the application of IOS was also statistically significant (pMYO=0.006). Patients experiencing pain in at least three areas on the same side were 1.56 times the odds of receiving IOS compared to those with pain in less than three sites on the same side of the face. The presence of pain at the T site significantly influenced the recommendation for PT (pT<0.001). Patients with pain at the T site had 12.13 times the odds of receiving PT, compared to those without pain at the T site.

The association between two categorical variable groups, pain site (joint/muscle) and type of treatment, was evaluated. The principal inertias suggested a moderate level of relationship in the five-dimensional solution (0.522–0.696), accounting for 57.5% (2.875/5) of the total variation. However, the overall chi-square test of inertia was not statistically significant (χ² = 17.304, p = 0.995). Accordingly, there was no statistically significant association between the pain site and the provided type of treatment.

Discussion

Examination findings and the recommended treatments of patients who admitted to a specialist TMD clinic were reviewed retrospectively in this cross-sectional study. The predictive effect of examination findings at specific palpation sites on the selection of conservative treatment methods was evaluated. The null hypothesis was accepted as none of the painful examination sites, pain in the T and M muscles together on the same side, or simultaneous pain findings on at least three sites on the same side had a determining effect on the treatments given.

The diagnosis of TMD via the RDC/TMD criteria is primarily based on the signs and symptoms of the disease rather than etiological factors [9]. However, etiological factors should be taken into consideration in selecting treatment options so that treatments that eliminate the causes should be preferred. Clinical decision-making is a complex process that can be influenced by various factors [16, 20, 29]. In clinical settings, practitioners may have inconsistencies in the management of TMDs. Although subjective pain findings are considered for treatment decisions, practitioners’ perspective, varying knowledge, and experience affect the reproducibility, validity, and treatment outcomes. Treatment options must be individualized while developing guidelines that consider different cultural, social, educational, and healthcare requirements, control other variables, and allow for more consistent approaches [15, 29]. The main goal is eliminating the pain and movement restriction, and then maintaining the condition as long as possible without recurrence. The preliminary treatment methods for acute and chronic TMDs focus on addressing the source or cause of the patient’s problem. They should be noninvasive, reversible, and they should include measures to prevent the progression of the disease [30, 31]. Conservative methods such as self-management treatments, behavioral changes aiming to eliminate the factors that cause the disease, and physiotherapy are primarily recommended [30]. Other conservative treatments include pharmacological treatments, exercise regimens, posture training, and low-level laser therapy [10, 15, 16, 29, 32]. In some cases, IOS may be used for limited periods, however, indication of surgical approaches are quite limited [15, 29]. These treatment modalities may be recommended to each patient in varying combinations based on individual needs. The course of these diseases is adversely affected by stress and daily parafunctional habits. An increase in anxiety and depression scores has been correlated with an increase in TMD symptoms [33–35].

In this study, the JPRT, PE, and EX treatments were not related to the presence of pain at the examined sites. The majority of the patients (95.7%) admitted to the clinic were informed about joint health protection measures and received written patient education forms to help them prevent TMDs. The small number of patients who did not receive patient education may have been diagnosed with conditions other than TMD and subsequently referred to other clinics.

In the literature, JPRT and PE are primarily recommended for patients who apply to specialty clinics for TMD management [36]. Cognitive behavioral changes and the regular practice of JPRT have been reported to reduce the risk factors and frequency of symptoms associated with TMD [37]. Therefore, it is not surprising that the pain conditions examined in the joints and muscles did not significantly influence the administration of JPRT, and PE in this study. Low-cost self-exercises have been reported to be effective in reducing pain and improving the jaw opening range, but to achieve these benefits the patient should be correctly informed about the disease and must be ready to make necessary behavioral changes [10, 37].

Jaw mobility exercises are usually given to increase or maintain mobility in cases where mouth opening is restricted due to disc derangement [38, 39]. Therefore, it is an expected result that there was no correlation between palpation-induced pain and the EX treatment method. In this study, mobility exercises were performed at the clinic for patients with restricted jaw movements and patients were demonstrated to perform these exercises also at home. Clinical findings were reevaluated at monthly follow-up appointments, although attendance at these appointments was variable. The reasons for this situation can be investigated in future studies.

In this study, it was observed that C/HP, IOS, and PT were administered more frequently when palpation-induced pain was present at various muscle examination sites. When all other independent variables were controlled, the odds of receiving C/HP were 2.70 times higher for those with pain at the LP site than for those without pain at the LP site. Similarly, those who reported pain at the M and SCM sites were 2.57 and 1.99 times more likely to receive C/HP, respectively. Since LP functions to depress the jaw, pain in the LP muscle may be associated with the limitation of mandibular movement. The restricted jaw range detected in clinical examination might be related to LP muscle spasm or acute disc displacement without reduction where C/HP can be used [18]. In this series, C/HP was recommended depending on the pain site, and the presumed etiology of the patients as in the literature.

The effectiveness of the given treatments was evaluated by randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses in the literature [40–42]. Treatment options need to be diversified according to the variety of etiological factors in TMDs. The concurrent application of multiple treatment options may contribute to patient recovery in a shorter period. However, to understand the contribution of each treatment to recovery, only one method needs to be administered and investigated. If studies are planned to question the effectiveness of only one treatment method such as the IOS, ethical concerns may arise since the treatment options for the patient’s benefit might be restricted. It is difficult to determine the sham or placebo treatments for comparisons, so, they are compared with different splint types [43], education [44] or exercise [39]. There is no high-quality evidence that there is a significant difference between occlusal splint treatment and exercise treatments in clinical effectiveness [40]. In the current study, the recommendation of the IOS was 1.56 times more likely when there was palpation-induced pain in at least three sites on the same side of the face (MYO).

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, muscle relaxants, corticosteroids, hyaluronic acid, opioids, antidepressants, sedatives, and hypnotics can be prescribed to patients according to different etiologic factors such as inflammation, muscle spasm, anxiety, depression, and neuropathic causes [19]. Analgesics may often be prescribed to patients with acute TMDs [29]. In this study, the examiner was a prosthodontist, and patients were referred to other physicians for neurological or psychological evaluations. Therefore, the prescribed drugs were limited to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and muscle relaxants, and the study results should be interpreted accordingly. In this study, PT was applied to 48 of the 948 patients, with those experiencing pain at the T site being 12.13 times more likely to receive PT than those without T-site pain. Palpation-induced pain at the T site, often associated with headaches, may have led to the prescription of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs to alleviate symptoms. Although medication is not a definitive solution for TMD, it can offer relieve from the pain and discomfort [45].

A limitation of this study is that the study considered only treatments available at the prosthodontics clinic, excluding those referred to other departments such as physical therapy, or oral and maxillofacial surgery. Therefore, some treatment options were not explored. On the other hand, the strength of this study is that the findings of pain on palpation obtained by examining a great number of patients, such as 1020, were analyzed in terms of their relationship with the conservative treatments given to the patients. Future research may include a broader range of multidisciplinary treatment approaches, including referrals to other specialized clinics.

Conclusions

Although palpation findings are crucial in diagnosing TMD, the distribution of painful sites does not appear to significantly guide the selection of treatment methods. However, the presence of pain in the assessed sites had a notable impact on the recommendation of C/HP, IOS, and PT. The likelihood of these treatments being suggested varied depending on the specific pain sites involved. Further research is needed to improve the understanding of the clinical decision-making processes.
