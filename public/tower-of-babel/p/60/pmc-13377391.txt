Socioeconomic Disparities in Lung Cancer Screening Participation
Authors: Sara Delilovic, Diego Yacaman Mendez, Nina Markholm Nordgren, Gunnar Wagenius, Anne Richter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study evaluates whether sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors are associated with participation across stages in a risk-based lung cancer screening pilot program in Sweden.

Introduction

The global impact of lung cancer remains significant. Disparities in lung cancer incidence and mortality persist across demographic groups, largely reflecting unequal exposure to tobacco smoking and other key risk factors. Socioeconomically disadvantaged groups bear a disproportionate burden.1,2,3,4 Among European women, the incidence is rising, with mortality projected to exceed that of breast cancer, and in Sweden women represent the majority of lung cancer cases. Gender-specific screening data remain scarce, as most trials have predominantly enrolled men.5

Screening for Lung Cancer

Evidence shows that targeted screening of heavy smokers, both current and former, using low-dose computed tomography (LDCT), leads to detection in early stages of the disease progression and reduces mortality.6,7 As early as 2011, the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) demonstrated that screening with LDCT reduced lung cancer mortality.8 The NELSON study, a landmark trial in the field of lung cancer screening, assessed the effectiveness of LDCT screening in reducing lung cancer mortality among high-risk individuals.6,9

Addressing Health Inequalities

The substantial burden of lung cancer has catalyzed the development of comprehensive risk-based screening initiatives across the European Union. In 2022, The European Commission established ambitious targets for lung cancer screening implementation.10,11,12 Lung cancer disproportionately affects socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, who often have higher smoking rates and face additional barriers to health care access.4,10,11 Addressing health inequalities is therefore central to lung cancer screening initiatives, yet paradoxically, those at elevated risk, including individuals with lower socioeconomic status and current smokers, are least likely to participate in initial recruitment phases.4,13,14 Given this paradoxical relationship, it becomes essential to assess the feasibility of screening programs specifically designed to reach high-risk populations.

While Sweden does not yet have a national lung cancer screening program, several regions have launched ongoing pilot projects.15,16 This study investigates whether sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors are associated with participation in 1 of the Swedish risk-based lung cancer screening pilot programs.

Method

Study Setting and Sample

The pilot project aimed to assess the feasibility of questionnaire-based screening for lung cancer detection among women with smoking history.5 Women aged 55 to 74 years (including women who turned 55 during the invitation year, meaning some were 54 at the time of invitation) in 1 hospital service area in Stockholm County were invited to participate in a questionnaire on their smoking habits. This approach was chosen to reach a large and defined population. The age range of 55 to 74 years was informed by a health economic analysis commissioned by Region Stockholm, which found this interval to be the most cost-effective. Broader age intervals were associated with substantially higher costs relative to marginal health gains.17

Inclusion criteria for further investigation with LDCT were based on the NELSON criteria and included individuals who reported smoking at least 15 cigarettes per day for a minimum of 25 years, or at least 10 cigarettes per day for at least 30 years, and who were either current daily smokers or former daily smokers who had quit within the past 10 years. Exclusion criteria were smoking cessation of 10 or more years, ongoing lung cancer treatment, or regular chest CT.

Invitation letters were physically mailed once to each potential participant with no follow-up reminders (questionnaire provided in the eAppendix in Supplement 1). Eligible women were automatically assigned an LDCT appointment with no formal shared decision-making, additional patient education, or communication to primary care physicians beyond the initial study information letter. Smoking cessation support was available via self-referral to the Swedish National Tobacco Quitline. All examinations were performed at Karolinska University Hospital, where women diagnosed with lung cancer were also managed within specialized thoracic oncology care. The pilot started on the initiative of Stockholm Region and was conducted by the Regional Cancer Centre Stockholm Gotland (RCC SG) in collaboration with Karolinska University Hospital. The follow-up period extended from September 2022 to May 2024. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies. The study has been approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board, Stockholm with a waiver of informed consent because obtaining it would involve a disproportionate effort.

Data Sources

This study combines data from 2 sources: the Regional Health Control (RHK) system and registry data. Data were linked using unique personal identification numbers and were subsequently pseudonymized. Data from the RHK system included information collected within the pilot study conducted by RCC SG, including age, response to the initial questionnaire, eligibility for screening with LDCT, and attendance at the baseline LDCT examination. Attendance referred specifically to the single scheduled baseline scan following eligibility confirmation; subsequent follow-up LDCTs for intermediate findings were not included.

Registry data included sociodemographic and socioeconomic variables and were obtained from Statistics Sweden’s Longitudinal Integration Database for Health Insurance and Labour Market Studies (LISA). From LISA, we extracted information on income, educational attainment, marital status, region of origin, and demographic statistical areas (DESO) for the year 2022.

Study Variables and Outcome

For each screening stage, we examined sociodemographic and socioeconomic differences in 3 binary outcomes: questionnaire participation, NELSON-based LDCT eligibility and LDCT attendance. These outcomes together capture the sequential nature of participation across the screening pathway.

Sociodemographic and Socioeconomic Individual- and Area-Level Covariates

Individual-Level Variables

Sociodemographic and socioeconomic variables were derived from registry data corresponding to the year 2022. Income was defined as annual disposable income based on total household income, including wages, welfare benefits, other social subsidies, and pensions, and was categorized into tertiles (lowest, middle, and highest). Marital status was categorized as married or cohabiting, single, or divorced or widowed. Education attainment was categorized as presecondary education (equating to up to 9 years of education), secondary (10-12 years), and postsecondary education (≥12 years). The categories correspond to the Swedish school system. Region of origin was used as a proxy for migration background and categorized into 4 groups: Sweden, other Nordic countries, non-Nordic European countries, and countries outside Europe.

Area-Level Variables

A Living Conditions Index was used to highlight differences between residential areas based on social and economic circumstances that affect residents’ living conditions. The index provides a comparable picture of the social and economic characteristics of different residential areas. Seven indicators reflecting living conditions are included: proportion of individuals who have been unemployed in the past year, proportion with presecondary education, proportion of single parents, proportion of foreign-born residents, median income, and mean living space. For each DESO18 a living condition index was calculated using the standardized values of the variables described previously and applying principal component analysis. The DESO areas were categorized into 3 categories: vulnerable living conditions (<−0.5 SDs from the mean), moderate living conditions (−0.5 to 0.5 SD from the mean), and favorable living conditions (>0.5 SD from the mean).19

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study sample were described overall and stratified by the 3 study outcomes: participation in the screening survey, invitation to LDCT among participants, and attendance at LDCT among those invited. Continuous variables were presented as means with SDs, and categorical variables as counts and percentages.

To examine the association between socioeconomic and sociodemographic variables with the study outcomes, we fitted multilevel regression models for individuals clustered within DESOs, allowing for random intercepts at the cluster level. Between-area variation was quantified using the SD and the median rate ratio (MRR), which expresses the median difference in the outcome between individuals from different geographical areas. We used robust standard errors to directly estimate risk rations (RR) using Poisson regression models and inverse probability weights to address for potential selection bias arising from attrition from the initial survey through to LDT screening invitation and participation.

Separate models were fitted for each outcome, adjusting for age, educational level, income category, area level variables, region of origin, and marital status as possible confounders. Given that selections of high-risk individuals were based on smoking according to the NELSON criteria, we did not adjust for smoking. Statistical significance was assessed based on 95% CIs; associations were considered statistically significant when the 95% CI did not include the null value. All models reported RRs with 95% CIs. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 19 (StataCorp).

Results

A total of 34 592 women (mean [SD] age, 63.2 [5.6] years) received invitations to participate in the risk-based lung cancer screening pilot (for more information, see Table 1). Of these, approximately one-third (11 509 women [33.3%]) responded to the smoking habits questionnaire. Among questionnaire respondents, 1104 women (9.6%) met the eligibility criteria for screening with LDCT. Of those eligible, 980 women (88.8%) completed the LDCT examination (see the Figure). Missing data on socioeconomic variables was less than 1.5% for the total women invited to the study.

Characteristics of Participant and Socioeconomic Differences in Participation

The likelihood of participating in the initial questionnaire was lower among women with lower educational attainment than postsecondary, lower income, those born outside of Sweden, singles, and those residing in areas characterized by vulnerable living conditions. Differences in screening eligibility were observed across all variables. Educational attainment showed an inverse association with eligibility, with primary education having the highest rates. Similarly, the lowest income tertile and individuals born outside of Sweden demonstrated higher eligibility rates. Married or cohabiting participants and those residing in vulnerable areas also showed higher screening eligibility compared with their counterparts. Among those invited and attending screening with LDCT, no significant differences in participation were observed across groups except for income and marital status (Table 1).

Multilevel Multivariable Analysis of Sociodemographic and Socioeconomic Determinants Across 3 Stages

Participation in the Initial Questionnaire

Women with secondary and postsecondary education had higher risk ratios (RR) for participation compared with women with presecondary education (RR, 1.41; 95% CI, 1.31-1.52 and RR, 1.71; 95% CI, 1.59-1.84, respectively). The risk of participation was higher among women in the highest income tertile, corresponding to an RR of 1.72 (95% CI, 1.64-1.81). Similar patterns were found for those in median income tertile compared with their counterparts (RR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.35-1.48). Compared with women born in Europe (non-Nordic countries), individuals born outside Europe had lower risk of participation (RR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.59-0.75). In contrast, individuals born in Nordic countries had higher risk of participation (RR, 1.89; 95% CI, 1.72-2.10), with the highest risk observed among those born in Sweden (RR, 2.14; 95% CI, 1.97-2.34). Marital status showed a modest association with participation. Compared with single women, married or cohabiting women and divorced or widowed women had higher risk ratios for participation (RR, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.01-1.09 and RR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.17-1.26, respectively). Area-level socioeconomic status demonstrated a clear gradient in participation. Women residing in areas with moderate and favorable living conditions were more likely to participate in the initial questionnaire compared with women living in areas characterized by vulnerable conditions (RR, 1.50; 95% CI, 1.34-1.68 and RR, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.42-1.79, respectively). The area effect of clustering by geographical DESO areas was negligible (SD, 0; MRR, 1) (Table 2).

Meeting Eligibility Criteria for Screening With LDCT

Among women who completed the questionnaire, educational attainment showed association with eligibility for screening. Women with secondary and postsecondary education were less likely to meet the eligibility criteria (RR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.51-0.76; RR, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.31-0.46, respectively). Women in the highest income tertile had lower risk ratios for meeting the eligibility criteria compared with women in the lowest income tertile (RR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.54-0.77). Divorced and widowed women were less likely to be eligible than single women (RR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.43-0.59). Compared with women born in Europe (non-Nordic countries), those born outside Europe had lower risk of meeting the eligibility criteria (RR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.24-0.57). No significant difference was found for women born in Sweden (RR, 0.79; 95% CI, 0.62-1.01) and in other Nordic countries (RR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.72-1.31). Results show those residing in areas with very favorable living conditions were less likely to meet the eligibility criteria for further screening with LDCT (RR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.43-0.72) compared with women living in areas characterized by vulnerable conditions. A similar pattern was observed among women residing in areas with moderate living conditions, who also had a reduced risk of meeting the criteria (RR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.54-0.84). There was a moderate area variation, with a 41% difference between areas (SD, 0.36; MRR, 1.41), reflecting geographical clustering of high-risk individuals (Table 2).

Attendance at Screening With LDCT

Among participating women who met the eligibility criteria for screening with LDCT, no differences were observed in participation, except for those originating from other countries compared with Europe (RR, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.06-1.24). There was no evidence of an important area level effect (SD, 0; MRR, 1) (Table 2).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate whether sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors were associated with participation in a Swedish risk-based lung cancer screening pilot program. Our findings suggest that individual- and area-level sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors influenced participation in a targeted lung cancer screening pilot. These disparities were particularly pronounced at the initial stage. Among participating women, those living in socioeconomically vulnerable areas characterized by vulnerable living conditions were less likely to participate, as were those with lower income, lower education, those born outside Sweden, and singles. Conversely, among participating women, the likelihood of meeting eligibility criteria for screening was higher for individuals from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds. Finally, once identified as eligible for screening with LDCT, participation was high regardless of socioeconomic status.

Based on the findings of this study and in line with other initiatives in Europe,10,11 a stronger focus on reducing inequities in the screening process should be prioritized. Today this is done by targeting underrepresented and high-risk populations such as women, hard-to reach groups, and individuals with comorbidities. By tailoring recruitment strategies and testing different context-based approaches, the aim is to facilitate realization of lung cancer screening programs in the region. Recruitment strategies for underrepresented populations have included coordination with existing cancer screening programs to enhance outreach, mobile units to improve accessibility and recruitment via general practitioners’ practices.10,11 In our study, the most significant disparity found occurred at the point of entry. This pattern may be attributed to the approach of using questionnaire-based risk stratification to identify high-risk individuals. Research demonstrates that survey nonresponse is systematically associated with demographic factors. Underrepresented populations are particularly socioeconomic disadvantaged groups, foreign-born groups, and individuals with risk behaviors.20,21,22,23

Our results highlight critical equity concerns for future implementation of a screening program for lung cancer. Participation inequalities must be addressed when considering wider implementation of risk-based lung cancer screening, especially if eligibility is determined by questionnaire responses. Otherwise, there is a risk that socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, who also carry a higher burden of risk, may be less likely to be offered screening. This is particularly concerning as these groups have higher smoking prevalence driven by factors such as low education, low income, unemployment, and psychosocial stress.3,4,24,25

Limitations and Strengths

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the pilot was restricted to women only, limiting the generalizability of results to male populations. Second, the study was conducted within a specific geographic region, which may limit the applicability to other populations with different sociodemographic and/or socioeconomic characteristics. However, the geographical region in our study is 1 of 3 serving approximately one-third of the region’s population and is considered more representative of the socioeconomic and demographic diversity within the county compared with the other ones. The recruitment method employed a single-mode data collection through postal questionnaires which may have systematically excluded individuals who prefer alternative communication methods or have limited literacy skills. Additionally, the questionnaire was only available in Swedish, potentially creating barriers for participation among nonnative speakers. Selection bias represents a concern, particularly regarding the differential response patterns observed across socioeconomic groups and outcomes. The higher eligibility rates among respondents from vulnerable areas may reflect a systematic bias whereby individuals with higher socioeconomic status respond regardless of smoking status, while those with lower status may be less likely to respond unless they perceive the questionnaire as relevant, such as current or former smokers. Higher response rates among those with higher socioeconomic status could have reduced the proportion who met screening criteria, potentially overestimating the association. Alternatively, lower response rates among smokers with low socioeconomic status may have reduced the proportion of individuals meeting the screening criteria, leading to missed high-risk individuals and a potential underestimation of the association between socioeconomic status and screening eligibility. However, selection bias could operate in both directions, and in this study, it is likely to be largely nondifferential. The sequential design may introduce selection bias, as socioeconomic and demographic factors can influence participation at each stage of the screening process. Socioeconomic and demographic status may affect initial survey participation, subsequent classification as high-risk, and attendance at LDCT screening, and because later outcomes are conditional on earlier stages, this may introduce collider bias. Smoking prevalence among nonresponders could not be ascertained, and individual-level smoking data were not retained for ineligible responders. While national surveys suggest higher smoking rates among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, census-level confirmation was unavailable. Descriptive data from an overlapping sample showed that 47% of respondents were current or former smokers, with 56% reporting more than 30 years of smoking duration, though these data were not stratified by socioeconomic characteristics.5

The study’s strength lies in its use of validated criteria for risk assessment, which enhances the clinical relevance and comparability of eligibility determinations. In addition, high-quality registry linkages were used to obtain individual and area-level variables, and clinical data was used to assess whether the individuals attended screening for lung cancer or not.

Conclusions

In this register-linked cohort study of 34 592 women, both individual- and area-level socioeconomic and sociodemographic factors were associated with participation. As Sweden reviews conditions for a national screening program, barriers to participation must be addressed to reach underrepresented populations. Such a program must identify barriers to participation and develop strategies to effectively reach underrepresented populations and reduce disparities in participation, regardless of socioeconomic status. Unlike other cancer types, lung cancer screening is unique in that only a small proportion of the population is at sufficiently high risk to benefit from screening.
