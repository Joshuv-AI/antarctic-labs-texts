Introduction

Cataract produces visual impairment in nearly 100 million people worldwide, of whom 20 million experience blindness.1 Prevalence in the USA is approximately 25% in the 65–69-year-old population and 68% in those over 80.2 Until now, the only therapeutic approach to reduce cataract-derived blindness has been surgery.3 Around 3.8 million cataract surgeries are performed annually in the USA.4

Despite surgical advances, pain and inflammation after ophthalmic surgery continue to be a burden. Around 45% of patients reported any degree of pain after surgery, and 5% suffered moderate to severe pain.5 6 Likewise, postoperative inflammation can result in patient discomfort, delayed recovery, suboptimal vision and increased risk of complications such as corneal oedema, progression of pre‐existing glaucoma, postoperative pain or cystoid macular oedema (CME).7,10 Thus, the minimisation of postoperative inflammation and pain is relevant.

Corticosteroids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are the options available for both prophylaxis and treatment of post-cataract surgery ocular inflammation. However, no consensus has been reached on best practices for managing post-cataract surgery inflammation.11 12

Clobetasol propionate is a synthetic fluorinated corticosteroid of the glucocorticoid class. It has a well-known anti-inflammatory, anti-pruritic, vasoconstrictive and antiproliferative activity. It has been widely used in humans for many years as a topical anti-inflammatory drug and shows a favourable benefit–risk profile with a good long-term safety profile.13,17

Non-clinical studies have assessed its efficacy and safety profile. Clobetasol propionate is structurally similar to difluprednate, a corticosteroid already approved in the USA for treating inflammation and pain associated with ocular surgery.18 Consequently, an ophthalmic nanoemulsion with 0.05% clobetasol propionate should be superior to placebo in terms of efficacy for treating post-cataract surgery inflammation and pain.

This manuscript reveals the main results of two clinical trials (CLOSE-1 and CLOSE-2) designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of clobetasol propionate ophthalmic nanoemulsion 0.05% (clobetasol) compared with placebo for the treatment of inflammation and pain associated with cataract surgery. The primary study objective was to assess the effect of clobetasol on ocular inflammation associated with cataract surgery after 1 week of treatment. The key secondary objective was to evaluate the effect of clobetasol on eye pain associated with cataract surgery after 1 week of treatment.

Patients and methods

Study design

Data from two randomised, parallel-group, double-masked, placebo-controlled, phase 3 clinical trials conducted in individuals who had inflammation associated with cataract surgery were pooled together for this analysis. CLOSE-1 (NCT04246801) and CLOSE-2 (NCT04249076) had an identical design that was discussed and approved by the US Food and Drug Administration as pivotal studies for the regulatory approval of the formulation.

Six study visits were performed: visit −1 (screening; day −7±3 days), visit 1 (baseline; day 1, 24 hours after the surgery), visit 2 (day 3±1 day), visit 3 (day 8±1 day), visit 4 (day 15±2 days) and visit 5 (day 29±2 days). Investigators attempted to schedule patients at approximately the same time during the day at each study visit. Investigators administered the first dose of study medication on day 1 at the study site. Patients self-administered the study medication for the rest of the study.

Patient selection

The patients included had undergone routine unilateral cataract surgery, after which they presented a level of inflammation of at least five cells in the anterior chamber on the first day after surgery. They met all the eligibility criteria (online supplemental table 1).

The CLOSE-1 trial involved 19 sites, while the CLOSE-2 trial involved 17 in the USA.

Interventions

These studies compared the efficacy and safety of clobetasol to placebo (vehicle). One drop of the study medication was self-administered four times a day (QID) for 14 days after routine unilateral cataract surgery. Clobetasol was a clear or slightly yellowish oil-in-water nanoemulsion. Placebo was identical in appearance and composition to clobetasol but without the active substance. Patients recorded their daily dosing, any missed dose and the reason for it in a patient diary.

Sample size

In each study, a two-group χ2 test with a 0.05 two-sided significance level would detect differences in the complete resolution of anterior chamber cells (ACC) between a proportion of 22% in the active group and 7% in the placebo group (OR of 3.7) with 80% statistical power using a sample of 202 patients. Considering a 4% loss to follow-up rate, a final sample size of 210 patients was estimated (140 clobetasol, 70 placebo). An interim analysis was not planned.

Figure 1 shows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials flow diagrams of each trial. The full analysis set (FAS) was the primary analysis set for efficacy endpoints and included all randomised patients. Sensitivity analyses were performed by analysing all patients included in the FAS population. The FAS non-rescue medication set (FAS non-RM) was used for secondary efficacy analyses and included all randomised patients except rescued patients. The primary analysis set for the safety endpoints included all randomised patients who took at least one dose of study medication.

Randomisation and masking

Patients were randomised into one of two arms based on a computer-generated 2:1 ratio randomisation schedule. Block randomisation was conducted through an Interactive Web Response System that assigned a patient randomisation number to identify the patient throughout the study. Patients not meeting the eligibility criteria were registered as screen failures.

All patients, investigators, subinvestigators and site personnel involved in measuring, monitoring and obtaining data in both studies were masked to treatment assignment.

Efficacy outcomes

The primary efficacy endpoint was the proportion of patients with ACC grade 0 (absence of cells) at day 8. A key secondary efficacy endpoint was the proportion of patients with a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain score of 0 (no eye pain) on day 8. Other secondary efficacy endpoints included the previous variables on days 3, 15 and 29. The following variables were assessed at all study visits: proportion of patients with both ACC grade 0 and flare grade 0, proportion of patients with changes from baseline in anterior chamber flare grades, frequency and severity of different signs of ocular inflammation, VAS photophobia score, the Snellen best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) score, and the VAS pain score, proportion of patients with no eye discomfort, and proportion of patients with changes from baseline in eye discomfort.

Investigators assessed ACC grade during the slit-lamp examination and graded it on an adapted 6-point scale proposed by the Standardisation of Uveitis Nomenclature working group.19 Grade 0 indicates 0 cell; 0.5+ corresponds to 1–5 cells; 1+ to 6–15 cells; 2+ to 16–25 cells; 3+ to 26–50 cells and 4+ to more than 50 cells.

Each patient scored pain and photophobia before any ocular examination using a VAS where 0 meant no symptomatology and 10 indicated the worst imaginable symptomatology. Eye discomfort was assessed using a categorical scale (0=none, 1=mild, 2=moderate, 3=severe).

Signs of ocular inflammation included chemosis, bulbar conjunctival injection, ciliary injection, corneal oedema and keratic precipitates, which the investigator assessed during the slit-lamp examination as absence (grade 0) or presence (grade 1–3). Monocular visual acuity was completed with the patient’s best-corrected vision using the standard of care Snellen chart.

Safety outcomes

Safety data were assessed by the investigator with regards to seriousness, severity and relationship to study medication and collected on the electronic case report form, coded by MedDRA system version 24.0, and tabulated by system organ class and preferred term (PT). The protocol defined treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) as events that emerge during treatment. Safety was evaluated by the number and frequency of TEAEs and their seriousness and relatedness. Intraocular pressure (IOP) was monitored at all study visits.

Statistical methods

CLOSE-1 and CLOSE-2 were identical in design and methods. Therefore, data were pooled to assess efficacy and safety. Descriptive statistics were used to present efficacy and IOP data. Changes from baseline to each post-baseline visit were also described using descriptive statistics. Categorical data were presented as absolute counts (n) and percentages (%). Summary statistics were presented by treatment group and visit, as applicable. A two-sided alpha level of 0.05 was allocated to the primary and the key secondary efficacy endpoints. P-values for the rest of secondary variables are descriptive and should be interpreted as exploratory.

Significance was calculated with a χ2 for both studies individually. For the pooled analysis, a stratified Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test was used to assess the overall association between treatment and outcomes.

Between-treatment comparison of the evolution over time was analysed using a mixed model of repeated measures. The analysis model included the change from baseline as a dependent variable, baseline score as a covariate, the categorical fixed effects of study medication and visit, and the interaction of treatment and visit.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

A total of 426 patients were randomised, treated and analysed: 281 were assigned to the clobetasol arm and 145 to the placebo arm. Seven patients discontinued treatment in the clobetasol arm and 11 in the placebo arm (figure 1).

CLOSE-1 trial ran from 28 May 2020 (first patient first visit) to 19 May 2021 (last patient last visit) and included 211 patients (140 clobetasol and 71 placebo). CLOSE-2 study ran from 29 May 2020 to 14 April 2021 and included 215 patients (141 clobetasol and 74 placebo). Both studies ended on the completion of recruitment.

The median age was 68 years (range 35–93 years), and 236 (55.4%) were women. Basal and demographic characteristics were balanced between clobetasol and placebo arms. The mean time since cataract diagnosis was 23.7 (40.3) months. The study eye was the right eye in 224 (52.6%) patients (online supplemental table 2).

The primary efficacy endpoint was the proportion of patients with absence of inflammation (ACC grade 0) on day 8 on the FAS population, which was 105 patients (37.8%) in the clobetasol arm and 25 patients (18.1%) in the placebo arm (OR 2.8; 95% CI 1.7 to 4.7; p<0.0001) (figure 2). In the CLOSE-1 study, these percentages were 48.2% of patients in the clobetasol arm and 26.9% in the placebo arm (p=0.0036); in CLOSE-2, they were 27.3% in the clobetasol arm and 9.9% in the placebo arm (p=0.0035) (online supplemental table 3). Clobetasol superiority was also observed in the FAS non-RM population (table 1).

Discussion

CLOSE-1 and CLOSE-2 are the first randomised studies to test the efficacy and safety of clobetasol ocular administration for treating inflammation and pain after cataract surgery. The primary efficacy variable was the absence of ACC after 1 week of QID administration. Clobetasol showed superiority compared with placebo, not only after 1 week but also after the first 3 days and 2 weeks of treatment. The key secondary efficacy variable’s assessment revealed that clobetasol effectively reduces cataract surgery-associated pain as early as 2 days after the initiation of therapy and that this reduction is maintained during the subsequent 15 days.

The pooled analysis across different study populations consistently demonstrated the robustness of the results. Importantly, the sensitivity analyses indicated that neither the use of prohibited medication nor rescue medication had an impact on the overall primary efficacy endpoint. These findings confirm the stability of the primary results and suggest that the efficacy of clobetasol propionate is not confounded by concomitant treatments. This strengthens the conclusion that clobetasol propionate represents an effective and safe therapeutic option for managing postoperative inflammation and pain following cataract surgery.

Uncontrolled inflammation may lead to serious side effects, such as uveitis, CME or secondary glaucoma. Consequently, appropriate inflammation and pain management should be provided to improve the patient’s quality of life during recovery and avoid future complications.20 21 Currently, corticosteroids such as loteprednol, dexamethasone, difluprednate and rimexolone are commercialised for ocular inflammation and pain following ophthalmic surgery.22,25 Difluprednate and loteprednol have previously been demonstrated to reduce pain and inflammation after cataract surgery.23 25

Clobetasol produced greater improvements from baseline in all the signs of inflammation evaluated after 3 days, 1 week and 2 weeks of treatment, except for keratic precipitates, for which improvement was only present on day 8. In parallel, clobetasol promoted greater changes than placebo in photophobia, discomfort and pain, as reported by patients, as well as in visual acuity, typical problems resulting from cataract surgery.20 26 27

The nanoemulsion formula ensures uniform product distribution, fast penetration and no discomfort or blurring of vision after application. Another advantage of this clobetasol formula is that it comes in single-dose vials, which ensures the product’s sterility at each administration and avoids the risks of overdose or medication error.

The long-term use of corticosteroids has raised safety concerns, especially about elevated IOP.28 Thus, balancing the benefits obtained with ocular corticosteroids and the plausible risks of increased IOP is challenging in managing cataract surgery patients. This limitation of traditional corticosteroids led to the development of C-20 ester corticosteroids, which effectively control post-cataract surgery inflammation without the elevation of IOP.12 In the CLOSE trials, one case of moderate IOP increase happened in the clobetasol arm (no action needed to resolve) and another in the placebo arm (concomitant medication required), accounting for 0.4% and 0.7% of each arm, respectively. This percentage is substantially lower than reported with difluprednate, prednisolone or rimexolone.22 23 29 30

Most TEAEs were considered unrelated to the study medication in both treatment groups. The frequency of TEAEs associated with the study medication was as low as 3.9% in the clobetasol arm and 5.5% in the placebo arm. Such differences may be due to an increase in ocular inflammation-related postoperative complications, which might result in pain and discomfort in those patients who did not receive anti-inflammatory medication. One related STEAE was reported in the clobetasol arm: a case of mild CME, which was resolved after study drug withdrawal. Notably, the incidence of related CME in this study (0.4%) was lower than the incidence of CME in routine cataract surgery with conventional steroids.

Although a tapering period based on patients’ responses was not applied in this study, a good response and no inflammation or pain rebound were detected within 2 weeks after the treatment period. Consequently, unlike other ophthalmic preparations with corticosteroids, clobetasol does not seem to require tapering after the 14-day treatment period.

The current study has several limitations. First, in the CLOSE trials, patients in the control arm received a placebo and no other commercial products. As a result, the study does not provide insights into the efficacy of clobetasol compared with existing therapies, focusing solely on its anti-inflammatory effects. Second, a 2:1 randomisation ratio was adopted. Although unequal allocation may influence outcome interpretation, the approach is consistent with standard clinical trial practice for placebo-controlled studies and to optimise study power while limiting the number of subjects exposed to placebo when approved therapies are available. Third, the observation period lasted 29 days; while differences in efficacy were noticeable up to day 15, they were generally undetectable by the final visit on day 29. This decline in observable effects may be attributed to the natural resolution of most inflammation-related postoperative complications 1 month after surgery. Lastly, discrepancies in pain assessment results were noted between the CLOSE-1 and CLOSE-2 trials, although the pooled analysis favoured clobetasol.

In conclusion, clobetasol nanoemulsion 0.05% in single-dose vials, administered 1 drop QID for 14 days, is a valid option for treating patients after cataract surgery. It effectively reduces inflammation and pain associated with routine cataract surgery and has demonstrated superiority over placebo on multiple clinical endpoints. Clobetasol was safe and well tolerated, with no elevation of IOP while on treatment. Patients treated with clobetasol experienced no rebound inflammation or pain during the 2-week follow-up period.
