In vivo noninvasive study of diabetic peripheral neuropathy plantar morphology and mechanical properties based on ultrasound
Authors: Qiang Zhou, Ying Li, Lei Ren, Boxin Yu, Xiao Zhang, Zhihui Qian, Jianan Wu, Ruixia Xu, Jing Liu, Luquan Ren
Published: 2023

To the Editor: The incidence of diabetes complications increases with the increase in diabetes incidence. Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is one of the most common complications in diabetes patients. DPN is very harmful and is an important cause of diabetic foot and amputation. The amputation rate of DPN patients is 10–20 times higher than that of nondiabetic patients. Every 30 s, somewhere in the world, a patient's lower limb or part of a lower limb will be surgically amputated due to DPN.[1] DPN seriously interferes with the physical and mental health of patients. At the same time, the high cost of treatment and rehabilitation care also causes huge economic pressure on patients. Therefore, it is particularly important to identify, diagnose, and prevent DPN in the early or ultra-early stage.

Although neuropathy is the main pathological change in DPN, it does not mean that DPN is a purely neurodegenerative disease. Research has shown that foot lesions caused by DPN can involve multi-level soft tissues such as the foot epidermis and plantar adipose connective tissue. In the early stages of DPN, when neuropathy could not yet be indentified by standardized auxiliary examination, the morphology and mechanical properties of plantar soft tissue may have undergone corresponding pathological changes.[2] Therefore, a comprehensive and in-depth study of the morphology and mechanical properties of plantar soft tissue from the perspective of biomechanics can provide a richer theoretical basis for the injury mechanism and early diagnosis of DPN.

At present, there are no definitive results on the effect of DPN on the morphology and mechanical properties of plantar soft tissues. There are few studies that simultaneously detect the thickness and biomechanical properties of soft tissue in multiple parts of the plantar distribution of DPN patients and the research on the differences in the influence of multi-level soft tissues such as the fat pad epidermis (FPE), microchambers (MIC), and macrochambers (MAC) are still insufficient. Plantar soft tissue is a highly differentiated tissue composed of skin, connective tissue, adipocytes, etc, which has characteristics such as nonlinearity, viscoelasticity, and complex mechanical behavior. Now, it is far from sufficient to study the morphology and mechanical properties of plantar soft tissue as a whole. It is not yet clear whether there is a correlation between the thickness of soft tissue in the entire plantar area and changes in Young's modulus in DPN patients, or whether such changes only occur at specific locations in the foot. Therefore, it is necessary to explore the influence of DPN on the thickness and mechanical modulus of plantar soft tissue from a full plantar and multi-level perspective.

This study utilized ultrasonic B-imaging and shear wave elastography (SWE) technology, and the noninvasive quantitative thickness distribution and mechanical modulus of plantar soft tissue FPE, MIC, MAC, and overall thickness (OT) were measured. At the same time, the influence of gender and age on the thickness and mechanical modulus of plantar soft tissue was explored, and the differences in plantar soft tissue thickness and mechanical modulus between healthy subjects and mild DPN patients were compared and analyzed. This study not only provides in vivo data for foot soft tissue biomechanical modeling, but also provides new ideas for exploring new mechanisms of DPN lesions and seeking early diagnostic methods for DPN.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Jilin University (No. 2021-187) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. All studied participants signed the written informed consent form. This study randomly recruited 60 patients with mild DPN (30 men and 30 women) and recruited 60 healthy volunteers matched for age and sex. There was no significant difference in the body mass index (BMI) between the two groups; the BMI of all participants was within the normal range (18.5–24.9 kg/m2) for excluding any differences caused by obesity. The mild-DPN and healthy subject groups were divided into three groups according to age: Group 1 (40–49 years old), Group 2 (50–59 years old), and Group 3 (over 60 years old). There were 20 people in each group: 10 men and 10 women. The detailed information on subject selection is displayed in the Supplementary Materials, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B779 for the selection and grouping of subjects.

Considering the anatomy of human feet and the ulcer-prone parts of plantar soft tissue, this study selected eight plantar soft tissue parts as the test objects: under first toe soft tissue (U1T), under first metatarsal soft tissue (U1M), under second metatarsal soft tissue (U2M), under third metatarsal soft tissue (U3M), under fourth metatarsal soft tissue (U4M), under fifth metatarsal soft tissue (U5M), sagittal position of the soft tissue under the heel (SUH), and coronal view of the soft tissue under the heel (CUH) [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B779]. The details of measurement methods of the thickness distribution and Young's modulus of eight soft tissue sites are shown in Supplementary Materials, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B779 for the experimental equipment and methods and Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B779.

SPSS 21.0 software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the statistical analysis, and all data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to investigate the difference in the plantar soft tissue thickness and Young's modulus between healthy subjects and patients with mild DPN, and a Student–Newman–Keuls test was performed. The effects of gender and age on plantar soft tissue thickness and Young's modulus were studied using Point-biserial correlation and Pearson correlation, respectively. A value of P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The basic characteristics of the participants are shown in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B779. In this study, sex had a significant impact on the thickness of the OT and MAC and Young's modulus of the MIC and MAC in the plantar soft tissues of healthy subjects (P <0.001); the thickness of the OT and MAC in women and Young's modulus of the MIC and MAC were smaller than those in men [Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B779]. The postulated reason was that the BMI value of men was generally higher than that of women, and they engaged in more daily weight-bearing behaviors and intense exercise. Both the thickness and hardness of the plantar fat pad were higher in men than in women, which may be due to self-adaptation caused by increases in weight and height.[3] Therefore, it is necessary to explore the influence of height, weight, and BMI on plantar soft tissue thickness and Young's modulus.

In this study, age had a significant impact on the thickness of the OT and MAC in the soft tissues of the soles of healthy subjects (P <0.001), which had a negative correlation with age; that is, the thickness of the OT and MAC gradually decreased with increasing age [Supplementary Table 4, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B779]. Age had no significant effect on the thickness of FPE and MIC of the plantar fat pad in most subjects. Considering that the OT change of the plantar soft tissue may be caused by the thickness change of the MAC of the fat pad, the soft tissue structure of the MAC of the fat pad is relatively loose compared with the MIC of the fat pad, which plays a more important role in the shock absorption function of the plantar soft tissue to the body. The results of this study show that with increasing age, a thicker MAC is more easily damaged by daily exercise and posture maintenance, suggesting that after middle age, shoes with good shock absorption function should be used to protect the foot's soft tissue.

Age had a significant impact on Young's modulus of the MIC and MAC of soft tissue in the soles of healthy subjects (P <0.001), showing a positive correlation; that is, Young's modulus of MIC and MAC gradually increased with age [Supplementary Table 5, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B779]. We postulated the reason why Young's modulus of the MIC and the MAC of the plantar soft tissue fat pad gradually increased. It may be that with increasing age, the aging of the body leads to a corresponding reduction in the collagen fibres, elastic fibres, and water content of the plantar soft tissue, resulting in a change in its thickness and mechanical properties, further resulting in a decrease in elasticity and an increase in hardness.

The results of this study showed that the forefoot, in the distribution area of the maximum pressure impulse on the U1M, U2M, and U3M plantar, the OT of the U1M and U2M and the thickness of the MAC fat pad in patients with mild DPN were smaller than those in healthy subjects [Supplementary Tables 6 and 7, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B779]. Young's modulus of the U1M, U2M, and U3M plantar soft tissue fat pads and the MIC and MAC in patients with mild DPN were larger than those in healthy subjects [Supplementary Tables 8 and 9, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B779]. Results of the research showed that the increase in hardness of the soft tissue in the forefoot area of DPN patients can reduce the ability of the feet to withstand mechanical stress during weight-bearing.[4] Other research results showed that it is very important to maintain a sufficient thickness of plantar soft tissue as a shock absorber, and there is a negative correlation between plantar tissue thickness and plantar pressure.[5] These findings all remind us that with a decrease in the thickness and an increase in the hardness of the plantar soft tissue in the relevant areas of the forefoot of patients with mild DPN, the stress during contact between the plantar and the ground in these areas will increase sharply, which increases the risk of a diabetic foot ulcer. This is also consistent with the fact that U1M, U2M, and U3M are prone to ulceration. Therefore, the study of plantar soft tissue thickness and Young's modulus distribution in patients with mild DPN can predict the occurrence of plantar ulcers, and early intervention can reduce the risk of ulcers and amputation.

For the mechanical modulus of plantar soft tissue, most patients with mild DPN had significant differences in the Young's modulus of plantar soft tissue under higher stress compared to healthy subjects, and patients with mild DPN had a higher modulus than healthy subjects. For the morphology of the plantar soft tissue, patients with mild DPN only had significant differences in the OT of U1M and U2M (the most stressed area of the plantar) and in the thickness of the fat pad MAC when compared with those in healthy subjects, and those values were lower in patients with mild DPN than in healthy subjects. Therefore, it is speculated that the mechanical properties (Young's modulus) of multisite plantar soft tissue in patients with mild DPN change before the overall change in plantar morphology (thickness); that is, the changes in the mechanical properties of the plantar soft tissue may occur earlier than the occurrence of morphological changes.

This study has some limitations. The number of grouped samples is small. In the future, more data should be collected from a larger sample to calculate the critical value of the plantar soft tissue thickness and Young's modulus of mild DPN, which may provide important auxiliary diagnostic criteria for diagnosis or treatment.

In summary, mild DPN has a significant impact on the morphology of individual plantar soft tissues and the mechanical modulus of plantar soft tissues with higher stress. Before the comprehensive changes in plantar soft tissue morphology occur, the mechanical modulus of multiple plantar soft tissues in mild DPN patients has already changed, indicating that changes in the mechanical properties of plantar soft tissues may occur earlier than the morphological changes. These findings provide a more comprehensive theoretical basis for studying the damage mechanism and early diagnosis of DPN.
