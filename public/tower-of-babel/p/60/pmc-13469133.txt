A likelihood-based method for identifying replay from spike sequences
Authors: Namjung Huh, Injae Yun, Jong Won Lee, Min Whan Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hippocampal replay—sequential reactivation of place cells—has been implicated in memory recall, consolidation and planning, but traditional analyses rely on spatial tuning and exclude non-place cells, limiting their scope. Model-based analyses without spatial templates often depend on predefined parameters and constrained pattern definitions, making them parameter-sensitive and potentially biased. To address these limitations, we developed an analysis that estimates the likelihood of spike sequences based on pairwise firing order probabilities observed during active behavior regardless of their behavioral correlates. We validated the method using simulations, rat single-unit recordings, and mouse calcium imaging, showing strong agreement with conventional replay metrics and broad applicability. When applied to head-fixed mice passively viewing naturalistic movie stimuli, the method detected significant replay in both hippocampus and visual cortex, demonstrating its utility in uncovering structured replay across brain regions and sensory domains.

Introduction

Hippocampal replay—the time-compressed reactivation of sequential place cell activity—has emerged as a central mechanism in systems neuroscience, particularly in the study of learning and memory. Physiological studies in rodents have consistently demonstrated that such replay occurs during slow-wave sleep and quiet rest1–4, and is thought to support core cognitive functions including memory consolidation, retrieval, and prospective planning5–8. These replay events are predominantly observed in association with sharp-wave ripples (SWRs)—brief high-frequency oscillations (150–250 Hz) superimposed on large-amplitude negative deflections in the CA1 pyramidal layer, typically lasting 30–100 ms5. To detect such events, studies typically first identify SWRs and then assess whether sequences of place cell activity—defined based on spatial firing fields during behavior—are reactivated during these events relative to shuffled controls9.

Analyzing place cells offers the advantage of a predefined sequence structure anchored in physical space, facilitating the detection of replay. However, this approach inherently excludes non-place cells and confines analysis to the spatial domain. Recent work has extended replay analysis beyond the spatial domain—sequential firing of ‘time-sensitive’ hippocampal neurons during movie viewing was found to replay during a subsequent rest period in head-fixed mice10. However, as with place cell-based approaches, replay detection based on ‘time-sensitive’ neurons is constrained by the criteria used to define these cells, potentially excluding neurons that participate in replay but lack well-defined temporal tuning. As a result, both approaches fall short of capturing broader forms of replay that reflect experiences lacking well-defined spatial or temporal structure, limiting our understanding of general event-related neural dynamics.

Several alternative methods have been proposed to overcome these limitations, aiming to detect replay without relying on tuning functions. These approaches identify replay by detecting neural activity patterns during candidate events that resemble patterns observed during active periods11. To achieve this, a range of unsupervised and probabilistic techniques have been developed. For example, population time-series factorization methods extract recurring temporal motifs from high-dimensional activity without requiring sequences to be specified a priori12, while temporal pattern clustering approaches group similar spike sequences based on pairwise dissimilarity metrics13. In addition, probabilistic frameworks such as hidden Markov models infer latent state sequences that best explain the observed spike trains to detect replay structure14. These approaches provide flexible frameworks for uncovering sequential structure directly from neural activity and can reveal multiple coexisting patterns without relying on predefined trial or task structure. However, these approaches typically involve choices regarding model structure—such as the number and dimensionality of latent patterns, temporal resolution, or clustering criteria—which can influence the inferred sequences. More broadly, as these methods aim to infer latent structure from data, the resulting representations depend on how well the chosen model captures the underlying temporal organization of neural activity, rendering replay detection inherently sensitive to model specification and parameter choices.

Complementary findings have also emerged from human studies. Using methods such as magnetoencephalography (MEG), functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), and single-unit recordings in clinical patients, studies have reported neural sequences resembling replay-like activity in non-spatial domains15–19. However, similar to non-spatial replay analyses in animals, these approaches typically rely on population-level decoding frameworks that require explicit assumptions about the structure of sequential activity. These analyses are further limited by methodological constraints, including the coarse spatiotemporal resolution of noninvasive techniques and the restricted neuronal sampling available in clinical single-unit recordings, complicating efforts to determine whether human replay reflects the same underlying circuit-level processes observed in animals11.

Building on these approaches, we developed an analysis method that estimates the probability that a spike sequence observed during a candidate replay event recapitulates the sequential structure expressed during the active period. Rather than inferring complex latent states or multiple competing sequence templates, the method captures sequential structure through pairwise spike-ordering statistics — a low-order temporal model that requires neither spatial information nor explicit behavioral correlates, involves minimal parameter tuning, and generalizes across brain regions and behavioral paradigms. In this framework, sequential structure is defined by trial type, enabling controlled comparisons between active and offline activity while maintaining a relatively simple and transparent model structure. We validated the method using simulated data, single-unit recordings from navigating rats, and calcium imaging from mice, demonstrating strong consistency with conventional replay metrics. When applied to Neuropixels recordings from head-fixed mice during passive visual experience, the method uncovered robust non-spatial replay in both the hippocampus and visual cortex, demonstrating its utility in uncovering structured replay across brain regions and sensory domains.

Results

Detection of replays based on sequence likelihood estimation

To identify replay events independent of predefined spatial or temporal templates, we developed a method that quantifies the likelihood of a given spike sequence based on the relative firing order of neuron pairs during an active period. Specifically, we computed the probability that one neuron fires after another across all cell pairs to construct an ordered firing probability matrix. This matrix captures the statistical structure of population firing sequences and is used for computing the log-sequence likelihood (Lseq) of candidate events during rest. Replay significance is then assessed by comparing the observed Lseq values against a null distribution derived from shuffled sequences.

The conventional method includes several variants9; here, we employ a widely used approach that quantifies replay strength as the weighted correlation (Wcorr) between time and decoded spatial position20–22. In contrast, our method estimates the probability that cell i fires after cell j during the active phase (P(Cj→ Ci)) based on the number of temporally ordered spike pairs (Nj→i) and the total number of spike pairs between the two cells (Nij) across trials, yielding an ordered firing probability matrix (Fig. 1f). This matrix is shown in Fig. 1g for the subset of cells active in the sample candidate event, arranged in their firing order during the candidate event (Fig. 1b). We then calculate Lseq based on active-phase order statistics (see Methods), representing the log-likelihood of the observed spike sequence under the active-phase ordering model. Positive and negative Lseq values indicate forward and reverse sequences, respectively. Statistical significance is determined by comparing observed values to a null distribution generated by spike identity shuffling (Fig. 1h).

Validation with simulated data

To validate our method, we compared its Lseq values with Wcorr scores across a range of simulated place cell configurations. The peak firing rates within place fields were systematically varied from 1 to 21 Hz, while the baseline firing rate outside the fields was held constant at 1 Hz. For each condition, 100 candidate replay events were generated by randomly sampling spikes from active-phase trials and compressing their timing 50-fold (Fig. 1b). Replay scores from the two methods were highly correlated across all firing conditions (Fig. 1i, shown for peak firing rate = 11 Hz). Linear regression analyses confirmed strong associations between Lseq and Wcorr scores (e.g., R² = 0.41, P = 6.9 × 10−13 at peak firing rate = 2 Hz; R² = 0.68, P = 5.5 × 10−26 at 11 Hz; R² = 0.57, P = 1.5 × 10−13 at 21 Hz), indicating strong consistency between the two approaches.

To evaluate whether the agreement between methods generalizes to non-spatial sequential structure, we performed additional simulations using temporally ordered responses to a natural movie stimulus (see “Methods”). Replay scores derived from the sequence-likelihood method and from the time-field–based weighted correlation approach introduced by Yang et al. (2024)10 were strongly correlated across simulated replay candidates (linear regression: R² = 0.64, P = 1.7 × 10⁻²³; Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating that the consistency between the two approaches extends beyond spatial trajectories to non-spatial, sensory-driven sequential structure.

Validation with rat single-unit recordings

We next validated our method using CA1 recordings from rats performing a four-alternative navigation task20 (Fig. 2a). Each session consisted of four 20-trial blocks, each with a distinct correct trajectory. We focused on the session with the largest number of simultaneously recorded cells (n = 35). A total of 313 candidate replay events (SWR-associated spike bursts during reward consumption involving at least five place cells) were identified (Fig. 2b). Following the task, mice were placed on a pedestal for 30 min. Population synchrony events (PSEs23) were extracted from this rest period across three mice, defined by synchronous calcium events during immobility (see “Methods”; Fig. 3b). Although local field potentials were not recorded, prior work indicates substantial overlap between PSEs and SWRs23,24. Replay detection was performed using both the weighted correlation method (Fig. 3c–e) and our sequence likelihood method (Fig. 3f–h), with CW and CCW data analyzed separately. Although the number of detected replays was relatively low, likely due to the sparse nature of calcium events, the number of replays identified using the weighted correlation metric significantly exceeded chance (P = 8.0 × 10−3; Fig. 3i). The two replay metrics, Lseq and Wcorr, were strongly correlated (example session: CW: R² = 0.36, P = 3.3 × 10−35; Fig. 3j; CCW: R² = 0.35, P = 1.1 × 10−36; Fig. 3k; all sessions: CW: R² = 0.34, P < 1.0 × 10⁻100; CCW: R² = 0.29, P < 1.0 × 10⁻100), indicating strong concordance between the two approaches under calcium imaging conditions.

Including both place and non-place cells increased replay detection rates compared to analyses using only place cells (CW: 36 ± 18 and 14 ± 5, respectively; Mann-Whitney U-test, W = 40.00, P = 7.9 × 10–3; CCW: 37 ± 21 and 14 ± 4, respectively; W = 39.50, P = 1.6 × 10⁻2). The absolute log-sequence (|Lseq | ) score did not show a significant correlation with the proportion of non-place cells (linear mixed-effects model, CW: t(181) = 0.23, P = 0.82; Fig. 3l; CCW: t(189) = −1.77, P = 0.08; Fig. 3m). Moreover, the proportion of non-place cells in the whole population did not differ significantly from their proportion among replay-participating neurons, indicating that neither cell type was disproportionately represented in replays (two-tailed χ2-tests: CW: χ2 (1) = 0.68, P = 0.41; CCW: χ2 (1) = 1.56, P = 0.21; Fig. 3n). These results suggest that both place and non-place cells contribute comparably to replay.

To examine whether this conclusion generalizes to datasets with higher temporal resolution and SWR identification, we also analyzed the rat single-unit dataset described above. Given that non-place cells comprised a relatively small proportion of simultaneously recorded neurons in this dataset (Supplementary Fig. 2a), we present these results as supplementary analyses. The replay sequence score did not significantly correlate with non-place cell participation (Supplementary Fig. 2b), consistent with the mouse dataset. Although non-place cells were underrepresented among replay-participating neurons (Supplementary Fig. 2c), this is likely due to their lower firing rates during active behavior (Supplementary Fig. 2d). These results support the conclusion that non-place cells contribute to SWR-associated replay.

Replay events identified by our method (Fig. 4c–e) exceeded chance levels in both CA1 and visual cortex for both the original and shuffled movies (one-tailed binomial test, CA1, movie, P < 1.0 × 10⁻100; shuffled movie, P < 1.0 × 10⁻100; visual cortex, movie, P = 8.6 × 10⁻14; shuffled movie, P = 6.7 × 10⁻9; Fig. 4f). To examine whether replay also occurred for randomized stimulus sequences, we analyzed replay events for Gabor patch sequences. As expected, no significant replay was observed in the visual cortex (P = 0.36); however, CA1 replay remained significantly above chance (P = 7.8 × 10⁻16; Fig. 4f). Similar results were obtained with a shuffling-based test (Supplementary Fig. 3). The number of recorded neurons and detected replay events per movie is summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Replay probabilities (Preplay) were compared across stimulus types and brain regions using a generalized linear mixed-effects model, which revealed significant main effects of both stimulus type and brain region, as well as a significant region × stimulus interaction (χ2region (1) = 86.25, Pregion = 1.3 × 10⁻15, χ2stimulus (2) = 71.62, Pstimulus = 7.1 × 10⁻21, χ2interaction (2) = 12.05, Pinteraction = 1.8 × 10⁻5). Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction revealed that replay was significantly more likely for the movie than for the Gabor stimuli (P < 1.0 × 10⁻100), but did not differ significantly between the original and shuffled movies (P = 1; Fig. 4f). Consistent patterns were found in |Lseq| values, analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model with animal included as a random effect, which revealed significant main effects of both stimulus type and brain region, as well as a significant region × type interaction (Fregion(1,58867) = 122.55, Pregion = 1.9 × 10⁻23, Fstimulus(2,58867) = 94.14, Pstimulus = 1.5 × 10⁻41, Finteraction(2,58867) = 20.29, Pinteraction = 1.6 × 10⁻9). Bonferroni post hoc tests confirmed significantly higher |Lseq| for the movie compared to Gabor patches (P = 1.0 × 10⁻25), but no significant difference between the movie and shuffled movie (P = 0.27; Fig. 4g).

To validate our method against an established approach, we applied the time-field–based weighted correlation analysis10 to the same Allen Institute dataset and directly compared the resulting replay scores. Across both CA1 and visual cortex recordings, replay scores from the two methods were strongly correlated in all recording sessions (linear regression: P < 0.001 for all cases; Supplementary Figs. 4–5), and replay probabilities did not differ significantly between methods under any condition (two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test, all P values > 0.05). The overlap between replay events identified by each method, quantified by the Jaccard index, was significantly greater than chance in all cases (permutation test: P < 0.001 for all cases; Supplementary Figs. 4–5). Furthermore, when we analyzed the identical recording sessions used in Yang et al. (2024)10, the two methods yielded consistent results (Supplementary Fig. 6), confirming strong agreement between the two methods.

In summary, replay was more robust in CA1 than in the visual cortex and remained significant in CA1 even for randomized stimulus sequences (Gabor patches), whereas visual cortex replay was only detected for movie stimuli. Replay strength was comparable between the original and shuffled movies in both regions, likely due to the repeated exposure to identical sequences.

Replay dynamics across rest

Detection of replays for novel events

Although our method relies on neural activity patterns observed during an active phase to construct the ordered firing probability matrix, it can be extended to detect replays of novel events by rearranging experienced sequences. Here, using simulated neural data, we demonstrate the potential of our method in revealing replays of novel events.

One straightforward approach is to rearrange an actually experienced event consisting of discrete sub-events into a new sequence. Figure 6a shows the results of a simulation in which an animal was repeatedly exposed to a sequence of five discrete sensory stimuli (S1-S2-S3-S4-S5). We simulated the discharges of 100 neurons that are selectively responsive to a specific stimulus (mean discharge rates of 11 and 1 Hz for the preferred and non-preferred stimuli, respectively; see “Methods”) and constructed the corresponding ordered firing probability matrix (Fig. 6c). We then rearranged the sequence into a new (i.e., unexperienced) one (S5-S2-S1-S3-S4; Fig. 6b) and constructed the corresponding ordered firing probability matrix (Fig. 6d). We also generated replay candidates (SWR-associated spike bursts) by randomly selecting 10 spikes from a randomly chosen trial of the rearranged neural data and compressing the time scale by a factor of 50. A similar approach can reconfigure structured spatial trajectories into new ones. To illustrate this, we generated simulated spike trains as a hypothetical rat navigated a plus maze along two trajectories (left-to-right and top-to-bottom) multiple times (Fig. 6g). We then reconfigured spatial trajectories into a novel path (top-to-right; Fig. 6h) and constructed the corresponding ordered firing probability matrix (Fig. 6j). Replay candidates were generated by randomly selecting 10 spikes from a randomly chosen trial of the reconfigured trajectory and compressing the time scale by a factor of 50. Because the original and reconfigured trajectories overlap substantially, more candidates are classified as significant replays than expected by chance even when using the original active-period data. However, both Lseq and Preplay were significantly higher when using the ordered firing probability matrix from the reconfigured trajectory compared to the matrix derived from the left-to-right trajectory (two-tailed paired t test: t(198) = 6.41, P = 1.0 × 10⁻9; Fig. 6k; two-tailed χ2-test: χ2 (1) = 20.51, P = 5.9 × 10⁻6; Fig. 6l). Similar results were observed when using the matrix derived from the top-to-bottom trajectory. These findings confirm that our method can detect replays of novel spatial trajectories in structured navigation environments.

Discussion

Replay detection methods have evolved considerably, from pairwise temporal ordering analyses27 to identification of temporally compressed multi-neuron spike sequences during sleep28, and subsequently to template-based matching of population activity to behaviorally defined sequences2,3,29,30. More recent approaches have extended replay detection beyond spatially tuned neurons using unsupervised and probabilistic frameworks11. Building on this foundation, we developed and validated a method that addresses key limitations of these existing approaches.

Unlike these conventional approaches, our method estimates the likelihood of spike sequences based on pairwise firing order probabilities observed during the active phase, which define a first-order temporal template of population activity. While this constitutes a form of template-based analysis, it requires neither spatial tuning nor explicit behavioral correlates, relying solely on low-order spike-ordering statistics derived from task-relevant experience. Rather than inferring all possible latent sequences from the observed neural activity, the approach estimates a dominant sequential structure from a defined behavioral context and uses this structure as a reference model for replay detection. When multiple distinct sequential structures are expected across task conditions — such as block types, movement directions, or stimulus types — ordered firing probability matrices are estimated separately for each condition, representing an explicit, task-informed assumption about the number of distinct sequential structures in the data. While unsupervised sequence-discovery methods can reveal multiple latent patterns in neural activity12,13,31, our framework prioritizes interpretability and computational robustness by constraining the reference model using experimentally defined task structure.

The choice of behavioral trials as the unit of analysis, however, may not be optimal in all settings. When strong neural rhythms are present, cycle-based trial definitions such as theta cycles32,33 or slow oscillations34, or spontaneous burst epochs14, offer data-driven alternatives that do not rely on experimenter-defined behavioral epochs. In the paradigms examined here, behaviorally defined trials were preferred as they directly anchor the reference model to task-relevant experience and are broadly applicable regardless of the presence of clear rhythmic structure.

Our approach demonstrated strong concordance with the widely used weighted correlation metric across multiple modalities—including simulated data, rat single-unit recordings, and mouse calcium imaging—indicating its robustness and generalizability. An important advantage of our method is its inclusion of all recorded neurons rather than place cells alone, revealing that non-place cells contribute meaningfully to replay — a finding consistent with previous reports that these cells can encode spatial or contextual information35. More importantly, the method robustly detected non-spatial replay events in both hippocampal and visual cortical neurons in head-fixed mice, demonstrating its utility for studying replay across brain regions and sensory domains. Furthermore, because the method operates directly on spike timing data without relying on external variables, it can be applied to systems and species for which behavioral correlates are unavailable or unknown.

Our method also showed strong concordance with a time-sensitive neuron-based approach10. Despite this methodological agreement, the two studies diverged in their findings: whereas we detected significant replay in both hippocampus and visual cortex, Yang et al. reported significant replay in the hippocampus but not in visual cortex10. This discrepancy likely stems from differences in candidate event selection rather than the scoring method itself: when Yang et al.‘s scoring method was applied to the same ripple-associated candidate events used in our study, it detected significant replay in visual cortex, indicating that the divergence in findings is attributable to how replay candidates were defined. Unlike our study, Yang et al. identified replay candidates based on ‘global mean spiking signals’ spanning tens to several hundreds of milliseconds at 50-ms time resolution, which may have diluted weaker replay signals in visual cortex. Together, these results indicate that our method provides reliable replay detection across diverse experimental contexts, while highlighting that candidate event selection is a critical determinant of replay detection outcomes.

Our findings from head-fixed mouse recordings revealed regional and stimulus-dependent differences in offline sequential activity, suggesting distinct contributions of experience-driven and intrinsic circuit dynamics. In visual cortex, replay-like activity was detected for movie stimuli but not reliably for Gabor stimuli, suggesting a dependence on temporally structured sensory input. In contrast, hippocampal replay-like activity was observed for both movies and, albeit more weakly, Gabor stimuli — even though the latter lack a consistent temporal sequence — pointing to a stronger contribution from intrinsic network dynamics, potentially mediated by CA3 recurrent circuitry36. Notably, neocortical circuits can generate structured sequential activity independent of recent experience, as seen during spontaneous down–up state transitions34,37, raising the possibility that the absence of Gabor-driven replay in visual cortex reflects the specific constraints of our experimental context rather than a fundamental property of neocortical circuits. Together, these observations suggest that offline sequential activity reflects an interaction between stimulus-driven temporal structure and intrinsic network dynamics whose balance differs across brain regions, and caution against interpreting all offline sequences as evidence of newly formed memory traces. A definitive demonstration that the detected sequences reflect experience-dependent replay would require a formal comparison of pre- and post-experience periods to determine whether these sequences are strengthened or newly emerge following the relevant experience.

Beyond characterizing the origins of replay, we examined its functional consequences for neural representations. CA1 activity changed over rest more than could be attributed to intrinsic temporal drift, and replay participation predicted the magnitude of firing rate changes in both regions—an effect substantially stronger in CA1 than in visual cortex. These relationships could not be explained by arousal fluctuations (see Methods), suggesting that hippocampal replay actively contributes to the rapid reorganization of subsequent representations. In visual cortex, replay was less frequent and its relationship to representational change was weaker, though this need not imply functional irrelevance: prior work has shown that visual cortical representations evolve gradually over days, with offline reactivations predicting future responses more strongly than initial ones38. Together, these findings suggest complementary timescales of plasticity — hippocampal representations are updated rapidly through frequent replay, whereas cortical representations undergo slower, cumulative reorganization39,40.

Although our method requires active-phase activity to define ordered firing probabilities, we showed that it can detect replays of novel sequences by rearranging experienced events into new configurations. This approach extends naturally to the detection of preplay—spontaneous sequential activations during rest that anticipate future experiences41,42—broadening it utility beyond retrospective analysis to encompass predictive and generative neural dynamics.

Methods

Neuronal simulation

We generated four sets of simulated neural data. First, to compare our method with the weighted correlation-based approach for replay identification, we simulated spatial firing of 100 place cells while a hypothetical rat traversed a linear track 20 times in one direction, with the peak firing locations of the place cells evenly distributed across the track (Fig. 1). For each neuron, we set the mean instantaneous firing rate outside the place field to 1 Hz, while varying the maximum instantaneous firing rate within the field between 1 and 21 Hz to manipulate spatial selectivity. We simulated the rat’s spatial position in each trial using a random walk model, derived instantaneous firing rates, F(t), from their spatial tuning curves, and generated spike trains from these rates using a Poisson model.

Second, to test whether the agreement between the sequence-likelihood method and place field-based weighted correlation generalizes beyond spatial structure, we performed an additional set of simulations using temporally ordered sensory responses modeled after the natural movie paradigm used in the Allen Institute for Brain Science25. We simulated 100 neurons over 30 repeated presentations of a 30-s movie stimulus. The movie was divided into 60 equal time bins (0.5 s per bin), and each neuron was assigned a preferred time bin drawn uniformly from the stimulus duration. Within the preferred bin, firing rate was drawn from a Gaussian distribution (mean 10 Hz, SD 5 Hz); outside the preferred bin, firing rate was set to a low baseline (0.5 Hz). Each neuron expressed on average approximately one preferred time bin. Spike trains were generated independently for each neuron and each time bin using Poisson statistics, preserving stimulus-locked temporal tuning while introducing trial-to-trial variability across repetitions. Time-field templates were estimated from simulated spike trains following Yang et al. (2024)10. To construct candidate replay events, spikes were randomly sampled from the simulated spike trains and their timing was compressed during the simulated offline phase, analogous to the procedure used in the spatial simulations (see Spatial sequence simulations). Replay scores were then computed using both the sequence-likelihood method and a time-field-based weighted correlation approach, and their correspondence was assessed by linear regression across all simulated replay candidates.

Third, to demonstrate our method’s capability in identifying replays of novel stimulus sequences, we simulated responses of 100 neurons to a sequence of five discrete non-spatial stimuli (S1-S5; 1 s each; Fig. 6a) repeated 20 times. Each neuron was assumed to be selectively responsive to one stimulus, with mean firing rates set at 11 Hz for target stimuli and 1 Hz for non-target stimuli. Twenty neurons were assigned to each stimulus. To simulate replay of a novel stimulus sequence, we rearranged neural responses to match the order of the new sequence.

Fourth, to demonstrate our method’s capability in identifying replays of novel spatial trajectories, we simulated spatial firing of 100 place cells on a plus maze. Thirty-five non-directional place cells were assigned to each arm of the maze, and some cells were allowed to have multiple place fields. Two distinct spatial trajectories, left-to-right and top-to-bottom, were simulated for 20 trials each (Fig. 6g), assuming a rat’s constant running speed (2 s per trajectory). For a novel trajectory (top-to-right), spike trains were generated by substituting the south-arm neural data with right-arm data for each trial (Fig. 6h).

For each set of simulated neural data, we generated spike sequences associated with SWRs during an inactive phase (replay candidates) by randomly selecting 10 spikes from a randomly chosen active-phase trial and compressing the time scale by a factor of 50.

Single-unit datasets

We analyzed single-unit data from previous studies20,25. To validate our method, we analyzed CA1 spike data recorded from rats performing a sequential spatial navigation task20 (Fig. 2). Of the 698 CA1 cells recorded from three rats, 648 (92.8%) were classified as putative pyramidal cells. Among these, 497 (76.7%) met the criteria for place cells—a high proportion likely attributable to the large maze size (1.5×0.6 m). Candidate replay events were defined as SWR-associated spike bursts involving at least five place cells20,21, yielding a total of 2,573 events. For the present analysis, we selected the session with the largest number of simultaneously recorded place cells (n = 35).

To test whether our method could detect non-spatial replays, we analyzed CA1 and visual cortex spike data recorded from head-fixed mice (n = 20) engaged in a passive viewing task (Allen Institute Neuropixels data; ‘functional connectivity’ stimulus set25). The analysis included only putative pyramidal neurons (mean firing rates during movie stimuli <10 Hz) and 9816 candidate replay events, defined as spikes occurring within a 100-ms window following SWR onset26.

Calcium imaging

Three male C57BL/6 J mice (8-18 weeks old; 25-30 g) were anesthetized with isoflurane for stereotaxic injection of AAV1-CamKII-GCaMP6f (Addgene #100834-AAV1; 0.4 μL per site, 0.05 μL/min) into the dorsal CA1 (AP: −1.98 mm; ML: 1.15 and 1.65 mm; DV: −1.15 mm). Two weeks later, a 1.0-mm-diameter GRIN lens (length 4.0 mm; Inscopix, CA, USA) was implanted above CA1 (AP: −1.94 mm; ML: 1.4 mm; DV: −1.7 mm) following cortical aspiration. After 5 weeks of recovery, a baseplate was affixed for imaging.

Mice were water-restricted to 80-85% of their initial body weight and trained for two weeks to shuttle between two reward platforms (10 × 10 cm) on a 1-m-long arc-shaped linear track (width, 3 cm). During five consecutive daily calcium imaging sessions, mice completed an average of 19.3 ± 3.7 laps per session. Sliding doors delayed re-entry to the track for 15-25 s after delivery of a 4 μL water reward. Calcium activity was also recorded during 25-min pre- and post-task rest periods on a separate platform located 70 cm away from the track.

Calcium fluorescence images were acquired using the nVista imaging system (Inscopix, CA, USA) at 20 Hz with 0.2mW/mm2 LED power and 1/4x spatial downsampling. Motion correction was performed using Inscopix Data Processing Software (v1.6), and the corrected videos were analyzed using the constrained non-negative matrix factorization for endoscopic data (CNMF-E, v1.1.2) method. Deconvolved spike event signals extracted from CNMF-E were used for all calcium imaging analyses43.

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST; approval number KA-2022-31). Mice were housed in the KAIST animal facility under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with ambient temperature maintained at 20–22°C and humidity at 30–70%, in accordance with institutional guidelines.

We analyzed the last two sessions of calcium imaging data collected over five consecutive days. Only neurons with a mean event rate > 0.05 Hz (to exclude low-activity cells) and <10 Hz (to exclude putative inhibitory cells) were included in the analysis.

Place cells were defined based on their spatial information content44. To assess the statistical significance of each neuron’s spatial tuning, we circularly shifted calcium spike events relative to the animal’s position data, disrupting spatial associations while preserving intrinsic spike timing45,46. This procedure was repeated 1,000 times per neuron to generate a null distribution of spatial information scores. A neuron was classified as a place cell if its observed spatial information score exceeded the 95th percentile of the null distribution (i.e., P < 0.05). Online firing templates were constructed using calcium imaging data acquired at movement speeds ≥ 5 cm/s.

To identify population synchrony events (PSEs), we computed spike counts within 900-ms time windows (sliding in 50-ms steps) and classified time windows with spike counts in the top 5% of the distribution as PSEs. The use of a 900-ms window reflects the substantially slower temporal dynamics of calcium signals compared to action potentials and is comparable to time windows used in prior calcium imaging studies of sequential activity23. We note that this temporal scale differs from the brief (~100–200 ms) sharp-wave ripple–associated replay events typically identified in electrophysiological recordings.

To identify PSE-associated replay events, we selected all replay candidates occurring within PSEs at movement speeds <5 cm/s during the post-task rest period, lasting 250-900 ms (5 to 18 time bins), and comprising calcium events from at least five distinct neurons. Once a candidate was classified as a replay event, subsequent detection was restricted to candidates occurring at least 500 ms thereafter to prevent multiple counts of the same replay event.

An important consideration when interpreting these results is the difference in temporal scale between replay identified using calcium imaging and SWR-associated replay detected electrophysiologically. SWR-associated spike replay is typically confined to brief events lasting ~100–200 ms, whereas the replay-like activity identified here spans longer windows (~900 ms), reflecting the slower dynamics of calcium indicators. This difference likely reflects methodological constraints inherent to calcium imaging, though it may also point to fundamentally different structures of sequential spiking during online and offline states — a possibility that cannot be resolved with the current data. Whether calcium-based and electrophysiology-based replay represent the same underlying phenomenon at different timescales, or distinct forms of sequential reactivation, remains an open question that warrants direct comparison in future studies.

Weighted correlation

We identified replay events using the conventional weighted correlation method, as previously outlined20. Briefly, Bayes’ rule was used to decode spatial positions from the spikes of a candidate replay event, based on each cell’s spatial tuning curve during the active period. A linear regression was then performed between decoded positions and spike times, weighted by the posterior probabilities of the decoded positions. Replay significance (P-value) was assessed by comparing the original R² value to a distribution of R² values obtained from spike-identity-shuffled surrogates; the P-value was defined as the proportion of shuffled correlations exceeding the original (Fig. 1e).

Selection of active and inactive periods

For the rat single-unit dataset (Fig. 2), active periods were defined as epochs during which animals were running (movement speed ≥ 4 cm/s), and inactive (offline) periods were defined as reward-zone occupancy epochs, consistent with the original study. For the mouse calcium imaging dataset (Fig. 3), active periods were defined as epochs with movement speed ≥ 5 cm/s during task performance, and inactive periods as stationary epochs (speed <5 cm/s) during rest periods.

For head-fixed movie-viewing datasets (Figs. 4, 5), active and inactive periods were distinguished using pupil diameter as an index of arousal. Inactive periods were identified within the rest epoch. Inspection of the pupil width distribution during rest revealed a consistent bimodal structure across recording sessions (Supplementary Fig. 7a), suggesting that rest epochs comprise two distinct arousal states. A two-component Gaussian mixture model was fit to the pupil width distribution from each rest period, and the intersection of the two Gaussian components was taken as a rest-period-specific threshold. Replay candidate events occurring in windows where pupil width exceeded this threshold—corresponding to high-arousal epochs—were excluded from further analysis (11.36% of all candidates).

Active periods were defined as movie-viewing trials in which animals were in a high-arousal, attentive state. Because pupil width reflects not only arousal but also luminance-driven and light-adaptation effects, baseline pupil levels can differ systematically across movie blocks independent of arousal—reflecting luminance history, retinal adaptation, or slow brain-state drift across the session. Pooling measurements across blocks may therefore confound arousal-related variability with stimulus-history–dependent pupil dynamics. Accordingly, low-arousal trials were identified separately within each movie block. Because within-block pupil distributions were often skewed, we used a robust criterion based on median absolute deviation (MAD): trials with pupil width below (median – 3 × MAD) were classified as low-arousal and excluded (Supplementary Fig. 7b). The numbers of excluded trials were 11, 13, and 12 (1.83%, 2.17%, and 2.00%) for the Gabor stimulus, pre-rest movie, and post-rest movie, respectively; for the pre- and post-rest shuffled movie, 4 and 3 trials were excluded out (2.00% and 1.50%). The remaining trials were used to compute ordered spike probability matrices. These criteria were applied consistently across all analyses: online firing templates were constructed exclusively from high-arousal active periods, and replay candidate events were evaluated exclusively from low-arousal inactive periods. Results were robust to more stringent thresholds (lower 10th and 25th percentiles of pupil width).

Estimation of ordered firing probabilities during active phase

In our framework, ordered firing probability matrices are constructed from pairwise spike ordering statistics accumulated across trials to capture the dominant sequential structure expressed by the neural population during a defined active phase. This formulation assumes that neural activity within the selected active period reflects a relatively consistent sequential organization, which then serves as a reference model for replay detection. When multiple sequential structures are expected—such as across task blocks, movement directions, or stimulus types—we construct separate ordered firing probability matrices for each condition. This constitutes an explicit modeling choice regarding the number of assumed sequential structures present in the data, and these assumptions define the reference temporal structure against which candidate replay events are evaluated.

Replay detection based on log-sequence likelihood

Replay events during rest can reflect either forward replay, where spike sequences follow the same temporal order as during the active phase, or reverse replay, where the order is reversed. To capture both types, we calculated the difference in log-likelihoods between the forward sequence (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{1:n}$$\end{document}R1:n) and the reverse sequence (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{n:1}$$\end{document}Rn:1) for each candidate event:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{{seq}}=\log \left({P}_{2}^{*}\left({R}_{1:n} | {S}_{1:n}\right)\right)-\log \left({P}_{2}^{*}\left({R}_{n:1} | {S}_{1:n}\right)\right).$$\end{document}Lseq=logP2*R1:n∣S1:n−logP2*Rn:1∣S1:n.

Replay events were identified by comparing each candidate’s Lseq value to a distribution derived from shuffled sequences (P < 0.05). Events with significant positive Lseq values were classified as forward replays, and those with significantly negative Lseq values as reverse replays. When the number of neurons contributing to each candidate differs, Lseq was normalized by the number of neuron pairs contributing to the likelihood calculation. For group-level analysis, all Lseq values were z-scored within each recording session to account for inter-session variability.

Correlation of population activities

To assess population-level similarity across trials, firing rates were estimated using Gaussian smoothing (σ = 1 s) and each trial was divided into 30 equally spaced time bins. For each time bin, the correlation between the population activity vectors of the first trial and each subsequent trial was computed, and these correlations were averaged across time bins to yield a single similarity score per trial pair.

Replay participation and response change

To quantify each neuron’s engagement in replay, we defined the participation index (Iparticipation) as the number of replay candidate events in which the neuron was active, normalized by its pre-rest firing rate. To assess firing rate changes across the rest period, we computed the difference between post-rest and pre-rest firing rates, normalized by the pre-rest rate (ΔF). To examine the relationship between Iparticipation and ΔF across neurons from all sessions, both measures were z-scored within each session. To exclude potential outliers, data points with absolute z-scored values greater than 5 for either variable were removed from the analysis.

To examine whether arousal modulates replay strength, we analyzed the relationship between pupil size—used as an index of arousal—and |Lseq| values of replay candidates. For each session, mean pupil sizes were computed separately for the pre- and post-rest periods and then z-scored within session to control for inter-session variability. Statistical tests

All statistical tests were conducted using MATLAB (R2023a). Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t test, Mann–Whitney U-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and χ2-test, as appropriate. Where multiple observations were drawn from the same animal (e.g., replay candidates), linear mixed-effects models were used with animal included as a random effect to account for within-animal correlations. Observed proportions of replay were compared against chance levels using a binomial test. To further validate this approach, chance-level replay probabilities were also estimated by randomly shuffling decoded sequences, and observed replay probabilities were compared against shuffle-derived chance levels using a one-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test. All tests were two-tailed unless otherwise specified and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results are reported as mean ± SEM unless noted otherwise.
