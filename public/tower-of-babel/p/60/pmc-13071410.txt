Clinical Outcomes of Endoscopic Radiofrequency Stretta Therapy for Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease Treatment: A Retrospective Analysis From 2 Tertiary Centers in Korea
Authors: Hyun Lim, Yuri Kim, Jin Hee Noh, Jung In Lee, Eun Jeong Gong, Boram Cha, Chan Hyuk Park, Da Hyun Jung, Ju Yup Lee, Sun Hyung Kang, In Kyung Yoo, Joo Young Cho, Do Hoon Kim, Endoscopic Therapy Instrument Research Group of the Korean Society of Neurogastroenterology Motility
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsEndoscopic anti-reflux therapy is a therapeutic option for gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), providing durable effects. However, clinical data from Korea remain limited. This study evaluates the clinical outcomes of endoscopic radiofrequency Stretta therapy in Korean patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 71 patients with GERD who underwent Stretta therapy at 2 tertiary hospitals in Korea between November 2015 and July 2021. Clinical outcomes, including patient satisfaction, medication cessation or reduction, and complications, were evaluated. Pre- and post-procedural esophageal manometry and 24-hour pH monitoring test results were also analyzed.ResultsPatient satisfaction rates at 1, 6, and 12 months post-procedure were 54.7% (35/64), 70.0% (28/40), and 75.0% (21/28), respectively. Medication cessation or reduction was achieved in 31.2% (20/64) at 1 month, 70.0% (28/40) at 6 months, and 67.9% (19/28) at 12 months. Esophageal manometry (n = 21) showed no significant changes in mean lower esophageal sphincter pressure (18.7 mmHg [2.5-52.9] vs 17.4 mmHg [0.0-43.0], P = 0.702) or mean integrated relaxation pressure (8.2 mmHg [0.0-28.0] vs 10.1 mmHg [0.0-31.0], P = 0.840). The 24-hour pH monitoring (n = 18) demonstrated a nonsignificant decrease in acid exposure time (pH < 4) from 2.3% (0.0-8.4) to 1.6% (0.0-7.3) (P = 0.182). Similarly, the DeMeester score decreased non-significantly from 8.4 (0.8-27.7) to 6.6 (0.8-21.8) (P = 0.352). No procedure-related complications occurred.ConclusionEndoscopic radiofrequency Stretta therapy appears to be a safe treatment option for GERD and may provide favorable patient satisfaction and medication reduction.

Introduction

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is defined as a condition in which the reflux of gastric contents into the esophagus causes troublesome symptoms and/or complications.1,2 The prevalence of GERD has been steadily increasing in East Asian countries, including Korea.3,4 Although GERD can lead to serious outcomes such as esophageal adenocarcinoma in a minority of patients, it is more commonly associated with a marked reduction in quality of life.5,6 Moreover, GERD is a chronic condition characterized by frequent relapses following discontinuation of treatment.

The fundamental approach to treating GERD is the prevention of gastric contents, particularly gastric acid, from refluxing into the esophagus. However, first-line therapy for GERD typically involves acid-suppressive agents, such as proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) or potassium-competitive acid blockers, together with lifestyle modifications.2,7,8 Despite the widespread use of pharmacologic therapy, many patients fail to achieve adequate symptom control, and most experience relapse upon cessation of treatment. Surgical procedures aimed at preventing reflux, such as Nissen fundoplication, has been developed as an alternative treatment option.9,10 Nevertheless, surgery is associated with potential morbidity due to its invasive nature and may be less accessible in countries where anti-reflux surgery is not commonly performed.

Various endoscopic procedures designed to prevent reflux have been introduced as minimally invasive alternatives to surgery.11-15 These procedures have demonstrated varying degrees of long-term efficacy in managing GERD. Among them, Stretta therapy, which delivers radiofrequency energy to the gastroesophageal junction (EGJ), has shown promising results, although its precise mechanism of action remains unclear.16-18 Stretta therapy is currently performed in Korea; however, studies specifically evaluating its efficacy in the Korean patient population remain limited.

The present study was conducted to assess the current status and clinical effectiveness of endoscopic radiofrequency Stretta therapy in Korean patients with GERD.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective study conducted in Korea. Patients with GERD who underwent endoscopic radiofrequency therapy using the Stretta system were enrolled from 2 tertiary hospitals where the procedure was routinely performed. Clinical data were collected from procedures carried out between November 2015 and July 2021. The study protocol adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the participating institutions (Approval No. 2025-1284).

Data Collection and Outcomes

The following data were collected and analyzed: patients' baseline characteristics, including age, sex, height, weight, and underlying medical conditions; duration and severity of GERD symptoms, along with response to PPI therapy; and pre-procedural endoscopic findings, such as the Los Angeles (LA) classification of esophagitis, presence of hiatal hernia, and gastroesophageal flap valve grade. Additional pre-procedural evaluations included 24-hour ambulatory pH monitoring and high-resolution manometry (HRM). Procedural details, including the date of Stretta therapy and any procedure-related complications, were recorded. Post-procedural assessments included patient-reported satisfaction, changes in GERD-related symptoms, status of medication cessation or reduction at 1, 6, and 12 months, and follow-up findings from 24-hour ambulatory pH monitoring and HRM.

GERD was classified as either proven GERD or symptomatic GERD.2 Patients were considered to have proven GERD if they demonstrated an acid exposure time (AET) ≥ 4% on 24-hour pH monitoring or LA grade A or higher esophagitis on endoscopy. Patients who either had normal or minimal findings (LA-M) on 24-hour ambulatory pH monitoring and endoscopy, or those who did not undergo these tests but who reported typical GERD symptoms (eg, heartburn or regurgitation), were classified as having symptomatic GERD.

The primary outcome of this study was the cessation or reduction of GERD-related medication use following Stretta therapy. Secondary outcomes included changes in patient-reported satisfaction scores, GERD-related symptoms, and objective physiological parameters before and after the procedure. These parameters, assessed by HRM and 24-hour ambulatory pH monitoring, included resting lower esophageal sphincter (LES) pressure, integrated relaxation pressure (IRP), AET (percentage of time with pH < 4), and the DeMeester score. HRM and 24-hour ambulatory pH monitoring were performed when feasible; however, test availability and indication for Stretta therapy varied across the participating centers.

Outcome Measurement

Stretta therapy was offered to patients with persistent GERD symptoms despite at least 8 weeks of standard-dose PPI treatment, or to those intolerant or unwilling to maintain long-term pharmacologic therapy. The procedure was performed according to the standardized protocol provided by the manufacturer.19 Procedure-related complications were categorized as mild, moderate, or severe based on clinical severity. Response to PPI therapy was defined as a reduction of > 50% in typical GERD symptoms, including heartburn and regurgitation. Changes in GERD-related symptoms were assessed using a 4-point scale as follows: 0, asymptomatic; 1, mild, symptoms with spontaneous remission that do not interfere with normal activity or sleep; 2, moderate, symptoms with spontaneous but delayed remission that slightly interfere with normal activity or sleep; and 3, severe, persistent symptoms without spontaneous remission that significantly interfere with normal activity or sleep. Discontinuation of GERD-related medication, specifically PPIs, was classified into 3 categories: 0, complete cessation; 1, reduction by > 50%; 2, continued use without reduction. The decision to discontinue GERD-related medication was based on symptom relief as assessed by the physician, and the continuation of medication was determined according to the presence or absence of symptoms. Patient satisfaction was assessed using a binary 2-point scale: 0, satisfied and 1, unsatisfied.

Statistical Methods

The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate associations among categorical variables, and the independent-samples t test was used for non-categorical variables. Pre- and post-procedural esophageal manometry and 24-hour ambulatory pH monitoring results were also analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed rank test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 29.0; SPSS, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA), and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 71 patients with GERD who underwent Stretta therapy were enrolled. Baseline characteristics of these patients are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 47.6 ± 13.6 years, and 47 patients (66.2%) were male. The mean body mass index was 23.0 ± 3.5 kg/m². Regarding GERD-related symptoms, heartburn was reported in 58 patients (81.7%) and regurgitation in 55 patients (77.5%). The response rate to PPI therapy was 18.3%. The mean duration of GERD-related symptoms was 44.2 ± 7.2 months. Endoscopic findings revealed esophagitis in 40 patients (56.3%), with LA grade A in 33 patients (46.5%) and grade B or C in seven patients (9.8%). Hiatal hernia was observed in 22 patients (31.0%). The distribution of gastroesophageal flap valve grades was as follows: grade 1 in 33 patients (46.5%), grade 2 in 26 patients (36.6%), and grade 3 in 12 patients (16.9%). The mean follow-up duration was 11.3 months (range: 1 to 69 months). No moderate or severe procedure-related complications were reported.

Clinical Outcomes After Stretta Therapy

Clinical outcomes at 1, 6, and 12 months following Stretta therapy are summarized in Table 2 and Figures 1 and 2. At 1 month (n = 64), complete cessation of GERD-related medication was achieved in 5 patients (7.8%), and medication reduction by > 50% was reported in 15 patients (23.4%). The majority of patients (68.8%) continued GERD-related medication without reduction. Regarding symptom improvement, only 1 patient (1.6%) reported being asymptomatic, whereas 23 patients (35.9%) had mild symptoms that did not interfere with daily activity or sleep. Moderate and severe symptoms were reported in 14 (21.9%) and 26 (40.6%) patients, respectively. Patient satisfaction was noted in 35 patients (54.7%).

At 6 months (n = 40), the rate of complete GERD-related medication cessation increased to 17.5%, and 52.5% of patients reported a reduction in medication use by > 50%. Continued medication use without reduction decreased to 30.0%. Symptom scores also improved, with 1 patient (2.5%) being asymptomatic, 17 (42.5%) reporting mild symptoms, 13 (32.5%) reporting moderate symptoms, and only 9 (22.5%) experiencing severe symptoms. Patient satisfaction improved to 70.0%.

At 12 months (n = 28), clinical outcomes remained stable. Complete cessation of GERD-related medication was observed in 5 patients (17.9%), and 14 (50.0%) reported a significant reduction in GERD-related medication use. The proportion of patients with no symptoms increased to 7.2%, whereas 35.7% and 32.1% reported mild and moderate symptoms, respectively. Only 25.0% of patients continued to experience severe symptoms. The satisfaction rate further improved to 75.0%.

Esophageal Manometry and Ambulatory 24-hour pH Monitoring

HRM and ambulatory 24-hour pH monitoring (n = 63) prior to the Stretta procedure revealed near-normal findings, with a mean AET of 2.1% (range: 0.0-9.0), mean DeMeester score of 7.2 (range: 0.8-29.6), mean basal LES pressure of 20.7 mmHg (range: 2.5-60.0), and mean IRP of 9.5 mmHg (range: 0-28).

HRM was performed in 21 patients to assess LES function before and after Stretta therapy (Fig. 3). The mean resting LES pressure decreased slightly from 18.7 mmHg (range: 2.5-52.9) before the procedure to 17.4 mmHg (range: 0.0-43.0) after the procedure; however, this change was not statistically significant (P = 0.702). Similarly, the mean IRP increased from 8.2 mmHg (range: 0.0-28.0) to 10.1 mmHg (range: 0.0-31.0), also without statistical significance (P = 0.840).

Ambulatory 24-hour pH monitoring was conducted in 18 patients to evaluate acid exposure and reflux control before and after Stretta therapy (Fig. 4). The mean percentage of time with esophageal pH < 4 (acid exposure time) decreased from 2.3% (range: 0.0-8.4) at baseline to 1.6% (range: 0.0-7.3) after the procedure, with no significant difference (P = 0.182). The mean DeMeester score also showed no significant change, decreasing slightly from 8.4 (range: 0.8-27.7) to 6.6 (range: 0.8-21.8) (P = 0.352).

Clinical Outcomes of Patients With Proven Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease or Symptomatic Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease After Stretta Therapy

Among the 71 patients, 45 (63.4%) were classified as proven GERD and 26 (36.6%) as symptomatic GERD. In the proven GERD group, complete cessation or ≥ 50% reduction of GERD-related medication was achieved in 65.2% at 12 months, and patient satisfaction increased from 33.3% at 1 month to 69.6% at 12 months (Supplementary Table 1). In the symptomatic GERD group, medication reduction was observed in 80% at 12 months, and symptom improvement was reported in all patients, with satisfaction rates maintained above 80% throughout follow-up (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

The prevalence of GERD has been steadily increasing in Korea, accompanied by an increasing demand for both pharmacologic and surgical treatment options. However, a therapeutic gap persists between these approaches, prompting the development of endoscopic modalities that provide a less invasive yet effective and durable alternative.20 Among these, Stretta therapy, an endoscopic intervention delivering radiofrequency energy to the EGJ, has shown promising results. In Western populations, it has been associated with significant improvements in reflux symptoms, enhanced quality of life, and reduced dependence on GERD-related medication.16-18 Despite its growing global adoption, evidence regarding its efficacy and safety in Korean patients has been limited. As the largest study conducted in Korea to date, this study suggests that Stretta therapy is a safe treatment option and may improve symptoms and reduce GERD-related medication use.

The present study provides clinical evidence supporting the utility of Stretta therapy in a Korean cohort. At 6 and 12 months, more than two-thirds of patients achieved either complete cessation or significant reduction in GERD-related medication use, and over half reported satisfaction with symptom control. These findings are consistent with previous studies. A comprehensive meta-analysis of 28 studies involving 2468 patients demonstrated that Stretta therapy significantly reduced heartburn symptom scores (mean difference −1.53, P < 0.001) and markedly improved GERD-specific health-related quality of life scores (mean difference −14.6, P < 0.001).21 Furthermore, PPI dependence reduced substantially, with only 49% of baseline PPI users continuing to require them at follow-up.21 Collectively, these findings support the clinical efficacy of Stretta therapy in improving GERD-related symptoms and reducing medication dependence in Korean patients.

The primary pathophysiological abnormality in GERD is dysfunction of the EGJ as an anti-reflux barrier, although a certain degree of EGJ incompetence may be considered physiological.22 Although the exact mechanism remains incompletely understood, Stretta therapy is hypothesized to induce submucosal fibrosis and muscularis propria hypertrophy, thereby reducing the frequency of transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxations and EGJ compliance, while increasing LES and gastric yield pressures.23 However, in the present study, despite significant improvements in subjective symptom control and reduced medication use, objective esophageal function parameters assessed by HRM and pH monitoring did not show statistically significant changes. The mean LES pressure and IRP before and after the procedure remained comparable, and no significant changes were observed in AET or DeMeester scores. A similar absence of measurable physiological improvement was reported in a meta-analysis of 4 randomized controlled trials, which found no significant differences in esophageal acid exposure or LES pressure between patients treated with Stretta therapy and those receiving PPI therapy or sham procedures.24 These findings suggest that the clinical benefits of Stretta therapy may stem more from sensory modulation and functional remodeling rather than from measurable anatomic or mechanical alterations at the EGJ.

In this study, contrary to expectation, Stretta therapy demonstrated comparable efficacy in patients with symptomatic GERD and those with proven GERD. Although pH monitoring is considered the gold standard for detecting abnormal esophageal acid exposure, conventional 24-hour ambulatory pH monitoring has limited sensitivity (85-93%). Prior studies have shown that a substantial proportion of patients with endoscopically confirmed GERD or erosive esophagitis exhibit normal acid exposure times (false negative rate: 15-30%), suggesting that some patients classified as symptomatic GERD may actually have unrecognized pathological reflux.25,26 Furthermore, the pathophysiology of PPI-refractory GERD extends beyond acid reflux to include bile reflux, esophageal hypersensitivity, and other mechanisms, which may also be positively influenced by Stretta therapy. Taken together, these factors may explain the absence of significant differences in clinical outcomes between the 2 groups in this study.

Importantly, Stretta therapy was found to be highly safe in this study, with no moderate or severe complications reported. Serious adverse events, such as esophageal perforation or aspiration pneumonia, are exceedingly rare, with an overall complication rate of < 1%.20,24 Most adverse events, when they occur, are self-limited and include transient chest discomfort or dysphagia. This favorable safety profile supports Stretta therapy as an appealing option for patients who are poor surgical candidates or prefer a less invasive approach.

Despite the encouraging findings, this study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design is inherently prone to selection and reporting biases, which may affect the internal validity of the results. Moreover, the symptomatic GERD group without objective evidence of pathologic reflux may encompass patients with functional heartburn or reflux hypersensitivity, categorized as disorders of gut–brain interaction, potentially influencing the interpretation of the observed clinical responses. Second, follow-up data were incomplete for a subset of patients, potentially limiting the accurate assessment of long-term therapeutic outcomes and hindering analysis of predictive factors associated with good responders. In addition, interpretation of parameters assessed by HRM and pH monitoring should take into account the substantial follow-up loss over time and the limited number of patients who underwent post-procedural HRM (n = 21) and pH monitoring (n = 18), which may have reduced the statistical power to detect meaningful changes. Third, the absence of standardized, validated instruments for symptom assessment and health-related quality of life limits the generalizability and comparability of the subjective outcome measures. Finally, the indications for Stretta therapy were not uniformly defined across participating centers, and objective evidence of GERD was not consistently available in all patients.

Stretta therapy is currently approved in several countries as a therapeutic option for patients with GERD who have persistent symptoms despite pharmacologic therapy and who are either unwilling or unsuitable candidates for surgery. However, in Korea, its use remains limited, and standardized therapeutic indications have yet to be established. The present findings suggest that Stretta therapy may serve as a viable treatment option for Korean patients with refractory or recurrent GERD, particularly those who are PPI-dependent or relapse-prone. Moving forward, establishing national consensus guidelines to define optimal patient selection criteria and conducting prospective studies to evaluate long-term outcomes in Korean populations will be essential.
