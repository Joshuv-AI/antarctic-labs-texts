Introduction

CAR-T cell therapy has transformed the treatment of several hematological malignancies, but its extension to solid tumors has been slow and uneven. Solid tumors are spatially heterogeneous, physically compartmentalized and metabolically hostile; they restrict immune-cell trafficking, impose suppressive myeloid and fibroblast niches, and expose therapeutic T cells to hypoxia, nutrient competition and chronic antigen stimulation [1–4]. These barriers mean that receptor engineering alone is unlikely to be sufficient.

Recent clinical studies have nevertheless changed the tone of the field. Locoregional IL13Rα2 CAR-T cells have shown safety and clinical activity in recurrent high-grade glioma; intraventricular CARv3-TEAM-E cells and intrathecal bivalent EGFR/IL13Rα2 CAR-T cells produced rapid but often transient responses in recurrent glioblastoma; repeated intracerebroventricular B7-H3 CAR-T dosing has been tolerable in diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma; and the CLDN18.2-directed CAR-T product satri-cel has provided randomized evidence of survival benefit in a solid tumor population [5–10]. These signals indicate that solid-tumor CAR-T therapy is biologically feasible, but they also reveal how fragile responses can be when antigen escape, poor persistence or local immune suppression remains unresolved.

A parallel body of research has begun to explore whether small molecules, biological materials, probiotics and natural products can condition either CAR-T cells or their target tissues. TCM-derived compounds are relevant in this setting because they contain chemically defined monomers, standardized botanical formulations, polysaccharide fractions and microbiota-linked metabolites that may act on immune metabolism, chemokines, stromal barriers or inflammatory toxicity. In this review, the term TCM-derived is used pragmatically: classical formulations and compounds are considered within the TCM scope, whereas microbiota-derived metabolites or synthetic drugs are discussed only as mechanistic analogues or development blueprints rather than as TCM agents themselves [11–17].

Why solid tumors remain difficult for CAR-T cells

The major barriers to CAR-T activity in solid tumors can be grouped into six interdependent categories. First, antigen specificity is limited. Unlike CD19 or BCMA in hematological malignancies, most solid-tumor antigens are tumor-associated rather than tumor-specific. Targeting HER2, mesothelin, GD2, B7H3, CLDN18.2, PSCA, GPC3 or CD70 can produce tumor recognition, but low-level antigen expression in normal tissues creates a narrow therapeutic window. Second, antigen heterogeneity and clonal evolution allow escape. Even when an antigen is abundant at baseline, expression may vary across lesions, within a lesion or over time under treatment pressure.

Third, CAR-T cells must physically reach tumor cells. Abnormal vasculature, high interstitial pressure, dense extracellular matrix and cancer-associated fibroblast networks create an access problem. Brain tumors add the blood-brain barrier and cerebrospinal-fluid compartmentalization. Fourth, the tumor microenvironment actively suppresses T cells through PD-1/PD-L1, TGF-β, IL-10, adenosine, arginase, IDO, lactate, hypoxia and suppressive myeloid or regulatory lymphocyte subsets. Fifth, chronic antigen exposure and metabolic competition accelerate exhaustion, senescence and loss of memory-like states. Sixth, potent CAR-T activation can cause cytokine-release syndrome, neurotoxicity or local inflammatory injury, whereas overly cautious activation may produce only transient disease stabilization.

These barriers are linked. A dense stroma can limit infiltration, which reduces the number of CAR-T cells exposed to tumor antigen; low antigen dose may produce suboptimal stimulation, whereas chronic stimulation in an immunosuppressive niche can drive terminal exhaustion. Therefore, a useful adjuvant should not be evaluated only by whether it kills tumor cells in vitro. The key question is whether it changes the in vivo state of the CAR-T-tumor system: trafficking, antigen visibility, myeloid suppression, metabolic fitness, memory formation, cytokine balance and tissue safety [18–24]. Search strategy and evidence classification

This article is a critical narrative review rather than a formal systematic review. To improve transparency, the literature was surveyed across PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, ClinicalTrials.gov and major Chinese-language biomedical databases through May 2026. Search terms included combinations of CAR-T, chimeric antigen receptor, solid tumor, tumor microenvironment, traditional Chinese medicine, botanical drug, natural product, immunometabolism, mitochondrial fitness, Astragalus polysaccharide, YIV-906, ginsenoside, curcumin, urolithin A, Scutellaria, macrophage polarization, PD-L1, STING, hypoxia and chemokine.

Studies were prioritized when they directly tested CAR-T cells, adoptive T cells, CAR-T-relevant manufacturing or a clearly defined tumor-microenvironment mechanism. Purely predictive network-pharmacology or molecular-docking studies, reports without immune-functional validation and studies limited to tumor-cell cytotoxicity were not treated as primary evidence. When a cited study contained both experimental functional data and predictive network-pharmacology or docking components, only the experimental component was used to support the evidence hierarchy.

Conceptual rationale for TCM-CAR-T combinations

TCM-derived agents are often described as multi-target regulators. In conventional drug development, this feature can be a liability because it complicates target attribution, pharmacokinetic control and regulatory assessment. In solid-tumor CAR-T therapy, however, controlled network-level modulation may be useful if it acts on the correct nodes. The tumor microenvironment is not a single pathway but a system of cellular, metabolic and physical constraints. The most relevant TCM activities for CAR-T therapy are therefore not generic anticancer effects but mechanisms that support engineered T cells: increasing tumor chemokines, improving vascular or stromal access, decreasing M2-like macrophages or MDSCs, reducing PD-L1 and TGF-β signaling, protecting mitochondrial function, inducing immunogenic cell death, activating innate immune sensing or dampening excessive inflammatory amplification.

Directly relevant evidence is emerging. YIV-906, a botanical drug derived from Huang Qin Tang and composed of Scutellaria baicalensis, Paeonia lactiflora, Glycyrrhiza uralensis and Ziziphus jujuba, has been shown to enhance NFAT activity, induce downstream TCR-signaling phosphorylation, inhibit SHP1/2 and increase CAR-triggered NFAT activity and CAR-T killing in experimental systems [25]. Astragalus polysaccharide enhanced GPC3 CAR-T effects in hepatocellular carcinoma models by promoting CD122 + CXCR3+PD-1- memory-like CD8 + CAR-T cells, STAT5 activation, CXCL9/CXCL10 expression and CAR-T chemotaxis [26]. Ginsenoside Rg1 improved adoptive T-cell and CAR-T effector function, apparently through metabolic reprogramming and mitochondrial biogenesis [27]. Curcumin enhanced anti-CD19 CAR-T cytotoxicity in vitro while reducing IL-2 and IFN-γ production, suggesting a possible decoupling of killing from excessive cytokine release [28]. Fe-curcumin nanoparticles provide a more solid-tumor-oriented example by combining CAR-T cells with an immunomodulatory nanomaterial that mitigates exhaustion and senescence, alleviates hypoxia, reprograms macrophages and improves tumor control [29].

These studies should be interpreted carefully. Only a small number directly test CAR-T cells, most remain preclinical, and their tumor models, CAR constructs and dosing strategies differ substantially. Nevertheless, they define a plausible development space: TCM-derived adjuvants may be used before manufacturing to improve T-cell fitness, during ex vivo expansion to preserve memory, before infusion to condition the tumor microenvironment, concurrently to sustain infiltration and survival, or after infusion to reduce inflammation and support persistence. Each use case requires different pharmacology and safety testing. Evidence hierarchy: separating proof from hypothesis

A central requirement for this field is evidence discipline. A direct CAR-T study in a solid-tumor model should not be weighted the same as a tumor-cell study showing antioxidant or anti-inflammatory activity. However, the hierarchy should be interpreted within the intended use case. For ex vivo CAR-T conditioning, primary human T-cell or CAR-T expansion data may be more informative than a reporter-cell CAR assay, whereas in vivo tumor-conditioning claims require models that can test trafficking, immune suppression and tumor ecology. Likewise, a quality-marker paper that improves reproducible manufacturing is important for translation but does not demonstrate CAR-T synergy. We therefore propose a five-level hierarchy. Level 1 evidence directly evaluates a defined TCM-derived product with a defined CAR-T construct in a solid-tumor model and includes CAR-T-specific readouts such as expansion, infiltration, persistence, exhaustion markers, cytokines and tumor control. Level 2 evidence directly evaluates CAR-T activation or killing but in a non-solid-tumor setting, reporter line or simplified in vitro system. Level 3 evidence evaluates adoptive T cells or primary T cells and can support ex vivo conditioning hypotheses. Level 4 evidence demonstrates a CAR-T-relevant microenvironmental mechanism such as PD-L1 degradation, macrophage repolarization, STING activation or hypoxia relief but has not yet tested CAR-T cells. Level 5 evidence defines chemical consistency, processing, target annotation or formulation quality and should be used to design rather than validate CAR-T combinations.

This hierarchy protects the field from overclaiming while clarifying development priorities. Level 4 and Level 5 studies are not weak; they are pre-translation assets. Their value lies in generating testable CAR-T hypotheses and standardizing product quality. The practical question is therefore not only how high a study ranks, but whether its model, exposure window and product class match the proposed clinical use.

From chemical consistency to functional consistency

The main obstacles to combining TCM with CAR-T therapy are unclear biological mechanism, variable product composition and insufficient linkage between chemical quality and immune function. CAR-T products undergo strict identity, potency and release tests. A botanical or natural-product intervention used with engineered cells should satisfy equivalent expectations: authenticated source, defined processing, reproducible extraction, marker quantification, impurity control, pharmacokinetics and immune-functional potency.

Related research has moved TCM quality control from single-marker detection toward efficacy-relevant standardization. High-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry, fingerprinting and chemometrics have been used to screen characteristic quality markers of herbal medicines (Table 2). In the CAR-T setting, however, chromatographic similarity should be treated as necessary but not sufficient. Release criteria should include immune-functional assays that are relevant to the intended CAR-T bottleneck [37, 38].

TCM-derived quality markers and CAR-T-relevant functional standardization

Scutellaria-related evidence should be separated into quality-marker studies, formulation-level immunopharmacology and purified-flavonoid mechanisms. Quality-marker studies define processing-dependent glycoside/aglycone ratios. YIV-906 provides formulation-level evidence for NFAT- and SHP1/2-related T-cell signaling modulation. Purified flavonoids such as baicalein or baicalin provide mechanistic hypotheses for PD-L1, macrophage and inflammatory regulation, but they require direct testing in primary CAR-T systems before being described as CAR-T adjuvants.

A study on Sophora flowers revealed that high chromatographic similarity cannot guarantee equivalent pharmacological efficacy. By correlating HPLC peaks with DPPH, ABTS, FRAP antioxidant assays and hyaluronidase inhibitory activity, researchers identified rutin, kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside and isorhamnetin-3-O-rutinoside as efficacy markers [39]. This does not establish CAR-T-enhancing activity. Instead, it provides a methodological lesson: chemical similarity is insufficient, and future TCM-CAR-T products should be released using immune-functional assays rather than chromatographic similarity alone.

The ferulic-acid derivative HMY-PAA provides a third lesson. By designing a molecule around succinate dehydrogenase inhibition, mitochondrial localization and ROS control, a broad antioxidant natural-product scaffold was converted into a target-annotated lead [40]. Solid-tumor CAR-T cells are strongly affected by mitochondrial fitness, ROS, hypoxia and metabolite competition. Thus, TCM-derived compounds with defined effects on mitochondrial metabolism may be used either to condition CAR-T cells ex vivo or to reduce the metabolic hostility of the tumor. The requirement is target clarity: antioxidant language alone is insufficient; the relevant mitochondrial node, exposure window and effect on T-cell cytotoxicity must be quantified. Direct evidence for TCM and natural-product enhancement of CAR-T therapy

At present, the direct evidence base is small but mechanistically diverse. It includes botanical formulations that enhance T-cell signaling, polysaccharides that support memory and trafficking, saponins that improve effector metabolism, polyphenols that modulate cytokines and nanomaterials that combine phytochemicals with tumor-microenvironment remodeling. The key interpretive step is to rank evidence by proximity to CAR-T therapy: direct CAR-T studies should be separated from general immunotherapy or tumor-cell studies.

YIV-906 is one of the most mechanistically informative examples. Lam and colleagues showed that YIV-906 enhances NFAT activity in T-cell models, rescues PD-1/PD-L1-suppressed signaling, induces phosphorylation of TCR downstream proteins and enhances CAR-CD19-CD3ζ-triggered NFAT activity and target-cell killing [25]. The Scutellaria component and several compounds from it contributed to this activity. Because NFAT is central to CAR-triggered transcriptional activation, this work supports the idea that a standardized botanical drug can tune the CAR signaling cascade. However, the study was largely based on Jurkat models and CD19 systems; translation to primary human CAR-T cells and solid tumors remains necessary.

Astragalus polysaccharide provides one of the strongest direct solid-tumor CAR-T examples. In GPC3-targeted CAR-T models of hepatocellular carcinoma, APS improved CAR-T persistence, increased CD122 + CXCR3+PD-1- memory cells, enhanced STAT5 phosphorylation, increased CXCL9/CXCL10 and improved tumor control [26]. These findings support APS as an in vivo or peri-infusion maintenance candidate rather than a manufacturing additive. The model nevertheless used severely immunodeficient hosts and cyclophosphamide preconditioning, so endogenous myeloid remodeling, lymphoid crosstalk and lymphodepletion drug interactions remain incompletely tested.

Ginsenoside Rg1 has been tested in adoptive cell therapy and CAR-T-adjacent contexts. Rg1-treated T cells displayed stronger effector functions, with transcriptomic evidence for metabolic reprogramming and mitochondrial biogenesis, and a brief CAR-T readout suggested functional enhancement [27]. This evidence is most appropriately classified as Level 3 because the CAR construct, tumor model and in vivo CAR-T context were not fully defined. Rg1 and related mitochondrial modulators should also be paired with CAR architecture: 4-1BB-based CARs are generally more compatible with oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial biogenesis, whereas CD28-based CARs are more glycolytic and mTOR-driven, so excessive OXPHOS bias could blunt early effector output in some constructs [41].

Curcumin studies provide a more complex message. In vitro, curcumin-treated anti-CD19 CAR-T cells showed enhanced tumor killing while reducing IL-2 and IFN-γ output [28, 42]. This may be useful if cytotoxicity is preserved while inflammatory toxicity is limited. However, IFN-γ also supports MHC upregulation, antigen presentation, CXCL9/CXCL10 induction and epitope spreading in solid tumors; excessive suppression could therefore compromise durable control. The mechanistic network-pharmacology and docking components of this study should be considered hypothesis-generating, whereas the killing and cytokine readouts provide the experimental evidence. For free curcumin and several flavonoids, poor oral bioavailability is a barrier mainly when direct tumor-core exposure is required; it is not a universal limitation for agents acting through systemic immune priming or microbiota-derived intermediates.

Taken together, these studies support a cautious conclusion: TCM-derived and natural-product agents can plausibly enhance selected CAR-T functions, but the field remains preclinical and mechanism-selective. The next stage should avoid broad claims that TCM enhances CAR-T therapy and instead ask which product class, in which exposure window, improves which CAR-T bottleneck without impairing manufacturing, lymphodepletion, cytotoxicity or safety.

Indirect but actionable mechanisms

Many TCM-derived compounds have not been directly combined with CAR-T cells but modulate mechanisms that determine CAR-T efficacy. These indirect mechanisms are valuable if they are framed as hypotheses for testing rather than proof of clinical synergy.

First, several flavones from Scutellaria baicalensis, including baicalin, baicalein, wogonin and oroxylin A, have been associated with regulation of inflammatory signaling, PD-L1, macrophage polarization, MDSC activity and tumor-cell death. Baicalein has been reported to promote autophagic degradation of PD-L1 and boost antitumor immunity, while reviews of baicalin and baicalein emphasize their capacity to reshape the tumor microenvironment [43–46]. For CAR-T therapy, reduced PD-L1 and suppressive myeloid tone could preserve activation and limit exhaustion, but this requires testing in CAR-T-specific models because CAR signaling differs from endogenous TCR activation.

Second, natural products can influence innate immune sensing and immunogenic cell death. Quercetin-coordinated manganese nanomaterials, STING agonist nanoparticles and natural-product-induced immunogenic cell death provide analogous strategies for converting cold tumors into chemokine-rich tissues [47–49]. The question for TCM-CAR-T combinations is whether such innate activation can increase CAR-T trafficking without inducing excessive systemic inflammation or harming T-cell viability.

Third, TCM-derived agents may act through macrophage and fibroblast niches. Solid-tumor CAR-T cells often fail at the stromal border; suppressive macrophages and fibroblasts create exclusion zones and metabolic sinks. Natural products and polysaccharides that appear to repolarize macrophages or remodel stroma are therefore relevant, but this claim requires appropriate immune models. Severely immunodeficient xenografts can measure human CAR-T expansion and tumor control, but they cannot fully test endogenous macrophage repolarization, Treg/MDSC dynamics or lymphoid crosstalk [50–53]. TME-directed adjuvants should therefore be validated in syngeneic models with murine surrogate CARs, humanized-immune-system mice or orthotopic models with spatial immune profiling.

Fourth, the gut microbiota may connect orally administered TCM to systemic immune-cell therapy. The intestinal microbiome has been associated with response and toxicity after anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy, and antibiotic exposure may affect outcomes [54, 55]. Urolithin A illustrates how a microbiota-derived natural metabolite can improve T-cell mitophagy and memory programs, even though it is not a classical TCM monomer [56–58]. Polysaccharides such as APS may act through systemic immune priming and gut-derived metabolites; in such cases, parent-compound tumor penetration is not necessarily the key pharmacological bottleneck.

Finally, TCM-derived agents could mitigate toxicity. Cytokine-release syndrome and neurotoxicity are not simply signs of efficacy; they can limit dose intensity and patient eligibility. Curcumin-related cytokine modulation, YIV-906 gastrointestinal cytoprotective history and anti-inflammatory TCM formulations suggest possible supportive roles. Yet toxicity mitigation must not blunt anti-tumor immunity. The ideal agent would maintain cytotoxic granule release and tumor chemokines while reducing systemic inflammatory amplification. This separation is experimentally testable and should be treated as a potency criterion [59, 60].

Designing TCM-CAR-T combinations

A rational TCM-CAR-T program should begin by defining the intended point of intervention and then matching the exposure window to the product class. Ex vivo CAR-T conditioning is the most stringent use case because any agent added during activation or expansion becomes part of the living-product manufacturing process. This setting should realistically be limited to structurally defined, highly purified monomers or metabolites, such as ginsenoside Rg1 or urolithin A, for which residual compound, endotoxin, sterility, identity and release-testing consequences can be quantified. Crude multi-herb extracts, complex botanical formulations and poorly defined polysaccharide fractions should not be introduced into ex vivo manufacturing unless they meet a substantially higher CMC and residual-impurity standard.

The second use case is in vivo pre-infusion tumor conditioning. Here, standardized formulations or polysaccharides may be more plausible because they are administered to the patient rather than added to the cell product. The aim is to enhance antigen presentation, chemokine secretion, vascular normalization, innate immune activation or stromal remodeling before CAR-T infusion. The third use case is concurrent maintenance after infusion, in which an oral or injectable agent supports persistence and migration. APS is a candidate example because of its reported effects on memory-like CXCR3 + CAR-T cells and CXCL9/CXCL10, but this strategy requires PK/PD testing and lymphodepletion interaction assessment.

The fourth use case is toxicity modulation, which may be relevant for high-potency CAR-T products or regional therapies that produce local inflammation. The fifth is delivery engineering, in which natural-product nanomaterials, plant-derived vesicles, polysaccharide scaffolds or engineered bacteria localize immune activation. Probiotic-guided CAR-T cells represent a related non-TCM conceptual advance: tumor-colonizing probiotics released synthetic antigens that labelled tumors for CAR-T attack, thereby bypassing endogenous antigen heterogeneity [11]. Such examples are included as mechanistic blueprints for biological adjuvant design, not as evidence for TCM formulations.

Dosing sequence is likely to determine success. A compound that enhances NFAT signaling may be useful during tumor encounter but detrimental if it drives premature differentiation during manufacturing. A macrophage-repolarizing agent may help if given before or with CAR-T infusion but may be irrelevant after tumor escape has occurred. A mitochondrial modulator may be best used ex vivo when it is purified and removable, whereas a chemokine inducer may require tumor exposure in vivo. Each combination should therefore include a pharmacodynamic timeline: baseline, tumor conditioning, lymphodepletion, manufacturing or ex vivo exposure where applicable, infusion, early expansion, peak inflammation, contraction and persistence.

Safety, interaction risks and clinical pharmacology

Safety assessment is not optional in TCM-CAR-T combinations. A botanical or natural-product agent may change lymphodepletion toxicity, infection risk, hepatic metabolism, coagulation, platelet function, endothelial activation, cytokine kinetics or the management of cytokine-release syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome. These risks are particularly important because CAR-T therapy already combines lymphodepleting chemotherapy, immune activation and living-cell expansion.

The first safety issue is manufacturing exposure. If the TCM-derived agent is used during CAR-T activation or expansion, it becomes part of the cell-manufacturing process. Its impact on transduction efficiency, vector copy number, expansion, memory phenotype, exhaustion, sterility, endotoxin, residual compound and release potency must be quantified. For this reason, ex vivo conditioning should be restricted to highly purified and structurally defined monomers or metabolites unless a complex formulation can satisfy cell-therapy CMC requirements. An agent that increases early activation but drives terminal differentiation, residual impurity or release-test failure would be unsuitable even if it improves short-term killing.

The second issue is lymphodepletion compatibility. Most CAR-T regimens use fludarabine plus cyclophosphamide, and cyclophosphamide is a prodrug whose activation depends on hepatic CYP450 metabolism, with CYP2B6 as a major contributor and CYP3A4 and CYP2C9 also contributing [61, 62]. Flavonoids and Scutellaria-derived constituents can modulate CYP enzymes, including CYP2C9 activity [63, 64]. Co-administration of a TCM-derived product in the pre-infusion window could therefore increase marrow toxicity by raising active-metabolite exposure or reduce CAR-T engraftment by weakening lymphodepletion. Because at least one primary APS-CAR-T study used cyclophosphamide preconditioning without formally assessing this interaction [65], CYP-mediated drug-interaction testing should be elevated to a go/no-go criterion rather than treated as a generic safety footnote.

The third issue is immune-toxicity interpretation. An apparent reduction in IL-6, IFN-γ or TNF-α may be beneficial if cytotoxicity and persistence are preserved, but harmful if it reflects broad T-cell suppression. Conversely, innate immune activation may improve trafficking but increase systemic inflammation. Early trials should therefore include paired efficacy and toxicity biomarkers, not only symptom scores.

Clinical translation and regulatory framework

A TCM-CAR-T combination is a combination of two complex products: a living engineered-cell product and a botanical or natural-product intervention. This creates unique translational demands. The TCM component should be defined by botanical identity, processing method, extraction conditions, chemical fingerprint, quantitative markers, impurity profile, microbial and endotoxin testing, stability and PK/PD. The formulation class should also be linked to the intended exposure window: purified monomers may be compatible with ex vivo conditioning, whereas complex formulations and polysaccharides should generally be developed as in vivo patient-conditioning or maintenance interventions. The CAR-T component should be defined by vector, receptor architecture, transduction efficiency, memory phenotype, exhaustion markers, cytotoxic potency, cytokine profile and release criteria. Suggested translational framework for TCM-assisted CAR-T studies

Trial design should avoid weak add-on studies. The strongest early design would be a biomarker-rich phase Ib trial in a tumor type with a defined CAR-T product and a clear adjuvant mechanism. For example, APS plus GPC3 CAR-T in hepatocellular carcinoma could test whether APS increases CD122 + CXCR3+PD-1- CAR-T cells, intratumoral CAR-T infiltration and CXCL9/CXCL10, but it should also include lymphodepletion PK/PD and CYP-interaction monitoring. A Scutellaria/YIV-906-derived strategy should distinguish formulation-level patient dosing from purified-compound ex vivo conditioning and should not be advanced during lymphodepletion without drug-interaction data.

Regulatory acceptance will require mechanistic plausibility, reproducible manufacturing and clinically meaningful endpoints. Disease control alone may be insufficient if it does not translate into durable responses. For solid tumors, endpoints should include objective response, progression-free survival, overall survival, duration of response, lesion-level response, ctDNA kinetics, CAR-T expansion, tissue infiltration, cytokine profiles, organ-specific toxicity, lymphodepletion exposure and spatial immune biomarkers. TME-remodeling claims should be supported by immune-competent, humanized or otherwise immune-informative models, not by immunodeficient xenografts alone. Conclusions

The next generation of solid-tumor CAR-T therapy will probably not be a single intervention. It will combine receptor design, regional or targeted delivery, tumor-microenvironment conditioning, metabolic support and safety control. TCM-derived agents could contribute to this strategy if they are developed with the same rigor expected of synthetic small molecules and biologics. The most promising route is not empirical add-on use, but product-class-specific and exposure-window-specific development [66–70].

Integrating the modular barriers of solid-tumor CAR-T therapy with standardized functional analysis of TCM-derived products creates a distinctive research strategy. Candidate agents should be quality-controlled, matched to an existing CAR-T limitation and verified in spatially resolved solid-tumor models. TME-directed claims require immune-informative systems, and metabolic modulators should be matched to CAR costimulatory architecture. Only agents that improve infiltration, persistence, cytotoxicity or safety without impairing manufacturing, lymphodepletion or release quality should move forward [37, 38, 41, 50].

The field remains early. Direct evidence is limited to a handful of preclinical studies, and no standardized TCM-CAR-T combination has yet established clinical benefit in solid tumors. However, the biological logic is strong enough to justify systematic development. If pursued with rigorous chemistry, immunology, CMC control and clinical pharmacology, TCM-assisted CAR-T therapy could become a distinctive form of mechanism-defined systems adjuvant therapy rather than another empirical supportive intervention [71–74].
