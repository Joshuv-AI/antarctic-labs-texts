Anesthetic adaptations for robotic-assisted pediatric surgery in a resource-constrained setting: a case report
Authors: Maria Teresita B. Aspi, Jeffrey Paolo M. Nuñez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Robotic-assisted pediatric surgery enhances visualization and dexterity but poses anesthetic challenges in low-resource settings. These challenges are compounded by prolonged operative times, pneumoperitoneum, and restricted patient access. This report describes the first pediatric robotic-assisted choledochal cyst excision performed at a Philippine tertiary hospital, highlighting anesthetic considerations and adaptive strategies in a resource-constrained environment.Presentation of the Case:A female child with a 2-year history of intermittent epigastric pain and no comorbidities was diagnosed with a choledochal cyst and scheduled for elective robotic-assisted excision. General anesthesia was supplemented by redundant monitors and intravenous accesses, an extended precordial stethoscope, improvised padding, cling-wrap insulation, and microwave-warmed intravenous fluids. Atracurium infusion ensured deep paralysis, while ventilatory adjustments countered the effects of pneumoperitoneum. The surgery was uneventful.Discussion:Anesthesia was complicated by altered respiratory and hemodynamic physiology, the risk of hypothermia, the need for paralysis, and limited access after docking. Careful preoperative assessment, vigilant monitoring, redundancy, and evidence-supported improvisation mitigated these risks. This case illustrates the disparity between advanced surgical technology and limited anesthetic infrastructure in developing countries.Conclusion:This case highlights the conditional feasibility of pediatric robotic-assisted surgery in a resource-limited setting when performed on a carefully selected patient. Safety under such constraints depends on meticulous preparation, anticipation of physiologic challenges, monitoring redundancy, and adaptive anesthetic strategies. As robotic surgical platforms become increasingly adopted in developing countries, strengthening perioperative infrastructure and support systems remains essential to ensure patient safety.

Introduction

Choledochal cysts are congenital dilatations of the biliary system, more common among Asian children[1–3]. Early surgical excision prevents cholangitis, pancreatitis, and malignant transformation[1,2]. Minimally invasive approaches reduce surgical trauma, augment visualization, hasten recovery, and improve cosmesis[4–7]. In pediatric cases, robotic surgery enhances dexterity, three-dimensional imaging, tremor filtration, and improves precision[6,8–10].

Robotic pediatric surgery remains uncommon in low-income countries due to cost, limited compatible instruments, and insufficient infrastructure[10]. Anesthetic management is complicated by the effects of pneumoperitoneum, prolonged surgery, and restricted patient access – all magnified when advanced monitors, warming systems, and positioning tools are unavailable. This report describes the first pediatric robotic-assisted choledochal cyst excision at a public tertiary hospital in the Philippines, emphasizing anesthetic challenges and adaptations.

Presentation of the case

An Asian female in middle childhood presented to the outpatient clinic with a 2-year history of occasional epigastric pain, vomiting, and fever, without jaundice. The patient had no allergies, comorbidities, or prior surgeries. Family and psychosocial histories were non-contributory. Physical examination was normal. Blood tests revealed a slightly increased partial thromboplastin time and direct bilirubin. Abdominal computed tomography confirmed a choledochal cyst. She was scheduled for elective robotic-assisted excision using the da Vinci Si Robotic Surgical System following routine preoperative preparation.

After the induction of general anesthesia, a second set of standard monitors was applied. Because a pediatric BIS sensor was unavailable, anesthetic depth was assessed clinically with physiologic monitor trends. An extended precordial stethoscope was used for continuous auscultation. A second peripheral line was secured. Infusion pumps controlled fluid administration. A nasogastric tube was inserted for gastric decompression. The head and extremities were wrapped in cling film for insulation. All pressure points were padded using egg-crate foam, and the torso was stabilized with foam supports and bed straps. Eyes were covered. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane and atracurium infusion. After insufflation, ventilatory parameters were adjusted to maintain normocapnia. Hemodynamics, capnography, and respiratory mechanics were monitored continuously. Temperature was monitored using an esophageal probe. Forced-air warming, cling-wrap insulation, and temperature-verified, microwave-warmed intravenous fluids maintained normothermia.

Following docking, access became restricted. Airway, intravenous lines, and pressure points were assessed regularly. The procedure lasted more than 8 hours without significant instability. After undocking, paralysis was reversed, and the patient was extubated awake before transfer out.

Recovery was uneventful. The patient was discharged after 6 days. The family expressed satisfaction with the overall care. The timeline of events is presented in Supplemental Digital Content Appendix 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A74.

Discussion

This case highlights the complexities and infrastructural contradictions of pediatric robotic surgery in a low-resource setting. Although guidelines promote patient safety, several recommendations remain unattainable in low-income countries[11]. In this case, targeted adaptations and improvisations enabled safe anesthesia despite significant limitations.

Physiological challenges of pneumoperitoneum and docking constraints

Preoperative evaluation is essential in determining tolerance to pneumoperitoneum and prolonged docking. Pneumoperitoneum decreases lung compliance and venous return, and increases airway and intracranial pressures, as well as systemic vascular resistance[12–14]. These effects are more pronounced in children because of their compliant chest walls, lower functional residual capacity, and heart-rate-dependent cardiac output[15]. In this case, the absence of significant comorbidities likely reduced the risk of perioperative complications.

Physiologic vulnerabilities are compounded by the robot’s rigid configuration[8,16]. Significant crowding beneath the robot limits direct access to the airway and torso[17]. Consequently, there is minimal margin for error should pneumoperitoneum decrease lung volume, causing rapid desaturation. In addition, repositioning becomes limited after docking. The operating table can no longer be tilted to counteract hemodynamic instability. Emergency interventions may also be delayed because access is obstructed until the robot is undocked[8,16]. The combined physiologic and technical constraints influenced the preparation, intravenous access, monitoring, anesthetic technique, and ventilation strategy employed in this case.

Airway and ventilation management

Docking limits direct access to the airway and torso. In this case, an extended precordial stethoscope enabled continuous auscultation of breath and heart sounds, improving safety[18]. Its utility has been demonstrated in low-resource settings[19–21]. Pneumoperitoneum reduces lung compliance and increases carbon dioxide absorption, requiring vigilant capnography monitoring and ventilatory adjustments[7,8] as implemented in this case. In accordance with recommendations, a nasogastric tube was inserted for gastric decompression to facilitate ventilation and optimize exposure[6]. While pulmonary ultrasound assesses atelectasis during robotic surgery[6], the necessary expertise was unavailable in this case. Nevertheless, adequate oxygenation and ventilation were maintained through protective lung ventilation and limitation of insufflation pressure following guidelines[6].

Monitoring redundancy and vascular access

Continuous physiologic monitoring is recommended[6,22]. Equipment malfunction remains a potential risk, and rapid replacement may be challenging because of restricted access. In this case, redundant monitors ensured uninterrupted surveillance in the event of primary device failure. Guidelines recommend at least two peripheral lines, as emergency vascular access becomes difficult after docking, and the use of infusion pumps to control fluid administration, minimizing fluid overload while preserving visibility[6]. Both recommendations were implemented.

Thermoregulation and low-cost warming strategies

Children are susceptible to hypothermia due to their high surface-area-to-mass ratio and limited thermogenic capacity[23]. Guidelines recommend the use of fluid warmers and heated mattresses[6]; however, the unavailability of equipment in low-resource settings necessitates improvisation[24]. Cling-wrap insulation reduces evaporative heat loss[25,26], while microwave-warmed intravenous fluids, when carefully temperature-checked, have been demonstrated to be a feasible low-cost warming method in resource-limited settings[27,28].

Patient positioning

Guidelines recommend specialized positioning devices to prevent compression injuries and patient displacement[6]. These devices were unavailable; thus, egg-crate foam padding was used to distribute pressure and stabilize the patient. At a lower cost, egg-crate foam provides support comparable to commercial devices[29].

Neuromuscular blockade and prevention of robotic-related injury

Paralysis ensures immobility and stabilizes insufflation pressures[6]. Children are vulnerable to injury because of their relatively thin abdominal walls[30]. Guidelines recommend monitoring to confirm adequate depth of relaxation and to guide reversal[6]. However, train-of-four (TOF) monitoring was not feasible because restricted hand movement results in inaccurate measurements. A continuous atracurium infusion was the safest alternative in this case to maintain deep paralysis.

Systemic challenges and broader implications

This case illustrates a disparity in low-income countries, where robotic surgical systems coexist with limited anesthetic infrastructure. Safety depends on meticulous preparation, optimization of limited resources, adaptive interventions, and vigilant intraoperative management. Although the procedure was completed safely, this single case has limited generalizability. Its hybrid environment combined advanced robotics with severe resource constraints, making strategies difficult to apply to either standard low-resource or well-funded centers. Improvised thermoregulation and positioning carry thermal risks and narrower safety margins than standard equipment. Reliance on clinical indicators without BIS and TOF monitoring may increase the risk of inappropriate anesthetic depth and residual paralysis. Institutional and methodological constraints limit reproducibility. Future studies should evaluate the safety, effectiveness, and reproducibility of these adaptive strategies.

Ethical and health-system considerations of robotic surgery in a resource-limited setting

Introducing a robotic platform into a resource-constrained setting raises ethical concerns regarding cost, equity, and resource allocation. In this case, the procedure was performed in a state-owned university hospital, where costs are covered by government allocations. Robotic-assisted surgery is, therefore, available to all patients, democratizing access to its benefits. In a national university hospital, robotics also supports pediatric surgical training and long-term capacity-building.

Conclusion

This report demonstrates the conditional feasibility of pediatric robotic-assisted choledochal cyst excision in a resource-limited setting when performed in a highly selected patient, managed by an experienced team. The uneventful outcome is not evidence of broad safety or universal applicability; rather, it reflects the importance of rigorous preoperative assessment, vigilant physiologic monitoring, equipment and intravenous access redundancy, careful airway and ventilation planning, and evidence-supported adaptive strategies despite significant constraints. As robotic surgery becomes increasingly available in developing countries, strengthening perioperative infrastructure remains essential before wider implementation is considered.
