Synergistic exacerbation of periprosthetic osteolysis by inflammatory bowel disease and titanium ions: Impaired osteogenesis of BMSCs via PI3K/AKT signaling
Authors: Panpan Ming, Kang Li, Wenqing Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Periprosthetic osteolysis remains a major cause of implant failure, driven by inflammatory responses to titanium (Ti) wear particles and ions. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), a systemic chronic inflammatory condition, exacerbates bone loss, yet its synergistic effects with Ti ions on periprosthetic osteolysis remain unexplored. Here, we investigated how IBD and Ti ions impair bone-implant integration by disrupting osteogenesis via the PI3K/AKT pathway. A rat model of IBD with localized Ti ion exposure was established, alongside in vitro studies using bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). Micro-CT and histomorphometry revealed severe trabecular bone loss and reduced bone-implant contact (BIC) in the Ti + DSS group, accompanied by increased osteoclast activity and suppressed osteogenic markers (Runx2/Sp7/OPN). Luminex assays showed elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as CXCL1, CCL5 and CCL20) in bone marrow microenvironment, further amplified by Ti ions. RNA sequencing of BMSCs identified downregulation of osteogenic differentiation genes and inhibition of the PI3K/AKT pathway. In vitro, AKT activation rescued osteogenic differentiation and mineralization in BMSCs exposed to Ti + DSS-conditioned inflammatory factors. These findings demonstrate IBD and Ti ions synergistically exacerbate periprosthetic osteolysis by enhancing osteoclastogenesis and suppressing BMSC osteogenesis via PI3K/AKT signaling. Targeting this pathway may offer therapeutic strategies to improve implant longevity in IBD patients.

Introduction

Bone loss after total hip arthroplasty (THA) is one of the main causes of medium- and long-term revision surgery. In many studies, aseptic loosening caused by periprosthetic osteolysis was more common than the sum of all other complex complications, reaching up to 66.6 % [[1], [2], [3]]. Aseptic loosening can be caused by a variety of reasons, but mainly because of the immune inflammatory response caused by titanium wear particles and titanium ion release around the implant [[4], [5], [6]]. When these titanium by-products are released from the implant surface and enter the body fluid, they stimulate abnormal phagocytosis by macrophages, resulting in a sterile inflammatory response to the phagocytosis of titanium particles or titanium ions [7,8]. Then, the cytokines produced by these aseptic inflammatory responses will be involved in the inflammatory activation of osteoclasts, leading to osteolysis between the bone and the implant, resulting in aseptic loosening [9,10].

Normal bone maintenance depends on a balance between bone formation and bone resorption, mainly involving the synergistic action of osteoblasts and osteoclasts [11,12]. Systemic bone loss is often associated with chronic inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes, and chronic nephritis [[13], [14], [15]], and the interaction with inflammatory bowel disease (IBDs) has become a research hotspot in recent years [[16], [17], [18]]. The incidence of IBD is rising globally, with significant impacts on bone health, such as increased risk of osteoporosis and fractures [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Despite growing evidence of the impact of IBD on exacerbating local bone resorption around implants [24,25], the combined effects of IBD and titanium ions on periprosthetic osteolysis remain largely unexplored. Titanium by-products in the form of wear particles or corrosion ions between the bone and the implant can produce an immunogenic response, rapidly activating the phagocytic activity of macrophages, inducing a more intense aseptic inflammatory response, generating more cytokines and chemokines, thereby promoting osteoclast maturation [[26], [27], [28]]. From this point, IBD may provide systemic inflammatory support for local titanium by-product-induced aseptic loosening.

In studying the pathological mechanisms of aseptic loosening and IBD-related bone resorption, people usually focus on the osteoclast perspective, suggesting that it is the overactivation of osteoclasts that accelerates bone resorption [[29], [30], [31]]. In fact, bone resorption involves bone resorption and subsequent coupled bone formation [32,33], and few studies have reported on the role of bone formation. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is crucial for osteogenesis, and its disruption can lead to impaired bone formation. Recent studies have shown that inflammatory factors can inhibit the PI3K/AKT pathway, affecting osteoblast differentiation and mineralization [34,35]. Osteoblast formation is mainly mediated by bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). In the current strategy of THA, one solution to reduce bone loss is to form a composite graft of allogeneic bone matrix and/or HA with BMSCs, thereby providing bone-induced and osteogenic properties [36]. In the IBD-related bone resorption studies, an increase in inflammatory factors in IBD have been proven to disrupt bone formation by inhibiting the proliferation and function of BMSCs, resulting in reduced bone mass and quality. However, the combined impact of IBD and titanium ions on peri-prosthetic osteolysis via the PI3K/AKT pathway has not been investigated.

In this study, we constructed an intraosseous implant model by placing sterile titanium implants in the distal femur and simulated the inflammatory response and osteolysis process with IBD modeling construction and titanium ion exposure around the implants. We demonstrated that the combined model exacerbated the enrichment of inflammatory cytokines around the implant, which in turn led to osteoclastic activation and abnormal bone formation. Additionally, we investigated the tendency and underlying molecular mechanism of titanium ion exposure on BMSC differentiation in IBD models, confirmed that the microenvironment of co-exposure could inhibit osteogenic differentiation and mineralization of BMSC, and revealed its association with inhibition of the PI3K-AKT pathway. This study uniquely explores the combined effects of IBD and titanium ions on periprosthetic osteolysis, revealing the disruption of osteogenesis via the PI3K/AKT pathway, and future development of targeted materials or drugs targeting the PI3K-AKT pathway may effectively reduce the risk of periprosthetic osteolysis in such environments (Scheme 1).Scheme 1Schematic diagram of synergistic exacerbation of periprosthetic osteolysis by inflammatory bowel disease and titanium ions.Scheme 1

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Protection and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC-2504053). All experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. The rats were euthanized by CO2 asphyxia and re-confirmed by cervical dislocation and observation of heart rate and breathing. All authors were able to view the study data and approve the final manuscript.

Primary reagent consumables

Ketamine (K3375; HPLC ≥98 %, Sigma), Xylazine Hydrochloride (Lumianning, Huamu, Jilin, China), Meloxicam (Puainta, China) Penicillin (North China Pharmaceutical Company.Ltd, China), Isoflurane (R510-22-10; RWD Life science Co., Ltd, China, Primacaine Adrenaline (Dentaires Pierre Rolland, France), Pure titanium implant (TC4; 99.5 wt% purity, Baoji, China), Titanium ion standard solution (170243; 1000 mg/L Ti in H2O, Sigma), Dextran Sulfate Sodium Salt for colitis modeling (DSS; 9011-18-1, MW: 36000–50000, Yeasen Biotechnology, China), 4 % paraformaldehyde (BL539A; Biosharp, China), Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; E809068, Reagent grade, 99.5 %, Macklin, China), Phosphate buffer (PBS, Gibco, USA), Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining solution (D006-1-1; Nanjing Jiancheng Technology Co., Ltd., China), Masson staining Kit (G1340; Solarbio, China), α-MEM medium (Sigma, USA), Trypsin (Gibco, USA), Fetal bovine serum (NEWZERUM, Australia).

Animal surgical procedures

The SD rats used in the experiment were all purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University. Twenty-four 380 ± 20 g 8-week-old male SD rats were raised in a standard SPF facility at Nanjing Medical University, with three rats in each cage. Environmental conditions: temperature 20–24 °C, humidity 40–50 %, light and dark cycling for 12 h. After one week of adaptive rearing, the animals were randomly divided into 4 groups, with 6 animals in each group: (1) Ctrl group; (2) Ti group; (3) DSS group; (4) Ti + DSS group. Rats were subjected to inhalation Anesthesia using a small animal respiratory anesthesia Machine RWD R540i Anesthesia Machine. Each rat inhaled a 4 % concentration of isoflurane to induced anesthesia and then switched to a 2–3 % isoflurane mask to maintain anesthesia. After confirming general anesthesia, the rats were placed on a thermostatic pad on their side, and the flat lateral surface of the femoral condyles on both sides was defined as the surgical site. The surgical site was shaved and disinfected with a mixture of iodophor and 70 % ethanol before the operation. Expose the lateral condyle of the femur through incision of the skin, fascia and periosteum. Under normal saline irrigation, a 1.5 mm diameter drill was used to drill through the first cortical bone to prepare the implant cavity, and a custom-made titanium implant (with a thread diameter of 1.6 mm and a thread section length of 1.8 mm) was implanted at the preparation site, with consistent implant depth and torque. One implant was implanted in each lateral femoral condyle. Then intermittent suturing was performed to close the wound. Oral administration of veterinary meloxicam tablets for three consecutive days after surgery to relieve pain (Puanta, China), and intramuscular injection of antibiotics (penicillin, North China Pharmaceutical Co., LTD., China) to prevent infection. One week after the surgical area healed, the sutures were removed and colitis models were constructed for each group (see DSS-induced colitis model construction and colitis evaluation), as well as titanium ion exposure model construction (see the section on titanium ion exposure model construction). Implants and the surrounding bone were collected in the fourth week of the construction of the colitis model and the titanium exposure model. After the bone specimens were trimmed, they were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 2 days, then rinsed overnight with running water and stored in 70 % ethanol for subsequent radiological and histological evaluations.

Construction of DSS-induced colitis models and evaluation of colitis

In this experiment, rat model of ulcerative colitis was conducted using sodium dextran sulfate (DSS) (MW:36000–50000, CAS 9011-18-1, Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., China). Specifically, 3 % DSS was dissolved in sterile drinking water and fed to rats, with a change every 2 days. The control group was fed sterile drinking water. At the fourth week of model construction, distal and proximal colonic segments of rats in each group were collected, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, routinely embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were analyzed by hematoxylin-eosin staining (H&E) for histopathological analysis, and by Alcian Blue-Periodic Acid Schiff (AB-PAS) staining for histological analysis of mucus and goblet cells. Goblet cells were measured and counted, with an average of 20 goblet cells in each compartment counted. All obtained slides were scanned and imitated using the digital section scanning and analysis system Pannoramic MIDI (3DHISTECH, Hungary) for preservation, and images were viewed and analyzed using slide observation software SlideViewer (3DHISTECH, Hungary).

Construction of the titanium ion exposure model

In this experiment, titanium ions (20 μg/ml, 0.5 ml) were locally injected into the lateral condyle of the femur of rats every week based on previous studies [37,38]. Rats in each group were sacrificed and both lateral femoral condyles were taken for histological analysis after the fourth week of modeling, because within 4 weeks after implant implantation, bone tissue undergoes significant remodeling and inflammation, and these changes are essential for osseointegration [16,39,40].

Micro CT analysis of trabeculae

The femur samples were collected and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde buffer (Servicebio, G1101) at 4 °C for 48 h, then placed in 70 % ethanol. All bone samples were scanned using a commercial Micro-CT scanner (Skysacn 1176, Kontich, Belgium). Scan parameters (55 kV, 72 mA, 17.5 μm). All reconstructed images were segmented, registered and quantized using Skyscan CTAn (Version:1.13.8.1), and cortical bone and cancellous bone parameters were analyzed. For femoral samples containing titanium implants, the adaptive threshold method was used to distinguish the implant from the bone tissue, and the BV/TV within the region of interest (ROI) was calculated. Three-dimensional images of the implant and the surrounding bone tissue were reconstructed using Mimics Medical (Version 20.0) software.

Hard tissue analysis

After Micro-CT imaging, a portion of the non-decalcified femoral specimens were gradient dehydrated with anhydrous ethanol and then immersed in the photopolymer resin Technovit 7200 VLC (Heraeus Kulzer Gmbh, Wehreim, Germany) with an increased concentration. After complete dehydration infiltration, the tissue blocks were embedded with fresh Technovit 7200 VLC and resin particles. The embedded specimens were cut into 200 μm thick sections along the direction of the bone face tongue and the long axis of the implant center. The slices were further ground and polished to 50 μm thickness. The above operations were carried out using the EXAKT precision cutting and grinding system (EXAKT Apparatebau, Nordestedt, Hamburg, Germany). Hard tissue sections were subjected to Goldner staining according to the manufacturer's instructions, followed by image scanning in the SLIDEVIEW VS200 (Olympus, Japan). Image viewing was performed using OlyVIA V3.4.1 (Build 26606, Olympus, Japan) software, and bone-to-implant contact (BIC) was calculated. BIC% is the ratio of the length of bone in contact with the implant surface to the total length of the implant surface.

Histopathological, immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical analysis

After Micro-CT imaging, some femoral specimens were decalcified in 17 % ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 21 days. For femurs containing implants, the implants were removed first, and then all femoral tissue was dehydrated in a series of graded ethanol. The femoral specimens were then embedded in paraffin to prepare continuous sections 5-8 μm thick. Some sections were subjected to conventional staining such as hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Masson staining. For the detection of osteoclast count and distribution, follow the manufacturer's instructions in the TRAP staining kit (387A, Sigma, USA). The dark red cells are counted as TRAP positive cells. Digital images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Immunofluorescence staining was used to analyze tight junction proteins, macrophage typing, osteogenic differentiation and inflammatory indicators at the thread of the implant in intestinal tissue. Specifically, consecutive sections were permeated with 0.1 % TritonX-100 for 20 min. For tight junction protein, using anti-α-SMA antibody (1:200, 67735-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-Vinculin antibody (1:200, 66305-1-Ig, Proteintech), and anti-CD61 antibody (1:200, 18309-1-AP, Proteintech); For osteogenic indicators, using anti-Runx2 antibody (1:200, 20700-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Sp7 antibody (1:200, ab209484, Abcam), and OPN antibody (1:200, 22952-1-AP, Proteintech); For macrophage markers, using anti-iNOS antibody (1:200, 18985-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-CD206 antibody (1:200, 18704-1-AP, Proteintech), and anti-CD68 antibody (1:200, 28058-1-AP) to incubate with samples, followed by incubation with the fluorescently labeled secondary antibody and DAPI. The primary antibody used for immunohistochemical staining was Collagen I (1:200, AF7001, Affinity). All tissue slides were scanned and imitated using the digital section scanning and analysis system Pannoramic MIDI (3DHISTECH, Hungary), and images were viewed and analyzed using the slide observation software SlideViewer (3DHISTECH, Hungary).

Luminex liquid suspension microarray for multiple cytokines analysis

Luminex liquid suspension chip detection was performed by Wayen Biotechnologies (Shanghai, China). Simply put, the tibial lavage fluid samples collected in each group were subjected to 10,000 rpm, centrifuged for 10 min, the supernatant was taken and tested with 50 μl stock solution. Chip detection was performed according to the instructions of the Bio-Plex Pro Rat 23-plex Cytokine Grp I Panel manufacturer. This included sample incubation at room temperature in the dark for 1 h, antibody detection incubation for 30 min, and Streptavidin-PE chromogenic incubation for 10 min. Finally, the obtained fluorescence was detected, read and output by the Bio-Plex MAGPIX system (Bio-Rad).

Cell culture and osteogenic potential analysis

Bone marrow from the femurs of each group of rats was collected, isolated and cultured to obtain BMSC. We then isolated the lower limbs and soft tissues in a sterile environment. The two ends of the femur were dissected, the bone marrow was rinsed with 1 ml of a syringe containing PBS, and then cultured in α-MEM complete medium containing 2 % of the double antibody. Subsequent experiments were performed under P3-P5 generations.

Identification of BMSCs: Flow cytometry was used for specific cell surface antigens. Briefly, cells were washed with PBS and isolated with 0.25 % Trypsin/EDTA (Gibco, USA), cell suspension containing 2.5 × 105 cells were then incubated with 5 μl of plant erythromin conjugated anti-mouse CD29, CD90 and CD45 (Biolegend, USA) at 4 °C for 30 min. After washing twice in PBS, the cells were resuspended in PBS and identified by flow cytometry (FACSVerse, BD, USA).

Alkaline phosphatase staining (ALP): ALP activity was qualitatively evaluated after a 14-day-period osteogenic induction. For details, adherent cells were rinsed with PBS, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, and stained using the BCIP/NBT ALP chromogenic kit (C3206, Beyotime, China).

Alizarin red staining (ARS): BMSCs were stained with ARS after 21 days of culture in osteogenic induction medium to assess calcification deposition. After osteogenic induction, the cells were immortalized on different substrates with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min, then with 1 % ARS solution (C0138, Beyotime, China), and the samples were washed with ultrapure water to remove excess ARS dye. The stained cells were observed under a stereomicroscope (SZX16, Olympus, Japan).

Transcriptomic sequencing

Total RNA of BMSC was extracted according to the operating procedure of Trizol reagent (AG, China). RNA purity and quantification were evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated using the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). The library was constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were performed by Pasenno Biotechnology Co., LTD. (Shanghai, China).

Cell immunofluorescence assays

BMSCs were placed on different substrates in a 24-well plate at 5 × 104 cells/well. The medium was replaced with osteogenic medium twice daily for a fixed period. Anti-Akt (1:200, 10176-2-AP, Proteintech), anti-p-Akt (1:200, 66444-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti- GSK3β (1:200, 51065-1-AP, Proteintech), and anti-p-GSK3β (1:200, 67558-1-Ig, Proteintech) primary antibodies were used for immunofluorescence analysis. The Secondary Antibody was a Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) ReadyProbes™ Secondary Antibody (Alexa Fluor™ 488; 1:500, R37118, Invitrogen, USA). The cytoskeleton was re-stained with Phalloidin Labeling Probes Alexa Fluor™ 594 (A12381, Invitrogen) and the nucleus was re-stained with DAPI (P0131, Beyotime). The images were imaged using a laser confocal platform.

Western blotting

The method of BMSC cell inoculation was like that of cell immunofluorescence detection. Cellular proteins were extracted and subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Anti-Runx2 (1:1000, 20700-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Akt (1:1000, 10176-2-AP, Proteintech), anti-p-Akt (1:1000, 66444-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-GSK3β (1:1000, 51065-1-AP, ProteinTech), anti-p-GSK3β (1:1000, 67558-1-Ig, Proteintech) and GAPDH (1:1000, 10494-1-AP, Proteintech) primary antibodies were incubated for Western blots at 4 °C. Secondary goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000, ZB-2301, ZSGB-BIO) was incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, imaging was performed using a VILBER multifunctional imaging system (VILBER, Fusion FX, France) using the ECL Western blot kit (Millipore, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis and plotting were performed using OriginPro 2023 and GraphPad Prism 10 (v10.4.1). Under the condition that the data followed a normal distribution and had homogeneity of variance, independent sample t-tests were used to compare the differences between the two groups, analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student–Newman–Keuls (S-N-K) multiple comparisons were used to analyze the differences among multiple groups. When P < 0.05, it means there is a statistical difference.

Results

Titanium ions exacerbated the occurrence and development of intestinal inflammation

We established a rat model of Ulcerative Colitis (UC, one type of IBDs) using sodium dextran sulfate (DSS) based on previous reports [41]. To investigate the effect of local titanium ion exposure on colitis, we administered regular local injections of titanium ion to the distal femur in normal rats and rats treated with DSS (Flow chart, Fig. 1A). Four weeks after modeling, rats exposed to titanium ion and induced by DSS (Ti + DSS group) had more severe colitis and significantly shortened colons compared with rats induced by DSS (Fig. 1B and C). Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) stained colon tissue sections showed significant inflammatory cell infiltration, epithelial destruction, and glandular abnormalities in the Ti + DSS group (Fig. 1E). The intestinal mucosal barrier is a component of the intestinal barrier function, and goblet cells and the mucus they secrete are important parts for constructing the intestinal mucosal secretory barrier [42,43]. AB-PAS staining showed that the number of goblet cells in the Ti + DSS group was further reduced compared with the DSS group, and the colonic crypt structure changed (Fig. 1D and F). The mucosal barrier function of titanium ion exposed colitis rats was more severely impaired than that of standard DSS-induced colitis tissue. To better determine whether titanium ions exacerbated intestinal physical barrier damage caused by colitis, we used immunofluorescence staining to detect and analyze the expression of tight junction proteins in colonic tissues of each group. α-SMA, Vinculin and CD61 are three classic intercellular tight junction (TJ) proteins associated with intestinal integrity in colon tissue [[44], [45], [46]]. In our study, the mean fluorescence intensities (MFI) of the three tight junction proteins α-SMA, Vinculin, and CD61 in the Ti + DSS group were significantly lower than those in the control group (P < 0.001), with Vinculin significantly lower than both the Ti group and the DSS group (P < 0.01), CD61 was significantly lower than both the Ti and DSS groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A, 2C, 2D and 2E). This indicated the most severe damage to the intestinal epithelium in this group. Intestinal tissue integrity is also closely related to its immune barrier function, and mucosal tissue damage leads to macrophage infiltration, especially polarization M1 macrophages. Therefore, we detected the polarization of immune-representative macrophages in the intestinal epithelium of each group by immunofluorescence staining. Typically, iNOS, CD206 and CD68 are three surface markers associated with macrophage typing in colonic tissue [[47], [48], [49]]. M1-like macrophages, which are characterized by the expression of iNOS, are known for their pro-inflammatory functions and ability to promote tissue damage and inflammation. In contrast, M2-like macrophages, identified by the expression of CD206, are associated with anti-inflammatory functions and tissue repair [50,51]. The results indicated that in the Ti + DSS group, the mean fluorescence intensity of M1-like macrophages (iNOS) increased significantly (P < 0.001), while that of M2-like macrophages (CD206) decreased significantly (P < 0.001), suggesting the transformation of macrophages in the gut to the M1-like phenotype (Fig. 2B, 2F and 2G). In addition, compared with the control group, there was relatively less CD206 fluorescence expression and more iNOS expression in both the Ti group and the DSS group (P < 0.001), suggesting that titanium ions themselves would be immunogenic and could induce inflammation in the intestinal immune system and further intensify the existing immune response to IBD.

Inflammatory bowel disease and titanium exposure synergistically increased trabecular bone loss

We used micro-CT to evaluate the effect of IBD combined with titanium ion exposure on extra-intestinal bone loss. After 4 weeks of modeling, compared with the control group, the Ti + DSS group showed the greatest reduction in trabecular volume to total volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb. Th) and trabecular number (Tb. N) (P < 0.001), and a significant increase in trabecular separation (Tb. Sp) (P < 0.001) (Fig. S2A and Fig. S2B). H&E and Masson staining consistently showed increased inflammatory infiltration within the bone and decreased collagen area (Fig. S3). Immunohistochemical staining of the early osteogenic indicator Collagen I showed that the positive rate of Collagen I cells was the lowest in the Ti + DSS group, indicating that osteogenic collagen formation was most severely impaired in the Ti + DSS group. To assess whether there was a change in the number of osteoclasts before bone loss, we performed tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining on femoral sections. Histologically, we observed a significant increase in the number of osteoclasts on the bone surface of the femurs in the Ti + DSS group of rats (Fig. S4). To further investigate osteogenesis in the tissues, multi-color immunofluorescence staining of osteogenic indicators RUNX2/SP7/OPN was performed on the distal femoral tissues of each group, which represent typical osteogenic differentiation and mineralization functions. Compared with the control group, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of RUNX2/SP7/OPN decreased the most in the Ti + DSS group, followed by the DSS group, and osteogenic differentiation and mineralization were most severely impaired in the Ti + DSS group (Fig. S5A). In addition, some fluorescence downregulation of osteogenic indicators was also observed in the Ti group, which indicated the osteogenic inhibitory effect of titanium ions themselves. The results suggested that IBD, with the synergistic effect of titanium exposure, lead to a further increase in trabecular bone loss (Fig. S5B).

Inflammatory bowel disease and titanium exposure synergically inhibit bone formation around the implant

The actual effect of titanium ions on early titanium implant osseointegration in the state of IBD was evaluated through in vivo implantation experiments. Titanium implants were implanted in the femoral condyle of rats, and after 4 weeks of healing, the DSS-induced rat model of IBD was initiated. The modeling and surgical procedures were detailed in the supplementary document (Fig. S1). Titanium particles-stimulated cranial osteolysis models have been widely used to study inflammation and osteolysis, while in clinical practice, models with wear particles injected into the distal femur and intramedullary tubes are closer to aseptic loosening [52,53]. In addition, due to the presence of electrochemical corrosion in the body fluid microenvironment, free titanium ions are mainly exposed in the form of titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO2 NPs) and have been proven to accumulate in the lymphoid tissue of the intestine, particularly at the base of Peyer's patches [54,55]. In this study, we used sterile titanium ions as a by-product of titanium for local injection at the bone junction interface of titanium implants. Four weeks after the operation, the distal femur of rats with titanium implants was collected. The trabecular microstructure around the titanium implant was then evaluated by Micro-CT analysis (Fig. 3A). With the same CT scan threshold and volume of interest (VOI), the Ti + DSS group had the lowest trabecular volume to total volume (BV/TV), the lowest trabecular thickness (Tb. Th) (P < 0.001), and the highest trabecular separation (Tb. Sp) (P < 0.001) around titanium implants compared with the control group. There was also a significant reduction in trabecular number (Tb. N) (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3B–E). Osteolysis under the synergistic effect was associated with inhibition of osteogenic gene expression in BMSCs

To reveal the pathogenesis of bone loss at the molecular level in the Ti + DSS group, RNA sequencing (RNA-SEq) of BMSCs was performed after stimulating cells with mixed inflammatory factors collected from Ti + DSS group. KEGG analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes were enriched in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, Calcium signaling pathway, Osteoclast differentiation, etc (Fig. 7B). GO enrichment analysis showed that it was closely related to biological process (BP) such as bone development, skeletal system development and ossification (Fig. 7C and E). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) also showed consistency between PI3K-AKT signaling pathway and osteogenic differentiation-related gene expression changes in BMSC (Fig. 7D). Specifically, most of the key genes related to osteoblast differentiation and maturation, such as Bmp6, Sp7, Zbtb16, Runx1, Wnt5a and Twist2, etc, were all downregulated after treatment in the Ti + DSS group (Fig. 7F).

Discussion

Our study revealed that the synergistic interaction between IBD and titanium ions significantly exacerbated periprosthetic osteolysis by enhancing osteoclast genesis and inhibiting osteogenesis via the PI3K/AKT pathway. While it is well established that IBD induces systemic bone loss and titanium ions contribute to aseptic loosening, our findings highlight the critical role of their combined effects on implant failure. We found that the presence of titanium ions under IBD conditions leads to a more severe inflammatory microenvironment around the implant, characterized by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which was more severe than that caused by IBD or titanium ion exposure alone (Scheme 1).

Aseptic osteolysis around the prosthesis after artificial joint replacement has long been an unavoidable issue in the field of orthopedic research. The wear particle fragments, and titanium corrosion products produced around the prosthesis have been identified as the main causes of periprosthetic osteolysis because they often stimulate a series of inflammatory cell responses (including foreign body responses), which may eventually lead to osteoclast generation and bone resorption. Clinical trials have confirmed a significant increase in serum titanium ion concentration in patients with prosthesis failure, suggesting a certain correlation between titanium ion release and aseptic loosening of the prosthesis [57]. It has also been confirmed in our previous studies that titanium ions can induce osteoclastic differentiation and maturation of macrophages [58] In this study, we exposed the area around the distal femoral implant in rats to titanium ions and detected active osteogenesis at the bone-implant interface by Trap staining. We also found that abnormal osseointegration of the implant was associated with an increased inflammatory response driven by M1 polarization of macrophages by immunofluorescence analysis. In addition, the bone formation function around the implant is often overlooked, especially the investigation of the osteogenic fate of BMSCs around the implant is particularly rare. In fact, along with changes in the local inflammatory microenvironment, persistent inflammatory factors can lead to abnormal osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, especially inhibition of bone mineralization. In this study, we chose to establish the implant model in the distal femoral metaphysis region, which is an ideal center for osteogenic development [59]. We observed that titanium ions inhibited the expression of osteogenic indicators such as Runx2, Sp7, and OPN, and that BMSCs showed significant inhibition of ALP osteogenic differentiation and ARS osteogenic mineralization during in vitro induction, which was consistent with previous reported results.

In addition to bone loss caused by changes in the local microenvironment, inflammatory bone loss caused by systemic chronic disease may amplify the bone resorption process induced by local stimulation. IBD can induce systemic inflammation from the intestine, which can promote extra-intestinal bone structural defects and increase the incidence of hip fractures. The polarization of macrophages towards the M1 phenotype in the bone microenvironment, as observed in our study, is a critical factor in this process. M1 macrophages, characterized by the expression of iNOS, produce pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, which can activate osteoclasts and promote bone resorption. In contrast, M2 macrophages, identified by the expression of CD206, produce anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, which inhibit inflammation and promote tissue repair. Our findings indicate that the Ti + DSS environment promotes the polarization of macrophages towards the M1 phenotype, leading to increased bone resorption and impaired bone formation. Previous studies have suggested that inflammation was associated with increased permeability caused by the disruption of the imbalanced intestinal barrier, leading to the transfer of abundant intestinal inflammatory factors to bone tissue, resulting in the continuous expression of inflammatory factors/chemokines in bone tissue and the activation of osteoclastic differentiation, leading to inflammatory bone loss [[60], [61], [62]]. Our study further demonstrates that the presence of titanium ions exacerbates this process by promoting M1 macrophage polarization and enhancing the inflammatory microenvironment around the implant. In addition, IBD-related bone destruction processes may be associated with changes in bone marrow-derived osteoclast progenitor cell populations such as LSK hematopoietic stem cells, osteoclast progenitor cells OCP, and monocyte populations, and may promote osteoclast formation and maturation through pathways such as altering the abundance of osteoclast-related multicellular factors in the bone and activating the surface receptor MDL-1D for osteoclast differentiation, leading to bone loss [16]. In this study, significant enrichment of inflammatory factors and chemokines was detected in the bone marrow cavity around femoral implants in rats with DSS-induced IBD. Including GM-CSF, IL-1b, IL-17, KC/CXCL1, MCP-1/CCL2, MIP-1a/CCL3, RANTES/CCL5 and MIP-3a/CCL20, which were consistent with the significantly increased cytokine types reported in previous literatures in DSS-induced colitis. These cytokines have been found to be closely associated with the risk of IBD in several observational studies.

Since IBD patients are also one of the target groups for artificial joint replacement surgery, the risk between IBD and periprosthetic osteolysis is worthy of exploration. This will be of great value for understanding the prognosis and early risk intervention of artificial joint replacement surgery under IBD, but there have been no reports so far. This study compared the micro-CT microstructure changes of osteolysis at the bone-implant interface in local titanium ion exposure models, IBD models, and IBD combined with titanium ion exposure models. It is the first time that titanium ions have been used to evaluate aseptic osteolysis around implants under chronic inflammatory conditions such as IBD. Therefore, the study of using a rat model to implant aseptic titanium rods into the femur and introduce titanium by-products to simulate the inflammatory response and osteolysis process around the prosthesis was considered more in line with the actual aseptic loosening of human joint prostheses. Many previous studies have built osteolysis models by injecting titanium particles into the crevices of animal skulls [63,64]. Our results suggested that IBD combined with titanium ion exposure was accompanied by increased bone resorption and reduced bone formation, leading to final implant-osseointegration destruction.

There is limited evidence that IBD combined with titanium exposure inhibits bone formation around the implant and osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. The rat model of IBD combined with titanium ion exposure (Ti + DSS) showed significant bone integration abnormalities at the femur implant-bone interface, manifested as active osteoclastic resorption and lower new bone formation. This highlighted the potential role of IBD in bone loss by influencing bone resorption and bone formation. Through immunohistochemical staining and immunofluorescence staining experiments, we found that osteogenic function around the implant was significantly suppressed in the Ti + DSS-induced rat model, while immune-representative macrophages transformed into M1 type that promoted inflammatory responses. High abundance of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines was shown to inhibit osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Compared with the DSS group, the expressions of KC/CXCL1, RANTES/CCL5 and MIP-3a/CCL20 were further increased in the Ti + DSS group. Among them, MIP-3a/CCL20 is a representative inflammatory factor for inflammatory enteritis and is considered a potential target for future treatment strategies of IBD [65,66]. CCL20 regulates the migration and localization of local immune cells in the intestine by binding to its receptor CCR6. It plays an important role in the pathogenesis of IBD [67]. The presence of titanium ions exacerbated the occurrence of intestinal inflammation and promoted the overflow of intestinal cytokines, which was consistent with the results of the destruction of the physical and immune barriers of the intestine under titanium ion exposure in this study. The increased abundance of inflammatory factors affected the expression of osteogenic indicators such as Runx2 by inhibiting the PI3K-AKT pathway, which would be a potential mechanism for abnormal bone formation [68]. When we activated the AKT pathway in isolated BMSCs, these complex mixed pro-inflammatory factors immediately controlled the osteogenic differentiation and mineralization formation function of BMSCs, which deepened our understanding of the abnormal osteogenic behavior of BMSCs in Ti + DSS. We also realized that the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs suppressed by the Ti + DSS environment was difficult to recover spontaneously. The development of various treatments that could activate the PI3K-AKT pathway through targeted intervention may be a future development need.

Limitations of this study

For several reasons, the scope of this study is limited. First, this study only constructed a model of ulcerative colitis (UC), one of the subtypes of IBD models, and the current study lacks more experimental animal models to illustrate the issue of periprosthetic aseptic osteolysis in Crohn's disease (another subtype of IBD) combined with titanium ion exposure [41,69]. Secondly, the design of rat femoral implants is still different from that of real human titanium joint prosthesis devices, but animal models are important tools for helping to understand the biological mechanisms associated with titanium by-products after joint replacement. Finally, in previous studies, titanium wear particles have been used to simulate the stimulant of osteolysis around the prosthesis, but there are few models that use titanium ions as the stimulant. Both wear particles and corrosion products are the main causes of periprosthetic osteolysis. Since titanium particles may undergo biotransformation in the body to form different compounds or structures, it is impossible to accurately simulate the response mechanism of titanium particles to the bone-implant interface simply by morphology and size, while the transformation forms of titanium ions in the body are more direct and diverse. They can combine with proteins to form complexes or be reduced by microorganisms to titanium dioxide nanoparticles. Therefore, using titanium ions alone can more realistically simulate the in vivo form of titanium. However, the differences in biochemical reactions induced between titanium ions and titanium particles remain to be studied, which will be an important direction for our subsequent research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, these data still revealed some interesting findings that titanium ion exposure combined with inflammatory bowel disease could exacerbate changes in the inflammatory microenvironment around the implant, amplify local inflammation around the bone implant by increasing the abundance of inflammatory cytokines, induce osteoclast formation, and inhibit osteogenic fate of BMSCs. Alterations in the PI3K-AKT pathway may be a potential mechanism for periprosthetic osteolysis under this microenvironment change, and further research is needed to reveal the underlying interactions.
