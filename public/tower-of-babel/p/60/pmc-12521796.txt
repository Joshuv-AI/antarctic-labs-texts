Distinguishing Mental From Physical Fatigue in Patients With Low‐Grade Glioma
Authors: Femke F Siebenga, Sandra E Rakers, Floor Gelmers, Hiska L van der Weide, Miranda C A Kramer, Anouk van der Hoorn, Roelien H Enting, Ingeborg Bosma, Rob J M Groen, Hanne‐Rinck Jeltema, Michiel Wagemakers, Jacoba M Spikman, Anne M Buunk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFatigue is a common consequence of low‐grade gliomas (LGG), but its specific nature has not been extensively studied. In particular, it remains unclear if the prevalence and determinants of mental fatigue differ from those of physical fatigue. Therefore, this study aimed to examine (1) whether the prevalence and severity of mental fatigue differ from physical fatigue in patients with LGG; (2) the specific determinants of mental and physical fatigue, including cognitive impairments, affective symptoms and radiological abnormalities.MethodsA total of 148 patients with LGG were included and assessed with the Dutch Multifactor Fatigue Scale (DMFS; mental and physical fatigue) and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS; affective symptoms). Neuropsychological tests were administered to measure cognitive impairments, particularly in basic and complex information processing speed.ResultsSevere mental fatigue affected 38% of patients, a significantly different prevalence than that of severe physical fatigue (22%), with an overlap of 15%. Patients with severe mental fatigue had significantly lower scores on a divided attention task, compared to patients with non‐severe mental fatigue. In a hierarchical linear prediction model with demographical, radiological and psychological variables, different sets of determinants were found for mental fatigue (anxiety and depression) and physical fatigue (depression, educational level and tumor volume).ConclusionsMental fatigue can be distinguished from physical fatigue in patients with LGG, characterized by a high prevalence and unique determinants. Therefore, mental fatigue should be considered as a distinct construct and presumably requires a different (neuro)psychological therapeutic approach compared to physical fatigue.Keywords: affective symptoms, cognitive impairments, low‐grade glioma, mental fatigue, physical fatigue

Introduction

Low‐grade gliomas (LGG) are primary malignant brain tumors, with a peak incidence in adults between 30 and 40 years old [1, 2]. Treatment often consists of surgery followed by radiotherapy and chemotherapy, with a median survival exceeding 10 years after diagnosis [3, 4]. In patients with LGG both the tumor itself and therapy‐induced neurotoxicity, may lead to impairments in cognitive functions such as attention, memory, language, and executive functions. In addition to cognitive impairments, fatigue is one of the most distressing and long‐lasting symptoms in patients with LGG [5, 6, 7, 8]. Fatigue is described as a multidimensional phenomenon and broad consensus exists that a distinction between mental and physical fatigue can be made. In particular mental fatigue, defined as chronic exhaustion leading to a reduced mental capacity for effortful complex cognitive tasks [9], is considered characteristic for patients with brain disorders, such as traumatic brain injury and stroke [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. However, the majority of studies in patients with LGG have considered fatigue as a unitary construct or focused on physical fatigue only [5, 6, 7, 8, 14, 15]. Until now, only one study investigated specifically mental fatigue in 31 patients with LGG, with 55% of the patients reporting severe mental fatigue [16]. It is crucial to examine this in a large sample of patients with LGG to reliably conclude about the prevalence and determinants of mental fatigue, and how this differs from physical fatigue.

Mental fatigue in patients with LGG may have several determinants. Studies in other patient groups with brain injury show a relationship between mental fatigue and cognitive impairments, in particular attention deficits [11, 12, 13, 17]. This can be explained by the 'Coping hypothesis' proposed by Van Zomeren et al. (1984) [18, 19] which states that brain damage results in cognitive deficits in processing speed and attention, which requires patients to exert extra effort in complex tasks to maintain performance at an optimal level and to compensate for these cognitive deficits, which in turn results in fatigue [9, 13]. Given the frequent occurrence of attention deficits in patients with LGG [20, 21] and the established link between mental fatigue and attention deficits in other patient groups with brain injury [11, 17], investigating this relationship in LGG patients is of particular interest. Specifically, complex information processing speed tasks—tasks that require a higher cognitive load, such as divided attention tasks—are hypothesized to be stronger related to mental fatigue compared to more basic information processing speed tasks. This emphasizes the importance to objectively differentiate basic from complex information processing speed, using neuropsychological tests. Since radiological measures, such as white matter lesions or tumor volume, as indices of brain damage visible on neuroimaging, have been related to deficits in information processing speed in glioma patients [22, 23], the question arises whether these factors are also related to mental fatigue.

Another possible determinant for mental fatigue in patients with LGG pertains to the fact that patients who have a neurological disorder resulting in brain damage experience this as a psychologically distressing event. Specifically in patients with LGG, the constant awareness of having a malignant tumor, combined with the incurable nature of the disease and limited treatment options, often contributes to increased feelings of anxiety or depression [24, 25]. To understand the specific origin of mental fatigue, it is therefore important to also investigate whether there is a relationship between mental fatigue and affective symptoms in patients with LGG. Depression, and to a lesser degree anxiety, have been found to be related to fatigue in cancer patients [26]; however, this association is not yet thoroughly examined in patients with LGG.

Understanding the distinction between mental and physical fatigue is crucial as recognizing the unique mechanisms of each type of fatigue may require a different therapeutic approach in patients with LGG. Therefore, the aims of the study were to examine (1) whether the prevalence and severity of mental fatigue differ from physical fatigue in a large sample of patients with LGG; (2) whether mental fatigue is related to cognitive impairments (in particular basic and complex information processing speed), affective symptoms, and radiological abnormalities, and whether this differs from physical fatigue.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Adult patients with diffuse IDH‐mutated 1p/19q co‐deleted oligodendroglioma and diffuse IDH‐mutated astrocytoma (WHO grade 2 and 3) eligible for proton therapy [27], were included in the current study. Patients were evaluated after surgery and before adjuvant therapy, between November 2017 and March 2023, at the University Medical Center Groningen (UMCG). All patients are monitored intensively in a prospective, longitudinal, multidisciplinary clinical registration program including an extensive battery of standardized neuropsychological tests as part of routine clinical care at the UMCG. Exclusion criteria were age younger than 18 years, insufficient command of the Dutch language, previous chemo‐ or radiotherapy, severe neurological disorders, severe psychiatric disorders (conditions requiring active psychiatric treatment) and alcohol or drug abuse. In addition, all patients who did not complete the Dutch Multifactor Fatigue Scale (DMFS) (see paragraph Fatigue) were excluded from the current study. Data obtainment occurred in compliance with the ethical regulations of the Medical Ethical Committee of the UMGG. All participants gave written informed consent at the Department of Radiotherapy, regarding the whole form of care concerning prospective data registration, including consent for collecting clinical and neuropsychological data from medical records.

Fatigue

Fatigue was measured with the DMFS [9], a multidimensional questionnaire to measure fatigue in patients after acquired brain injury. The DMFS is subdivided into five scales, of which the subscales “Mental fatigue” (DMFS‐Mental, 7 items) and “Physical fatigue” (DMFS‐Physical, 6 items) were used in the present study. Questions were answered on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating higher levels of fatigue. Scores on the DMFS‐Mental and DMFS‐Physical were dichotomized into “non‐severe mental/physical fatigue” (0–88 percentile score) and “severe mental/physical fatigue” (89–100 percentile score).

Affective Symptoms

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [28] is a self‐report questionnaire used to examine the presence and severity of depression (7 items) and anxiety (7 items) symptoms. Questions were answered on a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating higher levels of anxiety or depression. A score of 8 or higher indicated symptomatology indicative of anxiety or depression.

Neuropsychological Measures for Basic Information Processing Speed

Basic information processing speed was measured with the Reaction Time (RT)‐S1 (RT‐S1) and the RT‐S2 of the computerized Vienna Test System (VTS) [29]; the VTS‐RT‐S1 and VTS‐RT‐S2. These tasks measure reaction time and motor time separately. The participant is instructed to place their dominant index finger on a rest key and react as quickly as possible to optical (VTS‐RT‐S1) or auditory (VTS‐RT‐S2) stimuli by pressing a response key. Mean reaction time, the mean time between the appearance of the stimulus and lifting the index finger, was used as outcome.

Radiological Measures

The gross tumor volume (GTV), was defined as the postsurgical tumor bed, including the resection cavity, and the residual T2/FLAIR hyperintense zone. GTV was assessed on the pre‐radiotherapy MRI scan, and used as a surrogate for tumor volume. In addition, the Fazekas score, index of the amount of white matter T2 hyperintense lesions, was used as a measure for radiological appearance of the surrounding healthy brain tissue. Scores range from 0 to 3, with 0 indicating none or single punctate white matter hyperintensity (WMH) lesion; 1: multiple punctate lesions; 2: beginning confluency of lesions (bridging) and 3: large confluent lesions.

Statistical Analysis

The analyses were conducted in Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), Version 28.0. Raw test scores were calculated for each patient, and adjusted for age, sex and education (leading to norm scores, in this case, percentile scores). Educational level was scored according to the Dutch classification system of Verhage (1964) ranging from 1 (no primary school) to 7 (university degree) [30]. Impairments were defined as a test performance below the 10th percentile [31]. Subsequently, one‐sample t‐tests were used to compare standardized scores on the tests for basic and complex information processing speed with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10. A Chi‐Square Test was conducted to examine if the proportion of patients with severe mental fatigue significantly differed from the proportion of patients with severe physical fatigue. Percentile scores on neuropsychological measures for basic and complex information processing speed were compared between patients with severe versus non‐severe mental and physical fatigue using independent t‐tests. Likewise, raw scores on both HADS‐Anxiety and HADS‐Depression were compared between patients with severe versus non‐severe mental and physical fatigue using independent t‐tests. Hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted for DMFS‐Mental and DMFS‐Physical as outcome variables separately. The following blocks were entered in the hierarchical multiple regression analysis: block 1; demographics; block 2; radiological measures; block 3; divided attention; block 4; affective symptoms. Effect sizes (Cohen's d) were calculated for all between‐group comparisons (Cohen, 1988). The overall alpha level (p) was set at 0.05. In the case of multiple comparisons Bonferroni–Holm corrections were applied.

Prevalence of Mental and Physical Fatigue

Analyses show that 37.8% of the patients with LGG experienced severe mental fatigue and 21.6% experienced severe physical fatigue. The prevalence of severe mental fatigue was significantly higher than the prevalence of severe physical fatigue (χ (1) = 16.587, p < 0.001). The percentage of patients experiencing both severe mental and severe physical fatigue was 14.9%. A significant, moderate correlation coefficient was found between raw scores for mental and physical fatigue (r = 0.548).

Discussion

This is the first study that investigated mental fatigue as a distinct construct in a large homogenous group of patients with LGG, during a critical phase of illness—after surgery and before adjuvant therapy, in which cognitive functioning might be less affected compared to a later stage in the disease. Our study found that mental fatigue was present in a substantial proportion of the patients, namely more than one‐third, while physical fatigue was reported significantly less frequently. The significant differences between the rates of mental and physical fatigue, with only 14.9% overlap, in addition to the different set of determinants identified for both types of fatigue, emphasize the critical need to identify mental and physical fatigue as separate constructs. This highlights that mental fatigue should be recognized and addressed independently of physical fatigue in patients with LGG, at this stage of the disease.

The present study was the first to investigate whether determinants of mental fatigue were significantly different from determinants of physical fatigue. Our first hypothesis was that mental fatigue is related to decreased cognitive functioning, in particular in speed of information processing, as a result of brain damage caused by the tumor. Within this patient cohort, patients with severe mental fatigue indeed showed worse performance on the VTS‐DT‐S1, a task for complex information processing speed measuring divided attention, compared to patients without severe mental fatigue. No differences were found in performance on the VTS‐DT‐S1 between patients with severe versus non‐severe physical fatigue. Other measures for information processing speed did not discriminate between the two groups. This may be due to the VTS‐DT‐S1 imposing the highest cognitive load on patients among the battery of neuropsychological tests used in the current study. In addition, the highest percentage of cognitive impairments was found for the VTS‐DT‐S1, with 20% of the patients showing an impairment, further substantiating that this is the most cognitive demanding attentional task. Thus, evidence was found for the Coping hypothesis [19] in patients with LGG, which states that mental fatigue is caused by brain damage leading to deficits in information processing speed, which requires patients to exert extra effort to compensate for those deficits. However, when included in a multivariable hierarchical linear prediction model, the VTS‐DT‐S1 task did not emerge as significant predictor for mental fatigue in conjunction with other variables. The discrepancy between the univariate and multivariate outcomes indicates that, alongside divided attention, additional factors contribute to the level of mental fatigue.

One important additional factor identified in the present study was the presence of affective symptoms. A subgroup of patients with LGG experienced affective symptoms, possibly due to the constant awareness of a malignant tumor transformation in combination with the incurable nature of the disease. Feelings of anxiety and depression emerged as significant determinants of mental fatigue, which can be conceptualized in two ways. Firstly, the presence of affective symptoms may deplete mental resources, thereby leading to increased levels of mental fatigue. Secondly, elevated levels of mental fatigue may diminish psychological resilience, rendering patients more susceptible to increased anxiety and depressive symptoms. This is consistent with other studies showing a relationship between (cancer‐related) fatigue and both anxiety [26] and depression [14, 26] in patients with glioma or cancer patients in general. Until now, there has been one study [16] investigating the relationship between mental fatigue specifically and depression, finding no relationship. However, anxiety was not included in this study and the relationship with mental fatigue was not investigated in combination with other factors.

Approximately one‐fifth of the patients with LGG reported severe physical fatigue. The hierarchical linear prediction model revealed that depression was a significant predictor for physical fatigue. This finding is new in patients with LGG, but not unexpected, as the relationship between depression and fatigue has been extensively investigated and confirmed in different patient populations, such as meningioma, non‐central nervous system cancers, Parkinson's disease and Multiple sclerosis [26, 32, 33, 34]. There was only one study [14] showing a relationship between fatigue (investigated as a general construct) and depressive symptoms in specifically patients with glioma. Until now, the relationship between specifically physical fatigue and affective symptoms has not been investigated in patients with LGG, as most studies focused on fatigue as a general construct. Interestingly, the current study reveals that tumor volume and education level are also significant determinants of physical fatigue, yet these variables were not associated with mental fatigue. The determinant “tumor volume” for physical fatigue might be explained by the fact that larger tumors require more energy and nutrients to sustain their growth. It is however doubtful whether such relatively slow growing neoplasms can drain the level of energy required to induce physical fatigue. More research is needed to investigate if other factors play a role in this relationship. Additionally, educational level appeared to be an important determinant for physical fatigue, possibly because patients with a lower educational level usually have occupations that are more physically demanding, leading to higher levels of physical fatigue.

Some limitations of our study have to be taken into account. First, the current study is conducted during a critical disease period, relatively shortly after diagnosis and surgery, but before adjuvant therapy. It is therefore valuable to examine whether the prevalence and determinants of mental and physical fatigue change over time, when patients have undergone treatments and possibly reintegrated into work or social activities. In addition, it is important to note that while hierarchical multiple regression analysis enables the testing of multiple variables within one model, we cannot draw conclusions about causality between the identified determinants and mental and physical fatigue. The analyses solely evaluate associations between variables in a prediction model that relies on correlations.

In conclusion, our study shows that the percentage of severe mental fatigue (38%) was significantly higher than the percentage of severe physical fatigue (22%) in patients with LGG. Apart from the prevalence of mental fatigue being statistically higher, distinct sets of determinants were identified for each type of fatigue. Mental fatigue was related to worse performance on a divided attention task, however, anxiety and depression were found to be significant predictors when investigated in a linear prediction model with other variables. Physical fatigue was significantly associated with depression, larger tumor volume, and lower educational level. Distinguishing between mental and physical fatigue assists clinicians and researchers in developing or adapting new treatment methods that account for the different determinants of each type of fatigue in patients with LGG, such as Cognitive and Graded Activity Therapy (COGRAT) [35] ‐ a fatigue treatment that has proven effective in stroke patients, addressing both types of fatigue by combining cognitive therapy with graded activity therapy.
