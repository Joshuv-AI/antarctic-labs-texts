Prediction of suicidal ideation and depression in the general population with subthreshold insomnia using machine learning models
Authors: Polona Rus Prelog, Teodora Matić, Peter Pregelj, Aleksander Sadikov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsomnia is a significant independent risk factor for depression and suicidality. However, these conditions often go undetected, particularly in individuals presenting with sleep complaints. This study aimed to develop and validate machine learning (ML) models for the indirect screening of suicidal ideation (SI) and depression and to specifically evaluate their performance in a population reporting at least subthreshold insomnia.MethodsData were obtained from a Slovenian nationwide community sample (N = 2,989) via an online questionnaire. Logistic regression models were developed to predict SI (measured by SIDAS) and moderate-to-severe depression (measured by DASS-21) via indirect predictors, including socio-demographics, life satisfaction, behavioral changes, and 14 coping strategies from the Brief COPE inventory. The model performance was tested on a validation sample, which was stratified into groups with (Insomnia Severity Index [ISI] score ≥ 8; n = 917) and without (ISI < 8; n = 819) insomnia symptoms.ResultsThe models demonstrated strong and consistent predictive performance across both groups. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for the SI model was 0.78 in the insomnia group and 0.80 in the non-insomnia group. For the depression model, the AUROCs were 0.79 and 0.82, respectively. The minimal difference in performance indicates that the models are robust and equally effective regardless of the presence of insomnia.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that ML models using indirect questions can effectively screen for both suicidality and depression simultaneously. The models' robust performance in individuals with insomnia highlights their potential as feasible, ethical, and efficient tools for early detection. Given that sleep complaints are a common reason for seeking healthcare, this approach offers a critical opportunity for timely intervention in a high-risk population, potentially reducing preventable morbidity and mortality associated with suicide and depression.Keywords: Depression, Suicidal Ideation, Insomnia, Machine Learning, Indirect Screening, Suicide Prevention, Coping Mechanisms

Introduction

Depression is a severe and often debilitating illness that affects approximately 3.8% of the global population—equivalent to 280 million people [1]. It is characterized by persistent feelings of sadness, loss of interest in previously enjoyable activities, and significant emotional and physical distress. In addition to its devastating impact on daily functioning, depression is a leading cause of suicide, with many affected individuals experiencing overwhelming feelings of worthlessness, guilt, and despair [2]. Among its most pervasive and disruptive symptom is insomnia, which affects the majority of individuals with depression. Sleep disturbances not only exacerbate emotional suffering but also contribute to cognitive impairments, increased fatigue, and a heightened sense of hopelessness. Furthermore, subjective sleep disorders have been demonstrated to predict future depression [3, 4]. In fact, many longitudinal studies have identified insomnia as an independent risk factor for the development of emerging or recurrent depression among young, middle-aged, and older adults [5, 6]. Alarmingly, depression often remains undetected or misdiagnosed, partly because of the subjective nature of self-reported symptoms and the overlap with other mental health conditions. Although valuable, traditional screening methods may fail to identify those most at risk—particularly individuals struggling with insomnia [7, 8], who might not recognize its connection to underlying depression. As a result, many cases become untreated, increasing the likelihood of severe outcomes, including self-harm and suicide.

Increasing research indicates that sleep disturbances may confer increased risk for suicidal behaviors, including suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and death by suicide [9]. It has also been demonstrated that sleep complaints are associated with an increased risk of attempting suicide independent of psychopathology and should be included in suicide risk assessments, as these symptoms can provide targets for reducing the risk of suicidal behaviors [10, 11]. Suicide is increasingly recognized as an urgent public health issue. Every year, more than 720,000 people take their own life, and many more people make suicide attempts [12]. Deaths from suicide are a tragic yet preventable cause of mortality [13].

Multiple studies and meta-analyses have shown that insomnia and sleep disturbances are significant risk factors for suicidal ideation and suicide attempts across diverse clinical and nonclinical populations [14, 15]. Recent research demonstrates that individuals with insomnia have more than double the odds of experiencing suicidal ideation (OR ≈ 2.1) and a markedly increased risk for suicide attempts—even when controlling for depression and other psychiatric disorders [14]. Furthermore, a recent systematic review highlighted sleep disturbances as a key modifiable risk factor for suicidal behavior in individuals with first episode psychosis [16].

Owing to the strong link between chronic insomnia and suicidality, reported insomnia is a critical symptom to monitor in high-risk individuals. Despite extensive research, accurately predicting individuals at risk remains a challenge, with current tools showing prediction accuracy no better than chance [17].

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a promising tool for predicting medical illness [18]. In line with that, artificial intelligence in mental healthcare is currently reshaping the screening, diagnosis, and treatment of psychiatric disorders as well [19]. However, as many large-sample studies have mixed psychiatric and nonpsychiatric populations, a formal psychiatric diagnosis emerged as a strong predictor of suicide risk, overshadowing more subtle risk factors. Although only a few studies have implemented ML for suicide prediction, the results indicate considerable improvements in accuracy and positive predictive value [18]. Suicidal ideation is still considered a key precursor for suicide attempts and suicide deaths; therefore, performing routine screening is the way to identify people who are at high risk of death by suicide [20].

However, screening for suicide risk in the general population involves important ethical considerations and can be inappropriate in some contexts, on the basis of which we developed and validated a model for the indirect screening of suicidality in the general population [21, 22]. However, screening people with sleep complaints, which is a specific, at-risk population, might be considerably more justified, as insomnia and sleep problems carry important risks for depression and suicidality. More importantly, it could be effective (in terms of time and cost), acceptable (as these people commonly seek help), and quite feasible in some settings (GP ambulatory services, preventive program facilities, etc.). Given the urgent need for early and accurate detection, particularly in individuals experiencing insomnia, leveraging machine learning models for screening could play a crucial role in identifying at-risk individuals before their condition escalates.

Study aim

Our present study aimed to evaluate the performance of an ML-based pilot predictive model for suicidality and depression screening using indirect questions on the population reporting at least subthreshold insomnia. For this purpose, an additional goal was to build a model for the indirect detection of depression, using the same predictors as the model for suicidality.

Methods

Study design and sample characteristics

The data used for training and validating our machine learning models were obtained from an ongoing population-based study, which forms part of a larger international multicenter research initiative [23, 24]. Data collection was conducted via an online questionnaire administered to a nationwide community sample of adults in Slovenia. A more detailed description of the implementation procedure is available in both cited studies [25, 26]. The study received ethical approval from the Republic of Slovenia National Medical Ethics Committee (protocol no. 0120–283/2020/7) and was carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent for participation was obtained from all participants. Data collection spanned from July 23, 2020, to February 23, 2022. The total sample included 2,989 participants—1,790 in the first COVID-19 wave and 1,199 in the second wave. Data from the first wave were used for model training and an initial performance test using a 30% holdout. The second wave data were used exclusively to further validate the model’s performance [22].

Measures

A broad set of predictors, which were described in detail in our original paper on the model [21], was employed in our machine learning setup. We briefly recap them here.

The Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) was used to assess sleep quality. It is a widely used, brief self-report questionnaire designed to assess the nature, severity, and impact of insomnia [27]. It consists of seven items that evaluate difficulties with sleep onset, sleep maintenance, early morning awakening, satisfaction with current sleep patterns, interference with daily functioning, noticeability of sleep problems to others, and distress caused by sleep difficulties. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 28. Higher scores indicate greater insomnia severity. We used a cutoff scale of eight or higher (meaning at least sub-threshold insomnia) to define a group with insomnia.

Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, and the number of household members. Life satisfaction was assessed via three questions targeting satisfaction with (1) financial situation, (2) relationships with household members or closest people if living alone, and (3) housing. Each item was rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from “could not be worse” to “could not be better.” These items were treated as individual variables, as they do not constitute a predefined scale.

The behavioral change variables included increased use of substances (tobacco, alcohol, marijuana, other psychoactive substances, and nonprescribed drugs), changes in physical activity and eating (both increase and decrease), and the estimated daily amount of time spent online.

Stress coping was measured via the Brief COPE inventory [28], a 28-item multidimensional tool that assesses the frequency of use of various coping strategies on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (“I haven’t been doing this at all”) to 4 (“I’ve been doing this a lot”). We included all 14 subscales as individual variables: (1) self-distraction, (2) active coping, (3) denial, (4) substance use, (5) use of emotional support, (6) use of instrumental support, (7) behavioral disengagement, (8) venting, (9) positive reframing, (10) planning, (11) humor, (12) acceptance, (13) religion, and (14) self-blame. Each subscale score was computed by summing its two respective items.

Suicidal ideation (SI) was measured via the suicidal ideation attributes scale (SIDAS) [29]. This instrument comprises five items assessing the frequency of suicidal thoughts, controllability, closeness to attempt, associated distress, and interference with daily functioning over the past month. Each item is rated on a 10-point Likert scale, producing a total score between 0 and 50. If a participant selected “0—Never” on the first item, the remaining items were skipped, and the total score was recorded as zero. Given that any indication of SI suggests a potential suicide risk [27], we categorized the sample into those scoring zero (no SI) and those scoring above zero (presence of SI in the past month).

The second target variable was the Depression subscale of the 21-item Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21), a validated self-report tool for assessing mental health in the general population [30]. The depression score was split at the cutoff of 21 (cutoff for moderate depression), and all subjects scoring 21 or higher were classified as having elevated depression.

The two models also use the same machine learning pipeline for their development. They were trained on 70% of the data from the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic (1,253 respondents), stratified by the target variable, while the remaining 30% (537 respondents) were set aside for testing. Further independent validation was performed with data from the later (post)pandemic period of 2022 (1,199 respondents). Four machine learning algorithms were employed (logistic regression (LGR), random forest, XG Boost and support vector machines); however, for both models, we report here the results of the LGR because it slightly outperformed the other models, and especially because of its ease of interpretation.

Both models use the following predictors: (1) a set of sociodemographic variables (age, gender, and the number of people living in the household), (2) satisfaction with three aspects of life—personal financial situation, relationship with the people the person lives with, or relationships with their closest people if they lived alone, and their current housing, (3) a set of variables related to recent changes in behavior (increase in substance use—any among tobacco, alcohol, marijuana, other psychoactive substances, and drugs not prescribed by a physician, quantitative change in the level of physical activity and eating, and the estimated daily amount of time spent online), and (4) the Brief COPE scale [28], with all 14 subscales used as separate predictors. On the basis of the ISI, we divided the previously unseen (test and validation) subjects into two groups, using a cutoff scale of 8 (> = 8, meaning at least subthreshold insomnia): one with (ISI +) insomnia symptoms and one without insomnia symptoms (below subthreshold insomnia; ISI-). We tested the previously described models for SI and depression in these groups and observed little difference in the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) performance metric: 0.78 (n = 917) in the ISI + group versus 0.80 (n = 819) in the ISI- group for the SI. For depression, the AUROC was 0.79 (n = 917) in the ISI + group and 0.82 (n = 819) in the ISI- group. Discussion

The study examined the performance of machine learning-based predictive models for SI and depression screening using indirect questions in the subgroup with insomnia (defined as at least subthreshold as defined with an ISI score >= 8) in the general population. Within this study, we explored the validity of our previously developed and validated machine learning models for detecting SI indirectly [21, 22], without unnecessary exposure to direct questions on suicidality, specifically for people reporting insomnia. Additionally, alongside our previously developed model for SI prediction, we developed a model for predicting depression indirectly on the basis of the same predictors (allowing both models to be deployed by collecting the same data from the user, without extra time needed). Both models (for SI and for depression) were tested on two subgroups: those with ISI + and those with ISI-. We observed no significant difference in performance, indicating that the models work well in both subgroups. Our model can detect the risk for SI and depression, and we showed its equal performance in people with or without insomnia, indicating potential usefulness in the clinical population.

This study advances previous work in two important ways. First, we tested and demonstrated that our predictive models for both suicidality and depression maintain similar accuracy in individuals with and without insomnia, supporting their use in sleep-disturbed and general population. Second, by employing identical predictors, we show that dual screening for suicidal ideation and depression can be performed efficiently, which enhances practical applicability for broad mental health surveillance and digital health platforms. The study addresses two methodological gaps: validation of indirect, screening prediction models in targeted insomnia populations and operational efficiency in mental health risk assessment, highlighting the generalizability and practicality of our approach in diverse clinical and epidemiological contexts.

The machine learning models for suicidality and depression in the present study demonstrated relatively strong predictive capabilities, with AUROC scores above 0.78 across all the tested groups. Notably, the models’ performance remained consistent across individuals with and without insomnia symptoms, reinforcing its robustness and generalizability. The small differences in AUROC values between the insomnia-positive (ISI +) and insomnia-negative (ISI −) groups suggest that the model remains reliable regardless of sleep disturbances. This is an important finding, as insomnia is a well-documented risk factor for both depression and suicidality. We thus confirmed and validated our previously built model for suicidality, built a model for depression that can indirectly screen for depression, and more importantly, we proved that the models work in people reporting even sub-threshold insomnia with the same accuracy. Although insomnia is a well-documented risk factor and a symptom of depression and SI, these important conditions are often overlooked, or more targeted evaluations are not made for several (time/economic) reasons. A major advantage of a tool for indirect screening is its appropriateness for wider use, which can enable and justify further, more focused evaluation/diagnostics in people at risk.

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have robustly quantified the associations between sleep disturbances—particularly insomnia—and suicidality. For example, Wang et al. [31] reported that sleep disturbances are significantly associated with increased risk for suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and suicide, with pooled odds ratios indicating that individuals with insomnia are more than twice as likely to experience suicidal thoughts and behaviors than those without sleep problems. Bernert et al. [9] similarly highlighted that insomnia and nightmares are not only frequent comorbidities in suicidal individuals but also may serve as independent predictors of suicidality, even after adjusting for psychiatric disorders [9]. These findings are echoed by more recent meta-analyses, which confirm that the relationship between sleep disturbance and suicide risk persists across different populations and is not fully explained by depression or other psychiatric comorbidities [32, 33]. This growing body of evidence underscores the clinical importance of systematic screening for sleep disturbances as part of suicide risk assessment and highlights the added value of our ML-based approach, which leverages a broad array of behavioral and coping variables to identify at-risk individuals, including those who may not present with overt psychiatric symptoms.

Studies of ML-based models for SI and depression assessments are nevertheless rare and difficult to compare. To the best of our knowledge, no other study has explored indirect models for suicidality and depression using ML in people with (subthreshold) insomnia. However, few other studies have explored the relationships among insomnia, depression, and suicidality, using machine learning or statistical models to predict outcomes. For example, a recent study [10] highlighted the strong association between sleep disturbances and SI, stressing the importance of including sleep complaints in suicide risk assessments. Our findings align with this perspective, as our predictive models were able to capture the heightened risk of suicidality in individuals with insomnia. In contrast to previous studies [9–11], which focused primarily on the role of sleep disturbances in SI using clinical diagnoses, our models broaden the scope of suicide risk detection to a much larger segment of the population—those who may not meet strict diagnostic criteria but are nevertheless at elevated risk. Subjective, self-reported sleep disturbances have been shown to be robustly linked to both suicidality and depression in diverse populations, independent of objective sleep measures or formal psychiatric diagnosis. This finding supports screening even in people with self-reported insomnia [14, 30, 34–36]. By capturing individuals with subthreshold or self-identified sleep problems, our model offers a more sensitive and inclusive tool for early intervention, which could have substantial implications for public health and suicide prevention efforts.

Previous research has identified coping mechanisms as important in mitigating suicidality [37, 38], but our study goes further by incorporating these variables within a ML framework, offering a more comprehensive understanding of their role in suicide risk prediction and, at the same time, allowing the potential of the model to detect SI and depression indirectly. Our models identified key predictors of suicidality, such as self-blame, substance use, and behavioral disengagement, which is consistent with the findings of other studies on suicidality risk [37].

Perhaps the most important difference between our study and others is the methodological approach used. While studies [18, 38–40] have explored ML-based models for suicide prediction, the potential for indirect prediction is a novel aspect of our work. Moreover, the majority of studies that have applied ML models to predict suicidality have focused on more overt mental health conditions, such as major depressive disorder (MDD).

In terms of model performance, our results are consistent with those of comparable studies [20], with our study’s AUROC of 0.83 for SI prediction, demonstrating the robustness of our model. However, a major distinction in our study is the use of behavioral and coping-related variables [20] that not only add an additional layer of insight that can enhance the models’ precision and applicability in clinical settings but also prove that, on the basis of these, the model can efficiently predict SI and depression.

Implications

Our study has important implications. The key finding of our study is the potential of our ML models to enhance early detection in a cost-effective and time-efficient manner. While traditional risk assessments of both suicide and depression can be resource intensive, our ML-based models offer a scalable approach that could be easily integrated into routine screenings in primary care or mental health settings. This would allow for the timely identification of at-risk individuals and provide clinicians with valuable data to guide decision-making. Given that insomnia is often one of the first symptoms to appear in individuals with depression [3], it often serves as an early warning signal for at-risk individuals. Moreover, given that people with insomnia frequently seek help for their sleep problems and that many authors [10, 14] advocate including sleep complaint into suicide risk assessments, indirect screening for SI and depression in people with sleep disturbances could be an ethical and feasible approach for suicide prevention in clinical settings.

Indirect screening for suicidality could also offer significant practical and ethical advantages in population-level suicide prevention. Direct questioning about suicidal ideation may sometimes result in under-identification due to stigma, fear of disclosure, or discomfort—especially in digital or primary care settings where trust is still being established. Indirect approaches, as used in our study, leverage non-stigmatizing questions about coping, habits, and life satisfaction, increasing the acceptability of screening and allowing for earlier identification of risk without requiring explicit admission of suicidality.

Beyond potential use in clinical and occupational settings, machine learning-based mental health screening models can play a significant role in public health and epidemiological surveillance [41, 42]. Applying these models could broadly enable to monitor regional differences in depression and suicidality, aiding in the early identification of geographic areas or groups experiencing heightened risk. These insights can inform targeted resource allocation, enabling planners to strategically deploy psychiatric support and preventive interventions where they are most needed, and adapt public health policies to evolving trends. Stakeholders interested in such approaches may include public health authorities, national health systems, employers, educational institutions, and organizations responsible for at-risk populations—such as the military or emergency response sectors. While our model is not yet suited for individual clinical diagnosis or immediate operational deployment, it offers proof-of-concept utility for epidemiological research, population-level surveillance, and strategic planning. Further studies are required to validate the model in various settings and ensure ethically responsible, context-sensitive implementation.

Limitations and future directions

Despite these promising results, several limitations need to be considered. First, the study relied on self-reported data, which may introduce biases, particularly in terms of the accuracy of insomnia and other symptom reporting. Our study also has several limitations relating to sample representativeness. Data were collected via an online survey, which is known to overrepresent individuals with higher levels of education, digital access, and interest in the research topic, while underrepresenting more disadvantaged or digitally-excluded groups. Accordingly, our sample includes a higher share of participants with tertiary education compared to the full adult Slovenian population. This may limit the generalizability of findings, particularly regarding those of lower socioeconomic status, who are also at increased risk for suicidality. However, it is important to note that Slovenia’s working-age and young adult population is highly educated by EU and OECD standards. For example, 41% of 25–34 year-olds held a tertiary degree in 2023—a major increase over previous decades, and placing Slovenia well above many countries in the region. Therefore, the higher education level observed in our sample represents a relatively modest bias in the Slovenian context, though it remains a limitation for full generalizability. Nevertheless, future studies should prioritize recruitment of less advantaged and less digitally connected populations to further validate model performance, and also in different cultural contexts to ensure generalizability. Moreover, the present sample is composed exclusively of Slovenian adults surveyed during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Pandemic-related disruptions in Slovenia—such as heightened psychological stress, altered daily routines, and changes in health service access—may have influenced the coping mechanisms, sleep patterns, and mental health outcomes within the study population. Similar trends in increased depression, anxiety, and insomnia have been reported in other European countries, though cultural responses to stressors and approaches to coping may differ [43]. European-wide analyses show that while overall suicide rates did not markedly increase during the first pandemic year, changes in suicide patterns were observed across countries and demographic groups, influenced by social, cultural, and religious factors. For instance, in some regions, the elderly and certain religious communities experienced higher vulnerability [44]. In terms of coping strategies, cross-cultural studies demonstrate variability: social support from family and friends was predominant across sites, but levels of optimism, meaning-making, and psychological well-being are shaped by national, cultural, and pandemic contexts. Consequently, findings from the Slovenian context may reflect unique sociocultural and situational characteristics and should be interpreted with this in mind [45].

To strengthen external validity, future research is needed to test these models in culturally diverse populations and under varying public health circumstances. This will help determine whether the observed relationships between insomnia, coping strategies, and suicidality hold across settings, or are primarily shaped by specific local factors and cultural contexts. Finally, while the predictive accuracy of our models was relatively strong, the clinical utility of these models should be further evaluated in real-world settings to assess their impact on patient outcomes.

One of the most promising aspects of this approach is its potential for real-world application. If integrated into digital health platforms, online screening tools, or primary care assessments, our ML-based models could help flag individuals who may require further psychological or psychiatric evaluation. This is especially important for those who might not recognize the severity of their symptoms or those who hesitate to seek help due to stigma. Given the strong connection between sleep disturbances and mental health issues, healthcare providers could use this model to prioritize early intervention strategies for those at greater risk. These tools are of course most effective when paired with follow-up steps—clinical assessment, safety planning, and outreach for those flagged as at-risk. Our model is intended to provide actionable data to inform both population health strategies and individual risk review, but always in concert with ongoing clinician engagement and support. External validation in diverse populations and healthcare settings is planned, but prospective studies are needed to assess the impact of model-guided screening on clinical outcomes. Future research should also focus on integration with digital health tools and electronic health records and exploration of additional predictors, including biological and digital phenotyping data.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that our ML models can effectively indirectly predict SI and depression at the same time and are effective also in individuals reporting insomnia symptoms. Our findings build on literature that highlights the role of insomnia as a risk factor for depression and suicidality but extend it by incorporating behavioral and coping variables into predictive models that, most importantly, can detect suicidality and depression via indirect questions. The models, which require less than 10 min to administer, allow for early identification of SI or depression in individuals with sleep complaints, facilitating timely intervention. Given the substantial burden of depression and its association with suicidality, integrating ML-based tools into public health strategies could significantly increase early detection and intervention, ultimately reducing preventable morbidity and deaths. The proposed tools, however (classification models used in clinical decision support or screening settings), are still considered a pilot model at this stage. Future work is planned to explore additional predictors, different international settings and longitudinal tracking, to refine the model’s predictive power.
