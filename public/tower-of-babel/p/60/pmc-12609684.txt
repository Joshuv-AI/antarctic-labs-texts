Pancreatic hypersecretion of amyloid-forming human amylin dysregulates the neuro-immune axis and B cell development in a model of type-2 diabetes
Authors: Edric D. Winford, Ryan Shahidehpour, Dallin Dressman, Jadwiga Turchan-Cholewo, Katherine M. Cotter, Noah Leibold, Thomas A. Ujas, Daimen R.S. Britsch, Domenico Mercurio, Mary K. Colson, Nirmal Verma, Sanda Despa, Adam D. Bachstetter, Florin Despa, Ann M. Stowe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
During Type 2 diabetes (T2D), the immune system becomes dysregulated, adopting a pro-inflammatory phenotype and secreting cytokines that exacerbate inflammation. Human amylin (i.e., islet amyloid polypeptide) secreted from the pancreas of HIP rats during T2D has been shown to deposit in the microvasculature and lead to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. Although T2D is linked to neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment, whether and how peripheral immune changes, driven in part by amyloidogenic amylin deposition, shape these outcomes is not well understood. We investigated how the accumulation of pancreatic human amylin impacts the peripheral and neuroimmune response in transgenic rat and mouse models with endogenous overexpression. In rats, hypersecretion of pancreatic human amylin reduced adaptive and innate immune cells in the spleen and brain, the latter associated with the downregulation of cerebrovascular ICAM-1, which is responsible for immune cell migration into the brain. Acute systemic injections of aggregated human amylin in wild-type (WT) rats led to immune alterations in the blood only, suggesting brain-specific changes may require chronic exposure. In mice that hypersecrete pancreatic human amylin, we identified defects in B cell development, including reduced B cell populations in the spleen and bone marrow. This immune dysregulation was associated with upregulated CXCR4 expression in precursor B cells, suggesting a potential mechanism for systemic B cell losses during chronic T2D. To further support these findings in human disease, single-cell sequencing of patients with T2D revealed elevated CXCR4 expression across multiple B cell subsets, highlighting the relevance of this axis in diabetic immune dysregulation. Taken together, our findings indicate that T2D-associated amylin may contribute to T2D by altering both peripheral and neuroimmune responses. Moreover, the CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling axis emerges as a promising target for immunotherapeutic strategies to mitigate type-2 diabetic cognitive decline and neurodegeneration.

Introduction

Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) is a significant risk factor for cognitive decline and dementia-related disorders such as Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) (Rojas et al., 2021). Hyperglycemia, altered insulin signaling, and neuroinflammation have all been implicated in AD pathology, but how the immune system orchestrates or exacerbates these interrelated processes remains poorly defined. Amylin (or islet amyloid polypeptide) is a hormone peptide co-secreted with insulin by the pancreatic β-cells (Westermark et al., 2011). During T2D, amylin is oversecreted and forms amyloid deposits in the pancreatic β-cells, contributing to pancreatic β-cell dysfunction. Amylin also accumulates in the cerebral blood vessels of patients presenting with comorbid T2D and dementia (Jackson et al., 2013; Ly et al., 2017). In rats that overexpress human amylin in the pancreas (HIP rat), amylin hypersecretion leads to the accumulation of amylin in the brain, neuroinflammation, and cognitive deficits (Ly et al., 2017; Srodulski et al., 2014; Verma et al., 2023). These findings suggest a pathological function for amylin in the brain, linking T2D with dementia.

The immune response plays a significant role in the progression of T2D, with its dysregulation linked to hyperglycemia and insulinemia (Berbudi et al., 2020). Although a well-established link exists between T2D and immune activation (Santopaolo et al., 2021), it remains unclear how impaired chemotaxis, leukocyte adhesion, and trans-endothelial migration of immune cells into the brain contribute to disease progression in patients who develop both T2D and dementia (Hill et al., 1983; Delamaire et al., 1997; Geerlings and Hoepelman, 1999; Waltenberger et al., 2000; SantaCruz-Calvo et al., 2022). While immune cells are most known for their role in the periphery, they are also essential in the maintenance and repair of the brain (Schwartz et al., 2022), monitoring the brain through central nervous system (CNS) niches such as the meninges, calveria marrow, the choroid plexus, and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF), and playing essential roles in neurodevelopment and cognition (Norris and Kipnis, 2019; Chen and Holtzman, 2022). Mice deficient in T cells have cognitive deficits, while mice deficient in both B and T cells showed impaired neurogenesis under otherwise healthy conditions (Kipnis et al., 2004; Ziv et al., 2006). Emerging evidence also suggests that peripheral immune cells contribute to neurodegenerative disorders by influencing neuronal loss and cognitive decline (Ziv et al., 2006; Garber et al., 2019; Subbarayan et al., 2020; Gate et al., 2021; Mildner et al., 2011; Lampron et al., 2013; Mrdjen et al., 2018). However, the involvement of immune cells in T2D-related neurodegeneration remains unclear.

The CXCL12/CXCR4 axis regulates multiple physiological processes, including leukocyte chemotaxis, cell survival, proliferation, lymphopoiesis, hematopoiesis, and inflammation (Bleul et al., 1996; Man et al., 2012; Yano et al., 2007; Broxmeyer et al., 2005; McCandless et al., 2006). Beyond these physiological functions, it also contributes to a range of pathologies, such as cancer, diabetes, pain, multiple sclerosis, stroke, and neurodegeneration (McCandless et al., 2006; Shi et al., 2020; Vidakovic et al., 2015; Luo et al., 2016; Ruscher et al., 2013; Selvaraj et al., 2017; Bonham et al., 2018). In T2D, serum CXCL12 levels are increased (Derakhshan et al., 2012), while CXCR4+ cells in the peripheral blood are reduced (Nathan et al., 2015; Egan et al., 2008), supporting a link between the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis and the pathogenesis of T2D. Blocking CXCR4 in a mouse model of T2D reduced M1 macrophage phenotype and improved insulin sensitivity (Kim et al., 2014). Contrasting studies suggest that CXCL12 can also have anti-inflammatory properties through polarizing T cells to a regulatory phenotype (Meiron et al., 2008) and macrophages to an ‘M2’ phenotype (Beider et al., 2014); however, these results were shown in non-diabetic models. The CXCL12/CXCR4 axis is also important for neuroinflammation and leukocyte migration in the CNS. Elevated brain levels of CXCL12 and/or CXCR4 have been reported in conditions such as multiple sclerosis, stroke, Parkinson’s and AD (McCandless et al., 2006; Ruscher et al., 2013; Li et al., 2019; Weeraratna et al., 2007). In autoimmune disease, CXCL12 recruits CXCR4+ leukocytes and glial cells to inflamed tissue in the CNS. These findings suggest that the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis regulates leukocyte migration and neuroinflammation. However, little is known about the relationship between amylin accumulation and the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis.

This study aimed to understand how amylin accumulation impacts immune cell populations in the brain and periphery. Using both rats and mice transgenic for human amylin expression (HIP rats and mice) as models of T2D, we found that the chronic accumulation of amylin caused a decrease in immune cells in the bone marrow, blood, spleen, and brain, and coincided with changes in proteins responsible for immune cell migration in the brain. Additionally, we found that the accumulation of pancreatic amylin leads to defects in B cell development, which was associated with the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis, indicating that amylin plays a crucial role in immune dysregulation during T2D-related neurodegeneration.

Methods

Rats and mice

Sprague-Dawley rats (~16–18 months) overexpressing the pancreatic hormone human amylin in pancreatic β-cells (HIP rats) (Matveyenko and Butler, 2006), along with age-matched wild-type (WT) controls were used in this study. Breeding pairs were obtained from Charles Rivers Laboratory. Sprague-Dawley WT rats at 12 months were used for amylin injection experiments. HIP mice (Tg-Ins2-IAPP; ~5–6 months) on the FVB/N background (Janson et al., 1996) overexpressing human amylin in the pancreas were obtained from Jackson Laboratory. Heterozygous HIP rats and homozygous HIP mice were chosen based on being transgenic for human islet amyloid polypeptide (IAPP), determined by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (Butler et al., 2004). Only diabetic male rats and mice with a non-fasted blood glucose of >200 mg/dL determined by a OneTouch Ultra glucometer were included in this study, as pancreatic amylin deposition and associated deficits develop ~6 months later in females, confounding potential differences with age effects (Verma et al., 2023). All animal experiments complied with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Kentucky. Rats and mice had ad libitum access to a standard diet and water. Rats were caged individually in ventilated cages, while mice were caged 3–5 mice/cage. Both species were kept in a 12-h light/dark cycle and randomly assigned to groups when appropriate.

Rat experiments

Rats were anesthetized with 3–5 % isoflurane (100 % O2), and blood was collected via heart puncture. Rats were then transcardially perfused with cold Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; Thermo Fisher). After perfusion, spleens were removed and kept on ice for flow cytometry, and the brains were then removed from the skull and dissected into hemispheres. One hemisphere was kept on ice and used for flow cytometry, while the other was used for immunohistochemistry, which was post-fixed in 10 % formalin for 24 h, then paraffin-embedded, and sectioned sagittally for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IFC) analyses. For plasma collection, blood from rats was collected in 5 mL Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-coated tubes (Vitality Medical). Blood was centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and plasma and red blood cells (RBCs) were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Rat single-cell isolation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from rats were isolated using Ficoll-Paque PLUS density gradient centrifugation (cytiva –density 1.077 + 0.001 g/mL). Briefly, 2 mL of blood was diluted with 3 mL of HBSS, layered upon an equal amount of Ficoll-Paque plus, and centrifuged at 500 ×g for 30 min with brakes off. The buffy coat containing cells was collected and resuspended in EAE media (Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium (RPMI) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum, HEPES, sodium pyruvate, minimum essential medium (MEM) non-essential amino acids (NEAA), penicillin-streptomycin, l-glutamine and beta-mercaptoethanol) until use. Spleens were homogenized through a 70 μm strainer and washed and centrifuged at 500 ×g for 5 min. The pellet was suspended in 5 mL of EAE media, layered on an equal amount of Rat-lympholyte (Cedarlane Labs), and centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 20 min at RT with the brakes off as published previously (Selvaraj et al., 2021). The buffy coat containing splenocytes was then collected and used for flow cytometry, and extra cells were cryopreserved in freezing media. For immune cell isolation, brain tissue was homogenized using dounce homogenizers, and the homogenate was made into a 30 % Percoll solution and centrifuged at 500 ×g for 30 min at RT with no brakes. The debris was removed, the cell pellet was washed, strained through a 70 μm filter, and resuspended in EAE media.

Rat tissue homogenization and western blot

Frozen rat brain tissues were hemisected, and cortical tissue was dissected from one hemisphere and homogenized in 1 % Triton buffer (25 times tissue volume) containing 20mM Tris-HCl, 150mM NaCl, 1mM EDTA, 1mM EGTA, 1 % Triton X-100 (v/v), 1 % (v/v) protease and phosphatase inhibitors, pH 7.5. The homogenates were incubated on ice for 15min and then centrifuged at 22,000 ×g for 15min at 4°C. The supernatant (Triton-soluble fraction) was separated from the pellet and used for western blot experiments as previously described (Verma et al., 2023). Western blot analysis was performed on brain tissue from rats as reported recently (Verma et al., 2023). Total protein levels were quantified using a Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA kit) (Thermo Fisher). Anti-vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 or VCAM-1 (1:500, MA5–31965, Thermo Fisher), anti-intracellular adhesion molecule 1 or ICAM-1 (1:250; MA5407, Thermo Fisher), mouse anti-β actin (1,10,000; clone BA3R; MA5–15739; Thermo-Fisher), anti-rabbit IgG HRP conjugated (1,30,000; NA934VS; GE Healthcare), and anti-mouse IgG HRP conjugated (1,20,000; NXA931; GE Healthcare) were the primary and secondary antibodies used. Signal detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (Thermo Fisher). 50μg of protein from tissue homogenate were loaded on SDS-PAGE gels. Equal protein loading was verified by re-probing with a monoclonal anti-β actin antibody (raised in mouse, clone BA3R, Thermo Scientific; 1:2000). Protein levels were quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software.

ELISA

SDF-1/CXCL12 levels were measured in cerebral cortex homogenates dissected similarly for western blot from WT and HIP rats using a commercial ELISA kit (A77295, antibodies.com) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Frozen brains were homogenized in buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaF, 2 % Triton X-100, and 0.1 % SDS. Phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktails (1 % each; Calbiochem) were added immediately before use. The buffer pH was adjusted to 7.5 with HCl/NaOH and brought to a final volume of 100 mL with distilled water.

Rat Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For IHC, an adapted quantitative multiplex Immunohistochemistry with Visual colorimetric staining to Enhance Regional protein localization (QUIVER) protocol was used to repeatedly stain a single tissue section (Shahidehpour et al., 2023). CXCL12 antibody, 1:100 (Thermo Fisher; PA5–114344), was used with a soluble chromogenic substrate (ImmPACT AMEC Red Substrate kit; Vector Laboratories). After the first round of staining, tissue was digitized using a Zeiss Axio Scan Z.1 slide scanner to capture and digitize complete slide images at 20× magnification. Tissue was then chemically destained and serially stained for neuronal marker protein (NeuN 1:250) with a permanent chromogen (DAB Substrate Kit, Peroxidase (HRP) (3,3′-diaminobenzidine) (SK-4100), and immediately co-labeled with IBA1 (1:1000, Synaptic Systems, RRID AB_2493179) and (ImmPACT AMEC Red Substrate kit; Vector Laboratories). Images were subsequently scanned, registered, deconvolved, and consolidated into a single pseudo-color image using the HALO software (Indica Labs, version 3.6).

For the quantification of cell number and colocalization for each marker, HALO software utilized the Object Colocalization algorithm to assess the overall cell number for each marker and the number of cells colocalized. To establish a region of interest for each rat, brightfield images were used to outline the cortical region of similar size in each animal. Each brightfield image was registered, deconvolved, and merged to make a single pseudo-fluorescent image. This was achieved by separating the chromogenic stain from each staining round using the HALO deconvolution algorithm. This algorithm uses color selection and thresholding to create a single-channel image. Then, using the HALO Serial Registration module, the multiple rounds of staining were merged into a pseudo-fluorescent image. After merging images, the fluorescent object colocalization algorithm automatically detected each marker. Parameters for marker detection were determined based on stain intensity, signal-to-noise ratio, and relative cell size-including processes, and then applied to all sections. To ensure that only cell bodies were being counted and not aggregates of intersecting processes, a larger minimum size threshold was set (30 μm-10,000 μm). After setting detection parameters, previously outlined cortical grey matter in each section was analyzed. The results presented are based on the number of cells per mm2 in each section.

Rat immunofluorescence

Prepared brain sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through xylene and graded alcohols, followed by antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Slides were washed in TBS, permeabilized with 0.25 % Triton™ X-100, and blocked with 5 % BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C in 1 % BSA, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL; Invitrogen #D1306) for 5 min, and slides were mounted with ProLong™ Glass Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen #P36984). The following antibodies were used: ICAM-1 (mouse IgG, Invitrogen #MA5407; 1:100) with goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor Plus 488 (Invitrogen #A32723; 1:2000).

Rat amylin aggregation and injection

Lyophilized amidated human amylin peptide (Anaspec #AS-60254–1) was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4 at a concentration of 50μM. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 72h with intermittent shaking to promote amylin aggregation. Aggregation was not directly assessed in this study, as this protocol has been previously published and shown to produce amylin accumulation in the blood and brain of injected rats (Ly et al., 2017; Verma et al., 2023). The aggregated human amylin solution was administered to 12-month-old WT rats via tail vein (80μg/kg) (n = 5 male rats), 2× daily via tail vein for 2 weeks as previously described (Verma et al., 2023). After 2 weeks, rats were euthanized, and their organs were collected for flow cytometry and IHC as described above.

Rat RNA-seq

RNA sequencing from WT and HIP rats (n = 10 males/group) were published previously (Verma et al., 2023) and reanalyzed to determine genes changed by pancreatic human amylin following the development of cerebrovascular amylin deposits (Ly et al., 2017; Verma et al., 2023). RNA from the cerebral cortex was isolated using Qiagen’s RNeasy Mini Kit (Ref # 74104). RNAseq library preparations and sequencing were performed by Omega Bioservices using the Illumina HiSeq 2500. PartekFlow (Partek, MO) was used to analyze the resulting RNAseq data. Briefly, RNAseq fastq files were imported and aligned to the rat reference genome (Rattus norvegicus-rn7), and gene level quantification was performed using Ensembl105 annotation. RNAseq read counts were normalized and differential gene expression between WT and HIP rats was analyzed using DESeq2 algorithm. 403 genes were identified as significant (p-values ≤ 0.05) between WT and HIP rats. These differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were further analyzed for the enrichment of canonical pathways using Ingenuity Pathway Analyses software (IPA, Qiagen) and for Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes using Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) NIH.

Mice experiments

Mice were anesthetized in the same manner as the rats, and blood was collected via heart puncture similarly. Mice were also transcardially perfused with HBSS (Thermo Fisher). For plasma collection, blood was collected in a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube with .5 M of EDTA. Blood was centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and plasma and RBCs were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Mice single-cell isolation

Mouse bone marrow (BM) cells were obtained by flushing dissected tibia and femur bones with EAE media using a 25-gauge syringe. The resulting suspension was filtered through a 70 μm filter to remove debris, centrifuged, and resuspended in EAE media. Spleens were homogenized through a 70 μm strainer and washed and centrifuged in the same way as rats. The pellet was suspended in 5 mL of RPMI, layered on an equal amount of Mouse-lympholyte (Cedarlane Labs), and centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 20 min at RT with the brakes off. The buffy coat containing splenocytes was then collected and used for flow cytometry, and extra cells were cryopreserved in freezing media. Brains were dissected into hemispheres. One hemisphere was used for flow cytometry, and the other for immunohistochemistry was post-fixed in 10 % formalin. Immune cell isolation was conducted in the same manner as the rats, as explained previously.

Flow cytometry

Cell suspensions from rats and mice were washed with cold RPMI and PBS. Cells were stained with live/dead marker Ghost dye-Alexa Fluor 700 (Tonbo Biosciences) for 30 min at 4̊ C according to the manufacturer’s instructions, then washed with EAE media. Fc receptors were blocked using CD16/32 (Thermo Fisher) for 5 min at RT. Cells were stained with antibodies for 30 mins at 4 °C, followed by two washes and resuspension in EAE media. Antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. BD Symphony A4 was used to acquire cell populations. Analysis was conducted with FlowJo v10.8 software. Absolute counts for rats were determined by multiplying total cell numbers obtained from a Nexcelom Bioscience cellometer by the percentage of the population of interest and then normalizing to spleen weight (cells per gram). For mice, absolute counts were calculated by multiplying total cell counts from the cellometer by the percentage of the population of interest. Fluorescence-minus-one controls were used to identify cell populations, and each experiment conducted had single-stained and negative controls. Gating strategies for cell populations are shown in Supplementary Figs. 7–11.

scRNA-seq

We downloaded publicly available PBMC single-cell RNA-seq data from patients with type 2 diabetes, as listed in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE268210 (Gu et al., 2024), and a B cell dataset from healthy controls. B cells were subsetted from the diabetes dataset, and reference mapping was used to assign B cell subtypes based on transcriptional similarity to annotated subtypes in the healthy control dataset. Because the T2D dataset did not include its own matched control samples, we used an independent dataset of healthy individuals to enable case–control comparison. Individual-level demographic information (age and sex) was not available for the T2D cohort despite multiple requests to the corresponding author; therefore, we selected control data with aggregate age and sex distributions closely resembling those of the T2D cohort (average age 55.9 vs. 56 years; 67 % vs. 62 % male, respectively). Furthermore, raw counts from the healthy control dataset were not available. UMAP visualization clustered cells separately by dataset (healthy vs. diabetes), and we were not able to compare raw counts. To overcome this, the subsetted diabetes and healthy control datasets were integrated to allow comparison of equivalent B cell subtypes across groups. CXCR4 expression in B cell subtypes was visualized using violin plots, and pseudobulk count matrices were generated with the AggregateExpression function in Seurat. Differential gene expression analysis between B cell subtypes from diabetes and healthy samples was performed at the single-cell level with FindMarkers at the pseudobulk level with edgeR. Naïve-IFN B cells and CD5+ B cells were excluded from these analyses due to low counts in the diabetes dataset. All single-cell analyses were conducted using functions in the Seurat package in R (Butler et al., 2018). To determine the relative enrichment of B cell subtypes in diabetes versus healthy controls, we applied Mixed-Effects Association of Single Cells (MASC) (Fonseka et al., 2018). MASC was run with sample ID as a random effect and batch as a fixed effect, providing odds ratios and 95 % confidence intervals for enrichment in one condition versus the other. A cell type was considered enriched in diabetes if the log₂-transformed 95 % confidence interval of the odds ratio was entirely above 0 and reduced in diabetes if the interval was entirely below 0.

Statistical analysis

All individuals performing experiments were blinded to experimental conditions. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For studies comparing WT and HIP animals, a Student’s t-test was used. Power analyses were performed to determine the appropriate number of rats for injection experiments, and rats were randomly assigned to each group. Statistical analysis of differences between groups was performed in Prism 8.1.2. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Hypersecretion of pancreatic amylin alters the immune response in HIP rats

Previous RNA-sequencing studies of the brains of WT and HIP rats, the former expressing non-amyloidogenic rat amylin only, identified an enrichment of genes involved in inflammation, metabolism, and hypoxia (Verma et al., 2023). An analysis of this dataset using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) further revealed multiple pathways enriched for neuroinflammation, neurovascular coupling, T and B cell signaling, and neuronal signaling specifically in the context of human amylin overexpression (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, when DEGs were annotated based on gene ontology (GO) using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) software, biological pathways relating to immune response, hypoxia, and neuronal development were also enriched (Fig. 1B). As six of the top 10 canonical pathways identified were immune/inflammation-related, we investigated the impact of the hypersecretion of pancreatic human amylin on the immune system. To this end, we conducted flow cytometry on cells from the blood, spleen, and brains of WT and HIP rats to understand how human amylin impacted immune abundance (cell counts) and distribution (frequency). In the blood of HIP rats, we saw a decrease in the frequency of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and an increase in the frequency of neutrophils compared to WT counterparts (Fig. 1C). In the spleens of HIP rats, we found a significant decrease in the amount of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, B cells, neutrophils, and macrophages compared to WT controls (Fig. 1D) and an increase in the frequency of NK cells among CD45+ cells, while no differences were seen for the frequency of remaining cell types in the spleen (Supplementary Fig. 1B & 1D). In the brains of HIP rats, we also found a significant decrease in the amount of total CD45+ cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and neutrophils, with no significant changes seen in B cells and macrophages/microglia (Fig. 1E & Supplementary Fig. 1C). We also saw no differences in the frequency of immune cells in the brains of HIP rats compared to WT counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 1E). These results indicate that pancreatic human amylin activates canonical immune pathways in the brain, leading to sustained decreases in immune migration into the brain concomitant with peripheral downregulation of adaptive populations in the spleen and blood.

Hypersecretion of pancreatic amylin alters the frequency of CXCR4+ splenocytes

The CXCR4/CXCL12 axis plays a crucial role in immune cell migration and homing to peripheral organs and tissues in both healthy and diseased states, and has specifically been implicated in the pathogenesis of T2D (Liu and Dorovini-Zis, 2009; Gallagher et al., 2007). CXCL12, a chemoattractant for immune cells, has been linked to increased immune cell presence in the CNS, regardless of whether or not the cells express CXCR4 (Krumbholz et al., 2006). In patients with pre-diabetes and T2D, there are increased concentrations of plasma CXCL12 levels and decreased expression of CXCR4 on PBMCs (Derakhshan et al., 2012; Nathan et al., 2015; Egan et al., 2008), suggesting a defect in their ability to migrate into tissue. To understand how pancreatic human amylin impacts the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis, flow cytometry was used to determine the amount of CXCR4+ cells in the spleens of HIP rats, while IHC was used to determine the expression of CXCL12 in the brain. We found a significant increase in the frequency of CD4+ T cells expressing CXCR4 in HIP rats compared to WT controls (Fig. 2A) but not in any other cell type (Supplemental Fig. 2A). In the brains of HIP rats, we found no significant differences in the number of CXCL12-expressing cells, total neurons (NeuN+ cells), or numbers of microglial (Iba1+ cells). Furthermore, we found no differences in the percentage of CXCL12-expressing neurons or microglia (Fig. 2B&C). Consistent with these findings, quantification of CXCL12 by ELISA revealed no significant difference in CXCL12 concentration between WT and HIP rats (Fig. 2D). These results suggest that although pancreatic amylin increases CXCR4 expression in CD4+ T cells, the available data do not support a role for the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis in the defect of immune cell migration into HIP brains.

Hypersecretion of human amylin alters brain ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 expression

Cell adhesion molecules, or CAMs, are cell surface molecules that mediate the interaction between circulating immune cells and the vascular endothelium (Ren et al., 2011). Recent studies in T2D show that soluble levels of CAMs are altered (Qiu et al., 2019; Hegazy et al., 2020). Since we observed no differences in brain CXCL12 expression, we investigated whether CAM expression may be responsible for the decreased migration of immune cells into the brain. Western blot analysis of VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 shows that in the brains of HIP rats, there was a significant increase in the protein expression of VCAM-1, while ICAM-1 was significantly decreased compared to WT controls (Fig. 3A&B). Of note, ICAM-1 expression remained predominantly vascular in the parenchyma of HIP rats despite downregulation (Fig. 3C). These data suggest that the accumulation of pancreatic amylin in the brain and/or cerebral vasculature can alter adhesion molecules important for leukocyte migration into the brain.

Acute responses to aggregated human amylin do not alter brain immune populations

Previous studies showed that injection with human amylin into amylin knockout rats (AKO) injured endothelial cells and induced blood-brain barrier dysfunction (Ly et al., 2017), while studies in non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice suggest that treatment with human amylin induced Treg proliferation (Zhang et al., 2018). Considering these studies, we wanted to understand how acute amylin exposure would alter the peripheral immune response and immune cell migration into the brain of WT rats. We injected rats, via tail vein, with PBS or aggregated human amylin twice a day for 2 weeks, as previously published (Ly et al., 2017; Verma et al., 2023), then conducted flow cytometry. There was a significant increase in the frequency of circulating CD4+ T cells and a concomitant decrease in the frequency of CD8+ T cells compared to PBS-injected rats (Supplementary Fig. 3A). At the same time, there was no change in B cells, neutrophils, and macrophages. Acute amylin exposure also increased splenic CD4+ T cell numbers, with no differences in the remaining cell type total populations (Supplementary Fig. 3B) and decreased the frequency of splenic macrophages with no changes to other immune populations (Supplementary Fig. 4A). In contrast to upregulation of CD4+ T cells in amylin-injected rats, we found no significant difference in the migration of any immune cell subpopulation into the brain (Supplementary Fig. 3C), nor a change in the frequency of immune cells in the brains of amylin injected rats (Supplementary Fig. 4B), suggesting that sustained decreases in neuroimmune populations may be associated with chronic (at least greater than 2 weeks) exposure to human amylin, although further experiments would be required to validate this interpretation.

Impact of Acute aggregated human amylin on the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis

Since acute exposure to aggregated human amylin led to an increase in CD4+ T cells in the spleen, we hypothesized that this may be driven by the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis. To test this, we used splenocytes from WT rats injected with PBS or aggregated human amylin and conducted flow cytometry and IHC, as done in the WT and HIP rats, to determine the frequency of peripheral CXCR4-expressing cells and CXCL12-expressing cells in the brain. We found no significant differences in immune cells expressing CXCR4 in the spleens of amylin-injected rats (Supplemental Fig. 5A). We also did not find any significant differences in the number of CXCL12-expressing cells, neurons or microglia, nor changes in the percent of CXCL12-expressing brain cells of amylin injected rats (Supplemental Fig. 5B&C) further suggesting that human amylin exposure, either acute or chronic, does not involve the CXCL12 neuroinflammatory axis in parenchymal immune cell migration.

Hypersecretion of pancreatic human amylin leads to a decrease in B cells in the spleen and bone marrow of mice

Studies in injury models such as spinal cord injury (SCI) and warts, hypogammaglobulinemia, infections, and myelokathexis (WHIM) syndrome show that decreases in peripheral immune cells, as seen in our HIP rats, are usually associated with bone marrow dysfunction and defects in hematopoiesis (Carpenter et al., 2020; Zehentmeier et al., 2022). Given the paucity of tools to study bone marrow dysfunction or defects in hematopoiesis in rats, we employed a HIP mouse model to characterize the immune response secondary to pancreatic overexpression of human amylin (Fig. 4A). We confirmed splenic decreases in the total amount of all immune cell subsets, except neutrophils, in HIP mice similar to what we found in HIP rats (Fig. 4B). Interestingly, the frequency of HIP mouse splenic populations showed a decrease of CD4+ T cells and an increase in B cells (Fig. 4C), though splenic counts confirmed a loss of B cell populations. To investigate bone marrow dysfunction and/or defects in hematopoiesis, we also conducted flow cytometry on cells from the bone marrow of HIP mice. HIP mice exhibited a specific decrease in the total amount of B cells, with no changes to other cell types of either the innate or adaptive subpopulations (Fig. 4D). HIP mice also had reduced frequency of B cells in the bone marrow (Fig. 4E) compared to WT counterparts. This decrease in B cells was not reflected in the total number of B cells in the brain (Fig. 4F) but in the frequency of B cells in the brain, with no changes in other cell types (Fig. 4G). We also checked for general defects in hematopoiesi s by investigating hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell populations (HSPCs), but we found no differences in LSK or (c-kit+) LK populations (Supplemental Fig. 6). These data indicate potential bone marrow defects through B cell hematopoiesis due to pancreatic human amylin independent of HSPC loss in the bone marrow.

Hypersecretion of pancreatic amylin leads to defects in B cell development and alters CXCR4 expression on developing B cells

The marked decrease in the B cell populations seen in the spleen and bone marrow led us to hypothesize that these decreases resulted from defects in B cell development. B cell development occurs in the bone marrow and is initiated from the hematopoietic stem cells that differentiate into common lymphoid progenitors, then follow the order of pre-pro B cells, pro B cells, pre B cells, and immature B cells, then they further mature in the spleen (Nagasawa, 2006) (Fig. 5A). We confirmed that in the bone marrow of HIP mice, there was a significant decrease in the total number of pre-pro B cells, pro B cells, pre B cells, and immature B cells (Fig. 5B). In the frequency of developing B cells, we also found a significant decrease in the frequency of pre-pro B cells, pre B cells and an increase in immature B cells in HIP bone marrow (Fig. 5C). In the spleens of HIP mice, both the frequency and count of transitional B cells and follicular B cells significantly decreased without changes to marginal B cells (Fig. 5D), although the frequency increased for this population (Fig. 5E). As the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis is also essential in B cell lymphopoiesis (Nagasawa et al., 1996; Egawa et al., 2001), we tested whether the expression of CXCR4 on B cell progenitors, measured by geometric mean fluorescence, was altered in HIP mice. CXCR4 expression was increased on developing B cell populations in the bone marrow, including pro B, pre B, and immature B cells, with no changes in expression on pre-pro B cells (Fig. 5F). In the spleen, CXCR4 expression was also increased on marginal zone B cells, while follicular B cells and transitional B cells showed no change (Fig. 5G).

CXCR4 expression is increased on B cells from patients with Type 2 diabetes

Finally, given that CXCR4 expression was altered on B cells in HIP mice, we examined whether similar changes occur in patients with T2D. For healthy controls, there were 20,427 B cells from 52 individuals, and in patients with T2D, there were 14,742 from 38 individuals. Single-cell sequencing data from healthy controls and patients with T2D revealed an increase in plasma B cells and a decrease in transitional B cells (Fig. 6B), which are early B cells originating from the bone marrow. We also found that CXCR4 expression is higher on activated, naive, non-switched memory, switched memory, and transitional B cells (Fig. 6C). The upregulation of CXCR4 in diabetes was confirmed at the single-cell level and with pseudobulk analysis.

Discussion

T2D disrupts the immune response in humans and animal models (Delamaire et al., 1997; Geerlings and Hoepelman, 1999; McLaughlin et al., 2014; Nishimura et al., 2009), and is associated with neuroinflammation and cognitive decline. However, the connection between peripheral immune dysregulation and neuroinflammation during T2D-induced neurodegeneration is poorly understood. Furthermore, the role that human amylin plays during this process is unknown. We hypothesized that the hypersecretion of pancreatic human amylin leads to a dysregulated immune response in mice and rats that would increase immune cell migration into the CNS, contributing to neuroinflammation. Instead, we discovered that the hypersecretion of pancreatic human amylin limits immune cell migration into lymphoid tissue and the CNS. Furthermore, we found that hypersecretion of human amylin leads to defects in B cell lymphopoiesis, which is associated with altered CXCR4 expression.

RNA-seq analysis revealed that the accumulation of pancreatic human amylin induces genes responsible for neuronal signaling, neuroinflammation, and the immune response, the latter two important for protection and tissue repair in response to CNS injury (Zang et al., 2022). Chronic neuroinflammation is associated with neurodegeneration mediated by the activation of resident immune cells of the CNS and peripheral immune cells migrating into the CNS, further contributing to the development of neurodegenerative disorders and cognitive decline (Chen and Holtzman, 2022; Groh et al., 2021). Thus, specific pathways related to immune signaling, including antigen presentation, B cell development, and T cell signaling, provide additional evidence for the role of pancreatic human amylin in contributing to sustained neuroinflammation and subsequent pathological signaling in the brain.

This led us to characterize how human amylin impacts the immune response in both the brain and periphery. Studies have shown that the peripheral immune response is important in regulating microglia, neuroinflammation, and Aβ plaque clearance in neurodegenerative disorders (Chen and Holtzman, 2022). However, how this translates during the progression of T2D is particularly underexplored. Studies in diabetic mice infected with West Nile virus (WNV) reported decreased CD8+ T cells in the brain (Kumar et al., 2014), suggesting a defect in immune cell migration into the CNS due to T2D. We found that the hypersecretion of pancreatic human amylin in HIP rats led to decreased CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and neutrophils in the brain, which was also reflected in the periphery. This suggests that the hypersecretion of human amylin leads to general lymphopenia. In AD transgenic mice, the genetic deletion of immune cells led to increased neuroinflammation and plaque burden (Marsh et al., 2016), highlighting how the immune cells are critical to maintaining CNS homeostasis under neuroinflammatory conditions. Overall, these data suggest that the peripheral immune response is critical to the brain’s immune status and that the insufficient peripheral environment – secondary to chronic human amylin exposure – decreases immune cell surveillance in the brain, driving brain-localized neuroinflammation, cognitive decline, amylin plaque accumulation in the brain (Ly et al., 2017; Srodulski et al., 2014), and general parenchymal immune responses.

To investigate potential mechanisms underlying the reduced parenchymal immune infiltration, we examined the expression of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 in the brains of HIP rats. ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 are cell adhesion molecules that facilitate leukocyte transmigration across the blood-brain barrier (BBB) (Singh et al., 2023) and are induced by inflammatory cytokines, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and hyperglycemia (Singh et al., 2023). Studies in diabetic mouse models show an increase in VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 (Takechi et al., 2017; Rom et al., 2019; Alshammari et al., 2024), while patients with T2D have increased soluble levels of these molecules (Keaney Jr et al., 2004). Despite this, T2D has also been characterized by reduced leukocyte adherence and transendothelial migration (Hill et al., 1983), preventing immune cells from reaching their target tissue. However, contradicting studies showed that high-fat diet (HFD) could increase immune cells in the brain, ostensibly through upregulation of these same mechanisms (Buckman et al., 2014). In contrast, diabetic mice infected with WNV showed decreased ICAM-1 expression and fewer immune cells in the brain (Kumar et al., 2014). Studies have suggested that ICAM-1 is the main contributor to the diapedesis across the endothelium, while VCAM-1 is more important in the adhesion of T cells to the endothelium (Steiner et al., 2010; Abadier et al., 2015; Laschinger and Engelhardt, 2000). We observed increased VCAM-1 expression and decreased ICAM-1 expression. This suggests that during the chronic accumulation of pancreatic human amylin, immune cells may be able to adhere to the BBB, but due to the loss of ICAM-1, they cannot diapedese. However, no other studies have investigated the relationship between immune cell migration and CAM expression during T2D, highlighting the need to focus on how T2D affects immune cell migration into the CNS.

When we employed HIP mice to confirm the decreases in immune populations and expand the available tools to study the bone marrow niche, we found similar results to those seen in HIP rats, suggesting that the accumulation of pancreatic human amylin leads to splenic lymphopenia. This lymphopenia did not extend to the bone marrow and brain in mice though, as only B cells were decreased at these sites, but it did point to a potential defect of B cell lymphopoiesis due to human amylin in mice. B cell lymphopoiesis is a well-controlled process in the bone marrow, and studies have shown that the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis is essential for both B cell development and mobilization from the bone marrow (Nagasawa et al., 1996; Egawa et al., 2001). CXCR4 deletion in mice reduced early B cell progenitors and myelopoiesis (Nagasawa et al., 1996). Furthermore, CXCR4 deletion in HSCs also reduced B cell production, suggesting that CXCR4 is necessary for B cell development (Egawa et al., 2001). We confirmed that pancreatic hypersecretion of human amylin led to a defect in B cell development in the bone marrow and spleen characterized by a general decrease in most developmental B cell populations. These B cell populations had an increase in CXCR4 expression, which would tether them in the bone marrow environment. Further supporting this, single-cell sequencing data from patients with T2D similarly demonstrated a decrease in transitional B cells in patients with T2D and an upregulation of CXCR4 across multiple B cell subsets, indicating that altered CXCR4 expression may be a shared feature of T2D in both humans and animals. However, we are unable to associate these B cell subsets and CXCR4 expression with human amylin, which would further clarify the relationship between B cell dysfunction and amylin. Additional studies are necessary to determine the specific role CXCR4 plays in amylin-induced T2D.

To our knowledge, no studies have shown that T2D or amylogenic disorders impair B cell development. However, diabetes is associated with bone marrow dysfunction. Diabetic patients undergoing autologous stem cell transplantation exhibit impaired hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell mobilization, reduced CD34+ yields, and delayed hematopoietic recovery (Ferraro et al., 2011), supporting a broader role for diabetes in disrupting immune homeostasis. Furthermore, mice deficient in growth hormones, including prolactin, insulin growth factor 1 (IGF-1), thyrotropin, and thyroxine, can impair B lymphopoiesis (Park et al., 2021). As pancreatic human amylin is a hormone, it could be added to the list of hormones impairing lymphopoiesis. It is also important to note that CXLC12 has been reported to exhibit both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory properties. In diabetes, CXCL12 promotes pancreatic β-cell survival and protection from necrotic death (Liu and Habener, 2009; Liu et al., 2011; Grdovic et al., 2014). In contrast, mice on an HFD have increased plasma CXCL12, which is associated with macrophage accumulation and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Kim et al., 2014). These contrasting findings highlight that CXCL12 signaling can have context-dependent effects, which may also influence how pancreatic amylin dysregulates B cell development in our model. In the brain, the role of B cells is controversial. In AD mice, depletion of B cells leads to an increased plaque burden and memory deficits through IL-35 signaling, suggesting a protective role for B cells (Feng et al., 2023). By contrast, another study found that the loss of B cells reduced plaque burden, cognitive deficits, and neuroinflammation (Kim et al., 2021). Future studies repleting B cell populations in HIP models would need to be tested to determine their role in amylin plaque clearance, cognitive decline, and neuroinflammation. In the context of T2D, the current understanding is that B cells are major drivers of the pathogenesis of T2D, contributing to the inflammatory response and insulin resistance (Winer et al., 2011). This study adds an interesting contradiction, showing that discrete hypersecretion of pancreatic human amylin yields fewer B cells, though it is still associated with T2D.

Our study has several limitations. Although our animal model offers physiological relevance for studying T2D pathogenesis, amylin is co-secreted with insulin and leads to hyperglycemia, making it challenging to isolate amylin’s specific role. Future studies may need to compare HIP animals to those with only hyperglycemia and insulinemia to clarify amylin’s direct effects on lymphopoiesis. Additionally, we only used male rats because female HIP rats express lower levels of human amylin and develop hyperglycemia much later in life (Ly et al., 2017), creating a challenge for power analyses and age-matched effects for this study, especially as age clearly affects the immune response during disease (Lutshumba et al., 2021). However, since T2D severity increases after menopause (Kautzky-Willer et al., 2023), future research should investigate how female rats progress at later ages (>18 mos.) to understand amylin’s role in post-menopausal females. We also acknowledge that our study did not include CNS border compartments such as the meninges or skull bone marrow. Recent work has shown that these niches serve as immunologically active reservoirs containing B and T cells that communicate with the CNS parenchyma via specialized vascular channels, and can be altered under inflammatory conditions (Shi et al., 2022; Brioschi et al., 2021). These compartments are likely to be altered in our HIP mice and rats, contributing to broader neuroimmune dynamics and potentially influencing immune cell exchange between the periphery and CNS. Future studies should specifically investigate how T2D and the role of amylin affect these border regions. Finally, our single-cell RNA-sequencing analyses for individuals with T2D and healthy controls were derived from independent datasets, and demographic metadata were unavailable for the diabetic cohort. Although the control dataset was matched as closely as possible by age and sex, subtle cohort differences may remain, so these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Overall, our findings provide new insight into T2D, how human amylin alters the immune response in the brain and periphery, and how amylin impacts B cell development. While hyperglycemia and insulin resistance have traditionally been viewed as primary drivers of T2D pathology, this study provides evidence for a direct role of amylin in dysregulating immune homeostasis, contributing to neuroinflammation and associated pathologies. Furthermore, this study highlights the importance of holistically investigating the neuroimmune axis from development through recruitment into the parenchyma, as they are critical in contributing to the development of diabetic cognitive decline. This study also supports the notion that peripheral immunity is important in the CNS during neurodegeneration, and insufficient immunity contributes to neuroinflammation and cognitive decline. Future directions should focus on identifying therapeutic compounds such as AMD3100 (Broxmeyer et al., 2005), an FDA-approved CXCR4 inhibitor, to reverse lymphopenia and restore B cell populations. Investigating whether AMD3100 can reverse cognitive deficits, neuroinflammation, and promote amylin plaque clearance in HIP rats could provide new insights into treating T2D-related neurodegeneration.
