Speaking up or staying silent? Confidence, barriers and institutional readiness among Syrian medical and dental students: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Rawan Al-Deeb, Taim Al Kheder, Mohammad Baraa Abu Bakr, Shahd Almansour, Muhammad Obada Al-Sebai, Ram Attaf, Malek Al-Zouabi, Nour Alnser, Fahd Alshreibati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRaising concerns (speaking up or whistleblowing) is essential to detect and prevent unsafe practice and to uphold professional standards. While medical and dental students are frontline observers during clinical rotations, they face barriers to escalation. However, to guide policy and training, it is essential to gather local data on students' confidence and the readiness of institutions to support them in raising concerns. This study aimed to assess Syrian medical and dental students' institutional awareness and exposure to safety concerns, confidence in speaking up, perceived barriers to raising concerns, and institutional readiness to support reporting during clinical training.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was administered to a total of 812 medical and dental students in clinical training from all 13 universities offering undergraduate medical education in Syria between 15 October and 22 December 2025. A scenario-based Arabic questionnaire, translated and back-translated, was delivered via online and paper modes to maximize inclusion, with a pilot study of 40 students demonstrating excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.95). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, t-tests, one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc tests, and multivariate linear regression to identify predictors of confidence (SPSS v27). Sample size calculations indicated a minimum requirement of 385 participants, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsAmong participants, 40.9% reported encountering unsafe or unprofessional behavior, while 17.6% reported the presence of an institutional whistleblowing policy and 4.8% had received formal training, despite 82.3% expressing interest in such training. Overall confidence in raising concerns was moderate, with the highest confidence reported for patient safety issues and reporting to clinical supervisors. Confidence declined significantly with each advancing academic year (p < .001) and was independently associated with formal training in multivariable analysis (p = .001). The most frequently reported barriers were reluctance to “cause trouble” (42.6%), uncertainty about whom to contact (41.9%) and perceived lack of institutional support (39.0%); top enablers were assurances of reporter safety (74.1%) and ensuring changes will be implemented following reporting (59.9%).ConclusionA substantial proportion of students reported exposure to unsafe or unprofessional behavior, while reporting policies, training, and institutional support were limited. These findings suggest gaps in reporting preparedness and perceived safety that may influence students’ willingness to raise concerns. Further research is needed to evaluate strategies to improve reporting confidence and institutional support. In this context, educational institutions should incorporate practical, scenario-based training into their curricula. Future research should also assess the long-term effects of these measures on students, and extend the evaluation to include residents and practitioners.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09906-0.Keywords: Raising concerns, Speaking up, Whistleblowing, Medical students, Dental students, Patient safety, Syria

Introduction

Safeguarding patient safety and maintaining professional ethics require effective communication, a strong safety culture, teamwork, and mechanisms that enable healthcare professionals to report unsafe practice and professional misconduct. Among these, raising concerns (also referred to as speaking up or whistleblowing) is a fundamental mechanism referring to the process of reporting medical errors or misconduct [1, 2]. While patient safety is a major global challenge, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 1 in 10 patients is harmed during health care, with more than half of this harm considered preventable and a substantial burden occurring in low- and middle-income countries [3, 4, 5]. Raising concerns is a primary mechanism by which health systems detect, halt, and learn from unsafe practice and professional misconduct [6, 2]. While effective upward voice is a robust factor for improving patient safety and professional accountability, its absence contributes to preventable harm [2, 7, 8, 9]. Due to their inevitable involvement in patient safety incidents, healthcare students are important information sources and change agents during their observing and training roles as clinical rotators at hospitals [10, 11]. In this context, medical and dental students play a key role in the whistleblowing dynamic, acting as active, relatively knowledgeable observers of unsafe practice [12, 10]. A robust body of recent literature indicates that a substantial proportion of healthcare students encounter patient safety concerns during their clinical training, yet many remain silent. For instance, Carrillo et al. [6], in a cross-sectional study among 771 healthcare trainees from Colombia, Mexico, and Spain, found that 88.3% had experienced patient safety concerns in the previous month, yet more than a third had remained silent in a risky situation. The study also reported that being male was significantly associated with speaking up [6]Taken together, students understand, theoretically speaking, the necessity of reporting concerns but often lack practical knowledge required to navigate the often confusing or intimidating reporting pathways [13]. A robust literature shows that speaking up is significantly constrained by hierarchical team structures, fear of negative reactions or reprisal, and uncertainty about the reportability of observed problems [6, 14, 15].

Although important, training on raising concerns and institutional reporting is often limited or implicit in curricula, leaving students with only theoretical knowledge but little practical skill [16, 17]. This dilemma manifests itself as a curricular-practice gap, which is further complicated in conflict zones, such as Syria, where the healthcare system has been strained due to over a decade of hardships, which have also disrupted medical education and, in turn, decreased efforts toward building frameworks that support future doctors [18, 19]. Although a growing body of literature has examined raising concerns among healthcare students and professionals globally [6, 15, 2], evidence from the Middle East remains limited. Existing studies, such as those conducted in Jordan, have highlighted low confidence levels in raising concerns among medical students [12]. However, data on this issue are lacking in conflict-affected educational settings, particularly in Syria. Related Syrian research has documented workplace mistreatment and ethical strain (such as bullying) among trainees, but no prior published studies have investigated speaking-up behaviors or institutional readiness for whistleblowing among Syrian medical and dental students [20]. Therefore, and to the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to conduct an in-depth examination of this topic within the Syrian context.

The purpose of this research is to assess Syrian medical and dental students' institutional awareness and exposure to safety concerns, confidence in speaking up, perceived barriers to raising concerns, and institutional readiness to support reporting during clinical training.

Methods

Study design

This national multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted to assess Syrian medical and dental students' institutional awareness and exposure to safety concerns, confidence in speaking up, perceived barriers to raising concerns, and institutional readiness to support reporting during clinical training. Data were collected between October 15 and December 22, 2025, using a structured, self-administered questionnaire.

The purpose of the study was explained at the beginning of the questionnaire, and voluntary consent to participate was obtained. No names or personal identifying information were collected, ensuring anonymous data collection. Completing the questionnaire took approximately 3–5 min on average per participant.

Given the challenging context in the Syrian Arab Republic, including prolonged economic constraints and damage to infrastructure, reliable internet access was not consistently available for all students, particularly in some regions. To ensure broader participation and equitable access, a mixed-mode data collection approach was adopted. Data were collected both electronically and through paper-based questionnaires. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling strategy combined with voluntary response sampling. Specifically, paper-based questionnaires were distributed in person to all eligible students present at clinical training sites during the data collection period to ensure inclusion of students with limited or no internet access, while the electronic survey link was shared via university-affiliated student groups on social media platforms. To facilitate recruitment across participating universities, designated student collaborators from multiple institutions assisted with distributing the survey link through university-affiliated student social media groups and class communication channels. Paper questionnaires were distributed in person by data collectors during clinical training sessions. Because recruitment was conducted through university-wide student communication channels, commencement of clinical training was included as a screening question to ensure that only eligible participants were included in the final analysis. This was particularly relevant for fourth-year students, who are considered clinical-stage students but may not yet have commenced hospital-based clinical rotations at the time of recruitment.

The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants and adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting cross-sectional studies [21, 22].

Ethical approval for this multicenter study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Homs University (Approval ID: 1998). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study. Permission to conduct data collection at the participating institutions was subsequently obtained from the respective university authorities.

Participants and inclusion criteria

The study included all 13 Syrian universities offering undergraduate medical education. Participating institutions included University of Aleppo, Damascus University, Latakia University, Homs University, Hama University, University of Tartous, Idlib University, Al-Furat University, Syrian Private University, University of Kalamoon, Al-Andalus University for Medical Sciences, Al Sham Private University, and Al Hawash Private University. Although additional institutions in Syria offer undergraduate dentistry programs only, these were outside the study sampling frame. Undergraduate medical and dental curricula in Syria are governed by the Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research and share comparable core clinical training requirements; however, no standardized formal curriculum specifically addressing raising concerns or speaking up is implemented across institutions.

All participants were current undergraduate medical students in the fourth to sixth academic years and dental students in the fourth and fifth academic years, from public and private universities who had current or previous exposure to clinical training environments such as hospital rotations.

The exclusion criteria were pre-clinical students (medical years 1–3 and dental years 1–3), students outside the specified academic years, students from faculties other than medicine or dentistry, postgraduate students such as residents or master's students, and respondents who submitted incomplete questionnaires.

Measures

The questionnaire was derived from a previously published study that investigated reporting concerns in clinical learning environments [12]. The original English version of the questionnaire was translated into Arabic by bilingual researchers with medical backgrounds. To ensure linguistic accuracy and conceptual equivalence, the Arabic version was independently back-translated into English, resulting in a finalized Arabic version suitable for use among the target population.

The questionnaire consisted of four sections:

The first section collected demographic and academic information, including gender, place of residence (urban or rural), academic year, field of study, university, and university type (public or private), and whether the participant had commenced clinical training.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula for cross-sectional studies based on an infinite population assumption. At a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96) and a 5% margin of error, with a conservative expected prevalence of 50% (p = 0.5) to maximize the sample size, the minimum required sample was 385 participants.

While the exact total population of clinical-year medical and dental students across the participating Syrian universities is dynamic, national estimates place the target population at approximately 8,000 students. We opted for the infinite population assumption in Cochran's formula as a conservative approach to establish the highest statistically rigorous threshold (n = 385). Applying a finite population correction for a population of 8,000 would yield a slightly lower required sample size of approximately 375; thus, our final sample of 812 students far exceeds the requirements of both approaches, providing sufficient statistical power (> 90%) to detect small-to-medium effect sizes in subgroup analyses.

Statistical analysis

Data were cleaned, coded, and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 [23]. Continuous variables (confidence scores) were presented as means and standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Given the large sample size (N = 812), the Central Limit Theorem was applied to justify the use of parametric tests (independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA). Additionally, the assumption of normality for the overall confidence score was empirically verified; the distribution displayed excellent normality, with a Skewness of − 0.388 (SE = 0.086) and a Kurtosis of 0.316 (SE = 0.171). Homogeneity of variances was also assessed and confirmed using Levene’s test (p > 0.05) across comparison groups. Furthermore, the 25-item 5-point Likert scale was treated as a continuous variable because the analysis was performed on the mean composite scores, a practice supported by the scale's excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.92). To examine differences in confidence levels based on demographic variables (e.g., gender, major), independent samples t-tests were utilized. Differences across academic years were analyzed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Furthermore, a multivariate linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of confidence in raising concerns, adjusting for potential confounders. Results were reported as standardized beta coefficients (β) and p-values, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Pilot Study

Prior to the full-scale data collection, a pilot study was conducted involving 40 students (comprising 25 medical students and 15 dental students) randomly selected from the target population to evaluate the clarity, readability, and internal consistency of the questionnaire. Participants confirmed that the items were clear, unambiguous, and easy to understand. The internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) for the 25-item confidence scale in this pilot sample was excellent (α = 0.95). Based on these satisfactory results, no modifications were made to the instrument, and the pilot data were included in the final analysis to maximize statistical power.

Results

Section 1: sample characteristics

A total of 874 students accessed the online survey. Of these, 3 students declined to participate, and 57 were excluded because they had not yet commenced their clinical training. Consequently, the final analytical sample consisted of 812 undergraduate students. The gender distribution was nearly balanced, with 415 females (51.1%) and 397 males (48.9%). The majority of participants were enrolled in public universities (n = 748, 92.1%), with Aleppo University representing the largest proportion (43.2%). Predominantly, the students were studying human medicine (n = 618, 76.1%) and resided in urban areas (n = 620, 76.4%). Regarding academic progression, most respondents were in their fourth (n = 328, 40.4%) or fifth (n = 342, 42.1%) years of study (Table 1).

Section 2: institutional awareness and exposure to safety concerns

Regarding institutional frameworks, 143 (17.6%) participants confirmed the existence of a whistleblowing policy at their university, while half of the sample (n = 406, 50.0%) were unsure, and 236 (32.4%) reported that no such policy existed. Accessibility remains a challenge; 115 (14.2%) participants perceived the policy as easily accessible. Formal education on this topic was remarkably scarce, with 39 (4.8%) participants reporting that they had received formal training. However, there was a high interest in receiving formal training, as 645 (82.3%) of the respondents affirmed their interest in receiving training on raising concerns.

When asked about practical experience, a substantial proportion (n = 332, 40.9%) reported having previously encountered a situation that warranted raising concerns. In contrast, 319 (39.3%) and 161 (19.8%) of participants responded “no” and “not sure”, respectively (Table 1).

Section 3: students’ self-confidence in speaking up across scenarios

Overall, participants demonstrated moderate-to-high levels of self-confidence in raising concerns across various scenarios. The highest confidence levels were observed when reporting issues related to patient safety (e.g., poor infection control) and integrity (e.g., falsifying records), particularly when the recipient of the report was a Clinical Supervisor (M=3.91, SD = 1.03) or Clinic Manager (M=3.88, SD = 1.11).

In contrast, participants expressed the lowest confidence when raising concerns regarding inter-professional conflict (reporting to Nurses/Assistants: M = 2.92, SD = 1.07 M = 2.92, SD = 1.07) or mistreatment of students (reporting to Nurses/Assistants: M = 2.92, SD = 1.12 M = 2.92, SD = 1.12). A consistent pattern emerged regarding the recipient of the report; students were most confident reporting to high ranking figures, such as Clinical Supervisors (M = 3.91,SD = 1.03 M = 3.91,SD = 1.03 for patient safety) and Clinic Managers (M = 3.88,SD = 1.11 M = 3.88,SD = 1.11 for record integrity), and least confident reporting to Nurses/Assistants, where mean scores dropped below the neutral midpoint of 3.0 (e.g., M = 2.92,SD = 1.07 M = 2.92,SD = 1.07 for reporting team conflict, and M = 2.92,SD = 1.12 M = 2.92,SD = 1.12 for student mistreatment) (Table 2).

Discussion

For years, the definition of raising concerns (often referred to as ‘speaking up’ or whistleblowing) has been the subject of numerous publications. However, it remains unclear and differs from one context to another. In clinical settings, for instance, it can be defined as a core mechanism for detecting and preventing threats to patient safety and professional standards [2]. Medical students, especially in the clinical stage, occupy a pivotal position as observers of clinical practice during their rotations, and their willingness to report concerns increases as clinical exposure grows [10]. Although raising concerns is globally recognized as a critical action to improve care quality and prevent harm, organizational culture and response are main factors influencing whether individuals feel able to speak up [24, 25]. Unfortunately, in conflict-affected settings and owing to the limited resources and operational constraints in Syria, core patient safety systems (such as infection prevention and control) are often inadequately implemented [19]. This makes health staff struggle to provide quality care [19]. Furthermore, the prolonged conflict in Syria has disrupted medical education and weakened mentorship over the past decade [18]. All of these factors may help explain lower confidence and perceived support among students when voicing their concerns.

Throughout this national cross-sectional survey of 812 medical and dental students across all 13 Syrian universities offering undergraduate medical education, we found that students demonstrated moderate confidence in raising concerns despite limited awareness of institutional whistleblowing policies and minimal formal education on speaking up. Confidence varied according to academic year and gender, while fear of causing trouble, uncertainty about reporting pathways, and perceived lack of institutional support emerged as the principal barriers to raising concerns. Conversely, assurances of protection and meaningful institutional follow-up were identified as key facilitators of speaking up.

Institutional awareness and exposure to safety concerns

Our study revealed low confirmed awareness of formal whistleblowing policies (17.6%), and only 14.2% reported easy accessibility to these policies. Participants also reported extremely scarce formal education on raising concerns (4.8%), but they had very high interest in training (82.3%), and about 41% of our sample had encountered an incident warranting reporting. On a regional level, a Jordanian study underscored that nearly half of students were uncertain about the existence of institutional policies on raising concerns [12]. Although formal training was scarce in both countries, our study found a significantly lower proportion of trained students. In contrast, interest in receiving training on raising concerns was considerably higher among Syrian students (82.3% to 50.48%), which may reflect greater unmet need [12]. A recent scoping review found that, in medical education, whistleblowing training is still scarce, unevenly applied, and rarely assessed [17]. Studies also highlighted that student frequently express fear, uncertainty, and a lack of institutional support,thus, they tend to withhold their voices, even while aware of safety concerns [17, 10]. In accordance with our findings, Johnson et al. reported that undergraduate curricula in the UK rarely prepare students for real-world dilemmas about reporting unsafe care [26]. For instance, formal teaching may mention “raise concerns” abstractly without scenario-based training. This curricular-practice gap may leave students unprepared to act. Taken together, the evidence suggests that people often pursue informal channels (such as private peer-to-peer communication) before formal whistleblowing [27].

Self-confidence in speaking up

Given the fact that speaking up behaviors vary substantially by both the nature of the concern and the perceived status of the recipient, confidence in raising concerns may vary accordingly [13]. Building on this, confidence levels in our study were highest for patient safety and record integrity issues and when reporting to supervisors or clinic managers, and lowest for team conflict and student mistreatment, particularly when the recipient was a nurse. When it comes to patients' safety, it may be easier to recognize and defend the reporting of transparent and objective risks to patients (such as prevention of infection or record falsification). Lower confidence in reporting to nurses may be related to the lack of formal authority, role expectations, or cultural undervaluing of nursing voices. However, our student sample may not accurately reflect real-world dynamics. Unlike our finding, a qualitative study found that the hierarchical clinical practice environment was a barrier to raising patient safety concerns to superiors [28]. Thus, this discrepancy may highlight a gap between perceived confidence among students and experienced confidence in actual clinical practice scenarios. Therefore, future research could evaluate confidence among students who have previously raised concerns in real clinical settings, rather than relying solely on self-reported intentions or imagined responses.

Regarding mistreatment of students by staff, participants reported the lowest confidence in raising concerns compared with other contexts. Although mistreatment is common in medical education, it often goes unreported [29, 30]. Interpersonal conflicts and mistreatment are more ambiguous and often carry social costs. Furthermore, students may fear being labelled troublemakers, as they are at the bottom of the hierarchy [29]. Notably, the data analysis highlighted higher confidence in raising mistreatment concerns with fellows. This finding could be related to the greater safety students have in peer communications, especially when fear of punishment is absent.

The current study indicated that male students had slightly higher confidence in raising concerns than their female peers. This is consistent with a recent international study of healthcare students, which found that male students reported a higher frequency of speaking up than female students and that women perceived more barriers in terms of competence, skill, and self-confidence [6].

Additionally, the analysis observed a decline in confidence with academic seniority (5th year and 6th year). A prior study at a UK medical school reported similar findings [26]. According to a recent empirical study in Northern Taiwan, one plausible explanation for this decline is that prolonged clinical immersion and experience may reduce overt expressions of voice, as students adapt to workplace norms [31]. On the contrary, and according to the Jordanian study, seniority and more clinical expertise were associated with higher levels of confidence [12]. The inconsistency among studies indicates that the relationship between academic seniority and speaking-up confidence is intricate and not yet fully understood. Future research could, therefore, address this gap using longitudinal designs (such as prospective cohort) that follow the same cohort of students as they progress through clinical training. This approach may allow for a clearer understanding of how confidence changes over time and whether observed changes reflect adaptation, empowerment, or suppression of voice.

Barriers to raising concerns and institutional enablers for speaking up

Participants reported a cluster of interpersonal and structural barriers that discourage escalation of concerns, led by reluctance to 'cause trouble’ (42.6%), uncertainty about where to report or whom to talk to (41.9%), and perceived lack of institutional support (39.0%). According to the literature, fear of retribution, negative reactions, and unpredictable responses are dominant barriers [32, 14, 15]. This may suggest that students weigh social and academic consequences, which may lead them to choose silence or informal resolution over raising concerns. While social and academic safety are crucial in the speaking-up process, substantial uncertainty about the correct recipient may indicate poor policy visibility and a lack of clear reporting routes. Similarly, research also identifies frequent pathway ambiguity as a barrier [33]. Moreover, 36.5% of our sample considered difficulty proving the issue as a barrier. This may encourage students to seek peer verification and informal routes, rather than formal reporting directly.

On the other hand, protected reporting, confidentiality, anonymity, and assurance of non-retaliation are core recommendations in the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) and policy guides worldwide [34, 33]. The current study also highlighted similar findings regarding enablers for speaking up. The proportion prioritizing assurances of safety (74%), in our sample, was higher than other recommendations for improvement. This may suggest a clear demand for protective measures. In addition to the mentioned suggestions, nearly half of participants (43.2%) indicated that they would benefit from clearer guidance on what types of issues should be reported. Beyond procedural confusion, this uncertainty about what to report may reflect deeper gaps in training, ethical considerations, and policymaking related to teaching in Syria.

Strengths and limitations

The current study has a number of methodological strengths that should be mentioned. First, the inclusion of a large sample size of 812 students across multiple universities and faculties (medicine and dentistry) improves the generalizability of our findings and their academic representativeness. Second, the use of a structured questionnaire with detailed scenario-based items allowed measurement of confidence across multiple reporting situations that students may face during their clinical rotations. Third, the study looks at a topic that has never been studied within the Syrian context, where the health system is weak and war-affected, making the study more crucial and policy-relevant.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged and taken into consideration. First, the use of non-probability sampling methods (convenience and snowball sampling) introduces a risk of selection bias, as participants who chose to respond may differ systematically from those who did not. Accordingly, although participants were recruited from all 13 Syrian universities offering undergraduate medical education, the findings should be generalized with caution to other populations or educational settings. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal or temporal relationships between the identified factors and students' confidence or reporting behaviors. Third, reliance on self-reported attitudes brings in social desirability and recall biases that may falsely estimate actual reporting behavior in reality. Fourth, the study did not evaluate confidence levels among students who have previously raised concerns. This limitation may have led to a false optimistic representation of their confidence, as real-world situations differ from imagining scenarios and self-reporting based on them. Fifth, although participating institutions follow comparable national curricula, variations in local teaching practices and clinical training environments may have influenced students' exposure to raising concerns and patient safety concepts. Finally, direct comparison with local literature is also limited, as there are no previous studies on raising concerns in Syria, not only among students but also among residents and practitioners.

Conclusion

As the first paper to address raising concerns in the Syrian medical education context, this study examined medical and dental students’ confidence, barriers, and institutional factors related to raising concerns in their clinical years (during clinical rotations). This study is relevant to medical education, as understanding how future healthcare professionals perceive speaking up is important for both patient safety and professional integrity. Accordingly, the results indicate a potential need for clearer and more accessible reporting pathways, as well as explicit guidance on what constitutes a reportable concern. Moreover, the findings highlight potential gaps in students’ preparedness to handle situations requiring raising concerns during their clinical rotations. Future research could adopt longitudinal designs to better understand how confidence evolves over time, particularly among students with previous experience in raising concerns. Expanding this work to include residents and practicing clinicians may also provide a more comprehensive understanding of the issue and help inform context-appropriate strategies in Syria.
