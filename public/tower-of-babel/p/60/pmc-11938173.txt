Incidental Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor in a Patient Undergoing Robotic Sleeve Gastrectomy
Authors: Adham Ahmed, Tara Ranjbar, Ricky Y. Lovell, Samantha Colon, Branden Tejada, Claire Platt, Caitlin Russell, Indraneil Mukherjee, Olivia Haney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bariatric surgery is the most effective treatment option for patients with refractory morbid obesity to help facilitate weight-loss and reduce long-term metabolic morbidity and mortality. Over 200,000 procedures are performed annually in the United States with sleeve gastrectomy being the most common surgical approach. However, the growing popularity of surgical bariatric interventions has led to an increase in incidental findings of gastrointestinal neoplasms in intraoperative specimens. We report our experience performing a robotic sleeve gastrectomy in a 58-year-old woman who was later found to have a gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) with positive immunohistochemical marking.

INTRODUCTION

For patients with morbid obesity refractory to lifestyle and noninvasive interventions, bariatric surgery, such as Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy, is an effective and safe option to facilitate weight loss and prevent long-term morbidity. In the United States, it is estimated that over 200,000 bariatric procedures are performed annually, with sleeve gastrectomy being the most common approach, likely due to the lower risk of postoperative complications and reoperation.1 Notably, it is estimated that nearly 2% of bariatric procedures result in incidental discovery of gastrointestinal neoplasms.2 Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal neoplasm of the gastrointestinal tract and are theorized to arise from interstitial Cajal cells, which line the corpus and fundus of the stomach to facilitate peristaltic contractions.3 Accordingly, these tumors are most commonly identified within the stomach, with small intestine, esophageal and extragastrointestinal GISTs contributing to a minority of cases. Herein, we report our experience managing a patient with morbid obesity who was incidentally found to have GIST in her surgical specimen after robotic sleeve gastrectomy.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 58-year-old female with past medical history of Class III obesity (body mass index [BMI] = 49.8, on semaglutide 0.5 mg), hypertension (on losartan-hydrochlorothiazide 100–25 mg), gastroesophageal reflux disease (on esomeprazole 40 mg), obstructive sleep apnea (on CPAP), and asthma (on albuterol 90 mcg) was referred to our Comprehensive Weight Loss Center to discuss bariatric surgical options for weight loss. Prior surgical history was significant for open cholecystectomy, and tubal ligation. The risks and benefits of surgery were extensively reviewed with her, and the decision was made to undergo robotic sleeve gastrectomy. Preoperative evaluation was significant for Class II Revised Cardiac Risk Index with an estimated 6% risk for 30-day mortality, myocardial infarction, or cardiac arrest and 58.2 points on Duke Activity Status Index. There was no preoperative ischemic or congestive heart disease, cerebrovascular disease or diminished renal function. Preoperative endoscopy was remarkable for diffuse nonerosive gastritis, which was biopsied. No visible masses were appreciated.

Robotic sleeve gastrectomy was performed in standard fashion. The patient was taken into the operating room and placed in a supine position for general endotracheal anesthesia. Her abdomen was prepped and draped in usual sterile fashion and preoperative antibiotics were administered before making a surgical incision at Palmer’s point and entering using Veress needle. The abdomen was insufflated to 15 mm Hg and a 15 mm Trocar was used to enter the left and superior to the umbilicus before introducing laparoscope and ruling out injuries. Next, 3 additional robotic trocars were placed along the left side of the patient’s abdomen approximately 7 cm apart from each other. After administering bilateral transversus abdominis plane block, the patient was positioned in the reverse Trendelenburg and the da Vinci ξ robot (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) was docked.

The gastrocolic ligament was incised and the lesser sac was entered along the greater curvature of the stomach. The dissection was carried down the stomach, with meticulous care taken to avoid injury to the gastroepiploic arteries until being 5 cm proximal to the pylorus. The dissection was then carried up towards the spleen, carefully dividing the gastrocolic and gastrosplenic ligaments to achieve hemostasis and cauterizing the short gastric arteries. The stomach was successfully mobilized from the spleen and the left crus of the diaphragm was completely exposed. After confirming no hiatal hernia, a 40-French suction tube was used to size the sleeve gastrectomy. Sleeve gastrectomy was initiated using a 60-mm blue load on a Sureform robotic stapler, starting approximately 5 cm from the pylorus before switching to white loads for the remaining portion of the sleeve (Figure 1). After completing transection and removal of the fundus, upper endoscopy was initiated to perform leak test under water, confirming hemostatic staple line. Vistasteal was applied along the staple line and the stomach was removed through the 15- trocar site before closing the port using 0 Vicryl suture. All trocars were removed, and the abdomen was deflated before closing all incisions during interrupted 4-0 Monocryl suture. The patient was weaned off anesthesia an extubated without complications.

Gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST): low power microscopic view (50X) of a hematoxylin and eosin-stained slide shows tumor formed by spindle cell proliferation with sclerosis in between (yellow arrow).

Gross examination of the specimen showed normal appearing tan rugae with focal erythema and walls measuring up to 0.5 centimeters in thickness. Pathological examination of the fundal specimen came back positive for low-grade (G1) serosal GIST (Figures 1 and 2). The tumor’s greatest dimension was 0.5 centimeters and immunohistochemistry tested positive for CD117 and DOG1 confirming GIST diagnosis (Figure 3). Notably, the specimen was negative for S100 and SOX2. Additionally, there was noncontributory SMA immunological stain. All surgical margins came back negative for GIST.

DISCUSSION

In light of the growing global obesity epidemic, there has been a substantial growth in the utilization of surgical interventions for management of morbid obesity over the past 2 decades. In particular, sleeve gastrectomy has become the most commonly performed bariatric operation in the United States with several studies reporting sustained weight loss and reduction in associated comorbidities at long-term follow-up, including arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and obstructive sleep apnea.4 However, as bariatric case volumes increase, healthcare providers must be aware of the potential rise in incidental neoplastic findings in surgical specimens. Notably, it is estimated that nearly 2% of bariatric operations result in incidental discovery of gastric neoplasms.2 To date, there is limited data identifying high-risk patient populations for this clinical phenomena and much of the evidence operators have been limited to isolated small retrospective series.6 We add to the literature by reporting our experience managing a 58-year-old woman with an incidentally discovered GIST during routine robotic sleeve gastrectomy.

GISTs are the most prevalent mesenchymal tumor of the gastrointestinal tract affecting nearly 10–15 individuals per million.3 While localized tumors can be fully excised with good outcomes, advanced, malignant GIST historically carried a poor prognosis due to limited chemo-radiation treatment coverage. Recent advances however, including the discovery of associated c-KIT (CD117) and platelet-derived growth factor receptor a mutations has led to the development of targeted agents and improved prognosis with timely intervention.5 Given that GISTs may not become clinically apparent until compression symptoms develop in later stages, identifying these lesions during their early asymptomatic stage during routine health examinations is paramount to optimize patient surveillance, intervention and long-term outcomes.

Several studies have previously explored the incidence and implications of incidental GIST findings during routine bariatric procedures. Briefly, a single-center retrospective analysis of 1252 patients undergoing bariatric surgery by Walędziak et al. found localized low-risk stomach GIST in 16 patients (14 sleeve gastrectomy, 2 Roux-en-Y) of which 15 were excised with a margins of at least 5 mm. All tumors measured less than 20 mm with 15/16 (93.8%) demonstrating CD117 mutation on immunohistochemistry. A recent systematic review of 46 studies (24,005 patients) by Fernández and collages found that over 95% of incidentally found GIST in bariatric procedures were gastric in origin with a mean size of 10.3 mm and a mitotic index of <5 in 99% of the patients, correlating with a 100% successful resection rate in the cohorts. Interestingly, there was a much higher incidence of GIST in bariatric surgical patients compared to the general populations (0.65% vs 0.0006–0.0015%).6 The association between bariatric surgical candidates and GIST has several plausible mechanisms. Firstly, patients undergoing surgical intervention are more likely to undergo preoperative diagnostic work-up, such as upper endoscopy, direct intraoperative gross tissue evaluation, and postoperative histopathological specimen analysis facilitating an extensive “diagnostic bias” compared to the general population.6 Additionally, there is some evidence to suggest that morbid obesity may initiative a direct carcinogenic effect on gastrointestinal tissues.7

Unfortunately, despite the elevated risk of gastrointestinal malignancy in obese patients and a non-negligible prevalence of incidental neoplasm findings in routine bariatric surgery, there is currently no societal recommendation for routine bariatric surgical specimen screening, and the practice is largely institution dependent. For example, in the Waledziak series, only patients with suspicious macroscopic findings (81/1,252) during their bariatric operation had tissue specimens collected, which may have led to an underreporting of neoplasms. Conversely, other centers advocate for routine endoscopy and gastric specimen pathological analysis in all bariatric patients regardless of intraoperative findings. In our institutional practice, all bariatric patients undergo preoperative endoscopy. Additionally, all excised surgical specimens undergo histopathological analysis regardless of macroscopic findings intraoperatively. Notably, the GIST in this case was not picked up during workup due to its location in the serosal layer of the stomach, a previously documented limitation of preoperative endoscopy.8 However, this variability in practice underscores the need for future research into risk stratification for gastrointestinal malignancy in bariatric surgery patients and the development of cost-effective and evidence-based screening protocols for surgical specimens.

In conclusion, the contemporary surge in bariatric surgery has brought with it an increased incidence of incidental neoplastic findings in surgical specimens. Multidisciplinary weight-loss teams should recognize the association between obesity and gastrointestinal malignancy and be vigilant in their diagnostic work up, including use of endoscopy and immunohistochemical analysis for patients with risk factors or suspicious macroscopic findings.
