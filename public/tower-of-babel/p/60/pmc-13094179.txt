UBQLN1 Inhibition reduces MASH progression through downregulating SIKE/p38 MAPK pathway in hepatocyte
Authors: Yifei Chen, Fuji Yang, Guojun Zheng, Yanjin Wang, Likang Liu, Yongmin Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is increasingly recognized as a major global contributor to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, the key regulatory molecules governing lipid metabolism dysregulation, remains incompletely understood.MethodsClinical sample analyses, cellular models, animal models (HFHC and HFD/CCL4-induced MASH mice), and molecular biology techniques (transcriptomics, LC-MS/MS, etc.) were employed to elucidate the mechanistic of UBQLN1-mediated regulation of hepatocyte lipid accumulation in MASH and evaluates the therapeutic potential of UBQLN1-targeted interventions.ResultsThe results indicated that UBQLN1 was significantly upregulated in both patients with MASH and in MASH mouse models, demonstrating a positive correlation with hepatic lipid deposition. Genetic knockdown of UBQLN1 markedly reduced hepatic steatosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, and fibrosis progression in MASH mice. Mechanistically, UBQLN1 initiated the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) pathway via the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of the suppressor of IKKε (SIKE) to promote lipid accumulation in hepatocytes. Furthermore, red blood cell-derived extracellular vesicles loaded with UBQLN1 siRNA (RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1) effectively mitigated lipid accumulation in hepatocytes and improved the progression of MASH in vivo.ConclusionsThese findings establish the UBQLN1-SIKE-p38 MAPK axis as a critical regulatory pathway in MASH pathogenesis and develop an RBC-EVs-targeted delivery system for MASH therapy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04202-x.Keywords: UBQLN1, Extracellular vesicles, Red blood cell, MASH, Lipid accumulation

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), often referred to as the ‘silent killer’, has emerged as the most widespread chronic liver condition globally, subtly endangering the health of hundreds of millions [1]. Without appropriate prevention and treatment, the condition may advance to irreversible cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), ultimately necessitating liver transplantation [2]. A key feature of MASH is the abnormal buildup of lipids in hepatocytes [3]. This excessive accumulation of lipids disrupts the normal function of hepatocytes, thereby triggering inflammation and apoptosis, which are the primary driving factors in the progression of MASH. Notably, we still lack a comprehensive understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms of MASH, particularly regarding the key regulatory molecules involved in lipid metabolism disorders.

UBQLN1, which belongs to the family of ubiquitin-like proteins, is vital for maintaining protein quality control [4]. Studies have demonstrated that UBQLN1 mediates the proteasomal degradation of soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE)-connected proteins, resulting in impaired autophagosome-lysosome fusion and exacerbating septic liver injury [5]. Furthermore, UBQLN1 mediates sorafenib resistance by regulating mitochondrial biogenesis and reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis through its interaction with PGC-1β in HCC [6]. Nonetheless, the specific mechanisms by which it functions in MASH are still not well understood. Thus, investigating the role of UBQLN1 in MASH is not only essential for understanding the molecular basis of disease progression but may also offer new therapeutic targets.

Extracellular vesicles derived from red blood cells (RBC-EVs) are being recognized as promising carriers for drug delivery, attributed to their excellent biocompatibility, minimal immunogenic response, and natural affinity for certain organs like the liver and brain [7]. Their unique capacity for immune evasion and prolonged circulation [8] enables sustained release of RNA therapeutics [9]. Recent studies have demonstrated their remarkable potential in delivering therapeutic nucleic acids, such as small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), to modulate disease-related pathways [10, 11]. Notably, Zhang G et al. demonstrated that RBC-EVs preferentially accumulate in the liver, where the hepatic microenvironment recruits RBC-EVs in a C1q-dependent manner [12]. Additionally, the liver is generally considered the primary site of accumulation and elimination of nanoparticles (NPs) and RBC-EVs is one type of NPs, thereby positioning them as optimal carriers for hepatic siRNA delivery [13]. From a clinical translational perspective, utilizing RBC-EVs for the liver-targeted delivery of UBQLN1 siRNA to treat MASH remains an undeveloped therapeutic strategy.

This research seeks to explore the molecular mechanisms by which UBQLN1 regulates lipid accumulation in hepatocytes during the progression of MASH, as well as to assess the effectiveness of RBC-EVs carrying UBQLN1 siRNA in inhibiting lipid deposition and the advancement of MASH. Specifically, we focus on how UBQLN1 activates the p38 MAPK signaling pathway through the ubiquitination and degradation of the SIKE protein, thereby promoting lipid accumulation in hepatocytes. Additionally, we evaluate the feasibility of targeting UBQLN1 for the treatment of MASH, such as through the delivery of siRNA via RBC-EVs. This study greatly enriches our comprehension of the molecular mechanisms driving disease progression and pinpoints potential therapeutic targets within MASH for the formulation of novel treatment strategies.

Results

Increased expression of UBQLN1 protein in lipotoxic hepatocytes is associated with the progression of MASH.

Lipid accumulation is a critical factor in the metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) progression. However, the regulatory molecules involved in lipid metabolism and their underlying mechanisms remain largely unclear. Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset GSE130970 revealed that the transcriptional level of UBQLN1 was significantly upregulated in the liver samples of MASH patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 1a). Notably, UBQLN1 expression was correlated with higher non-alcoholic fatty liver disease activity scores (NAS), ballooning degeneration, and inflammation scores in these patients (Fig. 1b-d). ELISA assays conducted on serum samples from healthy individuals and MASH patients demonstrated elevated UBQLN1 levels in the serum of MASH patients (Fig. 1e). Furthermore, serum UBQLN1 levels exhibited a strong positive correlation with liver function markers (such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and triglyceride (TG) levels (Fig. 1f-h). Immunohistochemistry and Western blot confirmed an increased expression of UBQLN1 protein in the livers of MASH patients, as well as in the livers of mice fed a high-fat high-cholesterol (HFHC) diet and those subjected to a high-fat diet (HFD) combined with CCL4 induction, particularly in steatotic hepatocytes (Fig. 1i, j; Fig. S1a, b). Additionally, compared to normal hepatocytes, UBQLN1 protein levels were elevated in oleic acid and palmitic acid (PAOA) treated mouse primary hepatocytes (MPH) and HepG2 cells (Fig. 1k; Fig. S1c-e). Subsequent Nile red staining indicated that UBQLN1 knockdown inhibited lipid accumulation in PAOA-treated HepG2 cells, whereas UBQLN1 overexpression promoted lipid accumulation in HepG2 cells (Fig. 1l, m). These findings suggest that UBQLN1 is upregulated in MASH and significant in regulating hepatic lipid accumulation.

UBQLN1 knockdown ameliorates lipid deposition and liver injury in diet-induced MASH mice.

To enhance our understanding of the function of UBQLN1 in hepatic lipid deposition in MASH mice, we administered a single dose of AAV8-U6-shUBQLN1 adenovirus via tail vein injection, followed by either HFHC or HFD feeding, or HFD combined with CCL4-induced liver injury, to observe the influence of UBQLN1 knockdown on hepatic lipid accumulation and disease progression. Both quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blot detection demonstrated that AAV8-U6-shUBQLN1 significantly reduced the mRNA and protein expression levels of UBQLN1 in the livers of HFHC-fed, HFD/CCL4-treated, and HFD-fed model mice compared to the control adenovirus-injected groups (Figs. 2a and b and 3a and b; Fig. S2a, b). In comparison to normal chow diet (NCD)-fed mice, HFHC-fed, HFD/CCL4-treated, and HFD-fed mice exhibited increased body weight, liver weight, and serum levels of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), ALT, and AST, alongside a reduced liver index (Figs. 2c-i and 3c-i; Fig. S2c-i). Conversely, hepatic UBQLN1 knockdown mitigated these effects, leading to decreased body weight and liver weight of mice, and decreased serum levels of TG, TC, ALT, and AST, while restoring the liver index (Figs. 2c-i and 3c-i; Fig. S2c-i). Glucose and insulin tolerance tests demonstrated that UBQLN1 knockdown improved insulin resistance in both HFHC-fed, HFD/CCL4-treated, and HFD-fed model mice (Figs. 2j-l and 3j-l; Fig. S2j-l). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), along with Oil Red O staining revealed that UBQLN1 knockdown led to a decrease in vacuolar degeneration and lipid accumulation in the livers of mice subjected to a HFHC diet, or treated with HFD/CCL4, or solely fed a high-fat diet (Figs. 2m and 3m; Fig. S2m). Furthermore, F4/80 staining indicated a decrease in inflammatory cell infiltration, and Sirius Red staining, Masson staining, and immunohistochemistry of α-SMA showed a decrease fibrosis in UBQLN1 knockdown mice (Figs. 2m and 3m; Fig. S2m). Additionally, qRT-PCR analysis indicated that UBQLN1 knockdown upregulated fatty acid oxidation-related genes (CPT-1 A, PPARα) while downregulating lipogenic genes (SCD1, FASn, Srebp1). This intervention also led to a reduction in the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and fibrogenic markers (COL1A1, COL3A1) (Figs. 2n and o and 3n and o; Fig. S2n, o). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that UBQLN1 knockdown alleviates hepatic lipid accumulation and liver injury in MASH mice.

UBQLN1 induces hepatocyte lipid deposition through activating the p38 MAPK pathway.

To elucidate the mechanism by which UBQLN1 regulates hepatic lipid deposition, we conducted transcriptome sequencing analysis on PAOA-treated HepG2 cells with UBQLN1 knockdown (HepG2-shUBQLN1) and control cells (HepG2-shNC). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that UBQLN1 knockdown enriched the lipid metabolism and MAPK signaling pathways in PAOA-injured HepG2 cells (Fig. 4a). The p38 MAPK, ERK1/2, and JNK are three principal subtypes of the MAPK signaling pathway, which play a crucial role in hepatic lipid metabolism during the development of MASH [14]. Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses demonstrated that UBQLN1 knockdown reduced p-p38 protein levels and the transcriptional expression of lipogenic genes while increasing the expression of fatty acid oxidation-related genes in PAOA-treated HepG2 cells (Fig. 4b, c). Conversely, UBQLN1 overexpression in normal HepG2 cells produced opposite effects (Fig. 4d, e). Notably, neither UBQLN1 overexpression nor knockdown altered the levels of p-ERK or p-JNK proteins in either normal or PAOA-treated hepatocytes (Fig. 4b, d). Transcriptome sequencing of liver tissues from HFHC-fed mice injected with control adenovirus or AAV8-shUBQLN1 similarly showed enrichment of lipid metabolism and MAPK signaling pathways upon UBQLN1 knockdown (Fig. 4f). Western blot confirmed that UBQLN1 suppression decreased the expression of p-p38 protein in both HFHC-fed and HFD/CCL4-induced mice liver (Fig. 4g, h).

Previous studies have reported that p38 MAPK phosphorylation exacerbates hepatic lipid deposition [15]. To investigate this mechanism, we treated hepatocytes with dehydrocorydaline (Deh), a p38 MAPK phosphorylation activator, and adezmapimod (Ade), a p38 MAPK phosphorylation inhibitor. Subsequently, we analyzed the samples using Western blot, qRT-PCR, and Nile red staining. Deh treatment resulted in increased p-p38 protein expression in normal HepG2 cells, suppression of fatty acid oxidation-related genes, and enhancement of lipogenic gene expression, thereby promoting lipid accumulation (Fig. S3a, b, e). Conversely, Ade treatment reduced p-p38 levels in PAOA-injured HepG2 cells, increased the expression of genes related to fatty acid oxidation, and lowered the expression of lipogenic genes, thereby ultimately reducing lipid accumulation (Fig. S3c, d, f). Notably, treatment with Deh counteracted the inhibitory effects of UBQLN1 knockdown on the expression of p-p38 protein and the accumulation of lipids in HepG2 cells treated with PAOA (Fig. S3g, h). In contrast, Ade treatment diminished the enhancing effects of UBQLN1 overexpression on p-p38 activation and lipid accumulation in healthy HepG2 cells (Fig. S3i, j). Collectively, these findings indicate that UBQLN1 promotes hepatic lipid deposition by increasing the phosphorylation of p38.

UBQLN1 induces hepatocyte lipid deposition by regulating activation of the p38 MAPK pathway through ubiquitination of SIKE.

To identify potential interacting proteins with UBQLN1, we conducted mass spectrometry screening and identified SIKE, CYLD, and SAM50 as candidates that could interact with UBQLN1 (Fig. 5a). Notably, UBQLN1 knockdown resulted in an increase in SIKE protein expression without affecting its mRNA levels in PAOA-treated HepG2 cells. Conversely, UBQLN1 overexpression led to a decrease in SIKE protein expression without altering its transcription in normal HepG2 cells (Fig. 5b-e). However, neither UBQLN1 overexpression nor knockdown influenced the protein expression of CYLD or SAM50 in either normal or PAOA-treated hepatocytes (Fig. 5b, c). Both endogenous and exogenous co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments demonstrated a physical interaction between UBQLN1 and SIKE (Fig. 5f, g). As a part of the ubiquitin-like protein family, UBQLN1 is vital for the intracellular quality control of proteins through the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS). The findings indicated that the overexpression of UBQLN1 led to reduced SIKE protein levels in normal HepG2 cells. Furthermore, this inhibitory effect was significantly counteracted by the proteasome inhibitor MG132, but not by the lysosome inhibitor CQ (Fig. 5h). Additionally, cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays and ubiquitination experiments demonstrated that UBQLN1 overexpression accelerated SIKE protein degradation and increased its ubiquitination levels in normal HepG2 cells (Fig. 5i, j). This suggests that UBQLN1 likely regulates SIKE expression through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. To investigate the mechanism by which UBQLN1 regulates the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of SIKE, we performed mutations in the region of UBQLN1 responsible for the ubiquitin-proteasome system [16]. The results indicated that, compared to the UBQLN1 WT, the UBQLN1 E54D mutant did not reverse the decrease in SIKE caused by UBQLN1 (Fig. S4a). Considering that UBQLN1 typically functions as a chaperone rather than a direct E3 ligase, we screened for potential E3 ligases that interact with UBQLN1. Results from IP-MS and Co-IP experiments indicated an interaction between UBQLN1 and UBE3A, as well as between UBE3A and SIKE (Fig. S4b, c). The knockdown of UBE3A resulted in an increase in SIKE expression, whereas the overexpression of UBE3A led to a decrease in SIKE levels (Fig. S4d, e). CHX experiments demonstrated that UBE3A overexpression accelerates the degradation of the SIKE protein in HepG2 cells (Fig. S4f). Furthermore, Co-IP experiments revealed that the overexpression of UBQLN1 recruits UBE3A protein to SIKE, and the recruited UBE3A enhances the ubiquitination level of SIKE (Fig. S4g, h). Conversely, the knockdown of UBE3A produced the opposite effect (Fig. S4i). These results suggest that UBQLN1 functions as a molecular chaperone, recruiting UBE3A and thereby enhancing the ubiquitination level of SIKE, which ultimately leads to its degradation (Fig. S4j).

SIKE is a negative regulator of p38 MAPK phosphorylation that alleviates liver injury in MASH mice by inhibiting p38 MAPK activation [17]. Compared to NCD-fed mice, HFHC-fed and HFD/CCL4-induced model mice showed decreased SIKE expression, while UBQLN1 knockdown increased SIKE levels in liver tissues (Fig. 6a, b). To clarify the significant function of SIKE in the activation of p38 MAPK mediated by UBQLN1 and in hepatic lipid accumulation, we conducted functional studies. The results indicated that reducing SIKE levels in standard HepG2 cells caused an elevation in p-p38 protein levels and facilitated lipid buildup (Fig. 6c; Fig. S5a). Conversely, SIKE overexpression in PAOA-treated HepG2 cells resulted in a reduction of p-p38 expression and suppressed lipid deposition (Fig. 6d; Fig. S5b). Furthermore, the treatment with Deh reversed the inhibitory effect of SIKE overexpression on p38 MAPK activation and lipid deposition in PAOA-treated HepG2 cells (Fig. S5c, d). Notably, SIKE knockdown counteracted the inhibitory effects of UBQLN1 deficiency on p-p38 expression and lipid accumulation in PAOA-injured HepG2 cells (Fig. 6e, f). In contrast, the overexpression of SIKE reduced the ability of UBQLN1 to promote p-p38 activation and lipid accumulation in normal HepG2 cells (Fig. 6g, h). These findings demonstrate that UBQLN1 mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of SIKE, thereby enhancing p38 phosphorylation and ultimately facilitating hepatic lipid deposition.

RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 alleviates steatosis in hepatocytes and facilitates recovery of liver injury in diet-induced MASH mice.

Extracellular vesicles derived from red blood cells (RBC-EVs) were isolated from healthy donors’ type O blood via RBC extrusion. The purified RBC-EVs expressed characteristic markers including ALIX, CD47, TSG101 and RBC-specific hemoglobin A (HBA), featuring an average size of around 100 nm (Fig. 7a-c). The RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 were prepared by electroporation-mediated siRNA loading. When PAOA-injured mouse primary hepatocytes (MPH) were treated with Dir-modified RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 containing FAM-tagged siRNA, both the Dir-modified EVs and FAM-siRNA were successfully internalized by PAOA-injured MPH cells (Fig. 7d). Notably, compared to control siRNA-loaded EVs (RBC-EVs@siCtr), RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 treatment significantly reduced UBQLN1 and p-p38 expression while increasing SIKE protein levels in PAOA-injured MPH cells, accompanied by attenuated lipid accumulation (Fig. 7e-g). Furthermore, RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 administration markedly decreased the number of TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells in PAOA-MPH cultures (Fig. 7h).

Subsequently, we explored the therapeutic efficacy of RBC-EVs loaded with UBQLN1 siRNA (RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1) in mitigating liver injury in HFHC-fed and HFD/CCL4-induced mouse models (Fig. 8a; Fig. S6a). The results of in vivo imaging demonstrated that Dir-modified RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 and RBC-EVs@siCtr predominantly accumulated in the liver after 12 h post-injection (Fig. 8b; Fig. S6b). Analysis of liver tissue revealed the co-localization of FAM-labeled siUBQLN1 and Dir-labeled RBC-EVs within hepatocytes (Fig. 8c; Fig. S6c). Compared to the PBS and RBC-EVs@siCtr groups, the RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1-treated group significantly reduced the expression of UBQLN1 and p-p38, along with an increase in SIKE expression in liver tissues (Fig. 8d, e; Fig. S6d, e). Furthermore, treatment with RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 effectively decreased liver weight in both HFHC-fed and HFD/CCL4 model mice, significantly reduced TG, TC, ALT, and AST levels, and increased liver index (Fig. 8f-k; Fig. S6f-k). We further investigated the effects of RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 treatment on hepatic lipid deposition, collagen accumulation, and inflammation in both HFHC-fed mice and HFD/CCL4-induced model mice. The treatment with RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 led to a marked reduction in hepatocyte vacuolar degeneration and lipid accumulation when compared to the PBS and RBC-EVs@siCtr groups, while also lessening collagen accumulation and the infiltration of inflammatory cells in the liver tissues of both experimental mouse models (Fig. 8l and Fig. S6l). Moreover, the treatment with RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 enhanced the genes expression associated with fatty acid oxidation while simultaneously decreasing the expression of genes related to fatty acid synthesis in the livers of mice that were fed a high-fat high-cholesterol diet and treated with HFD/CCL4. Concurrently, there was a reduction in the expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and collagen-associated genes (Fig. 8m, n; Fig. S6m, n). Additionally, histological analysis using H&E staining showed no major pathological changes in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys of the mice receiving RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1, suggesting good biosafety (Fig. S7a, b). In summary, these results indicate that the targeted delivery of RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 significantly decreases UBQLN1 protein expression in MASH mice liver, diminishes the activation of the UBQLN1/SIKE/p-p38 pathway, and facilitates the repair of liver injury.

Discussion

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) represents a significant risk factor for the progression to end-stage liver disease, which includes conditions such as cirrhosis and primary liver cancer. Nonetheless, the underlying mechanisms of MASH, particularly the key regulatory molecules involved in lipid metabolism disorders remains unclear. Current research indicates that UBQLN1 is abnormally expressed in various metabolic diseases. However, its role in MASH has not been systematically investigated, and the molecular pathways through which UBQLN1 regulates hepatic lipid deposition remain undefined. In this research, we attempt to elucidate the mechanism by which the ubiquitin-like protein UBQLN1 regulates lipid accumulation in hepatocytes during the progression of MASH. Our results indicate that UBQLN1 expression is significantly upregulated in both MASH patients and animal models, demonstrating a positive correlation with hepatic lipid deposition. Furthermore, UBQLN1 activates the p38 MAPK signaling pathway by mediating the UBE3A-mediated ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of SIKE. Additionally, treatment with RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 effectively ameliorates liver injury in mouse models. This research presents the thorough explanation of the UBQLN1-SIKE-p38 MAPK regulatory axis and develops an innovative red blood cells derived from extracellular vesicles (RBC-EVs)-based targeted delivery system, offering new perspectives for clinical translation. The results enhance our comprehension of the mechanisms underlying the progression of MASH and highlight UBQLN1 as a potential therapeutic target, setting the stage for the formulation of new treatment approaches for MASH.

The components of the MAPK signaling pathway, namely JNK, ERK1/2, and p38 MAPK, play a crucial role in the development of fatty liver disease. Typically, the regulation of MAPK signaling is mediated by the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), which governs the stability and abundance of MAPK pathway components [18]. However, the specific regulatory role of UBQLN1 in MAPK signaling remains to be elucidated. Our transcriptome analysis indicated a significant enrichment of the MAPK pathway following the knockdown of UBQLN1. Functional studies demonstrated that overexpression of UBQLN1 enhanced, while its knockdown suppressed, p38 MAPK phosphorylation in hepatocytes, without affecting the phosphorylation levels of ERK1/2 or JNK. This finding contrasts with previous observations in colorectal cancer (CRC), where UBQLN1 knockdown inhibits CRC progression by decreasing ERK1/2 phosphorylation [19]. Furthermore, pharmacological modulation through the use of a p38 MAPK phosphorylation activator or inhibitor reversed the effects of UBQLN1 knockdown or overexpression on p38 MAPK pathway activation and lipid deposition. These results suggest that the regulation of the MAPK signaling pathway by UBQLN1 may differ in various diseases, with p38 MAPK activation playing a central role in UBQLN1-mediated hepatic lipid accumulation.

UBQLN1 mediates the recognition, transport, and degradation of misfolded or damaged proteins through its ubiquitin-associated (UBA) and ubiquitin-like (UBL) domains by interacting with ubiquitinated proteins and the proteasome, thereby maintaining intracellular protein homeostasis [20]. Co-IP and mass spectrometry analysis have revealed an interaction between SIKE and UBQLN1. Overexpression of UBQLN1 suppressed SIKE protein expression, while knockdown of UBQLN1 enhanced SIKE levels in hepatocytes without affecting SIKE transcription. As a negative regulator of p38 MAPK phosphorylation, SIKE alleviates liver injury in MASH mice by inhibiting p38 MAPK activation [17]. Consistently, our findings indicate that SIKE overexpression inhibits but SIKE knockdown promotes p38 MAPK phosphorylation and hepatic lipid accumulation. However, the mechanisms by which UBQLN1 regulates SIKE expression and whether SIKE mediates UBQLN1-induced p38 MAPK activation to promote hepatic steatosis remain unclear. Our study demonstrates that UBQLN1 overexpression enhances SIKE ubiquitination and subsequent protein degradation by recruiting UBE3A. Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 blocked SIKE degradation, whereas the lysosome inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) did not exhibit such an effect. These findings highlight the diverse roles of UBQLN1 in regulating protein homeostasis. Specifically, UBQLN1 promotes hepatic lipid deposition by degrading SIKE via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, thereby activating the p38 MAPK signaling cascade.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) acts as an efficient, rapid, and economical instrument for RNA interference (RNAi), widely employed in the development of novel drug targets [21]. However, siRNA encounters significant challenges as a gene-silencing agent, including low delivery efficiency, poor in vivo stability, immunogenicity, and off-target effects [22]. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) serve as an effective delivery system for siRNA due to their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and scalability for mass production. Surface modifications further enhance targeted delivery and efficiency [23]. It is important to acknowledge that high doses of LNPs, particularly those incorporating cationic lipids, carry hepatotoxicity risks. While multivalent cationic lipids outperform their monovalent counterparts in delivery efficiency, they induce greater cytotoxicity [24]. Conversely, EVs have surfaced as a compelling RNAi delivery platform due to the inherent RNA-loading capacity, protective function, and cell-specific targeting [25]. Despite their superior biocompatibility and natural tropism, issues such as batch-to-batch variability, manufacturing scalability, and variable targeting precision impede clinical advancement [26].

Extracellular vesicles derived from red blood cells (RBC-EVs) offer a promising solution as an effective and non-immunogenic delivery system [27]. Red blood cells are the most abundant cells in the human body, making it relatively easy to prepare clinically relevant extracellular vesicles. Group O-RBCs can be used as universal donors for large-scale EVs production since they are readily available in blood banks and they are devoid of DNA [9]. Allogeneic transfusion is associated with high safety, and extracellular vesicles derived from red blood cells are natural components of red blood cells, thereby overcoming the limitations of autologous sources and exhibiting high biocompatibility [12]. Based on the mature experience in transfusion medicine, the standardized application of autologous or allogenic RBC-EVs can effectively address the clinical translation bottleneck of red blood cell vesicle therapeutic products [28]. Notably, the use of RBC-EVs for the delivery of safe, non-carcinogenic siRNA therapeutics has shown notable protective and therapeutic effects in conditions like acute kidney injury and cancer cachexia [10, 11]. In this investigation, we utilized RBC-EVs as carriers to deliver siUBQLN1 and evaluated its therapeutic efficacy against MASH both in vitro and in vivo. Our findings demonstrate that RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 effectively suppresses UBQLN1 transcription and protein expression in hepatocytes, inhibits the activation of the UBQLN1/SIKE/p38 MAPK pathway, and ameliorates hepatic lipid deposition, inflammation, and fibrosis.

However, this study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. The clinical samples were collected from only two medical centers, leading to a relatively small sample size. Second, although RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 demonstrated promising therapeutic effects in MASH mouse models, its long-term safety profile and treatment efficacy require further validation through extended preclinical studies. Besides, while our research mainly concentrated on hepatocytes, the potential roles of UBQLN1 in other cell types of liver, including Kupffer cells and hepatic stellate cells, remain unexplored. Investigating these aspects could provide more comprehensive insights into the pathogenesis of MASH.

Conclusions

This research highlights the significant role of UBQLN1 in hepatic lipid deposition during the progression of MASH, demonstrating its molecular mechanism of activating the p38 MAPK pathway through ubiquitination-mediated degradation of SIKE. Furthermore, we developed a targeted RNAi therapeutic strategy based on RBC-EVs against UBQLN1, which effectively ameliorated lipid accumulation, inflammation, and fibrosis in mouse models of MASH. These findings enhance our mechanistic understanding of MASH pathogenesis and lay a theoretical groundwork for creating precision therapies aimed at the UBQLN1 pathway.

Experimental methods

Clinical specimens

Serum samples and liver specimens were obtained from both control individuals and patients diagnosed with MASH at the Changzhou Third People’s Hospital. The research was carried out in adherence to the ethical guidelines established by the Declaration of Helsinki and obtained approval from Jiangsu University (Reference No. 2012258). This study strictly adhered to ethical guidelines, and all sample collections were conducted following the acquisition of written informed consent from participants.

Cell culture

The HepG2 cell line (Fenghui Biological, China, CL0137) was maintained in DMEM high-glucose medium (SenBeiJia, Nanjing, China, BC-M-005) supplemented with 100 U Ml− 1 penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, C0222) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, SenBeiJia, Nanjing, China, BC-SE-FBS01). Male C57BL/6 mice, aged between 6 and 8 weeks, had their livers perfused with Collagenase Type IV (Gibco, USA, 17-104-019) to aid in the separation of parenchymal from non-parenchymal cells. Hepatocytes were obtained through a Percoll gradient and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA, 11320033). Both HepG2 cells and murine primary hepatocytes (MPH) were subjected to treatment with palmitic acid (Sigma, USA, P9767) and oleic acid (Sigma, USA, 26125) at a 2:1 ratio (1.0 mM OPA) for a duration of 24 h to induce lipotoxic damage.

Animal models

All animal experiments conducted in this study adhered strictly to the “Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” established by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and received approval from the Animal Experiment Ethics Review Committee at Jiangsu University (Approval no. UJS-IACUC-AP-2020033127). C57BL/6 male mice were maintained in an environment with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to food and water. The MASH mouse models were developed following established protocols [29, 30]. After reaching an age of 8 weeks, the mice were divided into three groups: one group received a high-fat and high-cholesterol diet (HFHC; Trophic Diet, Nantong, China, TP26304) for 20 weeks (n = 10), while the other group was given a 60% high-fat diet (HFD; HUAFUKANG Bioscience, Beijing, China, H10060) for a duration of 16 weeks (n = 6). The third group received the 60% HFD for 20 weeks (n = 10) and was administered intraperitoneal injections of carbon tetrachloride (CCl4, 0.5 µL g− 1 body weight) three times a week during the last month of the high-fat diet regimen. In contrast, the control group was kept on a normal chow diet (NCD). Once finishing the study, all mice were euthanized following a fasting period of 12 h. Isoflurane (2%, 0.5 L min− 1) was utilized for the euthanasia procedure.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To measure the levels of circulating UBQLN1 in serum, we utilized the human UBQLN1 ELISA kit (Byabscience, Nanjing, China, BY-EH1101335) following the provided guidelines. The absorbance readings were recorded at a specific wavelength of 450 nm using the SpectraMax M2 multifunctional microplate reader from Molecular Devices. The concentration of UBQLN1 was calculated by employing a four-parameter regression analysis based on the absorbance readings in relation to the standard curve.

Immunohistochemistry

Liver sections embedded in paraffin were subjected to a dewaxing and rehydration process. After antigen retrieval, endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited by 3% hydrogen peroxide for 40 min, and non-specific binding sites were subsequently blocked using 5% BSA. The primary antibody was allowed to incubate overnight at a temperature of 4 °C, after which the secondary antibody was introduced and left to react at room temperature for a duration of 60 min. Counterstaining was accomplished by hematoxylin. Imaging was performed utilizing an Olympus CX41 microscope sourced from Tokyo, Japan. The primary antibody employed in this procedure was UBQLN1, provided by AiFang Biological, Hunan, China (catalogue number AF14124).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Livers underwent perfusion with PBS and were subsequently fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution. The tissue samples were then fixed with paraffin and sectioned into slices of 4 μm thickness. To examine the histological architecture of the liver sections, the paraffin was eliminated using xylene, followed by rehydration through a series of ethanol solutions with varying concentrations. After the staining with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), the morphological alterations in the tissues were examined using a Leica microscope from Germany, following standard procedures.

Oil red O staining

Freshly obtained mice liver was embedded in OCT and then sectioned into frozen slices. The specimens were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for half an hour and treated with Oil Red O (Sigma, USA, O1516), according to the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. Lipid droplets were acquired utilizing the microscope (Leica, Germany).

Western blot

Cells grown in vitro, along with tissue samples from the liver, underwent lysis using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, P0013B) for 10 min at 4 °C. After sonication, the supernatant was obtained through centrifugation. Then the protein concentrations were assessed using the BCA kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, P0009). The prepared samples underwent analysis via SDS-PAGE at concentrations ranging from 10% to 12%, and proteins were subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA, IPVH00010). Next, the PVDF membrane was blocked using TBS/T with 5% skim milk for 60 min. The primary antibody was diluted in a specific diluent and combined overnight at 4 °C. Antibodies used were included: UBQLN1 (AiFang Biological, Hunan, China, AF14124), MYC tag (ABclonal, Wuhan, China, AE010), HA tag (Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 51064-2-AP), V5-Tag (ABclonal, Wuhan, China, AE101), Flag-Tag (ABclonal, Wuhan, China, AE092), p-p38 MAPK (Santa Cruz, USA, sc-166182), ALIX (Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 12422-1-AP), p38α/β (Santa Cruz, USA, sc-7149), p-ERK (Santa Cruz, USA, sc-7383), ERK1/2 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 11257-1-AP), p-JNK (Santa Cruz, USA, sc-6254), JNK (Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 24164-1-AP), CD47 (proteintech, USA, 20305-1-AP), SIKE (Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 22432-1-AP), SAM50 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 28679-1-AP), HBA (Abclonal, Wuhan, China, A9293), CYLD (ABclonal, Wuhan, China, A3821), TSG101 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 28283-1-AP), UBE3A (Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 10344-1-AP) and β-actin (ABclonal, Wuhan, China, AC006). After incubating the samples with HRP Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Secondary Antibody (ABclonal, Wuhan, China, AS014) at 37 °C for 60 min, color development was performed using the ECL luminescent solution (E412-01) from Vazyme, Nanjing.

Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR)

RNA was obtained from liver tissues and HepG2 cells utilizing RNA isolater Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China, R401-01) following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. The synthesis of complementary DNA (cDNA) was carried out using the Vazyme reverse transcription kit, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was executed with CWBIO SYBR Green (CW0659), incorporating β-actin as the reference gene. The 2−ΔΔCt method was conducted to analyze the relative gene expression. Each group underwent six independent replicates. The primers utilized in the analysis are detailed in Supplementary material: table S1.

Nile red staining

Cells were treated with the 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 20 min. Subsequently, 0.5 µg mL− 1 Nile Red staining solution (Sigma, USA, 72485) was used for a 5-minute incubation period to facilitate the visualization of cellular lipid droplets. The nuclei were visualized utilizing Hoechst 33,342 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, C1005). Fluorescence images were acquired utilizing the microscope (Leica, Germany) and quantified with the assistance of ImageJ FIJI software.

Adeno-associated virus 8 (AAV8)-U6-shUBQLN1 injection

In order to create adult-onset hepatic UBQLN1 knockdown mice, a cohort of 7-week-old mice underwent intravenous injection of either AAV8-U6-GFP or AAV8-U6-shUBQLN1 (5 × 1011 vg per mouse; WZ Biosciences Inc, Shandong, China) through the tail vein, subsequently followed by a diet designed to induce MASH. Control group was mice that received AAV8-U6 containing empty vectors. The subjects included three distinct groups: (1) mice on a normal chow diet (NCD) that received control vector injections (n = 10); (2) mice on a MASH diet that received control vector injections (n = 10); and (3) MASH mice that were injected with AAV8-U6-shUBQLN1 (n = 10). Body weight measurements were taken weekly during the duration of the experiment. After the research concluded, the mice were euthanized in a humane manner, and samples of serum and liver tissue were obtained for subsequent biochemical and histopathological evaluations. The liver index (the ratio of liver weight to body weight) was determined by excising the entire liver tissue and measuring its weight.

Analysis of biochemical indicators of mice serum

Total alanine aminotransferase (ALT), cholesterol (TC), aspartate transaminase (AST), and triglycerides (TG) in mouse serum were assessed following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer, utilizing a MINDRAY BS200 chemistry analyzer from MINDRAY Medical International Co. located in Shenzhen, China.

Glucose and insulin tolerance tests

Blood glucose levels were measured at designated time points. For insulin tolerance test (ITT), mice underwent a fasting period of 6 h before receiving an intraperitoneal injection of 0.75 units kg− 1 insulin. Subsequently, a glucose tolerance assessment via intraperitoneal injection (IPGTT) was measured at the end of the experiment, wherein the mice were subjected to a 16-hour fasting period prior to the administration of glucose via intraperitoneal injection at a dosage of 1 g kg− 1. The area under the curve (AUC) for glucose concentrations was recorded throughout these experimental procedures.

Immunofluorescence

For the purpose of conducting immunofluorescence analysis, liver tissue sections underwent treatment with 4% paraformaldehyde for a duration of 30 min, followed by a permeabilization step using 0.1% Triton X-100 for 30 min. Subsequently, 5% BSA was employed to block for one hour, the liver sections were incubated with primary antibodies for 12 h at 4 °C. Following incubation, the sections underwent staining with the appropriate isotype-specific secondary antibodies for one hour while being shielded from light. To visualize cell nuclei, Hoechst 33,342 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, C1026) was employed, and images were acquired utilizing an inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with a confocal imaging system (Leica Microsystems, Germany). The primary antibody utilized in this analysis was F4/80 (Santa Cruz, USA, sc-52664).

Sirius red staining

The detailed procedure for Sirius red staining was as follows: an initial staining with haematoxylin for a duration of 5 min was performed, followed by Sirius red staining for a period of 2 h. Then it was succeeded by a second haematoxylin staining lasting 2 min, an acid differentiation treatment for 5 s, and subsequently the dehydration of the film along with blocking. Confocal microscopic images were acquired utilizing a Leica Microsystems imaging system manufactured in Germany.

Masson staining

Fixed liver tissues were initially embedded and sectioned, followed by deparaffinization, xylene removal, and Masson staining according to established protocols. The deposition of collagen fibers in the tissues was examined using a Leica microscope from Germany, in accordance with standard procedures.

mRNA-seq analysis

Liver mRNA-seq analyses were conducted in mice (HFHC/AAV8-U6 control vector and HFHC/AAV8-U6-shUBQLN1; n = 3). Additionally, mRNA-seq evaluations were carried out on lipotoxic-injured HepG2shNC and HepG2shUBQLN1 cell lines (n = 3). The RNA libraries were generated utilizing the Illumina NovaSeqTM 6000 platform, facilitated by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., based in Guangzhou. Subsequent bioinformatics analyses were executed using the OmicStudio platform (accessible at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool).

Plasmid and SiRNA constructs

Full-length cDNA of human UBQLN1 (WT or E54D mutant), SIKE and UBE3A genes was amplified using PCR technology and was cloned into the pcDNA3.1-V5/MYC/Flag vector. Ubiquitin-HA plasmid was purchased from Fenghui Biotechnology (Hunan, China), and UBQLN1/SIKE/UBE3A specific siRNA along with the control was designed by GenePharma. Transfection experiments were conducted according to the instructions using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA, 11668500).

Lentiviral overexpression and knockdown of UBQLN1

The lentiviral vector designed to incorporate the shRNA sequence targeting UBQLN1 (pLKO.1-GFP-Puro-shUBQLN1; Vigen Biotech, China) was specifically engineered to silence the UBQLN1 gene. Furthermore, a control vector without any specific targeting (pLKO.1-GFP-Puro-shRNA) was also created. The oligonucleotides for both UBQLN1 shRNA and non-targeting control shRNA were integrated into the pLKO.2-U6-hPGK-copGFP-puro lentiviral vector, resulting in the creation of expression vectors for both UBQLN1 and the control. To facilitate the overexpression of UBQLN1, a lentiviral vector (pLKO.1-GFP-Puro-UBQLN1 OE; Vigen Biotech, China) containing the sequence of UBQLN1 plasmid was employed. Furthermore, pLKO.1-Puro-RNA, as a control vector, was also developed. For the establishment of stable cell lines (HepG2UBQLN1 OE and HepG2shUBQLN1), the HepG2 cells transduced with either pLKO.1-GFP-Puro-UBQLN1 OE or pLKO.1-GFP-Puro-shUBQLN1 using a recombinant lentivirus that underwent selection with puromycin (Sigma, USA, 540411; 3 mg mL− 1) for 10 days. Subsequently, the induction of RNA expression was achieved using doxycycline at 80 µg mL− 1.

LC–MS/MS assay

All proteins were obtained from HepG2 cells overexpressing V5-tagged UBQLN1 using ultrasonic methods, and subsequently analyzed via LC-MS/MS to identify proteins that interact with UBQLN1. Following this, the proteins underwent separation through Western bolt and were stained using Coomassie Blue (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, P0017). The total protein bands obtained were excised and subjected to reductive alkylation, followed by enzymatic digestion, which produced peptide fragments. These fragments were subsequently separated and analyzed using a long gradient LC-MS/MS protocol. To identify the proteins, the MaxQuant software was utilized to search the resulting data against a target protein database.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

HepG2 cells transfected with the specified plasmids underwent lysis and sonication for the purpose of total protein extraction. Following the binding of specific primary antibodies, Protein A/G agarose beads (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, P2108) underwent extensive washing procedures. Total proteins were then added to the beads and allowed to incubate on a shaker at 4 °C for 3 h. The proteins that were immunoprecipitated were subsequently retrieved by utilizing a magnetic rack to adsorb the beads. After washing the magnetic beads with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 15 µL of protein loading buffer was incorporated, and the resultant mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min to ensure complete denaturation of the proteins prior to conducting Western blot analysis.

Cycloheximide chase assays

HepG2 cells were transduced with either a lentiviral vector for UBQLN1 overexpression (UBQLN1 OE) or a control vector. Additionally, HepG2 cells were transfected with a control plasmid or a UBE3A overexpression plasmid. All cells were treated with cycloheximide (MCE, USA, HY-12320; 20 µg mL− 1) at various time intervals. Subsequently, proteins were extracted from the lysate for the purpose of Western blot analysis.

Ubiquitination assay in vitro

HepG2 cells were transfected with MYC-tagged SIKE, HA-tagged Ubiquitin (HA-Ubi), V5-tagged UBQLN1, or Flag-tagged UBE3A. Subsequently, MG132 was introduced at a concentration of 20 µM to impede cellular degradation before protein extraction. The cellular proteins were collected using lysis buffer, followed by the separation of the supernatant through ultrasonic centrifugation. The resulting supernatant was subsequently incubated with protein A/G agarose beads covalently linked to anti-MYC antibodies. Following a wash with PBS, the complexes immobilized on the beads were subjected to analysis via Western blot. An anti-HA antibody was utilized to assess the extent of MYC-SIKE ubiquitination.

Preparation of extracellular vesicles derived from red blood cells (RBC-EVs)

The preparation of extracellular vesicles extruded from red blood cells (RBC-EVs) was performed using an extrusion method, specifically based on the protocols reported in the literature [9, 31] with appropriate optimizations. The detailed steps are as follows: First, whole blood was subjected to centrifugation at 500×g for a duration of 10 min to facilitate the separation of red blood cells. These cells were subsequently purified with the aid of a leukocyte removal filter (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan). The purified red blood cells were then diluted in PBS to achieve a concentration of 6% before being processed through polycarbonate membrane filters (Whatman) with pore sizes of 5 μm, 1 μm, and 0.4 μm, utilizing a mini-extruder (Avanti Polar Lipids, USA), ensuring that each filter was extruded three times. To enhance recovery efficiency, the extruded product was purified by ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g for 60 min at 4 °C. The final RBC-EVs were then resuspended in PBS and stored at -80 °C for future applications. The expression of characteristic marker proteins such as ALIX, TSG101, CD47, and HBA was detected by Western blot. Additionally, the morphological particle size distribution and structure of the RBC-EVs were characterized using a nanoparticle tracking analysis system (NTA, ZetaView, Germany) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai 12, Philips, Netherlands), respectively.

UBQLN1 SiRNA loading into RBC-EVs by electroporation

The Mus-UBQLN1 siRNA that was chemically synthesized and utilized in the study includes the following sequences: sense strand: GAG CAA CCU AGA AAG UAU UTT; antisense strand: AAU ACU UUC UAG GUU GCU CTT. The FAM-labeled Mus-UBQLN1 siRNA, and scrambled control siRNA were all obtained from Suzhou Jima Gene Co., Ltd. During the experiment, 45 µL of RBC-EVs suspension (1 × 1011 particles mL− 1) was first mixed with 5 µg of UBQLN1 siRNA, FAM-labeled siRNA, or scrambled control siRNA. The mixture solution was then transferred to a 4 mm electroporation cuvette, and pulsed twice at a voltage of 0.7 kV using an electroporator (each pulse lasting 0.35 s), following the parameters set in the literature [32]. After electroporation, the mixture solution was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C to facilitate membrane structure repair. Subsequently, unbound siRNA was removed using pre-cooled PBS through two rounds of ultracentrifugation (100,000×g, 4 °C, 70 min each), and finally, the samples were resuspended in PBS for further use.

TUNEL assay

After a 20-minute incubation in 4% paraformaldehyde and rinse with PBS, the fixed cells were then treated with the TUNEL reaction mixture (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, C1088) for 60 min at 37 °C. Following this, Hoechst 33,342 was used to the nucleus. Finally, using the CytationTM5 instrument (BioTek, USA) to assess the fluorescence intensity.

RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 injection

MASH mice were systematically allocated into five distinct experimental groups. These groups comprised: a control group consisting of mice on a normal chow diet comprising 10% kcal from fat (NCD group); MASH mice administered with PBS (HFD/CCL4 group and HFHC group); MASH mice injected with RBC-EVs@siCtr (1.8 × 109 particles); and MASH mice injected with RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 at two different concentrations (1.2 × 109 particles or 1.8 × 109 particles). Each group has six mice repetitions. The RBC-EVs@siCtr and RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 were labeled with Dir (purchased from UElandy, Suzhou, China, D4006) according to manufacturer’s protocol. The RBC-EVs@siCtr and RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1 were tagged with Dir (sourced from UElandy, Suzhou, China, D4006) following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. To evaluate the uptake of Dir-tagged RBC-EVs@siCtr and RBC-EVs@siUBQLN1, a sum of 1.8 × 109 labeled particles were as administered into the mice through the tail vein. The uptake condition was observed by an in vivo imaging system (BLT, Guangzhou, China, AniView600). Upon completion of the study, all subjects were euthanized, and liver tissues along with blood samples were collected.

Statistical analysis

All biological replicates were conducted with a minimum of three repetitions. We present our results as mean value ± standard error of the mean. Both the D’Agostino test and the Pearson normality test were utilized to assess the normality of the data. The analysis of the collected data was carried out using Prism software (GraphPad 8.0, San Diego, CA). A one-way ANOVA or Student’s t-test was used to assess differences between groups. The association was investigated using Spearman Pearson correlation analysis. All data analyses were conducted with a two-tailed distribution.
