Late‐life methionine restriction attenuates neuroinflammation in Alzheimer's disease mice via FGF21 activation in a metabolism‐independent manner
Authors: Yuyu Zhang, Yiju Li, Qianxu Wang, Jingxi Pang, Da Wang, Tian Yuan, Zhigang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAging worsens Alzheimer's disease (AD) peripheral metabolism and central pathology, yet few interventions are effective when started late. Methionine restriction (MR) induces the hepatokine FGF21 and may protect brain function, but its efficacy and mechanisms when started late are unclear.METHODSFourteen‐month‐old male APP/PS1 mice received 17 weeks of MR (0.17% methionine); behavioral, histological, and molecular assays were performed and hippocampal FGFR1 was knocked down by adeno‐associated virus.RESULTSLate‐life MR improved peripheral glucose/lipid profiles, reduced Aβ deposition, preserved synaptic markers, and suppressed neuroinflammation. MR‐induced hepatic FGF21 and brain FGFR1‐AMPKα signaling to inhibit NFκB; hippocampal FGFR1 knockdown abolished MR's neuroprotective effects while leaving peripheral metabolic changes intact.DISCUSSIONEven when initiated in late life, MR robustly reduces AD pathology via the hepatic FGF21‐brain FGFR1 axis, independent of peripheral metabolic changes. These preclinical findings position MR and FGF21‐FGFR1 axis as actionable late‐life intervention targets with potential for clinical translation.Keywords: Alzheimer's Disease, fibroblast growth factor 21, metabolism, methionine restriction, neuroinflammation

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is an incurable neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia. 1 , 2 The incidence of AD increases with age, particularly after 65, with approximately 1 in 10 people aged 65 and older affected. 3 As such, aging is considered the primary risk factor for AD, which is characterized by progressive synaptic dysfunction, neuronal loss, and cognitive decline. The etiology of AD is complex, with the main pathological features being the excessive accumulation of amyloid‐β (Aβ) peptides, aggregation of hyperphosphorylated tau proteins, and neuroinflammation, which is closely intertwined with oxidative stress. Neuroinflammation and oxidative stress may arise in the early stages of AD, preceding overt neuronal loss. 4 Targeting bioenergetic pathways linked to these processes represents a promising therapeutic strategy for AD. Additionally, disrupted systemic energy metabolism has gained attention in recent years. 5 Notably, both preclinical studies and longitudinal clinical investigations have demonstrated that Alzheimer's‐related pathologies, such as progressive Aβ deposition and age‐associated declines in glucose metabolism, intensify with advancing age, 6 , 7 , 8 and to date there are no approved disease‐modifying treatments capable of stopping or reversing age‐associated amyloid deposition, neuroinflammation/oxidative stress, or systemic metabolic imbalance in AD patients. Therefore, exploring novel therapeutic strategies that target these underlying mechanisms is crucial for developing treatments to slow the progression of aged AD.

Dietary pattern alterations represent a moderately effective and modifiable intervention, which has been linked to a reduced risk of cognitive decline and incident dementia in epidemiological studies. 9 Replacing processed red meat with one serving per day of nuts and legumes has been associated with a 19% lower risk of dementia, 10 suggesting that adherence to a plant‐based diet may reduce AD risk. 11 The methionine restriction (MR) diet, which aligns with plant‐based dietary patterns, has shown various positive effects on healthy aging, including lifespan extension, 12 systemic metabolism regulation, 13 and neuroprotection. 14 These benefits may, in part, be due to the robust MR‐induced response to the nutrient‐sensing signal fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21). FGF21 plays a critical role in regulating energy balance and glucose and lipid homeostasis through a heterodimeric receptor complex composed of FGF receptor 1 (FGFR1) and β‐klotho. Administration of FGF21 to rodents or nonhuman primates results in significant benefits in a range of obesity‐related metabolic complications, including reduced fat mass and alleviated hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and steatohepatitis. 15 The therapeutic mechanisms of FGF21 primarily involve anti‐inflammatory effects, energy metabolism regulation, and oxidative stress reduction. Additionally, FGF21 may represent the only member of the FGF family with the ability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) under physiological conditions. 16 FGFR1 and β‐klotho are also widely expressed in brain tissue, including in microglia, 17 indicating FGF21's potential for neuroprotective and anti‐neuroinflammatory effects. Our previous studies have shown that MR activates hepatic FGF21 in db/db mice, producing dual effects. Specifically, MR reduces peripheral FGF21 resistance and insulin resistance while enhancing central glucose metabolism, which further alleviates diabetes‐associated cognitive impairment. 18 While MR is known to induce FGF21 and improve peripheral metabolic health, its dual effects on the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral metabolism have not been thoroughly investigated in established neurodegeneration models. Moreover, many antiaging interventions, such as caloric restriction, are typically implemented in mid‐ to late life but often exhibit diminished efficacy when initiated at advanced ages. 19 , 20 The impact of late‐life MR on aged individuals remains unclear, highlighting a significant knowledge gap.

To address these gaps, we evaluated late‐life dietary MR in aged APP/PS1 mice. Fourteen‐month‐old male APP/PS1 mice received an MR diet for 17 weeks, followed by behavioral and neuropathological assessments. To examine mechanism and target specificity, AAV‐mediated hippocampal FGFR1 knockdown was used to interrogate liver‐derived FGF21‐brain FGFR1 signaling. Our findings indicate that MR's central neuroprotective effects depend on a hepatic FGF21‐brain FGFR1 axis and are separable from systemic metabolic improvements, supporting MR as a targeted late‐life precision‐nutrition strategy for AD.

METHODS

Animal experiments

Fourteen‐month‐old male APPswe/PSEN1dE9 (APP/PS1) transgenic mice and their wild‐type (WT) littermates (B6C3 background) were obtained from Nanjing University (Nanjing, China). After a one‐week acclimation, WT mice were maintained on standard chow (0.86% methionine, w/w; cystine omitted), whereas APP/PS1 mice were randomized to receive either the same chow or a methionine‐restricted diet (0.17% methionine, w/w; cystine omitted) 14 for 16 weeks. Behavioral testing was then conducted sequentially over a one‐week period while dietary intervention continued, until the mice reached 18 months of age (n = 7–8 per group). To minimize potential carryover effects, each task was separated by at least 1–2 rest days. Cystine was omitted to avoid compensating for MR, as it can be reduced to cysteine and contribute to sulfur amino acid metabolism, potentially blunting the effects of MR. 21 All diets were custom‐prepared by Trophic Animal Feed High‐Tech Co., Ltd. (China); detailed composition is provided in Table S1.

To selectively silence FGFR1 in the brain, a separate cohort of 16‐month‐old male mice underwent stereotaxic delivery of adeno‐associated virus vectors into the lateral ventricles (coordinates: 0.3 mm posterior to bregma, 1.0 mm lateral, 2.5 mm depth). Each mouse received 3 µL of either AAV‐U6‐shFGFR1‐CMV‐EGFP‐WPRE or AAV‐U6‐shNC‐CMV‐EGFP‐WPRE (OBiO Technology Co., Ltd., China). The specific shRNA and control sequences are listed in Table S2. Because AAV‐mediated gene knockdown requires approximately 2 weeks postinjection to achieve stable transgene expression, with viral expression peaking at around 4 weeks and gradually declining thereafter, 22 MR feeding was initiated 2 weeks after AAV injection and maintained for 4 weeks, followed by a 1‐week behavioral assessment, resulting in a total experimental duration of 5 weeks until 18 months of age (n = 5–9 per group), coinciding with robust FGFR1 knockdown. To minimize potential carryover effects, each task was separated by at least 1–2 rest days. This timeline ensured that MR feeding and viral knockdown co‐occurred over the final two months of life, and that all experimental groups were evaluated at the same age of 18 months (n = 5–9 per group), thereby maintaining comparability with the original cohort.

All animals were housed in the Northwest A&F University Experimental Animal Center under controlled conditions (22 ± 2°C, 50 ± 15% humidity, 12:12 h light–dark cycle) with free access to food and water. At the end of the study, mice were anesthetized before euthanasia, using procedures designed to minimize suffering. Prior to tissue collection, animals were transcardially perfused with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). Brains were rapidly dissected on ice, with one hemisphere fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis and the other was separated and snap‐frozen for molecular assays. Blood for plasma hormone measurements was collected into EDTA‐coated tubes, centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the plasma was separated and stored at −80°C until analysis. All protocols conformed to the National Research Council's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th ed.) and were approved by the Northwest A&F University Animal Ethics Committee (approval XN2023‐0717).

Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance

Glucose tolerance was assessed by intraperitoneal glucose tolerance testing (IPGTT). After an 8‐h fast, each mouse received an intraperitoneal injection of glucose (2 g per kg body weight). Tail‐vein blood samples were taken immediately before injection (0 min) and at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min thereafter, and glucose concentrations were determined using a handheld glucometer.

Novel object recognition

Long‐term recognition memory was assessed using the novel object recognition (NOR) paradigm. 23 After habituation to the testing arena, mice underwent a 24‐h delay before the testing procedures. During the familiarization phase, each mouse was allowed to explore two identical objects for 5 min. In the test phase (5 min), one of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel object, and exploratory behavior was recorded. Both the preference index and discrimination index were analyzed, as they provide complementary information about recognition memory performance. The preference index was defined as the time spent investigating the novel object divided by the total object‐exploration time, and the discrimination index as the difference between time with the novel versus familiar object, normalized to the total exploration duration. Reporting both indices increases the robustness of interpretation by capturing subtle variations in exploratory preference and overall recognition accuracy. All sessions were video‐tracked and analyzed with SuperMaze software (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd., China).

Y‐maze

Each mouse was introduced into the central area of the Y‐maze and permitted to explore freely for 8 min. Arm entries and movement patterns were recorded and analyzed with SuperMaze software (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd., China). An alternation was defined as entries into three different arms on consecutive choices; the alternation percentage was calculated by dividing the number of actual alternations by the maximum possible alternations (total arm entries minus two) and multiplying by 100%.

Barnes maze

The Barnes maze tests spatial memory in animals using a circular platform with 20 holes, one concealing an escape box. The platform is divided into four quadrants, each marked with distinct colors and shapes. 24 The day before the experiment, mice were acclimated to the escape box for 4 min. During training (days 1–4), mice started under a box in the maze center for 30 s, then explored for 3 min. If the mouse did not locate the escape box within 3 min, it was manually guided to it for 1 min. On day 5, the escape box was removed, and mice explored the platform for 5 min. Escape latency (time to first explore the target hole) was recorded. The maze and box were cleaned with 70% ethanol after each trial, and the maze rotated. Data were recorded using SuperMaze software (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., Ltd., China).

Liver weight and biochemical analysis

The entire liver was collected after excising surrounding adhesions and soft tissues. Liver weight was measured in grams to determine the wet weight. Serum levels of fasting glucose (A154‐1‐1), triglycerides (TG) (A110‐1‐1), total cholesterol (TC) (A111‐1‐1), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) (A113‐1‐1), and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) (A112‐1‐1) were measured using commercial kits (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay

Plasma insulin (MB‐3315A) and FGF21 (XL‐EM1943) were measured using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Jiangsu Meibiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) and (Shanghai Xinle Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Prefrontal cortex levels of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α) (MB‐2868A), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) (MB‐2899A), interleukin 1 beta (IL‐1β) (XL‐EM0187), and FGF21 (XL‐EM1943) were measured using ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meibiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) and (Shanghai Xinle Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) was calculated as: (fasting insulin concentration (mU/L) × fasting glucose concentration (mg/dL) × 0.05551)/22.5.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry

For liver, a representative lobe (left lateral lobe) was used for histology. For brain, one hemisphere was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histology. After deparaffinization and rehydration, 5 µm sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Solarbio G1120, Beijing, China) to evaluate overall tissue architecture. For immunohistochemistry, slides were incubated overnight at 4°C with rabbit anti‐FGF21 (Abcam ab171941, 1:250), followed by incubation with biotin‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG and visualization using an avidin‐biotin complex and DAB substrate. Sections were then counterstained lightly with hematoxylin and cover slipped. H&E and immunohistochemistry images were acquired on a Leica DM5000 B microscope (Germany) with a digital camera under consistent exposure settings. The percentage of hepatic steatotic area was quantified from H&E‐stained sections using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA). For each group, three mice were analyzed, and for each mouse, three nonoverlapping fields were selected; steatotic vacuoles were identified by threshold‐based segmentation in ImageJ, and the steatotic area was calculated as a proportion of the total tissue area. H&E‐stained sections of the prefrontal cortex were used to quantify the percentage of abnormal nuclei. For each group, three mice were analyzed, and for each mouse, three nonoverlapping cortical sections were evaluated. The proportion of abnormal nuclei was calculated as the number of abnormal nuclei divided by the total nuclei count within the defined ROI. FGF21 immunohistochemistry in liver sections was quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA). DAB staining was separated by color deconvolution, and the integrated optical density (IOD) of FGF21‐positive signals was measured and normalized to the corresponding tissue area to obtain mean optical density.

For immunofluorescence, brain sections were first blocked in 5% normal goat serum, then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: mouse anti‐IBA‐1 (Abcam ab178847, 1:100), mouse anti‐Aβ (BioLegend SIG‐39320, 1:500), rabbit anti‐PSD‐95 (Abcam ab18258, 1:200), and rabbit anti‐BDNF (Abcam ab108319, 1:500). After three PBS washes, samples were labeled for 1 h at room temperature with species‐appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to CY3 or Alexa Fluor 488. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma‐Aldrich F6057, Germany), and sections were mounted in antifade medium. Fluorescence images were captured on a Leica DM5000 B microscope (Germany) using identical exposure settings across groups. For each group, three mice were analyzed, and for each mouse, three nonoverlapping ROIs per brain section were imaged for quantification of average IBA‐1+ cell numbers around each Aβ plaque. Microglial cell bodies were identified as IBA‐1+ signals colocalized with DAPI+ nuclei, and microglial processes were not counted as independent cells. Only microglia directly contacting or immediately adjacent to the plaque perimeter were included in the counts. Cell counting was performed manually using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA), and the average number of microglia per plaque was calculated across all analyzed plaques within the ROIs.

Oil Red O staining

For liver, a representative lobe (left lateral lobe) was used for histology. The tissues were embedded in OCT medium, and 5 µm frozen sections were prepared at −20°C. Saturated Oil Red O solution (Solarbio G1261, Beijing, China) was diluted with distilled water (3:2, v/v) after dissolving in isopropanol and filtered. The sections were stained with the working solution, counterstained with hematoxylin (Solarbio H8070, Beijing, China) for 1–2 min, sealed with neutral gelatin, and imaged using a microscope (DM5000 B, Leica, Germany) under consistent exposure settings. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Real time‐qPCR (RT‐qPCR)

Total RNA from prefrontal cortex, liver, and hippocampus was extracted by homogenizing tissue samples in AG RNAex Pro reagent (AG21102; Accurate Biotechnology, Hunan, China). RNA purity and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop OneC UV‐Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). One microgram of total RNA was reverse‐transcribed into cDNA with the All‐in‐One RT mix containing DNase (R2020L; US EVERBRIGHT, Suzhou, China). Quantitative PCR was carried out using the 2× SYBR Green qPCR Mix with ROX (AH0104‐B; Shandong Sparkjade Biotechnology, China) on a real‐time PCR instrument, following the manufacturer's protocols. Relative transcript levels were calculated by the ΔΔCt method and normalized to β‐actin. Primer sequences are provided in Table S3.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from liver (n = 6 per group) and hippocampal (n = 5–6 per group) tissues using the PL001 Protein Extraction Kit (ZhongHuiHeCai Biotechnology, China). Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay (A55864; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Liver samples (conventional Western blot): Aliquots (30–50 µg) of denatured liver lysates were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat milk and then incubated overnight at 4°C with rabbit anti‐FGF21 (Abcam ab171941, 1:1000) or mouse anti‐β‐actin (Santa Cruz sc‐47778, 1:2000). After incubation with appropriate HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies, immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and captured on a ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio‐Rad, USA). Densitometry was performed in ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Hippocampal samples (Jess capillary assay): Hippocampal lysates were mixed with sample buffer, denatured at 95°C for 5 min, and then 3 µL loaded per capillary of the ProteinSimple Jess system (ProteinSimple, USA). Each plate was programmed for automated protein separation, immobilization, blocking, antibody incubation, and chemiluminescent detection. Primary antibodies—rabbit anti‐phospho‐NFκB (CST #3033, 1:50), rabbit anti‐NFκB (CST #8242, 1:50), rabbit anti‐FGFR1 (CST #9740, 1:50), rabbit anti‐phospho‐AMPKα (CST #2535, 1:50), rabbit anti‐AMPKα (CST #5831, 1:50), and mouse anti‐β‐actin (Santa Cruz sc‐47778, 1:50)—were followed by HRP‐conjugated secondaries. Data acquisition and quantification were carried out with Compass software (ProteinSimple, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). All values are expressed as mean ± SEM. To compare more than two groups, we performed one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple‐comparisons test. Differences were deemed statistically significant at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Effects of late‐life MR on cognitive function and neuronal morphology in aged AD mice

After a one‐week adaptation period, 14‐month‐old male WT mice were fed a normal diet (ND, 0.86% methionine, w/w), whereas age‐matched male AD mice were assigned to either the ND or the methionine‐restricted diet (MR, 0.17% methionine, w/w) for 17 weeks until they reached 18 months of age. In the final week, the NOR test and Y‐maze test were conducted. The experimental design is illustrated in Figure 1A.

MR significantly increased the discrimination and preference indices in aged AD mice compared to WT+ND and AD+ND groups (p < 0.01), with no significant differences observed between aged WT and AD mice on these measures (p > 0.05) (Figure 1B–D). In the Y‐maze test, aged AD mice displayed a lower percentage of alternation (p < 0.05), whereas MR significantly improved the percentage of alternation in aged AD mice compared to those fed ND (p < 0.05) (Figure 1E). Quantitative analysis of H&E‐stained prefrontal cortex sections revealed that the percentage of abnormal nuclei in the AD+MR group was significantly lower than that in the WT+ND and AD+ND groups (p < 0.01) (Figure 1F). Histological analysis revealed sparsely arranged cells, reduced cell body size, and condensed or fragmented nuclei in the hippocampus (CA1, DG, and CA3) and prefrontal cortex of aged WT and AD mice, indicative of apoptosis or necrosis (Figure 1G; Figure S1A). These pathological changes were alleviated in aged AD mice following MR intervention (Figure 1G; Figure S1A).

Additionally, the mRNA expression of postsynaptic density protein 95 (Psd‐95), a biomarker of postsynaptic density, was significantly downregulated in aged AD mice compared to aged WT mice (p < 0.05), but MR treatment restored its expression (p < 0.01) (Figure S1C). Consistently, MR significantly increased the mean fluorescence intensity of PSD‐95 in the prefrontal cortex of aged AD mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 1H; Figure S1B). Furthermore, the mRNA expression of brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (Bdnf) was significantly reduced in aged AD mice compared to aged WT mice (p < 0.05), and MR intervention markedly elevated its expression (p < 0.01) (Figure S1E). Protein levels of BDNF were also significantly decreased in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of aged AD mice (p < 0.01) (Figure 1I,J; Figure S1D). MR treatment significantly increased BDNF expression in both regions of aged AD mice (p < 0.01), although levels remained below those measured in the prefrontal cortex of aged WT controls (p < 0.01) (Figure 1I,J; Figure S1D).

These findings suggest that late‐life MR improves cognitive function and ameliorates impaired cell morphology and synaptic plasticity in aged AD mice.

Effects of late‐life MR on Aβ pathology and neuroinflammation in aged AD mice

Abnormal Aβ accumulation and the resulting neuroinflammation in the brain are central pathological hallmarks of AD progression. In this study, aged AD mice exhibited a significantly higher Aβ‐positive area in both the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex compared to aged WT mice (p < 0.01) (Figure 2A,B; Figure S2A). MR intervention significantly reduced the Aβ‐positive area in the hippocampus (p < 0.05) and prefrontal cortex (p < 0.01) of aged AD mice; however, the levels remained higher than those in aged WT mice in both the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (p < 0.01) (Figure 2A,B; Figure S2A). The Aβ burden in aged AD mice led to an increased average number of IBA‐1‐positive microglia surrounding each Aβ plaque in both the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (p < 0.01) (Figure 2A,C; Figure S2A). MR‐treated aged AD mice showed a higher average number of IBA‐1‐positive microglia around each Aβ plaque compared to ND‐treated aged WT and AD mice (p < 0.01), indicating that MR more effectively activates microglia to clear Aβ plaques (Figure 2A,C; Figure S2A).

Additionally, the mRNA expression levels of App and Ps1 were significantly upregulated in the prefrontal cortex of aged AD mice compared to aged WT mice (p < 0.01), while MR treatment reversed these changes in aged AD mice (p < 0.01) (Figure 2D). The mRNA levels of pro‐inflammatory factors, including inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNos) (p < 0.01), Tnf‐α (p < 0.05), IL‐1β (p < 0.01), and IL‐6 (p < 0.05), were significantly elevated, while the anti‐inflammatory factor interleukin‐10 (IL‐10) remained unchanged (p > 0.05) in the prefrontal cortex of aged AD mice (Figure 2E). MR treatment significantly decreased the expression levels of iNos (p < 0.01), IL‐1β (p < 0.01), and IL‐6 (p < 0.05), while increasing IL‐10 expression (p < 0.05) in the prefrontal cortex of aged AD mice (Figure 2E). Consistent with these findings, ELISA tests demonstrated that IL‐1β expression was elevated in the prefrontal cortex of aged AD mice compared to aged WT mice (p < 0.05). MR treatment significantly reduced the levels of IL‐6 (p < 0.05) and IL‐1β (p < 0.01) in the prefrontal cortex of aged AD mice (Figure 2F,G). However, no significant changes were observed in TNF‐α levels in the prefrontal cortex among groups (p > 0.05) (Figure S2B).

These results suggest that late‐life MR enhances microglial clearance of Aβ, thereby reducing Aβ deposition and neuroinflammation in aged AD mice.

Effects of late‐life MR on glucose and lipid metabolism in aged AD mice

AD patients frequently exhibit metabolic derangements, such as impaired insulin signaling and disrupted glucose utilization—that progressively worsen as the disease advances. 25 In this study, we evaluated the effects of late‐life MR on peripheral metabolism in aged AD mice. Both ND‐treated aged AD and WT mice showed similar body weight gain, but MR significantly reduced body weight gain in aged AD mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 3A). The AD+MR group demonstrated lower fasting glucose (p < 0.05) and fasting insulin concentrations (p < 0.05) compared with both the WT+ND and AD+ND groups (Figure 3B,C). Consistently, the HOMA‐IR index was significantly reduced in the AD+MR group compared with the WT+ND and AD+ND groups (p < 0.01) (Figure 3D).

The liver, as the critical organ for regulating peripheral glucose and lipid metabolism, showed distinct changes after MR intervention. H&E staining demonstrated pronounced hepatic steatosis in aged mice under ND conditions, with comparable microvesicular fat accumulation between WT and AD groups. MR intervention in aged AD mice significantly attenuated lipid deposition, reducing lipid droplets (Figure 3E). The percentage of hepatic steatotic area (%) in the AD+MR group was significantly lower than that in both the WT+ND and AD+ND groups (p < 0.01) (Figure 3G). Oil Red O staining corroborated these findings: aged WT and AD mice on a normal diet exhibited comparable hepatic lipid accumulation (p > 0.05), whereas MR significantly reduced liver fat area in aged AD mice to below that of aged WT controls (p < 0.01) (Figure 3F,H). Consistently, liver weight was comparable between the WT+ND and AD+ND groups (p > 0.05); however, the AD+MR group exhibited significantly lower liver weight compared with both WT+ND and AD+ND groups (p < 0.01) (Figure 3I). Additionally, MR improved serum lipid profiles in aged AD mice. Serum levels of TG (p < 0.05), TC (p < 0.01), and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) (p < 0.01) were significantly lower in the AD+MR group compared with the AD+ND group (Figure 3J). High‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) levels showed no significant difference between the AD+MR and AD+ND groups (p > 0.05), but both were markedly lower than those in the WT+ND group (p < 0.01) (Figure 3J).

These findings suggest that late‐life MR is beneficial for improving glucose and lipid metabolism in aged AD mice.

Effects of late‐life MR on hepatic FGF21 signaling and brain FGFR1 in aged AD mice

FGF21, a hepatokine induced by MR, plays a pivotal role in energy balance by enhancing glucose uptake, promoting lipid oxidation, and increasing energy expenditure. 18 Consistently, the mRNA expression of hepatic FGF21 signaling‐related genes, including Atf4 (p < 0.05), Pparα (p < 0.01), and Fgf21 (p < 0.01), was significantly upregulated following MR intervention compared to ND‐treated aged AD mice (Figure 4A). Western blot demonstrated that MR markedly upregulated hepatic FGF21 protein levels compared to ND‐treated aged AD mice (Figure 4B,C; Figure S3A). Immunohistochemistry analysis further confirmed increased protein expression of FGF21 in the liver of MR‐treated aged AD mice compared to ND‐treated aged WT (p < 0.05) and AD mice (p < 0.01) (Figure 4D,E). Additionally, the mRNA levels of hepatic β‐Klotho and Fgfr1 were significantly upregulated in MR‐treated AD mice (p < 0.05) (Figure S3B). Circulating FGF21 is primarily derived from the liver. 26 Our results showed that MR markedly increased serum FGF21 levels in aged AD mice compared to both ND‐treated aged WT (p < 0.01) and AD mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 4F).

These findings indicate that late‐life MR activates hepatic FGF21, elevates circulating FGF21 levels, and upregulates brain FGFR1 expression in aged AD mice.

Effects of brain FGFR1 on MR‐improved cognitive function in aged AD mice

To investigate the role of FGFR1 in mediating FGF21 liver–brain communication, we conducted experiments on 16‐month‐old male WT and AD mice. After a one‐week adaptation period, Sh‐NC control virus or Sh‐FGFR1 knockdown virus was injected into the lateral ventricle to specifically knock down brain FGFR1. MR feeding was initiated 2 weeks after AAV injection, when FGFR1 knockdown becomes stable, and maintained for 4 weeks, followed by a 1‐week behavioral assessment, yielding a total experimental duration of 5 weeks until 18 months of age. The experimental design is illustrated in Figure 6A. The specificity of AAV‐mediated FGFR1 knockdown was verified by the expression of enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP), a fluorescent reporter encoded by the AAV vector, which was co‐expressed with the FGFR1 shRNA and was exclusively detected in the hippocampal region, indicating region‐specific viral transduction (Figure S4A).

Compared with the WT+Sh‐NC+ND group, the alternation percentage was significantly lower in the AD+Sh‐NC+ND group (p < 0.01) (Figure 6B). MR intervention significantly improved this percentage (p < 0.01); however, knockdown of brain FGFR1 markedly reduced the alternation percentage in MR‐treated aged AD mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 6B). In the AD+Sh‐NC+MR group, both the discrimination index and preference index were significantly increased compared with the AD+Sh‐NC+ND group (p < 0.05) (Figure 6C,D,G). These improvements were abolished by FGFR1 knockdown, as indicated by significant reductions in both indices (p < 0.01) (Figure 6C,D,G). Long‐term memory was assessed using the Barnes maze test. After a four‐day training period, escape latency on the fourth training day was significantly higher in the AD+Sh‐NC+ND group compared with the WT+Sh‐NC+ND group (p < 0.05) (Figure 6E). MR reduced the escape latency in AD+Sh‐NC mice (p < 0.01), but this effect was reversed by brain FGFR1 knockdown (p < 0.05) (Figure 6E). On the test day, the AD+Sh‐NC+ND group had significantly longer escape latency compared to the WT+Sh‐NC+ND group (p < 0.01), while MR intervention reduced the latency in AD+Sh‐NC mice (p < 0.01) (Figure 6F,H). Again, FGFR1 knockdown negated this improvement (p < 0.01) (Figure 6F,H).

Histological analysis showed nuclear condensation and loose cell arrangements in the CA1, DG, and CA3 regions of the hippocampus in the AD+Sh‐NC+ND group and the AD+Sh‐FGFR1+MR group, which were relatively mild in the WT+Sh‐NC+ND and AD+Sh‐NC+MR groups (Figure 6I; Figure S5A). Furthermore, MR significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of Psd‐95 (p < 0.01) and Bdnf (p < 0.05) in AD+Sh‐NC mice compared with those fed ND, but brain FGFR1 knockdown offset these beneficial effects, with Psd‐95 showing a marginally significant decrease (p = 0.0576) and Bdnf showing a significant reduction (p < 0.05) (Figure 6J; Figure S5B). Similar trends were observed in BDNF protein levels (Figure 6K,L; Figure S4C).

These findings demonstrate that brain FGFR1 is crucial for the cognitive benefits of late‐life MR in aged AD mice. Knocking down brain FGFR1 offsets the positive effects of late‐life MR on working memory, recognition memory, long‐term memory, and hippocampal structure and function.

Effects of brain FGFR1 on MR‐reduced Aβ deposition and neuroinflammation in aged AD mice

To determine whether brain FGFR1 knockdown affects the beneficial effects of MR on Aβ pathology and neuroinflammation in aged AD mice, we examined relevant indicators. Compared with the WT+Sh‐NC+ND group, the AD+Sh‐NC+ND group exhibited a higher Aβ‐positive area (p < 0.05) (Figure 7A,B; Figure S6A). MR intervention significantly reduced the Aβ‐positive area in AD+Sh‐NC mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 7A,B; Figure S6A). However, the positive effects of MR on Aβ pathology were reversed after brain FGFR1 knockdown (p < 0.01) (Figure 7A,B; Figure S6A). Furthermore, the mRNA expression levels of iNos (p < 0.05), Tnf‐α (p < 0.05), IL‐1β (p < 0.01), and IL‐6 (p < 0.01) were significantly upregulated in the AD+Sh‐NC+ND group compared with the WT+Sh‐NC+ND group (Figure 7C). MR treatment downregulated the mRNA expression of iNos (p < 0.01), IL‐1β (p < 0.01), and IL‐6 (p < 0.05) while upregulating IL‐10 (p < 0.05) in AD+Sh‐NC mice (Figure 7C). However, these effects were reversed following brain FGFR1 knockdown (Figure 7C).

To explore the underlying mechanism, we assessed the protein expression of NFκB. Consistent with the mRNA levels of neuroinflammatory factors, the AD+Sh‐NC+ND group showed a higher p‐NFκB/NFκB ratio compared to the WT+Sh‐NC+ND group (p < 0.01). MR significantly downregulated p‐NFκB protein expression in AD+Sh‐NC mice (p < 0.05), while FGFR1 knockdown reversed this effect (p < 0.01) (Figure 7D,E). AMPKα, which is upstream of NFκB, was activated in the AD+Sh‐NC+MR group compared to the AD+Sh‐NC+ND group (p < 0.01), and this activation was reversed by FGFR1 knockdown (p < 0.05) (Figure 7E,F). Additionally, MR significantly increased both FGFR1 protein (p < 0.05) and mRNA (p < 0.01) expression in AD+Sh‐NC mice compared to the ND‐treated AD+Sh‐NC mice (Figure 7E,G,H). In contrast, i.c.v. injection of Sh‐FGFR1 successfully blocked FGFR1 expression (p < 0.01) (Figure 7E,G,H).

These findings suggest that late‐life MR reduces Aβ deposition and neuroinflammation, at least in part, through the FGFR1/AMPKα/NFκB pathway.

Effects of brain FGFR1 on MR‐alleviated peripheral metabolism in aged AD mice

We further explored whether brain FGFR1 knockdown would influence the beneficial effects of MR on peripheral FGF21 signaling and metabolism. MR significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of key genes in the FGF21 signaling pathway, including Atf4, Pparα, and Fgf21, as well as co‐receptors β‐Klotho and Fgfr1, in the liver of AD+Sh‐NC mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 8A,B). However, brain FGFR1 knockdown had no effect on the expression of these genes (p > 0.05) (Figure 8A,B). Consistent with these findings, MR treatment significantly elevated serum FGF21 levels in AD+Sh‐NC mice (p < 0.05). Importantly, this increase was not affected by brain FGFR1 knockdown (p < 0.05) (Figure 8C), indicating that MR‐induced hepatic FGF21 production operates independently of central FGFR1 signaling.

In terms of glucose metabolism, MR reduced fasting blood glucose levels and the area under the curve of the IPGTT in AD+Sh‐NC+MR group compared to the AD+Sh‐NC+ND group (p < 0.05) (Figure 8D,E). Specifically, blood glucose levels at 0, 15, and 120 min after intraperitoneal glucose injection were significantly reduced in the AD+Sh‐NC+MR group compared to the AD+Sh‐NC+ND group (p < 0.05) (Figure 8F). However, brain FGFR1 knockdown did not significantly influence these improvements in blood glucose‐related parameters induced by MR (p > 0.05) (Figure 8D–F). Regarding lipid metabolism, MR significantly reduced liver weight (p < 0.05) and fat accumulation in the liver of AD+Sh‐NC mice (p < 0.01) (Figure 8G–I). Additionally, MR decreased serum levels of TG (p < 0.01), TC (p < 0.05) and LDL‐C (p < 0.05) in AD+Sh‐NC mice (Figure 8J), while MR had no effect on the reduced HDL‐C levels observed in AD+Sh‐NC mice (p > 0.05) (Figure 8J). Importantly, brain FGFR1 knockdown did not significantly impact the MR‐induced improvements in peripheral lipid metabolism (p > 0.05) (Figure 8D–F,J).

These findings suggest that brain FGFR1 does not interfere with the late‐life MR‐mediated improvements in peripheral metabolism in aged AD mice.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we show that late‐life MR improves peripheral metabolism while alleviating AD pathology in aged mice, including reduced Aβ burden, restored synaptic integrity, and suppressed neuroinflammation. Hippocampal FGFR1 knockdown abolishes MR‐induced neuroinflammatory improvement without affecting peripheral metabolic benefits, indicating that MR's central effects are mediated by a hepatic FGF21‐brain FGFR1 axis independent of systemic metabolic changes. Mechanistically, MR elevates hepatic FGF21, activating brain FGFR1‐AMPKα signaling to inhibit NFκB‐driven microglial inflammation, thereby promoting a homeostatic, phagocytic microglial phenotype that facilitates Aβ plaque clearance, supporting MR as a targeted, late‐life nutritional strategy for AD.

Increasing evidence underscores the significant impact of dietary patterns on cognitive function and the risk of AD. Replacing processed red meat with plant‐based protein sources, such as nuts and legumes, has been associated with a reduction in dementia risk by approximately 20%, 10 suggesting that plant‐based diets may offer substantial benefits for brain health. Methionine, predominantly found in red meat, is relatively scarce in vegetables and fruits. 27 MR, typically associated with plant‐based foods, 28 has been shown to have beneficial effects. Reduced methionine intake has been linked to improved cognitive function in individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Furthermore, MR in 4‐month‐old APP/PS1 mice improved cognition, reduced Aβ accumulation, and alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. 29 A three‐month MR regimen improved working, short‐term, and spatial memory in 15‐ and 18‐month‐old C57BL/6J mice by preserving synapses, enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, and reducing brain malondialdehyde. 30 However, the potential variations in the neuroprotective effects of late‐onset MR in APP/PS1 mice remain unclear. Consistent with previous research, our results indicated that MR enhanced cognitive function and improved synaptic plasticity and cell morphology in aged AD mice (Figure 1, Figure S1). Moreover, pro‐inflammatory dietary patterns, such as high‐fat and high‐calorie diets, have been linked to an 84% and 88% increased risk of all‐cause dementia and AD, respectively. 31 Although aging exacerbates AD pathology, 32 our study found that MR still alleviated key AD pathologies, including reduced Aβ deposition and neuroinflammation in aged AD mice (Figure 2, Figure S2). AD in the elderly involves not only Aβ pathology but also tau neurofibrillary tangles, which correlate more closely with cognitive decline and disease progression. Clinical and imaging studies have shown that tau pathology increases with age and interacts with Aβ burden to exacerbate neurodegeneration and cognitive dysfunction in older adults. 33 , 34 Therefore, while our current findings focus on MR's effects on amyloid deposition, future studies are needed to examine whether MR also influences tau pathology and the combined pathophysiological cascade in late‐life AD. Notably, although 18‐month‐old 3×Tg‐AD mice usually show impaired NOR performance versus WT, 35 we observed no significant difference between 18‐month‐old male APP/PS1 and WT mice. This is supported by another study reporting similar results in 15–18‐month‐old APP/PS1 mice. 6 These discrepancies may reflect differences between 3×Tg‐AD and APP/PS1 mice. The lack of significant NOR deficits suggests age, rather than APP/PS1 pathology, drives recognition memory impairment. These findings indicate that MR could be effective even when started late in life.

Dietary protein and essential amino acid sensing regulate FGF21; for example, MR rapidly induces hepatic FGF21 transcription, sustaining elevated serum levels in young and aged mice. 30 , 36 Consistently, our results confirmed that MR activated the hepatic ATF4/PPARα/FGF21 pathway, further upregulating serum FGF21 levels in aged AD mice (Figure 4, Figure S3). FGF21 is possibly the only FGF capable of crossing the BBB, 16 allowing it to exert its effects on the CNS. 37 Correspondingly, cortical FGF21 and its co‐receptor FGFR1, rather than β‐Klotho, were increased in aged AD mice treated with MR (Figure 5). FGFR1 serves as a crucial target for the restoration of neurogenesis and hippocampal‐related behaviors. 38 Knockdown of brain FGFR1 in aged AD mice confirmed its essential role in MR's effects on memory and hippocampal structure and function (Figure 6, Figure S4). This may be partly due to Aβ clearance and the anti‐inflammatory response mediated by FGF21 and FGFR1 activated by MR. Specifically, MR enhanced microglia‐mediated Aβ clearance, likely via the FGF21/FGFR1/AMPKα signaling axis, which suppresses NFκB‐driven inflammation and promotes a homeostatic, phagocytic microglial phenotype that facilitates plaque recognition and removal. Aβ deposition induces microgliosis, driving AD pathogenesis through the release of pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐1β, IL‐4), chemokines, and reactive oxygen species. 39 Although MR reduced Aβ burden, we observed an increase in plaque‐associated Iba1+ microglia in AD+MR mice (Figure 2). This does not necessarily indicate exacerbated neuroinflammation; rather, increased microglial clustering around plaques is often associated with enhanced Aβ engagement and clearance. 40 Indeed, MR did not further elevate pro‐inflammatory cytokines in AD mice (Figure 2), suggesting a shift toward a more plaque‐responsive, potentially protective microglial phenotype that may contribute to the reduced amyloid load. FGFR1 has been shown to be widely expressed in brain tissue, including in microglia. 17 AMPKα is downstream of FGFR1, which can be activated directly by endocrine FGF21, enabling AMPKα signaling in its target tissues. 41 The activation of AMPKα inhibits NFκB signaling, a key regulator of inflammation. 42 Recombinant human FGF21 promotes functional recovery by modulating microglia/macrophage neuroinflammation via NFκB signaling. 43 Such inflammatory states often trigger excessive ROS production, which damages proteins, lipids, and DNA and ultimately contributes to synaptic injury. 44 Studies have shown that MR‐induced FGF21 upregulates the antioxidant enzymes HO‐1 and NQO1 via transcriptional activation of Nrf2, thereby promoting cognitive improvement in aged mice. 30 Consistently, FGFR1 knockdown blocked MR‐induced FGFR1/AMPKα/NFκB activation, increasing Aβ deposition in aged AD mice (Figure 7, Figure S5) and possibly affecting oxidative stress and synaptic function. These findings suggest that late‐life MR enhances cognitive function in aged AD mice by promoting hepatic FGF21 binding to brain FGFR1, activating AMPKα/NFκB signaling, suppressing inflammation, and facilitating microglial Aβ clearance. While circulating FGF21 is likely to contribute to the observed effects, locally produced FGF21 within the brain may also play a role, 45 which we did not specifically address. Additionally, the cellular sources and mediators remain unclear, but our results still support MR's potential as an anti‐inflammatory dietary strategy in AD.

Beyond cognitive protection, MR‐activated FGF21 is well studied for regulating glucose and lipid metabolism. MR enhances overall insulin sensitivity 46 and inhibits lipid accumulation 47 in obese mice through FGF21‐dependent mechanisms. Peripheral glucose 7 and lipid metabolism 48 tend to deteriorate with age. APP/PS1 mice are particularly susceptible to aging‐induced metabolic dysregulation, 49 such as elevated glucose levels in the pancreas of AD mice. 25 Consistent with previous studies, aged WT and AD mice showed marked glucose and lipid disturbances, which MR alleviated in aged AD mice (Figure 3). While late‐life dietary, protein, or isoleucine restriction 20 , 50 improves metabolic health in aged mice, this study is the first to examine MR's effects in aged AD mice. Notably, knockdown of brain FGFR1 significantly reversed the neuroprotective effects of MR in aged AD mice (Figures 5 and 6). However, brain FGFR1 knockdown did not affect the peripheral metabolic benefits of MR in aged AD mice (Figure 7), suggesting that brain FGFR1 is not involved in this process. Nevertheless, studies indicate that AD brain pathology and peripheral metabolism influence each other and may synergistically regulate neuroinflammatory status. 6 , 51 , 52 These findings indicate that although late‐life MR enhances peripheral metabolism via FGF21, this alone does not reverse cognitive deficits in aged AD mice; effective neuroprotection requires intact FGF21 signaling through brain FGFR1. Recent evidence shows that brain insulin resistance drives AD pathogenesis, linking peripheral metabolic dysfunction to neurodegeneration, 53 highlighting the need for interventions targeting both metabolic and neurodegenerative processes.

A limitation of our study is that we focused solely on Aβ deposition and did not assess tau pathology, which is common in late‐life AD. 54 We also did not examine whether peripheral FGF21 deficiency affects cognition or neuroinflammation in aged AD mice, highlighting the need to explore links between metabolism and brain function. Future studies should employ AD models with more pronounced metabolic abnormalities and combined Aβ and tau pathology, and investigate genes relevant to late‐onset AD, such as APOE. Although previous work has established MR's metabolic and cognitive effects in WT mice and the role of FGF21, 18 , 30 we did not include a WT+MR group here to focus on AD‐specific effects, which limits the design and precludes genotype‐by‐diet analyses. Finally, because MR improved cognition in male but not female APP/PS1 mice, 29 likely due to estrogen interference, we studied only males. Future work should include aged female AD mice to assess sex‐specific responses.

In summary, late‐life MR improves systemic metabolism in aged APP/PS1 mice and provides neuroprotection via a hepatic FGF21‐brain FGFR1 axis, independent of broad metabolic changes. These results show that a diet‐based, endocrine‐targeting intervention can modify AD‐related Aβ pathology post‐onset. Importantly, the circulating FGF21‐FGFR1 pathway offers a potential route for clinical translation. Future work will assess the safety and feasibility of low‐methionine diets in older adults and explore MR‐mimetics or FGF21/FGFR1‐targeted strategies as precision‐nutrition approaches for late‐life AD.
