Economic evaluation of prenatal GBS immunization in China: A modeling analysis
Authors: Wenjing Ji, Jiaxi Du, Simon R Procter, Yingdan Cao, Hang Zhao, Mingyue Zhao, Yu Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInvasive Group B Streptococcus (GBS) infection is a fatal disease that causes a significant disease burden in young infants worldwide. GBS vaccines being developed are a potential means of preventing GBS; however, their cost-effectiveness in China is unknown. Using a decision analysis model, we simulated the cost-effectiveness of a GBS vaccine for pregnant women compared with no intervention over the lifetime of their newborns from the Chinese healthcare system perspective. We estimate the incremental cost–effectiveness ratio (ICER) and analyzed the impact of uncertainties in key parameters through deterministic sensitivity analysis and probabilistic sensitivity analysis. The vaccine could reduce GBS cases by 49% and yield an ICER of $25,267 per QALY. Based on WTP of 0.5x, 1x, 1.5x, and 3x GDP per capita in 2021, the cost-effective vaccine price would be $23, $37, $51, and $92 per dose, respectively. The results were most sensitive to the cost of the vaccine, GBS incidence, and vaccine efficacy. When the vaccine price is $65, and the vaccine efficacy is 80%, 90% of iterations fell under the $37,663 threshold. When the vaccine price changed to $33, the vaccine is cost-effective under $12,554 (1xGDP per capita) and all iterations fell under $37,663. GBS vaccination is likely to be cost-effective in China at WTP thresholds of $37,663 (3xGDP per capita). Enhancing vaccine efficacy and lowering costs further improve cost-effectiveness. A budget impact analysis will be crucial upon licensure.KEYWORDS: Group B Streptococcus, GBS vaccine, cost-effectiveness analysis, China

Introduction

Globally, invasive Group B Streptococcus (GBS) leads to neonatal sepsis, meningitis, pneumonia, and long-term neurological impairment, and is a major contributor to neonatal death.1 Group B streptococcal infections occurring within the first 7 d of life are defined as early-onset disease (EOD) and those occurring between 7 d and 89 d after birth are defined as late-onset disease (LOD).1 Estimates demonstrated that GBS causes a large disease burden in East and Southeast Asia, with 45,700 EOD cases (95% Posterior Intervals (PI): 21,600–92,900); 22,600 LOD cases (95% PI: 5,700–68,200); and 9,700 infant deaths annually (95% PI: 4,200–22,600).2 A previous meta-analysis showed that the case-fatality rate of GBS infection in China is 5% (95% Confidence Interval (CI): 3–6%).3

China contains around one-fifth of the world’s population. Prevention and treatment of invasive infections, including those caused by GBS, have a pivotal role in reducing the rate of neonatal mortality and neurological impairment in China, which is one of the key goals of Sustainable Development Goals 2030. As an important step for GBS disease prevention in China, the Chinese Medical Association published an expert consensus on the prevention of perinatal Group B Streptococcal disease (China) in 2021.4 This consensus recommended a protocol for intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) use. Its publication may promote the implementation of antenatal GBS screening and IAP. However, it will be a challenge at secondary hospitals and primary care institutions due to the lack of adequate laboratory facilities for GBS culture and identification of invasive GBS. In addition, IAP does not prevent LOD, and it may affect infants’ microbiota, which may lead to allergies, asthma, and obesity.5

GBS maternal vaccine protects newborns through placental antibody transfer during pregnancy.6 This approach could protect against both EOD and LOD while avoiding the risks of antibiotic resistance and microbiota disruption. Developing and licensing safe, effective, and affordable GBS vaccines has been identified by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a priority.7 Polysaccharide–protein conjugate vaccines and the protein subunit vaccines are the two main forms of GBS vaccines in development.8 Although no maternal vaccine against GBS is currently available, a phase 1/2 randomized controlled trial (NCT03170609) conducted in America has shown that a GBS hexavalent vaccine (GBS6) has excellent safety and immunogenicity.9 The Group B Streptococcal vaccine candidate (GBS6: PF-06760805) was granted PRIME designation by the European Medicines Agency’s Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) in April 2022 and breakthrough therapy designation by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in September 2022.10 Due to the limitations of low incidence, vaccine approval may require alternative approaches to tranditional phase III clinical trials. One such approach was implemented in an observational case–control study (NCT02215226), which examined serotype IgG thresholds associated with reduced infant GBS disease risk.11 These findings could provide supporting evidence for licensure of a polysaccharide–protein conjugate vaccine. WHO highlights cost-effectiveness as a crucial factor in decision-making for vaccine inclusion in national programs.7 Previous studies in several countries have already shown that a maternal GBS vaccine could be cost-effective in these settings.12–18 Previously, we conducted the first national multi-center population-based study in China to investigate the epidemiology of neonatal invasive GBS disease.19 To our knowledge, no cost-effectiveness analysis of GBS vaccine has been conducted in China. The evaluation of the potential cost and effectiveness of the GBS vaccine is crucial for Chinese policymakers and funders; it will also provide evidence that might inform future decisions on the inclusion of the GBS vaccine in the national immunization program (NIP) and provide evidence for setting GBS vaccine prices. This study, therefore, aims to carry out a cost-effectiveness analysis of the GBS maternal vaccine in China.

Materials and methods

Study overview

We constructed a decision tree model embedded with Markov nodes using Treeage pro 2019 (Treeage Inc, Williamstown, MA, USA). Markov nodes refer to the different health states in the model, such as GBS-related diseases or death, through which individuals transition over time. In order to assess the cost-effectiveness of the GBS hexavalent vaccine, the model captures GBS-related events including sepsis, meningitis, pneumonia, and long-term health outcomes, following the cohort of newborns in China in 2021 (10.62 million newborns) through their lifetime. The study took a healthcare system point of view and reported findings at both the national level and for each of the 31 provinces in mainland China. We reported our results according to Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 (CHEERS 2022) (See supplementary material Appendix.7).20

Model design and disease parameter inputs

Markov models can simulate health events related to GBS based on the natural course of GBS disease. We evaluated the benefit for the annual birth cohort under two strategies: maternal vaccine without IAP versus no maternal vaccine without IAP. The model defined eight health states based on GBS disease natural history, in addition to meningitis, sepsis, and pneumonia, long-term neurological impairment brought on by GBS was also included in the model (Described in detail in supplementary material Appendix.1). A 1-y cycle length was used for each Markov state. Table 1 lists all the base case values, range, and distribution of parameters.

We derived the incidence of GBS, GBS EOD, and LOD from the only multi-center prospective and retrospective study conducted in China.19 The rates of meningitis and sepsis due to early and late-onset GBS were based on a global meta-analysis.1 Meningitis and sepsis sequelae rates were also acquired from a global estimate.21 Estimates of GBS-related meningitis, pneumonia, and sepsis mortalities were obtained from a previous cost–effectiveness study because these values deemed appropriate for China based on availability.17 Background age-specific mortality was obtained from the China Population & Employment Statistics Yearbook in 2021 (Appendix.4).

Costs, outcomes, and discounting

The costs of meningitis, sepsis, and pneumonia were estimated using original studies conducted in different provinces in China, to account for regional differences in China, we adjusted costs using GDP per capita by province and national GDP per capita.34 All costs are adjusted to 2021 current values and converted to USD using an exchange rate of 6.45 obtained from World Bank Data35(See Appendix.5). Only direct medical costs were considered. The cost of pneumonia, meningitis and sepsis with similar treatments is used as alternatives when the cost of GBS-specific health outcomes are unavailable.22,23,25

We used quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) as the measure of health outcome. To estimate QALYs we used utility values for the different health states based on results measured by the health utility measurement tool Health Utilities Index (HUI) or EuroQol 5-Dimension (EQ-5D) that widely used to quantify the health-related quality of life.13,28,29 Alternatively, we used utility values based on published cost-effectiveness analyses. We assume the utility value of 1 for healthy state and 0 for death. In the QALYs estimation, no age-weighting was applied (i.e., all life-years were valued equally regardless of the individuals’ age in the model). By dividing the difference in total cost by the difference in total QALY gained, we calculated the incremental cost–effectiveness ratio (ICER). An annual discount rate of 5% was used for both cost and health outcomes.

We applied $12,554.42 (1xGDP per capita in China in 2021) per QALY gained as the base case threshold, as recommended by the Chinese pharmacoeconomic evaluation guidelines (2020). And we also did the analysis under 0.5xGDP per capita, 1.5xGDP per capita, and 3xGDP per capita according to latest empirical studies.32,33

Vaccine

For developing countries like China, to our best knowledge, there is no evidence to illustrate the actual impact of IAP on GBS after its use. All serotypes leading to GBS in China would be covered if hexavalent vaccines were licensed.19 So, we considered immunization of pregnant women with a hexavalent vaccine without IAP compared with no intervention. We assume for each pregnancy, a pregnant woman is assumed to receive a single dose of vaccine.36

We used 61.6% for vaccine uptake rate, which was based on findings from a previous cross-sectional study on women’s attitudes and intentions toward the GBS vaccine.31 The cost of delivering maternal immunization consists of vaccine cost per dose ($65.12), vaccine storage and transport cost ($2.02), and vaccine administration cost ($3.41). We assumed that the GBS vaccine would be delivered in a manner similar to the flu vaccine considering similar delivery mechanisms and costs.27 Since the GBS6 vaccine is not yet on the market, based on their similarities in development principles, we used the price of the ACYW135 meningococcal polysaccharide conjugate vaccine (CRM197 vector) instead,26 in order to account for the differences between GBS vaccine post-marketing price and assumed price, we also performed a price threshold analysis. Though preliminary immunogenicity data show that the vaccine may have a great impact on the reduction of GBS infection, the exact efficacy remains unknown due to no phase III clinical trial being available. We assumed 80% vaccine efficacy (an 80% reduction in infection) according to the WHO preferred product profile7 for a GBS vaccine and performed sensitivity analysis using an efficacy of 70%–90%.

Sensitivity analysis

Deterministic sensitivity analysis (one-way and two-way) and probabilistic sensitivity analysis were conducted to assess the extent to which uncertainty of the parameters affect the results. One/two-way sensitivity analysis was conducted by varying 1 or 2 parameters while keeping other parameters consistent with the base case. We used 95% CIs as the uncertainty range where possible, if 95% CIs were not available, we used either the interquartile range or a ± 20% range around base-case values (Table 1). A tornado diagram was drawn to show the parameters to which the results were sensitive. We did the probabilistic sensitivity analysis using a Monte-Carlo simulation of 10,000 iterations, varying parameters by sampling them from appropriate distributions (Table 1). The results of the sensitivity analysis were used to calculate a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve.

Results

Base-case analysis

Without maternal vaccination, we estimate that 3,292 infants in China will develop GBS disease each year (1911 EOD, 1381 LOD). This estimate reflects current epidemiology and does not account for future population growth. Compared with no intervention, the vaccination would reduce GBS cases by 49.28%, assuming base-case vaccine uptake and efficacy. Compared with no immunization, the total annual cost of the vaccine program is estimated at $749,241,000, which comprises vaccine cost, transportation cost, storage cost, and administration cost. The vaccination strategy would incur an additional cost of $34 per vaccinated person. From a healthcare perspective, the incremental cost per QALY gained is estimated at $25,267.42. At the provincial level, GBS6 vaccine is cost-effective in Beijing and Shanghai at 1x GDP per capita. It is also cost-effective in Jiangsu, Fujian, Tianjin and Zhejiang at 1.5x GDP per capita. GBS vaccine is not cost-effective even at a threshold of 3x the provincial GDP per capita in Hebei, Guizhou, Guangxi, Heilongjiang and Gansu (See Figure 1).

One-way sensitivity analyses

By conducting the one-way sensitivity analysis, we assessed the impact of parameter uncertainty around the base case assumptions ($65.12/dose, 80% efficacy, 61.6% vaccine uptake rate). The tornado diagram (Figure 2) summarizes highly influential parameters. The most influential parameters were the vaccine price, GBS incidence, and vaccine efficacy. Lower vaccine prices substantially decrease in the ICER. Vaccine uptake rate has no impact on the ICER, since delivery costs are assumed to apply only to women who receive the vaccine.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first cost-effectiveness analysis of a GBS maternal vaccine conducted in China. We found that compared to no maternal immunization, the GBS vaccine result in an ICER of $25,267.42 per QALY gained. This finding was most sensitive to the price of the vaccine, and a lower price of the vaccine would make the GBS vaccine more likely to be cost-effective. At the same time, sensitivity analysis shows that GBS vaccine efficacy for preventing GBS disease also has a significant impact on cost-effectiveness, underscoring the need for highly efficacious vaccines.

The Monte Carlo simulations suggest that if the vaccine price changed to $32.56, 100% of iterations fell under the WTP of $37,663.26. However, the latest empirical studies shown that a WTP of 3xGDP per capita might be too high for Chinese population.32,33 According to Cai et al.32 who used 1.5xGDP per capita as a threshold, the vaccine would likely become cost-effective only if its price falls below $44.03.

A previous study conducted in the USA in 201713 showed that under 70% vaccine efficacy and a total vaccine cost of $151.74/dose, maternal GBS vaccination alone versus no prevention would cost $56,609 per QALY. The result is comparable to our study, when we use the same vaccine efficacy and price as the USA study, the ICER was estimated at $65,007.36 per QALY. The main difference between the two studies is that we did not include the potential prevention effect of a GBS vaccine against preterm and stillbirth in Chinese settings because of the lack of data on these outcomes.

For the Chinese birth cohort in 2021, vaccination would yield 14,290.34 QALYs compared to no vaccination, additionally costing $361,080,000. Our study can provide a reference for other countries in Asia. Currently, some progress has been made in the epidemiology of GBS disease, further studies in countries like Japan37 and Singapore38 would be valuable for regional policy decisions. The results of this study indicate that vaccine efficacy rates are an important sensitive parameter, but there is no GBS vaccine available all over the world. Licensure of candidate vaccines has some obvious challenges. If the incidence of GBS is low, conducting placebo-controlled trials would require a large sample size. Specifically, over 122,000 patients would be needed to validate a vaccine with 80% efficacy at an incidence of 0.5/1000.39 In the future, it is important to develop a global GBS vaccine development strategy that promotes other possible pathways beyond clinical trials to help bring vaccines to market. Possible approaches include the animal models and defining serological protection as surrogate endpoints for accelerated regulatory approval.40

Our study also has implications for the future inclusion of GBS vaccine in China’s NIP. In the future, the cost-effectiveness of the inclusion of GBS vaccines in the NIP could be further investigated. After the GBS vaccine is licensed, pilot programs select regions could improve vaccine uptake and implementation.

It is worth noticing that the GBS vaccine is not cost-effective even when the threshold reaches three times the provincial GDP per capita in Hebei, Guizhou, Guangxi, Heilongjiang, and Gansu. GBS vaccine coverage in these regions should be prioritized once vaccines become available.

Our study has some limitations. Data on the incidence of GBS in China were obtained from a multi-center study in which the participating hospitals were all tertiary urban hospitals. The diversity of healthcare settings likely led to underreporting and underestimation of vaccine cost-effectiveness. Besides, the analysis was performed without capturing the co-morbidity of GBS sepsis, meningitis, and pneumonia. Due to the world-universal scarcity of data on long-term neurological development impairment and the major data gap of health utilities based on the Chinese population, some epidemiological data and health utility values were proxied using data from other countries, and while it is the reliable estimates available, it has risks of inaccuracy. Recent studies in Denmark, the Netherlands, South India, Mozambique, and South Africa have filled the data gaps on the outcome of GBS neurological disorders,41–44 and when feasible, similar studies should be conducted in China to gather local data and further inform policy decisions. Considering that the vaccine characteristics are not available, we assume every woman would be offered a 1 dose GBS vaccine during each pregnancy; however, in China, there may be cases where a mother gives birth to more than one child within a relatively close number of years. If the protection of the vaccine is lifelong, pregnant women may not need to be vaccinated for every pregnancy. We also did not consider the possibility of multiple doses of vaccine, which may increase the effectiveness of protection but also the corresponding cost. In addition, if vaccine hesitancy is addressed through public education campaigns, leading to a higher vaccine uptake rate, this would affect both effectiveness and economic outcome. Adverse vaccine reactions and the corresponding management costs were not included in the study owing to severe systemic adverse reactions that are rare and thus not modeled here.9 It is worth noting that we did not assess the positive effect of vaccination on long-term patient productivity and savings in special education expenditures for children with neurodevelopmental disorders because the study perspective was from a healthcare perspective. These societal benefits, though excluded, could further improve the vaccine’s cost-effectiveness if considered.

Conclusions

In conclusion, maternal vaccination against GBS may be an effective strategy that could reduce GBS burden and be a cost-effective intervention in China compared to no vaccine, assuming that the vaccine is affordable and accessible. Further research to address data gaps on the long-term health and economic consequences of invasive GBS disease in China, could enhance the precision and relevance of future cost-effectiveness analyses. Coverage of GBS vaccine in economically disadvantaged regions such as Gansu, Heilongjiang, Hebei, Guangxi and Guizhou will be a key issue in the future, and attention should be paid to conducting targeted surveillance and implementation studies in these regions.
