Delay in malaria care-seeking for children under five in Africa: a systematic review
Authors: Mustapha Amoadu, Amidu Alhassan, Edward Odoom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDelays in seeking appropriate treatment for malaria in children under five remain a major contributor to poor outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa, where multiple socioeconomic, geographic, and health-system factors influence when care is accessed. This review synthesised current evidence on the prevalence and determinants of delayed malaria care-seeking, as well as the associated outcomes among children under five in sub-Saharan Africa.MethodsThis systematic review followed the guidelines by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI). This review was reported in accordance with following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).ResultThe prevalence of delayed malaria care-seeking among children under five ranges from 2% to 95.8%. Factors associated with these delays include financial constraints, long travel distances, reliance on traditional treatments, and the perception of malaria as a mild illness. Additionally, health workers’ attitudes and shortages of essential health facility logistics further hinder timely care-seeking by caregivers. These delays are associated with increased mortality, progression to severe malaria, and serious neurological and haematological complications.ConclusionTimely access to effective malaria treatment for young children can be enhanced by interventions that address financial barriers, improve access to care, and strengthen health-system readiness to respond promptly.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05868-2.Keywords: Malaria, Caregivers, Children under five, Delay, Care seeking

Introduction

Malaria remains one of the most pressing public health challenges in developing economies, particularly affecting children under 5 years old [1]. Malaria in young children remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality [2]. In 2023, an estimated 263 million malaria cases and 597,000 malaria-related deaths occurred across 83 countries worldwide [3]. In 2023, children under five accounted for 76% of all malaria deaths globally [4]. In regions like sub-Saharan Africa, where malaria is endemic, malaria-related mortality rates for children under five remain unacceptably high [5, 6]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO, 2021), children under 5 years of age account for approximately 80% of all malaria-related deaths in the African region [7]. These high mortality rates are associated with delays in accessing appropriate medical treatment [6]. Thus, in developing countries, delays in seeking timely medical care for malaria continue to pose a critical challenge [5].

Despite the availability of preventive measures such as Artemisinin-Based Combination Therapies (ACTs) [8], delays in seeking timely medical care for malaria continue to be a critical issue. These delays are associated with the progression of malaria to severe forms, significantly increasing the likelihood of death or long-term health complications. Malaria in young children is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality [2], with a complex interplay of socio-economic, cultural, and geographical factors that prevent caregivers from seeking prompt medical attention [9]. These challenges highlight the importance of understanding the timing of care-seeking behaviour, as delays in accessing appropriate treatment remain a key determinant of malaria severity and outcomes among young children.

Early care-seeking is essential for reducing the severity of malaria and improving health outcomes in children under five. The WHO strongly recommends that malaria should be treated within 24 h of symptom onset to prevent the disease from worsening [10]. Evidence from a systematic review shows that among adults with malaria, delayed care-seeking is associated with increase disease severity. Patients who seek care more than 24 h after symptom onset are more likely to develop severe malaria compared with those who seek care earlier [11]. Furthermore, early intervention can prevent the development of severe and cerebral malaria, which are among the most serious complications associated with delayed treatment [12].

Several socio-economic and cultural factors contribute to the delay in seeking care for malaria. Economic barriers such as poverty [13], out-of-pocket costs for healthcare [14], and inadequate health insurance coverage prevent caregivers from accessing timely medical treatment [15]. The WHO estimates that in low-income countries, over 50% of households with malaria cases face catastrophic health expenditures, which significantly deters them from seeking treatment [16]. In addition, geographic isolation, particularly in rural areas, makes healthcare facilities inaccessible due to long distances and the lack of transportation options [17], further exacerbating delays in care-seeking. These initial delays in seeking treatment significantly increase the chances of malaria progressing to severe or complicated malaria, leading to higher mortality rates in children under five.

While the prevalence of malaria and the impact of delayed care-seeking are well-documented [11, 18, 19], there remains a significant gap in understanding the specific factors that contribute to delayed care-seeking among children under five in Africa. Although several studies have examined malaria burden and care-seeking behaviour [20, 21], the evidence on specific barriers to timely care-seeking among caregivers of children under five remains fragmented across regions, particularly in rural and underserved settings. In addition, the absence of region-specific data on the outcomes of delayed care-seeking may limit the ability of health systems to implement effective, targeted interventions. This knowledge gap prevents the development of strategies tailored to address the unique challenges faced by caregivers in the region. This gap may exacerbates the burden of malaria and impedes progress in reducing child mortality if not well addressed. However, addressing delayed care-seeking requires more than acknowledging its existence. It demands a comprehensive synthesis of evidence on context-specific determinants that shape caregivers’ actions in Africa. This review bridges this gap by consolidating existing studies to provide practical insights for designing timely, locally relevant interventions to reduce malaria-related child deaths. Furthermore, without a deeper understanding of how delays impact the progression of the disease, interventions will remain disjointed and insufficient, preventing meaningful reductions in malaria mortality rates.

This systematic review examined the rate and determinants of delayed malaria care-seeking among children under five in Africa. This review is guided by the three-delay model framework [22]. The three-delay model argues that caregivers face sequential barriers in accessing timely healthcare. The first is delays in deciding to seek care, influenced by knowledge, beliefs, and perceived severity. The second is delays in reaching a healthcare facility, due to geographic, transportation, and cost barrier. The third is delays in receiving appropriate care upon arrival, stemming from health system constraints such as staff shortages and logistical deficiencies. These delays compound risk, prolong the disease course, and increase the likelihood of severe malaria and child mortality. Evidence from this review provides a robust foundation for formulating policies and interventions that address the real-world barriers preventing caregivers from accessing timely treatment. In doing so, the review directly contribute to Sustainable Development Goal 3.2, which seeks to end preventable deaths of newborns and children under 5 years of age by 2030 through equitable access to quality healthcare. Beyond its contribution to global malaria control, this review has generated context-specific insights that strengthen health systems in resource-limited settings and help reduce preventable deaths among children under five.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in line with the guidelines recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and the principles outlined by Aromataris et al. [23]. The guidelines for the review process are: defined research question, the establishment of inclusion and exclusion criteria, a comprehensive search strategy, quality appraisal of the selected studies, data extraction, thematic analysis, and transparent reporting of the methods undertaken. Additionally, this review was reported in accordance with following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [24]. The protocol for this review is registered in open science framework with the 10.17605/OSF.IO/64QRW.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria• Studies conducted on children under five diagnosed with malaria in Africa• Studies focusing on caregivers seeking care for children with malaria• Studies investigating the rate of delay in seeking malaria care for children under five• Studies exploring the determinants or risk factors of delayed malaria care-seeking for children under five (e.g., socio-economic, cultural, healthcare system-related)• Studies analysing the outcomes of delayed care-seeking, such as severity, mortality, and long-term health consequences• Studies conducted in developing economies (facility-based, community-based, rural, or urban settings)• Studies published in peer-reviewed journal articles, theses, and dissertations• Studies published in English, between 2000 and 2025• Studies published between 2000 and 2025• Studies that used qualitative, quantitative and mixed-study designsExclusion criteria• Studies conducted on populations outside children under five• Studies not focusing on malaria care-seeking delays in children under five• Studies not providing primary data on delayed malaria care-seeking, editorials, commentaries, pre-prints, reviews, opinions, and letters to editors• Studies focused solely on other diseases or those without disaggregated data on malaria care-seeking• Studies conducted with participants from developing countries living in Africa• Studies that do not examine the health outcomes associated with delayed care-seeking• Studies conducted outside Africa• Editorials, commentaries, pre-prints, reviews, opinions, and letters to editors

Comprehensive search strategy

The search for relevant studies was conducted across five main databases (PubMed Central, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, JSTOR, and Embase). A search strategy was developed with support from a Chartered Librarian. The search strategy was developed using a combination of free-text keywords, MeSH terms, and Boolean operators to capture relevant studies on delayed malaria care-seeking in children under five in developing economies. Boolean operators AND and OR were used to combine these terms and refine the search. The strategy was modified and applied to other databases such as Scopus, Web of Science, JSTOR and PubMed. Additional hand searches were conducted in other relevant sources like CINAHL, WHO reports, government publications, and grey literature databases [Google Scholar, Dimensions AI, ProQuest, and ResearchGate]. The reference lists of the retrieved full-text papers were also manually searched to identify additional studies. The last search was conducted on June September 30, 2025. Study selection

The study selection process was carried out in three -steps approach. Initially, all retrieved records were imported into Mendeley reference manager, where duplicates were identified and removed. In the next step, a team of 24 trained graduate students, under the guidance of MA screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining records using the eligibility criteria. Full-text articles were then retrieved for studies that met the initial screening criteria. The reference lists of these full-text eligible records were checked to identify additional relevant full-text records studies that may have been missed in the primary search. Finally, two authors (AA and EO) independently screened the full-text articles against the eligibility criteria, with discrepancies resolved by MA through discussion. This comprehensive and transparent process minimised selection bias, ensuring the integrity and robustness of the review.

Appraisal of the included studies

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the JBI [25]. The JBI tools provide checklists for different study designs, including qualitative research, cross-sectional studies, quasi-experiment and randomised controlled trials. Additionally, the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) was used for assessing mixed-methods studies [26]. The grading of the studies followed the framework established existing studies by [27]. For example, the JBI critical appraisal to for analytical cross-sectional studies contains 8 items. Studies were rated high quality (7–8), moderate quality (4–6), and low quality (0–3). The goal of this appraisal was to assess the rigor of all studies, rather than to exclude any full-text articles. The appraisal was carried out independently by two reviewers, with oversight from senior researchers to ensure consistency and objectivity. A detailed overview of the appraised studies is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Data extraction

A standardised data extraction form was designed for this review to ensure consistency and accuracy in capturing key information from the included studies. Data extracted include author (s), county, year of publication, design, sample size, and population), as well as the rate and determinants of delayed care-seeking for malaria additionally, health outcomes such as the severity of malaria, mortality rates, disease progression, and long-term health consequences were recorded. To ensure consistency and reduce bias, two authors (AA and EO) independently extracted data and inconsistencies and disagreements were resolved by MA through discussions during regular meeting by the review team. The extracted information was further reviewed to ensure completeness and relevance to the study objectives. See Supplementary File 2 for extracted data.

Data analysis

Qualitative synthesis was undertaken using a thematic analysis approach, guided deductively by the three-delay model as the analytical framework. Rather than deriving themes inductively from the data, reviewer familiarisation with extracted content was followed by applying pre-established, theory-informed coding categories aligned with the model (delay in deciding to seek care, delay in reaching care, and delay in receiving appropriate treatment). Data segments related to determinants of delay, caregiver behaviours, and health-system challenges were mapped onto these categories. The research team engaged in collaborative discussions to ensure that theme allocation remained conceptually grounded in the theoretical framework. A thematic structure was developed to demonstrate how the identified factors fit into the model’s domains and contribute to delayed malaria care-seeking among children under five in developing economies. A convergent qualitative synthesis approach, based on Hong et al. [28], was used to combine findings from studies of diverse designs. The final synthesis was organised to offer a thorough summary of the evidence, emphasising key insights, identifying gaps, and exploring implications for policy and practice.

Searches conducted in these seven main databases produced 9769 records. Additional 39 records were retrieved from a hand-search in other databases making a total of 9808 records. These records were exported into the Mendeley referencing Software and 316 duplicates were removed bringing the number of unique records to 9492. Following this, the titles and abstracts of The 9492 records were screened, and 86 full-text eligible records were retrieved after the removal of 9406 records. The authors also searched the reference lists of the included studies, and this produced an additional seven (7) records. Findings

Among the 92 included studies, 47 were rated as having a low risk of bias, 36 as moderate risk, and 9 as high risk. All the nine studies rated as high risk were all cross-sectional studies [30–38]. All the 72 cross-sectional studies defined inclusion criteria of sample and detailed description of subject setting. The most frequently missed items were measurement of validity and reliability of measures. All nine included qualitative studies demonstrated low risk of bias, each achieving a perfect score of 10 on the JBI qualitative appraisal tool. All case-control studies were rated as low risk of bias and demonstrated strong methodological rigour. However, seven out of eight of the case control studies did not clearly report whether cases and controls were appropriately matched. All the three mixed method studies were rated as of low risk [39–41]. The cohort study was of low risk [42] but failed to account for follow up losses and how it was accounted for. The single longitudinal study was rated to have a moderate risk [43]. See details of appraisal results in Supplementary File 1.

Delayed care-seeking beyond 24 h

Forty (40) of the included studies reported delayed care-seeking beyond 24 h from onset of symptoms. The lowest delay was observed in DR Congo (6.7%) [44], while the highest was seen in Cameroon (95.8%) [45]. Studies with sample sizes ≥ 2000 reported mid-range prevalence [29, 46–50], while much higher prevalence of delays were mostly reported in studies with < 1000 participants [45, 51–53]. Only one longitudinal study [43], conducted in Zambia, reported a 58% delay prevalence. See details in Table 3.

Prevalence of care givers delay (after 24 h) in seeking care for children under five

Across the African Union Regional Coordinating Centre (RCC), delays in seeking malaria care for children under five show wide sub-regional differences. In the West Africa RCC area, delay prevalence ranged from 8.1% in Ghana [32] to 87.8% in Benin [53]. In the Eastern Africa RCC region, the burden was generally higher, with delays spanning from 23% in Ethiopia [54] to 86% in Uganda [51]. The Southern Africa RCC region also demonstrated notable delays, ranging from 16% in Malawi [55] to 86.1% in Zambia [52]. Central Africa RCC reported both the lowest and highest delay figures across all regions, from 6.7% in DR Congo [44] to 95.8% in Cameroon [45], indicating extreme disparities in timely access to treatment.

Delayed care-seeking beyond 48 h were reported by 17 studies and prevalence ranged from 2% in Burkina Faso [40] to 75.6% in both Nigeria [56] and Guinea [57]. Determinant of delay of seeking treatment for children under five with malaria

Delays in seeking care for children under 5 years with malaria identified three main themes, following the three delays framework [22]. This comprises of delay in decision to seek care, Delay in reaching care and delay in receiving adequate healthcare.

Delay in decision to seek care

Under this theme, two main sub-themes were deduced from the reported evidence.

Socio-economic and demographic factors

Low socioeconomic status of caregivers [30, 31, 34, 47, 52, 53, 60, 64, 65, 68, 89–93], low family wealth [29, 94, 95], large household size [40, 69, 79, 96] and caregiver who are unemployed [79] or working in the informal sector [97] were reported to be associated with delayed care-seeking. Caregiver’s age, whether young (≤ 30 years) [97] or older (> 30 years) [54, 67] have been reported to be associated with delayed care seeking. Caregivers with inadequate funds to pay healthcare cost [39, 47, 50, 60, 65, 68, 76, 96, 98, 99] or without health insurance [35, 74, 81, 95, 100] were reported to delay health seeking. Also, being a farmer [101], a female caregiver [92, 94, 95] or having a female child [79, 92], being a housewife [67, 102], a widower [45], cohabiting [61], and living with non-biological parents [69], have been reported to be associated with delayed care-seeking. Some caregivers delayed seeking care because they required permission from their parents or husbands [33, 38, 54, 67, 76, 84].

Knowledge, attitudes and perception

Perceiving the illness as not severe [30, 36, 39, 51, 57, 68, 74–76, 78, 84, 91, 97, 99, 101] or mild [75, 79] and perceived high cost of treatment [35, 39, 77, 84, 93, 103] were reported to discourage early decision to seek healthcare. Cultural and religious practices [30, 60, 76, 83, 90, 96], as well as preference for herbal treatment [34, 37, 74, 104, 105], also contributed to delays because caregivers often resorted to traditional remedies first. caregivers from seeking prompt formal care. Additionally, high perceived barriers to treatment-seeking [63], and fear of being misdiagnosed [103] prolonged decision to seek healthcare by caregivers. Caregivers frequently engaged in home management of malaria symptoms [35, 37, 38, 47, 48, 50–52, 74, 92, 95, 104] or sought initial treatment from pharmacies or drug shops before visiting a health facility [38, 51, 104, 106, 107], which prolonged decision to visit health facilities. Inadequate malaria knowledge [31, 37, 41, 50, 52, 54, 77, 90, 92, 96, 107] further influenced care-seeking decisions, while mistrust of treatment and concerns about side effects [30, 54, 92, 100, 108] may prolonged decision to seek early care.

Delay in reaching care

This theme also featured two sub themes which contributed to the delay in reaching care, namely distance and location barriers, transportation barriers.

Distance and location barriers

Remoteness [60] or long distances to health facilities [30, 37, 39, 40, 64, 68, 76, 84, 96, 108, 109] were reported as factors that delay caregivers in reaching care. For example, caregivers living more than 3 km or 5 km away from a health facility commonly postponed or avoided formal care [47, 52, 69, 77, 91, 92]. Also Residing in rural areas [35, 74, 88, 90, 96] or poorly serviced regions [72] also limited immediate access.

Transportation and logistics barriers

Transportation barriers included unavailability of transport, lack of accessible transportation options, and high transport costs [30, 93, 97, 100]. Lockdowns during the COVID-19 Pandemic, suspension of public transport and increased transport cost during crises further exacerbated delays [110]. The time required to locate transportation may also lengthened delays in reaching care [33, 109].

Delay in receiving adequate healthcare

Three sub-themes were identified, namely availability and supply issues, facility process and service quality issues, and point-of-care financial barriers.

Availability and supply issues

Stock-outs of antimalarial drugs [111], shortages of medications [112], lack of diagnostic capacity [112], absence of health workers [106, 109] and limited number health workers [109, 112] contributed to delays after caregivers reached facilities. Low coverage of health facilities in certain areas further compounded these delays [105].

Facility process and service quality issues

Long queues [112], prolonged waiting times [109, 112], procedural runarounds [109], and poor facility performance[105] are associated with delays in receiving care. Mistreatment [112], lack of respect [94], and inadequate attention[109] from health professionals were also documented as barriers to timely treatment.

Point-of-care financial barriers

Even after reaching a facility, high expenditure on treatment bills [99, 105, 110], inability to pay bills[99], and unexpected costs [110] slowed down the receipt of adequate care. These financial obstacles may force some caregivers to delay or interrupt treatment. See Table 5 for details in the risk factors for delayed healthcare seeking.

Risk factors for delayed health seeking for malaria for children under five

Outcomes in delayed of seeking care for under five children with malaria

Delayed malaria care-seeking among children under five is associated with several adverse outcomes, including mortality, morbidity, inappropriate treatment, and increased burden on healthcare systems.

Mortality related issues

Included studies consistently reported that delayed care seeking for malaria after the onset of symptoms is associated with increased mortality [29, 31, 33, 43, 58, 60, 61, 67, 83, 84, 86, 90, 92, 100, 109, 114], contributing to preventable deaths [87, 111]. Delay also worsens survival odds [115]. For those experiencing complications, the case-fatality rate has been observed to be up to 25% [51, 54].

Morbidity illness

Delayed health seeking is associated with higher morbidity [86, 106] and contributes to an increased malaria burden [78]. These effects manifest as prolonged illness [58, 65, 68, 81], which may include prolonged fever [65, 97, 107] and severe malaria cases among children under five [32, 35, 37–39, 49, 56, 57, 60, 61, 67, 73, 77, 81, 84, 87, 91, 92, 94, 101, 110, 114–116]. Other associated adverse outcomes include poor prognosis [79] and progression to dehydration [94] and jaundice [73]. The delayed management of illness can also lead to severe neurological and haematological complications. For examples, cerebral malaria [73] is a documented outcome, alongside other neurological events such as convulsions [65, 107] and unconsciousness [107]. Longer-term consequences include cognitive impairment [29], paralysis and speech loss [109]. Haematological outcomes are anaemia [29, 60, 61] often necessitating the need for blood transfusion [61].

Inappropriate treatment and health-system burden

The delay in seeking care introduces significant challenges for diagnosis and treatment efficacy, thereby increasing the health-system burden. Delayed action results in late diagnosis [68, 87] and missed early treatment opportunities [32], leading to suboptimal access to prompt care [89] and an increased risk of untreated malaria [84]. Furthermore, delayed presentation is associated with inappropriate treatment [82, 101], including overtreatment [101] and irrational drug use [78]. These practices increase the risk of drug resistance [101] and may reduce the uptake of ACT [67] During these delays, there is an increased use of informal treatment sources [88]. The operational consequences for health facilities include higher hospitalisation rates [49, 57] and prolonged hospitalisation [61, 106], resulting in delayed recovery [97]. Collectively, these issues impose an increased financial burden [110]. See Table 6 for details.

Discussion

Summary of findings

Delayed care-seeking for malaria among children under five is widespread in sub-Saharan Africa, with delays beyond 24 h ranging from 6.7% in DR Congo to 95.8% in Cameroon, and delays beyond 48 h ranging from as low as 2% in Burkina Faso to 75.6% in Nigeria and Guinea. These delays arise from a combination of decision-making challenges, barriers to reaching care, and constraints within the health system. Low socioeconomic status, lack of health insurance, inadequate funds, cultural norms, poor malaria knowledge, and reliance on home or informal treatment commonly slow initial decisions to seek care. Geographic disadvantages such as rural residence, remoteness and high transportation costs further prolong access, while health-system limitations including drug shortages, long queues, and poor service quality delay treatment even after arrival. These barriers increase the risk of severe illness, mortality, inappropriate treatment, and additional financial and health-system burden. These findings highlight the need for strategies that ensure prompt access to effective malaria care for young children.

Prevalence of delay in malaria care-seeking for children under

The prevalence of delayed treatment for malaria, reveals substantial variability across SSA countries, confirming that prompt treatment, defined as receiving care within 24 h of symptom onset still remains a major challenge [117]. The delay in seeking treatment is critical, as treatment delayed by > 24 h significantly increase the odds of severe malarial outcomes by 33% (odds ratio of 2.79 for severe anemia with a 2–3 days delay) [118]. The WHO recommends that confirmed malaria cases must be treated within 24 h [117]. The review findings confirm the trend across SSA for children under five who received antimalarial treatment shows that about 60% experienced delayed treatment (> 24 h) in 2022, with 28.8% facing severe delays (> 48 h) [119]. This translates to over 16 million children receiving treatment outside the recommended prompt timelines [119].

The prevalence of delayed malaria care-seeking is not only widespread but also highly variable across and within countries in SSA, reflecting differences in health-system capacity, social vulnerability, and community beliefs [119]. The consistently high burden of delay in parts of West, Central, and East Africa suggests persistent structural challenges, even where malaria control programmes are well established. Conversely, very low delay estimates found in some Ghanaian and DRC settings indicate that prompt care-seeking is achievable when health-service coverage, affordability and community understanding of malaria are strong [32]. The patterns observed across countries also show that urban–rural disparities, perceptions of illness severity, treatment costs, and reliance on informal care continue to influence early decision-making [86, 106]. Cross-regional comparisons with the Greater Mekong Subregion further confirm that these drivers extend beyond Africa [119], highlighting that socioeconomic disadvantage and access barriers are universal determinants of delay in malaria-endemic areas. Importantly, increasing delay trends in Central and West Africa despite ongoing malaria investment suggest that health-system improvements alone are insufficient unless coupled with community-level interventions that improve trust, reduce financial burdens, and shift treatment-seeking norms toward timely formal care.

These findings also suggest that national malaria indicators focusing only on treatment coverage may mask critical timing gaps in access to care. Although many children eventually receive antimalarial treatment, the delay before reaching appropriate services continues to drive severe disease progression and avoidable deaths [87, 111]. The high variability observed between neighbouring countries further indicates that timely treatment is influenced as much by social and behavioural conditions as by availability of services, reinforcing the need for interventions that improve care-seeking readiness at household level. Additionally, early care-seeking is increasingly important in the context of emerging parasite resistance to ACT, where delays heighten treatment failure and transmission risks [119]. Strengthening community awareness and first-response decision-making therefore remains essential to achieving WHO targets for malaria morbidity and mortality reduction [117].

Delay in seeking care for children under 5 years

The socioeconomic and demographic risk suggests that many caregivers delay treatment not because they lack concern, but because they are constrained by household vulnerability and structural inequalities. When financial resources are limited, caregivers may legitimately delay seeking care until symptoms worsen enough to justify the burden of transport, treatment fees, and income loss [39, 47, 99]. Under these pressures, delayed care becomes a coping strategy rather than negligence [30, 31, 34]. The influence of paternal or extended-family authority, where women require approval from husbands or relatives before seeking care, further reflects how entrenched gender norms restrict timely decision-making [76, 84]. Associations with both younger and older caregivers [67, 97] indicate that economic capacity, autonomy and family hierarchy are more decisive than age alone. These findings suggest that early treatment cannot be improved by knowledge alone, reducing financial dependence and strengthening women’s decision autonomy remain essential to accelerate timely malaria care.

When fever is perceived as minor or familiar, families often prioritise home responsibilities or low-cost remedies before committing scarce resources [74–76, 78, 101]. Regular reliance on herbal medicine and pharmacies reflects active treatment attempts within informal spaces viewed as more accessible and socially accepted than health facilities [34, 106, 107]. Fear of misdiagnosis, mistrust of treatment, and concerns about side effects, strengthened by previous negative facility experiences, can rationalise delaying formal care even when danger signs emerge. High treatment costs and perceived financial risk may further reduce willingness to act early [35, 39]. These patterns reinforce that communities respond to malaria based on perceived value, convenience, and expected benefits of care, indicating the need to build trust, credibility, and respectful care practices to shift norms around when care is sought.

Geographic and transport barriers reveal that the path to care is itself a determinant of care-seeking. Caregivers living in remote settings or more than 3–5 km from a health facility commonly postpone or avoid formal treatment [30, 92]. In rural and underserved regions, the cost, time and uncertainty of travel force caregivers to delay until illness clearly threatens the child’s wellbeing [72]. Transport inaccessibility and high transport costs worsen the burden [100], and during crises such as the COVID-19 lockdown, movement restrictions prevented or delayed travel entirely [110]. These conditions shows that delayed care cannot be solved by demand-side messaging alone because even motivated caregivers face structural barriers that make timely treatment physically and financially unattainable.

Outcomes of delay in caregivers seeking care for children under five with malaria

Delayed care alters the entire clinical trajectory of malaria because the infection progresses quickly, and treatment becomes increasingly less effective once complications set in. Children who face barriers to early care typically come from households with limited nutrition, poorer immunity, and fewer social supports, which heightens vulnerability to severe outcomes [29, 73]. When treatment finally begins late, some organ and neurological damage may already be irreversible, meaning the health system must intervene more intensively to achieve smaller gains. These patterns show that delay acts as a reinforcer of inequity, where children already disadvantaged by poverty and poor access to care are the most likely to suffer long-term developmental harm [109]. Early treatment is therefore not only a clinical necessity but a protective factor against a cycle of reduced learning potential and lifelong social disadvantage.

Late presentation also transforms malaria from a condition that could be managed efficiently in routine care into one that consumes far greater health-system resources. Managing severe illness requires prolonged admission, advanced medication, and close monitoring, stretching facilities that are already under pressure [61, 106]. During the delay period, informal or inappropriate treatment practices can contribute to sub-optimal drug exposure, with implications for resistance development and future treatment effectiveness [101]. When families repeatedly experience health facilities only during emergencies, confidence in seeking early care declines, and late presentation gradually becomes normal behaviour. Improving outcomes therefore requires a dual approach, preventing serious disease through timely access and ensuring that health-system readiness and quality reinforce the value of early care-seeking [94].

The three-delay model

The three-delay model provides a useful explanatory lens for interpreting the drivers and consequences of delayed malaria care-seeking observed in this review. Rather than viewing delays as isolated actions, the model shows how household-level decision constraints, physical access challenges, and health-system inefficiencies interact in a sequential chain that amplifies risk [22]. The evidence demonstrates that when caregivers first struggle to decide to seek care due to low autonomy, financial insecurity, or misperceived severity, they enter the pathway to delay with heightened vulnerability [30, 39, 47]. Geographic and transportation barriers then transform what might have been a short delay into potentially dangerous postponement, revealing how structural inequities shape the speed at which symptoms transition from “manageable” to life-threatening [30, 100, 110]. Even after reaching a facility, stock-outs, workforce shortages, user fees, and poor service experience prolong the final stage of access, signalling that health systems can reinforce late presentation rather than reverse it [94, 105, 109, 112]. Collectively, these findings illustrate that delays accumulate rather than occur in isolation, meaning a small breakdown at one stage quickly cascades into severe clinical and social consequences. Strengthening only one delay point may therefore be insufficient unless interventions address all three dimensions simultaneously through financial-risk protection, community-level behavioural support, and reliable quality of facility-based care.

Implications for policy, practice and research

Effort towards improving timely malaria care must focus on the entire care-seeking pathway, not only on caregiver behaviour. When poverty, fear of costs, and lack of autonomy make families hesitant to seek formal care, financial protection policies such as fully subsidised treatment, waiver of informal charges, and transport support become essential to reduce the delay in deciding to seek care. Governments, health agencies and policy makers in SSA must recognise that distance and transport are not inconveniences but determinants of survival, and should prioritise improved rural road networks, reliable community transport arrangements, and stronger referral systems to speed up the journey to care. Facility-level readiness must also be visibly improved, because caregivers will only change long-standing norms of delayed presentation if they can trust that arriving early guarantees rapid evaluation, immediate initiation of treatment, and respectful communication. Strengthening medicine supply chains, staffing levels, and triage processes can prevent harmful delays within facilities themselves and help demonstrate the true value of early care. Community health workers, pharmacies and drug vendors, who are often the first point of contact, should be empowered to identify danger signs, provide accurate guidance, and make timely referrals before complications emerge, bridging gaps between households and formal services.

Future research needs to move beyond documenting prevalence and focus on where time is lost across the three delay points so that interventions directly target the most harmful bottlenecks. Studies that explore how caregivers interpret symptoms, costs, and risks are needed to illuminate the decision logic behind delay and design behaviour-change strategies that align with real-world constraints rather than idealised assumptions. Evaluating combined approaches that simultaneously support household decision-making, improve physical access, and guarantee fast and dignified care at facilities will be crucial to break the cycle in which delay has become routine. Research should also capture long-term developmental and economic consequences of delayed care, showing how improvements in timeliness can strengthen child survival, learning, productivity and household resilience. It worth noting that reducing delay is not only a clinical intervention but a health equity mandate that protects children from the compounding effects of poverty and ensures that the health system does not fail them at the moment they most urgently need its protection.

Limitations of the study

Only studies published in English were included, which may have excluded valuable evidence from regions where French, Arabic or Portuguese are commonly used, particularly in parts of Africa where malaria research is also abundant. This language restriction may therefore narrow the breadth of perspectives captured. The geographical spread of the included studies was also uneven, with some countries under-represented, meaning the findings cannot be assumed to reflect the experiences of all caregivers across the continent. In addition, all studies meeting the inclusion criteria were retained regardless of their methodological quality and while this allowed for a broad synthesis, it may have introduced variations in the reliability and robustness of the evidence. Besides, included studies were conducted across diverse healthcare systems, malaria transmission settings, and socioeconomic contexts in sub-Saharan Africa. This heterogeneity may influence both care-seeking behaviour and health-system responses, and therefore the findings should be interpreted with caution when generalising across all settings.

Another limitation relates to the variation in how delayed care-seeking was defined across the included studies. While many studies defined delay as seeking care more than 24 h after symptom onset, others used longer thresholds such as 48 h. This inconsistency may affect comparability of prevalence estimates and limits the ability to directly compare the magnitude of delay across settings. In addition, most studies relied on caregiver self-reported recall of the timing of symptom onset and care-seeking behaviour. Such recall-based reporting may introduce recall bias or social desirability bias, which could lead to underestimation or overestimation of the actual duration of delay. Furthermore, the majority of included studies were cross-sectional in design, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the identified determinants and delays in care-seeking. Despite these limitations, the review draws on robust search, screening, extraction and appraisal procedures. Also, the reviews included 92 papers from 26 countries in SSA.

Conclusion

This review suggests that delayed care-seeking for malaria among children under five remains a common challenge in sub-Saharan Africa, with substantial variation between and within regions. Multiple factors contribute to the timing of treatment, including household financial constraints, limited decision autonomy, cultural beliefs, distance and transport challenges, and health-system readiness. These factors often interact across the three delay points, reinforcing patterns where children most affected by socioeconomic vulnerability are also those most likely to arrive for care late. The synthesised evidence highlights that improving timely access is not only about encouraging caregivers to seek care early but also ensuring that health systems and environments make early care realistically achievable. Strengthened financial protection, enhanced transport and referral systems, and reliable, respectful facility-based care could support more prompt treatment. Also, continued research is needed to identify where the longest delays occur and evaluate interventions that act simultaneously across household, access and service-delivery levels. Ensuring that children receive care as soon as symptoms begin will support better health outcomes and contribute to ongoing malaria control efforts across the region.
