Combining 2.5D deep learning and conventional features in a joint model for the early detection of sICH expansion
Authors: Peng Wang, Junfeng Zhang, Yi Liu, Jialing Wu, Hongmei Yu, Chengzhou Yu, Rui Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study aims to investigate the potential of training efficient deep learning models by using 2.5D (2.5-Dimension) masks of sICH. Furthermore, it intends to evaluate and compare the predictive performance of a joint model incorporating four types of features with standalone 2.5D deep learning, radiomics, radiology, and clinical models for early expansion in sICH. A total of 254 sICH patients were enrolled retrospectively and divided into two groups according to whether the hematoma was enlarged or not. The 2.5D mask of sICH is constructed with the maximum axial, coronal and sagittal planes of the hematoma, which is used to train the deep learning model and extract deep learning features. Predictive models were built on clinic, radiology, radiomics and deep learning features separately and four type features jointly. The diagnostic performance of each model was measured using the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), Accuracy, Recall, F1 and decision curve analysis (DCA). The AUCs of the clinic model, radiology model, radiomics model, deep learning model, joint model, and nomogram model on the train set (training and Cross-validation) were 0.639, 0.682, 0.859, 0.807, 0.939, and 0.942, respectively, while the AUCs on the test set (external validation) were 0.680, 0.758, 0.802, 0.857, 0.929, and 0.926. Decision curve analysis showed that the joint model was superior to the other models and demonstrated good consistency between the predicted probability of early hematoma expansion and the actual occurrence probability. Our study demonstrates that the joint model is a more efficient and robust prediction model, as verified by multicenter data. This finding highlights the potential clinical utility of a multifactorial prediction model that integrates various data sources for prognostication in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage. Introduction

Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH) is a condition characterized by the non-traumatic rupture of blood vessels in the brain, resulting in the accumulation of blood in the brain parenchyma1,2. It is the second most common subtype of stroke, following ischemic stroke. The incidence rate of cerebral hemorrhage is approximately (12–15)/100,000 person-years, accounting for 15% of all strokes in Western countries and 10–30% of all hospitalized stroke patients3. In China, the proportion of cerebral hemorrhage is even higher, accounting for 18.8–47.6% of all strokes4–6. Cerebral hemorrhage has a high risk of mortality, with a rate of 35–52% within 30 days of onset, and only about 20% of patients regain their ability to live independently after 6 months, causing a significant burden to society and families3,4,6–8. Previous study shows that early hematoma expansion (HE), The volume of hematoma in sICH patients increased by > 6 ml or > 33% compared to the initial hematoma volume during follow-up examination within 24 h9, is an independent risk factor for poor prognosis and deterioration of neurological function than no hematoma expansion (NHE) in sICH patients. Therefore, the early prediction of HE is crucial for a better prognosis in patients with cerebral hemorrhage10.

Deep learning (DL) is a new research direction in the field of machine learning (ML)11. This method aims to bring machines closer to the original goal of artificial intelligence (AI) by endowing them with analytical and learning abilities equivalent to those of humans12,13, With capabilities of mimicking human activities such as perception and reasoning in available AI/ML-based medical devices and algorithms14. In light of its achievements, deep learning model based on CT scans to predict early HE and assess its forecasting performance has been established15–18. The aforementioned studies demonstrated strong predictive performance. However, the stability of results in test set verification is inconsistent. Additionally, there is a lack of comprehensive comparison and correlation with Radiomics, Radiology, and Clinic features19. Furthermore, the interpretability of deep learning algorithms from a clinical standpoint is inadequate, and the utilization of deep learning algorithms based on 3D images necessitates increased computing resources20.

In this study, we created a 2.5D mask by utilizing the maximum axial, coronal, and sagittal slices of the hematoma. This represents a novel approach to the analysis of hematoma morphology. Our aim is to explore the feasibility of pre-training efficient deep learning models with limited computational resources based on the 2.5D mask, and to assess the potential impact on the effectiveness of the extracted deep learning features as predictors in machine learning. Additionally, we aim to determine whether the joint model effectiveness of merged deep learning features surpasses that of pre-trained deep learning models. Image acquisition, preprocessing and segmentation

CT scanning in this study was conducted using Toshiba Aquilion ONE 320 CT and SIEMENS Somatom 64 CT. Standardization of images from various sources was necessary. Image preprocessing involved following steps: Firstly, CT images were normalized by resampling image voxels to 3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm and subjected to gray-scale discretization processing (256 bins). Subsequently, the images were reconstructed using brain windows (window width, 90 HU; window level, 35 HU). Finally, two senior radiologists (Dr. Zhang and Du), each with over 5 years of diagnostic experience, manually outlined the hematoma contours on the initial and follow-up CT images using 3D Slicer software (version 4.11, Harvard University, NY, 3D Slicer image computing platform | 3D Slicer) to derive 3D mask images and calculate hematoma volume. The sICH patients were categorized into hematoma enlargement (HE) and non-hematoma enlargement (NHE) groups based on the difference in hematoma (3D mask) volume between the two groups.

One month later, 25 patients in the train set were randomly re-delineated to assess intra- and inter-observer consistency in hematoma division by calculating the interclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which was found to be 0.900.

The region of interest (ROI) of hematoma was cropped based on the 3D mask from NCCT (Non-contrast computed tomography) images. The 2.5-dimensional concept in this study included the maximum axial, coronal, and sagittal planes of the hematoma, a total of 762 images (254 × 3).

Clinic signature and radiology markers collection

In this retrospective study, all patient clinic data were retrieved from the electronic medical record system. Clinic signs, scores, and laboratory results were extracted for analysis. Two attending radiologists (Dr. Jiang and Yu), each with over 10 years of experience in neuroimaging diagnosis, independently analyzed the imaging data in a double-blind manner. Patient names were randomly assigned to observers who were blinded to clinic data, including medical history and other imaging findings. The NCCT images were presented on the workstation, and each radiology morphological characteristics of sICH was assessed and documented based on the standard provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Material 1 for diagnostic criteria). In cases where the radiologists’ opinions diverged, a discussion was conducted to achieve a consensus.

Radiomics feature extraction

The radiomics features were extracted from NCCT images and the 3D mask of each patient using the open-source Python package Pyradiomics (https://pypi.org/project/pyradiomics/). The extracted features were categorized into first-order features, two-dimensional features, gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM), gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM), gray-level size-zone matrix (GLSZM), and gray-level run length matrix (GLRLM). In total, 1906 radiomics features were obtained from the NCCT images of each patient, “exampleCT.yaml” file as an attachment with detailed parameters for radiomics feature extraction.

Training deep learning models and DL feature extraction

Upon analyzing the 762 ROI images, models were conducted using various deep learning algorithms, namely AlexNet, DenseNet121, GoogleNet, MNASNet0_5, MobileNet_V2, ResNet50, SqueezeNet1_0, and VGG19. The deep learning parameters were set as follows: epochs = 200 and batch size = 32. After comparing the results, ResNet50 demonstrated superior performance, achieving a train set AUC of 0.958 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.942–0.975) and a test set AUC of 0.853 (95% CI 0.782–0.924). Consequently, ResNet50 was chosen as the pre-trained deep learning model for following feature extraction, showed in Fig. 2.

Pre-training deep learning models. This figure illustrates the pre-training process of various deep learning models based on the 2.5D mask of hematoma for predicting hematoma expansion (HE). The models evaluated include AlexNet, DenseNet121, GoogleNet, MNASNet0_5, MobileNet_V2, ResNet50, SqueezeNet1_0, and VGG19. Among these, the ResNet50 model demonstrated the highest area under the curve (AUC) for predicting HE. Consequently, the pre-trained ResNet50 model was selected for extracting deep learning features for subsequent modeling and comparative analysis.

We applied linear interpolation to resample the input images by downsampling the 254 layers of the ROI to a 64 × 64 resolution in order to obtain the features of the deep learning model. Subsequently, we normalized and standardized the pixel intensity, ensuring that the mean and standard deviation of the values were set to 0 and 1. Given that the network input comprises cross-sectional images, the input channel was set to 1. In order to mitigate the risk of overfitting and enhance the model’s generalization ability, we opted for the penultimate layer of the model (the average pooling layer) as the feature, and utilized principal component analysis to reduce the dimension of the deep learning features to 32 dimensions, resulting in independent and stable features. The methods employed in previous deep learning studies were referenced in this study21.

Clinic signature and radiology markers selection

The Stats statistical software was utilized for data processing. For metric data following a normal distribution, the mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) was employed, and the comparison between groups was conducted using an independent samples t-test. On the other hand, non-normally distributed metric data was represented using the median and interquartile range (Q25, Q75), and the comparison between groups was carried out using the rank-sum test. Count data were presented as relative frequencies, and the chi-squared test was utilized for interclass comparisons. Radiomics and DL features selection

All radiomics and deep learning (DL) features were standardized using the Z-score. To mitigate redundancy, we initially computed the Pearson rank correlation coefficient for the features. Based on the correlation coefficient, when features exhibited relatively high correlation (typically with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.9, as per literature), only one of them was retained. Subsequently, we employed Lasso (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) for feature selection. Features with a coefficient of 0 were excluded, thereby eliminating the feature with the highest redundancy in the current set each time.

Predict model establishment and validation

We utilized the scikit-learn machine learning library to develop clinic models, radiology models, radiomics models, deep learning models, and a UNI model (a joint model combining all selected features) employing various machine learning algorithms including Support Vector Machine (SVM), k-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), Random Forest (RF), ExtraTrees (ET), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (Light GBM), NaiveBayes (NB), Adaptive Boosting (Adaboost), GradientBoosting (GB), Logistic Regression (LR), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), totaling 11 algorithms. All models were internally evaluated using tenfold cross-validation (training and validation set) and tested with test set. This was done to identify the best prediction model from the four types of features and feature combinations.

The predictive performance of different models for HE was evaluated using ROC AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, recall, F1, and the Youden index. We also pay attention to the performance indicators related to unbalanced data sets, such as accuracy, recall rate and F1, which can better reflect the performance of the model when dealing with unbalanced data sets.

To compare the AUCs of different predictive models, we employed the De-Long test, and the DCA was used to assess the clinical value of the models and select the optimal model based on net benefit.

All statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted using stats models version0.13. The “ggplot2” and “rmda” packages were primarily utilized to generate column plots and DCA. The statistical significance of all two-tailed tests was set at P < 0.05. The workflow is depicted in Fig. 3.

Nomogram model

We also constructed a Nomogram and attempted to utilize the outcomes of the five prediction models as factors to determine if we could enhance the predictive performance further. The diagnostic efficacy of the nomogram was assessed in the test cohort, ROC curves were generated to assess its diagnostic performance. Additionally, the calibration performance of the nomogram was assessed through calibration curves, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test was used to evaluate its calibration ability. DCA was employed to assess the clinical applicability of the predictive models.

Discussion

Early expansion of sICH is a key determinant in predicting outcomes. NCCT is the fundamental imaging modality for patients with cerebral hemorrhage, offering non-invasiveness, rapid imaging, and cost-effectiveness22. Utilizing clinic, radiology, and deep learning features, along with various machine learning techniques, enables the non-invasive reflection of internal lesion heterogeneity. This facilitates early diagnosis, differential diagnosis, and treatment response monitoring.

The study evaluated clinic and radiology factors. GCS score, onset examination time, midline shift, liquid level, and bleeding location were identified as independent risk factors for distinguishing between hemorrhagic and NHE, consistent with previous research findings9,23,24. In the test set, the Clinic Signature AUC was 0.680 (95% CI 0.486–0.874) and the Radiology Signature AUC was 0.758 (95% CI 0.611–0.905). Despite some researchers suggesting subjectivity and selection bias in the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)25. our study demonstrated its effectiveness as an indicator. In contrast to prior studies23, age, diabetes, smoking history, and history of hypertension did not exhibit adequate individual predictive power for association with HE.

Accurately determining the impact of vascular disease risk factors, such as hypertension or diabetes, on hemorrhage expansion is challenging, particularly across various stages or risk levels. Therefore, case selection criteria should not rely solely on the presence or absence of these risk factors, necessitating more detailed stratification1. Conversely, hemorrhage morphology and size lacked adequate predictive power, potentially influenced by the evaluators’ academic backgrounds and experience when assessing shape (regular/irregular). In future research, some markers of small vessel disease, such as white matter hyperintensities, perivascular spaces, microbleeds, or superficial siderosis deposits, may be more widely incorporated into the basis for grading patients.

The radiology model exhibited discrimination of HE similar to the findings of an international multicenter study24 (AUC 0.67 [95% CI 0.64–0.69] versus 0.71 [95% CI 0.69–0.74]; P = 0.025). The DeLong test revealed significant differences in the ROC of the Clinic Signature and Radiology Signature, suggesting that the predictive efficacy of the Radiology Signature may surpass that of the Clinic Signature.

Several imaging indicators have been shown to be closely associated with HE, including baseline intracerebral hemorrhage volume2,26, irregular and inhomogeneous hematoma morphology observed on CT27, intraventricular hemorrhage28, island sign29, satellite sign30, black hole sign31, mixed density sign32, swirl sign33, and black hole effect34.Nevertheless, there is overlap in the definitions of these signs35, potentially leading to confusion and perplexity among clinicians. Additionally, specific signs have a low incidence rate, for example, midline shift in 89 cases (35%) and liquid level in 8 cases (3%). When utilized in isolation or as part of a prediction score based on clinic variables, these indicators may have limited utility in hospitals with limited resources or where CT angiography interpretation is challenging.

In our study, the Radiomics AUC in the test set is 0.802 (95% CI 0.655–0.948), which outperforms both the Clinic Signature and Radiology Signature. This performance is superior to that reported by Xu et al. (AUC 0.776) in their external validation cohort30. Radiomics shows promise as a reliable and convenient method for predicting HE.

Zhong et al.18 demonstrated that, compared with traditional radiology markers and BAT (B for blend sign, A for hypodensity presence, and T for time from onset to NCCT) prediction models, deep learning algorithms offer advantages in predicting early HE in patients with cerebral hemorrhage, achieving the highest AUC of 0.80. In our study, the DL Signature AUC was 0.857 (95% CI 0.753–0.961). We introduced a concept similar to 2.5D, which provides innovation for a more effective and stable learning model16. Our novel approach incorporates information from three different anatomical planes and provides more comprehensive information about hematoma heterogeneity compared to simply superimposing adjacent slices. This approach differs from that of Takao36, and Avesta20 who built a 2.5D mask. Furthermore, we evaluated mainstream models on the same dataset and found that ResNet50 had the most favorable learning effect with our data. The predicted probability of the ResNet50 model achieved an AUC of 0.958 (95% CI 0.942–0.975) for the train set and 0.853 (95% CI 0.782–0.924) for the test set, representing a notably superior result.

By utilizing this model for extracting deep learning features and employing machine learning to model the test set, the performance did not decrease; instead, there was a slight increase in Test AUC to 0.857 (95% CI 0.753–0.961). By integrating these filtered deep learning features into the joint model, the performance on the test set was further enhanced, achieving a Test UNI Signature AUC of 0.929 (95% CI 0.854–1.000), which is significantly higher than that of the ResNet50 pre-training model. The results indicate that extracting and utilizing these features in a joint model is beneficial for improving overall performance. Another rationale for using the outlined mask to extract features for deep learning and subsequently employing them in machine learning is due to computational resource constraints. The models were trained and validated using a standard office computer in the radiology department, without the use of specialized servers.

The nomogram quantifies the contribution of individual predictions to the outcome variable by constructing a multifactor logistic regression model, assigning scores, and then calculating the predicted value of the individual outcome event using a function-transformation relationship between the total score and the probability of the outcome event. Despite performing slightly worse than the UNI model on the test set, with an AUC of 0.926 (95% CI 0.852–1.000), it still demonstrates good predictive performance. Based on this experimental result, it appears feasible to omit this operation in practical applications.

However, this study has limitations. Despite our introduction of the maximum axial, coronal, and sagittal planes of the hematoma as a 2.5D mask using innovative methods, the sample size remains small and may be subject to selection bias. Additionally, subjective collection of imaging features is a possibility. Consequently, larger prospective studies with multicenter validation are necessary in the future. Lastly, manual segmentation of hematomas may be time-consuming. Future research should explore the comprehensive application of artificial intelligence for rapid segmentation and diagnosis.

Conclusion

Our study has developed and validated a combined features model that enhances classification performance. This model provides personalized risk prediction for HE in patients, serving as a supportive tool to improve diagnostic decision-making for radiologists. It also holds potential as a non-invasive quantitative detection method for large-scale clinical applications. Additionally, it offers valuable support for primary healthcare institutions, which often lack advanced imaging capabilities and timely access to evaluations from stroke neurologists and/or neuro-radiologists. Furthermore, it presents potential benefits, including the reduction of contrast agents and radiation exposure, potentially leading to faster treatment times and theoretically improving treatment outcomes.
