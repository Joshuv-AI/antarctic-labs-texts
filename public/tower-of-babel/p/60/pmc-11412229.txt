The Salmonella Effector SspH2 Facilitates Spatially Selective Ubiquitination of NOD1 to Enhance Inflammatory Signaling
Authors: Cole J. Delyea, Malcolm D. Forster, Shu Luo, Bradley E. Dubrule, Olivier Julien, Amit P. Bhavsar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As part of its pathogenesis, Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium delivers effector proteins into host cells. One effector is SspH2, a member of the so-called novel E3 ubiquitin ligase family, that interacts with and enhances, NOD1 pro-inflammatory signaling, though the underlying mechanisms are unclear. Here, we report that SspH2 interacts with multiple members of the NLRC family to enhance pro-inflammatory signaling by targeted ubiquitination. We show that SspH2 modulates host innate immunity by interacting with both NOD1 and NOD2 in mammalian epithelial cell culture via the NF-κB pathway. Moreover, purified SspH2 and NOD1 directly interact, where NOD1 potentiates SspH2 E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. Mass spectrometry and mutational analyses identified four key lysine residues in NOD1 that are required for its enhanced activation by SspH2, but not its basal activity. These critical lysine residues are positioned in the same region of NOD1 and define a surface on the receptor that appears to be targeted by SspH2. Overall, this work provides evidence for post-translational modification of NOD1 by ubiquitin and uncovers a unique mechanism of spatially selective ubiquitination to enhance the activation of an archetypal NLR.

Introduction

Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium) is a Gram-negative facultative intracellular pathogen. Collectively, nontyphoidal Salmonella serovars (including S. Typhimurium) are a major cause of diarrheal disease worldwide, resulting in >4 million healthy life-year equivalents being lost annually.1 A hallmark of S. Typhimurium infection is its ability to induce its uptake into nonphagocytic cells, establish a replicative niche inside the cell, and modulate the host immune response.2,3S. Typhimurium uses two type 3 secretion systems encoded on Salmonella pathogenicity islands (SPIs) to inject bacterial effectors into host cells to instigate these hallmark processes.4 Among these effectors are the so-called novel E3 ubiquitin ligases (NEL) that interfere with host ubiquitination and subvert host cellular processes.5

Salmonella secreted protein H2 (SspH2) is one of four S. Typhimurium NELs that share physical similarities with other bacterial NEL proteins, e.g., IpaH4.5 in Shigella spp.6,7 Putative roles in bacterial pathogenesis have been uncovered for three of the NELs in S. Typhimurium, while a fourth NEL has only recently been described.8−12 These proteins all share a similar two-domain structure–an amino-terminal leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain, and a carboxy-terminal E3 ubiquitin ligase domain.6,7,13,14S. Typhimurium virulence is compromised in an animal model in the absence of SspH1 and SspH2, while deletion of SspH2 alone appears to reduce the virulence of the related serovar S. enterica serovar Enteritidis.13,15 Remarkably, SspH2 causes activation of inflammation in host cells that is dependent on its E3 ligase activity.10 Upon injection via the type 3 secretion system, SspH2 is localized to the host plasma membrane via palmitoylation on Cys9.16 This enables SspH2 to interact with proximal host proteins at this site.

The recognition of pathogen- or damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs and DAMPs, respectively) on the surface, or in the cytosolic compartment, of host cells is critical for host immunity. Nucleotide Binding Domain, Leucine-Rich Repeat containing proteins (NLRs) are associated with sensing PAMPs alongside DAMPs, and ER stress in mammalian cells.17 NLRs are characterized by their tripartite structure consisting of: (i) a carboxy-terminal LRR domain that senses bacterial structures, (ii) a central nucleotide-binding domain (NBD) that facilitates NLR oligomerization, and (iii) a variable amino-terminal domain, which is critical for interactions with downstream effector proteins.18 NOD1 and NOD2 are the founding members of the NLRs, first described in 1999,19 and have since been grouped as NLRCs as they contain a characteristic amino-terminal caspase activation and recruitment domain (CARD). NOD1 and NOD2 are commonly found in the cytosol of host cells,19 but they localize to actin-rich regions in the plasma membrane when activated.20,21

NOD1 is ubiquitously expressed in cells and is critical for epithelial cell sensing of primarily intracellular Gram-negative peptidoglycan by binding to γ-d-glutamyl-meso-diaminopimelic acid fragments of their cell walls.21,22 Upon ligand recognition through the LRR domain, NOD1 undergoes a change in conformation and oligomerizes to cause NF-κB activation through homophilic CARD domain interactions with RIP2.19,23,24 This leads to the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-8.17 NOD2, a NOD1 homologue, is found primarily in monocytes.25 NOD2 recognizes bacterial peptidoglycan through muramyl dipeptide.26,27 After ligand binding to the LRR domain and subsequent unfolding, NOD2 activates in a way similar to NOD1, where the NBD region facilitates oligomerization and the CARD domain interacts with RIP2, leading to activation of NF-κB.25

Bacterial E3 ligases have been reported to interfere with downstream inflammatory signaling processes, such as IpaH4.5 targeting TBK1 for degradation to prevent inflammation.28 Even though NLRs are critical intracellular sensors of pathogens, the literature on bacterial ubiquitination of NLRs is limited. Our previous studies showed that SspH2 interacts with both NOD1 and its cochaperone protein, SGT1.10 This interaction led to the ubiquitination of NOD1, causing increased secretion of the chemokine IL-8.10S. Typhimurium can exploit inflammation in intestinal epithelial cells to compete with the microbiota.29,30 The molecular details of how SspH2 specifically modifies NOD1, and how this leads to enhanced pro-inflammatory signaling remain to be identified. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether the SspH2 interaction with NOD1 is unique, or if it interacts more broadly with other NLRs.

In this study, we used mammalian epithelial cell culture to gain further insights into the biological and mechanistic interactions between the S. Typhimurium effector SspH2 and host NLRCs. We show that SspH2 selectively interacts with the LRR and NBD regions of NOD1. We also report that SspH2 interacts with NOD2, where catalytically active SspH2 drives NOD2 activation, analogously to SspH2’s effect on NOD1 via canonical NF-κB signaling. A direct interaction between recombinant NOD1 and SspH2 resulted in enhanced E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of the latter. Moreover, we provide evidence that SspH2 facilitates the ubiquitination of a specific region of NOD1 to trigger augmented pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, thus identifying a mechanism of spatially selective ubiquitination to enhance activation of an archetypal NLR.

Materials and Methods

Tissue Culture

HEK293T (ATCC CRL-3216) and HeLa (ATCC CCL-2) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) containing 4500 mg/L glucose, l-glutamine, and sodium bicarbonate (Sigma) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (123483–020; Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin (P4333; Sigma) and grown at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Cloning

Domain truncation variants of NOD1 were generated by PCR amplification of NOD1 domains using pcDNA3-NOD1-Flag as a template (pcDNA3-NOD1-Flag and pCMV2-Flag-NOD2 were kindly provided by Dana Philpott, University of Toronto). Primer sequences can be found in Supporting Table 1. Truncation fragments were cloned into pcDNA3.1 using the KpnI and XhoI restriction enzymes to replace wild-type (WT) NOD1. To create the NOD1 domain mutants the following primer combinations were used. CARD: NOD1_CARD_For1 and NOD1_CARD_Rev2; NBD: NOD1_NBD_For1 and NOD1_NBD_Rev1; LRR: NOD1_LRR_For2 and NOD1_LRR_Rev1; ΔCARD: NOD1_NBD_For1 and NOD1_LRR_Rev1; ΔLRR: NOD1_CARD_For1 and NOD1_NBD_Rev1. ΔNBD was created by separately amplifying the CARD and LRR domains using primer pairs: NOD1_CARD_For1/NOD1_CARD_Rev1 and NOD1_LRR_For1/NOD1_LRR_Rev1. These two PCR fragments were then combined together by crossover PCR. The lysine variants of NOD1 were generated in the pcDNA3.1 NOD1 background, using the Quikchange II site directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent) according to the outlined protocol from Agilent. All mutagenized plasmids were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. All constructs were propagated in Escherichia coli DH5α using standard methods. The SspH2 domain expression constructs were from ref (10).

Immunoblotting

Proteins were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (BioRad). Membranes were dried, rehydrated with Tris-buffered saline (TBS) and blocked with TBS blocking buffer (927–60001; Li-Cor) before incubation with primary antibody diluted in TBS blocking buffer overnight. Membranes were washed and incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in the same buffer for 1 h. The antibodies used in this study are mouse α-FLAG (M2; Sigma) 1:2 500 or 1:5 000; rabbit α-FLAG (SAB4301135; Sigma) 1:2 500; rat α-HA (clone 3F10; Roche Diagnostics) 1:2 500; mouse α-Myc (9E10; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) 1:2 500; rabbit α-GST (sc-459; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) 1:2 000; mouse α-GST (66001–2-Ig, Proteintech) 1:2 000; goat α-mouse (926–68020; Licor) 1:5 000; goat α-rabbit (925–32211; Licor) 1:5 000; goat α-rat (926–32219; Licor) 1:5 000. Blots were imaged with a Li-Cor Odyssey and Image Studio software.

Manual Immunoprecipitation (IP) of Cell Lysates

HEK293T cells were seeded at 1 × 106 cells per 10 cm dish and transfected when cells reached 60–80% confluency with a total of 6 μg of equivalently proportioned plasmid DNA using JetPrime transfection reagent (Polyplus). Lysates were harvested by washing cells with PBS, and applying lysis buffer (20 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 1% Nonidet P-40 [all from Sigma)] supplemented with EDTA-free protease inhibitor mixture cocktail (Roche). Debris was precipitated (16,000g, 20 min) and the supernatant fraction was immunoprecipitated as per the protocol from Invitrogen. In brief, 20 μL Dynabeads Protein G beads (Invitrogen) were coupled to 2.5 μg α-HA (high affinity) antibody (Roche) or 5 μg α-FLAG (M2 clone) antibody. Antibody-coupled beads were incubated with samples at 4 °C overnight and analyzed by immunoblotting.

NOD1/NOD2 Functional Assays

HeLa cells were seeded at 2.5 × 105 cells/well (6-well dish) or 6 × 104 cells/well (24-well dish) and transfected at 60–80% cell confluency with 1 or 0.25 μg of DNA, respectively, using JetPrime transfection reagent (Polyplus). Two days post-transfection the media was replaced with DMEM containing 0.5% fetal bovine serum and NOD1 or NOD2 agonist [1 μg/mL C12-iE-DAP (Invivogen) + 10 ng/mL human interferon γ (AbD serotec) or 5 μg/mL L-18-MDP (Invivogen) + 10 ng/mL human interferon γ, respectively]. Following overnight stimulation, secreted IL-8 was quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay according to the manufacturer’s specifications (BD-Bioscience). For NF-κB pathway inhibition experiments, 20 μM of Bay 11–7082 (Sigma) was preincubated with samples for 45 min before stimulation.

Purification of Recombinant SspH2 and In Vitro Ubiquitination Reactions

Recombinant SspH2 with tandem N-terminal GST and HA epitope tags was purified according to the procedure outlined in ref (10) before PreScission protease digestion to remove the GST tag. Briefly, 50 μg of purified GST-HA-SspH2 was mixed with 2 μg in-house purified GST-tagged PreScission protease and pre-equilibrated Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (GE Healthcare) in PreScission protease buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT). Reactions were incubated overnight to allow for protease cleavage between the GST and HA tags of SspH2 before beads were precipitated. Cleaved HA-SspH2 was recovered from the supernatant. For in vitro ubiquitination reactions, purified GST-NOD1 (Abnova) or GST (Sigma-Aldrich) were mixed with HA-SspH2 and purified human UBE1, UBE2D2, and FLAG-Ubiquitin (all R&D Systems) in ubiquitination reaction buffer (80 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM DTT, 3.3 mM ATP) as described previously.10 Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for the indicated times before being quenched by boiling in SDS-PAGE sample buffer.

Coimmunoprecipitation of Purified Proteins

Purified GST-NOD1 (Abnova) was coincubated with purified HA-SspH2 in ubiquitination reaction buffer with or without 3 mM ATP as indicated (80 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM DTT). Some samples also received purified human UBE1, UBE2D2, FLAG-Ubiquitin to initiate in vitro SspH2 ubiquitination activity. All samples were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C prior to immunoprecipitation. Immunoprecipitation was carried out using protein G-conjugated magnetic beads (New England Biolabs). Beads were prepared by washing three times with IP wash buffer [PBS + 0.1% Tween-20 (Thermo Scientific)], then incubated with 2 μg Rabbit α-GST polyclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat #sc-459) for 20 min at room temperature with agitation. Washing steps were repeated to remove unbound antibody, then beads were blocked with 3–4% milk powder solution for 1 h at 4 °C. After blocking, beads were washed again, and purified protein samples were incubated with the beads for 1 h at 4 °C with agitation. Washing steps were performed a final time before remaining bound proteins were eluted from the beads by addition of SDS-PAGE sample buffer and boiling at 100 °C for 5 min.

Automated Immunoprecipitation for Mass Spectrometry

Transfected HEK293T cell lysate was prepared as outlined above. Automated IP was performed using the KingFisher Duo Prime Purification System using a modification of the manufacturer’s protocol (publication No. MAN0016198; Thermo Scientific) and executed on BindIt software (v4.0.0.45). Reagents were added to a 96 deep-well plate for parallel processing. In brief, 20 μL of Dynabeads Protein G beads (Invitrogen; 10003D) were bound to 7 μg of anti-DYKDDDDK antibody (SAB4301135; Sigma) in 200 μL of PBS-T (Phosphate Buffered Saline with 0.02% Tween 20) for 10 min, and subsequently washed with 200 μL PBS-T. The Dynabeads with the bound antibody complex were then incubated with 800 μL of transfected cell lysates (∼2.5 mg protein) for 10 min at room temperature, and washed twice with 500 μL PBS. The bound proteins were eluted from Dynabeads using 30 μL of 4× Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad) without reducing agents and heated to 70 °C for 10 min. The eluants were collected from the plate, and diluted with 20 μL ddH2O to run on an SDS-PAGE gel.

In-gel Sample Preparation for Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

The eluted IP samples were separated by molecular weight using SDS-PAGE (precast 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Stain-Free Protein Gels, 10 well, 50 μL; Bio-Rad) at 160 V for 10 min. The proteins in the gel were fixed by incubating with 50% EtOH, 2% phosphoric acid at room temperature for 30 min, then the gel was washed twice with ddH2O for 10 min. The gel was subsequently stained by Blue-Silver stain (20% pure ethanol, 10% phosphoric acid, 10% w/v ammonium sulfate, 0.12% w/v Coomassie Blue G-250) at room temperature overnight.

After staining, the gel was washed twice with ddH2O for 20 min. Each lane was cut into four fractions of gel pieces and transferred to a round-bottom 96-well plate to be repetitively destained by 150 μL destaining solution (50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, 50% acetonitrile) in each well at 37 °C. The gel pieces were then dried by incubating with acetonitrile at 37 °C, rehydrated and reduced with 175 μL of reducing solution (5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate) at 37 °C for 30 min, and alkylated with 175 μL of alkylating solution (50 mM iodoacetamide, 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate) at 37 °C for 30 min. The gel pieces were washed twice with 175 μL of 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate at 37 °C for 10 min, and completely dried by incubating with acetonitrile at 37 °C. Proteins in each well were digested with 1 μg of sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega Inc.) in 75 μL of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate and incubated overnight. Tryptic peptides in the gel pieces were extracted by incubating with 2% acetonitrile, 1% formic acid, then with 50% acetonitrile, 0.5% formic acid, each at 37 °C for 1 h. The extracted peptides were transferred to another round-bottom 96-well plate, dried using a Genevac (EZ-2 plus). Each sample was prefractionated into four injections for LC-MS/MS.

Mass Spectrometry Analyses

Peptides were separated using a nanoflow-HPLC (Thermo Scientific EASY-nLC 1200 System) coupled to Orbitrap Fusion Lumos Tribrid Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A trap column (5 μm, 100 Å, 100 μm × 2 cm, Acclaim PepMap 100 nanoViper C18; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an analytical column (2 μm, 100 Å, 50 μm × 15 cm, PepMap RSLC C18; Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for the reverse phase separation of the peptide mixture. Peptides were eluted over a linear gradient over the course of 90 min from 3.85 to 36.8% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid. Data were analyzed using ProteinProspector (v5.22.1) against the concatenated database of the human proteome (SwissProt.2017.11.01), with maximum false discovery rate at 5% for proteins, and 1% for peptides. Search parameters included a maximum of three missed trypsin cleavages, a precursor mass tolerance of 15 ppm, a fragment mass tolerance of 0.8 Da, with the constant modification carbamidomethylation (C), and variable modifications of acetyl (protein N-term), deamidated (N/Q), oxidation (M), and GlyGly (uncleaved K). The maximum number of variable modifications was set to 4.

Protein Modeling

Molecular graphics and analyses performed with UCSF Chimera,55 developed by the Resource for Biocomputing, Visualization, and Informatics at the University of California, San Francisco, with support from NIH P41-GM103311. Predicted protein structure for NOD1 available via AlphaFold (Accession Number: AF-Q9Y239–F1).

Statistical Analysis

All error bars indicate standard deviation. NOD1 and NOD2 activation were analyzed in HeLa cells and statistical analyses were determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey multiple testing correction. Immunoblot quantification analysis was performed by one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple correction compared to a control sample (SspH2 only Ub reaction). The semiquantitative mass spectrometry peptide fragment analysis was analyzed by linear regression with 95% confidence intervals (seen as a solid line and dotted line, respectively). NOD1 lysine variant activation in HeLa cells was analyzed by 1-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test to a control sample (NOD1 + EV or against NOD1 + SspH2). All statistical comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism 6.01 or Prism 9.5.1. Some figures were generated with GraphPad Prism or BioRender.com.

Results

NOD1 NBD and LRR Domains Interact with SspH2

We previously reported that SspH2 interacts with SGT1 and NOD1.10 However, the mechanistic details of how SspH2 interacts with NOD1 remain unexplored. In HEK293T cells, we transiently transfected NOD1 single domain constructs [CARD (residues 1–160), NBD (residues 134–584), LRR (residues 697–1053)], or single domain deletion constructs [ΔCARD (residues 160–1053), ΔNBD (residues 1–160 and 580–1053), and ΔLRR (residues 1–584)] to identify which domains are critical for NOD1 interaction with SspH2 (Figure 1A). Through reciprocal coimmunoprecipitation, we determined that the NBD and LRR domains of NOD1, but not the CARD domain, interact with SspH2 (Figure 1B).

SspH2 is comprised of a carboxy-terminal NEL domain and an amino-terminal LRR domain.7 To investigate which domain of SspH2 is responsible for NOD1 binding, we transiently transfected HEK293T cells with individual SspH2 domains and NOD1. We observed that both the LRR and NEL domains of SspH2 appeared to interact with NOD1 (Figure 1C). However, we observed that less NOD1 coimmunoprecipitated with SspH2 NEL, compared to SspH2 LRR, indicating that there may be different binding strengths between the SspH2 regions (Figure 1C). We confirmed that SspH2, but not the catalytically inactive C580A variant, enhanced activation of NOD1 by ∼4 fold in the presence of NOD1 agonist compared to the empty vector control (Figure 1D). Together, these data show that NOD1 interacts with SspH2 through its NBD and LRR domains, resulting in increased activation due to the ubiquitin ligase activity of SspH2.

SspH2 Interacts with Multiple NLRCs

To ascertain whether SspH2 interaction with NOD1 was unique, we investigated whether it could interact with NOD2, another member of the NLRC family.25 Similar to NOD1, we observed that SspH2 interacts with NOD2 via reciprocal coimmunoprecipitation in cell culture lysate (Figure 2A). This interaction was specific, as NOD2 did not interact with SspH1 in this assay, despite its 69% amino acid sequence identity with SspH2.13 We cannot rule out weak interactions between SspH1 and NOD2 that are below the limit of detection since we observed lower SspH1 protein levels despite performing equivalent transfections. Furthermore, similar to previous observations with NOD1,10 catalytically inactive SspH2 interacts with NOD2 (Figure 2B). Again, we observed that both the SspH2 NEL and LRR domains interact with NOD2 (Figure 2C).

Having identified a complex between SspH2 and NOD2, we tested if this interaction functionally mimics that of NOD1 and SspH2 in mammalian cell culture in the presence of NOD2 agonist (Figure 2D). We observed that NOD2 activation was ∼3–4-fold higher in the presence of SspH2 as compared to the empty vector + NOD2 group. Notably, neither SspH2-C580A nor SspH1 significantly increased NOD2 activation relative to the empty vector + NOD2 group (Figure 2D). The latter observation is consistent with our finding that SspH1 and NOD2 do not interact. We confirmed that all proteins were expressed under our assay conditions (Figure 2D). Together, this suggests that SspH2 interacts with and activates NOD2, inducing increased IL-8 secretion, in a similar fashion to NOD1.

In control experiments, we determined the basal effect of SspH2 expression on IL-8 secretion in our in vitro model system (Figure S1). In the absence of NOD2 agonist, SspH2 did not significantly increase IL-8 secretion in the presence of exogenous NOD2. By contrast, in the presence of NLR agonist without exogenous NLR expression, we observed that SspH2 expression increased IL-8 secretion ∼10-fold in the presence of NOD1 (C12-iE-DAP + IFN-γ, Figure S1B) or NOD2 (L-18-MDP + IFN-γ, Figure S1C) agonists. However, it is worth noting that these levels are still ∼20-fold lower than enhancements observed in the presence of exogenous NLR. These data suggest that SspH2 specifically interacts with host NLRs to mediate enhanced activation and subsequent increases in IL-8 secretion.

SspH2-Mediated NLR Activation Utilizes NF-κB Signaling

NOD1 and NOD2 are thought to produce IL-8 through the activation of the NF-κB pathway.31 To study the downstream effects of SspH2 on NOD1 and NOD2 signaling, IL-8 secretion assays were performed in the presence of the NF-κB pathway inhibitor, Bay 11–7068 (Bay 11). Bay 11 irreversibly inhibits NF-κB activation by blocking the phosphorylation of IκBα, which suppresses the nuclear translocation of p65 and its binding to NF-κB response elements, thus effectively preventing pro-inflammatory cytokine production.32,33

As expected, our data showed that the NF-κB inhibitor Bay 11, reduced NOD1 activation as measured by IL-8 secretion. This reduction was ∼4.5 fold and ∼9 fold in the absence and presence of SspH2 respectively (Figure 3A). We found that in the presence of Bay 11, the levels of IL-8 secretion were comparable, with and without SspH2 (Figure 3A). We noted a slight decrease in SspH2 levels in the presence of Bay 11; however, the Bay 11 effect is more consistent with NF-κB inhibition because IL-8 secretion levels in Bay 11-treated SspH2 samples were lower than samples that lacked SspH2 (Figure 3A).

Due to the similar patterns of protein binding in NOD1 and NOD2, we also investigated the effect of Bay 11 in the NOD2 functional assay. We found that NOD2 signaling was also diminished with or without SspH2 in the presence of Bay-11, albeit to a lesser extent than what was observed with NOD1. This reduction was ∼2.5 fold and ∼3 fold in the absence and presence of SspH2, respectively (Figure 3B). We found that upon Bay 11-treatment, levels of IL-8 secreted in the presence of NOD2 + SspH2 were significantly increased by ∼4.5 fold compared to NOD2 + EV (Figure 3B). This indicates that SspH2’s effects on NOD2 activity may not be entirely dependent on NF-κB signaling. Again, SspH2 levels were slightly reduced upon Bay 11 treatment, which could partially contribute to the decreased IL-8 secretion observed in this experiment (Figure 3B). Taken together, these data indicate that SspH2 mediates increased NLR activation through NF-κB signaling downstream of NOD1, and partially downstream of NOD2.

NOD1 Potentiates SspH2 E3 Ubiquitin Ligase Activity through Direct Binding

Our cell culture experiments indicated that NOD1 and SspH2 interact through the LRR domains of each protein. Nevertheless, these binding interactions were observed in the context of the host cell, where additional factors could be involved in complex formation. Indeed, we previously observed that SGT1, a NOD1 cochaperone, was a common interaction partner for both NOD1 and SspH2,34,35 which could mediate the binding between these two proteins. To further define the interaction between these two proteins we tested whether NOD1 and SspH2 directly bound together by performing coimmunoprecipitations (co-IPs) of recombinant purified forms of these proteins. We found that purified HA-SspH2 coimmunoprecipitated with GST-NOD1 (Figure 4A). Importantly, this interaction was not dependent on the presence of host ubiquitination machinery or any other proteinaceous factors (Figure 4A).

Mechanistically, NEL effectors are proposed to adopt an autoinhibited conformation that is subsequently relieved by substrate binding to the LRR domain.7 Due to their direct binding, we investigated whether SspH2 E3 ubiquitin ligase activity was enhanced in the presence of NOD1, by performing in vitro ubiquitination assays with purified proteins. In this assay, SspH2 activity is assessed by the presence and intensity of high molecular weight ubiquitinated species,10 detected by immunoblotting for FLAG-tagged ubiquitin. As expected, we observed a notable increase in high-molecular weight FLAG-ubiquitinated protein signal intensity at multiple time points in the presence of NOD1 (Figure 4B), indicating that interactions between NOD1 and SspH2 alter the dynamics of the latter’s enzymatic activity. Importantly, this effect was observed to be specific to NOD1, as GST-NOD1, but not GST alone, increased SspH2 ubiquitin ligase activity (Figure 4C,D). While it seemed likely that some of these high-molecular weight species represented ubiquitinated NOD1, we did not detect this via immunoblotting nor by tandem mass spectrometry of Gly-Gly remnants on NOD1 peptides1. Together, these data suggest that while SspH2 binds to NOD1 directly, thereby potentiating SspH2 ubiquitin ligase activity, there may be a requirement for a host cofactor, such as SGT1, or a host-encoded E3 ligase, to direct NOD1 ubiquitination.

Four Lysine Residues on NOD1 are Important for Its Activation by SspH2

To assess whether these prioritized lysine residues were required for basal NOD1 activity, we individually mutated the lysine residues to arginine (to prevent ubiquitination) and tested their activity in our NOD1 functional assay (Figure 6A). Our data indicated that none of the lysine variants had a suppressive effect on the ability of NOD1 to be activated by its agonist. We did note that the NOD1 variants K784R, K802R, and K858R yielded small, but statistically significant increases in basal levels of IL-8 secretion when activated (Figure 6A). As suggested by the functional assay data, mutation of lysine to arginine did not alter the expression of these NOD1 variant proteins in transiently transfected cells (Figure S3).

To determine whether any of these lysine residues were important for SspH2-mediated enhancement of NOD1 activity, we repeated the NOD1 functional assay in the presence of SspH2. Intriguingly, lysine to arginine variants in the NOD1 CARD (K24) and NBD (K324 and K473) domains significantly inhibited SspH2 activation of NOD1 by more than 50%. Although not statistically significant, another lysine variant in the NBD (K328R) also showed >50% inhibition in SspH2’s ability to activate NOD1 (Figure 6B), and was included in follow-up experiments.

To ensure that lysine to arginine variants at positions 24, 324, 328, or 473 did not affect the ability of SspH2 to bind to NOD1, we performed reciprocal coimmunoprecipitations from cell lysates coexpressing SspH2 and the variant NOD1 constructs. This analysis indicated that these NOD1 variant proteins still interact with SspH2 (Figure 6C). Taken together, these data further support a model where SspH2, either directly or indirectly, facilitates ubiquitination of lysines in the CARD and NBD domains of NOD1 to augment its pro-inflammatory signaling.

Discussion

In this study, we uncovered novel aspects of bacterial effector biology through its interaction with an archetypal NLRC. Here, we report that the interaction between the S. Typhimurium E3 ubiquitin ligase SspH2 and NOD1 is direct and facilitated by the NBD and LRR domains of NOD1. This interaction leads to SspH2-mediated increases in activation of NOD1 via ubiquitination of a specific NOD1 surface, and reciprocal potentiation of SspH2 E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. SspH2 also interacts with and activates NOD2. Moreover, the interactions between SspH2 and the NLRCs appear to be specific, as SspH1 did not bind either NOD1 or NOD2. The enhancement of NLR activity by SspH2 appears to occur through the NF-κB pathway, although other signaling pathways may make important contributions downstream of NOD2. This difference could be due to differences in protein structure – NOD2 has an additional CARD motif,25 but there may also be different protein interaction patterns or ubiquitination of NOD2. In this work, our ubiquitination analyses focused on NOD1.

Due to the ability of SspH2 to interact with multiple NLRCs, it is tempting to speculate that it interacts with other proteins within this family, or even across NLR families e.g., NLRPs. NLRP3 inflammasome activity is regulated by ubiquitination. Ubiquitination via Pellino2,38 and deubiquitination on the NLRP3 LRR domain via BRCC339,40 have been shown to activate NLRP3. Alternatively, deubiquitination via IRAK1,38 and ubiquitination by TRIM31 have also been shown to decrease NLRP3 activity by inducing proteasomal degradation in the case of the latter.41 Notably, the bacterial E3 ligase, YopM, has been observed to decrease NLRP3 activation via K63-linked ubiquitination of NLRP3.42 YopM is an unconventional E3 ubiquitin ligase, in that it contains an LRR domain, but it does not contain a NEL domain.43 Here, we make the novel discovery that SspH2, a bona fide member of the NEL family, enhances NLRC pro-inflammatory signaling via mediating targeted ubiquitination.

Our observation that NOD1 potentiates SspH2 activity is consistent with a model of substrate-induced NEL activation. NEL family members are thought to be autoinhibited in the absence of substrate, whereby the LRR domain conceals the catalytic NEL domain until alleviated by substrate binding.7 This has been demonstrated in the interaction between SspH1 and its substrate, PKN1, which increases SspH1 ubiquitin ligase activity.44 Our results suggest that a similar mechanism occurs when NOD1 binds to SspH2, whereby NOD1 increases the activity of SspH2. This implicates NOD1 as a direct interactor with, and potential substrate of, SspH2. However, we did not observe the ubiquitination of recombinant NOD1 by SspH2 in in vitro ubiquitination assays. By contrast, we previously showed NOD1 ubiquitination in the presence of catalytically active SspH2 in cell culture.10 This implicates additional host cell factors in directing NOD1 ubiquitination, potentially the NOD1 cochaperone SGT1 that interacts with both NOD1 and SspH2,10,45,46 or perhaps another E3 ubiquitin ligase that is recruited by SspH2 to direct NOD1 ubiquitination. Alternatively, both SspH2 and NOD1 are reported to localize to plasma membranes as a consequence of S-palmitoylation.16,47 Therefore, it is conceivable that membrane association by one or both proteins is required for NOD1 ubiquitination. Future studies using targeted mutation of S-palmitoylation sites could delineate the contribution of protein localization changes to NOD1 activation by SspH2.

Due to the nature of trypsin cleavage of ubiquitin from lysine residues, we were unable to discern whether NOD1 might be poly- or monoubiquitinated. It has been reported that SspH2 creates K48-linked polyubiquitin chains, suggesting that its targets are meant for proteasomal degradation.48,49 While this would be consistent with the generation of the high molecular weight ubiquitinated species observed in our in vitro ubiquitination assays, it is difficult to reconcile with the NOD1 activation phenotype we observe for catalytically active SspH2. Based upon this dichotomy, coupled with the lack of detectable ubiquitinated NOD1 in vitro, we cannot rule out the possibility that another E3 ubiquitin ligase is recruited to the SspH2-NOD1 complex to direct NOD1 ubiquitination.

Interestingly, despite our analysis of LC-MS/MS peptide intensities revealing that the majority of differential ubiquitination between SspH2 WT and C580A occurred in the LRR domain of NOD1, mutation of LRR lysines had little effect on SspH2’s capacity to increase pro-inflammatory cytokine production, in contrast to lysines in the CARD and NBD regions. The spatial localization of lysines critical for enhanced NOD1 activation could suggest that this region of the NBD is where the catalytic cysteine of SspH2 is oriented, or alternatively, is the site of recruitment of another E3 ligase. Structural studies could shed further light on these possibilities and contribute to a greater understanding of how NELs interact with NLRs.

There are reports that NOD1 can interact with ubiquitin at Y88 and E84, and the corresponding sites on NOD2, I104, and L200, in the CARD regions of both proteins.50,51 This binding of ubiquitin is distinct from post-translational ubiquitin modification, and prevents RIP2 binding to NLRs, to negatively regulate signaling.50,51 There are also predictions of ubiquitin binding at K436 and K445 on NOD2.50 NOD1 and NOD2 interact with host proteins to mediate downstream activation of NF-κB through homotypic CARD domain interaction with RIP2.19,24,25,52 This process involves many proteins interacting with RIP2 to increase or decrease signaling capacity to maintain appropriate levels of inflammatory activation. Polyubiquitination of RIP2 by host E3 ligases, e.g., XIAP, cIAP1/2, ITCH, and Pellino3 induces RIP2 activation upon NOD1/NOD2 stimulation.24,25,52 The deubiquitinase proteins A20, OTULIN, and CYLD remove ubiquitin from RIP2 to repress its activation.39,50,53 Once RIP2 is ubiquitinated, TAK1 is recruited to phosphorylate IκBa of the IKK complex, leading to NF-κB translocation to the nucleus and production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.54 These conserved host signaling pathways downstream of receptors are where bacterial E3 ligases like IpaH4.5 and SspH1 usually modulate inflammation.11,28

In a recent report by Bullones-Bolaños et al., SspH2-NOD1 interactions were not detected in a yeast two-hybrid system.48 While yeast do encode a homologue of SGT1, we previously demonstrated that Saccharomyces cerevisiae Sgt1p can neither interact with SspH2 nor act as a cochaperone to facilitate NLR responses in planta(10) At face value, this would suggest that mammalian, but not yeast, SGT1 facilitates a productive interaction between SspH2 and NOD1. In this work, we show that SGT1 is not required for SspH2 and NOD1 to interact, but we suggest that SGT1 may stabilize the complex to facilitate NOD1 activation. This would be consistent with our earlier observation that human SGT1 can stabilize and increase the activity of SspH2 in vitro.10 It is also noteworthy that while Bullones-Bolaños et al. did not observe SspH2-dependent increases in IL-8 secretion from transfected HeLa cells as we report here, their study48 did not specifically examine NOD1-dependent cytokine secretion, e.g., by the addition of iE-DAP as performed by us and others.10,45

To our knowledge, this is the first report that mechanistically links NEL effector activity to functional enhancement of an NLR. Though our studies do not unequivocally demonstrate that SspH2 directly ubiquitinates NOD1, our demonstration of direct binding between NOD1 and SspH2, which results in changes to SspH2 ubiquitination patterns, provides strong evidence that this may be the case. Moreover, the report that YopM directly ubiquitinates NLRP342 suggests that SspH2 might also work directly. When NOD1 is activated, it undergoes a structural change where the LRR domain is repositioned to relieve its blockade of the CARD and NBD regions.24 It remains to be seen what ubiquitination of NOD1 does to physically alter NOD1 and its ability to interact with other proteins in the cell. One possibility is that ubiquitination of NOD1 leads to structural perturbations that relieve repression by the LRR domain, allowing more facile NOD1 oligomerization. Another possibility is that ubiquitinating NOD1 in the vicinity of adapter binding sites could alter interaction dynamics with RIP2, leading to increased IL-8 secretion (Figure 8). Our previous observation that SspH2 enhanced NOD1 activity in the absence of NOD1 agonist would be consistent with the first scenario,10 but further study is required to discern between these models. Nevertheless, our current work has illustrated the sophisticated nature of host–pathogen interactions and uncovered a mechanism whereby a traditionally antimicrobial pathway is subverted for pathogenesis.
