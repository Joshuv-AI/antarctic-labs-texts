Reimagining the Rheumatology Fellowship Interview: Using Participatory Design‐Thinking Process to Create a More Applicant‐Centered Experience
Authors: Bharat Kumar, Alick Feng, Gatr‐alnada Gheriani, Ayesha Iftekhar, Ruoning Ni, Mohamad Dimachkie, Gizem Gokalp, Izza Bazigh, Lindsay Moy, Chen Chao, Ankitha Lingamaneni, Axi Patel, Gretchen Santana Cepero, Tariq Iqtidar, Patricia Bruffey Thoene, Aaron Knaack, Melissa L Swee, Manish Suneja, Benjamin Davis
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDesign thinking is a creative problem‐solving process used to better understand users’ needs and experiences so that a product or service can be improved. Its emphasis on empathy, iterative prototyping, and participatory collaboration make it an ideal methodology for innovation in medical education. We apply this framework to the virtual rheumatology fellowship interview process so that interviews can become more applicant centered.MethodsThis educational quality improvement project uses a design‐thinking framework to identify opportunities and challenges for rheumatology fellowship applicants. The investigators use the 5‐step process (Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test) and incorporate rapid qualitative analysis of semistructured interviews to innovate the interview experience. The iterative and collaborative nature of this process has empowered participants to codesign an applicant‐centered interview experience.ResultsInterviews with fellowship applicants (n = 9), fellow physicians (n = 4), and faculty members (n = 3) identified three major dynamics of the interview process: (1) Is it a safe environment to ask questions? (2) How do I exchange information effectively? and (3) How do I fit all these data into the bigger picture? Creative brainstorming techniques at a series of three workshops yielded four prototypes emphasizing customization, hybridization, facilitation, and preparation. A finalized applicant‐centered interview template was devised in preparation for the 2023‐2024 application season.ConclusionDesign thinking has yielded insights into three important dynamics that drive applicant experiences. These insights allow for a redesign of processes so that virtual interviews can be more applicant centered. This framework allows for further iterations and modifications as the needs of applicants and programs evolve over time.

INTRODUCTION

Due to the COVID‐19 pandemic, interviews for residency and fellowship programs have largely transitioned to virtual platforms (1). These virtual interviews are important sources of information for both applicants and interviewers, but many unanswered questions exist as to how this transition impacts the fellow applicant selection process (2). With respect to residency interviews, data suggest that both applicants and interviewers consider virtual interviews to be preferable and overall as effective as face‐to‐face interviews (3). More specifically, data from the American College of Rheumatology's Committee on Training and Workforce suggest that only 20% of those applicants that went on match into rheumatology fellowship starting in 2022 would prefer in‐person‐only interviews (4). Therefore, it appears that virtual interviews provide advantages to applicants to rheumatology fellowship and are likely to remain a feature of the application process for the foreseeable future.

However, there are several drawbacks. Compared with face‐to‐face interviews, applicants view virtual interviews as less effective for communicating program culture and in providing adequate socio‐environmental information (3). Additionally, survey data suggest program director concerns about bias in selecting applicants and fatigue in using video‐conferencing software (5). Addressing these barriers requires creative and innovative solutions. Design‐thinking process is a framework for creative problem‐solving that emphasizes empathy as a way to understand users, challenge assumptions, redefine problems, and generate solutions (6).

At a large Midwestern academic medical center, an interdisciplinary design team of nine applicants, four fellow physicians, three faculty members, and two coordinators sought to reimagine the virtual rheumatology fellowship interview experience. Through this framework, the design team identified three underlying dynamics that govern the applicant experience and developed a finalized product for the 2023‐2024 rheumatology fellowship application season.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Settings and participants

The University of Iowa's rheumatology fellowship program is based in a large tertiary care hospital and health care system (University of Iowa Hospitals and Clinics) in Iowa City, Iowa. It is composed of 10 faculty members, four fellows, and three division coordinators; additionally, the Division of Immunology has two rheumatology Advanced Practice Providers and one full‐time pharmacist. Through the National Residency Matching Program, the program posts two positions every year. Typically, the program interviews approximately 25 to 40 applicants annually over the course of 3 to 4 days in September and October. Since 2020, this has been entirely virtual through the Thalamus platform.

In the 2022‐2023 application season, 23 applicants were interviewed, and one position was matched. After the match, 20 of the top‐ranked applicants were contacted to better understand the needs and preferences of applicants. Nine responded and agreed to be part of a participatory quality improvement project using design thinking.

During the 2022‐2023 application season, there were 3 interview days which lasted for 5 hours. For each day, there were approximately eight scheduled interviews with fellow applicants. Five to six rheumatology faculty members participated in the process, with each interview session lasting 15 minutes. The interview day began with a 30‐minute introduction from the rheumatology fellowship program director, followed by four to six interviews by rheumatology faculty members. These interview sessions lasted for approximately 20 minutes, with 5‐minute breaks in between each interview session. Staggered breaks, lasting in total for 85 minutes, were provided for both faculty members and fellow applicants. Interview days concluded with 40 minutes of fellow‐applicant time and a 10‐minute program director wrap‐up. Faculty members were provided access to the fellowship applications beforehand but did not have training about how to interview beforehand. Additionally, fellow applicants did not receive any physical elements through the mail prior to the interviews.

Data collection and analysis

This quality improvement project was submitted to the University of Iowa Institutional Review Board and determined not to be human subjects research because it constitutes a nongeneralizable educational initiative.

In line with design thinking, this project was conceptualized as having five interlinked, nonconsecutive phases: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, and Test (Figure 1). Additionally, rapid qualitative analysis was employed to facilitate iterative looping between phases, consistent with other quality improvement and implementation science initiatives.

The design team used the 5‐step process (Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test) to reimagine the fellowship interview process.

Empathize and define phases

To understand applicant experiences, motivations, and needs, the design team engaged in semistructured interviews with nine applicants over Zoom. A member of the design team used an interview script containing five open‐ended questions (supplemental material). These interviews were recorded and transcribed onto Microsoft Word (Microsoft®, Redmond, WA).

Hamilton's rapid qualitative analysis approach was utilized to ensure timely development and modification of implementation strategies while upholding rigor (7, 8). Rapid qualitative analysis is comparable to traditional qualitative methods for the purposes of quality improvement and implementation (9). First, neutral domain names were created corresponding with each interview question. Then, the design team developed an interview summary template. This template was applied for three transcripts and revised by the team for usability, relevance, readability, organization, and thoroughness. This revised template was then applied for all of the nine transcripts. Finally, summary points were transferred to a matrix. This display enabled the design team to create an empathy map (Figure 2) and define human‐centered problem statements.

An empathy map was constructed to summarize and visualize information regarding the applicants’ behaviors and attitudes.

Ideation phase

Once the problem statements and empathy map were generated and harmonized, a series of workshops were scheduled to facilitate creative brainstorming among faculty members, fellows, and applicants. Activities to generate a set of broadly based ideas included (i) “worst possible ideas,” (ii) storyboarding, (iii) analogizing, and (iv) diverge/converge thinking.

Prototype and Test phases

Based on these ideas, the design team proceeded to sketch prototypes. Two rounds of prototyping were performed: low‐fidelity, in which ideas were planned for in broad brushstrokes, and high‐fidelity, in which appealing prototypes were elaborated further. High‐fidelity prototypes were then tested sequentially in order to identify further areas for improvement and innovation. Users included applicants, fellows, and faculty members. Users were observed and were asked follow‐up questions to further empathize with applicants, define problems, and generate novel ideas.

RESULTS

Empathize and Define: the three dynamics governing the applicant experience

The initial Iowa design team (BK, BD, QG, AF, AI, and LN) partnered with nine candidates to initiate this design‐thinking process. Each of the nine candidates were interviewed by a member of the design team. Analysis of these interviews yielded three major dynamics governing the applicant experience: (i) security, (ii) communicability, and (iii) integrity.

Security: is this a safe environment for me to ask questions?

Applicants relayed that a fundamental prerequisite for a successful interview day was the creation of a safe environment where questions could be raised about the program and how the program's features relate to the applicant's interests and goals.

Applicants were particularly cognizant of power dynamics. Throughout the interview season, applicants felt pressured to couch their answers in appealing ways to interviewers. Some noted a judgmental aspect where they were being asked to perform under stress, particularly with pointed questions, clinical scenarios, and responding to criticism of their research. Navigating these power differentials made it difficult for applicants to ask the types of questions that were necessary to make an informed decision about a program. This was particularly so among applicants from self‐identified minoritized backgrounds, who voiced that they were looking for programs where trainees of their backgrounds would be treated equitably.

Conversely, applicants felt safer when (i) dealing with fellows and junior faculty, (ii) questions were invited by interviewers, (iii) faculty and fellows represented diverse backgrounds, (iv) interviewer–interviewee interactivity appeared conversational and natural, (v) nonverbal cues corroborated what was being stated, and (vi) interactions among interviewers appeared cordial.

Communicability: how can I effectively exchange information?

The second major dynamic is the ability to effectively exchange information during the virtual interview. Technological, logistical, and contextual barriers can prevent applicants from satisfactorily expressing themselves.

Technological barriers include poor internet connection, fear of technical failure, and poor usability of digital platforms. One applicant expressed frustration that during an interview session, his interviewer had trouble logging into the system, and that, as a result, “the warm‐up time” to create an impression was reduced. Even when logged in, applicants wondered whether information was being exchanged accurately when there were pauses or faculty interviewers seemed to change track suddenly: “There are times when you speak over each other and there's not much you can do apart from awkwardly just wait.” The digital platform was also a barrier to several applicants—“So many links! When you're trying to concentrate, it's hard to keep them all straight.”

Compounding these barriers were logistical issues. These include the short length of interview sessions and disruptions in flow due to background interruptions. One applicant described the length of interview sessions as “sometimes too short and sometimes too long. When you really hit it off with a faculty member, then it's time to go. It's easier the other way, where when there's a lot of quiet time, you can kind of find something else to talk about.” Another issue that prevents adequate exchange of information is the presence of interruptions, such as beepers, traffic, people in the background, and text messages or phone calls occurring in the interim. Even when they did not lead to an interruption in conversation, they seemed to, as one applicant described it, “take you out of the moment.”

Other barriers to effective information exchange were contextual in nature. Some applicants felt they could not pick up nonverbal cues as effectively. One mentioned, “How do the fellows interact with each other? When they're all in their little boxes, you can see each fellow but not the fellows.” Another applicant said that small but meaningful gestures like the warm welcome into a faculty member's office, the firmness of a handshake, and the offers to sit and have a drink of water were missing. On a larger scale, without the ability to see the physical space and facilities, it becomes difficult to make an informed decision about where to train and live for a significant portion of their early professional and personal lives.

Integrity: what do I fit all these data into the bigger picture?

Applicants also relayed that the interview experience was only part of a larger selection process to rank fellowship programs. Applicants generally divided this into three stages: preinterview, interview, and postinterview.

The preinterview stage starts off with applicants exploring fellowship programs based on word of mouth and publicly available information online. This determines whether or not applicants would like to apply to a given program. Once interview invites are sent, this information gathering process accelerates, with further scrutiny placed on sources of information such as websites, social media, and private internet forums. Some applicants also disclose researching faculty members and fellows online prior to the interview.

This background information obtained preinterview can be valuable in setting the stage for the interview but also overwhelming. The interview day helps to shape, humanize, validate, and contextualize this information. However, because interview sessions are time limited, there is a great deal of cognitive burden that is placed on applicants. Applicants try to manage this by proactively taking notes during and in between interview sessions. Applicants mention that, on the day of the interview, they compare and contrast content from different sessions with different faculty members to get a holistic view of the program.

Once the interview day is completed, applicants collate this information, either formally or informally. Some actively compare features of each fellowship program as they continue through fellowship interview season, whereas others contrast features once all the interviews are completed. Certain applicants reach out to programs to send letters demonstrating interest and for clarifications of any points from interviews. This culminates with ranking the fellowship programs.

Altogether, this process entails a great deal of cognitive burden, one that is multiplied with each successive fellowship program to which an applicant interviews. Therefore, the cognitive burden gets compounded as the interview season continues on.

Ideation: reorienting ourselves to fellowship interviews

A series of three workshops were set up to discuss the results of the Empathize and Define phases and generate ideas for improvement. The core design team was joined by five of the nine applicants as well as faculty members to rethink virtual fellowship interviews. Activities to promote idea generation included challenging assumptions, “worst possible scenarios,” storyboarding, analogizing, and diverge/converge thinking.

What is the purpose of the interview?

At these workshops, the multifaceted nature of fellowship interviews was discussed. Although the main purpose of interviews is to provide applicants with information about a given fellowship program so that they can decide how highly (or lowly) to rank the program, it serves other, important functions. For applicants, other functions include (i) networking with senior rheumatologists, (ii) understanding fellow life ahead of them, (iii) presenting their own research and scholarly activities, and (iv) representing the robustness of their residency programs. Similarly, for the fellowship program, interviewers noted additional purposes of the interview: (i) to publicize the fellowship program (“open house”), (ii) to connect with promising future junior rheumatologists, and (iii) to reflect on the strengths and weaknesses of the fellowship program for strategic planning.

These insights provide the foundation for the redesign of fellowship interviews. Diverge/converge thinking was used to generate ideas for how to accommodate these needs, as well as how to harmonize competing strategies of applicants and interviewers. Virtual interviews were analogized as having elements of face‐to‐face interviews, telemedicine visits, auditions, and speed‐dating (Figure 3). One major assumption that was challenged was that the virtual interview is a scheduled time to transmit information about a fellowship program. Rather, the interview should ideally be a discourse that helps applicants to determine whether they would fit into a program. Information about a program should be offloaded onto websites, social media, and other spaces to “clear the mind‐space” for such a discourse.

Virtual interviews have analogies to face‐to‐face interviews, telemedicine encounters, speed‐dating, and auditions. Fellowship programs can apply lessons from these types of encounters to optimize the virtual interview. DEI, diversity, equity, and inclusion.

How much should fellows be involved in the interview process?

Virtually all those involved agreed that current rheumatology fellows play important roles in the interview process. Because fellows are closer to applicants in their career trajectories, fellows are seen as senior and trusted colleagues who can provide insights into the lived experiences of fellows outside of clinical and educational activities. As such, fellows serve as “bridges” between applicants and faculty members.

Rapid brainstorming sessions during the workshop series yielded three important ideas. First, the role of the fellows should be as resources for applicants rather than as assessors of applicant quality. Second, fellow‐applicant time should be prioritized to help to establish a safe space for applicants. Third, fellow sessions need to be unstructured to preserve the authenticity of the experience.

Should interview segments be streamed like other online video content?

The most radical and polarizing idea that emerged from the brainstorming sessions was to stream interview segments on a platform such as YouTube® (Google®; San Bruno, CA). The rationale is that such segments can expand the reach of the program in a cost‐effective manner. A recording of segments such as the program director's welcome and conversation with the fellows may be standardized enough that they can be recorded and distributed for a larger audience. For applicants, this may help them in their preparation prior to the interview day. The spontaneity and unscripted nature of these segments may also help to present the program in an authentic manner.

Although intriguing, the majority of applicants, faculty members, and fellows were against this idea. Reasons for being against this included (i) that the accuracy of content cannot be guaranteed, (ii) the potential loss of anonymity for applicants, and (iii) the conversion of informational sessions into advertising. However, this suggestion highlights a deeper need to provide information in multimedia formats prior to interview day. The creation of short videos that contain background information may be helpful to promote more engaging discussions during interview day.

Prototype and Test: making a better interview experience

As an outgrowth of the Empathize, Define, and Ideate phases, the design team devised five prototypes for virtual interviews. Each prototype incorporated four key aspects of an applicant‐centered interview: customization, hybridization, facilitation, and preparation. These five prototypes were evaluated by the design team in order to identify features that would be most actionable and most beneficial. Out of these discussions and rounds of testing, a finalized deliverable, the applicant‐centered interview, was devised.

Customization of content during the applicant‐centered interview

Both applicants and interviewers relayed the importance of establishing rapport early in each encounter. Creating opportunities to develop this rapport while reserving sufficient time to discuss content is a delicate balance. One way to accommodate these two pressing needs involves the use of a more flexible scheduling arrangement.

Applicants will be sent a questionnaire a few days prior to their interview, inquiring about their clinical, scholarly, and extracurricular interests. Together with information from their Electronic Residency Application Service® applications, the program director and coordinator will schedule the applicant with each participating faculty member for either a 20‐minute (long) or 15‐minute (short) encounter. Under this prototype, each applicant would have eight interview sessions with faculty, four of which would be long encounters earlier in the day, focused on career development and mentorship, and four of which would be short encounters, focused more on compatibility and familiarity.

Hybridization of elements of the virtual interview

Virtual interviewing was overall considered preferable to face‐to‐face interviewing. However, the intangible nature of the virtual interview made the experience feel distanced and less personable. To help remedy this, the finalized prototype would include two tangible elements. The first is a “swag‐bag,” a small box of items sent to applicants that contains artifacts pertinent to our fellowship program. These artifacts are selected by the fellows themselves as representatives of what it feels like to be in the program itself. The second is a guidebook of the fellowship program. The guidebook helps to reduce the cognitive burden by emphasizing the important points of the program. It also has copious amounts of white space so that applicants can jot notes during the interview.

Facilitation of the interview day by fellow physicians

Results from the Empathize and Define phases consistently demonstrated the importance of fellow‐applicant interactions. Increasing exposure between fellows and applicants is highly preferable but pushes against the reality that fellows do not have unlimited time to spend with applicants. Currently, fellows spend about 40 minutes with applicants as a group to answer questions that applicants may have about their experiences toward the end of the interview day.

To become more applicant centered, the design team devised several prototypes in which there were multiple points of contact between fellows and applicants. After the introductory video and program director's welcome, the interview day starts with an informal, unstructured group discussion with fellows. Then, at the end of the interview day, a last 20‐minute group session is planned for any additional questions.

Preparation of faculty to embrace applicant‐centered interviewing

Applicants also voiced the need for interviewers to be adequately prepared prior to the interviews. To accommodate this, all of the prototypes included plans for faculty members to attend a preinterview huddle session, which reviews (i) National Resident Matching Program® guidelines, (ii) best practices for internal medicine subspecialty residency interviews, and (iii) training regarding diversity, equity, and inclusion. This will enable interviewers to create a safe environment for effective exchange of information. Additionally, prior to each interview day, interviewers will be provided a brief standardized synopsis of each application to facilitate conversations. These synopses focus more on extracurricular activities and points of mutual interest.

Initial testing of protocols: a finalized reimagined applicant‐centered interview process

These protocols were tested by the design team and ranked in order of preference. Based on mutual discussions, the top‐ranked design was selected and revised to accommodate any lingering concerns (Figure 4). The applicant‐centered interview process is conceptualized as having three phases (preinterview, interview, and postmatch). During the preinterview phase, applicant preferences for the interview are gathered, faculty members are briefed on applicants, and tangible elements of the interview are sent out. This prepares the applicant for a customized experience during the day of the interview. The interview day is then planned to prioritize meeting with fellows, deep engagement with potential primary mentors, and discussions with interviewers that share mutual interests. Finally, a postmatch survey will be sent to applicants with invitations for discussions to obtain further insights leading to further iterations of design thinking in the future.

The finalized co‐designed prototype incorporates four principles: facilitation, customization, preparation, and hybridization. DEI, diversity, equity, and inclusion; NRMP, National Resident Matching Program®.

This finalized version was evaluated by all nine of the prior applicants, fellows, and faculty members. The finalized product was deemed to be feasible and preferable to the current system, although concerns were raised about sustainability beyond 2024.

This top‐ranked design differs from the current process in several ways. First, it sees the interview process as lasting more than just one day of interactions, but rather having three phases. Secondly, applicant preferences for the interview day are prioritized, such as time with the fellows and with interviewers that share their interests. Thirdly, there is greater support for the interview process for fellows through hybridized elements that were not present before and for interviewers through dedicated briefing time about interviewees and the interview process.

DISCUSSION

The movement toward virtual interviews initially started as a response to the ongoing COVID‐19 pandemic but appears to be a permanent feature of the fellowship application process. The decreased amount of time, costs, and effort make it more appealing to both applicants and fellowship programs alike. However, as our findings corroborate, the principles of face‐to‐face interviewing cannot be properly translated into the virtual format. Rather, virtual interviewing also has elements of speed‐dating, telemedicine encounters, and auditions. Using design thinking, our team was able to reimagine what the rheumatology fellowship interview would look like at our institution if it were more applicant centered.

Of note, this project is a novel application of the design‐thinking process. As of June 2023, this represents the first known application of the design‐thinking process to address candidate selection in graduate medical education. Prior applications of design thinking in medical education include development of tools for care transitions, virtual health training, procedural simulations, and well‐being initiatives (10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15). Because interviews are, by their nature, human‐centered, it was a natural decision to use such a quality improvement framework to better characterize the interview process and to identify areas for improvement. As part of this innovation, we adapted several design‐thinking tools, including empathy maps, storyboarding, and analogizing, as key parts of this quality improvement project. This is in contrast to most prior scholarly works on fellowship interviews, which tend to employ more quantitative and qualitative research methods, like surveys and semistructured interviews, to create generalizable results rather than to guide process improvements within one particular setting.

It is validating that many of our findings broadly align with what has been previously described in the literature about virtual interviews. A systematic review of virtual interviewing for graduate medical education recruitment and selection demonstrates similar findings: that virtual interviews pose challenges in assessing “fit,” that applicants prefer three to five individual interviews lasting 15 to 20 minutes each, and that opportunities to connect with faculty and trainees are priorities for applicants (16). More specifically, multiple surveys have shown that fellowship applicants are interested in hybridized approaches to interviewing in order to better understand program culture (17, 18). Concerns about communicability and security, which we found in the Empathize and Define phases, are echoed in another survey of vitreoretinal surgery fellowship applicants who expressed similar concerns (19). Another survey, among hand surgery fellowship applicants, showed how fellow applicants prioritize time with current fellows, another key finding of our investigation (20). The concerns about how to fit data from interviews into larger assessments is also mentioned in a survey of reproductive endocrinology and infertility fellow applicants (21). The results from this investigation provide an actionable approach for fellowship programs to address these issues in practice.

Three major factors contributed to our success. First, our process was highly participatory, with applicants considered equal partners in the reimagination of the fellowship interview experience throughout all the aspects of the design‐thinking process. This participatory approach has been critical to understanding these dynamics, because power differentials characterize many facets of the interview process and may pose barriers to obtaining feedback through more conventional approaches such as surveys.

Second, we adhered to the core principles of design thinking. At the heart of design thinking is challenging assumptions about conventional processes through empathic responses. The design team sought to radically rethink how virtual interviews are conducted, resulting in certain aspects being preserved and certain other aspects being discarded. Moreover, this depth of discussions and interviews enabled us to interface the different phases, such as the Prototype and Empathize phases, more effectively.

Lastly, this project was undertaken in a relatively short period of time immediately after the match. Recognizing that memories become less reliable over time, ensuring that insights could be collected and analyzed as soon as possible was a priority. Additionally, our emphasis on rapid qualitative analysis and weekly workshops provided a brisk pace for reimagination.

Final testing phase

Of note, design‐thinking process is open‐ended, multifaceted, and dynamic. Therefore, the results presented herein are subject to rapid change in order to accommodate the needs of applicants. We envision that, starting in the 2023‐2024 season, we will critically test and evaluate the outcomes of this process so that we can revisit the Empathize, Define, and Ideate phases. Results from those will then empower us to modify the prototype further. As part of this continual reevaluation, we plan on gathering data to assess how increased applicant‐centeredness affects the interview experience, application process, and fellowship program operations as a whole.

Limitations

Our educational quality improvement project is not without its limitations. First, due to the annual nature of interviews, we do not have results from testing on applicants themselves. Formal evaluation during the application season through surveys and semistructured interviews presents ethical challenges due to the power differentials that we would like to avoid altogether. Moreover, the Hawthorne effect, that is, that the act of observation is likely to affect the response of participants, would skew outcomes anyway. Therefore, testing is limited to testing of prototypes rather than the final product.

Second, the results may not be generalizable even if the process can be used elsewhere. There is considerable diversity in viewpoints, perspectives, and needs within the community of applicants, and interviewees are only a small subset of total applicants. At our program, the program director uses a holistic review process based on personal statements, letters of recommendation, academic records, and standardized test scores, among other data, to select those applicants that are most likely to be compatible with the program. This homogenizes the group of interviewees, who subsequently participated in this project. Although the quality improvement team did consider using design‐thinking principles to identify areas for improvement in the earlier stage of review, it was deemed less appropriate as a quality improvement methodology for this purpose. Likewise, the specific recommendations that have come out of this process may not be applicable to other settings because each program has different needs and different baselines.

Third, we sought to have a collaborative process between the core design team and the fellow applicants. However, due to scheduling issues, only five of the nine were able to participate during the ideation phase. It certainly can be argued that greater involvement during the ideation phase could have led to more creative ideas and generated alternative prototypes for testing. To mitigate this, we did provide many opportunities in the prototype and testing phases to reexamine ideas with members of both the core design team and the fellow applicants. Nevertheless, this does represent a limitation.

Design thinking has yielded insights into the rheumatology fellowship interview process and enabled our program to develop an applicant‐centered interview. Three major dynamics underscore the interview day: (i) Is this a safe environment to ask questions? (ii) How can I effectively exchange information? and (iii) How do I fit all these data into the bigger picture? To optimize these dynamics, four prototypes were designed that emphasize four areas of innovation: customization, facilitation, hybridization, and preparation. These were then iteratively tested and harmonized to develop a final service that will be implemented and evaluated in 2023‐2024. As the needs and preferences of applicants and interviewers evolve, we will continue to apply the principles of design thinking to refine our interview processes so that they are more equitable and applicant centered.
