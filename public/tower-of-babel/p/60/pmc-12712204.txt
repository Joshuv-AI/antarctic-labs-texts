Feline vitamin D-dependent rickets type 2 caused by a missense variant in the vitamin D receptor gene
Authors: Sora SUZUKI, Hitomi MASUYAMA, Jiro MIYAMAE, Hiroaki HEMMI, Naoaki MATSUKI, Kohei MURAKAMI
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn 11-month-old female kitten was evaluated for lameness and an inability to close her mouth. The kitten had marked hypocalcemia with elevated intact parathyroid hormone and 1,25(OH)2D3 levels. Radiographic imaging indicated generalized osteopenia and dysplasia of temporomandibular joints. Since a nutritionally complete diet was being fed, dysfunction of vitamin D receptors (VDR) was suspected. Genetic tests revealed three variants (c.439A >G, c.509C >T, and c.529_530insGCA) in the VDR gene, and c.509C >T, replacing proline 170 with leucine (p.Pro170Leu), was predicted to be damaging by in silico analysis. VDR-deficient feline kidney epithelial cells were transfected with wild-type or p.Pro170Leu VDR expression vectors, revealing impaired responsiveness to 1,25(OH)2D3 in mutants. Based on these findings, the cat was diagnosed with vitamin D-dependent rickets type 2.Keywords: cat, missense variant, rickets, vitamin D-dependent rickets type 2

The vitamin D hormonal metabolite, 1,25(OH)2D3, is essential for calcium and phosphate metabolism. Many animals have the capacity to make vitamin D when exposed to sunlight, but cats are unable to synthesize it adequately in the skin [11]. Therefore, the only available source of vitamin D for cats is dietary. Following intestinal absorption, vitamin D is hydroxylated to 25(OH) D3, primarily catalyzed by CYP2R1 in the liver [2]. Subsequently, in renal proximal tubules, CYP27B1 converts 25(OH) D3 to the most hormonally active form, 1,25(OH)2D3 [10]. Failure to achieve normal bone mineral accretion by 1,25(OH)2D3 in young animals leads to secondary hyperparathyroidism and decreased bone mineralization, resulting in development of rickets.

Rickets causes inadequate mineralization of growing bone or osteoid tissue, and is mainly caused by nutritional or hereditary factors. Insufficient intake of vitamin D leads to nutritional rickets [3]; however, commercial pet foods contain two to ten times more than the minimum amounts of vitamin D recommended by the Association of American Feed Control Officials. Therefore, rickets due to nutritional causes is only seen in animals fed improperly formulated homemade foods [21]. Hereditary rickets is caused by a loss-of-function in enzymes needed to convert vitamin D to its active form (vitamin D-dependent rickets type I–VDDR-I) or in the vitamin D receptor (VDDR type II−VDDR-II). VDDR-I is further divided into VDDR-IA and VDDR-IB, caused by mutations in CYP27B1 and CYP2R1, respectively [2]. To date, only ten cats have been reported as suffering from VDDR (three with VDDR-IA, two with -IB, and five with -II), of which only four have clarified genetic mutations including two with VDDR-IA, one with -IB, and one with VDDR-II [6, 8, 9, 20]. The case with VDDR-II had a single nucleotide deletion in the VDR gene (XM_019834851:c.106delC), which is expected to create a stop codon in exon 2, affecting >90% of all defined transcripts. With this deletion, no enzymatic activity is expected [9]. The other cases also had single nucleotide deletions or a nonsense variant resulting in premature protein truncations in CYP27B1 or CYP2R1 [6, 8, 20], so functional assays were not performed. If genetic tests identify only missense variants that do not result in protein truncation, it is difficult to predict the effect of the amino acid substitution on function and a functional assay is required. Godfrey and colleagues obtained primary fibroblasts from a feline patient and a control cat to assess the ability of radiolabeled 1,25(OH)2D3 to bind to nuclear extracts [7]. However, this test is invasive for cats and can be performed only at a limited number of facilities.

In the present case, a 10-month-old, 2.7 kg, female domestic short-haired cat was examined by referring veterinarians for lameness and difficulty in closing her mouth. The cat was inbred, having been the offspring of sibling parents, and was fed wet commercial kitten food. The referring veterinarian noted reduced skeletal mineralization in a radiograph, and a serum biochemical analysis revealed hypocalcemia (total calcium 6.8 mg/dL; reference range 8.8–11.9 mg/dL) with a normal albumin concentration (3.5 g/dL; reference range 2.3–3.5 g/dL) and high alkaline phosphatase activity (1,757 U/L; reference range <58 U/L). All other values, including urea, creatinine, and phosphorus, were within normal limits.

On initial examination, the cat was responsive, had normal vital signs, and had a hunched, stilted gait. In addition, the cat kept its mouth open and had pain on palpation of the mandible (Fig. 1A, 1B). Orthogonal radiographic imaging revealed marked osteopenia and radiolucency of the skull and spine (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Dysplasia of the temporomandibular joint was also observed (Fig. 1C). Radiographs further showed abnormally wide growth plates of long bones (Fig. 1E).

The signalment, blood test results, and radiographic abnormalities were highly suggestive of rickets. Additional blood tests revealed low-ionized calcium (0.84 mM, reference range 1.22–1.50 mM) along with hyperparathyroidism (intact parathyroid hormone (iPTH) 273 pg/mL, reference range 8.0–25.0 pg/mL). Urinalysis revealed a specific gravity of 1.050, traces of protein, and mild hematuria. These findings were compatible with severe secondary hyperparathyroidism caused by low blood calcium concentration. Dietary insufficiency of calcium or vitamin D was unlikely because co-housed siblings exhibited no symptoms. Since renal dysfunction seemed unlikely, calcium deficiency due to gastrointestinal malabsorption was suspected. Then, we found that serum vitamin D metabolite 1,25(OH)2D3 was greatly increased (729 pg/mL, reference range 22–122 pg/mL [14]), suggesting vitamin D-resistance, despite sufficient dietary vitamin D and normal function of vitamin D-converting enzymes in the liver and kidney.

Treatment with oral calcium supplementation (precipitated calcium carbonate 300 mg/kg PO bid) and a vitamin D analog (alfacalcidol 50 μg/kg PO sid) was initiated. Twelve days later, total calcium concentrations were persistently low (ranging from 5.4 to 7.4 mg/dL) despite consistent increases in oral calcium (600 mg/kg PO bid) and 1,25(OH)2D3 (100 μg/kg PO sid) supplementation. Given the high levels of plasma 1,25(OH)2D3 and the lack of improvement in calcium levels, alfacalcidol was discontinued, and we controlled the calcium level with calcium supplementation (1,000 mg/kg PD bid) alone. Five years after initial presentation, the cat was thriving, had a normal jaw movement, appetite and energy level, weighed 3.4 kg, and had no problems other than an inability to jump to high places. Repeat radiographs showed improvement in general bone density and physeal lucency (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. 1B). Total calcium and phosphorus values were within normal ranges (9.4 mg/dL and 2.7 mg/dL, respectively), although iPTH levels were still high (309.8 pg/mL).

Since VDDR-II was suspected, we analyzed the genomic sequence of VDR in this cat, with written informed consent. The feline VDR gene is located on chromosome B4 and comprises 11 exons (accession number: XM_019834851). We designed 5 primer pairs to cover all exons (Table 1). Genomic DNA was extracted from blood with a QIAamp DNA Blood Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed using high-fidelity DNA polymerase, and KOD One PCR Master Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). PCR products were cut from an agarose gel after electrophoresis and cleaned using a Nucleo Spin Gel/PCR Extraction Kit (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan). Then, each was sequenced by a commercial DNA sequencing service (Azenta Japan, Tokyo, Japan).

Direct sequencing of PCR products identified variants in exon 4 (c.439A>G) and exon 5 (c.509C>T and c.529_530insGCA) of the VDR gene, which were suspected to be homozygous (Fig. 2). These variants change the amino acid sequence of feline VDR (accession number: XP_019690410.2), resulting in p.Thr147Ala, p.Pro170Leu, and p.Gly177_Gly178insSer, respectively. These variants were not found in the DNA of another cat carrying renal carcinoma, which was extracted with approval of the ethical review committee of the Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital of Okayama University of Science (approval number: 2020-0004). These variants are located in the hinge region of human VDR, and this region is essential for interaction with transcription factor TFIIB [12]. A direct comparison of deduced amino acid sequences of VDRs from various species revealed that proline 170 is highly conserved from lampreys to humans, and even in Drosophila (Table 2). The human NM_000376.3:c.467C>T variant (equivalent to feline XM_019834851.3:c.509C>T) has not yet been registered in ClinVar and OMIM (ID: 601769), which are public archives for interpreting clinical significance of variants for reported conditions. The phenotype of this variant was unknown. Therefore, we evaluated the impact of the amino acid substitution on the structure and function of VDR using in silico damage prediction tools [1, 18]. PolyPhen-2 predicted p.Pro170Leu as probably damaging, whereas p.Thr147Ala was expected to be benign. In addition, a human DNA variant effect prediction tool MutationTaster2021 also predicted p.Pro156Leu in human VDR (corresponds to p.Pro170Leu in feline VDR) to cause disease, whereas p.Gly163_Gly164insSer (corresponds to p.Gly177_Gly178insSer in feline VDR) is expected to be benign.

Based on these findings, we performed functional analysis of the VDR carrying the p.Pro170Leu variant. First, to establish VDR-deficient feline cells, we designed a guide RNA targeting exon 2 of the feline VDR gene (5′-CTGACCCTGGCGACTTTGAC-3′) and cloned it into the pX458 vector [16]. The pX458 vector drives expression of the feline VDR gRNA, Cas9 protein, and green fluorescent protein (GFP) as a marker for transfection. The vector was transfected into the feline kidney epithelial cell line, Crandell Reese feline kidney (CRFK), and then, GFP-positive cells were sorted. We performed single-cell cloning to obtain VDR-deficient CRFK (CRFK-VDR−). Because no antibody detecting feline VDR is available, lack of VDR protein expression in CRFK-VDR− could not be confirmed, but TA cloning of exon 2 of the VDR gene confirmed that CRFK-VDR− has mutations that cause a frameshift and a premature stop codon, suggesting disruption of the VDR. Subsequently, we constructed wild-type or p.Pro170Leu carrying the VDR expression vector. The coding sequence (CDS) of wild-type VDR (XM_019834851.3) was cloned from feline kidney tissue, and c.509C>T feline VDR CDS was generated using site-directed mutagenesis by overlap extension. Then, wild-type or c.509C>T feline VDR CDS was subcloned into pIRES2-EGFP mammalian expression vector (Takara Bio).

The active form of vitamin D, 1,25(OH)2D3, binds to the VDR in the cell, which leads to formation of a VDR-retinoid X receptor (RXR) heterodimer that subsequently binds to a specific vitamin D responsive element on target genes, such as CYP24A1 [15]. Indeed, treatment with 1,25(OH)2D3 (calcitriol; Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) induced remarkable CYP24A1 mRNA expression in original CRFK, but not in CRFK-VDR−, indicating the loss of VDR function in CRFK-VDR− (Fig. 3A). Then, overexpression of wild-type VDR, but not p.Pro170Leu VDR, in CRFK-VDR− rescued 1,25(OH)2D3-induced CYP24A1 expression (Fig. 3B), indicating that the p.Pro170Leu substitution in feline VDR leads to remarkable impairment of VDR protein function. Altogether, we diagnosed the cat as having VDDR-II due to the p.Pro170Leu variant in the VDR.

VDR contains an N-terminal dual zinc finger DNA-binding domain, a C-terminal ligand-binding domain and an extensive and unstructured region that links the two functional domains. Inactivating variants that affect any domain of VDR would lead to disease development [13]. Based on functional tests, we determined that VDDR-II in the present case was caused by a p.Pro170Leu mutation, which is not localized in the two functional domains. In human VDR, eliminating the sequence between leucine 116 (corresponds to leucine 130 in feline VDR) and serine 165 (corresponds to glycine 179 in feline VDR) abolished the interaction of VDR with TFIIB [12]; therefore, p.Pro170Leu in feline VDR may impair regulation of gene expression via TFIIB. Moreover, four RXR-binding sites are located in human VDR, including 37, 91–92, 244–263, and 317–395 in the binding region [4], so it is unlikely that p.Pro170Leu in feline VDR (corresponds to p.Pro156Leu in human VDR) affected the interaction with RXR. Given that proline has a cyclic side chain that confers conformational rigidity, the p.Pro170Leu mutation might result in a structural change, resulting in loss of function of VDR as an alternative possibility. This is supported by the finding that the p.Pro156Leu mutation in human VDR is predicted to be “likely pathogenic” with the protein structure prediction tool, AlphaFold2 (Score 0.626, reference limit <0.564). Of the variants we identified, c.439A>G, which results in threonine 147 changing to alanine (p.Thr147Ala), is likely to be benign because the homologous amino acid is alanine in humans and rats (Table 2). The effect of the other mutation, p.Gly177_Gly178insSer, is unknown, since its function has not been analyzed. This is a limitation of this study.

In previous papers, cats with VDDR-II were treated with calcium and vitamin D supplementation, and four responded well, but one did not [5, 7, 9, 17, 19]. These cats presented at 3 to 4 months of age and treatment was initiated. However, in the present VDDR-II case, treatment was initiated at nearly 1 year of age; therefore, the period of rapid skeletal growth had already passed. This may explain why the cat still had difficulty jumping to high places despite improvement in serum Ca levels. Given her age at the onset of clinical signs, impairment of VDR function due to this missense mutation may have been milder than that caused by previously reported mutations. Consistent with this hypothesis, stimulation with 100-fold more 1,25(OH)2D3 (10 nM) than in the experiment shown in Fig. 3B increased CYP24A1 mRNA expression in p.Pro170Leu mutant VDR-expressing CRFK cells, albeit more weakly than wild-type VDR (Fig. 4).

In the present case, VDDR-II was suspected based on clinical signs, radiography, biochemistry, plasma iPTH, and 1,25(OH)2D3 concentrations. The lack of clinical and biochemical responses to supplementation with calcium and vitamin D also supported this supposition. In addition, missense variants identified by direct sequencing and impaired responsiveness to 1,25(OH)2D3 in the VDR with p.Pro170Leu allowed a definitive diagnosis. Since cats with VDDR-II need to receive long-term medication, it is important to confirm the diagnosis based on functional analysis as well as genetic testing. Genetic tests with primers that amplify exons of feline VDR, which we have designed, will be helpful when examining future cases of suspected VDDR-II. Further, the test we constructed based on CRFK-VDR− and a rescue experiment will not only assist definitive diagnoses of VDDR-II, it will also facilitate selection of treatment and understanding the pathogenesis of the disease.
