Moving towards inclusion of perinatal mental health in midwifery curriculums in South Africa
Authors: Kagiso P. Tukisi, Kothatso Shernies Daisy Mosia, Andile G. Mokoena-de Beer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPerinatal mental health is a global concern amongst the women of childbearing age. The prevalence of perinatal mental health amongst women of childbearing age is linked to the life-changing moments of pregnancy and childbirth. Midwives are primary caregivers in the maternal care context. The midwives’ abilities to successfully diagnose and manage perinatal mental health lie in the academic preparation during midwifery training. The study aims to explore and describe the midwives’ perceptions of perinatal mental health within the midwifery training curricula of South Africa.MethodsA qualitative, descriptive, explorative research design was followed. Seventeen purposefully sampled midwives participated in the in-depth, face-to-face semi-structured interviews. A central question followed by probes were asked. Data were analyzed using Collaizi’s descriptive method and the emerging themes were organized into main themes and categories.ResultsThree themes emerged. In theme 1, the educational gaps within the midwifery curricula in south Africa. The educational gaps led to the disconnect in application of theory in clinical practice in theme 2. In theme 3, the midwifery curricula seemed to lack interprofessional education and collaboration to expose midwives to mental health.ConclusionThis study highlights gaps in perinatal mental health education within South African midwifery curricula. These gaps contribute to midwives’ inadequate preparation for management of perinatal mental health in clinical practice. The study calls for the curriculum review to integrate perinatal mental health content to capacitate midwives to address the maternal perinatal mental health needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04522-1.

Background

Perinatal mental health disorders represent a significant public health concern in South Africa, with prevalence rates among the highest globally [1]. Research indicates that 33% of South African women experience depression or anxiety during pregnancy or the postpartum period which is triple the rate of high-income countries in a global context [1]. The specific perinatal mental conditions include antenatal and postnatal depression, anxiety disorders, psychosis, post-traumatic stress disorder and suicides risks [2, 3]. Despite this high burden, perinatal mental health has historically received limited attention in midwifery education and practice within the country [4].

The South African healthcare system continues to prioritize physical aspects of maternal care, often at the expense of psychological wellbeing [5]. This gap is particularly concerning given the established links between maternal mental health and adverse outcomes for both mothers and infants [6]. Additionally, complications during pregnancy, impaired maternal-infant bonding, and compromised child development also predispose the mothers to perinatal mental health conditions [6, 7]. The consequences of untreated perinatal mental health conditions extend beyond the immediate postpartum period, potentially affecting intergenerational wellbeing and contributing to the social and economic burden on already strained communities [7].

Midwives, as frontline maternal healthcare providers, are ideally positioned to identify, support, and refer women experiencing perinatal mental health difficulties [8]. The tripartite in maternal healthcare context namely: International confederation of midwives, United Nations Population Fund and the World health organisation recommend perinatal mental health within midwifery training [3, 9]. The tripartite recommends that the midwives must render holistic midwifery care to address physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual human dimensions [9]. Therefore, it is a recommendation that midwifery training curricula and programmes must provide midwives with mental health knowledge and skills set [9, 10]. These mental health skills are essential for midwives to assess, manage and refer patient with conditions such as perinatal anxiety, depression, psychosis and post-traumatic stress disorder [9, 10].

The world health organisation recommends that midwives’ training on perinatal mental health must comprise of both theoretical and clinical training [11]. The integration of theory and clinical will provide midwives with hands on clinical skills and the confidence to address the perinatal mental health problems [12]. However, several studies have highlighted significant knowledge gaps among South African midwives regarding perinatal mental health screening, assessment, and management [4, 13].This deficiency can be traced back to midwifery education programs that have traditionally emphasized physical aspects of care with minimal focus on psychological dimensions [13] .The midwifery curriculum in South Africa, governed by the South African Nursing Council, provides limited explicit content on perinatal mental health [5, 14]. Midwifery was historically trained under two distinct phased out curricula in South Africa [15]. The phased out training curricula were a four year diploma or Bachelor of Nursing Science (general, psychiatry, community) and midwifery (Regulation R.425) and a one year diploma in midwifery (Regulation R.254) accessible to candidates who studied diploma in general nursing (Regulation R.683) [16–18].The training programmes comprise the bachelor’s degree (Regulation R.174) in nursing and midwifery and an advanced diploma in midwifery (Regulation R.1497) accessible to candidates who studied for a diploma in nursing (Regulation R.171) [19, 20, 21]. Within the Bachelor of Nursing and midwifery, about 240 to 360 h are dedicated to mental health nursing in a general context thus limiting opportunities for exposure to perinatal mental health conditions [19] .On the other hand, mental health is a module within a diploma in nursing with 240 h dedicated to mental health content [20]. The midwifery content has theoretical introduction to perinatal mental health without mandatory clinical requirements for perinatal mental health [21]. Therefore, the integration of perinatal mental health occurs incidentally in the clinical practice which creates a gap between theory and practice [21]. This educational gap perpetuates a cycle wherein midwives enter practice inadequately prepared to address the mental health needs of women during pregnancy, labour and postpartum period [21];14. Consequently, these gaps potentially contribute to the underdiagnosis and undertreatment of perinatal mental health disorders [14].

This research examines the status of perinatal mental health education in South African midwifery programs and explores pathways toward meaningful curriculum reform. By identifying gaps, barriers, and opportunities for integration, this study aims to contribute to the development of comprehensive midwifery education that acknowledges the inseparability of physical and mental health during the perinatal period.

Research method and design

The aim of the study is to explore and describe perinatal mental health within the midwifery training curricula of South Africa as perceived by the practicing midwife. A qualitative, explorative and descriptive research design was followed to explore and describe perinatal mental health gaps within the midwifery training curricula of South Africa as perceived by the practicing midwife. The study took place in the public facilities in Tshwane. The study was approved by the Sefako Makgatho Health Science University (SMUREC/H/345/2023:PG). The study falls within health science research category, and the study was registered in the national health research database (GP_202402_007).

Setting

The study took place in the four selected midwife-led obstetric units located within the community health centres in the Tshwane district, South Africa. The selected facilities provide 24 h maternal and neonatal healthcare services including the postnatal care to the South African citizens free of charge. The selected midwife-led obstetric units are in the semi-urban areas and are accessible to mothers from varying backgrounds.

Recruitment and sample

Once the ethical clearance from Makgatho Health Science University research ethics committee, permission from Gauteng Province and Tshwane health district were received, the researcher approached the respective assistant nursing managers of the selected facilities to gain access to the participants. The researcher targeted the staff-meetings and present the research information letter and used it as an opportunity to invite the prospective participants from a population of midwives in the selected facilities. The researcher employed the inclusion criteria from the approved research proposal to purposefully sample the 17 midwives. Data collection

Seventeen, semi-structure and in-depth one-on-one interviews lasting between 45 to 60 minutes with audio- recording were conducted by the researcher. Prior to the interview the researcher, informed the participants that the interview will be recorded. The informed consent for both participation in the study and recording of the interviews were obtained. The researchers followed an interview guide comprising of a central question:What is your perception of perinatal mental health education within South African midwifery training curricula

What is your perception of perinatal mental health education within South African midwifery training curricula

The central question was followed by the probing question which allowed the participants to share in-depth information about the phenomena [22]. The central question was developed specifically for the study and has not been adapted from any existing data collection tool (see annexure 1). The researcher used various communication skills such as paraphrasing, summarizing and limited responses to allow participants to express themselves and ensured their own understanding of the participants responses [23, 24].

Data analysis

The researcher listened to the interview recordings and transcribed them verbatim. This exercise marked the first step of Collaizi’s seven steps of qualitative data analysis which allowed the researchers to immerse themselves in data [25, 26]. From the data, the statements pertaining to the midwives’ perceptions of perinatal mental health education were extracted. The researcher drew meanings from the statements pertaining to perinatal mental education to formulate significant statements. The significant statements were used to cluster themes with meanings. Lastly the themes with similar meanings were clustered to form themes with categories that provide exhaustive description of midwives’ perceptions of perinatal mental health education within South African Midwifery curricula.

Trustworthiness

Four Lincoln and Guba’s principles of credibility, dependability, confirmability transferability were applied to ensure trustworthiness of the study [27]. To increase the credibility of the study the study had a prolonged engagement with the prospective participants by engaging them about the study during their staff meetings thus establishing rapport [26, 28]. The researcher provided a dense description of the participants demographics detailed in Table 1. Additionally, the researcher described the participants perceptions of perinatal mental health and used direct quotation to support interpretation of the research findings which ensures transferability of the research findings. Researchers adhered to the prescribed research proposal, and the description of participants’ demographics assures that the approved inclusion criteria were applied. Series of events that took place during recruitment of participants and data collection described under research methods affirms that the researchers kept an audit trail [26]. Furthermore, a code-recode method was applied during data analysis, and an independent coder was employed to eliminate bias which increased dependability of the study. The physical records pertaining to the study are kept under lock and key whereas the electronic records are kept in a password- encrypted file accessible to the researchers which enhanced confirmability of the study.

Ethical considerations

The study involved human participants and thus herald adherence to the ethical consideration laid down by Declaration of Helsinki for health science research [29]. The study was approved by the school of healthcare science research ethics (SREC) and Sefako Makgatho Health Science research ethics committee (SMUREC). Permission was sought from the Gauteng Department of Health (GDOH) and Tshwane District to access the selected facilities. Clinical trial number: not applicable. The researchers obtained a voluntary and informed consent from the participants. The researchers highlighted that it was the participant’s choice to withdraw from participating in the study which eliminated coercion [28]. Maintenance of participants privacy and confidentiality is pertinent in health science research; therefore, the researcher generated the alpha-numeric codes to ensure anonymity of participants during discussion of data [27, 30].

Findings

Their years of clinical experience within the midwife-led obstetric unit spanned between three to eighteen years. The sample was predominantly females accounting for sixteen participants and one male. All participants were midwives with nursing and midwifery qualification under regulation R.425 of the South African Nursing Council. Twelve midwives held a diploma in nursing and midwifery (R.425) and five held a bachelor’s degree in nursing and midwifery (R.425). Seven participants held an additional post basic diploma in midwifery under regulation R.212 of the South African Nursing Council. Eleven participants were recruited from Region 1 and six were from Region 2. The participants’ demographic data is summarized in Table 1.

Three themes emerged. In theme 1, the midwives perceived that there are educational gaps in midwifery training as far as perinatal mental health concerned. These identified gaps were associated with superficial coverage of perinatal mental health content over a limited period which resulted in inadequate assessment and preparation for practice. Theme 2 revealed a disconnect between theory and clinical practice which contributed to limited abilities to apply mental health skills in practice which contributed to deficiencies in their development of professional confidence. In theme three the lack of interprofessional education and collaboration led to midwives’ limited exposure and interaction to other mental health specialists. Consequently, the midwives were unable to define their professional roles and boundaries within the health team. The research findings are summarized in Table 2.

Theme 1: Educational gaps in midwifery training

Under this theme, it became evident that there are gaps in education and training of midwives on perinatal mental health in South Africa. These gaps are characterized by the superficial coverage perinatal mental health content across undergraduate and postgraduate midwifery programmes. Midwives argued that the teaching and learning time allocated for perinatal mental health content is limited within the midwifery training curriculums. Therefore, limiting assessment opportunities to establish the midwives’ competence in perinatal mental health.

Subtheme 1.1: Superficial coverage of critical perinatal mental health content

Theme 2: Disconnect between theory and clinical practice application

The evident disconnect between theory and clinical practice of perinatal mental health was amongst the midwives’ concerns. They argued that the existing disconnect may be caused by the current limited opportunities to practice perinatal mental health resulting in skills practice gaps amongst South African midwives. Consequently, midwives lack confidence in applications of perinatal mental health clinical knowledge and skills.

Discussion

The study explored and described midwives’ perceptions of the inclusion of perinatal mental health within the South African midwifery training curricula. The study findings highlighted that there is superficial coverage of perinatal mental health component within the midwifery education and training. The study argue that the physical obstetric conditions are prioritized over mental health in the midwifery context. Therefore, midwifery training in South Africa seems to prepare the midwives to manage obstetric conditions with narrowed focus on mental health. This finding suggest that perinatal mental health is a neglected topic in the midwifery practice, and substantiates the extensive affirming that midwives prioritize physical health over mental health [5]. The south African qualification framework for the advanced diploma in midwifery has a set of exit level outcomes for midwifery training [20]. The first two exit level outcomes specific to application of clinical and knowledge and skills stipulates that the midwives must have a knowledge of social sciences for counselling and advocacy role [20]. This exit level outcome excludes mental health knowledge required to navigate complexities of perinatal mental health [14]. The existing literature proves that the onset of obstetric condition at any point during pregnancy, labour, postnatal and neonatal period triggers psychological and emotional responses which may be pathological [14, 31].

The study found that there is limited time for clinical exposure to mental health condition during midwifery training as perinatal mental health condition are not managed in the midwifery units. Therefore, the student midwives have limited opportunities to interact with patients with perinatal mental health needs. According to the midwifery training completion form, the student midwife is required to conduct thirty mandatory mental health screenings during their training [31]. While the literature proves that 30% of women experience perinatal mental health challenges during and after pregnancies, the management of perinatal mental health conditions is not a mandatory clinical requirement [2, 32].

The study unveiled that the student midwives’ competencies on mental health are excluded from the clinical assessments which was associated with under-exposure to mental health conditions that require application of knowledge and clinical skills. This finding is valid in a didactic context for mastery of the clinical skills which require continued students’ exposure to clinical learning opportunities [33]. In clinical teaching, the students undergo learning process commencing with embedding theoretical knowledge that will be integrated to clinical practice [33]. Once theoretical knowledge is mastered, the students are exposed to clinical simulation for clinical teaching as a first step to integration of theory into practice in a safer environment that mimic real clinical situation [34, 35].Lastly exposure to the real life conditions that require the students to reinforce their integrated knowledge and skills through work integrated learning [35]. The study highlights that South Africa is not keeping up with international trends as the countries such as United Kingdom and Ireland which recommends that the midwives must be empowered on perinatal mental health assessment skills annually to ensure clinical competence [12]. Additionally, Australia and Canada subject their midwives to continuous professional development training on perinatal mental health with proven levels of competency to assess and manage perinatal mental health condition [36, 37]. This competency forms part of the competencies required to maintain registration status with the nursing and midwifery councils [36, 37].

The study found that the registered midwives are not actively participating in the preparation of student midwives for mastery of mental health skills required to address the perinatal mental health conditions amidst critical midwives’ shortages. This finding suggests that registered midwives are in contravention of their scope of practice stipulating that the midwives are educators and must empower their subordinates including student midwives [38]. The study highlights that the students are unaided in preparation for midwifery clinical assessments particularly on mental health skills. This finding suggests a contravention of principles of clinical assessment which mandates that the students are aided to achieve confidence in their clinical skills through formative assessments and constructive feedback [39]. Lastly, the student’s competence is tested using a summative assessment to confirm a mastery of a clinical skill [31].

The study elucidates that under the current midwifery curricula; the midwives are inadequately prepared to manage perinatal mental health. Consequently, the midwives may be unable to adequately respond to the existing perinatal mental conditions which could lead to avoidable deleterious outcomes. This finding is consistent with the literature on perinatal problem identification problems which state that some of the deleterious outcomes are caused by personnel related factors such as being inadequately skilled to address the potential and existing health problem [40]. The study argues that the limitations in midwives’ knowledge and skills may be due to scarcity of mental health conditions in midwifery units. This finding contradicts the statistics on perinatal mental that about 30% of women experience mental health conditions during childbirth [2]. Considering midwives’ inadequate training highlighted throughout the study, the scarcity of patients with perinatal mental health conditions may not be a true reflection of the reality of patients’ circumstances in the midwifery units.

The study highlights that midwives seem to have lost confidence in their own skills and abilities to assess, diagnose and initiate management of patients with perinatal mental health conditions. This finding contravenes the competencies of a midwife in South Africa which regard a midwife as an autonomous practitioner [41]. The study elucidates that midwives are competent with history taking and screening for mental health which might be insufficient to unmask underlying perinatal mental health symptoms. This finding is valid because to establish the presence of a mental health condition, mental health nurses employ therapeutic communication skills [42]. During therapeutic communication and diagnostic interactions with the patients such as mental health status evaluation and supportive interviews [22]. The mental health screening tool currently in use is primarily designed for healthcare professionals with limited training of mental health detect the possibilities of depression and anxiety disorders [43]. However, detection of these conditions requires a healthcare professional to establish the context and probing to gain a nuanced understanding of patient responses [22]. This exercise requires an understanding of aspects of mental health which the midwives in the study affirmed that they lack thus limiting their contribution to perinatal mental healthcare.

The midwives seem to have minimal to no interaction with mental healthcare professionals such as psychiatrists and psychologists who care for patients with perinatal mental condition which limits learning opportunities. This study findings elevate midwives value of interprofessional collaboration in the management of perinatal mental health. Additionally, this finding is consistent with the guidelines of mental healthcare which emphasize that the interprofessional collaboration is the cornerstone of mental healthcare service [13] .

Implications for midwifery education

This study recommends that mental healthcare forms an integral part of the midwifery care. Therefore, the study recommends that the perinatal mental health content must be strengthened within the midwifery curriculums. Additionally, the management of patients with perinatal mental health conditions must form part of mandatory clinical requirements to ensure clinical competence. The mental health screening in a midwifery context where all the clients run a risk of developing a perinatal mental health condition cannot be the only diagnostic tool. The midwives need to be trained on basic mental health nursing skills such as mental status examination to give account of the patients’ symptoms and prompt referrals.

Implications for practice

The fragmentation of mental healthcare services from the midwifery care warrants referral of the patients with perinatal mental health from midwifery to mental healthcare unit. The study recommends that the midwives must participate in the interprofessional collaborations from consultations with the mental health team for referral to mental healthcare units. Midwives need to be present during presentation of the referred patients to the interprofessional collaboration team for follow.

Implications for the research

The inadequate preparation of South African midwives highlighted in the study necessitate further research for mental health knowledge and skills audit amongst the midwives. The study recommends benchmark with midwifery curricula internationally on perinatal mental health to identify similarities and gaps that will be used to ensure alignment with the international trends. The findings of the knowledge and skills audit may aid in the formulation of recommendations for curricula revisions to include perinatal mental health.

Strengths and limitations

The qualitative nature of the study limited the sample size to seventeen. Although the sample size was small, the point of data saturation was reached which gave nuanced account of the midwives’ perceptions on midwifery training particularly on perinatal mental health. However, the findings cannot be generalized to broader South Africa as the study was localized to a single district within Gauteng Province.

Conclusions

This study elucidates the gaps perinatal mental health education within South African midwifery curricula. These gaps resulted in a disconnect between midwives’ theoretical knowledge and clinical practice. Consequently, midwives are unable to comprehensively address the perinatal mental health problems. In addition, the lack of interprofessional education and collaboration in perinatal mental health context limits midwives’ exposure to mental health competencies and learning opportunities. These findings suggest a need for a curriculum review to adequately prepare midwives with essential mental health skills to identify and initiate management and referral of patients with perinatal mental health needs. The study recommends leveraging the existing interprofessional collaboration within mental healthcare context to include midwives as a form of empowerment to improve perinatal mental health outcomes in South Africa.
