Multi-omics analysis of flavor and quality modulation in dry-fermented sausages with textured pea protein
Authors: Ying Li, Jian Zhang, Xin Liu, Danni Zhang, Imre Blank, Xiaoxiao Feng, Ninglong Zhang, Zheng Liu, Xi Ding, Chunbao Li, Xinglian Xu, Changbo Tang, Guanghong Zhou, Yuan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs textured pea protein (TPP) emerges as a sustainable strategy for plant-based meat analogues, its influence on flavor precursors and the resulting volatile profile and sensory attributes remains underexplored. This study investigated physicochemical properties, lipid composition, volatiles, and sensory characteristics of dry-fermented sausages formulated with varying levels of TPP substitution. TPP substitution yielded significant decrease in pH, water activity, glossiness and yellowness intensity, and increased weight loss. A 25 % TPP substitution substantially diminished free fatty acids content. Sensory analysis revealed that the 100 % TPP group exhibited significantly higher bitterness and astringency scores, accompanied by a notable reduction in texture acceptability (P < 0.05). Lipidomics identified 125 differential expressed lipids, with notable reductions in triglycerides and phosphatidylethanolamines. Volatile analysis revealed TPP suppressed sulfides and nitrogen oxides while enhancing bitterness- and plant-related compounds. This study highlights how TPP reshapes flavor from molecular to sensory levels, providing insights for optimizing plant-based meat products.Keywords: Plant-based meat analogues, Textured pea proteins, Dry-fermented sausages, Flavoromics, Lipidomics, Sensory analysis

Introduction

Dry-fermented sausage, a historic delicacy from the Mediterranean region, enjoys global popularity due to its distinctive flavor profile (Zheng, Sun, et al., 2024). These products demonstrate remarkable diversity, featuring renowned varieties such as salami, saussiçon sec and chorizo in Europe, along with summer sausage and pepperoni in the United States. Lean meat, fat and various ingredients (curing salt, spices and starter cultures) are stuffed into casings and undergo microbial fermentation and maturation, ultimately developing the characteristic flavor and texture of dry-fermented sausage (Flores & Piornos, 2021). Nowadays, excessive meat consumption raises health and environmental concerns, including higher cardiovascular disease risks and environmental impacts like greenhouse gas emissions, water usage, and land degradation. As awareness of health and environmental impacts rises, efforts have shifted toward developing plant-based meat alternatives that are energy-efficient, environmentally friendly and health-promoting (Hallström et al., 2015). Nonetheless, fully transitioning from an animal-based to a plant-based diet remains a formidable challenge due to entrenched dietary habits and factors related to flavor, texture and economic viability (Schösler et al., 2012; Sha & Xiong, 2020). In response, a novel approach of “hybrid meat alternatives” has emerged, which retains a portion of animal meat to satisfy consumer expectations of traditional meat's sensory qualities while improving the nutritional profile by reducing salt, fat, and adding fiber, vitamins, and minerals (Grasso et al., 2022). Current investigations about dry-fermented sausages largely focus on reducing sodium, curing agents (such as nitrite) and lipids. However, limited attention is paid to the introduction of plant-based proteins into these products (Chen, Hu, et al., 2021; Serdaroğlu et al., 2023).

Plant proteins, including pea protein, soy protein and wheat gluten, serve as the primary ingredients in plant-based meat alternatives (Chen, Zhang, et al., 2021; Samard et al., 2019; Yuan et al., 2021). Among these, pea protein has garnered attention for its lower allergenicity, higher nutritional value, moderate cost, high-viscosity ability, and exceptional emulsifying properties, thus significantly enhancing the fibrous characteristics of plant-based meat analogues (Lu et al., 2020; Yuliarti et al., 2021). Furthermore, pea proteins constitute about 36 % of global legume production and it is less frequently associated with genetic modification issues compared to soy protein. Accordingly, pea proteins have been seamlessly incorporated into a wide range of culinary applications, demonstrating their versatility and functional benefits. Moreover, extrusion cooking is widely employed in practical applications to optimize the texture and improve the digestibility of plant-based proteins (Gulati et al., 2020). Research indicates that textured pea protein products achieve a refined, fibrous texture compared to traditional pea flours (Beniwal et al., 2021). Despite the considerable focus on the application of textured pea protein in hybrid meat products, its utilization in fermented meat products remains relatively unexplored (Ebert, Jungblut, et al., 2022).

Flavor plays a critical role in meat products, originating from chemical and biochemical reactions during fermentation and maturation, including the hydrolysis and oxidation of proteins and lipids and the degradation of carbohydrates (Toldrá et al., 2014). Among these processes, lipid oxidation plays a particularly significant role in the formation of flavor during the fermentation and aging of sausages. Microbial enzymes and lipases hydrolyze triglycerides into free fatty acids. Among these, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), owing to their high degree of unsaturation, are preferentially oxidized to hydroperoxides, which degrade into volatile flavor compounds such as aldehydes, alcohols, esters, and ketones (Liu et al., 2022). These compounds, characterized by higher volatility and lower odor thresholds compared to other volatile substances (such as long-chain aliphatic hydrocarbons), play a crucial role in the flavor profile of fermented meats (Mi et al., 2018).

Lipid oxidation and lipolysis play a pivotal role in aroma formation, where enzymatic hydrolysis of lipids yields free fatty acids, which are subsequently oxidized into volatile compounds (Xia et al., 2023). Our previous work demonstrated that the incorporation of textured plant proteins promoted protein degradation and the development of characteristic flavors in salami (Li et al., 2025). Additionally, the inclusion of plant protein components alters the components of overall sausage matrix, which might impact lipid transformations and flavor formation. The incorporation of plant proteins may disrupt protein-fat interactions, altering lipolysis and oxidation, thereby resulting in distinct flavor profiles compared to those from animal proteins (Aruchunan et al., 2025). Currently, most studies about textured pea protein primarily focus on its technological or nutritional effects, with a significant gap in understanding its potential influence on lipid dynamics and flavor formation in hybrid meats. To manage lipid degradation and enhance flavor in fermented sausages, it is essential to investigate the dynamic fluctuations of lipids and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in dry-fermented hybrid sausages.

This study firstly evaluated the basic physicochemical, textural and flavor properties of fermented hybrid sausages produced by substituting lean meat with varying ratios (25 %, 50 %, 75 % and 100 %) of textured pea protein, aiming to explore the potential for developing fermented hybrid sausages from a combination of traditional dry-fermented sausages and textured pea protein (TPP). Following this, intelligent sensory technologies, including electronic nose (E-nose) and electronic tongue (E-tongue), along with flavoromics techniques such as headspace gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS) and headspace solid-phase microextraction-gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS), were employed to identify and quantify volatile compounds in the sausages. Additionally, lipid alterations were investigated using UPLC-Q-Exactive-MS/MS-based lipidomics combined with free fatty acid analysis. Finally, multi-omics integration was performed to elucidate the relationships between volatile compound profiles and lipid dynamics in fermented sausages incorporating textured pea protein. This research conducted a comprehensive evaluation of fermented sausage analogs containing textured pea protein, expecting to provide an insight enabling flavor enhancement in fermented sausage analogs from a lipid perspective.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Pea protein isolates (≥ 90 % protein on a dry weight basis) were obtained from Yantai Shuangta Food Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). Lean pork meat and pork back fat were sourced from the Ma'anshan Bairui Food Co., Ltd. (Anhui, China). The commercial starters culture containing Latilactobacillus sakei, Pediococcus acidilactici and Staphylococcus carnosus was supplied by Christian Hansen (Hoersholm, Denmark).

Acetonitrile, isopropyl alcohol and methanol were of high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade and obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). HPLC-grade formic acid and HPLC-grade ammonium formate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Madison, WI, USA). Other analytical grade chemical reagents were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany).

Preparation of TPP

The TPP was manufactured by a twin-screw extruder (AHT36, Shandong Arrow Machinery Co., Ltd., Jinan, China) according to the method of Zhang et al. (2023) with slight modifications. TPP and 25 % (w/w) of distilled water were premixed, followed by kneading the dough for 10 min using a kneader (M6; Haishi Baking Electrical Appliance Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China). Doughs were stored at 4 °C for 2 h to undergo hydration and homogeneity. After that, doughs were extruded and expanded through the twin-screw extruder with two screws (3.6 cm diameter × 102.4 cm length). The temperature ranged from 30 °C to 130 °C. Finally, The TPP samples were collected, cooled and subsequently dried in an oven (AHT36, Shandong Arrow Machinery Co., Ltd., Jinan, China) at 50 °C for 2 h until the final moisture content reached below 12 % to ensure storage stability. After the cooling process, the samples were transferred into self-sealing bags for further analysis or promptly frozen to facilitate subsequent sensory evaluation.

Preparation of fibrous-like fermented sausages

TPP were closer to the fiber structure and mouthfeel of real meat after soaked in distilled water at ambient temperature for 3 h. The rehydrated TPP was then cut into 3 mm particles to form meat pulp. Sausages were produced with varying TPP content: 0:100 % (control), 25 %:75 % (P25), 50 %:50 % (P50), 75 %:25 % (P75), and 100 %:0 (P100), based on the fresh weight of TPP and lean pork. Pork back fat was thawed and ground into 3 mm particles. The preparation followed the method of Rocchetti et al. (2021) with minor modifications, using a mixture of 80 % TPP with lean pork and 20 % pork back fat, combined with NaCl (21 g), sodium nitrite (0.24 g), sodium nitrate (0.08 g), potassium nitrate (0.05 g), sodium D-isoascorbic acid (0.7 g), glucose (7 g), mixed spices (8 g), and a commercial starter culture (0.4 g). The sausage batter was homogenized and mixed in a K504 bowl chopper (Seydelmann GmbH, Stuttgart, Germany), then filled into collagen casings with a diameter of 18 mm using a vacuum filling machine (VF 610 plus, Handtmann GmbH, Biberach, Germany). Sausages (approximately 10 g and 20 cm long of each) were subjected to a 10 day-process in a cabinet (UKOEOF100, UKOEO, Guangdong, China), which were fermented for a period of 3 days at 15 °C and 75–85 % relative humidity (RH), and then dried/ripened for 7 days at 17 °C and 65–80 % RH.

Determination of water activity (Aw) was performed utilizing an Aw-benchtop meter Novasina Lab Master (Novasina AG ltd., Lachen SZ, Switzerland). Instrumental color measurement

The color of final sausage samples was determined using the MINOLTA CR-400 Chroma Meter (Konica Minolta Inc., Japan). The colorimeter was calibrated using a standard white plate (CR-A43, Y = 85.5, x = 0.3197, y = 0.3374). Results were reported as L* (brightness), a* (redness and greenness intensity) and b* (yellowness and blueness intensity) values in the CIE color system. Total color difference (ΔE*) was also measured by comparing TPP treatments with the control group (without plant protein) and calculated by Eq. (2). To ensure data consistency, each measurement was carried out in sextuplicate.

Textural characterization

The textural properties of the final sausage samples were characterized using a TA-XT Plus analyzer (Stable Micro System, Godalming, UK) with a cylindrical probe (P50, 50 mm diameter, 19.85 cm2 of surface contact) at room temperature. All samples were cut into cylinder with a 15 mm diameter and 10 mm cross-sectional thickness before analysis. Three cycles of compression (50 % compression of sausage's initial height) were carried out according to the setting parameters: lift-up height 20 mm, trigger force 5 g, test interval 3 s, head speed 2.0 mm/s, post-test speed 1.0 mm/s. The hardness (N), chewiness (N), gumminess (Kg/cm2), resilience, cohesiveness and springiness were measured. The mean values are reported from the six different sausages of each group.

FFAs composition analysis

The FFAs of the final fermented sausages were extracted and quantified using a modified method from Xiao et al. (2021). A total of 50 mg sample of ground sausage (after casing removal) was mixed with 2 mL of 1 % sulfuric acid methanol solution in a 10 mL centrifuge tube and fully vortexed for 2 min. Esterification was performed in a water bath at 80 °C for 20 min. Successively, 2 mL of chromatographic-grade n-heptane was added for extraction, followed by thorough shaking for 1 min, resting for 10 min to allow stratification, and rinsing for 10 min with ultrapure water at 4 °C. The mixture was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (700 μL) was transferred into a new tube with 100 mg anhydrous sodium sulfate for dehydration, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged (11,000×g, 4 °C, 5 min). The supernatant (300 μL) was mixed with 15 μL of 500 ppm methyl salicylate as the internal standard, vortexed for 20 s, and analyzed by a Thermo Trace 1300 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) gas phase system coupled with Thermo ISQ 7000 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). GC operation conditions were set as follows: 1 μL extract was injected in split mode (8:1 ratio) and separated using a Thermo TG-FAME capillary column (50 m × 0.25 mm ID × 0.20 μm). The column temperature was initially set at 80 °C, increased to 160 °C at 20 °C/min, then to 196 °C at 3 °C/min, and finally to 250 °C at 20 °C/min. The carrier gas was helium with 99 % purity (0.63 mL/min). MS conditions were set as follows: Electron ionization source and SIM scanning mode was applied at the electron energy of 70 eV. All samples were analyzed in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Lipid composition analysis by untargeted lipidomics

Sample pretreatment and lipid extraction procedure

Lipids from the final sausages were extracted using a modified MTBE method (Yang et al., 2023). A total of 30 mg sample was mixed with 200 μL ultrapure water in a 2 mL centrifuge tube, vortexed, and then treated with 240 μL precooled methanol and 800 μL MTBE. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, followed by ultrasound treatment for 20 min at 4 °C. After standing for 30 min at room temperature, the solution was centrifuged (14,000×g, 15 min at 10 °C), and the upper organic layer was dried under nitrogen. The residue was reconstituted with 200 μL of 90 % isopropanol/acetonitrile, vortexed, and centrifuged again. The supernatant was analyzed by UHPLC-Q-Exactive-MS/MS.

UHPLC-Q-Exactive-MS/MS analysis

Lipids were subjected to a comprehensive analysis using a UHPLC Nexera LC-30A system (SHIMADZU, kyoto, Japan) coupled with a Q-Exactive Plus Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific™, MA, USA). Reverse phase chromatography equipped with a CSH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Waters) was selected for LC separation.

The mobile phase A consisted of an acetonitrile-water solution (6:4, v/v) with 0.1 % formic acid and 0.1 mM ammonium formate, while phase B was acetonitrile-isopropanol (1:9, v/v) with 0.1 % formic acid and 0.1 mM ammonium formate. The gradient program started with 40 % solvent B at a flow rate of 300 μL/min for 5 min, then increased linearly to 75 % B over 9.5 min, followed by a final increase to 99 % B over 6 min, and equilibrated at 40 % B for 5 min. Samples were kept in a 10 °C autosampler. A randomized sequence was employed for continuous analysis to minimize detection signal fluctuations. Electrospray ionization (ESI) in both positive/negative ion modes was used for ionization. ESI source settings were optimized with a heater temperature of 300 °C, capillary temperature of 350 °C, gas flow rates of 45, 15, and 1 arb for sheath, auxiliary, and sweep gases, respectively, with a spray voltage of 3.0 kV and curtain gas at 35 psi. The mass spectrometry scan range was m/z 200–1800, with 10 fragmentation profiles (MS2 scan, HCD) collected after each full scan, at a resolution of 70,000 (MS1) and 17,500 (MS2) at m/z 200.

The engine Lipid Search (Thermo, Massachusetts, USA) was selected for peak identification, peak extraction and lipid identification (secondary identification) of lipid molecules and internal standards after UHPLC-Q-Exactive-MS/MS analysis. A set of lipid internal standards covering major lipid classes (e.g., triglycerides, phosphatidylethanolamines, and phosphatidylcholines) was carefully selected to account for the significant variations in mass spectrometry signals among different lipid types. Both precursor and product tolerance were set as 5 ppm. The product ion threshold was set as 5 %. Lipid molecular species were further validated by matching their retention times, fragmentation patterns and isotopic distributions with authentic standards or literature data. For each sample, four parallel measurements were carried out to minimize experimental error.

E-nose analysis

The olfactory characteristics of sausages after fermentation and ripening were depicted by a PEN3 E-nose system (Airsense Analytics GmbH, Schwerin, Germany) with 10 metal oxide semiconductor type chemical sensors (Table Supplementary 1) according to the method of Shan et al. (2023) with minor adaptations. A 20 mL headspace vial (CNW Technologies, Germany) was employed to hold two grams of minced sausages and incubated in a water bath at 50 °C for 15 min. The constant initial injection flow rate was 400 mL/min and the measurement time was set as 120 s. Six replications were applied for each sample.

Taste profile

Taste attributes of all sausages were profiled using the SA402B E-tongue (Insent Company, Atsugi-Shi, Japan) according to the protocol of Chen, Zhang, et al. (2021) with slight modifications. The chemical sensor array of the SA402B E-tongue consisted of five cross-selective test sensors and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode as a calibration sensor. The five test sensors (AAE, CT0, CA0, C00 and AE1) specifically responded to umami, salty, sour, bitter, and astringent tastes, respectively. Prior to sample analysis, sensors were activated, calibrated and self-checked. A total of 5 g lean of each sample was minced and mixed with 50 mL of de-ionized water for homogenization (9000 rpm, 2 min). Then the mixed solution was centrifuged at 8000 ×g at 4 °C for 15 min. For analysis, the diluted solution was filtered twice with 12.5 cm filter paper (Ji Zhi, Shanghai, China). The filtrate (80 mL) was poured into a dedicated sample cup for subsequent instrument analysis. The detection results were converted to taste values using taste analysis application software. Each sample was measured six times to ensure data reliability.

HS-SPME-GC–MS detection of VOCs

VOCs in the headspace of sausage samples were extracted and identified using a TSQ 9000 triple quadrupole TRACE 1310 GC–MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., MA, USA), following the method of Sun et al. (2023). A total of 3 g sample of minced sausage was mixed with 1.5 μL of the internal standard 2-methyl-3-heptanone (1.68 mg/mL) in a 20 mL headspace vial. VOCs were extracted using a 50/30 μm SPME fiber (DVB/CAR/PDMS) at 60 °C for 40 min. Subsequently, the fiber was inserted into the GC inlet for automatic injection, followed by thermal desorption at 230 °C for 5 min. The inlet was maintained at 250 °C in splitless mode, with helium (> 99.99 %) as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. VOCs were separated using a DB-Wax column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm), with temperature programming from 40 °C (maintained for 1 min) to 200 °C (4 °C/min ramp) and then to 280 °C (8 °C/min ramp). The HP 5975C mass spectrometer (Hewlett-Packard, Palo Alto, CA, U.S.A.) was operated in electron ionization mode at 70 eV and 230 °C. The scanning range was m/z 33–500, with analysis based on the NIST 14 Mass Library and linear retention index (RI) for qualitative and quantitative analysis (Zheng, Tian, et al., 2024).

VOCs analysis by HS-GC-IMS

The VOCs in sausages with varying formulations were analyzed using a HS-GC-IMS Flavor Analyzer (FlavourSpec®, Gesell-schaft für Analytische Sensorsysteme mbH, Dortmund, Germany) according to Zhang, Hu, et al. (2021) with minor modifications. Sausage samples (3 g each) were minced and sealed with a magnetic cap in 20 mL headspace vials. An autosampler (Solid Phase Micro Extraction, 57330-U, Supelco, USA) and vibration heating unit were employed. After incubation at 60 °C for 20 min (agitator speed: 500 rpm), 500 μL of headspace gas was drawn into the GC injection port via a heated syringe (85 °C). VOCs were separated using an FS-SE-54-CB-1 capillary column with nitrogen as the carrier and drift gas (150 mL/min). The flow rate program was as follows: EPC1: maintained at 150 mL/min for 15 min. EPC2: initially set to 5 mL/min for 5 min, then gradually increased to 75 mL/min over 10 min. Three replicates were used per sample. VOCs were identified and quantified based on retention index (RI) and drift time, calculated with n-ketones (C4-C9) as external standards. Identification was performed using Laboratory Analytical Viewer 2.2.1 software (G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany) and the NIST 14 dB-5/HP-5 database in the GC-IMS Library Search 1.0.3 software (G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany). Sensory evaluation

The sensory attributes of the final products after 14 days of fermentation were evaluated by 26 experienced panelists (13 males, 13 females, aged 22–45) in a well-ventilated sensory laboratory, following the methods of Li et al. (2025). Prior to the evaluation, panelists underwent four weeks of training (four 2-h sessions per week), covering sensory analysis fundamentals, property identification, and scale development, with a focus on sausage attributes like color, texture, luster, odor, taste, and overall acceptability. An assessment ensured consistency in evaluations with product characteristics. All participants were informed about the study and signed an informed consent form, with their rights and privacy protected according to the Chinese national standard (GB/T 22210-2008). Using a double-blind method, 5 mm thick slices of sausages were randomly placed on white plates marked with three random digits to minimize bias. The evaluation was conducted at room temperature (25 °C) in individual booths under fluorescent lighting resembling daylight. Toothpicks and water were provided to cleanse the palate between samples. Additionally, to reduce sensory fatigue, panelists were given a cleaning and resting period between each sample, and each sample was presented three times. Sensory descriptive analysis was conducted using a 7-point scale for color (1 = dark; 7 = red), luster (1 = dull; 7 = glossy), odor (1 = subtle, non-fermented; 7 = rich, meaty with strong fermentation), texture (1 = fragile; 7 = dense and resilient), taste (1 = bland; 7 = rich and enjoyable), and overall acceptability (1 = minimally acceptable; 7 = highly acceptable). The sample's odor and taste were neither unpleasant nor harmful to the participants. The expert panel's evaluation of acceptability in this study served as a preliminary screening tool, with subsequent studies to be supplemented with consumer testing.

Statistical analysis

Six quality control experiments were performed to evaluate instrument stability, reproducibility, and data quality. One-way ANOVA (P < 0.05) was used to assess the significant differences between sausages with varying TPP replacement ratios. All analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago). Group differentiation was carried out through principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) using Metaboanalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/docs/RTutorial.xhtml). The PLS-DA model was validated by 7-fold cross-validation (Q2 > 0.5) to ensure model stability. The variable importance in projection (VIP) values of lipids were determined, with VIP > 1 indicating significant contributions. Results and discussion

Physicochemical properties

pH, aw and weight loss analysis

The pH value served as a critical indicator of quality deterioration and shelf life in fermented sausages. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the control group (traditional fermented sausage) exhibited the lowest initial pH, whereas treatment groups exhibited a higher initial pH, which were likely due to the neutral/alkaline natural pH of pea protein (Ebert, Michel, et al., 2022). During fermentation, the pH of all samples decreased significantly (P < 0.05) within 24 h, with TPP-substituted samples showing a greater reduction than the control group (except for the 100 % substitution). This sharp decline was possibly due to the production of organic acids during carbohydrate hydrolysis (Xiao et al., 2020). In the later stages of maturation, the pH slightly increased in the treatment groups, possibly due to the hydrolytic activity of Staphylococcus and the formation of ammonium compounds during protein and amino acid catabolism (Zhou et al., 2024).

The Aw value plays a key role in microbial control and safety. As highlighted in Fig. 1B, varying pea protein proportions significantly affected Aw (P < 0.05). The initial Aw values ranged from 0.951 to 0.969, with no significant differences (P > 0.05). After fermentation, Aw values decreased significantly (P < 0.05) due to moisture loss. The final Aw of TPP-treated samples was lower than the control, which may be attributed to differences in water-holding capacity between pea and animal proteins (Lin & Barbut, 2024).

Weight loss is associated with factors during maturation such as temperature, humidity, and formulation. As illustrated in Fig. 1C, weight loss significantly increased over time (P < 0.05), stabilizing by day 7 with final values ranging from 48.25 ± 0.06 % to 51.83 ± 0.04 %. This might be ascribed to fluctuations in environmental temperature and humidity, leading to moisture evaporation and transpiration, as well as the loss of certain nutrients occurring throughout the fermentation and maturation period (Liao et al., 2024). TPP addition resulted in higher weight loss values compared to the control (P < 0.05), which was correlated with reduced moisture retention, negatively impacting texture and firmness (Nunes & Emond, 2007).

Color analysis

Color is a critical attribute influencing food quality and consumer acceptance. As shown in Table 1, the TPP substitution ratio significantly affected sausage color (P < 0.05). A linear positive correlation was observed between TPP content (except for the 100 % substitution) and L* values, indicating that higher TPP levels increased lightness (P < 0.05). This was likely due to pH reduction during fermentation (Fig. 1A), resulting in the release of organic acids, thereby enhancing L* (Yin et al., 2002). In contrast, a* values showed no significant change (P > 0.05), while b* values increased significantly following a trend similar to L* (P < 0.05). The color variations are likely attributed to the dilution of nitrosomyoglobin and changes in matrix pH from TPP addition (Hu et al., 2019; Mokni Ghribi et al., 2018). The ΔE* value, reflecting total color difference, was significant when TPP substitution exceeded 50 %, with noticeable color changes beyond a ΔE* of 6.0 (Lenaerts et al., 2018). The enhancing effect of adding TPP on the sample's color was also confirmed by the sensory evaluation results (Section 3.7).

Textural analysis

Texture is vital for consumers, which is directly influencing purchasing decisions. Table 1 presented the texture profile of sausages with varying TPP substitution ratios. Hardness was higher in the treatment group with less than 50 % TPP substitution, although not significant (P > 0.05). However, hardness significantly decreased (P < 0.05) at TPP levels exceeding 50 %, indicating that high TPP concentration (> 50 %) was not beneficial for product hardness. Chewiness declined significantly with TPP substitution above 50 % (P < 0.05). Gumminess, resilience and cohesiveness also decreased significantly in all treatment groups (P < 0.05), while springiness increased with higher TPP substitution ratios (P < 0.05), which was likely due to reduced moisture activity (Kim et al., 2016). Overall, higher TPP substitution levels (≥ 50 %) significantly reduced hardness and chewiness, resulting in a more fragile texture. Such texture alterations may influence bolus formation and oral perception, contribute to the lower sensory texture scores (a finding further supported by the sensory evaluation results), and indirectly affect flavor perception by modifying the release and perception of volatile compounds, as discussed in sections 3.6–3.8. Moreover, sausages with lower TPP levels (around 25 %) displayed texture characteristics similar to traditional dry-fermented sausages.

FFAs analysis

FFAs are key substrates for lipid oxidation and play a crucial role in flavor development in fermented meat products (Xu et al., 2018). Changes in FFAs composition at different TPP substitution ratios in dry-fermented sausages are shown in Table 2. A total of 51 FFAs were identified, including 16 saturated fatty acids (SFAs), 21 monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), and 14 PUFAs. SFAs were the most abundant, comprising 35.49–40.75 % of total FFAs, followed by MUFAs (44.28–47.14 %) and PUFAs (14.51–19.32 %). As the TPP substitution rate increased, the SFA/UFA ratio in the treatment group decreased from 0.69 (25 % group) to 0.56 (100 % group), indicating that unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) were more prone to be released through lipolysis (Xu et al., 2018). This was consistent with previous reports showing that PUFAs underwent greater breakdown than MUFAs and SFAs in dry-fermented sausages (Molly et al., 1996).

Changes indicated by lipidomics

Lipid composition and dynamic changes analysis

The lipid characteristics of sausages with varying TPP ratios were comprehensively analyzed using UHPLC-Q-Exactive-MS/MS. A total of 1402 different lipid molecules were identified in both positive and negative ion modes. As illustrated in Fig. 1D, the number of identified lipid molecules was 1072 in positive ion mode while only 330 in negative ion mode, indicating that positive ion mode was more efficient for detecting most lipids. Among these, glycerolipids (GLs) were the most abundant with 476 lipid molecules identified.

As depicted in Fig. 1E, all lipid molecules were categorized into 5 lipid categories and 39 subclasses, including fatty acids (FAs, 2.4 %), sphingolipids (SPs, 22.2 %), sterol lipids (STs, 2.7 %), glycerophospholipids (GPs, 38.7 %) and GLs (34.0 %). GPs contained the highest number of lipid molecules, with phosphatidylethanolamine (PE, 8.4 %) and phosphatidylcholine (PC, 8.1 %) being the predominant lipids. This was in agreement with the findings that identified PC and PE as the main lipids in dry-fermented tilapia sausages (Wang, Zang, et al., 2024). Additionally, PC and PE are recognized as the most abundant GPs, playing key roles in energy regulation and lipid transformations (Liu et al., 2019). In contrast, SPs had a relatively lower quantity of lipid molecules (22.2 %), mainly ceramides (Cer) and hexosylceramides (HexCer), while STs and FAs comprised less than 6 % of the total.

As present in Fig. 2A, GLs were the most abundant species in all samples, followed by GPs, SPs, FAs, and STs. Moreover, the content of GLs significantly decreased with increasing TPP substitution ratios (P < 0.05), with only the P25 showing significantly higher GL levels than the control group (P < 0.05). Moreover, the addition of TPP resulted in significantly lower GPs compared to the control group (P < 0.05). While FAs were present in lower amounts across all samples, they served as key precursors in developing the flavor of fermented sausages (Wang, Wang, et al., 2024). Lipase breaks down lipids into saturated and unsaturated FFAs and these FFAs were further oxidized to aldehydes, esters, alcohols and other volatile compounds, contributing to the characteristic flavor profile of fermented sausages (Wang, Wang, et al., 2024).

The changes in lipid subclasses were visualized using a heatmap (Fig. 2B). The abundance of diacylglycerols (DG) and triglycerides (TG) exhibited opposite trends with increasing TPP substitution ratios. DG's relative abundance significantly increased with higher TPP levels, being notably higher in the P100 compared to the control group, while TG showed the opposite trend, with significantly lower relative abundance in the P100 sample (P < 0.05). DG is known as an intermediate product of lipolysis, closely linked to TG anabolism (Chen et al., 2022; Wang, Zang, et al., 2024). Moreover, DG acts as a second messenger in cellular activity, promoting β-oxidation of fats and influencing lipid transformations-related gene expression (Guo et al., 2022; Kamphuis et al., 2003). PC and PE predominated in GPs across all mammalian cells (Jia et al., 2021). Consistent with the changes in GPs, TPP addition significantly reduced the relative abundance of PE and PC compared to the control group. This might be due to TPP's impact on enzyme activity, oxidation reactions and phospholipase-catalyzed hydrolysis (Chen et al., 2023; Zheng, Wang, et al., 2024).

Multivariate statistical analysis of lipids

Fig. 2C demonstrates the cumulative variance contribution rate reaching 64.7 %, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 41 % and 23.7 % of the variance, respectively. The five groups displayed a scattered distribution in two-dimensional space, which indicated significant differences in lipid types and contents among the groups. Furthermore, PLS-DA model was utilized to further analyze the differential lipid characteristics (Fig. 2D). The first two components (component 1 and 2) accounted for 52.8 % of the total variance in the PLS-DA model. The PCA and PLS-DA findings revealed significant differences in lipid composition among the five sample groups.

Differential lipid analysis

VIP scores were utilized to evaluate and classify parameters distinguishing among the groups, with a higher VIP scores indicating a greater contribution of lipids to the classification (Zhang, Jin, et al., 2024). Based on the criteria of P < 0.05 and VIP > 1, 125 differential expressed lipids were identified, including 60 GPs, 44 GLs, 18 SPs and 3 FAs (Supplementary Table 1). As detailed in Supplementary Table 1, GPs comprised 17 types of PC, 17 types of PE, 7 types of Lyso-PC and other lipid molecules. Among GLs, 28 types of TG and 14 types of DG were identified. Clearly, TG and DG represented a high proportion (25 % each) among the top 25 lipids (Fig. 2E).

To visually compare the relative content of differential lipids among the groups, hierarchical cluster analysis was conducted (Fig. 2F). The 125 differential expressed lipids were grouped into two categories which included 72 and 53 lipids, respectively. In the first category, most lipids decreased in content as the TPP substitution ratios increased. The relative content of these lipids, particularly GLs (such as TG), was significantly lower in the TPP-treated groups compared to the control (P < 0.05). TPP substitution might cause hydrolysis of TG, LPC, and PE, producing fatty acids during fermentation that impact the product's taste, flavor, and color (Balakrishna et al., 2020). Interestingly, some lipid molecules peaked in the group with 25 % TPP substitution, including TG (48:1), TG (42:0) and others. In the second category, most lipids (mainly GPs) exhibited an upward trend with higher TPP substitution. However, some lipids, such as LPE (18:2) and PC (18:1e), first increased and then decreased, while others like TG (54:6e) and DG (36:1) showed the opposite pattern. Research indicated that GPs could be partially converted to GLs during lipid transformations (Guo et al., 2022). TPP substitution may enhance lipolysis, resulting in a decrease in GPs and an increase in GLs. These differential lipids are crucial for distinguishing fermented sausage samples with different TPP substitution ratios. Overall, these lipid compositional changes contributed to the elevated formation of aldehydes such as hexanal and benzaldehyde (section 3.8), indicating that TPP-driven lipid oxidation played a key role in flavor modulation.

E-nose analysis

The E-nose sensor arrays can mimic the biological olfactory system and effectively differentiate the overall flavor profile of different groups. As illustrated in Fig. 3A, key sensors such as W1C (sensitive to aromatic compounds), W2W (sensitive to organic sulfides), W2S (sensitive to alcohols, aldehydes, and ketones), W1W (sensitive to sulfides), and W5S (sensitive to nitrogen oxides) detected the variations between TPP-treated and control sausages. The responses of W1W, W2W, and W5S in the treatment groups were significantly lower (P < 0.05) and decreased with higher TPP substitution. This suggested that TPP substitution mainly impacts sulfide and nitrogen oxide compounds, reducing or masking meaty and roasted flavors (Li et al., 2022). Conversely, responses of W1C and W2S were higher in the treatment groups (P < 0.05), aligning with HS-SPME-GC–MS findings.

Radar chart (A) and PCA biplot (B) of the E-nose data for dry fermented sausages prepared by replacing different proportion of pork lean meat with TPP. Taste assessment (C) and PCA analysis (D) based on the E-tongue data.

Fig. 3B also presented the spatial distribution and distances of aromas, with PCA revealing a clear distinction between the control and treatment groups. PC1 and PC2 accounted for 70.5 % and 19.4 % of the variance, respectively, for a cumulative contribution of 89.9 % (> 85 %). This effectively captured the flavor characteristics. The P25 group clustered with the control group in the PCA plot, indicating minimal flavor differences. In contrast, higher TPP ratios (P50, P75, and P100) showed distinct flavor profiles, as reflected by their positions in separate quadrants.

E-tongue analysis

Electronic tongue devices, which translate electrical data into signals related to taste, simulate the human taste system and eliminate the subjectivity of sensory evaluation, making them ideal for assessing taste quality and differentiating varieties of dry sausages (Tan & Xu, 2020). The taste properties of all sausages were analyzed using eight sensors, detecting bitterness, saltiness, astringency, sourness, aftertaste-B, aftertaste-A, umami, and richness. The radar chart revealed TPP substitution significantly increased bitterness, astringency, and richness (P < 0.05), with bitterness and astringency rising with higher substitution ratios, although the increase in astringency was minimal (Fig. 3C). These changes might result from reduced Aw, which enhances bitterness, and from taste compounds such as free amino acids produced by protein hydrolysis (Zhao et al., 2016). Conversely, sourness, umami, and saltiness significantly decreased (P < 0.05), possibly due to increased weight loss (Zhang, Li, et al., 2024).

PCA results for the taste responses were presented in Fig. 3D, where PC1 (73.5 %) and PC2 (17.1 %) explained 90.6 % of the total variance indicating that they captured the overall characteristics of the sausages. PC1 showed a positive correlation with bitterness, saltiness, astringency, aftertastes, and richness, indicating these sensory attributes tended to vary in alignment with PC1. Similarly, PC2 was negatively correlated with umami. The P25 and P50 clusters appeared in the second quadrant, suggesting similar flavor profiles with variations in umami and sourness. The TPP groups were distinctly separated from the control, with the P75 group primarily differing in saltiness, astringency, and aftertastes, while the P100 group was characterized by increased bitterness and richness. The results highlighted the obvious impact of varying TPP ratios on the sausage flavor profile.

Sensory analysis

Sensory evaluation serves as a proxy for consumer experiences, directly reflecting product perceptions during use and supplementing instrumental analysis to provide comprehensive product insights. The median sensory attribute scores (color, odor, texture, taste, and overall acceptability) for fermented sausages incorporating TPP substitutes were presented in Table 1. The P100 sample exhibited the highest luster scores, which was consistent with L* measurements. Significant differences in odor and taste scores were observed between the treatment and control groups (P < 0.05), indicating that TPP addition influenced the overall sensory profile, with changes in the flavor characteristics, as reflected by E-nose and E-tongue analysis results. While sensory evaluation did not directly assess bitterness and astringency, the instrumental analyses suggested that TPP addition might have contributed to the reduction of meaty flavors and the enhancement of undesirable sensations, such as bitterness and astringency. These sensory alterations might be associated with the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids and changes in sulfide and nitrogen oxide compound concentrations. The P100 treatment had the lowest texture rating, likely due to TPP's reduced Aw and increased weight loss, which compromised the sausage's dense, resilient structure and caused pronounced softening. This softening was consistent with the lower hardness and chewiness observed at higher TPP levels (Section 3.2) and might influence aroma compound release during mastication. The P25 group closely mimicked the sensory attributes of the control group and presented a higher overall acceptability, suggesting that the incorporation of a low concentration of TPP has a negligible influence on the eating quality of sausages.

Volatile compounds analysis based on HS-SPME-GC–MS

To gain insights into the variations in aroma compounds among the sausages with different TPP substitution ratios, HS-SPME-GC–MS analysis was conducted. A total of 329, 387, 399, 386 and 354 compounds were identified in the T, P25, P50, P75 and P100 groups, respectively. Among these, 83 compounds were shared in all samples, including 6 alcohols, 15 ketones, 12 aldehydes, 16 esters, 12 acids, 3 pyrazines, 13 terpenes, 1 furan and 5 alkanes (Supplementary Table 2). Besides, the upset plot in Fig. 4A revealed that 27, 30, 11, 12 and 22 unique VOCs were detected in the T, P25, P50, P75 and P100 samples, respectively.

The differences of the VOCs based on GC–MS data. The upset plot (A), radar chart (B), PCA plot (C), PLS-DA plot (D), as well as VIP values of key volatile metabolisms (E). Hierarchical clustering heat map of key VOCs content changes with the screening conditions are VIP > 1, P < 0.05.

TPP substitution influenced both the quantity and types of VOCs (Fig. 4B). Obviously, the P25 group exhibited a significantly higher concentration of esters compared to the control group, which decreased with increasing TPP substitution ratios (P < 0.05). Esters, associated with floral and fruity aromas, were likely formed through esterification (Yao et al., 2022). Therefore, lower TPP substitution ratios (less than 25 %) might contribute to the floral and fruity notes in fermented sausages.

To further investigate the impact of TPP substitution on the variations of VOCs, a clustering heatmap was employed for the visual analysis of VOC content changes (Fig. 4C). Among all samples, the highest number of esters was identified. Most ester concentrations decreased with increasing TPP substitution ratios. The levels of all aldehydes increased with higher TPP substitution, indicating that TPP promoted aldehyde production (e.g. hexanal, (E)-2-hexenal, (E)-2-heptenal, (E)-2-octenal and benzaldehyde). This might stem from the hydrolysis of TG in higher concentrations, releasing free fatty acids. These acids likely oxidized to form aldehydes, aligning with the lipidomics results (Section 3.4). Hexanal was identified from the oxidative degradation of PUFA (Zhang et al., 2019). In addition, (E)-2-hexenal, (E)-2-heptenal and (E)-2-octenal significantly contributed to the fatty and green aromas (Sun et al., 2024). It was also observed that the Strecker degradation of tyrosine could produce benzaldehyde (Watanabe et al., 2015). Except for 3-methylbutanoic acid and ammonium carbamate which exhibited a gradual decrease, the trends for other acids and aldehydes were similar. Six alcohols (excluding [S-(R*,R*)]-2,3-butanediol and methanol) also showed a gradual decline with increasing TPP substitution. Studies indicated that fatty alcohols, predominantly resulting from the breakdown of oxidized fats, were critical for achieving optimal flavor and aroma (Yao et al., 2022). The concentration of 1-octen-3-ol increased, confirming that TPP substitution facilitated its formation. 1-Octen-3-ol has a relatively low threshold and a distinct mushroom aroma (Sun et al., 2023; Zhang, Mora, et al., 2024), contributing to the characteristic flavor of fermented sausages.

PCA and PLS-DA models were employed to further analyze the differences among the various samples (Fig. 4D and E). The results indicated significant separation between samples with different TPP substitution levels. As illustrated in Fig. 4F-G, nine key VOCs were identified based on VIP > 1, including 3 alcohols, 3 acids, 2 aldehydes and 1 pyrazine. The heatmap revealed substantial differences in the concentrations of the nine VOCs, with their variation trends categorized into three groups based on increasing TPP substitution ratios. The first category (3-methylbutanoic acid, methanol and trimethyl-pyrazine) manifested an upward trend with increasing TPP levels. Although the number of identified pyrazines was lower compared to other organic molecules, they significantly contributed to the flavor profile of fermented sausage, providing roasted and nut-like notes (Kantono et al., 2021). The second and third categories (benzaldehyde, 1-octen-3-ol, 2-methylpentanoic anhydride, butanoic acid, (E)-2-heptenal and propylene glycol) showed a marked decrease with increasing TPP substitution. In the P25 group, the key components benzaldehyde, 1-octen-3-ol and 2-methylpentanoic anhydride displayed the higher rOAV, contributing to bitter almond, mushroom and spicy notes, respectively. Ketones primarily originated from lipid oxidation and amino acid degradation (Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2021). The number of identified ketones was ranked in the second position, their contribution to the odor profile of the fermented sausage samples was less significant than that of other compounds.

Dynamic changes in VOCs analysis based on HS-GC-IMS

Qualitative analysis of the VOCs

The impact of TPP substitution on VOCs changes in fermented sausages was performed using GC-IMS. A total of 94 compounds were characterized in all the groups, including 29 alcohols, 14 aldehydes, 18 ketones, 8 esters, 11 acids, 3 terpenes and 6 other compounds (Supplementary Table 3). Thirty-seven compounds were detected by GC-IMS, but not by HS-SPME-GC–MS. The discrepancies in detected VOCs between GC-IMS and GC–MS are likely due to the differences in their detection principles, with GC-IMS providing real-time flavor compound identification and GC–MS offering more comprehensive compound profiling. The limitations of each technique's respective database may also contribute to these differences (Yao et al., 2024). These differences highlight the distinct detection principles inherent to each method (He et al., 2021).

Using the Reporter plugin, Fig. 5 presented a direct and comprehensive comparison of the spectral differences between samples. The three-dimensional spectrum illustrated the variations in VOCs among different groups regarding retention time, drift time and peak intensity. As evidenced in Fig. 5A, the peak intensity of compounds in the P25 group within the 380–700 range was higher than that in the other groups, showing a downward trend with increasing TPP substitution. This demonstrated that a TPP substitution ratio below 25 % facilitated the generation and accumulation of VOCs, which was likely linked to biochemical reactions such as lipid oxidation and amino acid metabolism during fermentation (Wen et al., 2019).

Fig. 5B provided a two-dimensional overhead view of the VOCs differences among the groups in terms of retention time, ion drift time and signal intensities. Red and white represent high and low signal intensities, respectively. A comparison of the normalized reactive ion peak (RIP) colors on the right side revealed significant differences between samples. For clearer visualization, the T group is used as a reference. The subtracted background appears white, while red indicates a higher concentration and blue indicates a lower concentration than the reference. As illustrated in Fig. 5C, most compounds displayed an increase in blue spots with higher TPP substitution ratios, primarily in the retention time range of 1050–650 s and drift time of 1.5–2.0 ms. This pointed to a significant reduction in the content of many substances. TPP substitution led to significant changes in the composition and concentration of VOCs in traditional dry-fermented sausages, thereby profoundly affecting their unique flavor.

Whole spectra analysis of VOCs

A gallery plot was employed to construct a fingerprint that elucidated the variation patterns of specific compounds (Fig. 5D), with the fingerprint derived from peak signals. Regions of red boxes, labeled A-E, indicate areas where compound concentrations exceed those of others. From region A and Supplementary Table 4, it was evident that TPP substitution significantly reduced the relative concentrations of 2-pentanone, diethylene glycol dimethyl ether and 1-methyl-4-isopropenyl-1-cyclohexene (P < 0.05). Among them, 2-pentanone, classified as a low molecular weight aromatic compound, has fruity notes (Jia et al., 2024). In region B, ketone compounds were present at significantly higher levels in the P25 group compared to others, characterized by fragrant and creamy notes typically arising from the Maillard reaction (Zeng et al., 2024). Ketones significantly influenced the oil flavor, as their interaction with other compounds could enhance overall flavor richness (Xi et al., 2024). Regions C and D revealed a gradual decrease in ketone VOCs and a corresponding increase in alcohols with higher TPP substitution ratios. Alcohols, associated with fruity and fresh notes, are generally produced through the oxidation of fatty acids or the reduction of aldehydes (Xu, Liu, et al., 2023). 1-butanol (caramel, roasted) is derived from the decomposition of linolenic acid (Zeng et al., 2024). The concentration of 1-octen-3-ol increased initially with TPP substitution before declining, with significantly higher levels in the P25 group. Previous studies have linked 1-octen-3-ol to an apparent mushroom aroma (Sunesen et al., 2004). According to region E and Supplementary Table 4, the concentrations of ketones and acids across all samples revealed no significant differences (P > 0.05), indicating that TPP substitution failed to significantly influence these compounds.

PCA analysis

To further analyze the differences among samples, PCA was conducted based on the signal peak intensities of volatile flavor components in Supplementary Table 5. The first two components (PC1: 55.2 %, PC2: 16.9 %) accounted for 72.1 % of the total variance (Fig. 5E), which was deemed adequate for supporting further data discussion (P < 0.05).

The score plot demonstrated a significant distinction between the treatment and control groups, indicating that TPP substitution markedly affected the flavor of traditional samples, corroborating the electronic nose results (section 3.5). The loading plot in Fig. 5F highlighted the correlation between TPP substitution ratios and volatile flavor compounds, confirming distinct distributions across quadrants. Most ketones exhibited a positive correlation with the control and P25 groups, clustered along the negative axis of PC1. In contrast, as the TPP substitution ratio increased, most alcohols were concentrated along the positive axis of PC1. These changes in flavor compounds aligned with the fingerprint results (section 3.8.2), validating the PCA and loadings plot findings. This close match with the fingerprint spectra in Fig. 5D provided enhanced visualization.

Additionally, the contribution of key flavor compounds to overall flavor was assessed by calculating the relative odor active value (rOAV). As detailed in Table 3, a total of 31 principal VOCs were identified across the samples, with 14, 15, 14, 13 and 14 key VOCs (1 ≤ rOAV ≤100) found in the T, P25, P50, P75 and P100 groups, respectively. Modified VOCs (0.1 < rOAV <1) were identified in quantities of 17, 16, 17, 18 and 17 for the same samples. Cis-Rose oxide (furan type) emerged as the most significant VOC contributing to the overall flavor in the T, P25, P50 and P75 groups (excluding P100 group). In the treatment groups, 3-octanone (characterized by fatty, nutty and spicy notes) and α-pinene (associated with pine and turpentine flavors) were identified as key VOCs, while in the control group, they were categorized as modified VOCs. Conversely, 3-methyl-1-pentanol (fruity, green and slightly irritating) and ethyl 2-methylpropanoate (fruity) exhibited the opposite trend. These findings indicated that TPP substitution significantly influenced the composition of these key flavor compounds. Furthermore, combined with the GC–MS results, the increase in aldehydes and decrease in esters likely contributed to the E-nose responses (section 3.5) and the enhanced bitterness detected by the E-tongue (section 3.6), suggesting that higher TPP levels drove a shift toward oxidation-derived, bitterness-associated volatiles.

Correlation analysis

To further elucidate the impact of lipid degradation and oxidation on the formation of flavor compounds, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted on key differential lipids (VIP > 2) and volatile compounds. As portrayed in Fig. 5G, a strong correlation exists between potential lipid markers and volatile organic compound markers. TG exhibited a significant negative correlation with several alcohols, including methanol, 1-octen-3-ol, 2-octanol, heptanol and 2-methyl-1-butanol. β-Oxidation may lead to the cleavage of carbon‑carbon bonds adjacent to these radicals, subsequently forming pentanol and 1-octen-3-ol (Chen et al., 2023). Moreover, various aldehydes (e.g. 3-methylbutanal, pentanal, heptanal and nonanal) demonstrated a significant negative correlation with DG, such as DG (36:1), DG (36:2), DG (32:3e), DG (32:1e), DG (36:3), DG (34:0e) and DG (15:0). Through glycerolipid degradation, diglycerides can convert to oleic acid, which then enters the oxidation pathway to yield nonanal (Sohail et al., 2022). Oleic and linoleic acids serve as critical precursors in the formation of aldehydes (Li et al., 2024). TG and DG were key precursors of lipid-derived volatile aromatic compounds. This study demonstrated that the oxidative decomposition of TG and DG resulted in significant alterations in the concentrations of various aromatic compounds, thereby contributing to the aroma profile of fermented sausages.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of replacing lean meat with varying proportions of TPP in traditional fermented sausages, focusing on physicochemical properties, flavor formation, lipid transformations, and sensory perception. The incorporation of TPP significantly lowered pH, Aw, glossiness, and yellowness while increasing weight loss during fermentation. Notably, sensory evaluation revealed critical consumer-centric insights: the 25 % TPP substitution group closely mimicked the sensory attributes of traditional sausages, achieving comparable texture scores, whereas the P100 treatment exhibited significant alterations in odor and taste profiles. Specifically, TPP addition obscured meaty flavors and introduced bitterness and astringency, as corroborated by E-nose and E-tongue analyses. These undesirable sensations were mechanistically linked to oxidative degradation of unsaturated fatty acids and dynamic shifts in sulfide and nitrogen oxide compounds. Textural deterioration in the P100 group, evidenced by reduced resilience and significant softening, aligned with instrumental texture profile analysis and was attributed to TPP's lower Aw and elevated weight loss disrupting the product's structural integrity. Lipidomics further elucidated these changes, identifying 125 lipid molecules as metabolic markers, with TGs and PEs declining proportionally to TPP levels, indicating accelerated lipid transformations. Flavoromics highlighted TPP's dual role in flavor modulation—diminishing sulfides and nitrogen oxides while enriching bitterness-associated volatiles (e.g., benzaldehyde, α-pinene) and texturally relevant compounds (1-octen-3-ol). Correlation analyses confirmed that oxidative breakdown of GLs directly drove flavor profile alterations. Collectively, this cross-scale investigation underscores that low TPP substitutions (e.g., 25 %) balance sustainability with sensory fidelity, whereas higher substitutions compromise palatability through physicochemical-sensory mismatches. By triangulating instrumental metrics with sensory panel data, this work provides practical guidance for optimizing hybrid fermented sausages, highlighting that appropriate TPP levels can improve product sustainability while maintaining consumer acceptance. Nevertheless, this study is limited by its focus on lipid metabolism, and the contribution of plant-derived proteins to flavor formation remains to be clarified. Future research should further explore this aspect to provide a more comprehensive understanding of flavor generation in hybrid fermented sausages.
