Prevalence and risk factors of self-medication with antibiotics in the general population of Tehran—a population-based study
Authors: Keyvan Karimi, Yosra Azizpour, Maryam Shafaati, Erta Rajabi, Maryam Zare, Arash Seifi, Ozra Tabatabaei-Malazy, Samaneh Akbarpour, Mahnaz Khalafehnilsaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven the ongoing global threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), largely fueled by self-medication with antibiotics (SMA), this study offers important insights from southern Tehran—an underrepresented region in national data—regarding the prevalence and determinants of SMA. We investigated several previously underexplored key factors, including socioeconomic status (SES), education, lifestyle, mental health, and COVID-19 history.MethodsA population-based cross-sectional telephone survey was conducted in three major regions of southern Tehran in 2023, involving 1,311 adult participants who were selected through stratified random sampling with probability proportional to size. A structured composite index was developed using data on lifestyle factors such as body mass index, physical activity, smoking, nutrition, mental and physical health, socioeconomic status, and frequency of SMA.ResultsOf the participants, 50.4% were male and 49.6% female, with an overall SMA prevalence of 21.3% (n = 279). Higher education (AOR = 1.93, 95% CI = 1.25–2.96, p value = 0.003), higher SES (AOR = 3.59, 95% CI = 1.82–6.82, p value < 0.001), basic insurance (AOR = 1.69, 95% CI = 1.10–2.63, p value = 0.019), and a history of uncomplicated COVID-19 infection (AOR = 2.30, 95% CI = 1.44–3.78, p value = 0.001) were significantly associated with increased SMA. In contrast, a healthy lifestyle (AOR = 0.66, p value = 0.033) and good mental health (AOR = 0.31, p value < 0.001) were associated with lower SMA rates.ConclusionOur findings primarily suggest that we should focus our public health efforts on specific subpopulations affected by SMA. Community-based antibiotic stewardship programs should use educational and behavioral approaches with an emphasis on mental health and health literacy. Policymakers should also consider whether to modify regulations to prevent the general public from obtaining antibiotics without a prescription. Finally, the findings from a representative sample from southern Tehran illustrate the value of local data in national AMR decision-making and avoiding assumptions.Keywords: Self-medication, Antibiotics, Antimicrobial resistance, Risk factors, Iran, Public health

Background

Antibiotics have been considered as one of the greatest medical breakthroughs of the 20th century in the fight against bacterial infections [1]. They possess considerable advantages and disadvantages [2]. While they have made a major contribution to public health and reduced mortality, their overuse and misuse can lead to antimicrobial resistance (AMR), which is now recognized as a global threat [3, 4]. In 2019, AMR was responsible for approximately 4.95 million deaths worldwide, with 1.27 million of these deaths directly attributable to AMR [5]. Antibiotic misuse due to self-medication practices is one of the main causes, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [6]. Estimates of the prevalence of self-medication with antibiotics (SMA) differ considerably among nations, with estimates ranging up to 67% worldwide. In the U.S., for example, it made up 42% [7]; in India, 55% [8]; in Indonesia, 91%; in Ghana, 46.6% [9]; in Ethiopia, 46.14% [10]; in Afghanistan, 73.2% [11]; and 61.6% [12] and 83% [13] among Iranian children and elderly populations.

The World Health Organization (WHO) describes self-medication (SM) as the improper use of medical products by individuals to treat self-diagnosed conditions or symptoms or the incorrect administration of prescription medications. This practice includes incorrect selection, dosage, or treatment duration, as well as self-prescription practices [14]. Antibiotics are frequently obtained for self-medication through non-prescription sales, using surplus medications, or sharing among relatives and friends, resulting in a global prevalence of self-medication of approximately 50% from both formal and informal sources [15]. Furthermore, previous studies suggest that numerous individuals are either uninformed or skeptical regarding the adverse effects of SM [16–18], which contribute to heightened healthcare costs, adverse drug reactions, improper dosing, and the rise of AMR [19, 20].

The available data on the prevalence of SMA in Iran, which surpasses the global norm, remains limited. Due to its possible adverse impact on the healthcare system and its considerable role in AMR in the region, further studies are essential to identify the underlying factors and develop strategies to promote the responsible use of antibiotics. This paradigm aimed to determine the prevalence and risk factors associated with SMA, thereby increasing our comprehension of its determinants among the Iranian population.

Methods

Study design and sampling

Data were collected through our prior study entitled “Integrated and Repetitive Public Health Surveillance System (IRPHS)” in 2023 [21]. A cross-sectional, telephone-based survey employed a stratified random sampling method with probability proportional to size (PPS) to select 1,311 adults from three districts in Tehran.

Assessment tools and operational definitions

Structured questions were used to assess socioeconomic status (SES), health conditions, and demographic characteristics associated with antibiotic self-medication, providing a score of 1 for correct responses and 0 for incorrect or “don’t know” responses. The key components of the structured questions were as follows:

SMA

We asked the participants if they had used antibiotics without a prescription in the previous year. We posed the question: “Do you use antibiotics without a doctor’s prescription?” Responses were divided into two options: (1) Yes, whenever I need it or sometimes, and (2) No.

Baseline characteristics and socioeconomic factors

Information on participants’ age, sex, education, marital status, employment, family size, and place of residence (urban/rural) was systematically collected. SES was self-reported on a three-level scale: Low, Middle (lower-middle and middle), and Good (upper-middle and high). We also asked participants if they had visited any healthcare facilities in the past year. Furthermore, we assessed participants’ basic insurance status as a potential factor associated with SMA practices, considering its relationship to healthcare access patterns.

Lifestyle and health indicators

I.We evaluated participants’ physical activity (PA), smoking status, body mass index (BMI), and dietary habit using dichotomous (yes/no) or scaled questions, following the guidelines of the global health survey [22–24]. For smoking behavior, a value of 0 was assigned to individuals who use at least one tobacco product, while others were assigned a value of 1. For BMI, individuals with normal weight (18.9 < BMI < 24.9) receive a score of 2, overweight individuals (25 ≤ BMI < 29.9) receive a score of 1, and obese individuals (BMI ≥ 30) receive a score of 0. In terms of diet, the daily consumption units of fruits, vegetables, dairy products, and meat were converted to a score between 0 and 1, according to the views of nutritional experts. The sum of these four items was then converted into tertiles, resulting in values of 0, 1, and 2 for the lowest, middle, and highest tertiles, respectively. Physical activity was assessed based on WHO recommendations and the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [25]. In the current study, only questions related to recreational activities were used. Participants with less than 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous PA per week were considered as insufficiently active and assigned a score of 0; those with 150 min or more were assigned a score of 1 [26]. Finally, the total scores of these four components (smoking, BMI, diet, and physical activity) were summed, and the total score was converted into tertiles. The lowest tertile represented an unhealthy lifestyle, while the highest tertile reflected a healthy lifestyle.

Mental health screening

We considered mental health by converting the total scores of anxiety, depression into tertiles. Anxiety and depression were measured using validated tools, such as the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2) and the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2), which use a 4-point Likert scale: 0 = not at all, 1 = some days, 2 = more than half of the days, and 3 = nearly every day. The tools were adapted for cultural relevance in the Iranian population. Higher total scores indicate more severe symptoms [27, 28].

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the data using STATA software. Missing values in the collected data were imputed with a multiple imputation approach. By determining the population-to-sample ratio in each age and gender-specific category using data from the 2016 Iranian census, we applied sample weighting to the data. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using skewness and kurtosis tests, as well as visual examination of Q-Q plots and histograms. To address these concerns regarding data distribution, all continuous variables in the study were categorized in the analysis [29]. We used a weighed percentages with associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to represent categorical variables. To determine the association between different variables and the use of antibiotics and self-medication, data were examined using ANOVA and Chi-square testing. We used logistic regression to identify factors associated with antibiotic self-medication. To evaluate the strength of relationships, odds ratios (ORs) and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% CI were calculated. Statistical significance was defined as p values less than 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The Tehran University of Medical Sciences Research Committee (Approval No. IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1400.599) granted ethical approval for the study’s design and data collection procedures. We obtained verbal informed consent from each participant in the telephone survey.

Results

Prevalence of SMA by socio-demographic characteristics

Table 1 outlines the socio-demographic factors of the participants and demonstrates the distribution of SMA across different socio-demographic categories. The total number of participants in the study was 1,311, with a sex distribution of 50.4% (n = 661, 95% CI = 47.4–53.4) and 49.6% (n = 650, 95% CI = 46.6–52.6) males and females, respectively. First of all, the percentage of people who had SMA in our overall study was 21.3% (n = 279); among them, 18.8% of males (n = 124, 95% CI = 15.2–22.3) and 21.9% of females (n = 143, 95% CI = 18.8–25.1) were the ones involved in this behavior. No significant differences in the two sexes were recorded (p value = 0.110). Regarding the age group, there was a higher SMA prevalence, which was 25–45, with 21.9% of them being affected (n = 141, 95% CI = 18.7–25.1), while among the older individuals, that is, the ones of age > 65, the SMA finding was the lowest, as their percentage was just 11.3% (n = 15, 95% CI = 3.3–19.3). Additionally, almost equal portions of our participants were single (21.4%, 95% CI = 15.5–27.3), divorced or widowed (21.5%, 95% CI = 12.1–30.9), or married (20%, 95% CI = 17.4–22.7), with no significant associations between marital status and SMA (p value = 0.657). Moreover, 23.3% (95% CI = 3.8–15.8) and 20.2% (95% CI = 17.7–22.6) were from rural and urban communities, respectively, with no significant difference between these groups (p value = 0.677). Likewise, the majority of our participants were unemployed (21.1%, 95% CI = 17.9–24.2), followed by employed individuals (19.7%, 95% CI = 16.1–23.1) and retired individuals (19.9%, 95% CI = 9.5–30.3), with no significant differences between employment status and SMA (p value = 0.839). Furthermore, 19.7% (95% CI = 16.5–23.1) of participants reported at least one visit to healthcare facilities during the past year. Conversely, 20.9% (95% CI = 17.6–24.2) indicated that they did not visit healthcare facilities, with no significant differences observed among these individuals (p value = 0.195). In terms of level of education, individuals possessing a higher level of education, especially those with a collegiate background, exhibited a greater prevalence of SMA (23.5%, n = 58, 95% CI = 18.6–28.3) in contrast to those who were illiterate or had only completed primary education (14.3%, n = 40, 95% CI = 9.5–18.9), demonstrating a significant difference (p value = 0.005). Participants categorized within the ‘low’ socio-economic status exhibited the lowest prevalence of SMA at 15.9% (n = 65, 95% CI = 13.1–18.7). In contrast, those in the ‘good’ socio-economic status category revealed the highest prevalence at 34.3% (n = 27, 95% CI = 21.4–47.2), indicating a highly significant difference (p < 0.001).

SMA distribution: health, lifestyle, and mental health factors

According to Table 2, those who engaged in unhealthy lifestyle behaviors were more likely to practice SMA (22.8%, 95% CI = 19.3–26.4). Those who led healthy lifestyles reported a reduced prevalence of 15.5% (95% CI = 11.5–19.4), whereas those who led partially healthy lifestyles reported a prevalence of 20.9% (95% CI = 15.9–25.9). Nevertheless, there was no significant difference (p value = 0.095). Concerning comorbidities, the prevalence of SMA was slightly higher in individuals who did not have any underlying health conditions (20.9%, 95% CI = 18.5–23.5) than in individuals who did have underlying health conditions (13.1%, 95% CI = 5.4–20.8). This difference was marginally significant (p value = 0.052). The analysis of COVID-19 infection history indicates that the prevalence of SMA varied between 30.0% (95% CI = 17.2–42.9) and 35.6% (95% CI = 26.2–45.1). This prevalence was significantly elevated when compared to the 18.4% (95% CI = 16.1–20.8) observed in individuals lacking a history of COVID-19 (p value < 0.001). No significant association was identified between health literacy levels and SMA (p value = 0.449). Individuals experiencing poor mental health exhibited a higher prevalence of SMA at 31.2% (95% CI = 24.4–37.9) when compared to those with moderate mental health at 20.5% (95% CI = 16.8–24.1) and good mental health at 16.1% (95% CI = 12.7–19.5). The difference observed was statistically significant (p value < 0.001). Finally, participants who reported dental issues exhibited a notably higher prevalence of SMA (24.9%, 95% CI = 19.6–28.5) in contrast to those without dental issues (18.7%, 95% CI = 15.9–21.4) (p value = 0.049).

Additional determinants contributing to SMA

Table 3 presents the factors associated with an increased tendency toward SMA. As demonstrated, there were no significant associations between sex, age, marital status, residency, visits to healthcare facilities in the past year, and health literacy with SMA use (p value > 0.05). Conversely, individuals with higher education levels, such as those with a collegiate education, had 1.93 times higher odds of engaging in SMA (p value = 0.003, 95% CI = 1.25–2.96) compared to participants with lower educational levels. Similarly, SES was strongly associated with SMA. SES followed the same pattern, as individuals with good and middle SES had 3.59 times (p value < 0.001, 95% CI = 1.82–6.82) and 2.16 times (p value < 0.001, 95% CI = 1.54–3.04) higher odds to practicing SMA than individuals with lower SES. Additionally, individuals with basic insurance had a 69% higher odds of practicing SMA (p value = 0.019, AOR = 1.69, 95% CI = 1.10–2.63). There was no significant association between a partially healthy lifestyle and SMA, as indicated by an AOR of 1.00 and a non-significant p value (p value = 0.966). However, maintaining a healthy lifestyle was associated with 34% lower odds of SMA (p value = 0.033, AOR = 0.66, 95% CI = 0.45–0.95). We observed that individuals with a prior history of COVID-19 infection without complications had more than twice the odds of developing SMA compared to those without such a history (p value = 0.001, AOR = 2.30, 95% CI = 1.44–3.78). Participants with good mental health demonstrated significantly lower odds of SMA in comparison to those with poor mental health (p value < 0.001, AOR = 0.31, 95% CI = 0.21–0.50). Additionally, participants with moderate mental health had 42% lower odds of SMA compared to those with poor mental health (p value = 0.008, AOR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.39–0.87).

Discussion

This study examined factors related to SMA in a population sample. Certain socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, and health conditions are associated with a greater tendency to engage in SMA. Significantly, factors such as higher education, SES, mental health, and a history of uncomplicated COVID-19 infections are substantially associated with SMA practices.

AMR has emerged as one of the most urgent public health challenges worldwide, driven by the inappropriate use and overuse of antibiotics, and SMA exacerbates the problem [30], as one study reported increased antibiotic misuse among individuals without a proper antibiotic prescription [31]. Here, we highlight that higher education, SES, health insurance, poor mental health, and a history of uncomplicated COVID-19 infection are key factors contributing to the increased prevalence of SMA, likely reflecting access to antibiotics and self-diagnosis confidence. Additionally, maintaining a healthy lifestyle was found to be significantly related to a lower possibility of SMA.

Studies have consistently reported higher prevalence of SMA in the Middle East compared to other regions [18, 32], with Saudi Arabia exhibiting alarmingly high rates approaching 80% [33].

Moreover, the unsupervised purchase of over-the-counter (OTC) antibiotics in the Middle East continues to be a significant issue, contributing to the rising rates of AMR [34–36]. Our study identified an SMA of 21.3%, which is notably lower than the findings of peer studies conducted in Iran, which reported rates of 57.9%, 43.6%, 42.6%, and 40%, respectively [37–41]. A recent study in Arak, Iran, found that 30.3% of people self-medicate with antibiotics. Younger people and those with less education were more likely to exhibit this tendency. Additionally, males were less likely than females to use antibiotics as a kind of SMA. These results emphasize the need to adopt health measures to prevent self-administration and educate the public about the proper utilization of antibiotics [7]. Muflih et al. demonstrated an SMA prevalence of 74.2%, indicating that individuals with elevated health literacy are less likely to engage in this behavior. The findings underscore the necessity of enhancing public health literacy to reduce inappropriate self-medication, particularly regarding the misuse of antibiotics [42].

The observed discrepancies can be associated with various factors, such as the lack of a unified national surveillance system and the prevalent access to antibiotics via informal pharmacy channels, both of which have been recognized as significant contributors to SMA practices [43]. A study conducted by SMA in the Middle East in 2017 revealed a significant range of prevalence, from 19 to 82%, underscoring the concerning levels of SMA in the region. SMA rates in Iraq range from 17.4 to 53.9% [44, 45].

The notable disparity in SMA rates all over the Middle East, especially in contrast to northern and western Europe or the U.S., illustrates the urgent need for enhanced and rigorously implemented antibiotic stewardship programs to address this escalating public health concern [46, 47].

Our study unexpectedly revealed that persons with elevated educational levels and superior SES demonstrated a heightened propensity for SMA. The risk of SMA was almost twice as high in participants with a postsecondary degree compared to those with only a primary-level education, corroborating findings from another study [48]. This could be due to the easy access to antibiotics among individuals with better SES or enhanced access to health information and a belief in self-diagnosis and treatment as more acceptable among those with greater education and superior SES, as one study reported a three-fold increase in SM among participants with higher incomes [49]. While various studies suggest that increased rates of SMA are due to a lack of knowledge—many individuals report using antibiotics incorrectly for conditions like the common cold and sore throat [31, 50, 51]—one study found a similar increase in SMA even among individuals with higher education [52]. Multiple studies reported high rates of SMA practices among medical and nursing students [37, 53, 54]. Furthermore, Onsori et al. demonstrated the significant role of higher education as one of the predictors of increased tendency toward antibiotic use without prescription [38]. A possible rationale for this tendency among educated individuals might be their enhanced access to health information and ability for self-diagnosis, frequently affected by online and social media resources [55, 56].

In addition to higher education and SES, our study identified significant associations between basic insurance coverage, previous COVID-19 infection without complications, poor mental health, and SMA practices. There is an ongoing debate about the role of basic insurance in promoting SMA. Some studies suggest that basic insurance plays a significant role in increasing SMA, while others have found no significant impact [48]. Meanwhile, a third group suggests that basic insurance may actually decrease SMA tendencies, a finding that aligns with our findings [41, 57]. This observation is intriguing, as it opposes previous reports where individuals with no basic insurance coverage had a higher tendency toward SMA due to the increased expenses of prescribed medications [37, 48, 51, 58–61]. Our findings imply that health insurance may be a risk factor regarding SMA, as insured individuals had 69% higher odds of practicing SMA. These results could be attributed to increased access to healthcare resources and antibiotics, which may ultimately lead to an increase in SMA practices [62, 63]. Moreover, such discrepancies regarding the role of health insurance in SMA could be due to differences in various health insurance policies [64]. Similarly, the impact of poor mental health on SMA is noteworthy and aligns with previous findings suggesting a direct association between mental health challenges and increased SMA tendencies [65, 66].

Additionally, during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, there was a notable shift toward increased SMA. For example, Khami et al. reported a 30% rise in SMA rates following the pandemic [67, 68]. The higher prevalence of SMA among individuals without complications following COVID-19 infection may be attributed to the heightened prescription of antibiotics in this era, the widespread misconception that antibiotics are effective for common colds or any viral infections that share similar symptoms with COVID-19 [68–70], and increased OTC antibiotic availability during the pandemic. Therefore, strengthening regulations on non-prescription antibiotic sales is critical.

In addition, although factors such as age and sex have been previously suggested as potential contributors to SMA prevalence, per our findings, two studies conducted on Iranian health sciences students and the Arabic general population revealed no significant associations [33, 37]. Health literacy was found to have no significant impact on SMA, which may be overshadowed by the stronger effects of health insurance, education level, and socioeconomic status, contrasting with previous research that found SMA decreased with higher health literacy [42]. Additionally, one study conducted in Iran reported a reverse association between health literacy and irrational antibiotic use, necessitating further studies to determine the impact of health literacy on SMA [71].

Lastly, a drawback of our study was the use of a telephone survey to collect data for assessing health-related indicators. As a result, we were unable to offer a comprehensive “reasons for use” table. Additionally, any biases present in self-reported telephone surveys may affect the results. Furthermore, a subset of data gathered at the Tehran University of Medical Sciences—with an emphasis on regions of southern Tehran—was the source of our data. Therefore, applying these results to the whole Iranian population may have limitations because of regional and demographic disparities.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that a history of uncomplicated COVID-19 infection, basic insurance coverage, higher education, better socioeconomic status, poor mental health, and a healthy lifestyle were significantly associated with SMA behavior. To address this public health concern and mitigate the threat of AMR, we recommend implementing targeted awareness campaigns, particularly among lower socioeconomic groups; strengthening regulatory measures to control over-the-counter antibiotic sales; and developing community-based interventions that promote rational antibiotic use. In addition, nationwide antibiotic stewardship programs should be integrated into Iran’s healthcare infrastructure to ensure responsible prescribing and public education.
