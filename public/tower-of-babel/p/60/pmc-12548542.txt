Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a progressive cerebrovascular disorder characterized by progressive stenosis or occlusion of the distal internal carotid arteries (ICAs) and their proximal branches, accompanied by the development of an abnormal network of fragile collateral vessels. While surgical revascularization, particularly direct superficial temporal artery–to–middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) bypass, remains the major treatment to reduce ischemic and hemorrhagic risks in adults, there is a possibility of developing fatal hemorrhagic complications due to hemodynamic changes in rare cases.

Herein, we present the case of a 34-year-old male patient with MMD who developed an intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) due to a ruptured aneurysm at the anastomosis site 2.5 months postoperatively.

Illustrative Case

A 34-year-old male had been diagnosed with MMD at a nearby hospital following cerebral infarction in the right internal capsule. At the same hospital, he underwent a combined surgical revascularization approach consisting of a direct STA-MCA bypass along with an indirect bypass on the right side (Fig. 1A). Although an STA loop was observed just proximal to the anastomosis on follow-up MR angiography (MRA), the bypass remained patent, and his postoperative course had been uneventful (Fig. 1B–D).

A: Postoperative MR angiogram. B–D: Time series of MRA of the anastomosis site showing STA loop without aneurysm formation.

At 2.5 months after surgery, he was admitted to our hospital after experiencing sudden severe headache, vomiting, consciousness disturbance, and right-sided anisocoria. CT demonstrated a massive ICH (approximately 100 ml) (Fig. 2A). Three-dimensional CT angiography revealed an aneurysm formation at the anastomosis site (4.6 mm × 2.5 mm), which was determined to be the source of the hemorrhage (Fig. 2B and C). Emergency surgery was performed to evacuate the hematoma and clip the aneurysm. On reopening the previous surgical site, we removed the bone flap and evacuated the intracranial hematoma. As the anastomotic site was embedded within firm adhesive scar tissue, sharp dissection was performed and both the anastomotic site and distal M4 segment were exposed (Fig. 3A). The aneurysm originated from the MCA wall opposite to the anastomotic site, suggesting a hemodynamic etiology, consistent with a true aneurysm of the M4 segment. We successfully clipped the aneurysm while preserving the anastomosis, and patency was confirmed using indocyanine green angiography before closure (Fig. 3B). The postoperative course was uneventful, and complete clipping with preserved anastomotic patency was confirmed on postoperative angiography (Fig. 4A and B). The patient was discharged on postoperative day 23, exhibiting severe left-sided hemiparesis but without speech or cognitive impairment. There were no changes in the therapeutic intervention during the course of treatment.

Discussion

Observations

MMD is a complex condition characterized by progressive stenosis or occlusion of the distal ICAs and their bifurcations, leading to the formation of compensatory collateral networks. We present the case of a 34-year-old male who developed a ruptured de novo anastomotic aneurysm just 2.5 months following combined bypass revascularization surgery for MMD, representing the shortest documented interval between surgery and such a complication. Surgical treatment for blood flow reconstruction in MMD patients includes direct revascularization via anastomosis of the STA-MCA bypass and indirect revascularization procedures, such as encephalo-duro-arterio-synangiosis, encephalo-duro-myo-synangiosis, or a combination of both techniques. In adult cases, surgical procedures including direct revascularization are recommended because they have shown significant benefits in preventing not only further ischemic events, but also hemorrhagic strokes compared with indirect revascularization.1,2 However, surgical interventions carry risks, including hyperperfusion syndrome, cerebral hemorrhage, or ischemia, which are the major postoperative complications that mainly occur in the acute phase. De novo anastomotic aneurysm formation is a less common but potentially fatal complication.

The development of a spontaneous aneurysm is influenced by multiple genetic factors, including abnormalities in extracellular matrix components, increased expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), polymorphisms in inflammatory cytokines, and variants in nitric oxide (NO) synthase genes. These genetic polymorphisms and expression abnormalities contribute to structural fragility of the vascular wall and enhanced inflammatory responses, thereby increasing the risk of aneurysm formation and rupture.3 Therefore, MMP inhibitors and NO donors or modulators have been investigated as novel therapeutic approaches for subarachnoid hemorrhage.4

Unlike spontaneous aneurysms, however, the pathophysiology of anastomotic aneurysm formation in MMD involves multiple interrelated mechanisms. The genetic background of MMD involves various mutations, particularly the p.Arg4810Lys variant in the ring finger protein 213 (RNF213) gene. These mutations lead to endothelial dysfunction, resulting in dysregulation of cellular adhesion, thrombosis, smooth muscle cell proliferation, and vessel wall inflammation. Consequently, they increase vascular fragility and promote abnormal angiogenesis.5,6

Unlike arteriosclerotic vascular walls characterized by macrophage infiltration into the subintimal layer and lipid pool accumulation, MMD exhibits distinct pathological features including fibrocellular thickening of the intima, irregular undulation of the internal elastic laminae, and medial thinness.7,8 These structural abnormalities contribute significantly to the fragility of blood vessels in MMD patients and increase susceptibility to aneurysm formation. In fact, approximately 3.4%–15% of patients with MMD have been reported to develop spontaneous aneurysm formations in the major and nonmajor intracranial arteries.9,10

For anastomotic aneurysms, the hemodynamic changes following surgical revascularization represent another important factor in their development. Unlike the gradual hemodynamic changes associated with disease progression in MMD, surgical anastomosis induces rapid alterations in cerebral hemodynamics, increasing shear stress on the vessel walls of adjacent or downstream arteries. Furthermore, this shear stress activates inflammatory pathways, leading to endothelial dysfunction and pathological remodeling, which makes certain segments of the vessel more susceptible to aneurysm formation.11,12

The development of de novo anastomotic aneurysms has been reported in a small number of patients with MMD or other diseases, with varying intervals between surgery and aneurysm formation, ranging from the early postoperative phase to several decades later (Table 1).13–25 This wide temporal range suggests the importance of comprehensive imaging follow-up throughout the patient’s clinical course. Our case is particularly important for the extremely short interval of only 2.5 months between surgery and aneurysm rupture, emphasizing the potential for rapid development of this anastomotic aneurysm in certain patients.

Lessons

Multiple pathophysiological mechanisms likely contribute to anastomotic aneurysm formation in MMD patients. These include genetic mutations associated with MMD, inflammatory and immunological factors, and hemodynamic alterations following revascularization surgery. These factors collectively compromise vascular fragility, creating a predisposition for de novo anastomotic aneurysm formation.

Anastomotic aneurysms develop at varying intervals following surgery, ranging from several months to decades postoperatively. This report is the earliest case of anastomotic aneurysm formation and subsequent rupture causing ICH following direct bypass surgery for MMD. Although these complications occur infrequently, they carry significant morbidity and mortality risks. Therefore, it is crucial to recognize that the risk of aneurysm formation and rupture exists not only in the long term but also in the early postoperative phase. We recommend performing postoperative surveillance imaging from the early postoperative period and maintaining regular follow-up. Imaging studies should focus not only on bypass patency but also on morphological changes at the anastomotic site to facilitate early detection of this potentially life-threatening complication.
