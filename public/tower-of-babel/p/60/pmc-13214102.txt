Integrated bioinformatics and experimental validation reveal that CENPF activates mTORC1 signaling to promote colorectal cancer progression
Authors: Haolin Jiang, Zhaoqiang Chen, Li Zhang, Chenlei Yuan, Shengjia Wang, Jiawei Zhang, Jiaxin Ji, Jin Wang, Zhe Zhang, Yanjin Bai, Wanying Zhang, Yujian Xia, Jianxin Ye, Xin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe incidence and mortality rates of colorectal cancer (CRC) continue to increase, underscoring the need to identify key gene networks and clinically relevant biomarkers to better understand its molecular basis. This study aimed to systematically screen and validate centromere protein F (CENPF) as a core gene associated with CRC. In this study, using the GSE74602 dataset from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) revealed that the blue and turquoise modules were significantly associated with CRC. The overlap of DEGs and module genes was imported into the STRING database to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network, and 39 hub genes were subsequently identified using Cytoscape software. Machine learning approaches, including least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and random forest (RF), prioritized CENPF and topoisomerase II alpha (TOP2A) as candidate genes. Given that TOP2A has been extensively investigated in CRC, we focused on CENPF for further analyses. CENPF overexpression was validated across multiple datasets (GEPIA, GSE113513, and GSE44076). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, including cross-validated evaluation, suggested strong diagnostic discrimination, and survival analysis indicated a possible association between high CENPF expression and poorer outcomes. Functional experiments demonstrated that silencing CENPF suppressed CRC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. Mechanistically, CENPF promoted CRC progression through activation of mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling pathway. Overall, these findings indicate that CENPF may serve as a candidate diagnostic marker and potential therapeutic target in CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04272-9.Keywords: CENPF, mTORC1 signaling pathway, Tumor cell proliferation and invasion

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, with an increasing incidence and mortality rate [1–3]. According to the 2020 global cancer statistics, 1.93 million new cases of CRC have been reported, accounting for 10% of all new cancer cases, making it the third most common cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [4, 5]. Importantly, CRC exhibits substantial intertumoral heterogeneity in molecular subtypes, tumor microenvironment composition, and therapeutic responsiveness, which poses major challenges for patient stratification and individualized treatment. Although early diagnosis and treatment have improved outcomes in high-income regions, CRC incidence and mortality continue to rise in low- and middle-income regions, which is largely due to population aging, unhealthy diets, and lifestyle changes [6]. The occurrence and progression of CRC is a complex process involving genetic mutations, epigenetic modifications, and abnormalities in molecular signaling pathways; however, the exact pathological mechanisms are still not completely understood [7–9]. Most patients are diagnosed in an advanced stage due to the lack of obvious early symptoms and low colonoscopy compliance, resulting in a poor prognosis [10, 11]. Immunotherapy and molecular targeted therapy have shown promising results in some patients in recent years, but not all patients are suitable for these therapies [12–14]. Thus, identifying novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets is crucial to improving early diagnosis, treatment efficacy, and patient survival in CRC.

With the advancement of bioinformatics, researchers have been able to explore CRC-related genes and molecular mechanisms in greater depth using high-throughput sequencing and Big Data analysis methods. Public repositories, including the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), are rich resources for such investigations [15–17]. Researchers can systematically identify gene modules associated with CRC development and progression by combining differential expression analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction [18, 19]. Moreover, the application of machine learning methods, such as least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and random forest (RF) algorithms, further enhances the accuracy of key gene selection, thus facilitating the discovery of potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets [20, 21]. Notably, integrating multi-cohort evidence with network interpretation and downstream experimental validation can improve robustness and biological interpretability, thereby helping address heterogeneity-related challenges in CRC.

In this study, we aimed to address the above challenges by implementing an integrated analytical and validation framework to prioritize CRC-relevant candidate genes and pathways. Specifically, we combined differential expression analysis with WGCNA to identify CRC-associated gene modules, constructed a PPI network to characterize core interaction structures, and applied machine learning–based gene prioritization to refine candidate targets. We then assessed candidate gene expression patterns in independent cohorts and performed in vitro experiments to support biological relevance and explore potential mechanisms. By linking multi-cohort bioinformatics evidence to network/module-level interpretation and experimental verification, this study provides a structured strategy to identify candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets for CRC and to generate mechanistic hypotheses for further investigation.

Materials and methods

Human samples

Thirty-four patients with CRC were recruited from the Department of General Surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between January and December 2024. Both tumor tissues and corresponding adjacent normal tissues (collected at least 5 cm from the tumor margin) were obtained and subjected to histopathological verification. The study cohort comprised 19 men and 15 women, aged between 33 and 75 years. All participants were treatment naive, with no prior history of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy. Tissue samples were divided into two parts: one was fixed with 10% neutral-buffered formalin for paraffin embedding, whereas the other was stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to sample collection, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee.

Data acquisition

Standardized gene expression matrices were downloaded from the GEO database to examine the DEGs in CRC transcriptome datasets (GSE74602, GSE113513, and GSE44076). At the initial stage of this study, we prioritized independent GEO cohorts with clearly defined primary CRC tissues and adjacent non-tumor/normal controls, complete annotation, and adequate sample size for cross-validation; therefore, these three datasets were included (Table S1). The GSE74602 dataset comprised 60 samples, including 30 CRC tissues and 30 adjacent normal tissues. The GSE113513 dataset contained 28 samples (14 CRC tissues and 14 adjacent normal tissues). The GSE44076 dataset consisted of 246 samples (98 CRC tissues, 98 adjacent normal tissues, and 50 normal tissues). All datasets were normalized to ensure comparability across various datasets.

Weighted gene co‑expression network analysis

WGCNA was performed to construct modules associated with CRC based on gene expression profiles. Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to generate a similarity matrix. An appropriate soft-thresholding power (β) was determined using the scale-free topology criterion, and β = 10 was selected for network construction. The similarity matrix was transformed into an adjacency matrix and then converted into the topological overlap matrix(TOM). Hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cutting were applied to identify gene modules, with each module containing at least 30 genes. A total of 46 distinct modules were identified, with the blue and turquoise modules considered the core modules because they showed the strongest associations with CRC status in the module–trait correlation analysis and were therefore selected for downstream intersection and network analyses.

Analysis of DEGs

Differential expression analysis between CRC and adjacent normal tissues was performed using the R package limma. Briefly, the expression matrix was inspected and log2-transformed when necessary, followed by quantile normalization using normalizeBetweenArrays. Probes were mapped to gene symbols according to the corresponding platform annotation file; when multiple probes mapped to the same gene symbol, we performed deduplication by retaining the probe with the highest mean expression to avoid repeated counting at the probe level. During WGCNA sample clustering, one paired outlier sample (GSM1923670/GSM1923671) was identified; to ensure that network analysis and DEG analysis were conducted on a consistent sample set, this paired outlier was also removed from subsequent differential expression analysis. A design matrix was constructed based on group labels, and differential expression was assessed by linear model fitting (lmFit) followed by empirical Bayes moderation (eBayes). P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and DEGs were defined as |log2FC| > 1 and FDR (adj.P.Val) < 0.05. Volcano plots were generated to visualize DEGs. The “VennDiagram” package was used to visualize genes shared between the WGCNA key module genes (blue and turquoise modules) and DEGs.

PPI network analysis

We used the STRING database to construct a PPI network for the identified genes, setting the confidence threshold of 0.7 to ensure the inclusion of high-confidence protein interactions. Isolated nodes were hidden to focus on informative interactions. The resulting network was visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.1, facilitating the exploration of complex interrelationships. Additionally, we used the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) plugin integrated within Cytoscape to identify key hub genes playing a crucial role in the structure and biological function of the network. In this study, “hub genes” were defined as hub candidate genes derived from the top-scoring MCODE modules rather than being selected solely by a single global topology metric (e.g., degree). MCODE was run with the following parameters: Degree Cutoff = 2; Node Density Cutoff = 0.1; Node Score Cutoff = 0.2; K-Core = 2; Max Depth = 100; Haircut = Yes; Fluff = No. The two top-scoring, non-overlapping modules were integrated to comprehensively capture distinct CRC-relevant interaction cores, and genes within these modules were carried forward as hub candidate genes for downstream validation.

Functional correlation analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed to explore the biological processes (BPs), cellular components (CCs), and molecular functions (MFs) of hub genes. Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed to uncover the signaling pathways in which these genes were involved. Both GO and KEGG analyses were performed using the “clusterProfiler” and “org.Hs.eg.db” R packages, with a screening criterion of adjusted P value < 0.05. The results were visualized using the “dotplot” function for a clear representation of enrichment patterns.

Machine‑learning algorithm screening for biomarkers

Two machine learning algorithms, LASSO and RF, were applied to identify potential candidate diagnostic markers for CRC. Both models used the binary phenotype (tumor vs. normal; Tumor = 1, Normal = 0) as the outcome variable. LASSO regression analysis was carried out using the “glmnet” package (alpha = 1). Cross-validation was applied to determine the optimal regularization parameter (λ), and λ.1se was selected for a more parsimonious and robust model. Before LASSO fitting, the feature matrix was standardized using scale(). RF analysis was performed using the “randomForest” package, with the number of trees set to 500. The mtry parameter was tuned using 10-fold cross-validation (caret::train), and the optimal mtry was used to train the final model. Feature importance was assessed using MeanDecreaseAccuracy and MeanDecreaseGini.

Evaluation and correlation analysis of infiltration‑related immune cells

The immune landscape in CRC was investigated by employing the Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) algorithm to estimate the relative proportions of 22 immune cell types in tumor and adjacent normal tissues. The algorithm analyzed tissue expression matrix using linear support vector regression and accurately quantified the abundance of 22 immune cell types in each sample. The analysis was conducted using the “CIBERSORT” R package with parameters set as “perm = 100” and “QN = TRUE”. The “ggpubr” and “ggplot2” packages were employed to visualize the relationship between biomarker expression and immune cell infiltration.

Screening of potentially effective drugs

Drug enrichment analysis was performed using the Enrichr platform to explore potential targeted drugs for CENPF. We comprehensively considered the P value, adjusted P value, and combined scores during the screening process to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the results.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted to identify biologically relevant pathways by comparing predefined gene sets between phenotypic sample groups. For this study, the GSE74602 dataset was analyzed using the Hallmark gene sets (“h.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt”) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) as the reference. A pre-ranked GSEA framework was implemented using a gene list ranked by differential expression statistics (log2FC) between the predefined groups. Normalized enrichment score (NES) was employed as the principal metric to evaluate the significance of enrichment, and statistical significance was assessed using nominal P values and false discovery rate (FDR) q values as provided by the GSEA algorithm. Tumor samples in GSE74602 were dichotomized by the median expression level of CENPF, and the resulting subgroup comparison was used to generate a ranked gene list for pre-ranked GSEA, aiming to capture tumor-intrinsic pathway differences associated with CENPF expression.

Western blotting

The cells were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (P0013C, Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P1045, Beyotime, China). Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (P0012, Beyotime, China). Protein samples were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, followed by transfer to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CENPF (1:1000, Affinity, DF2310, China), phosphorylated mechanistic target of rapamycin (p-mTOR) (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #5536, USA), mTOR (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #2983, USA), p-S6K (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #9205, USA), S6K (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #9202, USA), p-Akt (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #13038, USA), Akt (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #4691, USA), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #2118, USA). After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Following additional washes, the bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (MP102-01/02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) detection reagent and quantified using ImageJ software.

Reverse transcription–quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was isolated from CRC specimens and paired adjacent normal tissues using the RNeasy Mini Kit (RC112-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols. RNA concentration and purity were measured at 260/280 nm using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Model 80-3006-50, BioDrop, UK). qPCR was performed following the operational procedures of the SYBR Premix Ex Taq kit (R121-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), using glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal control. The relative expression levels of CENPF were normalized to GAPDH using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences were as follows:

CENPF forward: 5’-AGCACTGATCACCTGTTAGC-3’;

CENPF reverse: 5’-ACCCACATACAAACAGAGATTG-3’;

GAPDH forward: 5’-CGGAGTCAACGGATTTGGTCGTAT-3’;

GAPDH reverse: 5’-AGCCTTCTCCATGGTGGTGAAGAC-3’.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemical (IHC) investigations, the neoplastic tissue samples were fixed with 10% neutral-buffered formalin, followed by paraffin embedding. The processed specimens were then sectioned into continuous histological slices measuring 5 μm in thickness, which were prepared to meet the technical requirements for IHC assessment. The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CENPF antibody (1:200, CST, USA), followed by incubation with secondary antibodies and development using DAB chromogen. IHC staining was semi-quantitatively scored using the H-score method (n = 34).

Cell culture and transfection

Human CRC cell lines HCT116 and SW480 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (ATCC, VA, USA). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) DNA profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, C11995500BT, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO₂. Human CENPF-targeting small interfering RNA (siRNA; L-009774-00-0005) and negative control siRNA (CN-00100001-05) were designed and synthesized by GenePharma, China. Lipofectamine reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used for transfection following the manufacturer’s protocols. CENPF protein expression was measured by Western blotting to assess transfection efficiency. The sequences of the siRNAs used were as follows:

siCENPF forward: 5’- GACCCAGAAACUAGCUUAUTT-3’;

siCENPF reverse: 5’- AUAAGCUAGUUUCUGGGUCTT-3’;

siNC forward: 5’- UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’;

siNC reverse: 5’-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3’.

Cell proliferation assay

HCT116 and SW480 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells/well. The experiment included three distinct groups: the blank group, the negative control group, and the experimental group (CENPF-siRNA), with three replicate wells per group. When the cell density reached 70%–80%, 10 µL of cell counting kit 8 (CCK-8) reagent (C6005, NCM Biotech, China) was added to each well and incubated at 37℃ for 2 h in a 5% CO₂ incubator. The plates were gently shaken for 5 min, and the optical density at 450 nm (OD450) was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to obtain the 0-h baseline. Subsequent OD450 values were measured after 24, 48, and 72 h. The cell proliferation rate was calculated based on the readings from the microplate reader.

Wound healing assay

Straight horizontal lines were drawn on the back of six-well plates at 0.5- to 1-cm intervals to serve as reference markers, with at least five lines per well. The cells were seeded at a density of 6 × 10⁵ cells/well and incubated overnight to achieve 100% confluency. Linear scratches were made using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip after 24 h, perpendicular to the reference lines. The detached cells were removed by gently rinsing the wells three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and serum-free medium was added to each well. The images were captured 0, 6, 12, and 24 h after scratch. Wound closure was analyzed using ImageJ software by measuring the average intercellular distance or wound area.

Transwell invasion assay

Matrigel was diluted with serum-free medium in a ratio of 1:8 and thoroughly mixed. The mixture was then added to the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (24620035, Corning, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to allow gel solidification. HCT116 or SW480 cells (1.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL, 200 µL) were seeded into the upper chamber, and 800 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. The inserts were carefully washed with PBS and stained with 0.1% crystal violet after 24 h of incubation. Five randomly selected fields per insert were captured under a microscope, and the average number of invading cells per field was determined to assess cellular invasive capacity.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.3). Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.3.0 software. Different groups were compared using a two-tailed Student’s t-test (two-group comparisons) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA; comparisons among ≥ 3 groups). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified. A P value less than 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference. All tests were two-sided.

Results

Analysis of weighted gene co-expression networks and determination of necessary modules

The research process of this study is shown in Figure S1. We used the GSE74602 dataset and applied WGCNA to construct a gene co-expression network and identify significant gene modules. Using WGCNA, we systematically analyzed the co-expression patterns among genes and grouped genes with similar expression profiles into distinct modules, thereby identifying gene modules and key genes significantly associated with CRC. We performed sample clustering using the “Euclidean distance” and “average linkage method” (Fig. S2), and subsequently removed outlier samples to assign co-expression genes (Fig. 1A). Then, based on the independence and average connectivity of the scale, we chose 10 as the “soft-threshold power β” to ensure the creation of a biologically significant scale-free network (Fig. 1B). Forty-six modules were identified by hierarchical clustering analysis and dynamic TreeCut (Fig. 1C and D). According to the module–trait correlation analysis, the blue and turquoise modules displayed significant positive correlations with CRC prevalence. The blue module contained 2306 genes, and the turquoise module contained 2935 genes. The module–trait correlation analysis confirmed that both the blue and turquoise modules were significantly associated with CRC and were selected for further investigation (Fig. 1E).

Screening of DEGs in CRC

We obtained three mRNA expression microarray datasets (GSE74602, GSE113513, and GSE44076) from the GEO database to identify DEGs associated with CRC. Raw expression data were first normalized to ensure comparability across all samples. Differential expression analysis between tumor and adjacent normal tissues was conducted using the “limma” R package. In the GSE74602 dataset, 1630 DEGs were identified, including 905 upregulated and 725 downregulated genes in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues, which were visualized in a volcano plot (Fig. 2A). In the GSE113513 dataset, 1586 DEGs were detected, including 600 upregulated and 986 downregulated genes (Fig. 2B). In the GSE44076 dataset, 2349 DEGs were identified, with 1013 genes upregulated and 1336 genes downregulated (Fig. 2C). Besides the DEGs from these three datasets, genes from the blue and turquoise modules were also included in the Venn analysis, revealing 346 shared genes (Fig. 2D).

PPI network analysis and identification of hub genes

We imported the 346 DEGs into the STRING database to construct a PPI network. The confidence threshold for the PPI network was set at 0.7, and irrelevant nodes were hidden. The final PPI network consisted of 200 nodes and 1670 edges (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, the network was imported into Cytoscape software (v3.9.1) for further visualization and analysis (Fig. 3B). In Cytoscape, we identified two functional modules (Module 1 and Module 2) using the MCODE plugin. KEGG pathway analysis of the genes of these two modules revealed that the two modules were involved in distinct biological processes (BPs) (Figure S3A and S3B ), with no overlap in genes between the modules. Figure 3C shows the network structure of Module 1, whereas Fig. 3D shows the network structure of Module 2 (Fig. 3C and D). After integrating the genes from both modules, we identified 39 hub genes that may play a pivotal role in the development and progression of CRC.

We further investigated the expression patterns of hub genes in CRC and normal tissues using a heatmap (Fig. 4A). The heatmap results showed that these hub genes exhibited significant differential expression in CRC tissues. We performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to explore the potential biological functions of hub genes. The GO enrichment analysis showed that these hub genes were closely associated with several important BPs, including chromosome segregation, nuclear division, organelle fission, nuclear chromosome segregation, mitotic nuclear division, sister chromatid segregation, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) processing, and regulation of chromosomal organization (Fig. 4B). In terms of MFs, these hub genes were enriched in activities such as protein serine/threonine kinase activity, microtubule binding, snoRNA binding, microtubule motor activity, and cytoskeletal motor activity (Fig. 4C). From the perspective of CCs, these genes were primarily localized to subcellular structures such as chromosomal centromeric regions, condensed chromosome, chromosomal regions, spindles, and kinetochore (Fig. 4D). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed that these hub genes were mainly involved in several key signaling pathways and BPs, including cell cycle, ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes, oocyte meiosis, progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation, motor protein activity, and human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection (Fig. 4E). These results suggested that hub genes might play an important role in the occurrence and development of CRC by regulating key BPs such as cell cycle, chromosome segregation, and signal transduction, thereby providing a theoretical basis for further exploring their functional mechanism.

Identification and validation of candidate biomarkers

We employed two different machine learning algorithms to screen potentially key genes for CRC from 39 hub genes. First, the LASSO regression model was constructed, which identified nine genes associated with CRC (Fig. 5A and B). Subsequently, the RF model was employed, identifying 10 crucial genes related to CRC (Fig. 5C and D). CENPF and TOP2A were ultimately identified as potential candidate biomarkers closely associated with CRC through the joint screening of both algorithms (Fig. 6A). TOP2A is a well-established gene in CRC widely recognized for its role in promoting tumor cell proliferation [22, 23]. It is highly expressed in various cancers, including CRC, and serves as a specific marker for tumor cell proliferation [24–26]. TOP2A overexpression has a correlation with poor prognosis, increased tumor aggressiveness, and higher metastasis rates in patients with CRC [27–29]. The molecular mechanisms of TOP2A in CRC have been extensively elucidated, and its potential as a therapeutic target and biomarker has been confirmed in multiple studies [30, 31]. Given that the role of TOP2A in CRC has been extensively investigated whereas the role of CENPF remains relatively underexplored, CENPF was selected for in-depth investigation as a potentially novel biomarker and therapeutic target. We analyzed the GEPIA database to validate the differences in the expression of CENPF between tumor and normal tissues, revealing that CENPF expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 6B). Moreover, the expression levels of CENPF were significantly higher in tumor tissues than in normal tissues in the GSE74602, GSE113513 and GSE44076 datasets (Fig. 6C). Paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues were collected from patients with CRC for clinical validation. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction and Western blot analysis demonstrated that CENPF mRNA and protein levels were significantly upregulated in CRC tumor tissues (Fig. 6D and E). Furthermore, IHC staining confirmed that CENPF expression was higher in tumor tissues than in adjacent normal epithelium (Fig. 6F), suggesting potential clinical relevance.

Analysis of immune cell infiltration

We further explored the immune microenvironment of CRC by employing the “CIBERSORT” algorithm to investigate the differential infiltration of immune cells between the tumor and adjacent normal tissues. The results were visually presented using heatmaps (Fig. 7A) and boxplots (Fig. 7B). The analysis revealed that the tumor tissues displayed elevated infiltration of naive CD4 T cells, activated CD4 T cells, resting natural killer (NK) cells, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, activated mast cells, and neutrophils compared with the normal tissues, but the infiltration of memory B cells, plasma cells, CD8 T cells, activated NK cells, M2 macrophages, resting mast cells, and eosinophils decreased (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, the correlation analysis between the candidate biomarker CENPF and infiltrated immune cells demonstrated significant associations with multiple immune cell types (Fig. 7C).

CENPF as a diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic candidate marker in CRC

We evaluated the clinical application value of CENPF as a potential diagnostic marker of CRC by performing receiver operating characteristic curve analyses to assess its diagnostic potential. In the GSE74602 dataset, the area under the curve (AUC) of CENPF was > 0.9 (Fig. 8A). In the GSE113513 dataset, the AUC of CENPF was > 0.9 (Fig. 8B). In the GSE44076 dataset, the AUC of CENPF was > 0.8 (Fig. 8C). These findings indicated that CENPF consistently displayed high diagnostic performance across three datasets, supporting CENPF as a robust and consistent diagnostic biomarker for CRC. We performed a survival analysis through the GEPIA database to further explore the prognostic value of CENPF in CRC. We found that high expression levels of CENPF were associated with poorer overall survival in patients with CRC, suggesting a potential trend toward prognostic relevance (Fig. 8D). Moreover, we performed a drug screening analysis via the “Enrichr” database to explore potential therapeutic agents targeting CENPF. During the screening process, the P value, adjusted P value, and combined score were comprehensively considered to ensure the reliability of the results. The analysis revealed monobenzone, cryptolepine, trifluridine, malathion, and hycanthone as potential drugs targeting CENPF (Fig. S4). These results provide preliminary evidence that may serve as a candidate diagnostic marker and may have potential prognostic relevance, while also representing a putative therapeutic target in CRC. Further experimental and clinical validation will be required to establish its utility in diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy, including potential synergy with immunomodulatory strategies.

Silencing of CENPF suppressed proliferation, invasion, and migration in CRC Cells

Our previous bioinformatics analyses showed that CENPF was significantly overexpressed in CRC tissues and had a correlation with poor prognosis. We performed in vitro functional assays to further investigate the biological role of CENPF in CRC progression. HCT116 and SW480 cell lines were selected for these experiments. Endogenous CENPF was silenced by transfecting specific siRNAs into HCT116 and SW480 cells, and three siRNA groups were established. The silencing efficiency was evaluated by Western blot analysis. Based on the results, siCENPF#3 (hereafter referred to as si#3) with the most efficient knockdown was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 9A and C). CCK-8 assays revealed that CENPF knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation in both HCT116 and SW480 cells compared with the control cells (Fig. 9D and E). Transwell invasion assays demonstrated that CENPF knockdown markedly reduced the invasive capacity of both cell lines (Fig. 9F and H). Moreover, wound healing assays indicated that cell migration was significantly impaired in si#3-transfected cells, as evidenced by reduced wound closure rates (Fig. 9I and J). Collectively, these findings suggested that CENPF promoted the proliferation, invasion, and migration of CRC cells in vitro, highlighting its pivotal role in the malignant progression of CRC.

GSEA revealed a potential role of CENPF in tumor progression via the mTORC1 signaling pathway

GSEA was performed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the role of CENPF in CRC progression. Thirty CRC tissue samples from the GSE74602 dataset were classified into high-expression (n = 15) and low-expression (n = 15) groups based on the median expression level of CENPF. Subsequently, gene expression profiles were compared between the two groups. Enrichment pathways associated with CENPF expression were identified to explore its potential functional roles in CRC development. The results revealed that the high-CENPF-expression group was enriched in E2F targets, G2/M checkpoint regulation, MYC targets, and mTORC1 signaling pathways (Fig. 10A and B). This suggested that CENPF might promote CRC cell proliferation and growth by enhancing cell cycle progression. To assess robustness, we applied the same tumor-only median-split GSEA workflow to two independent cohorts (GSE44076 and GSE113513). Consistently, these cohorts showed similar enrichment patterns for cell cycle related signatures and mTORC1 signaling (Fig. S6A–D), supporting the stability of CENPF-associated pathway activation across datasets. Western blotting results showed that silencing CENPF markedly reduced the expression levels of p-mTOR, p-S6K, and p-Akt proteins in CRC cells (HCT116 and SW480), indicating that CENPF might regulate CRC progression through the mTORC1 signaling pathway (Fig. 10C). Rescue experiments using the mTORC1 activator MHY-1485 were performed on CENPF-silenced cells to further validate the aforementioned mechanism. The reduced levels of p-mTOR and p-S6K following siCENPF transfection were restored upon MHY-1485 treatment (Fig. 10D). These results indicated that CENPF promoted CRC progression at least in part via activation of the mTORC1 pathway.

Discussion

CRC remains a major global health challenge due to its high incidence and mortality, with most patients diagnosed in advanced stages because of the lack of early clinical symptoms [32, 33]. Despite advances in early screening and therapeutic strategies, effective biomarkers for precise diagnosis and prognosis are still limited [34]. In this study, we systematically discovered through integrated bioinformatics analyses and experimental validation that CENPF was a key gene associated with CRC progression. By integrating multi-cohort transcriptomic screening with gene prioritization and wet-lab validation, we provide a coherent evidence chain supporting CENPF as a CRC-relevant candidate. This highlighted CENPF as a novel focus, given that the involvement of TOP2A in CRC has been extensively explored. Our findings demonstrated that CENPF was significantly overexpressed in CRC tissues and promoted tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, highlighting its potential as both a diagnostic and a prognostic biomarker.

CENPF is a centromere-associated protein essential for chromosome segregation and mitotic progression [35, 36]. Aberrant expression of CENPF has been reported across multiple malignancies, including breast cancer [37, 38], lung adenocarcinoma [39], gastric cancer [40], hepatocellular carcinoma [35], and glioblastoma [41], often correlating with aggressive tumor phenotypes and poor prognosis. In breast cancer, high CENPF expression enhances cell cycle progression and contributes to chemotherapy resistance [42]. In lung adenocarcinoma and gastric cancer, CENPF overexpression is linked to Akt/ERK pathway activation, chromosomal instability, and increased mitotic activity [43–45]. Our CRC-focused results are consistent with these reports in that CENPF-high states are coupled with proliferative programs and enhanced malignant phenotypes, supporting a conserved oncogenic role across tumor types.

Mechanistically, CENPF may contribute to CRC progression through several interconnected pathways. Previous studies have implicated the role of CENPF in regulating Akt/ERK signaling, cell cycle checkpoints, and chromosomal instability, which collectively promote tumor aggressiveness [46]. In our datasets, CENPF-associated transcriptional programs were enriched for cell-cycle–related signatures ( E2F targets and G2/M checkpoint) and mTORC1 signaling, and this pattern remained directionally consistent when applying the same tumor-intrinsic median-split GSEA workflow across independent cohorts. In our experiments, the knockdown of CENPF inhibited CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. However, rescue experiments with the mTORC1 activator MHY-1485 restored p-mTOR and p-S6K levels suppressed by CENPF knockdown, indicating that CRC progression was at least partially reliant on the CENPF–mTORC1 pathway (Fig. 10D). These results support an axis in which CENPF contributes to proliferative capacity at least in part through mTORC1 pathway activity, providing functional support for the pathway-level signals observed in the transcriptomic analyses. Further investigation via co-immunoprecipitation assays is needed to determine whether CENPF directly interacts with key mTORC1 components, including Raptor and Rheb.

Despite its promising findings, this study had several limitations. First, CENPF was primarily identified through publicly available transcriptomic datasets. Thus, extensive prospective validation in large cohorts covering different CRC subtypes is required to substantiate the diagnostic and prognostic relevance of CENPF. Second, although in vitro assays demonstrated the oncogenic role of CENPF, further in vivo studies are essential to comprehensively elucidate its interactions with oncogenes, tumor suppressor pathways, mTORC1 signaling elements, and the immune microenvironment. Third, the potential influence of CENPF on chemoresistance and immunotherapy response in CRC remains to be elucidated, and exploring this can provide valuable insights for personalized treatment approaches.

Conclusions

In summary, our study systematically identified and validated the pivotal role of CENPF in CRC. Our findings revealed that CENPF was significantly overexpressed in CRC tissues and closely associated with enhanced tumor cell proliferation and disease progression-related phenotypes. Across multiple independent datasets, CENPF showed consistent diagnostic discrimination, supporting its potential as a candidate diagnostic marker. In addition, CENPF expression exhibited a potential trend toward prognostic relevance, although its prognostic value requires further validation in larger, well-annotated cohorts and multivariable models. Mechanistically, our results suggest that CENPF may promote CRC progression, at least in part, through activation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway. Collectively, these findings support CENPF as a promising candidate for further development as a diagnostic marker and potential therapeutic target in CRC.
