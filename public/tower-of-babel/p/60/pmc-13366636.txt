HMB supplementation combined with high-intensity functional exercise supports aerobic capacity but not strength in trained and untrained males: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Krzysztof Durkalec-Michalski, Michał Murawa, Michal Steffl, Tomasz Podgórski, Jakub Adamczewski, Małgorzata Marchelek-Myśliwiec, Tereza Jandova, Michal Vagner, Paulina M. Nowaczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Background β-hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate (HMB) supplementation may support aerobic capacity and isometric strength. Its utility has been studied mainly for 3 g HMB·day−1. Therefore, new and personalized HMB supplementation protocols should be verified. This parallel randomized placebo(PLA)-controlled triple-blind study was designed to evaluate the effects of liquid HMB free acid supplementation during customary training/lifestyle or combined with high-intensity functional training (HIFT) on aerobic fitness, physical capacity, and isometric strength indices following an incremental cycling test (ICT) in trained (TR) and untrained (UTR) males.MethodsFifty-three TR (29.1 ± 7.7 years; body mass (BM): 84.8 ± 10.0 kg; FFM: 71.2 ± 8.9 kg) and 37 UTR participants (32.3 ± 7.6 years; BM: 90.1 ± 16.5 kg; FFM: 68.4 ± 10.3 kg) completed two 3-weeks periods, including: 1) supplementation period (SUP)—ingesting 90 mg·kgFFM −1·day−1 of either HMB or PLA and performing usual training (TR) or lifestyle (UTR), and 2) HMB/PLA ingestion combined with the additional HIFT stimuli (2 units per week; SUP + EX). TR_HMB, TR_PLA, UTR_HMB, UTR_PLA groups were analyzed. Testing visits were performed at baseline (T BAS ), and after the SUP (T SUP ) and the SUP + EX (T SUP+EX ). The following evaluations were performed: BM and body composition, ICT, and maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) of the lower limb. A general linear model was conducted to examine the main effects and interaction of treatment (supplementation) (HMB/PLA), training status (TS; TR/UTR), and baseline values (BAS) over repeated measurements (T BAS , T SUP , T SUP+EX ). The following interactions were tested: visit × treatment; visit × TS; visit × treatment × TS; and visit × treatment × TS × BAS. One-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA (non-normal distribution) were conducted to examine differences in the degree of absolute changes (ΔT SUP ˗T BAS , ΔT SUP+EX ˗T SUP , ΔT SUP+EX ˗T BAS ) in the measured indices between studied subgroups.ResultsIncreases (T SUP+EX -T BAS ) in VO2MAX (p = 0.024), time to the anaerobic threshold (AT; p < 0.001), VO2AT (p = 0.001), power output at the AT (p < 0.001), heart rate at the AT (p < 0.001), time to the respiratory compensation point (RCP; p < 0.001), VO2RCP (p < 0.001), power output at the RCP (p < 0.001) were higher in TR_HMB vs. TR_PLA. Simultaneously, in UTR, greater increases in TimeRCP (p = 0.001) and PowerRCP (p = 0.002) occurred with HMB supplementation compared to PLA. MVIC indices did not change due to the treatment.ConclusionsSupplementation of high HMB dose combined with HIFT increases aerobic fitness and capacity, especially RCP and AT indices. HMB supplementation combined with HIFT evoked significant favorable changes in a greater number of the evaluated aerobic fitness and capacity indices in trained compared to untrained individuals. Thus, untrained individuals probably need greater exercise stimuli (>2 HIFT units per week) and a longer intervention period to obtained greater benefits. These findings suggest that incorporating liquid HMB supplementation at a dose of 90 mg·kgFFM −1·day−1 alongside HIFT is an effective strategy to enhance aerobic fitness and capacity in trained individuals, while further research including longer intervention periods and greater training stimuli is needed to determine the optimal supplementation protocol for untrained individuals.

Introduction

Beta-hydroxy-beta-methylbutyrate (HMB), a metabolite of the essential amino acid leucine, has the purported ability to modulate muscle protein turnover, thereby influencing muscle mass, strength, and physical performance [1–5]. HMB's mechanisms may include both anticatabolic effects, by inhibiting muscle protein breakdown (MPB), and anabolic pathways, through stimulating muscle protein synthesis (MPS) via mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) activation [3,6–10]. Among the putative mechanisms of HMB against MPB, there are insulin-independent processes such as suppression of the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, inhibition of myonuclear apoptosis via mitochondrial-associated caspase signaling, and suppression of the lysosomal autophagy pathway [3]. It was also previously observed that HMB calcium salt (HMB-Ca) supplementation combined with resistance training resulted in lower urine 3-methylhistidine concentration, which is byproduct of muscle contractile protein breakdown that is not metabolized or re-used for MPS [11].

However, the response to the HMB supplementation is not uniform across all individuals, as its effectiveness can vary significantly depending on the body's physiological status, fitness and physical capacity or performance level, training experience, and lifestyle [10,12–14].

In untrained individuals, HMB may improve strength and body composition. Early research indicated that 3 g·day−1 ofHMB increased fat-free mass (FFM) and strength [15–18]. HMB also alleviates muscle strength loss and improves joint flexibility after eccentric contractions [19,20]. Other earlier studies that examined a dose of 6 g HMB-Ca·day−1 by Kreider et al. [21] and Slater et al. [22] both reported no significant effects on muscle damage, strength, or body composition in resistance-trained individuals. Still, beyond active training, HMB benefits physically inactive or malnourished individuals, especially older adults [23–25] and those in catabolic states [26,27]. Studies indicate HMB improves various strength and muscle performance markers. HMB may be beneficial in preventing muscle loss during bed rest [28] or in conditions like sarcopenia [29], and often improves physical function [30] and gait speed [31], particularly when combined with exercise [5,23–25,30].

In addition, in trained athletes, HMB's benefits are more diverse and may be less pronounced [32]. Some studies suggest HMB's impact is diminished due to athletes bodies' adaptation to training stimuli [4,13,14]. However, with sufficient stimulus and longer durations, positive effects are observed [33]. In the study by Wilson et al. [33], 12 weeks of 3 g·day−1 of HMB free acid (HMB-FA) supplementation combined with a strictly controlled training (8-week-periodized resistance-training program, followed by 2-week overreaching cycle; and 2-week taper) led to greater increases in lean body mass and muscle thickness in resistance-trained males [33]. Similarly, in our previous study, we found 12-week HMB-Ca supplementation (3 g·day−1), to be effective in promoting advantageous changes in body composition and stimulating increases in aerobic and anaerobic capacity in highly trained combat sports athletes and rowers [34,35]. Available evidence suggests that HMB supports recovery by reducing muscle damage and improving readiness to train after high-volume efforts [9,36], and may also contribute to enhanced work capacity recovery in trained males following high-intensity exercise [37].

HMB supplementation has also been explored for its potential to improve aerobic performance, particularly in trained athletes. Studies on athletes supplementing 3 g·day−1 of HMB for 12 weeks indicated an advantageous effect on the increase in aerobic capacity in elite rowers [35] and combat sports competitors [34]. Additionally, trained athletes supplemented with HMB have also shown increased peak oxygen uptake (VO2PEAK) and improved time and load at ventilatory threshold (VT) [3,38]. Active college students in high-intensity interval training (HIIT) with HMB experienced greater improvements in maximal oxygen uptake (VO2MAX) and respiratory compensation point (RCP) compared to controls [13,39]. However, some investigations found no changes in aerobic capacity or lean body mass in trained subjects [13,14,38]. This variability suggests HMB ability to support aerobic fitness depends on training status, exercise modality, and intervention duration.

A significant consideration in evaluating the efficacy of HMB supplementation seems to be also directly related to the consistency and optimization of dosing protocols across studies. Research suggests a saturation point around 3 g·day−1 (or 38 mg·kg body mass (BM) −1·day−1), beyond which higher doses do not provide additional strength or FFM gains [9,15,25,40]. No unequivocal consensus on HMB dosing has been reached with respect to aerobic fitness and capacity outcomes. The timing of HMB administration is also inconsistent in past research, with most studies not aligning intake specifically with training sessions [33,34,37]. Differences in absorption kinetics between HMB forms (e.g. HMB-FA vs. calcium salt [HMB-Ca]) may also contribute to variable outcomes [6,41,42]. Beyond the specific dosage, the effectiveness of HMB supplementation appears to be also significantly limited by the lack of an adequate exercise stimulus. It seems to be less efficacious if the exercise intensity and volume are insufficient to cause muscle fatigue, damage, or meaningful hypertrophic or strength stimulation [9]. Similarly, the absence of significant adverse factors or conditions inducing muscle catabolism limits HMB's impact, as its most significant effects are seen when muscle proteolysis is elevated, i.e. during intense mechanical or catabolic stress [5,9,10,43]. Lastly, habitual exercise or training that does not stimulate adaptation may diminish HMB's benefits, as highly trained individuals already adapted to their routines may experience fewer improvements if the training program does not continuously challenge their physiological systems [13,14]. While direct studies combining HMB with high-intensity functional training (HIFT) are limited, insights from HIFT and resistance training are relevant. HIFT exercises demand significant strength, power, and endurance [44,45].

Referring to aerobic fitness and capacity indices, previous investigations on HMB supplementation are scarce [13,34,35,39], heterogeneous in methodology and studied populations, and to date, only the dose of 3 gHMB·day−1 has been evaluated in this regard. Thus, taking into account numerous inconsistencies related to the previous studies and the potentially beneficial effects of HMB supplementation in trained and untrained individuals, the aim of this parallel-group randomized placebo(PLA)-controlled triple-blind study was a multidirectional assessment whether an innovate, high, and personalized HMB dose (90 mg HMB·kgFFM −1·day−1), supplemented during customary training/lifestyle or combined with HIFT in trained (TR) and inactive/untrained (UTR) males within the same study protocol, influences aerobic fitness, physical capacity, and isometric strength indicators following incremental cycling test (ICT). It was hypothesized that HMB supplementation would result in improvements in aerobic performance, capacity, and fitness, and would preserve isometric strength of lower limb after strenuous exercises compared to PLA; furthermore, the improvements were expected to be amplified following the introduction of HIFT stimuli and more pronounced in UTR males.

Methods

Participants

As many as 120 participants were enrolled in the study protocol (Figure 1), and 90 participants completed the entire study protocol (Table 1), of which 53 were TR (29.1 ± 7.7 years; BM: 84.8 ± 10.0 kg; FFM: 71.2 ± 8.9 kg; percentage body fat (BFP): 15.9 ± 5.1%) and 37 UTR participants (32.3 ± 7.6 years; BM: 90.1 ± 16.5 kg; FFM: 68.4 ± 10.3 kg; BFP: 24.3 ± 7.7%). The detailed body mass and body composition data assessed during our study are provided and described in our earlier paper [46]. The eligibility criteria were: sex—males; age—20–45 years; an up-to-date medical clearance athlete's ability to practice sports; for TR individuals: regular physical activity (>250 min·week−1), training practice >10 years; for UTR individuals: lack of regular physical activity and lack of history of participation in competitive sports. The exclusion criteria were: current injury; any health-related contraindication; declared general feeling of being unwell; unwillingness to follow the study protocol; history of anabolic androgenic steroids or drugs use that may interfere with muscle mass control (e.g. corticosteroids), or affect physical performance; presence of infectious disease in the previous 4 weeks of the study. The TR participants could be categorized to the Tier 2 or 3 (trained/developmental or highly trained/national level) category according to McKay et al. [47].

The G*Power software (ver. 3.1.9.4, Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) was used to a priori calculation of the sample size required to obtain a power of approximately 80% (α = 0.05), and large effect size ƞp2 = 0.14. The required calculated total sample size suitable to detect a difference between three measurements and four groups for the analysis of variance with repeated measurements (RM ANOVA) within-between interaction was equal to 64 (16 per group). To account for possible dropouts (and an imbalanced dropout distribution across groups), 120 participants were initially enrolled into the study (30 per group).

Ethical approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Regional Medical Chamber in Szczecin (No. 12/2022 from October 6, 2022), the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Physical Education and Sport at the Charles University (No. 243/2021 from November 5, 2021), and the Bioethics Committee at the Poznan University of Medical Sciences (No. 733/19 from June 19, 2019), and registered prospectively at the ClinicalTrials.Gov (NCT05444959; September 10, 2021). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their participation in the study began. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and its further updates. The study complies with the CONSORT statement for randomized trials, as shown in the Supplementary file 1 and the TIDieR checklist is provided as Supplementary file 2.

Experimental design

The study was a randomized, triple-blind, PLA-controlled, parallel-group trial and consisted of two 3-week periods, of which 1) the first one was the only supplementation period (SUP)—participants were ingesting either HMB or PLA in an doses relative to FFM and were performing their usual training plan (TR) or lifestyle (UTR), and 2) during the second period HMB/PLA treatment was combined with the additional (in addition to their usual training plan/lifestyle) exercise stimuli in the form of 2 HIFT training units per week (SUP + EX) (Figure 1). Apart from a familiarization visit (FAM), testing visits were performed at baseline (T BAS ), and after completion of the SUP (T SUP ) and the SUP + EX (T SUP+EX ) periods. Furthermore, participants did not follow any specific nutritional strategies or make any changes to their usual diet. The habitual diet was controlled and monitored 48 h before T BAS , T SUP , and T SUP+EX visits via dietary recording using food diaries). The results of dietary recording are presented in the Supplementary file 3.1.

Due to the implemented blinding procedure, the study participants, the researchers, and results assessors were not aware of the supplementation received by each study participant. Randomization details were anonymized and revealed after protocol cessation. The randomization process was done by a staff member who did not directly participate in the investigations. FFM measured during T BAS visit was utilized as a stratification factor between HMB and PLA within the TR and UTR groups.

The main study was conducted in a few waves between September 2021 and June 2024 at the Department of Sports Dietetics (Poznan University of Physical Education, Poland) and the Sport Sciences–Biomedical Department (Charles University, Prague, Czech Republic). Due to COVID-19 pandemic and sanitary and epidemiological measures, the period of study conduction was longer than initially assumed (all research attempts made between March 2020 and August 2021 ended in failure due to lockdown or COVID-19 dropouts).

Supplementation

HMB was applied in the form of liquid β-hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate (Trec Nutrition Sp. z o.o., Gdynia, Poland). PLA was color-, taste-, and consistency-matched preparation. Both preparations were provided in glass bottles with a dropper tip. Based on a series of several replicates of measurements of the weight of a given number of HMB drops (30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, and 100 drops), the weight of a single HMB drop was established—it was 43mg HMB·drop−1 (Supplementary file 3.2). Taking into account individual FFM content and supplementation doses (90 mg·kgFFM −1·day−1), individual personalized total daily doses of HMB or PLA were calculated separately for each participant (based on FFM measured during the BAS visit) and expressed as number of drops per single portion. To specify, in the HMB_TR group the mean ± SD (range: min—max) number of HMB drops per day was 148 ± 15 (126–176), while in the HMB_UTR it was 141 ± 20 (111–182). The calculations of portions relative to FFM and labeling of the bottles with unique participant code numbers were carried out by a member of the research team who was not directly involved in conducting the study procedures. The total daily dose relative to FFM was split into 2 equal doses per day. Supplementation doses were established based on FFM measured with the BodPod® [46] .

The supplementation started at the day of T BAS visit, after completion of all procedures. On training days with one training session, participants were instructed to ingest the first portion of the HMB/PLA 45 min before the start of the training, and the second one right after completion of the training. On days with 2 training sessions, each of 2 doses were taken 45 min before each training. On the testing days (T SUP and T SUP+EX visits), participants ingested the supplemented preparation 45 min before the ICT commencement, and the second right after completion of the visit. While on non-training days, the first portion was taken right after waking up, and the second portion was taken before going to sleep. Each individual portion of the supplemented preparation was dissolved in 100–200 mL of plain water.

Participants received short text message reminders to take the supplement each day, regardless of whether it was a training day or non-training day. Compliance with HMB and PLA ingestion was monitored by researchers during and in-between testing visits, while participants were obligated to return the used bottles and submit a request if they have emptied the bottles between visits.

High-intensity functional exercise

During the SUP + EX period in addition to supplementation, participants were obligated to introduce 2 HIFT workouts to their usual training routine (TR group) or non-training routine (UTR group). The implemented HIFT training stimuli [48–50] consisted of the 5 following exercises: 1) throwing a medicine ball from a squat position, with repeated throws and catches (9 kg [TR] or 6 kg [UTR] ball) to a 3.0 m target; performing wide-stance deadlift high pulls (barbell with weights: 35 kg [TR] or 15–20 kg [UTR]); jumping onto a box (60 cm [TR] or 50 cm [UTR]); performing push presses (barbell with weights: 35 kg [TR] or 15–20 kg [UTR]), and rowing on an ergometer at the damper setting 7 (Concept2, Inc., Morrisville, VT, USA). Participants performed each exercise for 1 min, aiming to complete as many valid repetitions (or calories, in the case of rowing) as possible. Transitions between exercises within the same round were immediate and without rest. There were three rounds within each workout, with 1 min break between rounds. Total time of the full HIFT training unit was 17 min. All HIFT workouts were performed under the supervision of experienced researchers at the Department of Sports Dietetics or the Sport Sciences–Biomedical Department. In participants assigned to the UTR group, exercise intensity and complexity were individually adapted to match each participant's physical capabilities. Modifications involved, for instance, adjusting box height, allowing step-ups instead of box jumping, and scaling resistance loads including barbells, plates, and medicine balls as described above. Heart rate was continuously monitored during supervised HIFT sessions using the Polar Team Pro system (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland). The results are provided in the Supplementary file 3.3.

Body height, body mass, and composition

Body height and mass, body composition, and total body water (TBW) content were measured at the beginning of each testing visit. Height was measured using a professional medical scale with a stadiometer (WPT 60/150 OW, RADWAG, Radom, Poland) at an accuracy of 0.1 cm. Body mass, FFM, fat mass (FM), and body fat percentage (BFP) were assessed using the air displacement plethysmography analyzer—BodPod® (Cosmed, Pavona RM, Italy). Body volume was assessed following a standardized protocol, predicted values of thoracic gas volume were used. The BodPod® device was calibrated prior to each testing using a 50-L cylinder in accordance with manufacturer guidelines. Body mass was measured using a calibrated digital BodPod® scale immediately before the assessment. Participants wore minimal, tight-fitting clothing (swimsuit and swim cap) to minimize air displacement artifacts. Each single measurement was performed twice and, if the difference between measurements exceeded 150 mL, a third measurement was taken. BFP was calculated using the Siri equation [51]. The reproducibility of BodPod® measurements in our laboratory were previously verified and the results are published elsewhere [48,52]. TBW was assessed via electric bioimpedance method (BIA) using BIA 101 BIVA (Akern S.r.l., Pisa, Italy) as described previously [46] and with accordance with the best practices listed by Kyle et al. [53]. To specify, BIA evaluations were performed using compatible and dedicated Biatrodes™ electrodes (Akern S.r.l., Pisa, Italy), which are characterized by high conductivity, low polarization, and low intrinsic impedance (<30 Ohm); as well as by excellent adhesive and elastic properties of the gel, which assures strong contact to skin even in patients with hypertrichosis or dry skin. Evaluations were performed in the supine position, with arms separated from trunk by about 30° and legs separated by about 45°. Participants were wearing only underwear, and all jewelry/watches/glasses/were removed before evaluations. The external surfaces of the right hand and foot were cleaned with a skin disinfectant and left to dry. Biatrodes™ (to per each limb) were then applied so that the distance between the electrodes on each limb was 5 cm. The quality of each assessment was verified by the internal software of the BIA 101 BIVA (Akern S.r.l., Pisa, Italy) analyzer. Moreover, at the beginning of each testing session the analyzer was calibrated against the known ohmmeter. According to the study protocol, participants consumed a standardized meal (containing 2 gCHO·kgBM −1, 30 g of protein, and at least 7 mLwater·kgBM −1[54–56]) and ingested the prescribed supplement 45 min before the start of test exercises [46]. Thus, BIA assessments were not taken at fasting state; however, they were taken in a standardized manner at each visit for all participants. Moreover, TBW in conjunction with urine specific gravity (USG; URYXXON® Relax Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany), were assessed to verify the hydration status of participants prior to each measurement session. Only individuals demonstrating proper hydration (USG < 1.020) were eligible to proceed with further testing procedures during a given visit. TBW was not a primary or secondary outcome of the study, but served solely as a hydration control measure.

Exercise test

As a part of testing participants performed exercise tests in the form of the ICT to exhaustion (visits T BAS , T SUP , T SUP+EX ). ICTs were performed exclusively under standardized conditions (temp. 20–22 °C and humidity 60–70%) in the morning (from 8.00 to 12.00 a.m.). Furthermore, the volunteers were asked to wear proper workout clothing and shoes. All participants were familiar with the tests from T FAM . The ICTs were performed on a Ergoline E100P-K cycloergometer (Cosmed, Pavona RM, Italy) coupled to the K5 ergospirometric system (Cosmed, Pavona RM, Italy) allowing for breath-by-breath cardiorespiratory and metabolic markers measurements. Data were analyzed using the Omnia Cardiopulmonary Diagnostics Suite Software (ver. 1.6.10.0, Cosmed, Pavona RM, Italy). All ICTs were supervised by the experienced researchers.

After individual adjustment of the saddle and ergospirometry equipment, the participant sat on the cycloergometer at rest for approximately 5 min. Then the main exercise analyzes started. The ICT testing was divided into four phases: (a) REST phase, where resting analyzes were performed (for 1 min—up to the first min of the ICT); (b) WARM-UP phase—cycling with a load of 50 W and a cadence of 65 rpm (for 2 min—until the first to third min of the ICT); (c) EXERCISE phase—cycling from 50 W with incremental workload of 5 W every 12 s and; (d) RECOVERY phase—measurement conducted for 2 min after exhaustion (EXH). During the EXERCISE phase cadence revolution on the cycloergometer was strictly standardized to 60–65 rpm (workload: from 50 to 250 W), 70–75 rpm (workload: from 250 to 355 W) and 80–85 rpm (workload ≥ 355 W). The EXERCISE phase was conducted until the participant's complete EXH—in this respect, at the final stages of the ICT active verbal doping was provided by at least two members of the research team. The maximal oxygen uptake (VO2MAX) and corresponding maximal (MAX) exercise indices were considered to be reached when three of the following criteria were met: (a) a plateau in VO2 despite an increase in workload and minute ventilation; (b) respiratory exchange ratio ≥ 1.10; (c) heart rate (HR) ≥ 95% of maximum [57].

The anaerobic threshold (AT) was detected based on V-slope method (with simultaneous assessment of VE vs. VO2 [VE/VO2] and CO2 [VE/VCO2] and of end-tidal pressure of oxygen and end-tidal pressure of CO2). The RCP was identified when the VE/VCO2 ratio increased, and it was confirmed by a simultaneous reduction of end-tidal pressure of CO2. Finally, during ICT the following indices were evaluated: time to EXH (TimeEXH), volume of oxygen uptake (VO2), VO2MAX, maximal HR (HRMAX), and power (PowerMAX), as well as time to AT (TimeAT), and RCP (TimeRCP) and corresponding AT/RCP values: VO2AT/VO2RCP, PowerAT/PowerRCP, and HRAT/HRRCP. Results of each ICT for each participant were analyzed independently by two experienced investigators and then reviewed jointly.

Isometric strength

Maximal voluntary isometric contractions (MVICs) of the quadriceps femoris were assessed during knee extension of the dominant leg using an isokinetic dynamometer Biodex System 3 (Biomedical Systems, Newark, CA, USA). To minimize the contribution of other muscle groups acting on the knee joint, participants' torso, pelvis, and thighs were securely stabilized with straps affixed to the dynamometer seat. The hip and knee joint angles were set at 90° and 60°, respectively, to optimize torque production [58,59]. Participants performed three MVIC trials, each lasting up to 5 s, with a 1-min rest between attempts. The highest torque value recorded across the three trials was considered the peak torque (PT). In addition to peak torque, both average peak torque (APT) and PT and APT were normalized to body mass and included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

All variables were checked for a normal distribution with the Shapiro–Wilk test. The results are presented as the mean ± 1 standard deviation (SD) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Baseline comparisons between TR and UTR were performed using test t for independent variables. Taking into account the robustness of the F-test in terms of Type 1 error, if the normality assumptions based on the Shapiro–Wilk test were violated, the kurtosis and skewness variables were also evaluated [60]. A general linear model with Huynh–Feldt correction was conducted to examine the main effects and interaction of treatment (supplementation) (HMB/PLA), TS (TR/UTR), and baseline values (T BAS ) over repeated measurements (T BAS , T SUP , T SUP+EX ). Thus, the analysis tested for within-subject effects, between-subject effects, and their interactions (visit × treatment; visit × TS; visit × treatment × TS; and visit × treatment × TS × BAS). One-way ANOVA with Huynh–Feldt correction (normal distribution of data; post hoc comparisons were performed by Fisher's LSD test) or Kruskal‒Wallis ANOVA (non-normal distribution) were conducted to examine differences in the degree of absolute changes (ΔT SUP ˗T BAS , ΔT SUP+EX ˗T SUP , ΔT SUP+EX ˗T BAS ) in the measured indices between studied subgroups (TR_HMB, TR_PLA, UTR_HMB, UTR_PLA). Effect sizes were expressed as np2 or nH2 (interpretation: <0.010 no effect, from 0.010 to 0.059 small effect, from 0.060 to 0.139 moderate effect and ≥0.140 large effect). Assessments of the relationships between T BAS and ΔT SUP ˗T BAS or T BAS and ΔT SUP+EX ˗T BAS were performed using Pearson correlation analysis (normal distribution) or Spearman's rank correlation analysis (non-normal distribution). Only participants who completed full study protocol and with no missing data were considered in the statistical analysis. The data were analyzed by using the STATISTICA software ver. 13.3 (StatSoft Inc., USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Aerobic performance—maximal values

For TimeEXH there were significant main effects of visit, treatment, and BAS, and significant visit × treatment × TS (p = 0.039; np2 = 0.038; Table 2) and visit × treatment × TS × BAS (p = 0.029; np2 = 0.041) interactions. For VO2MAX, there were significant main effects of visit and BAS (in mL·min−1 and mL·kg−1·min−1), but only significant visit × TS interaction was observed (in mL·min−1·kg−1; p = 0.046; np2 = 0.036). For PowerMAX (in W) there were significant main effects of visit, treatment and BAS, and significant visit × treatment (p = 0.038; np2 = 0.038), visit × treatment × TS (p = 0.009; np2 = 0.054), and visit × treatment × TS × BAS (p = 0.007; np2 = 0.058) interactions. Discussion

The main findings of this randomized controlled triple-blind study are that the newly designed HMB supplementation protocol, utilizing and innovate individualized to FFM high HMB dose (90 mg HMB·kg FFM·day−1) combined with high-intensity functional exercise stimuli implemented in trained and untrained males significantly improved indices of aerobic capacity and fitness, and preserved isometric strength of lower limb measured after strenuous exercise. Throughout the study protocol significant improvements in TimeEXH, TimeAT, TimeRCP, and all other measured maximal, threshold, and respiratory compensation point values (volume of oxygen uptake, power output, heart rate) of aerobic capacity and fitness were observed. The implemented treatment (supplementation and exercise stimuli) significantly contributed to changes in TimeEXH, PowerMAX, TimeAT, PowerAT, HRAT, TimeRCP, VO2RCP, PowerRCP, and HRRCP. Training status alone affected changes in TimeAT, TimeRCP, and PowerRCP. The significant interactions of the implemented treatment and training status were observed for TimeEXH and PowerMAX. The most pronounced improvements in the indices of aerobic capacity and fitness were evoked in the TR_HMB group, while the least were in the TR_PLA group. Interestingly, baseline values of particular indices seemed to substantially affect subsequent changes that occurred during the implemented supplementation and HIFT intervention. There were numerous significant negative correlations between T BAS values and the subsequent changes in aerobic fitness and capacity indices. The greatest number of such relationships were observed in the TR_HMB. The calculation of EMM gave a valuable insight into the results interpretation, indicating the beneficial effect of HMB alone and combined with HIFT on aerobic performance and capacity—especially in trained individuals.

In this study, as high a dose as 90 mg HMB·kg FFM −1·day−1 was implemented. Based on individual FFM of study participants, the actual implemented HMB doses ranged from 4.8 to 7.8 gHMB·day−1. Therefore, the daily doses were higher comparing to those implemented in the previous studies evaluating the impact of HMB on aerobic capacity. In the randomized parallel group PLA-controlled study by Lamboley et al [13]. 5-week HMB supplementation (3 gHMB-Ca·day−1; ~46.7 mgHMB-Ca·kgBM 1·day−1) combined with interval training (3 training units·week−1) in college age males and females (mean baseline VO2MAX 51.74 and 50.63 mL·kg−1·min−1 in the PLA and HMB groups, respectively) resulted in 15.5% increase in VO2MAX (compared to 8.4% increase in the PLA treatment), and 13.4% increase in VO2RCP (compared to 8.6% increase in the PLA treatment). In our study, changes in the corresponding indices were: 4.7% increases in the TR_HMB (vs. 0.8% increase in the TR_PLA) and 7.4% increase in the UTR_HMB (vs. 6.1% increase in the UTR_PLA) for VO2MAX (in mL·min−1·kg−1); and 8.7% increases in the TR_HMB (vs. 0.4% decrease in the TR_PLA) and 10.7% increase in the UTR_HMB (vs. 6.5% increase in the UTR_PLA) for VO2RCP (in mL·kg−1·min−1). Interestingly, in the study by Lamboley et al. [13] there was a marginal difference in changes in VO2VT between HMB (11.08% increase) and PLA (9.03% increase), while in our study, the TR_HMB experienced 13.6% increase (vs. 0.28% decrease in the TR_PLA) and the UTR_HMB 8.5% increase (vs. 3.6% increase in the UTR_PLA). Compared to the protocol implemented in our study, the investigation by Lamboley et al. [13]. HMB dose was ~1.5–2.6 times lower, still the exercise stimuli seemed to be higher (in total 15 training sessions). Both protocols evoked increases in selected (various) indices of aerobic capacity, still the major contributors of the improvements might be different in those 2 protocols—in the study by Lamboley et al. [13], the effect of exercise stimulus on final outcomes might have been dominant. Robinson et al [39]. in their randomized parallel group study in healthy young males and females (VO2PEAK: 39.3 ± 5.0 mL·kg−1·min−1), found that 4 weeks of HMB supplementation (3 gHMB-FA·day−1; ~41.4 mgHMB-FA·kgBM −1·day−1) combined with 3 HIIT sessions per week (in total 12 HIIT sessions during the entire study protocol) resulted in greater improvements in VO2PEAK, VO2VT, and PVTcompared to HIIT + PLA or to control (no differences between HIIT + PLA vs. control). Moreover, in the study by Robinson et al. [39], HIIT + HMB and HIIT + PLA resulted in improvements TimeEXH, peak power, VO2RCP, and PowerRCP compared to control. Thus, in some of the measured indices of aerobic capacity the beneficial effect of HIIT was additionally amplified by HMB supplementation. The baseline aerobic capacity in participants studied by Robinson et al [39]. seems to be comparable to aerobic capacity level of UTR individuals in our study (based on VO2MAX). Finally, in our previous randomized crossover placebo-controlled studies in highly-trained combat sports athletes and elite rowers [34,35,38] we found a substantial effects of 12-week HMB supplementation during usual; however, professional elite training routine on selected indices of aerobic capacity. Supplementation with 3 gHMB-Ca·day−1 (~37.0 mgHMB-Ca·kgBM −1·day−1) in highly trained elite combat sports competitors (VO2MAX: 57.3–58.2 mL·kg−1·min−1) resulted in significant improvements in TimeEXH (~4.7% increase), PowerMAX (in W; ~4.6% increase), HRMAX (~1.1% increase), TimeAT (~11.7% increase), PowerAT (in W [~7.3% increase] and W·kg−1 [~7.4% increase]), and HRAT (~4.4% increase), while PLA supplementation was related to a decrease in TimeAT (~4.6% decrease). Simultaneously, post-supplementation TimeAT was higher after HMB compared to PLA ingestion. In elite rowers (VO2MAX: 67.3–68.8 mL·kg−1min−1) supplementation with 3 gHMB-Ca·day−1 (~34.7 mgHMB-Ca·kgBM −1·day−1) resulted in significant improvements in VO2MAX (in mL·kg−1·min−1; ~4.0% increase), TimeEXH (~8.0% increase), PowerMAX (in W·kg−1; ~9.1% increase); TimeAT (~9.6% increase); PowerAT (in W [~11.2% increase] and in W·kg−1 [~13.0% increase]), and HRAT (~5.7% increase). No significant pre vs. post differences was observed as a result of PLA supplementation. Based on the results of this study and the mentioned results of previous studies, both the duration of supplementation and the type of exercise stimuli (volume and intensity) appear to be key factors regarding the efficacy of HMB ingestion. With shorter supplementation periods and relatively low supplementation doses, the exercise stimulus itself may play a crucial role, whereas with habitual—but high training loads, the duration of supplementation seems to become more important. Interestingly, when the supplementation period is sufficiently long, even relatively low doses of HMB (comparing to the dose implemented in the current study) were associated with favorable aerobic improvements, including in elite athletes with very high training status and very high usual training loads, like those studied by Durkalec-Michalski et al. [34,35]. Based on the current scientific data, HMB supplementation may efficiently increase aerobic fitness and performance in male athletes, however supplementation protocols and strategies, including combinations of dose, duration and additional exercise stimuli must be individually tailored and adjusted to individual athletes training goals and training periodization, particularly to the time window available to achieve the intended training effect.

In the current study, we observed that the degree of changes in selected indices of aerobic capacity across particular period of the implemented intervention (ΔT SUP ˗T BAS , ΔT SUP+EX ˗T SUP , ΔT SUP+EX ˗T BAS ) were the most spectacular in the TR_HMB group. There were significant differences between the TR_HMB vs. TR_PLA in changes in TimeAT, PowerAT TimeRCP, VO2RCP, and PRCP in between ΔT SUP ˗T BAS ; in TimeEXH, TimeAT, VO2AT, PowerAT, TimeRCP, and PowerRCP in between ΔT SUP+EX ˗T SUP ; and in VO2MAX, TimeAT, VO2AT, PowerAT, HRAT, TimeRCP, VO2RCP, PowerRCP, and HRRCP in between ΔT SUP+EX ˗T BAS . While regarding comparisons between UTR_HMB vs. UTR_PLA, significant differences occurred for changes in TimeRCP in between ΔT SUP ˗T BAS , and TRCP and PRCP in between ΔT SUP+EX ˗T BAS . Based on this analysis, it could be stated that within the implemented here interventional protocol, TR individuals benefited more from HMB supplementation compared to UTR individuals. While the total daily doses of HMB were comparable in TR (6.3 ± 0.7 gHMB·day−1) and UTR (6.1 ± 0.9 gHMB·day−1) individuals. The most readily responsive to the HMB supplementation turned out to be RCP indices—differences in their changes between HMB vs. PLA were seen in both TR and UTR individuals, and AT indices—especially in TR individuals. It needs to be underline that the UTR participants in the current study were individuals who did not undertake voluntary purposeful physical exercise never in their lives (apart from “every-day life” activity). The HIFT stimuli introduced during SUP + EX period, must had been adjusted to their individual “maximal” (and sometimes unknown) physical capabilities. Thus, due to unawareness of own capabilities, some of UTR participants might have not perform their then current best during HIFT workouts, and the actual exercise stimuli might had been insufficient to evoke and/or amplify ergogenic potential of HMB.

In our study we observed a negative relationships between baseline (T BAS ) values of maximal, AT and RCP capacity and aerobic fitness indices and their changes between the protocol periods (T BAS vs. ΔT SUP -T BAS and T BAS vs. ΔT SUP+EX -T BAS )—the lower were the baseline values, the higher were the increases under the implemented intervention. Interestingly, the highest number of such associations were observed in the TR_HMB group. The observation confirms, that trained individuals may benefit from HMB supplementation, and that exercise stimuli must be strong enough to amplify ergogenic potential of HMB.

The placebo effect might have occurred during the test exercises (ICT)—participants ingested their prescribed preparations 45 min before the ICT at the visits T SUP and T SUP+EX . HMB and PLA preparations were taste-matched (bitter-sour taste). A bitter taste (i.e. during mouth rising) in the context of sports supplementation is often an inherent signal of the presence of biologically active compounds with potential ergogenic effects [61,62]. Thus, some improvements in the PLA groups even after the first period of interventions—supplementation only, were anticipated (especially in TR individuals who were ingesting prescribed supplements before their usual trainings during). The phenomenon of the repeated acute ergogenic potential related to the HMBs' sour and bitter taste is not a case in the supplements is provided in the form capsules. Thus, providing HMB in the liquid form dissolved in water may theoretically be beneficial and could be perceived as a strength of the current protocol; however, no direct comparisons between acute ergogenicity of various HMB forms were performed within the current study, The considerations are rather hypothetical, and may serve as directions for future studies.

Moreover in our study, we found no effect of HMB supplementation and HIFT stimuli on the lower limb isometric strength indices. This is in contrary to the study by Gallagher et al. [15], where 8-week of HMB supplementation (76 mgHMB·kgBM −1·day−1) combined with resistance training (3 workouts·week−1) in untrained college-age males resulted in significantly greater increase in concentric peak torque at 2.1 and eccentric peak torque at 23.15, and 24.2 rad·s−1 compared to the group supplemented with 38 mgHMB·kgBM −1·day−1 or no supplementation. Despite a seeming lack of coherence between the results obtained by Gallagher et al. [15] and the results of our study, apart from differences in the interventional protocol (duration, supplementation dose, type of exercise stimuli), the fact that in the current study MVIC monitoring was performed after the ICT must be mentioned. In this context, the implemented intervention might have prevent from decreases in performance during MVIC test. Theoretically, if study participants performed better during the ICT at the visits T SUP and T SUP+EX , ICT-induced muscle fatigue might have been more severe, and consequently, performance during MVIC evaluation would be worsen. Still, no such phenomena (decrease in MVIC) was observed—thus the hypothetical preventive effect of the implemented treatment might have occurred.

A key strength of the present study lies in the individualized and innovative supplementation protocol. Although only a limited number of previous studies have adopted such an approach, the International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand on HMB supplementation [3] explicitly indicate HMB dosing based on body mass—38 mg HMB∙kg BM −1∙day−1 (and in combination with exercise training) to potentially improve body composition, and 76 mg HMB∙kg BM −1∙day−1 to improve isometric strength. This methodological consideration enhances the physiological relevance and precision of the intervention. The approach implemented in our study is in line with the newest recommendations. Another notable strength is the inclusion of both TR and UTR individuals within the same experimental protocol, allowing for broader applicability of the findings across populations with different training statuses. The application of the liquid free acid form of HMB allowed for individualization of the administered doses relative to FFM, rather than the typical 3 g·day−1 recommendation.

Despite its strengths, the study also presents several limitations. The limitation of the study is, that the results of the current study refers solely to young and middle-age TR and UTR males, and cannot be extrapolated to other population groups, e.g. females or older adults. Thus, future studies must include other population groups, which can benefit from HMB supplementation in the context of aerobic capacity and isometric strength. Moreover, in this study we used a commercially available product, in exactly the form purchased by the consumer. As such, its purity was not independently assayed to confirm that it consisted entirely of HMB. In addition, to establish the administered dose, we measured the mass delivered per drop, which differed from the mass-to-volume conversion per drop provided by the manufacturer. This discrepancy should be taken into account by consumers when determining individual doses, as relying solely on the manufacturer's stated conversion may lead to inaccurate dosing. Another limitation is that the overall supplementation period (6 weeks) and the relatively small number of training sessions during the SUP + EX phase (6 sessions in total) may not have been sufficient to elicit the full potential effects of HMB on aerobic capacity and fitness, and isometric strength—especially in UTR participants. This suggests that HMB supplementation should be integrated into longer training cycles or paired with even more intensive, high-stress training blocks to maximize its biological impact. A notable limitation of the present study is also the use of a single individualized dose of HMB (90 mgHMB·kgFFM −1·day−1), which precludes conclusions regarding dose-response relationships. Future studies should consider incorporating additional dose conditions, such as 45 mgHMB·kgFFM −1·day−1, to determine whether a lower individualized dose yields comparable effects. This was not feasible in the current design due to the complexity introduced by two other variables—training status and supplementation combined with HIFT stimuli. Moreover, there was a relatively high dropout rate, particularly among UTR participants (23 participants dropped out). This is not unexpected, UTR individuals may perceive lower personal benefit from participating in experimental protocols (especially in combination with strenuous exercises) that focus on physical capacity, compared to TR individuals (7 participants dropped out) who often associate such changes with improved sport results. In the analysis by Collins et al. [63], it was observed that participants who dropped out from physical activity programs were significantly less fit at baseline than those who completed the intervention, and approximately two-thirds withdrew prior to reaching the prescribed exercise volume and intensity. Nevertheless, despite the dropout, the final sample size—including all experimental subgroups—exceeded the minimum sample size (n = 16 per group) calculated a priori in the power analysis, ensuring sufficient statistical power for the primary comparisons.

Conclusions

In conclusion, novel dosing protocol of free liquid HMB based on FFM (90 mg·kgFFM −1·day−1) in combination with HIFT stimuli resulted in significant increases in the indices of aerobic fitness and aerobic capacity, particularly in trained individuals. Respiratory compensation point and anaerobic threshold indices tended to more readily improve after the implemented treatment comparing to maximal indices. Among trained individuals, those with lower baseline values of aerobic fitness and capacity seemed to experience greater improvements due to the intervention. Finally, isometric strength of lower limb measured after strenuous exercise was preserved due the implemented interventions.
