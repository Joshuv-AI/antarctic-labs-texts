Pseudomonas mendocina Isolated from Anopheles Midguts has a Greater Potential to Build Thick Biofilms than Serratia marcescens
Authors: Margarida Marques, Vânia Pobre, Sofia Santos Costa, Sandra N. Pinto, Henrique Silveira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Malaria remains the most prevalent vector-borne disease. Manipulating the Anopheles midgut microbiota offers a promising malaria control strategy. Biofilm-forming Pseudomonas spp. may reduce Plasmodium infection in Anopheles mosquitoes because bacterial biofilms at the midgut epithelium may restrict the ookinete movement; therefore, it has a potential application as a malaria transmission blocking tool. Thus, the present study focuses on the characterization of the biofilm-forming potential of bacteria isolated from Anopheles midguts, with an emphasis on Pseudomonas and Serratia species. Bacteria were isolated, identified via 16S rDNA sequencing, and analyzed for antibiotic susceptibility and biofilm production. Isolates of interest were subjected to whole genome sequencing, and we focused our study on two distinct isolates (Serratia marcescens As_A1-C1 and Pseudomonas mendocina Ag_C1). The capacity of the isolates to form biofilms was evaluated by microbiological and biophysical tools, including quantification of biofilm maximum height using confocal microscopy. The various techniques allowed us to observe that S. marcescens As_A1-C1 formed poorly structured biofilms. In contrast, P. mendocina Ag_C1 produced highly thick and heterogeneous biofilms, which were more pronounced on complex culture media mimicking midgut conditions. Also, whole genome sequencing of S. marcescens and P. mendocina isolates revealed the carriage of relevant biofilm-related genes. Overall, our results highlight that the isolated P. mendocina has the potential to act as a tool for the development of new malaria control strategies.

Introduction

Malaria is an endemic public health problem in tropical and subtropical areas, being the most prevalent vector-borne disease, with the highest mortality impact in the world. It is caused by infection with the parasite Plasmodium and transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes. Currently, the main strategies to control this disease are the use of insecticides to reduce the mosquito population and the use of antimalarial drugs to treat the infection already established in the vertebrate host. However, as with other dynamic systems, resistance to these compounds has increased significantly. ,

Over the past few decades, the study and development of strategies based on the manipulation of the vector’s microbiota has emerged as a novel way of reducing the infection in the vector, which is directly reflected in a reduction of transmission of the parasite. −

Anopheles mosquitoes have symbiont microorganisms associated with several organs, such as midgut, sexual organs, and salivary glands, which have an influence on the physiological processes of the mosquito. Several bacterial species of the Anopheles microbiota have already been described to exhibit anti-Plasmodium effect, ,− which can be explored as a malaria control strategy, by genetically manipulating the bacteria to enhance this effect. This strategy is known as paratransgenesis.

Several paratransgenesis strategies have already been attempted in experimental studies, namely, one using an Escherichia coli strain modified to produce anti-Plasmodium molecules on its surface. However, this strategy proved inefficient as the bacteria could not efficiently colonize the mosquito midgut. Wang et al. (2017) described another application of this strategy using a strain of Serratia marcescens isolated from the ovaries of An. stephensi from a laboratory colony, which had been genetically modified to express anti-Plasmodium effector genes. This study showed the ability of this bacterium to colonize the midgut and to heterologously produce several anti-Plasmodium effector molecules with various mechanisms of action, which resulted in efficiently inhibiting the development of Plasmodium falciparum and, at the same time, did not result in a negative impact on mosquito fitness.

Bacterial communities in the midgut microbiota of mosquitoes are thought to live and grow in a biofilm mode; however, this hypothesis has not yet been studied closely enough. − So, biofilm production by the Anopheles midgut microbiota can be explored for developing paratransgenesis strategies. Growth in biofilm-like structures can facilitate colonization by the bacteria and, at the same time, act as a physicochemical barrier to the invasion of ookinetes through the epithelium of the midgut. The invasion of the mosquito midgut epithelium by the parasite typically occurs between 18 and 24 h after the blood meal. This time window is considered the most advantageous for interfering with the parasite’s life cycle, as it precedes the massive multiplication that occurs within the oocysts. As such, the genetic manipulation of bacteria to enhance their in vivo biofilm production within the mosquito may represent a potential strategy to reduce the success of Plasmodium development in the Anopheles midgut.

Species of the genus Pseudomonas are often producers of biofilms and are among the most abundant genera in the Anopheles midgut microbiota. ,,, Here, we evaluated the natural capacity for biofilm formation by bacteria isolated from the midgut of Anopheles species from laboratory colonies, with particular focus on Pseudomonas and Serratia species. We hypothesize that strong biofilm producers will colonize and persist more efficiently in the mosquito gut, thereby reducing malaria transmission. For that, our goal was to evaluate the Pseudomonas spp. and Serratia spp. (which are two relevant species in this context) biofilm formation capacities under varying environmental conditions and to select high biofilm-forming strains for future paratransgenesis applications/studies.

Results and Discussion

Bacteria Isolated from Anopheleś Midgut

The bacterial isolation assays covered the different Anopheles species maintained in laboratory colonies at Instituto de Higiene e Medicina Tropical (IHMT, Lisbon, Portugal): Anopheles stephensi, Anopheles atroparvus, Anopheles gambiae, and Anopheles coluzzii. The Anopheles species associated with malaria transmission have a wide geographical distribution, and the species covered in these isolation assays include African species (An. gambiae and An. coluzzii), , Asian species (An. stephensi), , and European species (An. atroparvus).

In total, eight different bacterial genera were isolated; the majority were Gram-negative and oxidase-negative (Table S1), which is in line with the available literature. ,, The bacterial species isolated are frequently identified in the literature as abundant components of the Anopheles midgut microbiota. ,,, S. marcescens was the bacterial species most identified, followed by other bacterial species, such as Elizabethkingia species and Aeromonas hydrophila. One Pseudomonas mendocina (referred to as P. mendocina Ag_C1) was isolated from the midgut of An. gambiae. The potential for bacterial biofilm formation within the Anopheles midgut has been described in the literature. ,, Biofilm-like structures produced by Serratia ureilytica can facilitate their colonization, contributing to a reduction in parasite infection. In addition to promoting a more stable colonization of the bacterium within the mosquito midgut, biofilm formation could minimize the bacterial clearance from the mosquito’s midgut because of the biofilm extracellular matrix, which promotes a more controlled, cohesive, and stable environment for the bacteria within the biofilm. Since midgut bacteria can modulate peritrophic matrix production and stability, we can speculate that biofilm can also be involved in this process. Bacteria in biofilm mode are more resistant to environmental fluctuations, thereby contributing to their persistence within the mosquito. Moreover, within the biofilm, there may be a higher local concentration of molecules with potential anti-Plasmodium activity, which could enhance the goal of reducing parasite infection in the mosquito. As previously mentioned, paratransgenesis strategies based on S. marcescens species have already been explored. In contrast, no such strategies involving Pseudomonas species have been reported to date. P. mendocina is primarily considered an environmental bacterium, with human infections being extremely rare. However, some severe infections have already been described, but the associated mortality rate is significantly low. Therefore, potential colonization of wild mosquito populations with this bacterium (or with a molecule that enhances its development) would likely carry a limited risk of pathogenicity and of causing infections in humans. This represents, in the context of paratransgenesis strategies, an advantage when compared to other bacteria, such as S. marcescens, which is a well-known human pathogen. , However, prior to their effective application in a paratransgenesis strategy, a thorough assessment of the virulence of these strains and their potential impact on human and animal health will be required.

Due to their abundance on mosquito midgut microbiota ,,, and their perceived differences in biofilm formation, we selected the S. marcescens isolates and P. mendocina Ag_C1 for assessing their capacity to form biofilms under in vitro conditions.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

S. marcescens and Pseudomonas spp. are both commonly found in the Anopheles midgut microbiota. ,,, We assessed the antibiotic resistance profiles of these bacteria to differentiate phenotypically the S. marcescens isolates and also, because studying the antibiotic susceptibility profile of the bacteria isolated would ease a future potential paratransgenesis strategy. The success of this type of strategy depends on the introduction and dissemination of the bacteria in wild mosquito populations. Therefore, it is also important to consider certain factors that may have unintended repercussions, such as the large-scale introduction into the environment of bacteria with resistance to antibiotics commonly used in clinical and agronomic practice.

We evaluated the antibiotic resistance profiles of P. mendocina Ag_C1 and eight S. marcescens isolates (of which seven were isolated from An. stephensi and one from An. coluzzii). All S. marcescens isolates showed a resistance phenotype to ampicillin, while susceptible to ceftriaxone, gentamicin, tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Table S2). The antibiogram results allowed us to identify two groups within the S. marcescens isolates, one comprising all isolates collected from An. stephensi and the second including the isolate from An. coluzzii, differing in the susceptibility profile to tobramycin and chloramphenicol (Table S2). This possibly suggests different S. marcescens strains colonizing the two Anopheles species.

S. marcescens is an opportunistic pathogen, often showing intrinsic resistance to several classes of antibiotics, namely ampicillins, first and second generation cephalosporins, and macrolides. Regarding S. marcescens isolated from mosquitoes, the antibiotic susceptibility profiles of strains isolated from An. stephensi revealed multidrug resistance phenotypes, displaying a resistance phenotype for most of the antibiotics tested, including ampicillin, tetracycline, and chloramphenicol. Comparatively to the literature, our results indicate a lower rate of antibiotic resistance for all of the S. marcescens isolates, with a susceptibility profile for most of the antibiotics tested (Table S2).

The isolate P. mendocina Ag_C1 revealed a resistance phenotype only to chloramphenicol (Table S3). Studies with P. mendocina environmental strains showed that they were susceptible to all the antibiotics normally used against Pseudomonas spp. infections, including tobramycin, gentamicin, and ciprofloxacin. In the same study, genetic approaches concluded that one of the environmental isolates did not encode antimicrobial resistance genes, while another encoded only one gene that confers resistance to chloramphenicol, but it was mutated. These data appear to be consistent with our results since the P. mendocina Ag_C1 from An. gambiae showed a susceptibility phenotype to all the antibiotics tested, except for chloramphenicol (Table S3). The fact that no multidrug resistance phenotype was observed represents an advantage in the possibility of releasing these bacteria into wild mosquito populations.

Based on these antibiotic susceptibility profiles, we selected the P. mendocina Ag_C1, as well as three isolates of S. marcescens (two from the group of An. stephensi isolates and the one from An. coluzzii), to proceed with biofilm studies. However, our preliminary characterization of biofilm (data not shown) revealed that the S. marcescens isolates presented a similar capacity for biofilm formation. Therefore, only the S. marcescens As_A1-C1 isolate (from An. stephensi), was further characterized, along sided with P. mendocina Ag_C1.

Quantitative Evaluation of the Initial Biofilm Formation Step

Another major objective was then to characterize the biofilm-forming capacity of the selected isolates (P. mendocina Ag_C1 and S. marcescens As_A1-C1). The presence of bacterial biofilms in the midgut epithelium of Anopheles mosquitoes might contribute to blocking the Plasmodium development in the mosquito, acting as a physical and chemical barrier to the passage of ookinetes through the midgut epithelium. , Biofilm formation potential can be assessed using different methodologies, with studies often focusing on metabolic assays and indirect measurements of biomass formed. Analysis of biofilm formation using confocal microscopy allows us to obtain a representation of the 3-D architecture and spatial structure of the biofilm and obtain quantitative structural parameters such as biofilm thickness (by visualization of the orthogonal xzy plane). This technique allows for real-time visualization of the biofilms, and it is possible, for instance, to understand whether the bacterial cells are in fact adherent to the surface.

So, we proceed our study by analyzing the biofilm formation potential of the selected isolates by testing different environmental conditions, using several media, namely, MHB, LB, TSB, and TSB + glucose (0.25%), which are routinely used to study biofilm formation. − Additionally, media more closely related to mosquitoes and Plasmodium cellular environment were also tested, as follows: Schneideŕs Drosophila medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and RPMIsup, used to culture Plasmodium parasite. With the use of more complex media, a greater complexity of the biofilm is expected.

The biofilm formation process is initiated with an adhesion step, which allows for the formation of more complex biofilm structures. As such, the adhesion step was studied as early as 3 h of bacteria incubation. The different methodologies used were consistent and indicated a higher quantity of adherent cells for P. mendocina Ag_C1 when compared to the S. marcescens As_A1-C1 (Figures –).

A clear difference in biofilm characteristics depicted by confocal microscopy was observed after 3 h of incubation between the two bacteria (Figure ), with a considerably higher density of adhered cells for P. mendocina Ag_C1. For P. mendocina Ag_C1, all media (except Schneider + FBS) allowed for the observation of a confluent growth of adherent cells covering nearly the whole well (only data from the RPMIsup and Schneider + FBS media are shown). Additionally, the development of microcolonies, which generally occurs after cell adhesion in biofilm formation, was observed in the TSB, TSBG, and RPMIsup media. In the Schneider + FBS medium, the cell density throughout the well was lower, but larger and more numerous microcolonies were observed (Figure C), suggesting that the biofilm formation process is faster in this medium. Regarding S. marcescens As_A1-C1, although in smaller quantities, some adherent cells are also observed on most media at this incubation time, but we were not able to observe any microcolonies at this time point (Figure B,D).

In addition to the above, a quantitative analysis of bacteria adhesion corresponding to this initial step in biofilm formation, using the crystal violet method (Figure ) was performed. Crystal violet is a cationic dye that binds to negatively charged molecules. As a result, this assay provided an indirect measurement of the formed biofilm biomass by measuring the absorbance at 590 nm. , In agreement with the confocal microscopy data, we observe that P. mendocina Ag_C1 shows a tendentially higher quantity of total biofilm biomass compared to S. marcescens As_A1-C1 (Figure ). Regarding the P. mendocina Ag_C1, there were no statistically significant differences between the absorbance values recorded for several media. Despite observing more microcolonies in RPMIsup and Schneider + FBS media, by confocal microscopy assays, a high density of adherent cells was also observed in the remaining media, which could justify the relatively close values for all media in this assay.

Characterization of 24 h Preformed Biofilms

The experiments after 24 h of incubation allowed us to analyze the potential of well-structured biofilms by the two bacteria. There were clear differences between P. mendocina Ag_C1 and S. marcescens As_A1-C1, with the P. mendocina Ag_C1 being significantly more successful in forming more structured and higher biofilms (Figures , and S1).

The formation of mature biofilms was first evaluated by confocal microscopy (Figures and ). For that, we labeled biofilms with the fluorescent probe SYTO 9, as described above. Bacterial movement is a common process influenced by biofilm structure and environmental conditions, and washing steps may also increase bacterial biofilm mobility, as described for static in vitro Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms; so this phenomenon may cause blur of the images, as seen in Figure .

Overall, our results show that all biofilms were quite heterogeneous, justifying the variation of results seen in the biofilm heights (Figure ). S. marcescens As_A1-C1 formed poorly structured biofilms, while P. mendocina Ag_C1 produced thicker heterogeneous biofilms (∼1.82 μm maximum height and ∼97.70 μm maximum height, respectively), which were highly dependent on growth conditions (Figures and S1). TSBG and RPMIsup stand out as the media where larger structures were obtained (Figures A and A,C); however, the biofilms formed were always quite heterogeneous. The most homogeneous biofilm was achieved in the Schneider + FBS medium (Figures A and C), probably due to the high percentage of serum, which was intended to mimic the blood ingested by mosquitoes (where the parasite could be found). Biofilm formation was particularly significant in the more complex media used to mimic the conditions under which Plasmodium and Anopheles cell lines are usually grown. This information leads us to hypothesize that similar events may occur within the midgut of the Anopheles mosquito, which could have some influence on the course of the infection of the Plasmodium parasite in the mosquito.

The S. marcescens As_A1-C1 biofilms formed in all media presented several microcolonies, but they corresponded to less thick biofilms (Figures B and B,D). S. marcescens is typically recognized for its ability to form biofilms, which contributes significantly to its pathogenicity. , Therefore, it is possible that this bacterium may exhibit a biofilm-forming capacity under different environmental conditions. However, we tested several culture media and multiple S. marcescens isolates (data not shown), obtained from various Anopheles species, and none of the isolates demonstrated a significant capacity for biofilm formation. We therefore hypothesize that when S. marcescens is part of the mosquito microbiota, it may not express this traitlikely due to the absence of pathogenic behavior in this context. In contrast, P. mendocina appears to exhibit this capacity intrinsically and in a symbiotic context. In summary, P. mendocina Ag_C1 had the highest potential for biofilm formation, which was particularly notable in the RPMIsup medium (Figure A).

In addition to structural characterization of the biofilms, we also evaluated the impact on biofilm metabolic behavior using the PrestoBlue assay, in which the active compound resazurin is converted into resorufin by metabolically active cells, with the latter being a highly fluorescent compound. Frequently, there are cells within the biofilm with drastically reduced metabolic activity, to ensure more resistance to antimicrobial agents, oxidative stress, and adverse conditions to which the biofilm may be subjected. Considering the same amount of CFU/mL initially inoculated, metabolic assays are usually slower in biofilm contexts compared to planktonic cells. This is due to microorganisms in biofilm mode having slower growth rates, therefore less affected by antimicrobial agents, , and to the physical and chemical barrier by the components of the extracellular matrix that prevent entry of resazurin into the biofilm. The reaction kinetics were slower for P. mendocina Ag_C1 than for S. marcescens As_A1-C1 for all of the culture media analyzed (Figure ). This is probably associated with the fact that there is a greater amount of biofilm, and therefore, the number of metabolically inactive cells will be higher. Additionally, resazurin might not be able to penetrate certain sections of the biofilm where there are structures with increased height. At 24 h of incubation, the superior capacity of P. mendocina Ag_C1 to form well-structured biofilms, when compared to S. marcescens As_A1-C1 was shown by the different methods.

Whole Genome Sequencing of Bacterial Isolates

WGS of P. mendocina Ag_C1 and S. marcescens As_A1-C1 isolates was performed. The raw reads were assembled by using the Unicycler tool, which produces accurate de novo assembly of Illumina short reads. The de novo assemblies were aligned with the respective reference genomes downloaded from NCBI by using the progressiveMauve software (Figure ). The alignment between the P. mendocina Ag_C1 and the respective reference genome had a good correspondence, but there were still over 5000 Gaps between the reference and the isolate de novo assembly. Moreover, 26.28% of the bases are missed in the reference genome, and 27.43% of the bases are extra in the de novo assembly [Figure (A)]. As for the S. marcescens As_A1-C1 isolate alignment with its reference genome, we found a much higher correspondence between the two sequences. In fact, there are only 1900 Gaps and 11.63% of bases missed in the reference genome and 14.75% of extra bases in the de novo assembly for S. marcescens As_A1-C1 (Figure B). There are 30 genomes for P. mendocina and 2713 genomes for S. marcescens in the NCBI genome database (as of September 2024). Of all these, only 10 P. mendocina genomes are completed, and S. marcescens only has 242 complete genomes. This scarcity of complete genomes is due to the difficulty in closing a genome since it usually requires several rounds of sequencing and most likely hybrid technologies (both short-read and long-read sequencing). In this study, we only performed one round of WGS for each isolate, and this is insufficient to close the respective genomes; however, our focus was on the characterization of the potential of these bacteria to form biofilms. Thus, it is not surprising that gaps were found between the assembled isolate genomes and the respective reference genomes.

These de novo assemblies were then annotated by using Prokka, a rapid prokaryotic genome annotation tool. Using this approach, 5106 genes were annotated for the P. mendocina Ag_C1 isolate and 5368 genes for the S. marcescens As_A1-C1 isolate (Table ). Genes involved in biofilm regulation were found to be shared by both species: bigR (Biofilm growth-associated repressor), bdlA (Biofilm dispersion protein), and bdcA (Cyclic-di-GMP-binding biofilm dispersal mediator protein). On the other hand, several small noncoding RNAs (sncRNAs) known to be involved in biofilm regulation were annotated and differed between the two isolates. In P. mendocina Ag_C1, annotated sncRNAs included RsmZ, SrbA, CrcZ, RsmY, and ErsA. RsmY and RsmZ are central components of the two-component regulatory system Gac/Rsm in P. aeruginosa, acting as sequestrators of repressor proteins, thereby promoting enhanced biofilm formation. − ErsA positively regulates the amrZ gene post-transcriptionally, which in turn promotes both biofilm formation and bacterial motility. Although these associations between sRNAs and biofilm formation mechanisms have been described for P. aeruginosa, it is plausible that similar regulatory pathways are conserved among species within the same genus.

In S. marcescens As_A1-C1, annotated sncRNAs included CsrB, GcvB, GlmY, OmrA/B, RprA, RybB, and GlmZ. In this case, most of the mechanisms described have been identified in E. coli; however, these regulatory pathways are often conserved within Enterobacterales. The sRNA CsrB sequesters a regulatory protein responsible for repressing exopolysaccharide production, thereby promoting biofilm formation. In contrary, GcvB represses the biogenesis of key components of the biofilm matrix. OmrA/B negatively regulate the expression of outer membrane proteins, affecting both membrane permeability and cell adhesion.

Additionally, only in S. marcescens As_A1-C1 were the genes bssS (biofilm regulator) and tabA (toxin-antitoxin biofilm protein) annotated, both of which are associated with reduced biofilm production. , bssS is a well-known biofilm regulator, and, under certain conditions, deletion of bssS leads to increased biofilm formation. Moreover, a bssS mutant was shown to allow significantly increased colonization by pathogens in the mouse gut environment. The other gene that was exclusively annotated to the S. marcescens As_A1-C1 isolate was the tabA toxin-antitoxin protein. Even though there are some conflicting results regarding the roles of TabA in biofilm regulation, it appears that it may act by decreasing biofilm dispersal. There are also several additional differences in the annotation of both isolates regarding small noncoding RNAs that are described to be biofilm regulators. Although we cannot state that the genomic differences observed between the two isolates are directly responsible for the phenotypic differences in biofilm formation observed in vitro, we hypothesize that the exclusive presence of genes involved in the repression of this process in the S. marcescens As_A1-C1 genome may contribute to the formation of poorly organized biofilm structures, contrary to what was observed for P. mendocina Ag_C1.

Colonization of the Midgut of An. stephensi with Pseudomonas spp

Finally, the success of paratransgenesis depends on the introduction of the genetically modified bacterium into the mosquitoes’ populations. , As such, it is extremely important that bacteria can easily colonize the midgut of several Anopheles species and not just the species from which it was isolated. An. stephensi is an important vector in Asia that has recently been introduced in Africa, while An. gambiae is associated with malaria transmission on the African continent. In this sense and as a proof of concept, experiments were carried out, in which An. stephensi mosquitoes were fed with either P. mendocina Ag_C1 (isolated from An. gambiae) or the reference strain P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 (control assay) at three different inocula (Figure ). For P. mendocina Ag_C1, an increase of about 100-fold was observed when comparing the low inoculum (3.9 × 103 CFU/mL) with the medium inoculum (3.9 × 105 CFU/mL); however, between the medium inoculum and the high inoculum (3.9 × 106 CFU/mL), the difference is no longer so pronounced, which may indicate that the amount of bacteria for colonization begins to reach its upper limit. Both bacteria have the capacity to colonize the midguts of these An. stephensi mosquitoes with their natural microbiota intact. However, our results demonstrated a higher midgut colonization potential for the P. mendocina Ag_C1, up to 72 h postfeeding, a time when the blood meal has already been digested, showing that P. mendocina Ag_C1 can survive digestion, which would be an advantage for the use of this bacterium as a biological control tool.

Inocula comparisons were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test and the Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01, ****p < 0.0001.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates the potential of P. mendocina Ag_C1 isolated from An. gambiae as a target for the development of strategies to control malaria, since its biofilm production can be advantageous when formed on the mosquitoes’ midgut epithelium, hampering the life cycle of the Plasmodium parasite within the Anopheles mosquito. Biofilm formation by P. mendocina Ag_C1 was most significant when grown on the culture media more appropriate for Anopheles cell culture and Plasmodium growth, suggesting an advantage for future use. Paratransgenesis strategies have enormous potential to overcome the problems currently encountered in the use of insecticides and antimalarial drugs to fight malaria. Therefore, studies such as this one are important for bringing this type of strategy closer to viable and real alternatives that can be applied in the context of countries where malaria continues to be one of the main public health problems.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Isolation and Identification from Anopheleś Midguts

Pools of 10 living adult mosquitoes (IHMT-NOVA, Lisbon, Portugal) were anesthetized at −4 °C for 1 min and sterilized by rinsing in 10% sodium hypochlorite for 1 min, followed by 1 min in 75% ethanol and finally 1 min in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (VWR Life Science, Avantor, USA). Midguts were collected in 200 μL of sterile PBS and homogenized using a motorized sterile pestle for 1 min. The pooled midgut macerate was added to 5 mL of MHB (BD Difco, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA) and incubated at 37 °C with 180 rpm agitation for 18–20 h. 10-fold serial dilutions of the overnight culture were prepared in PBS, and 100 μL aliquots of the 10–6, 10–7, and 10–8 dilutions were plated on: MHA (BD Difco); PIA (BD Difco); MacConkey II agar (BD BBL); CHROMagar Pseudomonas (CHROMagar, Kanto Chemical Co, Tokyo, Japan); and blood agar base (Sheep Blood 7%) (LiofilChem, Roseto degli Abruzzi (TE), Italy) (three replicates per dilution per medium). All plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–20 h. From each plate, morphologically distinct colonies were selected, and pure cultures were obtained. A presumptive identification of each bacterium was performed by analyses of the colony morphological characteristics, Gram staining, and oxidase test (BactidentR Oxidase, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) as per manufacturer’s instructions. For the bacteria of interest, a molecular identification was performed by 16S rDNA sequencing. For total DNA extraction, three to four single colonies were resuspended in 0.5 mL of TE buffer [10 mM TrisHCl (NZYTech, Lisbon, Portugal); 1 mM EDTA (Sigma-Aldrich); pH: 8.0 ± 0.2] + 1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) + 180 μg/mL proteinase K (NZYTech). The cellular suspensions were incubated at 56 °C on a heat block for 1 h and then at 100 °C for 10 min. The cellular suspensions were then cooled on ice and centrifuged at 13.000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatants were collected and maintained at −20 °C. Amplification of an internal region of the 16S rDNA gene (492 bp) was performed in 25 μL reaction mixtures containing 1× buffer NZY Tech (NZYTech), 1.75 mM MgCl2 (NZYTech), 0.2 mM dNTPs (NZYTech), 0.4 μM of each universal primer [27f (5-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG3) and 519r (5-GWATTACCGCGGCKGCTG3), MWG-Biotech AG, Ebersberg, Germany], 0.75 U of NZY Taq-II Polymerase (NZYTech), and 5 μL of total DNA. Total DNA of Staphylococcus pseudintermedius DSM21284 was used as a positive control, and sterile water was used as a negative control. All PCR reactions were performed in an UNO II thermocycler (Biometra, Gottingen, Germany) as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 50 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s with 5 min extra extension time in the last cycle. The expected size of the amplification products was confirmed by 1% (p/v) agarose (NZYTech) gel electrophoresis. To identify the bacterial species, the amplification products were purified using the NZYGelpure kit (NZYTech) and sequenced by Sanger sequencing at STAB-Vida (Caparica, Portugal) using the 27f universal primer. The sequences were then analyzed using the BioEdit Sequence Alignment Editor software (BioEdit v.7.2.5.). The bacterial species was identified using the BLASTn tool from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI, Maryland, USA), which allowed access to the bacterial species with the highest identity with the query sequence and the identification of the bacteria isolated.

Whole Genome Sequencing of Bacterial Isolates

For genomic DNA isolation, a single colony of the bacterial isolates was transferred to 5 mL of MHB and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h at 180 rpm. The genomic DNA was extracted with the NZY Tissue gDNA Isolation kit (NZYTech) from 1 mL of overnight culture, according to the manufactureŕs instructions. The genomic DNA was analyzed by 0.7% (p/v) agarose gel electrophoresis in TAE 1× buffer at 90 V for 45 min to assess DNA degradation and quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and NanoDrop 1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Genomic DNA was sequenced by Novogene Biotech (Beijing, China), using an IlluminaNovaSeq 6000 instrument, generating 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads. The raw reads were assembled using the Unicycler assembly pipeline for bacterial genomes. The assembled genomes were annotated using Prokka. ProgressiveMauve was used to align the assembled genome of P. mendocina Ag_C1 isolate to the reference genome of P. mendocina downloaded from NCBI (GCF 000733715.2). Likewise, the assembled genome of S. marcescens As_A1-C1 was aligned with the reference genome of S. marcescens downloaded from the NCBI (GCF 003516165.1) using progressiveMauve.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

The antibiotic susceptibility profiles of the bacteria isolated from the Anopheles midgut were determined using the Kirby–Bauer Disk Diffusion (for S. marcescens As_A1-C1) and E-test (for P. mendocina Ag_C1) methods, according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) recommendations. The reference strains P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 and E. coli ATCC 25922 were used as quality control strains. The antibiotic discs used were the following: ampicillin (10 μg); ceftriaxone (30 μg); gentamicin (10 μg); tobramycin (10 μg); tetracycline (30 μg); ciprofloxacin (5 μg); trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (1.25/23.75 μg); chloramphenicol (30 μg) (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, UK). The E-test method was applied to assess P. mendocina Ag_C1 susceptibility profiles toward gentamicin, tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, and chloramphenicol, using M.I.C. test strips (LiofilChem), according to the manufacturer. After incubation at 37 °C for 18 ± 2 h, the inhibition zone diameters and minimum inhibitory concentrations were registered, and isolates were categorized as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R), following the CLSI recommendations. Three independent replicates were performed.

Bacterial Isolates and Growth Media Used on Biofilm-Forming Assays

For these assays, the selected bacteria were grown in MHB; LB (NZYTech); TSB (BD Bacto); TSB + 0.25% glucose (Scharlab, Sentmenat, Spain) (TSBG); RPMI (Gibco Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts) supplemented with 5.94 g of HEPES, 5 g of Albumax, 0.05 g of hypoxanthine, 38 mL of NaHCO3; and Schneider’s Drosophila Medium (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 20% FBS.

Quantitative Analysis of Bacterial Adhesion in the Initial Steps of Biofilm Formation Using Crystal Violet and Colony Count Assays

Briefly, in a 96-well clear flat-bottom polystyrene plate (VWR Life Science), both isolates (1 × 106 CFU/mL) were inoculated in the different media. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h and washed with sterile MHB to remove nonadhered cells, and 200 μL of a 0.25% crystal violet solution (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each well and left for 30 min. The crystal violet solution was removed, and three washing steps with sterile MHB were performed. 200 μL of 95% ethanol was added to each well and left for 5 min under gentle agitation. The well volume was transferred to a new 96-well plate, and absorbance readings at 590 nm were taken in the microplate reader SPECTROstarNano (BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany). The experiment was performed in two independent assays with six technical replicates each. A negative control containing only medium was included in all assays.

Regarding the colony counting method, in a 96-well clear flat-bottomed polystyrene plate, both isolates were inoculated (1 × 106 CFU/mL) in three of the tested media: MHB, RPMIsup, and Schneider + FBS. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. To eliminate the planktonic cells, the media was removed from the wells and washed with PBS. PBS was added again, and the solution was homogenized by doing “up and down” steps with the pipet to loosen all the biofilm from the wells. The tip was also used to “scrape” the bottom and side of the well. After performing these steps repeatedly, 10-fold serial dilutions were prepared and inoculated in MHA. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The colonies on each plate were counted, and the CFU/mL was calculated. Each experiment was performed in two independent assays with six technical replicates each.

Analysis of Different Biofilm Step Formation Using Confocal Microscopy

Both isolates (1 × 106 CFU/mL) were inoculated in all the different media in uncoated 8-well plates (Ibidi GmbH, Munich, Germany) and incubated for 3 and 24 h at 37 °C. The media were carefully removed from each well, and a solution of 1% SYTO 9 was added. The biofilms were stained in the dark at room temperature with 3 μM SYTO 9 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min and rinsed with sterile PBS. The plates were then observed under a Leica TCS SP5 inverted confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems CMS GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) to check for cell adhesion and biofilm formation. The microscope is equipped with a continuous Ar ion laser (Multiline LASOS LGK 7872 ML05). Images were collected at 512 by 512 pixels and using a scan rate of 100 Hz per frame. A 63 × 1.2 N.A. water immersion objective was used (HCX PL APO CS 63.0 × 1.20 WATER UV). SYTO 9 images were recorded using a 488 nm excitation line, and emission was collected at 501–570 nm. Images were collected on the xyz plane view, using various digital zooms, and on the xzy plane view. For the determination of biofilm height, two images were acquired in two different channels (x–zvertical slice). One channel corresponds to the surface of the coverslip and the other channel to the fluorescence signal of the probe. Then, we identify the top of different biofilm positions, which correspond to the highest Z position (along the vertical axis) at different biofilm localizations along each image and draw a line from the base of the biofilm to its different highest point along the Z-axis (Figure S1), determining the biofilm thickness/height in micrometers (μm). For each isolate, this procedure was performed at least three independent replicates.

Evaluation of Metabolic Activity of Biofilm-Growing Bacteria

Briefly, in 96-well microtiter flat-bottomed polystyrene plates (VWR Life Science), both isolates were inoculated (1 × 106 CFU/mL) in the six different culture media for 24 h at 37 °C. To discriminate the bacterial metabolic activity within the different biofilms, we used the PrestoBlue assay as described by Pinto et al. (2019). The reduction of resazurin by the bacteria was evaluated by measuring the fluorescence intensity (excitation: 530 nm; emission: 590 nm) in a microplate reader POLARstar OPTIMA (BMG Labtech) every 10 min for 4 h, at 37 °C. Two independent assays were performed, with six technical replicates each, for each bacterial isolate.

72 h Colonization Assay of Anopheles stephensi by Pseudomonas spp

Bacterial suspensions of pure cultures of P. mendocina and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 were prepared in MHB with an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) corresponding to 1 × 106 CFU/mL. For bacterial enumeration in CFU/mL, aliquots were plated on MHA and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h for colony counting. From this suspension, three different bacterial inocula were prepared: low (103 CFU/mL), intermediate (105 CFU/mL), and high (106 CFU/mL). Each inoculum was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min, and the bacterial pellets were resuspended in blood from laboratory mice. Four groups of 30 mosquitoes each were fed with 500 μL of blood inoculated with a different bacterial concentration. The fourth group, for control purposes, was fed sterile blood. The mosquitoes were allowed to feed for 1 h in a temperature-controlled room (at 28 °C). All mosquitoes were left in a temperature-controlled room for 72 h at 20 °C. Pools of 10 midguts of each group were dissected and macerated in 200 μL of PBS (procedure described above). Each suspension was added to 5 mL of MHB and incubated at 37 °C and 180 rpm for 18–20 h. From the resulting overnight cultures, 10-fold serial dilutions were prepared in PBS, and 100 μL aliquots were plated on MHA, PIA, and CHROMagar Pseudomonas (three replicates for each dilution for each media). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–20 h. The CFU/mL counts were obtained for each morphologically distinct colony. Morphologically distinct colonies were characterized by Gram staining and an oxidase test and identified by 16S rDNA Sanger sequencing (procedure described above). For each bacterium, this procedure was performed in two independent replicates.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean + standard deviation of two independent experiments performed with six technical replicates each, unless indicated otherwise. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad version 7.00. As the data did not meet the assumptions of normality, as confirmed by a normality test, nonparametric tests were applied for the statistical analysis of our results. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare the differences between the several media tested and the two bacteria tested by implementing the Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons. The statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
