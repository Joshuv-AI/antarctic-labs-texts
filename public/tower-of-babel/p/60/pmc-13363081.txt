In conversation with Dr Harlan M. Krumholz: On preprints, the story behind medRxiv
Authors: Albina Arjuman
Published: 2026

Traditional systems of peer review have always met with healthy scepticism, as they are built on an overarching trust in science. However, the time required for peer review has long been a point of contention, and alternative strategies for assessing research quality beyond peer review, or for increasing access to research prior to peer review, are being explored. One such strategy to increase access to completed research before publication is the use of preprints.

Since the inception of the first preprint server, arXiv, in 1991, which was originally a discussion forum to exchange ideas by mathematicians and physicists, preprints have come a long way. This journey has been fraught with academic paranoia about the reliability of pre-peer-reviewed content and issues that challenge existing publishing models. In this conversation, we discuss the thoughts and motivation behind the concept of preprints and their evolution from the co-founder of medRxiv, Dr Harlan Krumholz.

Dr Krumholz is a physician-scientist and cardiologist. He is internationally recognised for his work in cardiovascular outcomes research, healthcare quality, evidence-based medicine, and digital health innovation. He has contributed extensively to patient-centred research and healthcare transparency initiatives. He has influenced innovation in biomedical publishing and has contributed to the development of preprint platforms for medical sciences. He has authored more than 1,500 publications with >300,000 citations, and an H-index of 251. He is an elected member of the US National Academy of Medicine.

Prof. Harlan M. Krumholz

Harold H. Hines, Jr Professor, Yale School of Medicine, New Haven, Director of the Yale New Haven Hospital Center for Outcomes Research and Evaluation (CORE), Connecticut, USA & Editor-In-Chief, JACC, the flagship journal of the American College of Cardiology

SPOTLIGHT: Dr Harlan M. Krumholz

Being one of the founders of medRxiv, please share your experiences in conceptualizing the contents of this server and particularly the motivation and vision for its use and access.

My interest in preprints emerged gradually from repeated encounters with what I considered a misalignment between the pace at which science advances and completed work is often made available to the scientific community. There were times when completed research took a year or more to be published. These long periods of waiting between the completion of the work and its entry into the public domain seemed suboptimal to me. I felt that if science needs to move quickly, then we need to find ways to facilitate, even encourage, communication at the speed of the science itself. The other thing was that sometimes information was coming out, but in very limited forms. For instance, important information would come out of major meetings, but all that would be published was an abstract, and if you weren’t there for the presentation or you didn’t get a chance to interact with the authors, you had no full picture of exactly what was presented or what was done. Sometimes investigators presented major work, including randomised trials, and there would still be a lag between when they presented it at the meeting and when it became available to the scientific community. So, there was a period of uncertainty when the community might hear a headline or see an abstract, but not have access to the full methods and results needed to judge the work. I came to believe that the traditional publication process, even with the essential value of peer review, was not fully meeting the need for timely scientific communication.1

With people increasingly using generative artificial intelligence (AI) for coinage and analysis of their research, there are chances of synthesis of findings based on a nascent version of a preprint or a preprint with flawed findings. What are your comments on this?

AI amplifies existing risk across all scientific communications. The risk is not limited to preprints. It applies to manuscripts, peer-reviewed articles, reviews, commentaries, and many other forms of scientific communication.

The principal challenge is scale. AI can generate large volumes of low-quality or misleading material quickly. That is separate from the issue of fraud, which has always existed but may be easier to produce and harder to detect with new tools. We need more sophisticated approaches to identify unreliable or fraudulent work and preventing it from entering both peer-reviewed literature as well as pre-print platforms.

But the solution cannot be restriction alone. We need stronger provenance, labeling, accountability, and transparency. In that sense, preprints can be part of the solution. A preprint is time-stamped, clearly identified as not yet peer-reviewed, and preserved as an enduring artifact on the Web. It provides a record that people can return to if the science is questioned; the concerns can be surfaced publicly, and readers can examine what was actually posted.

We are reluctant to remove preprints simply because they are later found to be wrong. If a preprint is fraudulent, that is different, and it may need to be withdrawn, but if the work is flawed or later superseded, we generally believe the record should remain visible with appropriate versioning or notices. People should be able to track what was first posted, when it was posted, and how the scientific discussion evolved.

Trackability offers anchorage to the content! How do you think that kind of anchorage could affect a certain research group? For instance, the preprint on hydroxychloroquine and azithromycin during COVID-19, with a flawed analysis was extensively cited till the time that it was retracted.2

This is an important example, and I would describe it somewhat differently. The hydroxychloroquine and azithromycin study was posted on medRxiv, but it also underwent peer-review and appeared in a reputable journal from a major publisher at essentially the same time. So, this was not simply a case of un-reviewed material being placed online. The same work was published in formal peer-reviewed literature and remained there for years before the journal article was retracted. Now the preprint also carries that information.

To me, that is not an indictment of preprints. The medRxiv version was clearly labeled as a preprint, as work that had not been certified by peer review. It also created a public space where readers could raise concerns, question the methods, and try to understand or replicate the findings. In the comments, people were already identifying problems and asking important questions. That kind of public scrutiny was visible in a way that is often not possible with a conventional journal article.

The lesson is that peer-review is important, but it is not a guarantee. A paper can pass through peer review and still have serious problems. During a crisis like the one in March 2020, everyone was moving quickly. Clinicians, researchers, journals, preprint servers, policymakers, and the public were all trying to make sense of emerging evidence in real time. In that setting, it was essential to make the science visible so that it could be examined, challenged, and corrected.

That is why I see the durable preprint record as part of the solution. It gives the community something concrete to evaluate. It allows comments and concerns to accumulate around the work. It creates a time-stamped record of what was claimed and how the scientific community responded. Without that, claims may circulate as headlines, press releases, or social media fragments. With a preprint, there is a clearly labeled, actual document that can be scrutinised.

So the point is not that preprints eliminate risk. They do not. The point is that transparent posting, clear labeling, and public commentary can help science self-correct, particularly when the stakes are high, and the world is moving fast. I believe that the world should always move fast because people are suffering and scientific knowledge is important. It’s just that it needs to be accompanied by healthy skepticism and, ultimately, peer-review evaluation as well as public evaluation.

What are your thoughts on the similarities or differences in uptake between preprints and articles published in Journals which are exploring post-publication peer-review scenarios?

Peer review has a different function, even when elements of review occur after publication. Peer-reviewed journals vary widely in their standards, selectivity, and editorial processes, but the best journals bring credibility, trust, and a record of careful editorial judgment. That does not mean everything they publish is correct, or that peer-reviewed articles are immune to errors in judgment. Papers are corrected, challenged, and sometimes retracted. As Dr Francis Collins, former Director of NIH said, that is part of science, which is progressive and self-correcting.

But journal peer-review does serve an important role. It can refine the work, test the claims, clarify the presentation, and help readers understand that the paper has undergone some form of evaluation before becoming part of the formal literature. It is not a guarantee of truth, but it is a signal that the work has been vetted in a particular way.

A preprint serves a different purpose. It is rapid, freely available, and intended to allow communication among scientists and others as soon as the work is ready to be shared. A preprint has not undergone journal peer-review, although reputable servers may screen for scope, ethics, plagiarism, and potential harm. It should therefore be read differently from a journal article. It is an opportunity for early dissemination, discussion, criticism, and improvement.

In terms of uptake, preprints may be taken up quickly because they are immediate, freely accessible, and available at the moment when a field is actively debating a question. They can generate discussion, critique, collaboration, and sometimes garner media attention before journal review has occurred. Journal articles, including those with post-publication peer review, tend to be taken up differently because they carry the signal of formal publication. Readers, clinicians, policymakers, and guideline groups appropriately treat them as having passed through a higher level of editorial scrutiny. I do applaud the movement toward post-publication peer review, as it engages the readership and allows the community to learn together, which is similar to what the preprint servers are doing now for the preprints.

I do not see preprints and peer-reviewed articles as substitutes. They occupy different points in the life cycle of scientific communication. A preprint makes work visible and open to scrutiny earlier. Journal publication provides a later stage of evaluation, refinement, and formal recognition. Both can contribute to science, but they need to be understood for what they are.

There are stakeholders always looking for potential opportunities to commercialise new initiatives. Do you see potential for preprints to soon transform as profit-making platforms leveraging on the open access and open science driven business models?

There are preprint servers operated by nonprofit organisations and others connected to commercial publishers or for-profit entities. The basic function may look similar, i.e. authors submit work that can be posted before peer-review. But I do think the governance and purpose of the platform matter.

For me, medRxiv was always intended to serve the scientific and medical community. It was not created for commercial purposes. Of course, even a nonprofit platform needs a business model in the practical sense: it requires resources, infrastructure, screening, staffing, technology, and long-term stewardship. But there is a difference between needing support to sustain a public good and building a platform whose purpose is to generate financial return.

In the case of medRxiv, the purpose was to serve science, medicine, and society by accelerating responsible communication of research. That vision was shared by Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory, BMJ, and Yale when we came together to create this platform. We did not build it as an asset to be sold or monetised. We built it as part of the infrastructure for open science.

I do not want to criticise for-profit preprint servers simply because they are for-profit. They may also serve authors and readers well. But ownership and incentives matter. A commercial entity ultimately has obligations to its owners or shareholders, while an independent nonprofit platform can be governed around its mission. For scientists, that is an important consideration. When they post on a nonprofit preprint server, they can have greater confidence that the platform’s primary obligation is to the scientific community and the public interest, not for monetisation or return on investment.

Some preprint servers now also allow publication of just a figure or even raw data sheets, etc. What are the consequences of this practice?

I think that practice can move away from the spirit of what a preprint is meant to be. In my view, a preprint should be a standalone scientific contribution, generally in a format that allows readers to understand the question, methods, results, and interpretation. It may be preliminary and may not have undergone journal peer-review, but it should contain enough information for others to evaluate the work.

A figure, an abstract, or a set of raw tables may be useful, but by itself, it’s usually not enough. Those materials may belong in data repositories, meeting platforms, or other forms of scientific exchange. But a preprint should give readers enough context to judge what was done, how it was done, and what the findings mean.

One of the motivations for medRxiv was to avoid a situation in which important findings circulate only as meeting presentations, abstracts, press releases, or headlines. If investigators are going to announce major findings, especially findings that may affect medicine or public health, then the community should be able to see the full methods and results. A preprint can provide that fuller record. It can accompany a presentation, but it should do more than repeat what can be shown in ten minutes. It should allow the work to be examined seriously.

Would you encourage different formats for deposition as a preprint for instance a State report or a survey report?

I would be open to different formats, as long as the work is presented in a way that others can evaluate. The key principle is not that every preprint must look exactly like a conventional research article, but that it should include enough context, methods, results, and interpretation for readers to understand what was done and how much confidence to place in it, and it should be scientific.

A State report, survey report, systematic review, methods paper, or other form of scientific work could be appropriate if it is complete enough to be judged. The methods should be transparent, the data sources should be clear, and the limitations should be apparent. The goal is to make the work reproducible, or at least sufficiently understandable, so that others can assess it.

There are other places for abstracts, figures, raw datasets, and supplemental materials. I strongly support open science and open data, and I encourage investigators to deposit data in appropriate repositories when possible. But a preprint should usually be a paper-like contribution, something that might reasonably be submitted to a journal, or at least something with enough substance that the scientific community can evaluate it.

Do you think preprints could be the answer for the extensive grey literature that is circulating online?

Preprints can be part of the answer, but only for the grey literature that is truly scientific. If a report, review, synthesis, survey, Delphi study, or analysis has clear methods and enough detail for readers to evaluate it, then a preprint server may be an appropriate place for it. In that case, posting it as a preprint can make it visible, citable, durable, and open to critique.

But not all grey literature belongs on a preprint server. Meeting transcripts, opinion pieces, advocacy documents, white papers, and informal arguments may be valuable, but they may require different repositories or platforms. They can also benefit from being preserved as durable artifacts on the web, but they should not necessarily be presented as scientific preprints.

For medRxiv, the principle was that the server should be a venue for science. Science can take many forms, including original studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, syntheses, methods papers, and structured consensus work. But the content should have enough methodological grounding and transparency that readers can judge it. People should not mistake a preprint server for a general archive of commentary or opinion. Its value depends on maintaining that distinction.

What are your thoughts on the skepticism around the first publication rights that countries are now focusing on? Countries have their own specific repositories where people are mandated to post their papers before they submit anywhere. These things are in fact discussed even earlier, at the time of a potential international collaboration, in multi country research contexts.

This is an important issue, and I understand why countries may want national repositories or policies around first disclosure of research. There may be reasons related to public accountability, national investment in science, language, access, or ensuring that work supported by public funds is visible within that country. Those are legitimate concerns.

At the same time, science is global. If every country, funder, or institution creates a separate repository with different rules, we risk fragmenting the scientific record. The goal should be harmonisation, or at least interoperability, so that work is visible locally while also discoverable internationally. Ideally, a paper posted in a national repository could also be linked to, mirrored, or simultaneously posted on a trusted preprint server, with clear versioning and metadata.

For me, the enduring principle is not what is best for medRxiv or for any particular repository. It is what is best for science and society. We should want systems that are nonprofit or mission-driven where possible, transparent, durable, citable, easy to search, and accessible to readers everywhere. We should also want clear standards for versioning, withdrawal notices, ethics statements, licensing, and links to eventual journal publication.

I do think there is value in moving toward common global norms, just as there would be value in harmonizing clinical trial registration and other parts of the research infrastructure. But we are not there yet. This will require international collaboration, with voices from many countries, so that the system respects local needs while also serving the global scientific community. The goal should be to reduce confusion, improve access, and manage risks while strengthening the communication of science for everyone.

Regarding the ethical issues around sensitive data that is posted on preprint servers, are manuscripts posted as preprints mandated to be ethically approved?

This is one of our important guardrails. For human-participant research, authors need to state that the work had appropriate ethical approval, or that it was determined to be exempt by an appropriate body. If that is unclear or if the manuscript raises questions, the server can return to the authors for clarification.

What is not acceptable is for authors to simply to decide on their own that ethics approval was not needed, without any institutional or ethics body standing behind that determination. If authors claim exemption, they should be able to identify the body that made that determination. The institution needs to stand behind the conduct of the work.

This is especially important in clinical medicine, where manuscripts may involve patients, sensitive health data, interventions, or vulnerable populations. In those cases, we would want to know, Who approved the work? Who oversaw it? Was consent required? How were participants protected? These are not questions about whether the science is persuasive in the way peer-review process would judge it but whether the work meets basic ethical expectations for being posted.

That was part of how we built medRxiv. It was not simply a matter of putting manuscripts on the web. The success of a medical preprint server depends on trust, and trust depends on clear guardrails around ethics, transparency, and accountability. So when there are questions, it is appropriate to raise them before posting.

As an Editor, do you feel that preprints should be cited in research articles? I am talking in the lines of Emilie Marcus’s (Former CEO, Cell Press, Elsevier) comment on certain pseudo-articles sneaking through the backdoor.3

Yes, preprints can be cited, but they need to be cited responsibly. A preprint is a durable scholarly artifact. It has listed authors, a date, a version, and often a DOI. If it is relevant to the work being discussed, it is better to cite it transparently than to leave the source of the claim vague or hidden.

But the citation should clearly indicate as to what is being cited. A preprint has not undergone peer review by a journal, and readers should not be led to believe that it has. If a preprint is cited as preliminary evidence, as a hypothesis-generating observation, as a description of ongoing work, or as a way to acknowledge priority or provenance, that can be entirely appropriate.

The problem arises when a preprint is cited as though it establishes a fact with the same authority as a carefully peer-reviewed and published article. Editors and reviewers should look carefully at how the citation is being used. If the author is saying, “This preprint reported such and such,” that may be appropriate. If the author is using the preprint to support a definitive clinical or scientific claim, the language should be qualified, or the authors should rely on stronger evidence if available.

I also think citations should distinguish preprints clearly, for example, by identifying them as preprints and including the version or posting date when possible. If the work has subsequently been published in a journal, authors should usually cite the peer-reviewed version unless there is a specific reason to cite the earlier preprint. The goal is not to smuggle “pseudo-articles” into the literature, but to acknowledge that preprints are part of the scientific record, while being honest about their status.

Any words of caution you’d like to share with young researchers on ethical issues in sharing sensitive data, media misreporting, and financial risk associated with servers?

My advice would be to share science, but to do it responsibly. Young researchers should recognise that once work is posted publicly, it can travel quickly and be interpreted by people far beyond the intended scientific audience. That creates an obligation to be careful about ethics, language, data protection, and the strength of the conclusions.

First, sensitive data require special care. Investigators need appropriate ethical approval or exemption, informed consent when required, and careful attention to privacy and confidentiality. They should not post anything that could identify or harm participants, even indirectly. The speed of preprints does not reduce those obligations.

Second, researchers should be clear about uncertainty. A preprint should not be written as if it has already been validated by peer-review. Furthermore, claims should match the strength of the evidence. If the study is preliminary, small, observational, or hypothesis-generating, that should be apparent in the language. Overstatement can lead to media misreporting, public confusion, and in clinical medicine, potentially harmful behavior.

Third, young researchers should choose platforms carefully. They should understand who owns and governs the server, whether it is transparent, whether it has appropriate screening and labeling, and whether the record will be durable and citable. Preprints are part of the scientific record, so the platform matters.

Finally, I would encourage healthy skepticism about everything we read, including peer-reviewed articles. Science is progressive and self-correcting. Evidence evolves, findings may be challenged, and some claims may ultimately be revised or reversed. Preprints require particular care because they have not undergone journal peer-review, but that does not make them unimportant. When used responsibly, they can accelerate collaboration, invite critique, and improve science.
