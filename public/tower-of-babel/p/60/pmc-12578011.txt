Background

Indirect inguinal hernias are the most frequent congenital anomaly in children. In infant girls, approximately 15–20% of these hernias include an ovary, occasionally accompanied by a Fallopian tube. However, hernias involving the uterus and adnexa are uncommon, especially in pediatric females[1]. Such cases often present as irreducible masses and may be linked to prematurity or genetic conditions like Mayer–Rokitansky–Küster–Hauser syndrome or Turner mosaic[2]. Since reproductive organs may be present in the hernia sac, meticulous surgical intervention is crucial to preserve fertility and avoid complications[1]. Case presentation

A 2.5-month-old female infant, born at term via normal vaginal delivery with no neonatal complications, presented to the emergency department with a 24-hour history of a firm, tender, irreducible left inguinal swelling. The infant had no associated vomiting or systemic symptoms. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no prior hernias, surgeries, hospitalizations, or family history of connective tissue disorders. She had no known drug allergies. Diagnostic ultrasound confirmed the presence of uterus and ovaries within the hernia sac, with preserved vascular flow and no evidence of bowel obstruction. Due to limited laparoscopic capabilities at our community hospital, emergency open herniotomy was performed. Intraoperative exploration revealed the entire uterus and both ovaries within the hernial sac, without adhesions or necrosis (Supplementary Digital Content Video 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/A916). The herniated organs were carefully reduced into the abdominal cavity, followed by high ligation of the hernia sac and internal inguinal ring closure. The postoperative course was uncomplicated, and at 2-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no evidence of recurrence. This case highlights the importance of considering reproductive organ involvement in female infants presenting with irreducible inguinal hernias.

Differential diagnosis for irreducible inguinal mass considered typical hernias and hydrocele, but ultrasound revealed unexpected uterine involvement, confirming this rare uterus-containing hernia. Unique diagnostic difficulties were limited imaging resources (e.g. MRI) and the exceptional rarity of uterine involvement in pediatric inguinal hernias. Additionally, the absence of pediatric laparoscopic equipment mandated an open surgical approach. These constraints required reliance on ultrasound and careful clinical judgment to guide management. The patient required only 5-day wound care with no routine follow-up. Parents were counseled about this rare condition and advised to seek prompt evaluation for any future groin or reproductive concerns. Lifelong clinical awareness was recommended despite expected normal development.

Clinical discussion

The processus vaginalis develops as an outpouching of the parietal peritoneum around the sixth month of gestation[4]. In females, it travels alongside the round ligament of the uterus, extending through the inguinal canal toward the labium major. Unlike in males, the female processus vaginalis is typically smaller and usually regresses by the eighth month of pregnancy[5]. About 15–20% of inguinal hernias in girls contain an ovary, occasionally accompanied by a fallopian tube[6]. However, uterine herniation in female infants is rare[7]. Embryologically, there is no clear explanation for uterine herniation unless an abnormality in the uterine suspensory ligaments exists. One proposed theory suggests that if Müllerian duct fusion fails, resulting in excessive ovarian mobility and no fusion of the uterine cornua, the likelihood of ovarian herniation increases[8]. This condition can also lead to fallopian tube herniation and, in rare cases, complete uterine herniation[8].

The presence of reproductive organs in an inguinal hernia sac presents distinct challenges in pediatric cases[9]. Early diagnosis and treatment are essential to avoid complications like ovarian torsion or vascular compromise[10]. Ultrasound is the primary diagnostic method, while MRI may be used for complex scenarios[11]. Surgical management should focus on organ preservation, with laparoscopic techniques providing superior visualization and minimally invasive repair[2].

This case documents a rare uterine/ovarian hernia in an infant, successfully managed with open surgery in a resource-limited setting, with good 2-year outcomes. However, MRI confirmation was unavailable, the open approach may not be ideal where laparoscopy exists, and longer follow-up is needed to assess fertility impact.

Conclusions

This rare case demonstrates that while standard herniotomy principles apply for uterus-containing inguinal hernias, the presence of reproductive organs requires fertility-preserving techniques and long-term follow-up. The case underscores the importance of considering gynecological involvement in female infants with irreducible inguinal masses. Early diagnosis and intervention still essential to avoid complications.
