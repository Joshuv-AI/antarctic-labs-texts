Retinoic acid is a translation inhibitor against chikungunya virus both in vitro and invivo
Authors: Yibo Chen, Zhiwei He, Ke Zhang, Xijing Qian, Yangang Liu, Xu Zheng, Ping Zhao, Zhongtian Qi, Cuiling Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), a mosquito-borne alphavirus, causes debilitating febrile and arthritic disease and remains a persistent public health threat in tropical and subtropical regions, with no clinically approved antiviral drugs currently available, which underscores the urgent need for targeted and effective therapeutic interventions. Through high-throughput screening of an FDA-approved compound library, we identified retinoic acid (RA) as a broad-spectrum inhibitor of multiple arboviruses, exhibiting potent activity against CHIKV. Time-of-addition experiments, together with assays on viral binding, endocytosis, membrane fusion, replication and translation, were performed to determine the specific lifecycle stages inhibited by RA. Notably, RA exerts anti-CHIKV effects by selectively targeting eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4B (EIF4B), thereby disrupting the viral translation, as revealed by limited proteolysis-mass spectrometry (LiP-MS). And, our results demonstrated that RA administration exerted potent protective effects against CHIKV infection in vivo. Specifically, RA significantly reduced cerebral pathological damage, relieved clinical manifestations, and enhanced survival in a murine model of CHIKV-induced encephalitis, while also markedly attenuating footpad swelling and joint pathological alterations in a CHIKV-induced arthritis mouse model. Collectively, our findings highlight RA as a promising anti-CHIKV candidate targeting EIF4B, supporting its further development as a therapeutic agent against CHIKV infection.

Introduction

Chikungunya fever (CHIKF) is an acute febrile illness caused by Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), a mosquito-borne alphavirus of the family Togaviridae (Bartholomeeusen et al., 2023). Since its first identification in 1952, CHIKV has triggered recurrent outbreaks across Africa, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and beyond, posing a growing public health threat worldwide (De Lima Cavalcanti et al., 2022). Clinical manifestations typically include abrupt fever, often accompanied by arthralgia or arthritis, rash, myalgia, and headache (Silva and Dermody, 2017), with a low fatality rate (<10%) (Ng and Rénia, 2024). Despite substantial morbidity and socioeconomic burden, no specific antiviral therapy against CHIKV is available, and management remains supportive care (Freppel et al., 2024). This critical unmet clinical gap underscores the urgent need for targeted and effective therapeutic interventions against CHIKV.

The genome of CHIKV is a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA, approximately 12 kb, which encodes four non-structural proteins (nsp1–4) and five structural proteins (C, E1, E2, E3, and 6K) (Wang et al., 2024). Containing a 5’ cap structure, the genomic RNA itself functions as mRNA for the translation of viral proteins (Law et al., 2023). As with other positive-strand RNA viruses, CHIKV translation is a host-dependent process that requires eukaryotic initiation factors (EIFs) (Bhardwaj et al., 2019). Among these, EIF4B facilitates ribosomal scanning and start-codon recognition (Burgess et al., 2022), and enhances the helicase activity of EIF4A to promote translation initiation (Andreou et al., 2017), making it a potential target for antiviral intervention.

Drug repurposing represents a strategic and efficient pathway for antiviral discovery. Retinoic acid (RA), also called all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA), is the biologically active derivative of vitamin A (Schubert and Germain, 2023) and a key mediator of growth and development (Liang et al., 2021). RA exerts its biological functions via activating nuclear receptors, namely retinoic acid receptors (RARs) and retinoid X receptors (RXRs), which serve as key regulators of cell cycle progression, differentiation, proliferation and apoptosis (Petkovich and Chambon, 2022). Clinically, RA is utilized for the management of dermatological disorders including ichthyosis, psoriasis and acne. It has achieved its most remarkable clinical success in acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), in which it markedly elevates complete remission rates through the induction of cellular differentiation (Liang et al., 2021). Moreover, RA displays broad-spectrum antiviral activity against a broad range of viral pathogens, including human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B virus (HBV), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and herpes simplex virus (HSV). The underlying mechanism of this antiviral activity is largely ascribed to the ability of RA to potentiate both the innate and adaptive immune responses (Bang et al., 2019; Han and Jang, 2023). Additionally, studies have demonstrated that RA inhibits viral entry and subsequent infection by binding to the spike (S) protein of SARS-CoV-2 (Tong et al., 2022). Thus, RA exerts inhibitory effects on viral infection via multiple mechanisms across diverse viral pathogens.

In the present study, we screened a library of FDA-approved drugs and identified RA as a highly potent inhibitor of CHIKV infection. Mechanistic investigations demonstrated that RA binds directly to the host translational initiation factor EIF4B, thus selectively abrogating CHIKV protein synthesis. Given the ubiquitous expression of EIF4B across eukaryotic cells, this targeted inhibition of viral translation renders RA a highly promising therapeutic candidate for the prevention and treatment of CHIKV infection.

Results

RA exhibits dose-dependent inhibitory effects against CHIKV infection

Via high-throughput screening of an FDA-approved compound library, we identified that retinoic acid (RA) exerted robust anti-CHIKV activity (Fig. 1A). We then evaluated its antiviral efficacy and cytotoxicity across a range of concentrations in Vero, SH-SY5Y, and HuH-7 cells. RA exhibited a notable dose-dependent inhibition against CHIKV infection, with half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 1.894 μM in SH-SY5Y, 0.782 μM in HuH-7, and 1.595 μΜ in Vero, respectively (Fig. 1B and C). RA suppresses CHIKV infection via blocking viral RNA translation

To pinpoint the stage of the CHIKV life cycle targeted by RA, we performed a time-of-addition assay. SH-SY5Y, HuH-7, and Vero cells were infected with CHIKV and treated with RA during distinct 2 h intervals, spanning from 2 h pre-infection to 8 h post-infection (hpi) (Fig. 2). The antiviral activity of RA was assessed by immunofluorescence (IF) staining, plaque assays, and RT-qPCR (Fig. 2A–D). RA treatment initiated as early as 2 h prior to infection (−2 hpi) or as late as 6 hpi still significantly inhibited viral infection, as evidenced by decreased percentages of CHIKV-positive cells and reduced viral titers in the supernatants (Fig. 2B and C). Notably, RA retained substantial antiviral activity even when added at 6 hpi, indicating that its mechanism of action does not depend exclusively on modulating the host cellular microenvironment prior to viral entry or early replication. Strikingly, the most potent antiviral effect was observed when RA was added within the 2–4 hpi window, leading to the lowest numbers of infected cells, the most pronounced reduction in progeny virus production, and the greatest suppression of viral RNA levels (Fig. 2B–D). To delineate the specific CHIKV entry steps targeted by RA, we subsequently assessed the effects of RA on viral endocytosis and membrane fusion. The impact of RA on CHIKV internalization was evaluated via an established proteinase K (PK) assay, followed by RT-qPCR analysis (Fig. 3A). Briefly, SH-SY5Y cells were allowed to bind CHIKV on ice for 2 h. Unbound virus was removed, and the cells were shifted to 37 °C for 2 h in medium containing RA to permit internalization. Extracellular, non-internalized virions were then digested with proteinase K before quantifying the intracellular viral RNA. No significant difference was observed in CHIKV RNA levels by RT-qPCR following RA treatment in this assay, demonstrating that CHIKV internalization was not impaired by RA treatment (Fig. 3E).

Subsequently, DiO-labeled CHIKV (CHIKVDiO), a fluorescent reporter that exhibits dequenching upon fusion with host membranes, was employed to detect whether RA affected the CHIKV membrane fusion process. As shown in Fig. 3F, the dynamic detection of the DiO fluorescence change confirmed the fact that, in contrast to the vacuolar ATPase inhibitor bafilomycin A1, RA did not impede the CHIKV membrane fusion process.

Given that RA did not impair CHIKV entry, we subsequently investigated whether it impairs viral replication. To this end, we constructed a single-round CHIKV-LR2006 replicon plasmid harboring an enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) reporter gene (LR2006-EGFP). In vitro-transcribed replicon RNA was transfected into SH-SY5Y cells, which were then treated with RA at the concentration matching its predetermined IC50. EGFP fluorescent signal expression, which is dependent on viral RNA translation and replication, was assessed 24 h post transfection. As presented in Fig. 3G, RA treatment potently suppressed EGFP expression driven by the CHIKV replicon, demonstrating that RA exerts inhibitory effects on viral gene expression at the post-entry stage.

To distinguish whether RA affects initial translation of the nonstructural proteins (nsPs) or subsequent RNA replication, we further constructed a mutant replicon. In this construct (LR2006-EGFP-GAA), the essential GDD motif in the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) domain of nsp4 was replaced with GAA, abolishing RNA replication while leaving translation of the non-structural polyprotein intact. As shown in Fig. 3H, RA treatment potently suppressed EGFP fluorescence expression from this LR2006-EGFP-GAA replicon in SH-SY5Y cells. This result definitively demonstrates that the primary antiviral mechanism of RA is the inhibition of CHIKV translation.

RA inhibits CHIKV translation by directly targeting EIF4B

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which RA inhibits CHIKV translation, limited proteolysis-coupled mass spectrometry (LiP-MS) was used to identify its direct cellular target. This technique detects ligand-induced protein conformational changes by quantifying protease protection of specific cleavage sites. LiP-MS of SH-SY5Y cell lysates was performed under three conditions: untreated control (SY group), treated with a low concentration of RA (20 μM; SY_20 μM group), and treated with a high concentration of RA (100 μM; SY_100 μM group). Comparative analysis identified proteins and peptides that exhibited differential abundance across these groups (Fig. 4A and B). Trend clustering of all identified peptides highlighted a subset with a consistent up-regulation profile across the RA concentration gradient. From this subset, EIF4B emerged as one of the top five candidate for RA binding based on statistical significance (Fig. 4D and E). To further validate the interaction between EIF4B and RA, we performed a drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay. DARTS is a relatively rapid and direct approach for assessing whether a small-molecule compound binds to a target protein. The principle of this method is that compound binding enhances the stability of the target protein, reduces its susceptibility to proteases, and thereby protects the protein from proteolytic degradation (Ren et al., 2021). Recombinant EIF4B protein was pre-incubated with RA prior to pronase digestion. The protective effect of RA against proteolytic degradation of EIF4B was then assessed by Western blotting. Our results demonstrated that RA conferred concentration-dependent protection to EIF4B from pronase-mediated proteolysis. This observation is consistent with our LiP-MS data, providing direct biochemical evidence that RA physically interacts with EIF4B (Fig. 4F).

Given its central role in translation initiation, we hypothesized that EIF4B is functionally required for the antiviral activity of RA. To investigate its functional relevance, we performed loss- and gain-of-function experiments on SH-SY5Y cells. Compared with cells transfected with a non-targeting control siRNA (siNC), knockdown of EIF4B with specific siRNA (siEIF4B) significantly attenuated the antiviral effect of RA against CHIKV infection (Fig. 4G–I). Conversely, overexpression of EIF4B potentiated the anti-CHIKV activity of RA (Fig. 4J–L). Collectively, these data demonstrate that RA suppresses CHIKV translation by functionally engaging the host translation initiation factor EIF4B.

RA protects mice from CHIKV infection

To evaluate the antiviral efficacy of RA in vivo, a C57BL/6 mouse model of lethal CHIKV infection was utilized. Six-week-old male mice were intranasally inoculated with 102 PFU of the CHIKV ROSS strain to achieve over 80% mortality. Mice were treated daily via oral gavage with RA (4 mg/kg) or vehicle (DMSO) from one day before infection to 5 days post-infection (dpi) and monitored daily for 12 days (Fig. 5A). Compared with the vehicle-treated group (ROSS + DMSO), in which mice developed progressive weight loss at 3 dpi and exhibited characteristic neurological signs (piloerection, trembling, hunched posture), the RA-treated mice (ROSS + RA) sustained normal weight gain, remained active throughout the observation period, and exhibited a 100% survival rate, and showed lower viral titers in the brain at 6 dpi (Fig. 5B–D).Fig. 5RA treatment protects mice from CHIKV infection. To further characterize the impact of RA on CHIKV-induced pathology, mice were humanely euthanized at 3, 6, 9 dpi, and inflammatory pathology in the brain was assessed by HE-stained brain sections. Blinded histological analysis demonstrated that RA-treated infected mice with repeated dosing exhibited significantly reduced pathological damage at 6 and 9 dpi (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, characteristic histopathological alterations, including neuronal necrosis, spongiosis, gliosis, and capillary congestion, were prominent in vehicle-treated controls but substantially attenuated by RA treatment (Fig. 5F). We further evaluated the antiviral efficacy of RA in a murine model of CHIKV-induced arthritis. Six-week-old male mice were inoculated in the hind footpad with 102 PFU of the CHIKV NAY strain. From −1 to 5 dpi, RA (4 mg/kg) or vehicle (DMSO) was administered daily by oral gavage. Body weight and footpad swelling were monitored daily. Mice in both groups showed steady weight gain (Fig. 5G); however, RA treatment significantly reduced footpad swelling compared with the vehicle-treated group (NAY + DMSO) (Fig. 5H). At the peak of swelling (6 dpi), footpad tissues were collected for RT-qPCR and H&E staining. The histopathological score and viral titers of the RA-treated group (NAY + RA) were significantly lower than that of DMSO-treated (Fig. 5I and J). In the DMSO-treated group, extensive myocyte necrosis was observed in the muscle tissue of the footpad, and infiltration of a few granulocytes and numerous lymphocytes and macrophages, extensive edema, and loose arrangement of connective tissue were noted. Many blood vessels exhibited adhesion and extravasation of inflammatory cells (Fig. 5K). In contrast, RA treatment markedly ameliorated all the above pathological changes (Fig. 5K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that RA confers potent protective effects against CHIKV infection and associated pathogenesis in vivo.

RA exhibits broad-spectrum antiviral activity against multiple arboviruses

To clarify the breadth of antiviral activity of RA on other arboviruses, we evaluated its efficacy against a panel of arboviruses, including West Nile virus (WNV), Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), Zika virus (ZIKV), tick‑borne encephalitis virus (TBEV), and dengue virus (DENV) in HuH-7 cells by dose-response assays. RA treatment potently and dose-dependently inhibited the infection of WNV, JEV, ZIKV, TBEV, and DENV in HuH-7 cells. The IC50 values were 9.469 μM for WNV, 7.633 μM for JEV, 10.42 μM for ZIKV, 5.183 μM for TBEV, and 12.73 μM for DENV (Fig. 6). Discussion

Arthropod-borne viruses (arboviruses) represent a persistent and escalating global health challenge, fueled by a confluence of factors including climate change, unplanned urbanization, and increased international travel and mobility (Liang et al., 2015; Côrtes et al., 2023; De Almeida et al., 2025). Among these, CHIKV has re-emerged as a significant human pathogen, causing large-scale outbreaks characterized by high attack rates and debilitating arthralgia that can persist for months to years (Khongwichit et al., 2021). In recent years, CHIKV transmission has expanded beyond its traditional tropical reservoirs, leading to autochthonous cases in temperate regions and underscoring its epidemic potential (Wang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026). Despite its clinical significance, no specific antiviral therapy is currently available for CHIKV infection. Management remains supportive, highlighting an urgent need for effective therapeutic strategies.

Drug repurposing offers a distinct development advantage by building upon established safety profiles, thereby reducing late-stage failure risks (Pushpakom et al., 2019; Ng et al., 2021). Furthermore, repurposed drugs can elucidate novel biological targets and pathways, extending their scientific value beyond the immediate indication (Pushpakom et al., 2019). In this study, RA (a repurposed agent), an already clinically used compound, presents a compelling anti-CHIKV candidate. Its established safety and tolerability in humans lower the barriers for rapid translation into antiviral use. Regarding analogs, several synthetic retinoids, including isotretinoin, acitretin, and bexarotene, have been developed with improved metabolic stability or receptor selectivity. As our next step, we will investigate their antiviral efficacy both in vitro and in vivo.

The CHIKV life cycle is dependent on the efficient translation of its positive-sense RNA genome following viral entry into the host cell cytoplasm. This early translational stage is critical to the establishment of infection and the production of viral replicase components. Agents that disrupt viral translation can potently abrogate subsequent viral replication and dissemination, thereby representing a potent and proximal therapeutic target for antiviral intervention. In contrast to late-stage antiviral inhibitors, viral translation inhibitors can mitigate the accumulation of viral proteins and RNA prior to extensive host cell reprogramming. EIF4B plays a critical role in cap-dependent translation initiation by facilitating the binding of the EIF4F complex to mRNA (Merrick, 2015) and promoting the helicase activity of EIF4A (Andreou et al., 2017; Shuvalova et al., 2025). Numerous viruses, including CHIKV, utilize the host cellular translation machinery to drive viral protein synthesis (Burgess et al., 2022). By targeting EIF4B, RA effectively uncouples host translational machinery from viral templates, thereby suppressing CHIKV protein synthesis without globally shutting off cellular translation. This selectivity preserves host cell function while exerting antiviral effects, highlighting the therapeutic advantage of targeting virus-specific host dependencies.

Our in vivo results strongly support the therapeutic potential of RA against CHIKV infection, while the specific mechanisms of action in different disease models remain to be elucidated. In the lethal encephalitis model, RA treatment significantly improved survival and ameliorated neuropathological manifestations, while in the arthritis model, RA alleviated footpad swelling and tissue pathology. These observations suggest that RA may exert its antiviral activity through multiple pathways, possibly including direct inhibition of viral translation as demonstrated in vitro, as well as immunomodulatory effects that limit excessive inflammation and tissue damage. The use of two complementary animal models (neurotropic and arthritogenic) strengthens the translational relevance of our findings, given that CHIKV infection in humans typically presents with arthralgia but can also involve neurological complications. However, it should be acknowledged that the intranasal inoculation route in the encephalitis model does not fully recapitulate the natural mosquito-borne transmission. Future studies using subcutaneous or intravenous infection routes, as well as evaluating RA in immunocompetent aged or humanized mouse models, would further validate its clinical applicability. Additionally, whether RA directly targets EIF4B in vivo and whether this interaction accounts for the observed protection remains to be confirmed by tissue-specific knockdown or knockout experiments. Despite these limitations, our results demonstrate that RA is capable of significantly suppressing CHIKV infection at the animal level, supporting its further development as a potential therapeutic candidate.

Notably, RA exhibited inhibitory activity against a panel of arboviruses beyond CHIKV, including JEV, WNV, ZIKV and DENV. This broad-spectrum antiviral activity indicates a conserved molecular mechanism centered on the host cellular translational initiation complex. Many arboviruses harbor structured 5′UTRs or cap-independent translation elements that depend on host cellular RNA-binding proteins, including EIF4B, to mediate efficient ribosome recruitment. Thus, EIF4B may serve as a potential conserved host factor hijacked by a broad range of RNA viruses to facilitate efficient viral translational activity. RA-mediated disruption of this host-virus interaction offers a mechanistic rationale for its broad-spectrum antiviral activity. Further investigation into the structural and functional interplay between EIF4B and distinct viral RNA motifs could elucidate a universal vulnerability in arbovirus replication.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identifies RA as a novel inhibitor of CHIKV replication that acts by targeting the host cellular translation initiation factor EIF4B. By disrupting viral protein synthesis at the early translational stage, RA potently suppresses CHIKV infection in vitro and exhibits broad-spectrum antiviral activity against a panel of arboviruses. Importantly, these in vitro findings are corroborated by in vivo evidence. In a lethal CHIKV mouse model, RA treatment significantly reduced mortality, while in a CHIKV-induced arthritis model, RA alleviated footpad swelling and ameliorated histopathological changes. Together, these findings validate the feasibility of novel host-directed antiviral strategies and underscore translational control as a promising therapeutic target for arboviral infections. Rational development and optimization of RA and its derivatives could yield much-needed antiviral therapeutics to combat CHIKV and other emerging and re-emerging arboviral pathogens.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and viruses

The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HuH-7 (SCSP-526, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China), African green monkey Vero cells (ATCC CCL-81), and human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells (ATCC CRL-2266) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Life Technologies, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1 × non-essential amino acids, 100 IU/mL streptomycin and penicillin, and 2 mM l-glutamine (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

The CHIKV NAY strain was isolated from the serum of a confirmed CHIKF patient in biosafety level 3 laboratory. The CHIKV ROSS strain was stored in our laboratory. The Zika virus (ZIKV), Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), and Tick-borne encephalitis virus (TBEV) were stored in our laboratory. The infectious cDNA clone of a West Nile virus (WNV) isolate WNNY 2000-crow3356 (GenBank: AF404756.1) was established in this laboratory. All flaviviruses were propagated in C6/36 cells and CHIKV was propagated in Vero cells. Viral titers were determined by plaque assay on Vero cell monolayers. Briefly, serially diluted virus inoculum was binding to cells for 2 h, after which the medium was replaced with a semi-solid overlay containing 1.5% carboxymethyl cellulose and 1% FBS. Following incubation for 2–3 days, cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet for plaque counting.

Immunofluorescence (IF) assay

Vero, SH-SY5Y, or HuH-7 cells seeded in 96-well plates were infected with CHIKV for 24 h. The cells were then fixed with methanol at −20 °C for 20 min. After removal of the supernatant, the cells were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, the cells were stained with the primary anti-CHIKV E1 antibody overnight at 4 °C and an Alexa Fluor™ 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI at room temperature for 10 min and then detected by the BioTek Lionheart FX Imaging Reader.

Cytotoxicity assay

The RA cytotoxicity was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent. Vero, SH-SY5Y, and HuH-7 cells seeded in 96-well plates were treated with serially diluted RA and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the supernatant was replaced with fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent, and the cells were further incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 1.5 h. The optical density (OD) of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The half-maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of RA was calculated for each cell line.

High-throughput screening of an FDA-approved compound library

HuH-7 cells were co-treated with 10 μM of each compound from a metabolism-focused library (Selleck, Cat# L3700, 3222 compounds) and CHIKV NAY at an MOI of 1. After 24 h, cells were fixed with methanol, and CHIKV infection was detected by IF assay. Antiviral activity was quantified as the percentage of CHIKV-positive cells normalized to DMSO controls, while cytotoxicity was assessed using CCK-8 reagent. Compounds achieving > 60% infection inhibition with no observable cytotoxicity were considered hits, leading to the identification of RA as a potential anti-CHIKV candidate.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

The RT-qPCR assay was conducted as previously described (He et al., 2025). The total cellular RNA was extracted from CHIKV-infected cells using RNAiso reagent (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the RNA was subjected to reverse transcription via a PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix kit (Takara, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed using the TB Green Premix Ex Taq™ kit (Takara, Japan) on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 system. GAPDH was utilized as an endogenous control for data normalization. The sequences of all primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Plaque assay

Confluent Vero cells in 24-well plates were infected with serially 10-fold diluted virus in DMEM with 2% FBS (2 h, 37 °C, with intermittent rocking). After removing the inoculum, cells were overlaid with carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) medium (1.5% CMC, 2% FBS) and incubated for 2–3 days. Monolayers were fixed and stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and plaques were counted.

Binding and internalization assay

For the binding assay, SH-SY5Y cells were incubated with CHIKV (MOI = 5) in the presence of RA at its IC50 concentration on ice for 2 h. The cells were then washed three times with pre-cold PBS to remove unbound viral particles. A portion of the infected cells was replenished with complete DMEM and further cultured at 37 °C for 24 h to assess the bound virions by IF assay, and the other portion of the infected cells were collected for total RNA extraction, and the level of CHIKV RNA was quantified by RT-qPCR.

For the internalization assay, SH-SY5Y cells were incubated with CHIKV (MOI = 5) on ice for 2 h. Then, the cells were replenished with complete DMEM with RA and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to allow for internalization. Subsequently, proteinase K was added to remove any non-internalized virus particles and the cells were collected. The internalized CHIKV RNA was quantified by RT-qPCR.

Membrane fusion assay

CHIKV (2 × 108 PFU/mL) was first labeled with DiO at a final concentration of 10 μM in the dark at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, SH-SY5Y or HuH-7 cells were infected with the DiO-labeled CHIKV in the presence of either RA (10 μM) or bafilomycin A1 (20 nM) as a control. The membrane fusion kinetics were monitored in real-time using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. The instrument temperature was maintained at 37 °C, and the DiO fluorescence intensity (excitation/emission: 484/501 nm) was recorded every 15 min for a total duration of 9 h. The data were used to plot the membrane fusion kinetic curve.

Replicon expression assay

The replicon plasmids of the CHIKV strain LR2006-OPY1 (carrying EGFP reporter gene) were synthesized as described (Ding et al., 2022; He et al., 2023). Detailed methods were described in Supplementary Data Files. The plasmids were then linearized by NotI and subjected to transcription assay using a HiScribe T7 ARCA mRNA Kit (New England Biolabs, USA). The viral replicon RNAs were then transfected into cells by Lipo2000. Six hours after transfection, the cells were treated with different doses of RA or DMSO for another 18 h. The expression of EGFP was captured using Cytation 5, and normalized to the DMSO group.

Limited proteolysis-mass spectrometry (LiP-MS)

SH-SY5Y cells were lysed, and 50 μg of total protein were treated with RA at low (20 μM; SY_20 μM group) or high (100 μM; SY_100 μM group) concentration, or with an equal volume of DMSO vehicle (untreated control, SY group), for 15 min at room temperature. The protein mixture was subjected to limited proteolysis by adding proteinase K at a 1:100 (w/w) enzyme-to-protein ratio. Digestion was allowed to proceed for 3 min at room temperature and then terminated by heating at 98 °C for 5 min. After cooling to room temperature, samples were digested with trypsin (1:50, w/w) overnight at 37 °C. Tryptic peptides were acidified with formic acid (final concentration 2%), centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was desalted, vacuum-dried, and analyzed by mass spectrometry.

Drug affinity responsive target stability assay (DARTS)

The DARTS experiment was carried out following a previously described protocol (Wu et al., 2025). In brief, recombinant EIF4B protein was first incubated with RA for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to digestion with pronase at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:1000 (0.1 μg pronase per 100 μg protein) for 30 min at 42 °C. The effect of RA on the stability of EIF4B was then assessed by Western Blotting analysis.

Western Blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation (10,000 ×g, 5 min, 4 °C), mixed with SDS loading buffer, and boiled (100 °C, 10 min). Equal amounts (20–40 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE (12.5% gel) and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (0.1% Tween-20) for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies (e.g., anti-EIF4B) overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (2 h, room temperature). Signals were visualized using chemiluminescence and quantified by ImageJ (normalized to loading control).

siRNA and plasmid transfection

Transfections were performed in 96-well plates using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, for each well, Lipofectamine 2000 was diluted in Opti-MEM and combined with an equal volume of Opti-MEM containing siRNA (final concentration 20 nM) or plasmid DNA (160 ng per well). After 15 min incubation at room temperature, the mixture was added to SH-SY5Y cells. Cells were then cultured for 48 h at 37 °C before subsequent infection with CHIKV.

Evaluation of in vivo infection

The in vivo experiment was conducted as previously described (Zheng et al., 2024). For the lethal model, six-week-old C57BL/6 mice were intranasally inoculated with 100 PFU of CHIKV-ROSS or an equivalent volume of PBS (serving as a mock-infected control). For the arthritis model, six-week-old C57BL/6 mice were injected subcutaneously with 100 PFU of CHIKV NAY or PBS into the left rear feet. Starting from one day prior to infection until day 5 post-infection (−1 to 5 dpi), the mice were administered RA or DMSO daily via oral gavage at a dose of 4 mg/kg/day. The general health status and body weight of the mice were monitored daily for a period of 10 days. At 3, 6, and 9 dpi, mice in the lethal group were humanely euthanized, and brain tissues were collected and fixed. Mice in the arthritis group were humanely euthanized, and ankle joint tissues were collected and fixed at 6 dpi. For histopathological analysis, the tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Pathological scoring was subsequently performed on the stained sections.

Statistical analysis

The continuous variables were shown as means and standard deviations (SD) in at least three independent experiments. The Student’s t-test or the nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparisons between two groups. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software and a 2-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
