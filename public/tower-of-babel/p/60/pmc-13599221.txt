Background

Equitable access to care depends on more than the formal presence of services. Health systems can look available on paper while still producing systematic differences in delay, continuity, and foregone care across geographically and socially defined groups [1–3].

The term medical desert sits inside this problem, but it does not yet refer to one stable concept. In some studies, it refers to structural scarcity of local services, often measured through provider supply or travel time. In others, it is used for waiting time, affordability, quality, or sociocultural barriers [4–7]. When the same term is used for both place-based scarcity and the full range of later barriers to care, it becomes hard to compare studies or to specify what a policy is trying to change.

Health equity research often requires three related analytic moves: defining an underserved place, measuring that place, and explaining why people living within the same place nevertheless experience access differently. When those moves are collapsed into a single label, structural scarcity and lived barriers become harder to disentangle.

This review uses rural service erosion in the United States as a high-stakes motivating case. Rural hospitals and emergency services remain central elements of care infrastructure in vulnerable communities, and their closure or conversion can alter referral pathways, continuity, and the meaning of local availability itself [8–10]. Recent mapping work also shows that hospital-desert risk is spatially uneven, with rural towns and metropolitan edges often facing sharp mismatches between demand and supply [11]. The U.S. case does not cover the whole problem of medical deserts, but it makes the gap between formal availability and usable care especially visible.

Related work from other settings likewise shows that healthcare deserts are not reducible to physician counts alone. Planning work in Nigeria, for example, treats service access, transport, and local infrastructure as linked parts of the same problem [12]. Structural scarcity and institutional credibility are closely linked in practice. Service loss changes the local supply of care, but it can also change local expectations about whether care will be dependable and worth seeking. A county that opens a new clinic has changed structural access. Whether residents use that clinic also depends on whether the institution is experienced as respectful, competent, and responsive. Medical desert therefore refers here to structural scarcity, while institutional credibility and medical mistrust explain uneven realized access within and across such places.

Methods

This article is a critical conceptual review. It brings together literature on medical deserts, service-specific desert terms, administrative underserved-area designations, access-to-care theory, rural service erosion, and medical mistrust across rural health, health services research, and health equity.

The review focuses on three linked questions: how medical desert is used across countries and service domains, how access theory informs a place-based definition, and how institutional credibility and medical mistrust shape realized access once care is nominally present.

Sources were identified through targeted searches in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, EBSCOhost, and Google Scholar, supplemented by citation chaining from key articles. Retained sources from database and Google Scholar searches were peer-reviewed sources published from 2010 onward; English-language sources were eligible, with one French-language source (Chevillard et al., 2018) included on the basis of its English-language abstract and the analytic relevance of its definitional content. Foundational sources establishing canonical access, trust, continuity, and mistrust concepts (Tanahashi 1978; Penchansky and Thomas 1981; Mechanic 1996; Hall et al. 2001; Gilson 2003; Haggerty et al. 2003; LaVeist et al. 2009) were identified through citation chaining and are cited as theoretical anchors. Searches were conducted in 2024 and 2025, and the search yield was reviewed in May 2026 prior to final manuscript revision. Search terms were organized around six domains: medical desert terminology, service-specific desert labels, administrative underserved-area designations, access theory, rural service erosion, and trust, trustworthiness, and medical mistrust. Terms included medical desert, healthcare desert, hospital desert, diagnostic desert, vascular desert, medically underserved area, health professional shortage area, rural health access, service availability, service stability, institutional trust, medical mistrust, health service coverage, and effective coverage. A summary of search domains, sources, example terms, and selection purpose is provided in Additional file 1.

Because desert terminology is inconsistent, the search included general, place-based, service-specific, and administrative labels. Sources were retained when they contributed to one or more analytic tasks: defining a desert or underserved place, operationalizing structural access or scarcity, explaining service instability or substitution after service loss, linking institutional trustworthiness to care use, or distinguishing mistrust from realized access. Priority was given to peer-reviewed conceptual, review, and empirical sources that made definitions, measures, or policy uses explicit. Sources were not retained when desert terminology was used only as metaphor or when the source did not include analytic discussion of access, scarcity, trust, mistrust, or realized use beyond passing mention.

Each retained source was read for five analytic features: the term used, the level of analysis, the access dimension emphasized, the measurement approach, and the explanatory or policy role assigned to the concept. These features were compared across literature domains to identify where terms overlapped, where they named different problems, and where place-level scarcity was being mixed with later access outcomes. The synthesis produced three outputs: the term distinctions in Table 1, the two-axis heuristic in Fig. 1, and the measurement domains in Table 2.

Why medical deserts need conceptual discipline

Medical desert is often asked to do three jobs at once: define a place, measure that place, and explain access outcomes. A mileage threshold, provider ratio, or utilization pattern may be useful for one of these jobs, but it does not cover all three [4–5, 13]. When that happens, the same term shifts between naming a place, operationalizing a place, and explaining behavior within that place. This collapse shapes what is measured, compared, and made accountable in health equity research [3].

Inconsistency is visible across countries and service domains. Brînzac and colleagues proposed a broad consensus definition that includes long waiting times, high costs, and sociocultural barriers; Flinterman and colleagues documented wide heterogeneity in indicators, service domains, and units of analysis; French and Spanish work likewise shows that the problem has been operationalized through different combinations of supply, accessibility, and territorial scale [4–7]. Work on European medical deserts also shows that workforce shortages, recruitment problems, and retention difficulties are often layered onto these definitional differences rather than analytically separated from them [14]. Rosik and colleagues further show that even modest methodological changes in service type, accessibility measure, or threshold choice can substantially change which municipalities are classified as healthcare deserts [13].

A second source of confusion is the mixing of related but different labels. Medical desert is usually a descriptive place concept. Service-specific terms such as diagnostic desert or vascular desert refer to narrower forms of structural scarcity tied to particular forms of care [15–16]. Administrative designations such as medically underserved areas or health professional shortage areas are policy categories with their own criteria. These labels are related, but they should be used for different analytic tasks.

Table 1 summarizes the distinctions used in this review. They keep medical desert focused on structural scarcity and help avoid a catch-all label for many different access problems.

Beyond proximity: what access theory already tells us

Access theory has long shown that care cannot be reduced to distance alone. Penchansky and Thomas described access as the fit between patients and the healthcare system, while later work emphasized access as an interface between health systems and populations [1, 17].

Tanahashi’s health service coverage framework is useful here because it treats coverage as a sequence of constraints between a target population and a service: availability coverage, accessibility coverage, acceptability coverage, contact coverage, and effectiveness coverage [18]. This helps locate medical deserts within a broader access pathway. Applied to medical deserts, this staged view places structural scarcity, including service stability and meaningful substitution, at the availability and accessibility end of the pathway. Institutional credibility and medical mistrust then help explain movement through acceptability, contact, and effectiveness coverage toward realized access.

Access theory broadens the analysis of care, but it does not remove the need for a clear place concept. Medical desert refers here to a structurally underserved place. Later barriers to care are analysed separately as part of realized access. On this basis, medical deserts are defined as places where needed health services are locally unavailable or ordinarily difficult to reach. Service stability and meaningful substitution after service loss are treated as part of structural assessment, while recognizing that these two elements still need further empirical testing.

Service stability matters because a formally open service may still function as fragile infrastructure when provider turnover, temporary closure, reduced operating hours, or repeated conversion disrupt continuity. Meaningful substitution matters because the loss of a local hospital, obstetric unit, pharmacy, or diagnostic service is not fully offset when alternatives require repeated long-distance travel, fragmented referrals, or unreliable transport links [8–10]. Ordinary distance measures remain necessary, but they often miss these additional structural problems.

This definition also avoids treating rurality as one stable exposure. Different definitions of rural identify different populations and places, and those choices shape who becomes visible in research and policy [19]. Recent French work also shows the value of a multi-professional first-contact perspective, where accessibility is assessed through the co-presence of general practitioners, other primary care providers, and emergency services [20].

Access theory and conceptual discipline work together here. Access theory expands the analysis of care experience. Conceptual discipline keeps medical desert useful as a place label.

U.S. rural service erosion as a motivating case

Rural service erosion in the United States illustrates why the distinction between structural access and lived access matters. Hospital closure does not simply increase drive time. It can reshape emergency pathways, weaken referral networks, reduce the local presence of clinicians, and destabilize expectations about where and when care will be available [8–10].

This literature also shows why service stability belongs inside structural assessment. A county may still contain some care infrastructure and yet function as a highly fragile access environment if services have recently closed, converted, merged, or narrowed their scope. In such settings, the relevant question is not only whether a building remains on the map, but whether the local care pathway still functions predictably for ordinary users.

Telehealth, mobile clinics, and targeted outreach can help. Mobile health clinics have been described as a way to widen access in underserved U.S. settings [21], and telemedicine improved follow-up for paediatric sickle cell care in a rural medically underserved area [22]. Patients in a resource-poor setting also rated short-term mobile medical aid as helpful [23]. Hospital-based behavioral-health programs in medically underserved areas also show that local adaptation can widen access to structured support [24]. These approaches are valuable as access supports and should be assessed alongside continuity-dependent primary care, emergency readiness, and broad diagnostic capacity. Continuity here refers to informational, management, and relational continuity across episodes of care [25].

Everyday primary care barriers in medically underserved areas look similar. In a Tennessee community, diabetic patients with multimorbidity who lacked a primary care provider were more likely to delay needed care [26]. In Delaware shortage areas, average wait times to see a primary care physician rose sharply over time, underscoring how administrative shortage designations and real appointment access do not always align [27]. These examples show how unstable supply becomes visible to patients as delayed care, broken continuity, and foregone treatment.

Institutional credibility, medical mistrust, and uneven realized access

Institutional credibility of local care is used here as a working analytic term drawn from literature on institutional trust and trustworthiness [28–30]. It refers to system-side properties of care environments: dependability, accountability, respect, responsiveness, and transparency.

Medical mistrust, by contrast, refers to suspicion that healthcare institutions, systems, or actors may not act in a patient’s or community’s best interests [31–33]. This distinction is important in medical desert research because structural scarcity and relational harm often appear together.

The language of institutional credibility also keeps attention on institutions rather than placing the problem mainly in patients. Terms such as psychological barriers can suggest that avoidance or delay begins inside the individual. Research on trust in organizations points instead to the experience of institutions as competent, fair, communicative, and willing to respond to needs or complaints [28, 30, 34].

Historical and group-specific harms also matter. Medical mistrust is tied to coercive histories, discrimination, disrespect, and repeated experiences of unequal treatment [31–35].

The same structurally underserved place can therefore be experienced very differently. Rural women in one study described healthcare barriers not simply as geographic inconveniences but as moral failings, with stories of poor encounters and disrespect moving through trusted local networks [36]. Qualitative work with a state-recognised Indigenous tribe similarly identified long distance to specialists, poor communication, long waits, unmet mental health needs, and gaps in prevention and health education as connected infrastructure barriers [37].

Structural scarcity and institutional credibility also interact over time. A clinic closure changes local supply. Repeated closures, poor communication, or unresolved complaints can also teach residents that local care is unreliable. A new clinic may shorten travel distance, but it does not automatically repair those expectations. Vicarious and mediated experiences matter as well, because stories of service loss, disrespect, or neglect can circulate through families, communities, and media and shape whether care is seen as usable or worth seeking [36, 38].

Conceptual heuristic: structural access and institutional credibility

Figure 1 presents a two-axis conceptual heuristic. One axis captures structural access to needed services: local availability, ordinary reachability, service stability, and meaningful substitution after service loss. The other axis captures institutional credibility of local care: whether local services are experienced as dependable, respectful, accountable, and responsive.

The figure is a conceptual heuristic. Empirical validation is needed before its quadrant labels can be treated as a typology. Its quadrant labels are working labels. The desert quadrants mark low structural access. Low-credibility care environments can also exist where services are geographically present, and these settings can still generate delay, discontinuity, and avoidance.

Taken together, the two axes show why structure and experience need to be studied jointly without being collapsed into one term. They also allow movement over time. Closures, conversions, workforce churn, communication failures, or repeated accountability failures can push a place toward compounded disadvantage. Service restoration and stronger institutional responsiveness can move it in the other direction.

Implications for measurement, research, and policy

The first implication is measurement discipline. Studies should state clearly whether they are defining medical deserts, operationalizing them, or explaining outcomes within them. Structural measures should focus on service availability, ordinary reachability, service stability, and substitution. Explanatory measures should then address the relational and organizational conditions that shape whether people can actually use care once it is nominally present [3, 13].

Table 2 outlines a multilevel measurement agenda. Structural access is measured at the place or service-area level. Institutional credibility is measured at the institution or community level. Medical mistrust is measured at the individual or group level. Realized access is measured through outcomes such as delayed care, foregone care, broken referrals, continuity loss, and emergency-only reliance.

Service-specific desert concepts remain useful when they are treated as targeted labels rather than as substitutes for a general medical desert concept. Diagnostic desert and vascular desert are clear examples because they identify narrower service shortages and help target interventions more precisely [15–16].

A second implication concerns study design. Because residents within the same county or service area may face different access environments, future work should combine place-level indicators with institution-level and individual-level measures. Qualitative and community-engaged research remains especially important for identifying how service instability, poor communication, and organizational history shape trust and willingness to seek care [36–37, 39].

Policy needs two linked tracks. One track restores or stabilizes services. The other improves the institutional practices that make care dependable, respectful, and worth using. Communities with unstable or absent services need infrastructure repair. Communities with poor communication, weak accountability, or repeated experiences of dismissal also need credibility repair. Workforce policy remains part of both tracks: evidence from European medical deserts and medically underserved areas points to overlapping strategies that combine educational pipelines, professional support, and retention measures rather than single incentives alone [14, 40–41].

An empirical next step is to assess whether structural access and institutional credibility are empirically distinguishable and whether their interaction predicts delayed care, foregone care, continuity loss, and preventive care uptake. Evidence from preventive services in medically underserved areas already suggests that provider communication, time, cost, and transportation can shape uptake even when a service is nominally available [42]. Longitudinal designs are especially needed because service instability, organizational reputation, and mistrust all change over time [10, 38].

Limitations

This review has four limitations. First, its U.S.-anchored framing uses rural service erosion as a motivating case, so the proposed distinctions require testing in other health-system settings. Second, institutional credibility is used as a working analytic term and needs further operationalization. Third, the heuristic is conceptual and requires empirical assessment. Future studies should test whether structural access and institutional credibility are empirically distinguishable and how their interaction predicts delayed care, foregone care, continuity loss, and preventive care uptake. Fourth, service stability and meaningful substitution are introduced as part of structural assessment but need further operational specification before they can be consistently measured across settings.

Conclusions

Medical desert is most useful when it stays anchored in structural scarcity: inadequate local availability, ordinary reachability, service stability, and meaningful substitution of needed health services.

Institutional credibility and medical mistrust then explain why the same place can produce different patterns of delay, discontinuity, foregone care, and preventive care uptake across groups.

This distinction can sharpen policy targeting. Structural desert mapping can help identify where services need to be restored, stabilized, or replaced, while credibility assessment can help identify where communication, accountability, and relational repair are most urgent.

An important empirical next step is a multilevel design that links place-level structural access, institution-level credibility, group- or individual-level mistrust, and outcomes such as delayed care, continuity loss, and preventive care uptake.

If future research shows that structural scarcity and credibility collapse into one reproducible empirical pattern, a broader reformulation of medical desert may become warranted. At present, keeping the place concept structurally bounded offers clearer comparison, cleaner measurement, and more precise policy use.
