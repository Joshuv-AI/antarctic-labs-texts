AI-augmented geothermal model for scalable energy uncertainties in buildings
Authors: A Markowitz, R Abuaamoud, S Ben Ayed, A Rupe, R Yang, T Kliphuis, V Vesselinov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rise of artificial intelligence in urban computing, particularly in response to power crises caused by extreme events, demands fast simulations that balance physical accuracy with computational efficiency. This work presents a scalable software tool for predicting geothermal energy use in residential buildings. The tool uses a reduced-order model to estimate energy consumption and benchmarks the results against EnergyPlus simulations. Three distinct methods, Latin Hypercube Sampling, Saltelli, and eFast, were used to generate parameter datasets to explore model sensitivity to input parameters. The simulated outputs were then analyzed using the Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm, a machine learning approach based on gradient-boosted decision trees. The trained model achieved near-perfect accuracy in predicting energy usage. This software represents a significant step toward scalable, computationally efficient urban energy modeling and analysis.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Urban environments influence the global energy and emissions landscape, accounting for approximately 75% of anthropogenic CO₂ emissions and 78% of total energy consumption, while occupying less than 2% of Earth’s land surface1. These proportions are expected to rise with accelerating urbanization and industrialization, highlighting the imperative to curtail cities’ environmental and energy footprints. Proposed mitigation strategies encompass biodiversity conservation, greenhouse gas abatement, urban heat island reduction, and, critically, the shift toward renewable energy deployment. The successful implementation of these measures depends on advanced methodologies for data quantification and analysis to forecast energy-related urban processes. Accordingly, this paper investigates the application of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) techniques within building energy simulation frameworks.

Urban AI has emerged as a game-changing framework for urban energy modeling by combining artificial intelligence and machine learning to drive sustainability improvements. It tackles escalating energy demands and environmental pressures in cities by ingesting real-time data streams, deploying machine learning algorithms, and leveraging cloud-based infrastructures to sharpen forecasts and refine energy management strategies. In contrast, conventional simulation engines such as EnergyPlus2 and TRNSYS3 are widely adopted for detailed building energy and system analyses but demand substantial computational resources and lack seamless real-time operation. Developing a dynamic modeling tool within an Urban AI ecosystem presents technical challenges yet offers the flexibility and scalability required to address the complexity of modern urban energy systems. A further limitation of conventional simulation platforms is their inability to represent interbuilding energy dynamics and urban-scale interactions. Buildings are typically modeled as independent entities, excluding essential interdependencies such as shared energy infrastructures, mutual shading effects, and thermal exchanges, both conductive and radiative, between adjacent facades. This compartmentalized framework impedes accurate simulation of district energy systems and smart-grid operations, which depend on cohesive energy flows and coordinated resource management4,5.

Artificial intelligence is rapidly becoming a cornerstone of urban-energy strategies, lowering consumption and emissions by fine-tuning household loads while coordinating city-scale assets such as smart-grid controls and EV-charging infrastructure6. However, conventional building- and urban-energy models were built for static inputs; they struggle to absorb real-time data, adapt to changing conditions, or scale reliably to entire districts, especially when local micro-climate effects must be captured7. Machine-learning techniques help bridge this gap. Zhou & Chang, for example, evaluated 12 algorithms to classify more than 3,700 Beijing buildings and found that a Gradient-Boosted Decision Tree delivered the best results, exceeding 90% precision and recall, demonstrating ML’s power to automate complex, city-wide analyses8.

Exploration of diverse AI and ML methodologies within building energy simulations is vital for advancing sustainability, cost efficiency, and energy conservation in contemporary building operations. AI-driven approaches facilitate continuous learning from historical performance data and enable real‐time optimization and adaptive control strategies that static models cannot provide9. The expanding application of machine learning in urban building energy analysis encompasses forecasting and identifying consumption patterns9,10. Reference9 offers a systematic review of ML techniques for energy forecasting, highlighting deficiencies in urban‐scale studies and the necessity of integrating climate variables. In contrast10, employs ten ML algorithms to characterize gas and electricity usage in London residences, revealing that electricity consumption is influenced by a broader array of physical and social factors and that spatial analysis can pinpoint areas with atypical energy behaviors. These investigations collectively demonstrate AI and ML’s potential to deliver nuanced insights into energy use trends, thereby informing optimization strategies and urban planning decisions when integrated with building energy simulation frameworks.

In the realm of urban energy modeling, machine learning has brought about a paradigm shift, moving beyond the limitations of conventional physics-based techniques and towards data-driven approaches. A critical contribution to this field is the Data-driven Urban Energy Simulation (DUE-S) framework introduced by11, which integrates machine learning with traditional simulation methods to capture the complex interdependencies between urban buildings, addressing factors such as shading and thermal dynamics often overlooked. Ensemble-based machine learning models have further improved the precision of energy consumption forecasts across multiple spatial dimensions12.

The diverse applications of machine learning in energy modeling rely on supervised and unsupervised learning methods, each offering unique advantages. Techniques like principal component analysis (PCA) and clustering, which fall under unsupervised learning, excel in preprocessing data and extracting meaningful features13. explored these techniques to investigate sustainability issues and understand urban dynamics more comprehensively. On the other hand, supervised learning methods such as gradient boosting and support vector machines (SVMs) have demonstrated superior predictive accuracy compared to traditional linear models14. underscored the effectiveness of these methods in simulating gas and electricity usage on an urban scale. This study aligns with existing research, reinforcing the potential of ML in urban energy analysis while emphasizing the importance of choosing the right model for different energy prediction tasks. The findings also support integrating advanced ML techniques in urban thermal models. By leveraging transfer machine learning, a potential thermal model may incorporate these insights to optimize predictive accuracy across cities and enable more effective urban energy planning.

An essential aspect of AI/ML software in building energy simulations is how it may be used to emphasize computational efficiency. Unlike traditional energy models that require extensive parameter tuning, ML-based methods such as transfer learning enable models to generalize across different building types and climatic conditions with minimal retraining15. This capability is handy for urban-scale energy simulations, where buildings exhibit diverse thermal behaviors influenced by location-specific factors. Transfer learning has been applied in various ML applications for urban energy management, including district heating optimization and HVAC system control. By integrating transfer learning mechanisms, AI/ML software in building energy simulations can rapidly adapt to different environments, reducing the need for extensive labeled datasets while maintaining predictive accuracy. Research has shown that integrating energy performance with structural resilience in building energy retrofitting can optimize sustainability and cost-effectiveness16–19. When combined with AI/ML tools, building energy simulations can simulate heating and cooling demands in different urban environments, identify optimal energy interventions, and improve energy performance predictions in retrofitted buildings.

One of the key challenges in ML-driven energy modeling is ensuring that surrogate models maintain fidelity to fundamental thermodynamic principles. Science-informed machine learning (SIML) approaches have emerged to address this challenge by integrating physics-based constraints within ML frameworks20. Following this trajectory by incorporating established principles of thermodynamics and fluid mechanics ensures that predictions remain interpretable and physically consistent for results for a potential energy simulation software that aims to incorporate AI/ML. This approach mirrors development in other domains, such as climate and renewable energy modeling, where hybrid physics-ML models improve forecast accuracy while preserving underlying scientific laws. Within this framework, geothermal energy, and particularly geothermal heat pumps21, have lately received strong attention as a viable option to reduce environmental greenhouse gas emissions. These studies have introduced computationally expensive models that may be unsuitable for massive ML training simulations. Moreover, several uncertainties arise in the geothermal parameters used for the simulation that may introduce significant errors in predicting seasonal heating/cooling loads. In this context, we propose to develop a physics-based ML platform that effectively predicts geothermal energy use in buildings.

Traditional building energy models’ high computational cost and complexity have been a significant bottleneck, restricting detailed analysis to single buildings. This paper introduces the Building Energy Replica Tool (BERT) and a corresponding analytical workflow to overcome this critical barrier. We demonstrate how BERT’s computational efficiency, advanced sensitivity analysis, and machine learning integration provide a scalable and actionable framework for the data-driven urban planning necessary to meet modern energy challenges.

Energy modeling framework

A thermal model capable of handling energy, environmental, and infrastructure data in near real-time is required to address energy consumption challenges. Integrating existing and newly created physical and machine learning models to accurately forecast urban processes, the used energy model needs to be fast and easily compiled by machine learning algorithms. We choose an RC-circuit reduced-order thermal model. The model takes climate data and building properties as inputs. It simulates the cooling and heating load demand with a geothermal heat pump over time. Compared to other thermal modeling methods, the RC-Circuit model is simple, accurate, and efficient, making rapid scaling and extrapolation of the model possible and ensuring later sensitivity analyses are performant. The RC-circuit model is considered simple because it reduces the complex thermal behavior of a building to a small set of differential equations using physically meaningful parameters, thermal resistances, and capacitances, requiring fewer inputs than full-scale simulation tools22,23. Its accuracy has been validated in multiple studies, showing it can reliably estimate heating and cooling loads within a few percent error when properly calibrated, even when compared to high-fidelity simulation platforms like EnergyPlus or ESP-r24. The model’s computational efficiency, derived from its low order and algebraic structure, enables rapid simulations over long time horizons or large building stocks. It is ideal for scaling up to city-wide analyses or running thousands of parametric variations25. These qualities also make RC models especially well-suited for sensitivity analysis, where many simulations are required to test the effects of varying parameters such as insulation levels, glazing properties, or HVAC efficiencies. Because each run completes quickly and maintains stable numerical behavior, analysts can explore a vast design space without the performance bottlenecks typical of more detailed simulation methods26,27.

The building is modeled in Julia using the thermal network approach, as explained in Figs. 1 and 2 below28. The heat comes to the building through the envelope loads, including the wall layers and the windows’ conductive and radiative components, ventilation tempering the outside air temperature and humidity to the internal ones, and internal heat gains like people and equipment. The convective component of the incoming heat flux is assumed to feed the zone at temperature Tz directly. However, the radiative component is stored as thermal mass and converted later to the zone.

Model comparison with EnergyPlus

EnergyPlus30 incorporates state-of-the-art building energy modeling techniques and effectively captures the physics associated with air, moisture, and heat transfer. However, directly integrating EnergyPlus into intensive simulation platforms could be challenging due to significant architectural differences between EnergyPlus and the reduced-order model.

A simple case study is developed on both software to benchmark the thermal model. The study examines a 650-square-feet housing unit at New Mexico State University (NMSU) in Las Cruces, New Mexico, used for student and faculty housing, as shown in Fig. 3. The house features concrete block walls, a concrete block roof insulated with spray foam, and single-pane windows measuring 34 square-feet on the front wall and 24 square-feet on the rear wall.

(a) Studied building location at New Mexico State University (Google Maps, 2026). (b) Family housing.

Given the region’s arid climate, energy efficiency is a priority, requiring advanced modeling techniques to optimize performance. To achieve this, a geometric model of the building is developed in SketchUp, a widely used 3D modeling tool for energy simulations. SketchUp’s intuitive interface and compatibility with energy analysis tools make it a practical choice for architectural visualization and simulation-based decision-making.

The SketchUp model accurately replicates the building’s geometry, incorporating spatial dimensions, orientation, and structural components such as walls, windows, and roof layouts. This digital twin is crucial for energy performance analysis and is a foundation for integration with OpenStudio, a plugin designed to enhance energy modeling capabilities within SketchUp. OpenStudio enables detailed energy simulations using EnergyPlus, allowing iterative optimization of energy efficiency. Prior studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of SketchUp and OpenStudio in urban energy modeling, highlighting their potential for rapid prototyping and sustainability assessments31.

Following the simulation setup using SketchUp and OpenStudio, EnergyPlus was employed to conduct a detailed analysis of building performance under seasonal peak conditions. The used model is depicted in Figs. 4 and 5. Results were compared to those generated by the Energy Plus model to validate the energy model outputs. To ensure consistency between both simulations, the same EnergyPlus weather file was used to drive external environmental conditions in both models, and efforts were made to replicate material properties, construction assemblies, and internal load inputs in Julia to mirror those defined in the EnergyPlus and OpenStudio environments.

BERT software performance

BERT predicted thermal performance and heating/cooling loads for model buildings over time. BERT’s modular design enables parallel processing, significantly reducing simulation time while maintaining scalability across different urban contexts. Its application in geothermal heating/cooling simulations at NMSU showcased highly reliable performance data, reinforcing the value of ML in optimizing energy efficiency. By integrating ML-driven classification with BERT’s predictive capabilities, urban planners can enhance decision-making, optimize resource allocation, and promote sustainable development32.

Using Julia provides multiple strategic advantages. It enables efficient cloud deployment and computing offload, crucial for performing extensive numerical simulations rapidly and at scale. Julia also supports automatic differentiation, a powerful tool for sensitivity analyses, optimization, and machine learning tasks. Furthermore, Julia facilitates straightforward integration with both open-source and proprietary third-party software, significantly simplifying software design and enabling streamlined, full-stack capabilities spanning backend computation, frontend visualization, API development, mapping, plotting, analytics, and diagnostics.

One of the key benefits of using Julia for BERT is substantial performance acceleration. Compared to traditional energy modeling tools like EnergyPlus, BERT executes simulations significantly faster—approximately 3.5x faster than EnergyPlus and up to 55x faster than Engineering Equation Solver (EES). This efficiency is essential for running thousands of simulations required for uncertainty quantification, sensitivity analyses, data assimilation, and integration into advanced machine learning workflows.

By leveraging Julia, BERT offers a highly efficient, scalable, customizable, and integrative building energy modeling tool, significantly enhancing urban energy management capabilities and supporting broader adoption in urban science and commercial applications.

Model diagnostics and decision support

Having established BERT as a validated and computationally efficient software, we now demonstrate its utility in providing actionable insights. The following sections detail a series of model diagnostics and decision support analyses designed to move beyond simple prediction and address the critical questions of model uncertainty, parameter importance, and economic viability. First, we explore the overall uncertainty in our predictions, then we pinpoint the most influential parameters driving that uncertainty, and finally, we assess the economic implications of these findings.

Utilizing the Model Analysis & Decision Support Julia framework, a series of model diagnostics were performed against BERT, including simulations, uncertainty, sensitivity analyses, and a demonstrative Return-on-Investment (ROI) analysis. Diagnostics were conducted to quantify uncertainties in seasonal heating and cooling energy requirements. This coupling demonstrates the ability to utilize advanced statistical methods to evaluate uncertainty in numerical models like BERT and support decision-making processes related to the model. In particular, BERT + Mads would enable assessing the cost-effectiveness of geothermal heat exchange systems (heat pumps) for our peak days. To understand the full spectrum of our model’s sensitivity, we employ three distinct methods: Latin Hypercube Sampling for a broad, efficient sampling of the parameter space; Saltelli’s method to distinguish between the individual and interactive effects of parameters; and eFast to provide a complementary, frequency-based perspective.

The analyses examine how variations in key input parameters, such as ambient conditions, material thermal properties, and geothermal parameters, affect the estimation of cooling and heating loads. By systematically adjusting these variables, the impact on simulation performance is quantified, and the most influential factors driving model outcomes are identified. This rigorous approach enhances the understanding of the underlying uncertainties and lays the foundation for refining model calibration and ensuring robustness across a range of operational scenarios, including ROI analyses.

Sensitivity analyses

Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) was used to produce a simulation dataset using our BERT simulator, for uniformly distributed values within the distribution ranges in Table 2. Reasonable test ranges were developed around the initial test case parameters. LHS divides the range of each input parameter into equally spaced intervals, shuffles the order of these intervals, and randomly selects one value from each interval to generate n input sets, where n is the desired number of samples and the number of input intervals.

Sensitivity analysis methods

Saltelli and Extended Fourier amplitude sensitivity testing (eFast) were conducted using Mads.jl to further examine the parameter space and identify the input parameters exerting the most significant influence on the predicted energy loads. Saltelli’s and eFast are global sensitivity analysis techniques that comprehensively examine how variations in model inputs affect the overall output. Saltelli’s method33 partitions the output variance into main and interaction effects, allowing for a thorough assessment of each parameter’s individual and combined impact. eFast, on the other hand, utilizes a frequency-based approach to generate complementary insights and further refine the evaluation of parameter importance. Figure 12 shows the posterior probability density functions for each of the eight model inputs along the diagonal. These plots demonstrate that AIMCMC revealed relatively normal posterior distributions of all parameters. By observing the histograms and scatter plots for a given variable, we can tell how “well constrained” a given parameter might be, or how limited the posterior distribution of a given parameter might be – a tighter PDF indicates that the particular parameter is well constrained within the parameter space. For example, “Temperature factor” (Row/Column 1) is well-constrained, suggesting higher sensitivity.

The off-diagonal scatter plots display relationships between inputs. We can think of the distribution of simulation data as the uncertainty in our model parameter space that allows for the same outcome – if we observe a nominal power consumption over a 24-hour day, given our RC-circuit thermal model, what is the space of possible inputs (parameters) that would produce this nominal consumption? The analysis reveals that the uncertainties related to the Geothermal (GT) heat exchange coefficient and the Geothermal Coefficient of Performance appear correlated (Fig. 12: outlined in purple), which makes physical sense - we expect the heat exchange coefficient to play a direct role in COP (efficiency) of the system. A higher heat exchange efficiency between the ground and the pipes of the geothermal loop would result in a higher coefficient of performance of the overall system. Thus, we would expect the heat exchange coefficient and COP to follow closely along a curve of possible values.

The scatter plots further support the physical intuition behind our thermal model. We see a high negative correlation between the resistance and capacitance of the concrete (Fig. 12: outlined in blue) – both resistance and capacitance tend to decrease cooling loads, so increasing either one would require a decrease in the other to maintain parity in power demand. A similar relationship and physical interpretation can be applied to the relationship between the resistances of spray foam insulation and concrete (Fig. 12: outlined in yellow), increasing one requires decreasing the other to maintain a nominal cooling load.

Counterintuitively, we also see a positive correlation between foam resistance and concrete capacitance (Fig. 12: outlined in orange). Intuitively, they are expected to show a negative correlation because they both lend themselves to a decrease in nominal power demand. The flipped correlation suggests that more sensitive parameters influence the less sensitive resistance and capacitance parameters.

The AIMCMC analysis is critical for the evaluation of uncertainty and interpretability of the thermal model. We can confirm much of the core physics of the system while simultaneously identifying fascinating non-linearities that could not have been found with a simple parametric study. The diagonal plots showing the probability density distribution of each variable are also very beneficial for reverse engineering and design purposes. When the curve is flatter, input parameter choice has more flexibility; when the curve is narrow, the parameter selection has to be around the central value. Understanding the parameter space in this way can be critical for implementing decisions of the modeled system.

By construction, the simulation generates a set of predictions from the parameter space that are essentially identical. Figure 13 shows that the predictions for the various parameter sets yield power consumption curves that are nearly indistinguishable when expressed in BTU/HR. Return on investment analysis

A critical application of this work is its ability to provide accurate, economic analyses for building energy development decisions. This effort focused, in particular, on implementing a Geothermal Heat Pump system for heating and cooling. The conventional HVAC heating was modeled using natural gas, with a high-efficiency gas furnace, 0.9. The base case for this study was defined by an uninsulated building envelope paired with a conventional HVAC system utilizing gas heating and refrigeration-based cooling. The geothermal heat pump’s energy demand was simulated using the ground‐source heat exchange model described in section 1. Wall insulation thicknesses of 2 in. and 4 in. were introduced, accounting for their respective thermal resistances and capacitances, to quantify impacts on annual energy savings and payback periods. The return‐on‐investment analysis drew upon Las Cruces electricity and natural gas costs37, material resistance and capacitance data36, and applicable New Mexico rebates38 and tax credits39 for energy efficiency and geothermal upgrades, thereby ensuring that model outputs reflected local energy pricing, insulation performance, and available financial incentives.

This contrasts with the baseline nominal scenario, which uses a conventional HVAC system without foam insulation to assess the value of incremental insulation.

Table 5 demonstrates the superior performance of geothermal heat pump systems over conventional HVAC configurations, particularly when considering tax credits and rebates. Geothermal systems deliver annual energy savings of 48% to 62% (depending on insulation thickness, 0–4 in.), significantly outperforming traditional HVAC systems, which achieve only 30% to 33% savings under equivalent conditions. The payback period for geothermal systems, including tax incentives, ranges from 2 to 15.3 years, substantially improving compared to 7.7 to 25.8 years for conventional systems. While increasing insulation offers minimal efficiency gains in geothermal installations, its impact is considerably more pronounced for traditional HVAC systems. Note that the “payback period” is assumed to be well within the lifespan of the HVAC equipment. These findings highlight that geothermal heat pump systems provide a superior blend of long-term energy efficiency and cost-effectiveness.

Machine learning results

A promising application of machine learning to building energy modeling involves integrating physics-based models like BERT with data-driven ML. A science-informed model that is both trainable and interpretable opens avenues for applications such as data-driven model validation and automated calibration.

An initial machine learning analysis was performed using the simulated data generated from Latin Hypercube Sampling of BERT model outputs described in section “Sensitivity analyses”. This data was divided equally into training and testing (Fig. 15a and b) sets. Based on gradient-boosted decision trees, the powerful and efficient XGBoost algorithm was then trained on the simulation data. XGBoost40, or Extreme Gradient Boosting, is a highly efficient and scalable implementation of the gradient boosting framework, which has become a dominant method for a wide range of machine learning tasks. It is an ensemble learning technique that sequentially builds decision trees, where each new tree corrects for the errors of the previous ones by fitting the residual errors, optimized via a gradient descent algorithm on a regularized objective function. To prevent overfitting and improve generalization, XGBoost introduces a novel regularization term on the model complexity, penalizing the number of leaves and the magnitude of their weights. Furthermore, it employs several system and algorithmic optimizations for performance and scalability, including a specialized sparsity-aware split finding algorithm to handle missing data, a weighted quantile sketch for approximate tree learning on large datasets, and out-of-core computation that allows for processing datasets that do not fit into memory.

(a) Training data derived from LHS parameter space. (b) Validation data derived from LHS parameter space.

XGBoost was selected for this initial analysis due to its speed and ability to capture complex dynamics in data. This ML analysis is performed to explore whether it could capture the physics of building energy transfer. Both the training and validation phases of the XGBoost40 models showed extremely high predictive accuracy on their respective datasets, using a relatively small training dataset (500 points), in an extremely short period of time (< 5 s) on an M1 Macbook Pro, with an R2 of 1.0 (Fig. 16). This strong performance confirms that machine learning can effectively capture the complex physical relationships represented by the physical model and reproduce sophisticated building energy modeling. This provides a promising framework and strong motivation for more advanced and in-depth implementation of scientific machine learning for building energy prediction.

Discussion and future work

The Building Energy Replica Tool (BERT) development represents a significant step towards scalable and computationally efficient urban energy analysis. It is substantial that BERT produces results comparable to industry standards like EnergyPlus for peak demand analysis. Still, implementing an RC circuit and utilizing Julia dramatically increases simulation speed. This efficiency is the key enabler for the future of urban-scale modeling; it makes extensive uncertainty quantification, sensitivity analysis, and large-scale parameter exploration computationally feasible, which can be prohibitive with many industry-standard tools. The ability to rapidly simulate thousands of building configurations is a first step in moving the feasibility of BEM application from single-building analysis to genuine urban-scale assessment.

Our analysis of the model’s parameter space yielded crucial insights into the drivers of energy performance in geothermal systems. The consistent finding that geothermal parameters, rather than building envelope characteristics, dominate the model’s uncertainty has profound implications for design and retrofitting priorities. It suggests that optimizing the ground loop and heat pump performance for buildings utilizing geothermal exchange offers a greater potential for energy savings than focusing solely on traditional building insulation and materials. This data-driven conclusion challenges conventional wisdom and provides a clear, actionable focus for engineers and architects aiming to maximize the performance of such systems.

Furthermore, our work demonstrates the powerful synergy between physics-based modeling and machine learning. By successfully training an XGBoost model to replicate BERT’s outputs with high fidelity, we’ve validated a pathway for creating surrogate models for real-time decision-making and optimization. The economic analysis, which showed a rapid ROI for geothermal systems, underscores the practical value of these tools. They can provide stakeholders with a robust technical validation and a compelling financial justification for investing in sustainable energy infrastructure. This combination of speed, physical insight, and economic analysis provides a holistic decision-support framework lacking the urban planning toolkit.

Building on our current findings, future research will focus on advancing the capabilities of our urban modeling and analysis framework. A primary objective is to enhance our Building Energy Model (BEM) simulator, BERT, by improving its computational efficiency, predictive accuracy, and applicability to a broader range of urban scenarios. This will involve incorporating more complex physical dynamics, such as microclimate effects, detailed geothermal heat pump models, and extensive benchmarking against established simulators, such as EnergyPlus and TRNSYS. The continued development will also expand the model’s ability to simulate more diverse building morphologies, materials, and HVAC systems, thereby increasing its utility for urban planning and energy analysis.

In parallel, we will advance our machine learning methodologies to better integrate with our physics-based models. We will employ SIML to create hybrid models that leverage the strengths of both data-driven and physics-based approaches. In particular, we will create a neural differential equation model41 with BERT as the physics component. Neural network terms will be added to Eqs. (1)–(4) and these will be trained against high-fidelity (but expensive) models like EnergyPlus and TRNSYS. The data-driven terms will increase the fidelity of BERT with negligible computational cost. The fast, efficient, and high-fidelity neural differential equation model can then be used with differential model predictive control42 to provide the most energy-efficient heating protocols.

Looking ahead, the development of BERT is a foundational step toward a more comprehensive software platform called UrbanAI. We plan to integrate BERT as a core component of this larger tool. The primary goal of UrbanAI is to provide urban planners, policymakers, and researchers with a robust decision-support system. It will aim to simulate complex urban dynamics, optimize city-wide energy consumption, and evaluate the potential impacts of new policies and technologies on urban sustainability and resilience. With UrbanAI, a city planner could simulate the effect of a new zoning regulation on the energy consumption of an entire district, or a utility company could forecast grid demand from the widespread adoption of geothermal heat pumps. By integrating physics-based models like BERT with machine learning and diverse data streams, UrbanAI will offer a holistic platform for designing future cities.
