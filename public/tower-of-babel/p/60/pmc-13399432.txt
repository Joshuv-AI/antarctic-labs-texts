Phylogenetic analysis of amino acid sequences indicates a central role of transmembrane domain structural elements in MHC class II function
Authors: Konstantina Karathanou, Jamie L Duke, Sophia M Cangialosi, Luke Marlow, Ann M Dixon, Dimitri S Monos, James R Drake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman leukocyte antigen class II genes are highly polymorphic. This raises the question of how polymorphisms are distributed across the entirety of the class II molecule and how this distribution affects function. We performed a comprehensive analysis of amino acid variability across the entire class II molecule, using class II amino acid sequences ranging from humans to ancient species such as cartilaginous fish. This analysis is unique in its scale and reveals that while the membrane distal antigen recognition domain exhibits a high frequency of amino acid variability, other domains exhibit much lower degrees of variability. Notably, the transmembrane domain (TMD) exhibits the lowest amino acid variability, consistent with its role in driving core class II functions. Further analysis of the TMD revealed that a cysteine residue and 3 GxxxG (GG4) motifs are 100% conserved throughout evolution. Mutagenesis and monoclonal antibody binding studies revealed that both the GG4 motifs and cysteine residue (the latter a site of class II palmitoylation) are involved in conformer formation. Molecular dynamics simulation of the palmitoylated class II TMD provides a framework for understanding the contribution of GG4 motif pairing and cysteine palmitoylation to class II structure. These results reveal a domain-specific organization of polymorphism within class II and a key role for the TMD and its functional residues/motifs in major histocompatibility complex class II immunobiology.

Introduction

Major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules mediate key interactions between CD4 T cells and class II–expressing antigen presenting cells such as dendritic cells, B cells, macrophages, thymic epithelium, and some tumor cells. After synthesis in the endoplasmic reticulum, class II molecules traffic to endocytic compartments where they bind peptides derived from the proteolytic processing of internalized antigen. The resulting peptide–class II complexes then traffic to the cell surface for recognition by the T-cell receptor (TCR) of antigen-specific CD4 T cells.1

The membrane-distal region of the class II molecule, the antigen recognition domain (ARD), is directly responsible for binding antigen-derived peptides and interacting with the TCR. The membrane-proximal region, transmembrane domain (TMD), and cytoplasmic tail of class II are also sites of multiple important class II functions. The binding of coreceptors such as CD4 and LAG-3 has been confirmed in crystal structures with human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DR1 to provide an atomic resolution description of binding to the membrane-proximal immunoglobulin (Ig)-like domains of the molecule.2,3 The cytoplasmic tail of class II controls protein trafficking indirectly by interacting with the invariant chain cytoplasmic tail to inactivate an endoplasmic reticulum–retention motif4 or directly when MARCH family proteins ubiquitinate a lysine residue in this region to drive molecules into deep endocytic compartments.5 MHC class II signaling is controlled by multiple motifs located in the molecule’s connecting peptide,6 TMD,7–9 and cytoplasmic tail.10,11

Regarding the TMD, an in-depth analysis of class II calcium signaling in B cells led to the discovery that class II molecules exist as 2 distinct conformers, formed by differential pairing of TMD GxxxG dimerization motifs.7,10,12,13 The GxxxG motif is a well-known driver of transmembrane helix dimerization via close packing of 2 helical chains and is part of the (small)-xxx-(small) motif family present in the TMDs of ∼50% of all single-pass membrane proteins.14,15 The 3 GxxxG motifs in the class II TMDs can associate to form 2 different conformers (termed M1- and M2-paired class II). Currently, these conformers are defined by the binding of conformer-specific monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that detect TMD pairing–driven differences in the molecule’s extracellular domain. In mice, the 11-5.2 mAb selectively binds M1-paired I-Ak class II. In contrast, the 10-3.6 mAb binds all I-Ak class II irrespective of TMD pairing.7,16 Recently, we reported that the AMS-32.1 mAb selectively binds M1-paired I-Ad class II, whereas the 34-5-3S mAb recognizes all I-Ad molecules.17

Although we do not have a mAb in humans that selectively binds M1-paired HLA-DQ conformers (DQ is the homolog of mouse I-A), the Tü36 mAb selectively binds M1-paired HLA-DR class II (the homolog of mouse I-E). In contrast, the L243 mAb recognizes all HLA-DR molecules irrespective of TMD pairing (L243 binds the monomorphic DR α-chain [DRA] chain).12 The conformational epitopes recognized by the 11-5.2, AMS-32.1, and Tü36 mAbs have been partially defined. The 11-5.2 mAb predominantly binds the I-Ak α chain.16,18 Moreover, because 11-5.2 and AMS-32.1 block TCR recognition of M1-paired I-A, the corresponding epitopes must be near the peptide-binding groove (the epitopes are discussed in detail by Busman-Sahay et al.16 and Sprent et al.18). Tü36 binding exhibits some allele-selective binding to M1-paired HLA-DR molecules, meaning DR β-chain (DRB) residues are involved (DRA exhibits limited amino acid variability)—likely those in the DRB membrane-proximal region of the molecule (the epitope is discussed in detail by Drake et al.12). The blocking activity of Tü36 (which would provide additional insight into the location of the Tü36 epitope) has not, to our knowledge, been tested or reported.

The M1- and M2-paired conformers of I-A and HLA-DR have distinct functions and bind different sets of antigen-derived peptides.10,19,20 M1-paired class II comprises about 10% of class II molecules,12,16 is found in lipid rafts,12,16 drives both B-cell and T-cell activation,8,9,18 and is preferentially loaded with peptide derived from the processing of B-cell receptor–bound cognate antigen.19 In contrast, M2-paired class II comprises about 90% of class II molecules,12,16 is excluded from lipid rafts,12,16 blocks B-cell class II signaling,8,9 lacks the ability to drive significant T-cell activation,18,21,22 and, in B cells, is loaded with peptides derived from the processing of antigens internalized by fluid-phase endocytosis.10 Although the function of M2-paired class II is more enigmatic, it is possible that it functions as a TCR co-agonist23 or is involved in induction or maintenance of peripheral T-cell tolerance (which we discuss later in this article).

The impact of allelic differences on the variability of amino acid residues in the ARD and membrane-proximal Ig-like domains of class I and class II molecules is well studied. However, the impact of allelic differences on the highly functional transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains of class II is poorly understood. Only 2 studies have touched upon this issue, to our knowledge. A 2003 publication by Reche and Reinherz24 reported the comparison of amino acid sequences of 485 human class II alleles. The authors noted the low variability of class II TMD amino acid residues but focused their analysis on the ARD of the molecule. A 2019 publication by Dijkstra and Yamaguchi25 reported the comparison of amino acid sequences of a single class II allele from 11 different organisms, from humans to ancient fish species. The authors noted the conservation of a set of TMD glycine residues and mentioned their likely role in TMD pairing, but they focused most of their discussion on the possible early evolutionary development of MHC molecules.

In light of this limited information and the explosion in reporting new full-length HLA class I and II alleles over the past 10 years, our goal for this study was to compare the complete amino acid sequence of thousands of MHC class II α- and β-chain alleles from multiple species, ranging from humans to sharks, and determine the distribution of amino acid polymorphisms across the whole molecule. Particular attention was paid to the molecule’s TMD, which possesses multiple amino acid motifs known to affect class II structure and function.10,13 The results of this analysis reveal the highly conserved nature of the MHC class II TMD and complete evolutionary conservation of a TMD cysteine residue and 3 TMD GG4 motifs, all of which control formation of MHC class II conformers. We then placed the results of this analysis into a refined in silico model of MHC class II TMD pairing.

Material and methods

Analysis of TMD consensus sequences

To identify conserved features within the MHC class II TMD, we first generated species-specific consensus sequences by collecting all available full-length TMD sequences for each species or gene, filtering incomplete sequences, and expanding them according to allele frequency. Human sequences were obtained from the Immuno Polymorphism Database–ImMunoGeneTix HLA (IPD-IMGT/HLA) database, version 3.60.26 Sequences for nonhuman mammals and selected teleosts (Macaca mulatta, Sus scrofa, Ovis aries, Equus caballus, Salmo salar, Oncorhynchus mykiss) were retrieved from the IPD-MHC database (build 221),27 and sequences for all remaining species were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Position-wise amino acid frequencies were calculated, and consensus residues were defined at an 80% agreement threshold, with positions below this threshold designated as variable (“X”). These species-level consensus sequences were then combined to derive an overall TMD consensus across species, using 2 agreement thresholds (80% and 100%) to assess conservation robustness. Final species-specific consensus sequences for the α and β chains, together with the number of alleles with complete TMDs included in each calculation, are presented in Tables 1 and 2. All analyses were performed using custom Python scripts (version 3.9).

Distribution of amino acid polymorphisms

Amino acid sequences for human HLA class II molecules were obtained from the IPD-IMGT/HLA database, version 3.60.26 For nonhuman species, homologous sequences were retrieved from the IPD-MHC database (build 221).27 Alleles annotated as null (N) or having questionable expression (Q) were excluded, and only 3-field alleles were retained for analysis.

We selected species to provide representative coverage across major evolutionary lineages, while considering sequence availability. We focused on species with available full-length class II sequences (DRB1, DQA1, DQB1) to enable analysis across all functional domains, including the TMD. For many species in the IPD-MHC database, sequence coverage was limited or incomplete, which restricted their inclusion. To improve coverage, we supplemented the dataset with sequences from the NCBI (Table S1), selecting additional species with sufficiently complete class II sequences.

We focused on 3 class II HLA genes: HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1, and HLA-DQB1, and homologous genes from selected nonhuman species including Mus musculus (mouse), Macaca mulatta (rhesus monkey), and Sus scrofa (feral pig). For each gene, we defined 5 functional domains: the leader sequence, ARD, membrane proximal domain, TMD, and cytoplasmic tail. All available alleles were aligned at both the nucleotide and amino acid levels using Clustal Omega28 with the following reference alleles: HLA-DRB1*01:01:01, HLA-DQA1*01:01:01, and HLA-DQB1*05:01:01. Multiple sequence alignments were used to determine the amino acids present at each aligned position. This allowed us to compute, for each position within a domain, the set of unique amino acids observed, the number of alleles containing each amino acid, and their corresponding frequencies.

For the 20 standard amino acids, Shannon entropy values range from 0 (complete conservation at a position) to a maximum of approximately 4.32, which occurs when all amino acids are represented with equal frequency. Shannon entropy captures both the number of distinct amino acids and the evenness of their distribution. A low entropy value (close to 0) indicates high conservation, where 1 amino acid dominates; higher values reflect more balanced and diverse distributions. Entropy values were aggregated by domain, and domain-level distributions were visualized using box plots. All analyses were performed using custom Python 3.9 scripts.

Phylogenetic analysis of class II TMD sequences

Phylogenetic relationships among MHC class II TMD sequences were inferred using the neighbor-joining method implemented in Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA) 12.31,32 Because TMD regions exhibit very low amino acid variability within a species, a single representative α- and β-chain TMD sequence was selected for each species. Selection was based on allele representation: For each species, unique TMD sequences were identified and the number of alleles including the particular TMD sequence was determined. The sequence associated with the highest number of alleles was chosen, prioritizing the most prevalent TMD sequence while minimizing the influence of rare variants.

For the phylogenetic analysis, representative sequences of class II α and β alleles were selected rather than species-specific consensus sequences. Consensus sequences are typically generated by assigning an amino acid only when it appears at a frequency greater than 80% at a given position. If this threshold is not met, the position is denoted as X. However, the use of X can obscure underlying sequence variation, potentially affecting distance estimates, particularly in short and highly conserved regions such as the TMD.

Multiple sequence alignments were generated for the complete 23-amino-acid TMD segments, and evolutionary distances were calculated using the Poisson correction method,33 expressed as the number of amino acid differences per site. Phylogenetic trees were constructed separately for the α and β chains, using identical analysis parameters. No filtering of nodes was applied in the phylogenetic analysis, and all nodes were retained as generated by the neighbor-joining method. Given the short length and high conservation of the TMD sequences, the analysis was intended to capture overall evolutionary relationships between species, consistent with known phylogenetic groupings. Additional phylogenetic methods (maximum likelihood and minimum evolution) were tested, and the overall topology was consistent with the neighbor-joining tree for both the α- and β-chain analyses, particularly in the grouping of major evolutionary lineages. Minor differences were observed in local branch arrangements, as expected given the short length and high conservation of the TMD sequences. The neighbor-joining method was retained for presentation due to its simplicity and consistent performance across analyses.

Impact of the HLA-DR α-chain cysteine residue on Tü36 binding

MHC class II molecules can be modified with palmitic acid via thioester linkage to a TMD cysteine residue.34–36 To investigate the possible role of palmitoylation in class II conformer formation, 293 T cells were transfected with either wild-type HLA-DR or HLA-DR in which the DRα TMD cysteine residue was mutated to serine (which cannot be palmitoylated). The cells were then stained with L243-Alexa488 (Invitrogen, AS1009) and Tü36-PE (BioLegend, 361606) and analyzed by flow cytometry, as previously reported.12

Molecular dynamics simulations of the class II TMD

Model building

The initial protein structure was generated using the AlphaFold337 web server, with sequences corresponding to the juxtamembrane and TMD of human MHC II α and β chains, as follows:

DRA_HUMAN (P01903)38: PLPETTENVVCALGLTVGLVGIIIGTIFIIKGLRKSNAAERRGPL

DRB_HUMAN (P01911)38: ARSESAQSKMLSGVGGFVLGLLFLGAGLFIYFRNQKGHSGLQPTGFLS

The membrane system was constructed using CHARMM-GUI39,40 Membrane Builder.41 The MHC II TMD dimer was embedded into a lipid bilayer consisting of 284 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) lipids, solvated with ∼28,000 TIP3P water molecules, and neutralized with 0.15 M NaCl. Palmitoylation of the α chain at residue C11 was completed during system preparation within the CHARMM-GUI interface prior to membrane insertion.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

Molecular dynamics was performed using GROMACS42 2024.4 with the CHARMM36m43 force field. Energy minimization was conducted using the steepest descent algorithm for 5,000 steps. After minimization, the system underwent the 6-stage equilibration protocol generated by CHARMM-GUI, comprising sequential number, volume and temperature as well as number, pressure and temperature equilibration phases with progressively reduced positional restraints and increased time step.

The production simulation was performed for 1.25 μsec using a 2-femtosecond time step. Separate temperature coupling groups were assigned for the peptide, lipids, and solvent, and maintained at 310.15° K using the V-rescale44 thermostat with a coupling constant of 1 picosecond. Semi-isotropic pressure coupling was applied at 1 bar using the C-rescale45 barostat with a coupling constant of 5 picosecond.

Bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the linear constraint solver (LINCS)46 algorithm, and periodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions. A cutoff distance of 1.2 nm was used for both Lennard–Jones and short-range electrostatic interactions. Lennard–Jones interactions were smoothly reduced to zero between 1.0 and 1.2 nm, and long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle mesh Ewald method.47 All visualization and analysis were performed using the Visual Molecular Dynamics48 (VMD) software package. Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) calculations were carried out using the VMD RMSD Trajectory Tool plugin.

Results

Amino acid sequence of the class II TMD is highly conserved

Even though the distribution of polymorphisms across the MHC class II protein has been studied,24,25 the plethora of recently published full-length sequences and our interest in the molecule’s TMD led us to perform a new analysis of the distribution of polymorphisms across the whole class II molecule. To perform such an analysis, we started by comparing the amino acid sequences of HLA-DR and HLA-DQ alleles available from the IPD-IMGT/HLA database (v. 3.60; >5,000 amino acid sequences).26 The human DRA was excluded from the analysis because it is highly conserved, with only 19 unique full-length protein sequences among all alleles, providing too few variants for a statistically meaningful comparison.

Results of the analysis of human DRB1 are presented in Fig. 1. As anticipated, the ARD of DRB1 exhibits a high degree of amino acid diversity in terms of the total unique amino acids per position, with up to 12 different amino acids observed in any 1 position across the sequences analyzed. This is in strong contrast to the TMD, as well as the leader peptide and cytoplasmic tail, which exhibit low variability (Fig. 1A). The membrane-proximal Ig-like domain of the molecule exhibits an intermediate level of variability. Given that characterization of the ARD of HLA alleles (ie sequencing of exon 2) is all that is required for submission to the IPD-IMGT/HLA database, substantially fewer alleles have been characterized for the TMD (n = 976) than for ARD (n = 3,307). To minimize potential bias of this analysis due to incomplete characterization of some alleles, Shannon entropy29,30 was calculated for each position based on the relative frequencies of amino acids. This provides a measure of variability independent of the number of alleles sequenced. Based on this analysis, the ARD remains the most variable region of the molecule, and the TMD exhibits the lowest variability and highest conservation among all 5 domains (Fig. 1B and C). To determine whether the pattern observed for DRB1 extends to other human class II loci, we performed the same analysis for both DQA1 and DQB1. Consistent with the DRB1 results, Figs. 2 and 3 present the results from a similar analysis of the 2 chains of HLA-DQ with comparable findings. For both DQα and DQβ, the ARD has the highest overall variability, whereas the TMD exhibits a very low level of variability.

To compare amino acid variability across HLA class II functional domains, we analyzed position-wise Shannon entropy values between the ARD and TMD for HLA-DRB1 (n = 93 and 29 amino acid positions, respectively), HLA-DQA1 (n = 83 and 29 positions, respectively), and HLA-DQB1 (n = 93 and 29 positions, respectively). To assess whether amino acid variability, measured by Shannon entropy values, differs significantly between the ARD and TMD of DRB1, DQB1, or DQA1, we applied the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, a nonparametric method appropriate for the non-normal distributions of entropy values and robust to the presence of outliers. Significant differences were observed in all cases, with the ARD exhibiting higher sequence variability than the TMD (HLA-DRB1: Z = 6.742, P < 0.0001; HLA-DQA1: Z = 3.690, P = 0.0002; HLA-DQB1: Z = 4.105, P < 0.0001). Although the Z value was highest for HLA-DRB1, all 3 chains showed strong evidence of domain-dependent variability differences consistent with the distinct functional roles of the ARD (eg peptide binding) and TMD (eg conformer formation).

Given that the human HLA-DR and mouse I-A class II molecules have been most extensively studied for TMD function.7,8,12,19,20,34,49–52, we conducted a similar analysis on the α and β chains of the mouse I-A class II molecule. The entropy-based analyses were also performed on additional species available in the IPD-MHC database (including Ovis aries, E. caballus, S. salar, and Oncorhynchus mykiss). These analyses showed the same overall pattern, with higher variability in the ARD and lower variability in the TMD. For clarity and to avoid redundancy, Figs. 1–5 focus on a subset of representative species (Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, Macaca mulatta, and Sus scrofa) that illustrate this pattern. The findings were consistent across all species analyzed.

Taken in total, the above analyses (Figs. 1–5) reveal that although evolution favors or allows a high rate of variation of the amino acid residues in the membrane-distal ARD of class II molecules, it maintains a very low rate of amino acid variation for the TMD and other membrane-proximal domains.

Identification of highly conserved MHC class II TMD amino acid residues

The finding that the TMDs of class II molecules from multiple species exhibit a very low level of amino acid variation suggests it might be possible to identify an MHC class II TMD consensus sequence. To this end, we determined a TMD consensus sequence for each species or chain (see Materials and Methods), aligned the resulting consensus sequences and determined the level of amino acid similarity across these consensus sequences (Tables 1 and 2).

As shown in Table 1, for the class II α-chain TMD, at an 80% conservation cutoff there is a mammalian consensus sequence that covers 19 of 23 amino acid residues (>80% of the TMD). If we shift to a 100% conservation cutoff, the consensus sequence still encompasses 14 of 23 residues (>60% of the TMD). If we extend the analysis to include 10 additional nonmammalian species, the only TMD amino acid residues that are 100% conserved across all species are the 4 glycine residues of the 2 GG4 motifs as well as a single upstream cysteine residue. Similar analysis of the class II β-chain TMD sequences (Table 2) reveals an 80% conservation cutoff mammalian consensus sequence that encompasses 16 of 23 amino acid residues (70% of the TMD) and a 100% conservation cutoff consensus of 11 of 23 residues (almost 50% of the TMD). If we again extend the analysis to include 9 nonmammalian species (reflecting available β-chain sequences), the only completely conserved amino acid residues are the 2 glycine residues of the single GG4 motif and 1 C-terminal glycine residue. Interestingly, the highly-conserved CxxGxxxGxxGxxxG string is also present in the α-chain TMD of a recently recognized ancient W-category MHC molecule first identified in sharks, and the TMD of the W-category β chain has a single GxxxG motif.53 These results further demonstrate the highly conserved nature of the class II TMDs and reveal the complete conservation of the TMD residues that guide formation of MHC class II conformers.7,12

The low level of TMD amino acid variability is likely a reflection of a relatively high density of functional amino acid residues or motifs (discussed later in Results). To gain insight into how these sequences or motifs may have developed across evolution, we generated 2 phylogenetic trees based on the class II α- and β-chain TMD amino acid sequences (Fig. 6). Both class II TMD-based trees are similar in overall organization and comparable to the overall organization of phylogenetic trees based on broader sets of parameters. With regard to the tree based on α-chain TMD sequences (Fig. 6A), there is an early divergence between fish and other species (tetrapods), with the cartilaginous fish (sharks) showing the earliest divergence. The group of bony fish is the next branch, with the coelacanth being an early branch off the line of bony fish. The phylogenetic tree based on the β-chain TMD sequences is similar in overall structure to the tree based on α-chain TMD sequences, with a slight shift in the relative placement of the coelacanth lineage, which is closely related to tetrapods.

Taken together, the results in Tables 1 and 2 define “consensus sequences” for the TMDs of the MHC class II α and β chains. The high degree of conservation of these sequences (assessed by identifying residues that are identical across all species included in the alignment) indicates that these residues and the functions they support (ie TMD pairing for the GG4 motifs7,12 and class II palmitoylation for the cysteine residue34,35) must be key to MHC class II structure and function. The 2 phylogenetic trees based on TMD amino acid sequence provide a framework for a deeper understanding the evolutionary development of class II molecules.

Role of highly conserved TMD cysteine in class II conformer formation

Our phylogenetic analysis reveals that the 3 pairs of glycine residues (ie the 3 GG4 motifs) and single TMD cysteine residue, are 100% conserved throughout evolution. Because we know the 3 GG4 motifs are involved in MHC class II conformer formation,7,12 the question is raised about the role of the cysteine residue in class II conformer formation. This residue has been shown to be the target of palmitoylation,34–36 and mice expressing class II with a TMD cysteine to serine mutation (which blocks palmitoylation) have a defect in MHC class II–driven thymic development of CD4 T cells.35 Therefore, we sought to determine the role of the TMD cysteine residue in class II conformer formation.

The DRA TMD cysteine residue was mutated to serine, and the resulting DRA*01:01 C195S chain (Fig. 7A) expressed in 293 T cells along with multiple DRβ chains (Fig. 7). Parallel analysis of the DRA*01:01 M1 G > V mutant (Fig. 7A), which is known to result in decreased formation of M1-paired HLA-DR molecules (ie decreased Tü36 mAb binding12) was included as a positive control (Fig. 7). The cells were double stained with the L243 and Tü36 anti-DR mAbs, which recognize total versus M1-paired class II, respectively,12 so a decrease in M1-paired conformer formation results in a leftward shift in staining (Fig. 7B). When paired with DRB1*04:01 (DR4), the DRA C195S mutation (like the DRA M1 G > V mutation) resulted in a > 80% decrease in binding of the Tü36 mAb, indicating decreased formation of the M1-paired conformer (Fig. 7C and D). In the context of DR15 (DRA*01:01 paired with DRB1*15:01), the C195S mutation led to a less dramatic but still significant ∼50% decrease in Tü36 binding (Fig. 7C and D). The lesser effect of the C195S mutant in DR15 is likely because of stronger binding of the Tü36 mAb to this class II allele.12

To test this notion, we analyzed the DRA C195S mutant paired with a DRB1*15:01 A140T mutant, which we have previously shown has weakened Tü36 binding.12 In the context of this mutant DR15 molecule, the inhibitory effect of the C195S mutant (and the M1 G > V mutant) on Tü36 binding (ie M1-paired conformer formation) is similar to that seen with DR4 (Fig. 7C and D). The results presented in Fig. 7 show that the highly conserved DRA TMD cysteine residue (Fig. 7A) is critically important to the formation of M1-paired MHC class II conformers, likely as a result of palmitoylation, which would drive M1-paired class II into membrane rafts.35 Together with our previously published work,12 these results demonstrate that all the class II TMD residues that are 100% conserved through evolution are critically involved in the formation of MHC class II conformers.

The finding that the M1 G > V and C195S mutations each has partial inhibitory effects on formation of M1-paired HLA-DR (ie Tü36 binding) (Fig. 7) suggests that formation of M1-paired class II is driven by both M1 GG4 motif pairing and class II cysteine palmitoylation.34,36 To test this possibility, we determined the impact of the C195S/M1 G > V double mutant on Tü36 binding. The results presented in Fig. 8 show that the C195S/M1 G > V double mutant has a significantly greater inhibitory effect on Tü36 binding than either mutation alone, indicating that both GG4 motif pairing and class II palmitoylation play independent roles in the formation of M1-paired class II molecules.

Molecular dynamics simulations of class II TMD in membrane bilayer

Molecular dynamics simulations of TMD interactions can provide valuable insight into molecular structure and function. We performed an atomistic simulation of the palmitoylated M1-paired class II TMD in a POPC lipid bilayer (Fig. 9) to gain greater insight into the dual roles of TMD GG4 motif pairing and cysteine palmitoylation in class II structure and function. The simulation was run for 1.25 µs, and 2 snapshots of the palmitoylated TMD structure at 300 ns and 1,100 ns are shown in Fig. 9A and B, respectively. The TMD structure equilibrates after ∼300 ns, yielding an average RMSD of 3.5 Å (Fig. 9C, RMSD plot, gray line), with the close packing of the M1 GG4 motif against the β-chain GG4 motif being maintained throughout the simulation. The higher average RMSD observed for the palmitoyl moiety (Fig. 9C, RMSD plot, black line) reflects the greater dynamics of the palmitoyl chain, adopting orientations that vary from perpendicular to the plane of the membrane to parallel to this plane. The distance between the center of mass of the palmitoyl chain and the center of mass of the M1 GG4 motif is shown in Fig. 9C (upper plot), and an overlay of structures sampled at regular intervals is shown in Fig. 9D.

These results show that although M1 GG4 motif–mediated TMD pairing is stable over the time frame modeled, the class II–linked palmitic acid cycles between close association with the class II TMDs and “reaching out” where it could interact more extensively with the hydrophobic core of the lipid bilayer. When palmitic acid is closely associated with the 2 protein helices, it is possible that it promotes or stabilizes M1 GG4 motif–mediated TMD interactions. When the palmitic acid is extended from the TMD, the 14-carbon fully saturated fatty acid (along with differences in the effect length of M1- vs. M2-paired TMDs) may drive the tropism of M1-paired class II molecules for lipid rafts.12,16 These findings provide a strong structural framework in which to conceptualize the finding that individually the M1 G > V or C195S mutations only partially decrease formation of M1-paired class II molecules, whereas the double mutant essentially ablates formation of the M1-paired class II conformer (Fig. 8).

This analysis revealed the highly conserved nature of the MHC class II TMDs and the complete conservation throughout evolution of the 3 GG4 motifs and single cysteine residue that affect TMD association and formation of MHC class II conformers. The in silico analysis provides a structural framework to better understand the dual roles of GG4 motif pairing and TMD palmitoylation in class II conformer formation. In total, these results provide compelling evidence of a key role for class II conformers in the immunobiology of MHC class II molecules.

Discussion

Our study integrates evolutionary, biochemical, and computational approaches to define the distribution of class II polymorphisms across the entirety of the class II molecule and reveals the highly conserved nature of the class II TMD. Moreover, detailed analysis of the molecule’s TMD reveals the highly conserved nature of multiple amino acid residues and motifs that control class II chain pairing and conformer formation. As noted, previous studies of the distribution of MHC class II polymorphisms or variability have focused primarily on the ARD and membrane-proximal Ig-like domain, with only passing reference to the molecule’s TMD and cytoplasmic tail.24,25 The present study substantially extends these previous reports by providing an extensive and detailed analysis of class II sequence variability across the entirety of the molecule, across thousands of alleles and multiple species. Particular emphasis was given to the TMD of both the α and β chains and the specific motifs encoded by this section of the molecule, because they can affect class II chain pairing. We then placed the results of the analysis into an in silico model of MHC class II TMD dynamics. A phylogenetic analysis of the TMD sequences was also performed. Together, these approaches allow us to connect patterns of amino acid sequence conservation with predicted structural behavior, providing a more complete picture of how the TMD contributes to class II structure and function.

One of the most striking findings of this analysis is the complete conservation across evolution of the residues of the 3 TMD GG4 motifs and α-chain cysteine residue. When considered in the context of the very high level of overall MHC class II polymorphism, this finding is a compelling argument that these residues are critical to MHC class II structure and function. One-to-one pairing of TMD GG4 motifs is known to support energetically favorable TMD interactions in many biological systems.15,54 As we have previously shown, differential pairing of these highly conserved TMD GG4 motifs is key to driving formation of 2 distinct MHC class II conformers, which have distinct immunological functions.7,10,12,13 Here, we show that the highly conserved α-chain TMD cysteine residue is also involved in class II conformer formation, likely via palmitoylation in the M1-paired class II conformer, which would help direct these class II molecules to lipid raft membrane domains.34–36 The observation that the C195S/M1 G > V double mutant is more profoundly impaired in formation of M1-paired class II than either single mutant indicates that both M1 GG4 motif–based pairing and class II palmitoylation are involved in conformer formation (Fig. 9). It is also known that TMD cysteine palmitoylation affects class II–dependent immune functions such as development of CD4 T cells,34–36 further implicating the M1-paired class II conformer in key immune functions.

To date, studies suggest a central role for M1-paired class II molecules in immune cell activation and development of a robust immune response.16,18,21,22,55,56 The function of M2-paired class II is somewhat more enigmatic. At a cellular or molecular level, M2-paired class II may fail to trigger TCR signaling or T-cell activation for 1 or more reasons. M2-paired class II may (i) fail to form TCR catch bonds critical for TCR signaling57; (ii) function like complexes of MHC and antagonistic peptide, recruiting SHP-1 phosphatase to the TCR to temper signaling58; and/or (iii) preferentially engage inhibitory LAG-3 molecules as opposed to activating CD4 molecules, resulting in decreased TCR signaling.2 Alternatively, M2-paired class II may function primarily as a TCR co-agonist, amplifying signaling triggered by TCR engagement of M1-paired peptide–class II complexes.23 At the level of the overall immune response, the function of M2-paired class II could be to drive homeostatic TCR signaling and/or to drive peripheral T-cell tolerance. However, additional studies are needed to address all these various possibilities. Overall, the findings reported herein highlight the key role of TMD pairing (ie class II conformers) in the function of MHC class II molecules.

Currently, the molecular mechanism that guides formation of the 2 class II conformers is unknown. We do know that class II conformers form early in the biosynthetic pathway, whereas class II is still invariant chain associated.7 Thus, class II TMD chain pairing is likely established in the endoplasmic reticulum. Consistent with this notion, protein palmitoylation, in general, is mediated by endoplasmic reticulum–resident palmitoyl acetyltransferases (the palmitoyl acetyltransferase that mediates class II palmitoylation has not been identified). The finding that the C195S and M1 G > V single mutants only have a partial inhibitory effect on formation of M1-paired class II suggest that although GG4 motif pairing and TMD palmitoylation both affect class II conformer formation, they are not linked by cause and effect (eg GG4 motif pairing is not required for subsequent TMD palmitoylation). Future studies on how these TMD interactions are coordinated (and any role of invariant chain or other accessory proteins) will be enlightening.

The high level of polymorphism of ARD residues and the high level of conservation of TMD residues raise the question of how this distribution of variability was developed through evolution. The most likely scenario is that there is a constant rate of mutation in the MHC genes, paired with strong selective pressure on both regions of the class II molecule. The ARD is likely subject to strong selective pressure to preserve key structural residues while promoting diversification of those residues involved in peptide binding and antigen recognition to enhance the range of pathogen detection. For the TMD, there would be little selective pressure for diversification, because this region of the molecule does not directly bind the antigen. However, there is likely strong selective pressure to maintain residues involved in key molecular functions such as conformer formation, lipid raft partitioning, signaling, and protein trafficking, especially the GG4 motif glycine residues and single TMD cysteine residue. Overall, the ARD tolerates greater sequence variation to support broad peptide recognition, whereas the TMD shows strong conservation because its architecture provides the structural basis for proper class II assembly and membrane positioning required for ARD function.

In conclusion, the purpose of this study was to assess MHC class II polymorphisms across the entirety of the molecule and ascertain whether there is evidence of an important role for the TMD in class II structure and function. The results of the analysis using a substantially more recently published new HLA class II sequences confirm the already known feature of the localization of polymorphisms to the class II ARD (which interacts with both antigen-derived peptide and the TCR), but they also reveal the highly conserved nature of the molecule’s TMD. Within these highly conserved TMD sequences we now identify the presence of multiple highly conserved TMD embedded interaction motifs (ie n = 3 GG4 motifs and a cysteine residue), which guide the formation of the 2 distinct class II conformers that have discrete cellular and immunological functions. These findings highlight that the TMD acts as an active architectural element of the class II molecule rather than a passive membrane anchor, further supporting its central role in MHC class II structure and function and providing a strong foundation for future studies.
