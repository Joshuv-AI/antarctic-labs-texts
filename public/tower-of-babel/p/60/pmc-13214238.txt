Risk of bias among Chinese-language randomized controlled trials on Chinese herbal medicine included in meta-analyses published in 2021–2022: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Yue Jiang, Claire Chenwen Zhong, Betty Huan Wang, Shanshan Xu, Fai Fai Ho, Ming Hong Kwong, Leonard Ho, Joson Hao-Shen Zhou, KC Lam, Jian-ping Liu, Bao-ting Zhang, Vincent CH Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe reliability of meta-analyses on Chinese herbal medicine (CHM) largely depends on the methodological quality of the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs). This study assessed the risk of bias (RoB) among Chinese-language CHM RCTs embedded in meta-analyses (MAs) published between 2021 and 2022.MethodsChinese-language CHM MAs were identified through literature searches across seven databases (three international and four Chinese databases). RCTs were extracted from the eligible MAs and their methodological quality was appraised using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) tool. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to explore associations between bibliographical characteristics and quality.ResultsOf 2060 RCTs (published between 1997–2021), 93.0% (n = 1916) had low RoB in incomplete data, but only 43.98% (n = 906) in random sequence generation. No RCTs had low RoB in selective outcome reporting. Non-patent CHM RCTs performed poorly in sequence generation, allocation concealment, and outcome assessment blinding but well in incomplete data. Government-funded RCTs showed better performance in sequence generation and outcome assessment but worse in incomplete data. No analysis was conducted for selective reporting due to lack of low RoB RCTs.ConclusionThe methodological quality of Chinese-language RCTs embedded in recently published MAs was found to be unsatisfactory, indicating their limited utility in informing clinical decision-making. There is an urgent need for enhancing clinical trial training for researchers, reviewers, and editors in China.Keywords: Randomized controlled trials, Clinical trials, Research design, Bias, Quality

Introduction

Chinese herbal medicine (CHM) is a popular therapeutic modality in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM),1 deeply rooted in history and widely used among Chinese communities globally. The integration of advanced technology has modernized the research on Chinese medicine (CM).2, 3, 4 But it still employs natural medicinal ingredients—plants, animals, and minerals—processed for medicinal therapeutic purposes.4 According to a study of TCM utilization status within TCM hospitals in China (2004–2016), it was found that TCM pharmacists comprised for 51.7 % of pharmacists, and TCM prescriptions accounted for 46.6 % of total prescriptions (TCM and conventional prescriptions combined).5 Internationally, CHM products achieved a market value of nearly 1.03 billion USD in 2016,6 while domestically, they contributed to 1/3 of China's pharmaceutical industry in 2017.7 In 2018, China boasted 60,738 health service organizations offering TCM services nationwide.8 With the extensive utilization of CHM, it is crucial to ensure its usage aligns with evidence garnered from rigorously conducted randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Over the past decade, the Chinese government has prioritized CHM development, emphasizing the generation, synthesis, and application of high-quality evidence in CHM.9,10

RCTs are widely regarded as the best primary study design for assessing the effectiveness and efficacy of treatments, and CHM is no exception. However, when RCTs are not appropriately conducted, significant risk of bias (RoB) can occur and this undermines the validity of study findings.11

The Cochrane Collaboration's risk-of-bias tool (RoB tool)12,13 is a widely applied instrument for identifying and evaluating sources of bias in RCTs. It encompasses the domains of random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of the outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, and selective reporting. Empirical research demonstrated the adverse impact of high RoB in these domains on the trustworthiness of RCT findings.14, 15, 16 For example, insufficient or unclear random sequence generation was associated with a 7 % overestimation of the intervention effect.17 Similarly, inadequate or unclear allocation concealment resulted in a 10 % overestimation of the intervention effect.17 The absence of blinding on participants and personnel contributed to a 23 % overestimation of subjectively assessed outcome (e.g., effect sizes from patient-reported questionnaires).18,19 The lack of blinding in outcome assessment inflated the intervention effects by 34 %.17 Finally, incomplete outcome data was known to be associated with inaccurate outcome estimation, or causing failure in detecting differences between groups.11

Existing RoB assessments have predominantly focused on CHM RCTs targeting specific conditions, or those published in English.20 An appraisal of 227 English-language CHM RCTs published between 2010 and 2019 highlighted significant methodological limitations amongst these trials. Currently, a substantial number of CHM RCTs have been published in Chinese-language, and they formed the basis of many meta-analysis (MA) in the field. While MA published in recent years are considered the most up-to-date synthesis for decision making,21 it remains uncertain whether the RoB of these Chinese-language CHM RCTs may limit the trustworthiness of their conclusions. Also, the determinants of RoB amongst Chinese-language RCTs are unclear. This underscores the necessity for a comprehensive RoB appraisal of a representative sample of Chinese-language CHM RCTs which support the conclusions of recently published MAs.

The research question of this study was (i) what is the RCT-level RoB performance of Chinese-language CHM RCTs identified from systematic reviews (SRs)/MAs? (ii) Are prespecified bibliographic features associated with RoB ratings? This study aimed to (i) assess the RoB among Chinese - language CHM RCTs included in MAs published between Jan 2021 to Sep 2022; (ii) characterize the bibliographical features of these identified RCTs, and (iii) explore the potential associations between RoB levels and bibliographical features. The findings of this study will offer policymakers, clinicians, and researchers insights into the RoB status of Chinese-language CHM RCTs. This endeavor is crucial in informing how the methodological quality of future CHM RCTs may be improved, and consequently such improvement will strengthen the evidence base for CHM, enhancing its global recognition and clinical utility. The units of analysis were the RCTs.

Methods

The RCT sample for this cross-sectional study was drawn from a larger meta-epidemiological study, that included RCTs (as analysis unit) incorporated within SRs that met the following eligibility criteria:

Inclusion criteria

According to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, an SR is a methodical structured review that identifies, evaluates, and synthesizes all pertinent evidence meeting predefined criteria, achieved via thorough planning tailored to address a specific research question.11 For this study, source SR eligibility required the inclusion of at least one eligible MA that comprehensively assesses RCTs on the effects or safety of CHM treatment. Furthermore, eligible MA must encompass both RCTs utilizing syndrome differentiation and those without (i.e., those prescribing a fixed CHM based on a conventional disease diagnosis). MAs focusing solely on RCTs with or without syndrome differentiation were excluded, as they would not have contributed to the analyses. Additionally, eligible MAs must incorporate at least 10 eligible RCTs to ensure sufficient statistical power for analysis.11

In instances where multiple eligible MAs were present, we prioritized the MA containing the highest number of RCTs.22 To eliminate duplicate RCTs from MAs with overlapping RCTs, we began with the MA featuring the highest number of RCTs and proceeded to the MA with the fewest RCTs. If this procedure resulted in fewer than 10 unique RCTs for a particular MA, it was excluded from consideration. This harmonization approach guaranteed that the dataset had no overlap between RCTs and MAs.23

For RCTs included in the MA, there were no limitations regarding participants and language. However, they must adhere to the following characteristics concerning interventions, comparisons, and outcomes:

Interventions and comparisons: Eligible interventions should be oral administration for any type of CHMs, which contains at least one ingredient listed in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition). Eligible comparisons were i) CHM versus conventional treatment; and ii) CHM plus conventional treatment versus conventional treatment. The comparisons between the RCTs in each MA must be consistent. This requires that each eligible MA included RCTs belong to only one comparison. If there were multiple arms of an eligible RCT, the arm whose comparison was consistent with other RCTs in the same MA was considered eligible.

Outcomes: The outcomes for each RCT should be either treatment effects or side effects of the CHM, as reported in the MA, whether it was categorized as binary or continuous outcomes.

SRs/MAs were used solely as the sampling frame to identify potentially eligible Chinese-language CHM RCTs. The unit of analysis was the RCTs. RCTs that fulfilled the afore-mentioned criteria were included in this cross-sectional study.

Exclusion criteria

Overviews of SRs, network MAs, protocols, conference abstracts, animal studies, SRs not focusing on CHM treatment effects or side effects, and SRs published in languages other than Chinese were excluded. Furthermore, SRs that solely comprised non-RCTs or lacked MAs were also excluded. Additionally, studies investigating any TCM modalities other than oral administration of CHMs, such as acupuncture and therapeutic massage (Tuina), were excluded. In cases where multiple versions of an SR were available, the most recent version was chosen, thus excluding RCTs from these excluded SRs. RCTs lacking full texts were also excluded. No exclusion was applied on the involvement of placebo.

Literature search

We searched for eligible SRs published from January 2021 to September 2022 across seven electronic databases: MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid), the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, WanFang, SinoMed, and Airiti Library. Specialized filters for SRs were applied in MEDLINE and EMBASE.24,25 No restrictions were imposed on publication status. The detailed search strategy is provided in Supplementary 1.

Literature screening and data extraction

After the literature search, all citations were imported into EndNote version 20 for deduplication. Titles and abstracts of the retrieved citations were screened against eligibility criteria. Full texts of the potentially eligible SRs were obtained for further evaluation. Before inclusion, full texts of the embedded RCTs from potentially eligible MAs were extracted and assessed for eligibility.

Before commencing the screening, reviewers underwent training and calibration until achieving good inter-rater agreement. One reviewer conducted literature screening, data extraction, and RoB assessment. A second reviewer independently replicated these tasks in one-fifth of trials randomly. Any discrepancies were discussed between two reviewers, with unresolved issues addressed by a third reviewer. Bibliographical features of the included RCTs were extracted using a predetermined data extraction form, detailed in Supplementary 2.

Risk-of-bias assessment

RoB was assessed de novo by trained reviewers, independent of any RoB judgments reported in the source SRs/MAs. The RoB tool12,13 was employed to assess the RCTs’ performance. This tool is considered less resource-intensive for evaluating large quantities of RCTs.26 There are 7 domains in the RoB tool, including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of the outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other bias. Due to the varied nature of other bias assessments across topics,13 we focused solely on the first 6 domains in all included studies. Each domain was assessed as "high RoB", "unclear RoB", or "low RoB". When reporting was insufficient to permit a judgment of low or high risk, the domain was rated as "unclear RoB". Further details on the operational definition of the RoB tool12,13 are available in Supplementary 3.

Data analysis

The unit of analysis was at the RCT-level. Descriptive statistics were utilized to present data on RoB performance and bibliographical features. Continuous variables were depicted as medians with ranges while categorical variables as frequencies with percentages. We performed descriptive analyses of RoB judgements across domains. The Kruskal-Wallis test was applied with the ordinal RoB rating (Low/Unclear/High) as the dependent variable and comparing distributions across RCT characteristics: publication year, funding category, number of centers, number of study arms, category of CHM comparison, category of CHM intervention, whether syndrome differentiation was incorporated, and the condition investigated (11th version of the international classification of diseases, ICD-11).

To investigate potential associations between bibliographic features and RoB ratings, logistic regressions were conducted for each RoB domain. In these logistic regressions, the "high risk" and "unclear risk" categories were merged into a control group and compared against the "low risk" category to identify factors associated with improved RoB performance. The logistic regression analyses were fitted with the domain-level RoB judgement as the dependent variable in models, where each RoB domain was dichotomized (Low vs non-Low [Unclear/High]). Logistic regressions were not applicable for domains with no RCTs categorized as "low risk".

For univariable binary logistic regression (UBLR), characteristics listed above were entered individually into the models (no additional covariates) as independent variable, reporting odds ratios (ORs) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). For multivariable binary logistic regression (MBLR), all prespecified RCT characteristics entered simultaneously as independent variables, reporting adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95 % CIs. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was employed to evaluate model fitness in multivariable analyses, with P values greater than 0.1 indicating a good model fit.

Reference categories consistent between the univariable models and the multivariable models. ORs, AORs and their 95 % CIs were utilized to quantify the relationship between RoB results for each domain and bibliographical features. Statistical significance was determined by a two-tailed P value less than 0.05. Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Sensitivity analysis (ordinal model)

Sensitivity analyses were further conducted using proportional-odds ordinal logistic models, Polytomous Universal Model (PLUM) for each RoB domain. The independent variables or covariates used in these models were the same as those employed in the logistic regression models. The outcome variable was ordinally coded as follows: 0=High risk, 1=Unclear risk, 2=Low risk. Under this coding scheme, a cumulative OR greater than 1 indicates a positive association with better domain performance (i.e., lower risk of bias). We reported cumulative ORs with 95 % CIs, Wald χ² statistic, p-value, and a direction-of-effect concordance column. Ordinal results were interpreted as sensitivity evidence.

Results

Literature screening

A total of 6140 records were identified from seven databases. After removing duplicates (n = 2158) and screening titles and abstracts (n = 3982), 1131 records were evaluated in full-text. Of these, 838 were excluded for various reasons, mainly including SRs of MAs with less than 10 eligible RCTs (n = 417), non-Chinese publications (n = 189), SRs lacking MAs (n = 94). Additionally, 156 records were excluded after assessing the original studies embedded in the SRs. In this process, a total of 137 SRs were considered eligible, which synthesized 2479 primary studies, while 419 were considered ineligible and excluded due to: (1) not focusing on orally administered interventions (n = 186), (2) ineligible comparisons (n = 183), (3) not being RCTs (n = 29), (4) duplicates(n = 13), (5) non-Chinese-language publication(n = 4), and (6) full-text inaccessible(n = 4). Finally, 2060 RCTs were included in this study. Bibliographical characteristics

The 2060 RCTs spanned from 1997 to 2021 and involved 184,833 patients, with sample sizes ranging from 28 to 560. The majority (81.7 %) of RCTs did not receive government funding. Most were single-center (93.1 %) and two-arm trials (96.6 %). In 66.7 % of the included studies, the comparison group consisted of CHM plus conventional treatment versus conventional treatment alone. Chinese materia medica prescription was the predominant CHM intervention type (79.0 %), followed by CHM patent medicine (17.3 %). The utilization of the syndrome differentiation approach was evenly distributed, with 50.8 % incorporating it and 49.2 % not. The diseases studied were mainly focused on the circulatory system (24.9 %), genitourinary system diseases (22.0 %), and digestive system diseases (15.7 %). RoB performance

In terms of incomplete outcome data domain, the RCTs generally performed well, with 93.0 % (n = 1916) rated as having a low RoB. Conversely, the majority of RCTs lacked sufficient information in several areas, resulting in an unclear RoB, particularly in domains such as blinding of participants and personnel (96.84 %, n = 1995), selective reporting (96.4 %, n = 1985), and allocation concealment (93.4 %, n = 1924), For the domain of random sequence generation, 44.0 % RCTs (n = 906) achieved low RoB, while for blinding of outcome assessment, this figure was 22.0 % (n = 452). Notably, no RCTs achieved a low RoB rating in the domain of selective reporting. An overview of the RoB performance is provided in Fig. 2.

Domain 1: random sequence generation (Table 8)

The performance of RCTs in this domain varied over different time periods. Compared with RCTs published before 2013, RCTs published from 2013 to 2015 (AOR 1.377; 95 % CI [1.004, 1.889]; P = 0.047), 2016 to 2018 (AOR 2.222; 95 % CI [1.678, 2.941]; P < 0.001), or 2019 to 2021 (AOR 3.651; 95 % CI [2.691, 4.953]; P < 0.001) exhibited progressively better performance. Government-funded RCTs (AOR 1.942; 95 % CI [1.515, 2.489]; P < 0.001) performed significantly better than those non-government-funded RCTs.

However, RCTs utilizing non-patent Chinese medicine (AOR 0.692; 95 % CI [0.540, 0.887]; P = 0.004), or focusing on circulatory (AOR 0.744; 95 % CI [0.581, 0.952]; P = 0.019), or genitourinary system diseases (AOR 0.740; 95 % CI [0.571, 0.958]; P = 0.022) demonstrated poorer performance in random sequence generation, compared to those using patent CHM or focusing on other diseases.

Domain 2: allocation concealment (Table 9)

The poorer quality (high RoB) of allocation concealment was strongly associated with two characteristics. Firstly, single-center trials (AOR 0.210; 95 % CI [0.062, 0.708]; P = 0.012) showed higher RoB than multi-center RCTs. Secondly, RCTs utilizing non-patent Chinese medicine (AOR 0.397; 95 % CI [0.178, 0.887]; P = 0.024) demonstrated higher RoB compared to those employing patent CHM.

Domain 3: blinding of participants and personnel (Table 10)

In this domain, RCTs published between 2013 and 2015 (AOR 0.291; 95 % CI [0.105, 0.805]; P = 0.017) were significantly associated with higher RoB compared to those published before 2013. Additionally, RCTs utilizing non-patent Chinese medicine (AOR 0.306; 95 % CI [0.098, 0.956]; P = 0.042) demonstrated higher RoB in comparison to those employing patent CHM.

Domain 4: blinding of outcome assessment (Table 11)

Several characteristics showed positive associations with enhanced performance in blinding of outcome assessment. Specifically, RCTs published between 2019 and 2021 (AOR 1.557; 95 % CI [1.063, 2.282]; P = 0.023), as well as RCTs with government funding (AOR 1.809; 95 % CI [1.336, 2.450]; P < 0.001), illustrated significantly better performance (lower RoB) compared to RCTs published before 2013 that received non-government funding. Furthermore, RCTs focusing on circulatory disease (AOR 4.973; 95 % CI [3.651, 6.773]; P < 0.001) or genitourinary system diseases (AOR 4.951; 95 % CI [3.506, 6.992]; P < 0.001) displayed superior performance compared to RCTs focused on other diseases.

Conversely, several characteristics were significantly associated with poorer performance. Larger sample sizes were slightly associated with higher RoB compared in the domain of blinding of outcome assessment (AOR 0.995; 95 % CI [0.992, 0.998]; P = 0.005). Single-center (AOR 0.437; 95 % CI [0.209, 0.913]; P = 0.028) or two-arm RCTs (AOR 0.388; 95 % CI [0.205, 0.735]; P = 0.004) demonstrated worse performance compared to multi-center or multi-arm RCTs. Additionally, RCTs using non-patent Chinese medicine (AOR 0.538; 95 % CI [0.400, 0.724]; P < 0.001) or incorporating syndrome differentiation (AOR 0.756; 95 % CI [0.590, 0.968]; P = 0.027) showed lower performance compared to RCTs using patent CHM and not incorporating syndrome differentiation. Moreover, RCTs focusing on comparisons between CHM and conventional treatment showed worse performance compared to RCTs comparising CHM plus conventional treatment versus conventional treatment (AOR 0.169; 95 % CI [0.115, 0.248]; P < 0.001).

Domain 5: incomplete outcome data (Table 12)

The performance in the domain of incomplete outcome data was notably satisfactory across four characteristics. Single-center designed RCTs exhibited superior performance (AOR 3.426; 95 % CI [1.579, 7.431]; P = 0.002) compared to multi-center RCTs. Additionally, RCTs involving non-patent CHM demonstrated better performance (AOR 2.070; 95 % CI [1.390, 3.083]; P < 0.001) in contrast to those using patent CHM. RCTs focused on genitourinary system diseases also displayed enhanced performance (AOR 1.736; 95 % CI [1.033, 2.917]; P = 0.037) compared to RCTs focused on other diseases. However, RCTs with government funding demonstrated poorer performance in this domain (AOR 0.456; 95 % CI [0.306, 0.679]; P < 0.001) compared to non-government-funded RCTs.

All P values in the Hosmer-Lemeshow tests for the above logistic regressions were greater than 0.1, indicating a good model fit. The sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings, showing consistent results across all risk-of-bias domains. Full details are available in Supplementary 6–10.

Discussion

Summary of findings

The RoB assessment conducted in this study revealed an unsatisfactory status of CHM RCTs cited in SRs published 2021 to 2022. Among the six domains of the RoB assessment, only in the domain of incomplete outcome data, 93.0 % of RCTs were evaluated as having low RoB. Additionally, nearly half of the RCTs (44.0 %) achieved low RoB in the domain of random sequence generation. However, none of the included RCTs achieved a low RoB in the selective reporting domain. All other domains were predominantly characterized by unclear RoB.

Factors influencing individual RoB domains encompassed year of publication (domains 1, 3, and 4), sample size (domain 4), funding sources (domains 1, 4, and 5), number of centers for RCTs (domains 2–5), arms of RCTs (domain 4), types of RCT comparison (domain 4), types of CHM (domains 1, 2, 4 and 5), whether RCT incorporated syndrome differentiation in CHM (domain 4) and diseases of patients (domain 1, 4 and 5). These findings provided nuanced insights into the methodological differences of RCTs, emphasizing the importance of considering various factors in assessing the RoB of RCTs.

Factors associated with RoB assessment of RCTs

Most of the included studies did not report sufficient information in the domain of random sequence generation and allocation concealment, resulting in a majority of RoB results being categorized as unclear. It is noteworthy that RCTs involving non-patent Chinese medicine consistently appeared lower performance in random sequence generation and allocation concealment.27,28 These two domains served as the methodological foundation of an RCT, and the consistently low performance suggested a lack of basic knowledge among CHM clinical researchers that needs to be addressed.

Furthermore, the absence of protocol and pre-registration amongst almost all trials has hindered our assessment of the selective reporting domain. Although over one thousand CHM trials have been conducted in China, more than 90 % of them remained unregistered as of 2016.29 Moreover, by 2023, among 258 medical journals published in Chinese language, only 22 % required trial registration upon submission.30 Even with the establishment of the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR)31 over two decades ago, challenges persist due to insufficient attention, lax implementation, and a lack of standardization in entry formats.

One of the significant challenges in conducting CHM RCTs is the design and use of appropriate blinding and placebos. Most CHM preparations have a distinctive bitter taste and specific aromas from various herb mixtures, making it nearly impossible to design a perfect placebo for different herbal formulae.32 As an integral component of TCM, CHM entails a multidimensional treatment approach, which can be challenging for blinding participants and personnel.33,34 The absence or failure of a placebo can also compromise the blinding of the outcome assessor. For clinical studies without adequate reporting of placebo details the purpose of any trial is compromised.35 However, there is a lack of generally accepted methods and standards for the evaluation of CHM placebos.36 Past studies have found inadequate reporting of placebo information in CHM RCTs, and in particular evaluation of blinding success was reported in less than 5 %.37 To solve this, it is important for CHM researchers to admit the artificial placebo assessment could maximize the simulation of patients' judgments of medications in a real clinical setting and the independent evaluation.38

In our study, the incomplete outcome domain outperformed other domains, possibly reflecting higher satisfaction and better patient adherence with CHM treatment. However, the trustworthiness of such performance is questionable. In 2016, a substantial portion (80 %) of Chinese trials were found to have reported fraudulent data.39 In response, the Chinese government implemented a series of decrees to regulate the conduct of clinical research,40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45 leading to trial withdrawal and serious repercussions for research involving data falsification. Despite these initiatives, skepticism remains regarding the result of this domain.27, 28, 29

Comparisons with similar studies

Compared to previous publications on RCT quality in the field, our study reveals consistently high RoB of RCTs on CHM. Similar findings were observed in RCTs on CHM. and acupuncture,46 with a large proportion of RCTs at unclear risk amongst majority of the RoB domains. In one study on CHM RCT quality,47 only 9 % of Chinese-language RCTs performed well in random sequence generation, while only 3 % of RCTs performed well in all four domains of sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding for patients and personal, and blinding of outcome assessment. In the study on acupuncture RCT,46 the proportions of low RoB in random sequence generation and the blinding of outcome assessment were similar to our results. Another study on 1041 CHM and acupuncture RCTs published between 1986 and 2013 found that only 3.1 % of RCTs were conducted with proper blinding method.48

Implications for evidence-based chinese medicine (EBCM) practice in China

Over the past two decades, there has been a concerted effort to explore the integration of CM with the principles of evidence-based medicine.49 This strategic direction is prominently reflected in recent Chinese policies, notably exemplified by the release of the "Opinions on Promoting the Preservation, Innovation, and Development of CM"50 by the Central Committee and the State Council of China in 2019. These directives outline a clear vision for the integration of evidence-based Chinese medicine (EBCM) practices into healthcare, prompting the development of 50 Clinical Practice Guidelines (CPGs) tailored to scenarios where conventional medical approaches are limited.50 Subsequent policy documents, such as the "Construction Plan for a Chinese Medicine Highland in the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area (2020–2025)" and the "14th-Five-Year Plan for Chinese Medicine Development", further emphasize the pivotal role assigned to EBCM in driving future advancements in the field.51,52

Within the realm of CPG development and the EBCM establishment, RCTs play a fundamental role,53,54 serving as the cornerstone of clinical evidence supporting the use of CHM in clinical practice. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE)55 Evidence to Decision framework emphasizes the vital importance of RCT findings and their RoB in the evidence-to-decision process. High RoB amongst CHM RCTs has significantly hampered the uptake of CPG recommendations in clinical practice. A nationwide survey involving 4495 CM practitioners unveiled an overall CPG adherence rate of only 50.4 %.56 Another survey indicated that 30.4 % of acupuncturists expressed hesitancy or unwillingness to adhere to CPG recommendations.57 Alarmingly, fewer than 10 % of respondents viewed RCTs as valuable resources for informing clinical practice,58 highlighting a significant gap in leveraging evidence-based approach in promoting CM development.

These observations emphasize the urgent need to reduce the RoB of RCTs, before embarking on CM CPG development. Well-conducted RCTs ensures the generation of trustworthy evidence, fostering greater confidence in clinical recommendations and facilitating the integration of evidence-based practices in CM.

Recommendations on future policy

The current challenges in improving the methodological quality of CHM RCTs include inadequate training for clinical researchers, lack of trial monitoring and auditing, and non-compliance with reporting standards.27,33 To address these challenges and enhance the methodological quality of CHM RCTs in the future, we propose the following recommendations.

First, the prevalence of substandard CHM RCTs highlights the necessity for specialized methodology training. Research institutions should prioritize enhancing training programs to equip clinical researchers with the necessary skills. Proper training and education for clinical researchers were found to be beneficial for trial conduct.59, 60, 61

Secondly, government and institutions should also bear responsibility for trial auditing. Addressing the deficient management of CHM RCT is crucial to bolstering internal trial management capabilities.27 CHM RCT trialists and auditors considered the incorporation of an audit team would be beneficial to trial quality assurance.27 There is no doubt that, as a clinical research paradigm that provides a higher level of evidence, RCTs must follow the basic principles of clinical trials in general. The National Health and Family Planning Commission has mandated clinical trials to operate under hospital research committees.62 Government and institutions should introduce a third-party audit team to monitor the process and design of CHM RCTs, ensuring the implementation of bias-reducing methods and the proper use of trial resources. Establishing specialized clinical trial centers within hospitals or universities, staffed with trained professionals, can also facilitate effective trial management.

Thirdly, as reporting transparency is needed for evaluators to assess the methodological quality for RCTs, training on the application of the CONSORT guidelines is indeed and can help enhance the reporting quality of RCTs.63,64 Chinese journal editors and reviewers should also undergo training to ensure adherence to the CONSORT guidelines for RCTs, particularly the CONSORT extension for Chinese herbal medicine formulas for CHM RCTs.63,65 Advocating for adherence to the CONSORT guidelines63 as a prerequisite for publication in Chinese journals can foster comprehensive methodology reporting and encourage compliance among RCT authors.

Strengths and limitations

We appraised over 2000 Chinese - language RCTs published between 1997 and 2021, providing a comprehensive assessment on the quality of CHM RCT conducted in mainland China over the past two decades. Despite the availability of the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool, we chose not to use it as the high human resource investment in its use. Potential deficiencies in reporting among included RCTs may influence the accuracy of our assessment results.66

We conducted RoB assessments for each domain listed in RoB tool independently, at the RCT level, and did not compare our judgments with the original RoB ratings reported in the source SRs/MAs. Given the heterogeneity of RoB tools (e.g., different versions) and judgment rules across SRs, such a concordance analysis lies beyond the scope of this study and merits separate investigation.

Conclusions

Our findings describe the RoB performance among Chinese-language CHM RCTs identified from SRs/MAs. This comprehensive assessment of 2060 Chinese – language CHM RCTs published during 1997–2021 revealed high RoB in almost all methodological domains. Elevating the quality of future CHM RCT in China necessitates imperative actions, including 1) government audits and effective administration of clinical trials; 2) comprehensive training in RCT methodology for researchers and editors; and 3) the adoption of the CONSORT extension64 for CHM formulas as a prerequisite for publication in Chinese journals.
