Protocol to record and quantify absence seizures in rats using EEG arrays
Authors: Ingrid Buller-Peralta, Lucy Pritchard, Danai Katsanevaki, Katerina Hristova, Sally M. Till, Alfredo Gonzalez-Sulser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe present a protocol for implanting 32-channel NeuroNexus skull-surface electroencephalogram (EEG) arrays in rats to record absence seizure-like events. We describe steps for surgical implantation and automated offline detection of seizures from Open Ephys EEG recordings. This approach enables brain-wide, multi-day electrophysiological monitoring to assess epilepsy and seizure dynamics in rodents.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Katsanevaki et al.1

Before you begin

Innovation

We present a detailed protocol for implantation of skull surface EEG grid arrays in rats. The method has been optimized for the specific needs of rat surgery. Grid arrays are become more frequently used to understand physiological activity at the network level and identify biomarkers of behavior and disease. Furthermore, we detail and share an analysis script for automated detection of spike and wave discharges, the electrographical correlate of absence seizures in EEG grid array data. The code detects increases in voltage signals at specific resonant frequencies, which is particularly well suited for identifying spike and wave charges that have large increases in power at the spiking frequency. The code has been modified to increase ease of use and, transferability to other datasets beyond those recorded with EEG grids and OpenEphys acquisition systems.

Surgical procedures

Ensure that you have a complete surgical setup, including a stereotaxic frame and a stereomicroscope for visual guidance during electrode implantation. Essential surgical tools and materials to have ready are summarized in the key resources table.1.Preparation of ground screw electrode.a.Use scissors to cut a length of 3–4 cm of Teflon-coated silver wire.b.Carefully strip 1–2 mm of Teflon coating from one end using small forceps.c.Apply a small dab of flux to the underside of the screw head.d.Lay the stripped wire end into the flux so it makes contact with the screw.e.Use the soldering iron to add a small drop of solder to the underside of the screw head to hold the wire in place.Note: Be careful not to cut through the wire core as it is very fine.2.Preparation for Pre- and Post-Operative Care.a.Prepare analgesic for injection (e.g. 0.1 mL of Carprofen diluted in 0.9 mL sterile saline at a dose of 5 mg/kg).b.Prepare four syringes with sterile saline for injection and for preventing the surgical incision site from drying. Prepare x 2 1 ml syringes for injection and x 3 1 ml syringes for use during surgery per rat.3.Preparation of surgical instruments.a.Autoclave surgical instruments, surgical drape, plastic pipette tips with about 2 mm of the tip trimmed off, cotton buds, microbrushes, hand towels and water prior to surgery.b.Sterilize non-autoclavable instruments (scalpel blade, screws, ground screw with soldered insulated silver wire, screwdriver, tips of EMG wires attached to electrode interface board) in a 5% solution of instrument disinfectant (e.g. Reprodis) in sterile water.c.Wash non-autoclavable items in sterile water before transferring to sterile field.4.Preparation of surgical suite.a.Collect isoflurane and ensure anesthetic equipment is filled and functioning properly.b.Thoroughly spray preparation area and surgical area with appropriate disinfectant (e.g. Vetasept) to ensure a clean and sterile environment.c.Prepare sterile field: lay down surgical drape and arrange autoclaved surgical instruments on the drape.d.Place a sterile hand towel at the edge of the surgical drape to hold non-autoclavable items such as saline syringes.Note: The protocol is optimized for rats aged 12–16 weeks. However, it can be implemented in older or younger animals after adjusting for proper anesthesia management.

Step-by-step method details

Surgery protocol: Implanting the NeuroNexus skull-surface EEG recording array

Timing: ∼3 hours per surgeryThis step describes the implantation of NeuroNexus skull-surface EEG recording array in a rat including set up, induction and maintenance of anesthesia and surgical procedure. Images describing the essential surgery steps are presented in Figure 1.1.Prepare rat for stereotaxic surgery.a.Place rat in the induction chamber.b.Set oxygen flow to 2 L/min and Isoflurane to 5% and administer Isoflurane until the animal is unconscious and the breathing rate has slowed significantly.Note: Stable anesthesia results in a drop to around 50% or less of the animal’s normal respiration rate; around 30–60 breaths per minute in rats.c.Remove animal from chamber and use clippers to remove fur from the head to expose surgical site.d.Weigh the rat, record its weight, and administer appropriate dosage of subcutaneous analgesic.Note: As an example, a 300 g rat would receive 0.03 mL of Rimadyl at a recommended dose of 5 mg/kg (5 mg/kg x 0.3 kg = 1.5 mg; volume = 2.5 mg/ 50 mg/mL = 0.03 mL).Note: If the animal reemerges from anesthesia during preparation, replace in the induction chamber. Reemerging entails having reflexes (e.g. ocular or muscular) when placing ear bars or making an incision.e.Secure the rat to the stereotaxic frame using non-puncture ear bars.CRITICAL: If the head is properly stable, ear bars should both be at 1 mm depth and the head should not bounce if pressed and feel solid to touch. Re-adjust as much as necessary to achieve stability.f.Lubricate the rectal probe with a small amount of sterile lubricant (e.g., petroleum jelly) and insert (2–3 cm).Note: Use tape to secure the wire to the tail to avoid removing probe with movement.g.Turn on heating pad to 37°C and monitor temperature throughout the surgery.h.Apply ophthalmic ointment (e.g. Bepanthen) to completely cover the eyes and then cover eyes with surgical tape to protect the cornea.Note: Ophthalmic ointment prevents dryness.i.Administer sterile saline subcutaneously: 2.5 mL pre-surgery and 2.5 mL post-surgery to maintain hydration.j.Use scissors to trim nails to minimize potential injuries.k.Cover the rat with a sterile surgical drape ensuring only the surgical site is exposed.l.Reduce the isoflurane to 2.5% for maintenance throughout the surgery.m.Use a cotton swab to clean the surgical site with three passes of antiseptic disinfectant (2% chlorhexidine).n.Wash hands with aseptic technique by removing all jewelry and watches and wetting both hands with warm water.CRITICAL: Apply antimicrobial soap and lather thoroughly, covering all surfaces and scrub hands for at least 30 seconds. Rinse thoroughly with warm water and dry with sterile paper towels. Change into aseptic gown and sterile gloves before continuing with surgery.2.Expose the skull.a.Before making any incisions, check the crossed extensor reflex by gently squeezing the hind paw to ensure appropriate depth of anesthesia.Note: If reflexes are present, increase isoflurane and check reflexes again to confirm proper depth of anesthesia.b.Use forceps to hold the skin at the front of the skull.c.Position the scalpel blade at the front of the skull and gentle apply pressure while dragging it to the back of the head to make an incision along the midline of the skull approximately 3 cm long (Figure 1A).d.Use skin clips to secure the incision open.e.Use the scalpel to remove any remaining tissue from the exposed skull.Note: Use saline and cotton buds to manage potential bleeding and to the exposed skin edges throughout the surgery to keep them wet. This will lead to optimal healing.3.Drill craniotomies and insert skull screws.a.Mark the location of the craniotomies.i.Attach a pointer tool such as a blunted needle or a fine-tip glass pipette with an ink-dipped tip to the stereotaxic arm.ii.Place the tip of the tool at bregma, the reference point on the skull where the coronal and sagittal sutures meet.iii.Set the stereotaxic frame coordinates to zero at bregma.iv.Use the stereotaxic arm to navigate the tool to the target coordinates: bilaterally 4.5 mm anterior to bregma and 11.5 mm posterior from bregma.v.At each location, lower the tip until it gently touches the skull surface enough to leave a small mark where the craniotomy will be.b.Drill a small craniotomy (0.7 mm – 0.8 mm diameter) at 3.6 mm anterior to bregma to label the location where the frontal electrode of the EEG array will be placed.c.Drill two bilateral craniotomies 0.7 mm – 0.8 mm in diameter for the holding screws 4.5 mm anterior to bregma (Figure 1B).d.Drill another craniotomy for the reference screw at 11.5 mm posterior from bregma (Figure 1B).e.Using forceps to stably hold each screw, implant the two holding screws using a screwdriver to tighten (Figure 1C).Note: Approximately three full turns of the screw should ensure it sits stably in the craniotomy without touching the brain.f.Using the same technique as with the holding screws, insert the wired reference screw into the final craniotomy (Figure 1D).Note: Ensure each screw fits tightly in the craniotomy, lowering enough to be secured to the skull but without touching the brain.4.Affix EEG array.a.Prepare the stereotaxic arm to hold the probe perpendicular to skull by gripping it at the Omnetics connector at the tail end of the probe.b.Position the arm so that the probe sits just behind the frontal screws and over the center of the skull (Figure 2).Figure 2Photo showing the correct position of the EEG array on the skullThe Omnetics connector is shown held by the stereotaxic arm and the cross symbol is aligned over bregma.c.Gently lower the probe onto the skull surface, using sterile distilled water and micro-cotton tips to push it down from front to back and allowing for adhesion through the distilled water.d.Ensure the EEG probe’s central cross mark aligns with bregma (Figure 1E).e.Smooth the probe over the surface of the skull by wiping it with a cotton tip from the center towards the edges.CRITICAL: It is essential to remove any trapped air bubbles between the probe and the skull.f.Apply a thick layer (3-5 mm) of dental cement at the front and back sides (over reference electrode) of the probe to hold it in place.CRITICAL: Be sure to also cover holding and ground screws with dental cement.CRITICAL: However, ensure not to cover the ground screw completely to allow manipulation of the silver wire.Note: Remove any sharp edges from the cement before it dries to avoid any surfaces that might irritate the skin.g.Wait until the cement is dry (∼5–10 minutes) (Figure 1F).h.Connect the probe ground wire (green) to the ground screw electrode using solder.i.Use forceps to tuck in any excess silver wire directly over the probe.Note: It is important to minimize the final width of the head implant, with the aim of limiting its size relative to the incision over the top of the skull.CRITICAL: Avoid wire being placed over muscle tissue.5.Closing the incision.a.Lower the Omnetics connector to the center of the skull and secure with dental cement (Figure 1G).Note: Make sure all electrodes are coated with a layer of cement.b.Increase the Isoflurane to 3% and insert the electromyography (EMG) wires into the neck muscle using a 21 g needle:i.Insert the EMG wire into the sharp end of the needle (Figure 3A).Figure 3Step-by-step schematic to insert electromyography (EMG) wire into neck muscle during surgeryImages depict key stages of the procedure: (A) load exposed EMG wire into needle, (B) position wire parallel to the needle, and (C) the exposed EMG wire pushed into the neck muscle.ii.Fold the remaining EMG wire so that it lies parallel to the needle (Figure 3B).iii.While still holding the needle, grip the top of the EMG wire with forceps and gently push the needle through the neck muscle to insert the EMG wire (Figure 3C).iv.While still holding the top of the EMG wire with forceps, carefully remove the needle, leaving the EMG wire in the muscle.c.Cover the EMG wires with a layer of dental cement approximately 3–5 mm thick (Figure 1H).d.Close the incision with interrupted or mattress sutures.e.Clean the sutured area with sterile saline to remove any blood (Figure 1I).f.Turn off anesthetic machine and transfer the animal to a recovery heat pad.g.Monitor the animal closely until fully awake.CRITICAL: Ensure the rat remains warm throughout the surgery and during recovery using a heat pad. Administer appropriate post-surgical analgesic to manage pain and promote recovery. For example, Buprenorphine can be given orally in flavored jelly for up to 5 days following surgery.Figure 1Step-by-step surgical protocol for NeuroNexus skull-surface EEG array and EMG implantation in ratsImages depict key stages of the procedure: (A) incision and skull exposure, (B) drill holes for holding and ground screws, (C) insert holding screws, (D) placement of screws, (E) placement of EEG array, (F) securing the implant with dental cement, (G) positioning of EMG wires, (H) additional cement to secure the implant, and (I) closure of the incision with sutures. Analysis protocol: Automated seizure detection algorithm

Timing: ∼10 min per animal 12.Automated Detection of Spike-and-Wave Discharges (SWDs) in OpenEphys EEG Recordings.This step describes an automated, offline procedure for the detection of SWDs from EEG recordings acquired with the OpenEphys system at 1000 Hz. The approach uses a custom MATLAB-based script to identify and characterize seizure-like high-amplitude SWDs within the theta frequency range (5–10 Hz), particularly those associated with absence seizure activity. The detection procedure is based on cepstral analysis, a signal processing technique that identifies periodicity in a frequency spectrum.2 This approach is effective given the particular structure of the SWDs; bursts of activity with a fundamental frequency (f0) in the 5–10 Hz theta band that resonates like an echo in several periodic harmonics across the frequency spectrum. The automatic identification algorithm searches for that high amplitude peak through the following steps.a.Deconvolution of the raw signal using Fast Fourier Transformation (FFT) and logarithmic transformation to obtain the magnitude of the respective components (Figure 5A).Figure 5Performance of automatic SWD detection under SWD-like events with not enough cepstral power and noise(A) Example spectrogram of 10 seconds EEG with both noise and undetected SWD.(B) Raw EEG trace corresponding to time interval of the spectrogram in A (bottom), with two selected epochs (0.2 sec each) marked with blue and red vertical boxes. Inserts (top) show respective cepstral power analysis showing the fundamental frequency (f0) peak on the pseudo-time domain (top inset) and pseudo-frequency domain (bottom inset).(C) Peak cepstral power on theta band range (5-10 Hz) calculated in 0.2 sec epochs, normalized to its absolute maximum value, and transformed into z-scores. Threshold for detecting SWD events marked with dashed red line.(D) Undetected SWD and noise event transformed into zeros (below threshold) or ones (threshold or over) on the time interval shown in A and B. Small SWD (red box) remained undetected due to low cepstral power, whereas noise (blue box) was filtered from detection.b.Inverse Fourier transformation (IFFT) to obtain the cepstrum and reveal the period of the fundamental frequency (f0) as a spike in a pseudo-time domain frequency (Figure 5B).c.Obtaining the power cepstrum in non-overlapping windows of 0.2 sec for the whole EEG signal length.d.Detection of peak power cepstrum values within the relevant frequency range (5-10 Hz) and normalization to their absolute maximum (Figure 5C).e.After transforming, the resulting vector is converted into Z-scores to homogenize potential power differences among recordings, a threshold of ≥ 2.2x10-5 standard deviations of the entire recording is set to identify SWDs (Figure 5D).Note: The threshold was established by comparing the values of visually scored seizures against other high magnitude artefacts that could create false positives, such as sudden movements or chewing behavior.f.Events are scored in 0.2 seconds epochs as 1 (equal or higher than the threshold) or 0 (activity lower than threshold), producing a vector of length = 5x the total recording in seconds.g.Events with intervals of ≤ 1 second between each other were considered as one, while isolated events shorter than 0.8 secs were discarded.13.Software Setup and Installation.All analyses were conducted using the SWD_autoscore_v2025.m MATLAB script.a.Extract and place the master analysis folder (SWD-Automatic-Identification-2025) and required dependencies, including the Chronux toolbox (v2.11), within the C:\SCRIPT\ directory.Note: Upon opening MATLAB, users should set the path to the C:\SCRIPT\ and add all folders and subfolders to the MATLAB path.CRITICAL: Care should be taken to remove any duplicate or obsolete functions to avoid conflicts, particularly with utility functions like find_in_interval.m. Compatibility notes

This pipeline is optimized for .continuous EEG files generated by OpenEphys. It is not directly compatible with other formats without pre-conversion. However, the code will also work if users modify the initial data loading steps to convert their data files into .mat Matlab arrays.

Expected outcomes

The protocol describes the implantation of a NeuroNexus skull-surface EEG array in rats. It also outlines capturing EEG recordings using an OpenEphys system and the subsequent automated detection of seizure-like SWDs.

The primary outcome is that the surgery protocol will conclude with the EEG array securely and stably affixed to the skull with dental cement. Once awake, the animal will be monitored to confirm successful anesthesia reversal, along with regulation of body temperature to promote optimal recovery. The overall goal is a smooth and uneventful recovery, with the rat ready for subsequent behavioral and data collection procedures. After recovery, the animals will successfully recorded when are successfully connected to a headstage amplifier via their implanted EEG probes and monitored over a defined time period. When recordings are performed well automated seizure detection will be possible. The script will detect and classify seizure events in EEG recordings. The automated seizure detection process will focus on high-frequency oscillations, allowing for quantification of the number and duration of seizure events detected within the recording. Additionally, the algorithm will save the start and end time stamps (in number of samples) of each seizure detected, allowing for manual validation. This process will streamline the detection of seizure events and provide precise data for further analysis.

Limitations

Detection of SWD Outside Normal Parameters: The automated seizure detection script uses cepstral analysis to search for high-amplitude peaks in specific frequency bands (approximately between 5 and 10 Hz). However, seizures that fall outside these parameters, either due to atypical frequency, low amplitude, or unusual waveform morphology, may not be detected. Researchers should be prepared to adjust the algorithm’s parameters to accommodate variability in seizure presentation across experimental conditions or animal models.

Dependence on High-Quality Recordings: Accurate detection of SWDs relies on a high-quality level of EEG recordings. Issues such as poor grounding or excessive noise can compromise the signal and reduce the sensitivity of the detection algorithm. Recordings could be filtered to remove noise artefacts, but this is unlikely to improve detection accuracy. Instead, care should be taken to improve grounding in surgery and proper grounding of all equipment during recordings.

Limited Scope of Automated Analysis: While the script efficiently detects seizure-like SWD events, it does not classify or analyze other EEG features, such as interictal spikes or background activity, which may also provide valuable insights. Additional analysis tools may be required for comprehensive characterization of EEG patterns.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Post-surgical head scratching and wound complications impacting recovery and usability for recording.

A common post-surgical complication is animals scratching their head wound, leading to bleeding, open stitches, or other health complications, which compromise recovery and suitability of the animal for subsequent recording experiments. These challenges are often caused by irritation from uneven or sharp cement edges at the surgical site. Such complications not only impact animal welfare but also jeopardize experimental outcomes.

Potential solution

• Smoothing the cement edges post-surgery:

Smoothing the cement edges post-surgery:

Ensure the dental cement applied during surgery is smoothed thoroughly before suturing. This reduces irritation and minimizes the risk of animals scratching the surgical site. Use cotton buds to smooth over wet cement and fine tools to remove any sharp or protruding edges once dried. A smooth surface helps facilitate healing and reduces the likelihood of wound reopening.•Clipping nails:

•Clipping nails:

Clipping nails:

Clip the animal’s nails immediately post-surgery to significantly reduce the risk of scratching and damage to the surgical site. Short and smooth nails decrease the animal’s ability to cause harm to the implant while still allowing natural grooming behaviors.•Post-operative monitoring:

•Post-operative monitoring:

Post-operative monitoring:

Closely monitor the animals during the first few days post-surgery for signs of scratching or irritation. Early identification of excessive scratching can allow timely interventions, such as applying topical treatments (e.g. Renasen and Flamazine).•Refining surgical techniques:

•Refining surgical techniques:

Refining surgical techniques:

Review and optimize surgical protocols to minimize trauma at the wound site. Use appropriate suturing methods and ensure the placement of implants or markers does not create points of irritation.

Problem 2

Wires touching post-surgery, causing artifacts or noise in recordings.

A common technical issue during experiments is the introduction of artifacts or noise when wires come into contact with each other. Wire contact is often due to inadequate isolation during the cementing process. By ensuring each wire is properly isolated with cement and positioned to avoid contact, you can reduce the risk of noise and artifacts, ensuring low-noise and reliable recordings.

Potential solution

• Isolating wires with cement:

• Isolating wires with cement:

Isolating wires with cement:

During the cementing process, ensure that each wire is individually coated with insulation and separated by cement. Carefully apply dental cement around each wire to create a physical barrier, preventing them from touching. Use tools like fine-tipped applicators or brushes to achieve precise application.•Positioning wires appropriately:

•Positioning wires appropriately:

Positioning wires appropriately:

Before applying cement, arrange the wires in a way that minimizes the chance of movement or overlapping. Spacing the wires apart and securing them in their final positions ensures better isolation and stability during subsequent handling and recordings.•Using insulation material:

•Using insulation material:

Using insulation material:

For additional security, consider using insulating materials like heat-shrink tubing or medical-grade coating on the wires before cementing. These materials could add an extra layer of protection and reduce the risk of electrical interference.

Problem 3

Issues grounding the EEG array causing artifacts and contamination of all channels.

Improper grounding of the EEG array can lead to significant recording artifacts, including identical signals in all channels, large peaks, and motion-related noise contamination. These issues often arise from poor connection to grounding screws, inadequate insulation of the ground wire, or unintended connections to the ground wire. By following these steps, including proper insulation, careful handling, and thorough testing, grounding issues can be mitigated and clean, artifact-free recordings from all channels will be ensured.

Potential solution

• Expose only a small amount of ground wire for soldering:

• Expose only a small amount of ground wire for soldering:

Expose only a small amount of ground wire for soldering:

When preparing the ground wire, expose only a small section of the wire at its end for soldering, leaving the rest coated in Teflon insulation. This reduces the risk of unintended connections or interference along the wire.•Avoid pulling the ground wire from the screw electrode:

•Avoid pulling the ground wire from the screw electrode:

Avoid pulling the ground wire from the screw electrode:

Ensure the ground wire remains securely connected to the screw electrode. Pulling on the wire during handling or soldering can disconnect it, leading to grounding issues. Use careful techniques to handle and secure the wire during the procedure.•Prevent muscle or tissue from contacting the ground wire or screw:

•Prevent muscle or tissue from contacting the ground wire or screw:

Prevent muscle or tissue from contacting the ground wire or screw:

Ensure that no muscles or tissue come into direct contact with the ground wire or screw electrode, as this can introduce artifacts. Completely coat the ground wire and screw in dental cement to provide insulation and prevent such interactions.

Problem 4

Animal movement during plugging into Open Ephys system.

Excessive movement or agitation in animals can make it hard to connect the head stage to the tethered recording system. Movement during tethering is often due to insufficient habituation to the recording setup and the handling associated with connection procedures. By gradually acclimating animals to the recording environment and the tethering process itself, you can minimize stress and ensure smoother, more stable recordings.

Potential solution

•Habituation to the recording environment:

•Habituation to the recording environment:

Habituation to the recording environment:

As discussed previously, exposing animals to the recording chamber or arena daily for 2-5 days prior to actual the recording sessions can help to reduce their stress and/ or exploration of the new arena. Gradually increasing the duration of these sessions from 20 minutes up to 2+ hours can help animals become comfortable with the space, reducing anxiety and movement during real experiments.•Desensitization to the tethering process:

•Desensitization to the tethering process:

Desensitization to the tethering process:

Simulate the handling and tethering process in the days leading up to recording. For 2-5 minutes each day, handle and restrain the animals, gently touch the head stage, and go through mock plug-in motions without actually connecting. Over time, begin briefly plugging the animal in for short sessions, slowly increasing duration.•Positive reinforcement or calm handling:

•Positive reinforcement or calm handling:

Positive reinforcement or calm handling:

Use calm and consistent handling techniques to reduce stress. In some cases, positive reinforcement can help animals associate the process with neutral or positive experiences. Treats (e.g. peanuts or sunflower seeds) can be used as a reward after habituation or as a distraction while plugging in. Introduce the treats before the recording session to avoid any novelty-induced anxiety. Letting the animal sit on a raised platform (i.e. as an upside-down plant pot) while it holds and eats the treats can keep them in place for a few seconds to plug them in. Keep the handling duration to a minimum to avoid agitation. If the animal becomes stressed (i.e. vocalizing, behaving aggressively, defecating or urinating), do not keep attempting to plug them in. Instead, return them to their cage for 5-10 minutes to calm down before trying again.

Problem 5

Electrical artifacts during recording due to system being improperly grounded.

Recordings may contain electrical noise when it is not grounded correctly. Noise looks like 60 Hz oscillations (or 50 Hz in Europe) that are present throughout the recording and can have varying degrees of amplitude. It is possible to see them by eye during the recording, they will look like constant sine waves that occlude the electrophysiological data. Performing a spectral analyses on the recording will give a definite answer if electrical noise is present if a large peak is seen at 60 or 50 Hz. It is important to minimize external sources of noise before starting experimental recording as this can prevent the detection of brain signals such as specific brain states or seizures.

Potential solution

•Minimize electrical noise when setting up the system:

•Minimize electrical noise when setting up the system:

Minimize electrical noise when setting up the system:

Make sure that everything is connected to a common ground on the surface holding the system such as an air table or Faraday enclosure. This can be achieved through wires with electrical crocodile clips or by attaching wires to specific ports on equipment such as amplifiers. For the Open Ephys system, the ground is the BNC output on the back of the acquisition box.•Identify the source of noise:

•Identify the source of noise:

Identify the source of noise:

If noise is present, systematically turn off or unplug any pieces of equipment used in the experimental system or in its vicinity such as pulse stimulators, lights, cameras, etc. Noise may also be generated by metallic pieces of the setup that are not properly grounded acting as antennas. Remove these pieces or connect them to the common ground with crocodile clips.

Problem 6

Detection of False Positives in SWD Automated Analysis.

Although in our datasets false positives are relatively infrequent, the program may detect additional false positive SWDs due to differing levels of seizure activity or noise.

Potential solution

•Optimize parameters for your recordings:

•Optimize parameters for your recordings:

Optimize parameters for your recordings:
