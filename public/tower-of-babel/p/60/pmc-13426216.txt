Apatinib resistance mediated by IRF9 nuclear translocation in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Yi Song, Jie Lou, Jin Xu, Wen Cao, Jiawei Wang, Hongbing Zhou, Yin Yuan, Qingtao Ni, Chunhua Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryApatinib is being increasingly utilized in the treatment of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) patients. However, clinical resistance to apatinib is commonly observed, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. In this study, we established apatinib-resistant HNSCC cell lines and performed SPIA (small molecule protein interaction assay) to identify interferon regulatory factor 9 (IRF9) as a critical determinant of drug resistance. Mechanistically, apatinib binds to IRF9, enhancing protein flexibility and ability to bind the nuclear transport protein RAN and translocate into the nucleus. Once translocated, IRF9 facilitates tumor progression by the basal cell carcinoma pathway and the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. HY-N1435 (Oroxin B), a small-molecule drug targeting IRF9, was found to reverse apatinib resistance in HNSCC. Moreover, higher IRF9 protein expression levels are associated with an increased likelihood of apatinib resistance. Taken together, the current study provided critical insights into the apatinib resistance mechanisms of HNSCC and proposed therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC) originate from the mucosal tissues of the mouth, larynx, or pharynx.1 While human papillomavirus (HPV) infection accounts for a growing proportion of HNSCC cases and may constitute the majority, this association is largely confined to specific subtypes.2 The main treatment for resectable HNSCC is surgery, and those with high recurrence risk factors usually undergo postoperative chemoradiotherapy.3 However, most individuals diagnosed with HNSCC have locally advanced disease and need multiple forms of therapy.4 However, despite the improvement in survival rates with targeted immunotherapy, fewer than 20% of HNSCC patients achieve a durable response.5 As an alternative or complementary approach, apatinib mesylate, a small-molecule inhibitor targeting vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2), has been developed for advanced cancers. It has been approved for the treatment of advanced gastric adenocarcinoma or adenocarcinoma of the gastroesophageal junction following chemotherapy failure, and has been reported to prolong survival and improve the quality of life of the patients.6 By inhibiting VEGFR2, apatinib can block tumor angiogenesis and confer survival advantages in the treatment of advanced liver cancer.7 Combined with transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE), it can prolong the progression-free survival (PFS). The clinical trial for maintenance treatment using apatinib against recurrent/metastatic HNSCC reported the median PFS period as 4.4 months, the overall survival period as 6.6 months, and the disease control rate as 86.8%.8 Apatinib is also increasingly being utilized in the treatment of patients with head and neck cancer. However, drug resistance is a major obstacle to successful cancer therapy,9 and the mechanism underlying apatinib resistance in HNSCC cells still remains unclear.

Apatinib is a small molecule inhibitor that specifically targets VEGFR-2 in tumor cells. It binds to the VEGFR-2 protein and competitively inhibits ATP binding, thereby suppressing VEGFR-2 autophosphorylation and downstream signal transduction, ultimately leading to the inhibition of tumor-associated angiogenesis.6 This study aims to investigate whether apatinib, in addition to its therapeutic binding to VEGFR2, also interacts with another key protein involved in drug resistance development. In this study, we developed apatinib-resistant HNSCC cell lines, designated as HSC-3R and CNE-2R, to explore their biological characteristics. To elucidate the molecular targets of apatinib, we conducted small molecule protein interaction assay (SPIA) on both wild-type and HNSCC cells treated with apatinib at different time points. Through further analysis of the SPIA results, interferon regulatory factor 9 (IRF9) emerged as the most significant protein with its expression decreasing over time in correlation with the development of drug resistance. Consequently, IRF9 was identified as a critical determinant of resistance. Subsequent studies have confirmed that apatinib gradually accumulates in the cytoplasm and specifically binds to IRF9, thereby facilitating its translocation into the nucleus and enabling its function as a transcription factor.10 Our research provides valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms of IRF9 in HNSCC apatinib resistance, providing a potential target for reducing apatinib resistance in HNSCC.

Results

IRF9 is a potential binding protein of apatinib in HNSCC

To explore the mechanism underlying apatinib resistance in HNSCC, we conducted SPIA proteomics utilizing DIA (data independent acquisition) quantification to identify the receptors of apatinib (Figure 1A). Wild-type HNSCC cell lines(HSC-3), as well as cell lines treated with apatinib at various time points (12 h, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months), were utilized in the SPIA experiment. We initially conducted a comprehensive analysis of the mass spectrometry data across the different experimental groups. The results demonstrated that apatinib interacts with VEGFR2 in drug-resistant cells, but it exhibits a more pronounced binding affinity for IRF9 (Figure 1B). Subsequently, we analyzed the mass spectrometry data collected at various time points following drug administration, identified the top 15 proteins exhibiting the highest increase in apatinib-bound levels, and visualized these results using a heatmap (Figure 1C). We compared the top 15 protein expression trends over time in cells resistant to apatinib (Figure S1). Compared to the control group, IRF9 expression decreased as drug resistance progressed, indicating that apatinib binding to IRF9 increased as drug resistance progressed (Figure 1D). To explore the interaction between IRF9 and apatinib, we performed a molecular docking analysis. This analysis revealed a strong binding affinity between the two, with a binding energy of −9.3 kcal/mol (Figure 1E). Consequently, IRF9 was identified as a critical determinant of resistance. We hypothesize that binding of apatinib to IRF9 reduces its interaction with vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2, thereby inducing drug resistance. Conversely, the binding of apatinib to VEGFR2 did not exhibit significant alterations during the development of cellular resistance (Figure 1F). Then, we aim to investigate whether the RNA and protein expression levels of VEGFR2 and IRF9 have undergone any changes during the development of apatinib resistance. However, transcriptome sequencing analysis indicated no significant changes in the mRNA expression levels of VEGFR2 and IRF9 throughout the drug resistance process (Figure 1G). To further validate this result, we performed q-PCR and WB validation. IRF9 promotes tumor progression in HNSCC

We successfully established stable apatinib-resistant HNSCC cell lines (HSC-3R and CNE-2R) by IRF9 knockdown and overexpression to investigate the impact of IRF9 on the tumorigenic properties of HNSCC cells (Figure 2A). IRF9 knockdown decreased HNSCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas IRF9 overexpression enhanced the capabilities (Figures 2B and 2C). In addition, the percentage of EdU-incorporated live cells was significantly decreased in sh_IRF9 cells (IRF9 knockdown) and increased in ov_IRF9 (IRF9 overexpression) cells compared to that in the controls (Figure 2D). Flow cytometry was employed to assess the impact of IRF9 on tumor cell apoptosis and the cell cycle. IRF9 inhibition promoted early and late apoptosis (Figure 2E). Inhibition of IRF9 increased the percentage of HNSCC cells in the G1/G2 phase and decreased the percentage of cells in the S phase (Figure 2F). In vivo experiments with nude mice showed that IRF9 knockdown inhibited tumor growth, whereas IRF9 overexpression accelerated tumor growth (Figure 2G). In the lung metastasis model, the number of lung metastatic nodules was significantly decreased in the sh_IRF9 group but increased in the ov_IRF9 group (Figure 2H). Additionally, the Ki67 index and VEGFR2 expression decreased in IRF9-knockdown tumors and increased in IRF9-overexpressing tumors (Figure 2I). RAN-mediated nuclear translocation of IRF9 leads to apatinib resistance

Based on the aforementioned findings, IRF9 has been identified as a critical determinant of resistance and a promoter of tumor progression. However, the mechanism underlying IRF9’s role in apatinib resistance remains unclear, particularly since IRF9 expression levels do not change in resistant cells. Given that IRF9 functions as a transcription factor and operates within the nucleus, we conducted immunofluorescence (IF) assays to investigate any changes in its subcellular localization. The results demonstrated that IRF9 translocates to the nucleus in response to apatinib resistance (Figure 3A). Western blot analysis further revealed that nuclear IRF9 expression is higher than in the cytoplasm (Figure 3B). Considering the potential interaction between IRF9 and VEGFR2, we questioned whether VEGFR2 also translocates to the nucleus. However, IF analysis indicated no significant difference in VEGFR2 expression between wild-type and apatinib-resistant cells (Figure S2). This raises the question of what changes occur due to the binding of VEGFR2 and IRF9. To explore this, a 25-nanosecond molecular dynamics simulation was conducted, yielding gyration, root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) values for each ligand-protein interaction. The binding of apatinib to the IRF9 protein results in increased flexibility and enhanced mobility of the protein (Figures 3C and 3D). This interaction leads to increased jitter of the N- and C-terminal peptides, thereby augmenting the overall mobility of the protein (Figures 3E and 3F). However, the mechanism by which IRF9 translocates into the nucleus remains unclear. To investigate this, immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed on both wild-type and resistant cell lines using an IRF9 antibody (Figure 3G). Subsequent mass spectrometry analysis of the IP samples revealed a significant increase in the binding of the nuclear transport protein RAN to IRF9 in resistant cell lines (Figure 3H). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) validation further confirmed the enhanced interaction between IRF9 and RAN in apatinib-resistant cell lines (Figure 3I). To substantiate the role of RAN in mediating the nuclear translocation of IRF9, RAN knockdown cells (sh-RAN) were established and validated (Figure 3J). RAN knockdown resulted in a marked reduction of IRF9 expression in the nucleus of resistant cell lines, while the overall expression of IRF9 in the cells remained unaffected (Figures 3K and 3L). Is the increased binding of IRF9 and apatinib attributable to the drug’s intracellular accumulation effect, given that their combination promotes nuclear translocation? To address this, LC-MS/MS was employed to quantify intracellular drug levels at various time points throughout the progression of apatinib resistance. As resistance to apatinib developed, the intracellular level of the drug gradually increased (Figures 3M–3O). We therefore propose that during the acquisition of drug resistance, apatinib accumulates within cells and continuously transports IRF9 into the nucleus, ultimately leading to the resistant phenotype.Figure 3RAN-mediated nuclear translocation of IRF9 leads to apatinib resistance(A) IF of IRF9 in WT and apatinib resistance cells. IRF9 affects basal cell carcinoma signaling pathway

As a classic transcription factor, IRF9 may regulate the transcription of downstream genes after binding with apatinib and translocating into the nucleus. To further explore the downstream effects of IRF9 in HNSCC, the differential expression of RNA between the ov_IRF9 group (HSC-3R) and control group (HSC-3R) was analyzed (Figure 4A). IRF9 ChIP signals were highly enriched near the transcription start sites (Figure 4B). IRF9 mostly bound to the promoters (Figure 4C). RNA-seq and ChIP-seq results were combined for BETA analysis (Figure 4D). A total of 1,041 upregulated and 371 downregulated genes were identified using both RNA-seq and ChIP-seq (Figure 4E). The KEGG pathway enrichment results revealed that the upregulated genes were most significantly enriched in basal cell carcinoma (Figure 4F; Figure S3). The heatmap shows that the basal cell carcinoma-related genes sonic hedgehog (SHH), WNT11, WNT16, frizzled class receptor 8 (FZD8), glioma-associated oncogene homolog 1 (GLI1), and transcription factor 7 (TCF7) were upregulated in ov_IRF9 (Figure 4G). Subsequent validation experiments demonstrated significant downregulation of these genes in the sh_IRF9 cells (Figures 4H and 4I). Literature reviews revealed that SHH promotes proliferation through GLI1, and further promotes FZD8-induced TCF7 proliferation and invasion through WNT11/16. The target genes within the basal cell carcinoma signaling pathway are primarily associated with the proliferation and invasion of tumor cells. IRF9 promotes the nuclear translocation of the STAT1/2 protein complex and activates the JAK-STAT signaling pathway

To elucidate the mechanism by which IRF9 influences apoptosis and cell-cycle progression in HNSCC cells, we investigated the potential modes of action of IRF9. As a TF, IRF9 may be implicated in a signaling pathway that potentially interacts with the basal cell carcinoma signaling pathway, thereby influencing the progression of HNSCC. Additionally, our findings revealed that the JAK-STAT signaling pathway associated with IRF9 was significantly enriched. This suggested that the JAK-STAT signaling pathway might play a role in the chemoresistance of HNSCC. We analyzed the DEGs in the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and found SOCS2/3 (suppressor of cytokine signaling 2/3), PIM1, CCND1/2 (cyclin D1), and MYC to be enriched in the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and upregulated in ov_IRF9 (Figure 5A; Figure S4). Subsequent validation experiments demonstrated significant downregulation of SOCS2, PIM1, CCND1/2, and MYC in sh_IRF9 cells (Figures 5B and 5C). We investigated the role of these phenotype-associated genes in the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, which primarily regulates apoptosis and cell-cycle progression. Our analysis further substantiated our previous hypotheses. In the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, IRF9 combines with STAT1 and STAT2 to form the ISGF3 complex, which is a key TF for interferon (IFN) signal transduction. When cells are stimulated by IFN, JAKs are activated and phosphorylate the tyrosine residues of STAT1 and STAT2, enabling them to form dimers and bind to IRF9.11 Eventually, they translocate to the nucleus and bind to ISREs (interferon-sensitive response elements) to initiate the transcription of ISGs (such as Mx1, OAS, and other antiviral genes).12 Additionally, IRF9, through its DNA-binding domain, cooperates with STAT1/STAT2 to enhance ISG transcriptional efficiency. This cooperative effect is crucial for antiviral response and immune regulation.13 In order to understand whether IRF9 forms a complex with STAT proteins and participates in nuclear import in HNSCC, IP was conducted to investigate the interactions across IRF9, STAT1, and STAT2. The results demonstrated that IRF9, STAT1, and STAT2 directly interacted with one another in the context of HNSCC (Figure 5D). In order to address whether IRF9 plays a dominant role in the nuclear import of the complex upon binding to STAT proteins, we investigated the nuclear expression of STAT1 and STAT2 in HNSCC cells after IRF9 knockdown. The results demonstrated that STAT1 and STAT2 cannot be efficiently transported into the nucleus without the assistance of IRF9. IRF9 activates the JAK3-STAT3 signaling pathway by enhancing the transcription of IL10 and JAK3

Unexpectedly, upon analyzing the IRF9 transcriptome sequencing and ChIP-seq data, we observed that IRF9 distinctly bound to the promoter regions of IL10 and JAK3. Furthermore, the transcript levels of IL10 and JAK3 were remarkably elevated following IRF9 overexpression (Figure 5G). We next investigated whether IRF9 could enhance JAK-STAT signaling by activating the IL10-JAK3-STAT3 pathway through the promotion of IL10 and JAK3 gene transcription. The expression of IL10 and JAK3 decreased after the inhibition of IRF9, as confirmed by qRT-PCR and western blotting, thereby confirming that IRF9 increased the mRNA and protein levels of IL10 and JAK3 (Figures 5H and 5I). It has been reported that IL-10 activates the expression of target genes by enabling the translocation of phosphorylated STAT3 homodimers to the nucleus.14 Therefore, we investigated the effect of IRF9 on total STAT3 protein levels and the phosphorylation of STAT3, which is essential for its nuclear transcriptional function. The results confirmed that IRF9 enhanced the phosphorylation of STAT3 protein Y705 (tyrosine 705), but not of S727 (serine 727), and had no significant effect on the total STAT3 protein level (Figure 4J). In the JAK3-STAT3 signaling pathway, STAT3 undergoes critical phosphorylation at two major sites, Y705 and S727, which are essential for its activation and functional regulation. Y705, located in the C-terminal region of STAT3, serves as a key site for its direct phosphorylation by JAK kinases, including JAK3. Following phosphorylation, STAT3 forms homodimers via its SH2 domain and translocates to the nucleus to regulate the transcription of target genes, thereby participating in processes such as cell proliferation and immune responses.15 S727, situated within the transcriptional activation domain (TAD) of STAT3, is phosphorylated by kinases such as MAPK and mTOR. Phosphorylation at S727 enhances the transcriptional activity of STAT3 and may influence cellular metabolism and tumor progression by modulating mitochondrial functions, including oxidative phosphorylation.16 Therefore, IRF9 could enhance the expression of p-STAT3-Y705 by upregulating the transcription of upstream IL10/JAK3, thereby promoting its nuclear translocation and ultimately modulating the transcriptional levels of downstream genes associated with tumor cell apoptosis and the cell cycle.

Oroxin B inhibits tumor progression in HNSCC

Given the critical role of IRF9 in HNSCC progression, we undertook several translational research initiatives. We conducted small-molecule docking experiments to identify potential functional inhibitors of IRF9, and the results are presented in Figure 6A. A total of 62,600 compounds were comprehensively screened in the MCE small-molecule drug database, and the top five candidates were selected for further investigation. p-STAT3 was identified as a key downstream molecule, and its inhibitory effect on small-molecule drugs was examined. Western blot analysis of STAT3 and p-STAT3 confirmed the inhibitory effects of the five small-molecule compounds (Figure 6B). HY-N1435 (Oroxin B) and Z56772943 (2,2'-((6-carboxypyrimidine-2,4-diyl)bis(azanediyl))dibenzoic acid) showed the strongest inhibitory effects on p-STAT3. Kinetic analysis of the binding between IRF9 and the tested drugs (HY-N1435 and Z56772943) was performed using an SPR-based Biacore instrument. Oroxin B exhibited higher dissociation constants than Z56772943 (Figure 6C). The IC50 of Oroxin B was determined using an apatinib-resistant HNSCC cell line (Figure S5). Oroxin B decreased the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities (Figures 6D–6F). In addition, the percentage of EdU-incorporated live cells significantly decreased in the Oroxin B group (Figure 6G). To confirm the on-target action of Oroxin B, we employed sh_IRF9 HSC-3R cells. The anti-proliferative effects of Oroxin B, were substantially attenuated in sh_IRF9 cells compared to HSC-3R controls (Figure S6), demonstrating that its efficacy is primarily mediated through targeting IRF9. In vivo experiments in nude mice showed that Oroxin B inhibited apatinib-resistant tumor growth (Figure 6H). In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed by injecting saline and Oroxin B into the tail vein of mice with cancer cells (Figure 6I). In the lung metastasis model, mice treated with Oroxin B showed a significant reduction in the number of metastatic lung nodules (Figure 6J). Discussion

Apatinib, an orally administered tyrosine kinase VEGFR2 inhibitor, has demonstrated significant efficacy in the treatment of HNSCC.17,18 However, it reverses the multidrug resistance of chemotherapeutic agents while suffering drug resistance.19 We hypothesized that apatinib interacts with proteins beyond VEGFR2, contributing to resistance. SPIA experiments revealed that IRF9 binding affinity to apatinib progressively increased during resistance acquisition, while VEGFR2 binding remained stable.

IRF9 not only regulates innate and adaptive immune responses but also plays an important role in inflammation, antiviral response, and cell development.20 Its overexpression leads to increased oncogenic behavior in lung cancer.21 IRF9 promotes CRC development by modulating the IL-6/STAT3 signaling axis.22 The loss of IRF9 leads to increased resistance to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors in breast cancer.23 In this study, IRF9 overexpression enhanced tumorigenic capacity in apatinib-resistant HNSCC cells, whereas IRF9 knockdown attenuated tumor progression. Moreover, clinical data indicated that IRF9 upregulation is associated with apatinib resistance.

Mechanistically, increasing of IRF9 nuclear translocation was observed. The nuclear translocation of transcription factors in response to therapeutic stress has been documented in various cancer contexts.24 The mechanism of improved nuclear import was uncovered by molecular dynamics simulations, showing that apatinib binding enhances the conformational flexibility of IRF9, especially in its N- and C-terminal areas. This alteration in structure probably reveals nuclear localization signals (NLSs) on IRF9, enhancing its identification by RAN. RAN is a small GTPase related to Ras that plays a key role in nucleocytoplasmic transport, switching between GTP- and GDP-bound forms to guide cargo.25 This interaction facilitates nuclear transport of IRF9. The RAN GTPase system is well-established as a key regulator of nucleocytoplasmic transport.26,27 Moreover, RAN knockdown effectively abolishes nuclear IRF9 accumulation. Interestingly, we observed that apatinib gradually accumulated in tumor cells. The concurrent rise in intracellular apatinib levels implies that nuclear import of the drug-IRF9 complex diminished VEGFR2 inhibition of apatinib. Our findings reveal that IRF9 undergoes nuclear accumulation in resistant cells via RAN-mediated transport, a process facilitated by direct interaction between IRF9 and apatinib.

Subsequently, co-analysis of RNA-seq and ChIP-seq data revealed that IRF9 was most significantly enriched in the basal cell carcinoma signaling pathway (significantly enriched genes were SHH, WNT11, WNT16, FZD8, GLI1, and TCF7). Inhibition of the SHH pathway is known to decrease proliferation.28 The SHH-GLI1 signaling pathway regulates epithelial-mesenchymal transition and cancer stem-like cells during prostate cancer development.29 Further, the SHH-GLI1 pathway is involved in cell proliferation.30 FZD8 promotes gastric cancer invasion and metastasis via the β-catenin signaling pathway.31 Inhibition of the TCF7 pathway decreases CRC cell proliferation in colorectal cancer.32 WNT11 promotes cell proliferation in small cell lung cancer cell lines.33 The increased expression of WNT11 and WNT16 induces leiomyoma stem cell proliferation and tumor growth.34 Taken together, SHH may be considered to promote proliferation through GLI1 and enhance FZD8-induced TCF7 cell proliferation and invasion via WNT11/16. We confirmed that IRF9 could regulate apoptosis and cell cycle in cellular phenotype experiments. Consequently, the basal cell carcinoma signaling pathway alone cannot fully elucidate the mechanism of IRF9 action, suggesting the involvement of additional signaling pathways.

The JAK-STAT pathway is recognized for its role in triggering apoptosis35 and regulating the cell cycle,36 with IRF9 also playing a direct part in the JAK-STAT pathway, as reported previously in the literature.37 The JAK-STAT signaling pathway (significantly enriched genes being SOCS3, SOCS2, PIM1, CCND1, CCND2, and MYC) was identified as highly significant and ranked prominently in our transcriptome-sequencing data and KEGG pathway analysis. Targeting SOCS2 resulted in the de-repression of the oncogenic kinase JAK2, leading to enhanced STAT3 signaling.38 PIM1-targeted degradation prevents chemoresistance in prostate cancer.39 CCND1 silencing suppresses liver cancer stem cell differentiation.40 MYC plays a central role in almost every aspect of oncogenesis by orchestrating proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, and metabolism.41 We found that the phenotype-related genes in the JAK-STAT pathway were primarily associated with cell apoptosis and the cell cycle. Consequently, we proposed that IRF9 synergistically facilitates the malignant progression of HNSCC through a crosstalk between the two pathways. In this study, we confirmed the downregulation of MYC, SOCS2, PIM1, and CCND1/2 after IRF9 knockdown, indicating that IRF9 can modulate the tumor phenotype of HNSCC cells by regulating these genes. In JAK-STAT signaling pathway, the occurrence of IRF9 is special since it mainly forms a complex with STAT1/2 and promotes the nuclear import of the complex through the exposure of its own NLS.42 Our results also confirmed that IRF9 directs the two proteins into the nucleus.

In addition, our findings revealed that IRF9 directly binds to the promoter regions of IL10 and JAK3. This binding regulates the expression of genes that are critical components of the JAK3-STAT3 signaling pathway.43 Our results demonstrated that IRF9 regulates the mRNA and protein expression of IL10 and JAK3. According to the KEGG pathway, IL-10 and JAK3 are responsible for phosphorylating STAT3 homodimers and transporting them to the nucleus. Hence, the phosphorylation of STAT3 was analyzed, revealing that IRF9 enhances phosphorylation at the key Y705 residue without altering the overall protein levels. Overall, our study indicated that IRF9 plays a dual role in JAK-STAT3 signaling, facilitating downstream gene transcription by promoting the nuclear translocation of STAT1/2 proteins, and functioning as a TF by upregulating IL10/JAK3 RNA and protein levels, thereby activating STAT3 phosphorylation and its subsequent nuclear translocation.

IRF9 plays a pivotal role in apatinib resistance in HNSCC cells. Consequently, we conducted preliminary translational research focusing on IRF9. This involved analyzing the protein structure of IRF9, conducting molecular docking simulations, screening tens of thousands of small-molecule compounds, and identifying the top five candidates for further validation. We confirmed that Oroxin B is the most effective inhibitor of IRF9 protein function. Furthermore, Oroxin B was identified to inhibit tumor progression both in vivo and in vitro. Oroxin B is a flavonoid that has significant anti-liver cancer properties.44 It has been identified as an anti-cancer agent that triggers apoptosis by deactivating the PTEN/PI3K/AKT pathway in liver cancer.45

Clinically, high IRF9 expression was associated with shorter PFS. And, a high N/C IRF9 ratio was also significantly associated with shorter PFS. These findings further supporting our mechanistic discovery that RAN-mediated nuclear import of IRF9 drives resistance to the clinical setting.

Therefore, we have identified the transcription factor IRF9 as a critical mediator of apatinib resistance in HNSCC. Mechanistically, apatinib accumulates within tumor cells and binds to cytoplasmic IRF9, inducing a conformational change that promotes its nuclear translocation via a RAN-dependent mechanism. Once in the nucleus, IRF9 drives multiple tumor-associated phenotypes that confer apatinib resistance by orchestrating crosstalk between the basal cell carcinoma signaling pathway, which enhances proliferation and invasion, and the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, which promotes cell-cycle progression and suppresses apoptosis (Figure 7E).

While our study elucidated apatinib resistance mechanisms in HNSCC, whether IRF9-mediated resistance generalizes to other VEGFR2 TKIs remains unclear. This raises the possibility that IRF9 activation represents a unique vulnerability of apatinib, though future studies must determine if other anti-angiogenic TKIs similarly engage this pathway.

Our findings identify IRF9 as a key transcription factor contributing to apatinib resistance in HNSCC. Apatinib accumulation and RAN-mediated nuclear translocation of IRF9 promoted tumor progression, and elevated IRF9 expression was associated with poor patient prognosis. Targeting IRF9 thus represents a promising therapeutic strategy. We further demonstrated that IRF9 mediates crosstalk between the basal cell carcinoma and JAK/STAT signaling pathways, inducing apatinib resistance in HNSCC. Accordingly, the small-molecule compound Oroxin B, which targets IRF9, effectively suppressed HNSCC progression. Together, these results elucidate the mechanistic role of IRF9 in HNSCC pathogenesis and highlight its potential as a therapeutic vulnerability.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the resistant models were derived from only two HNSCC cell lines, and the clinical cohort was retrospective and single-center. The precise NLS of IRF9 and its RAN-interacting domain have not been mapped. Whether IRF9-mediated resistance applies to other VEGFR2 TKIs is unclear. Because most patients received apatinib-based combination therapies, high IRF9 expression may reflect general poor prognosis rather than specific apatinib resistance.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

Human HNSCC cell lines HSC-3 and CNE-2 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Cell lines were authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination. No mycoplasma contamination was detected.

Animal models

Tumor xenograft model

In 4-week-old female BALB/c mice, 3 × 106 cells per group of wild-type (WT), IRF9 knockdown (sh_IRF9), and IRF9 overexpressing (ov_IRF9) HSC-3 cells were administered subcutaneously. The tumor sizes in the axillary region were observed and noted on day 3 after injection. On day 21, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumors were surgically extracted and stored for immunohistochemical examination.

Lung metastasis model

The HSC-3 cell line, with a fluorescent label, was used to establish a lung metastasis model in BALB/c-nude mice; 3 × 106 cells were resuspended in 100 μL PBS and injected into mice through the tail vein (in 10 mice). After consistent fluorescence detection, the BALB/c-nude mice were randomly divided into two groups (five mice per group), and the drug or saline was injected into the tail vein.

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hanjiang Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Approval No. HJSW 21081001) and conducted in accordance with the institution’s guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (Charles River Laboratories) were used for xenograft and metastasis studies. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle, controlled temperature and humidity, and free access to food and water.

Human subjects

This study randomly obtained 112 tumor sections from patients who underwent apatinib-based combination therapies for HNSCC at Taizhou People’s Hospital between January 2011 and December 2023. The enrollment criteria were as follows: postoperative pathohistological confirmation of HNSCC, and comprehensive follow-up data. All participants provided informed consent and the study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Taizhou People’s Hospital (Approval No. 2021-040-01) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Method details

Establishment of apatinib-resistant cell lines

Apatinib resistance was stably modeled using sublethal concentrations of apatinib in the growth medium (Figure S7A). The 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) of apatinib was determined in HNSCC cell lines (Figures S7B and S7C). The dose administered was approximately equal to the IC50 value for each cell line. Subsequently, the medium was removed to allow cell recovery, a process that spanned approximately six months. Finally, stable resistant clones were obtained (Figure S7D).

SPIA proteomics

The experimental method was according to the literature reports.46 Wild-type HNSCC cell lines (HSC-3), as well as cell lines treated with apatinib at various time points (12 h, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months), were utilized in the SPIA experiment. After protein in HSC-3 cells was degraded, the peptide fragments were desalted on a C18 cartridge (Sep-Pak plus C18 cartridge; Waters, Milford, MA). Desalted peptides were dried and reconstituted with 0.1% FA for liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. The LC-MS/MS raw files were imported into Spectronaut software for protein/peptide identification and quantification. The interaction loci-pairs were marked in the small molecule-protein complexes predicted via molecular docking simulation.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and transcriptomic profiling

RNA-seq libraries were created using the KAPA stranded mRNA-seq kit and analyzed using real-time quantitative PCR, with sequencing performed on an Illumina HiSeq platform. Splice sequences were extracted from raw downstream data using Cutadapt, whereas Trimmomatic removed the low-quality sequences. FastQC was used to calculate the data quantity and Q20 and Q30 ratios. Clean data were aligned with the reference genome in HISAT. Venn diagram was drawn using the online software (http://bioinformatics. psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/).

Protein extraction and western blotting

RIPA lysis buffer was used for protein extraction, and protein concentrations were measured using bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assays (ProCell Life Science & Technology, Wuhan, China). Proteins were separated via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto PVDF nanofiber membranes. Enhanced chemiluminescence was used to detect the immunoreactive proteins.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted with an RNA extraction solution and subjected to a 3-min trichloromethane treatment at room temperature. RNA concentration was evaluated spectrophotometrically using NanoDrop 2000. All PCRs were performed with 2× SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and a PE 9700 thermocycler (PE Applied Biosystems). The thermocycling protocol included an initial step at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 20 s, and 72°C for 40 s. Primer sequences were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biological Engineering (Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescence analysis

The cells were treated with a membrane-disrupting working solution and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. They were then washed three times with PBS for 5 min each time. The samples were evenly coated with 3% BSA and left to seal at room temperature for 30 min. Then they were incubated overnight at 4°C with the appropriate primary antibody. The cell pore plate was washed three times, each wash lasting 5 min. The appropriate secondary antibodies were then added and left to incubate at room temperature for 50 min. The plate was subsequently washed with PBS. Once the slices had dried partially, DAPI dye was applied within the circle and left to incubate for 10 min at room temperature, protected from light. The climbing film was immersed in PBS and washed by shaking 3 times for 5 min each. The slide was then gently dried and sealed with a sealant to prevent fluorescence quenching. Fluorescence microscopy was then used to observe the immunofluorescence signals.

Immunohistochemistry and nuclear/cytoplasmic (N/C) localization analysis

Tumor sections were fixed and stained using anti-human polyclonal antibodies against Ki67, VEGFR2, MYC, TCF7, p-STAT3, and IRF9 and then incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase. The sections were treated with a developing solution and counterstained with hematoxylin. The stains were fixed with acidic alcohol and dehydrated. The subcellular localization of IRF9 was semi-quantitatively assessed by two independent pathologists blinded to the clinical data. Both staining intensity (0: negative, 1: weak, 2: moderate, 3: strong) and the percentage of positive tumor cells (0: <5%, 1: 5–25%, 2: 26–50%, 3: 51–75%, 4: >75%) were scored separately for the nucleus and cytoplasm. A nuclear score and a cytoplasmic score were calculated by multiplying the respective intensity and percentage scores. The nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio was then derived for each case. Cases with an N/C ratio >1.5 were classified as having nuclear-dominant IRF9 localization.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For the Co-IP assay, cells were lysed with a modified TNE buffer (50 mM Tris [pH 8.0], 1% Nonidet P-40 [NP-40], 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 10 mM sodium fluoride, and 10 mM sodium pyrophosphate) supplemented with 1 mg/L aprotinin, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate (Na3VO4), and 1 mg/L leupeptin. The lysates were centrifuged and cleared by incubation with 25 μL of Protein A/G gel for 1.5 h at 4°C. The precleared supernatant was subjected to IP using the indicated primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Subsequently, the protein complexes were collected by incubation with 30 μL of Protein A/G gel for 2 h at 4°C. Protein complexes were resolved by SDS-PAGE, and a western blot analysis was performed.

IP-mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

For proteomic identification of IRF9-interacting proteins, Co-IP was performed as described above. After stringent washing, immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted by boiling in SDS sample buffer and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. Each gel lane was excised, destained, reduced, alkylated, and digested with trypsin overnight. The resulting peptides were desalted and analyzed by LC-MS/MS. Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) was used with HCD fragmentation. Raw files were searched against the human UniProt database using MaxQuant (v2.4.2.0) with trypsin specificity, up to two missed cleavages, and FDR <1%. Label-free quantification (LFQ) was enabled.

Transfections of cell lines

Lentiviral packaging plasmids were generated for the empty vector, overexpression, and knockdown. Fluorescence microscopy was used to evaluate the transfection efficiency 48 h after transfection, and overexpression was measured by western blotting.

Transwell assay

The transwell assay was used to conduct cell migration and invasion assays. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and examined under a 200× microscope; the images were captured in five randomly chosen fields.

Cell proliferation assay

Proliferation was evaluated by the clonogenic assay and 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay. For clonogenic assay, cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well. Plates were incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator, with medium changed every 3 days. After visible colonies formed, the medium was aspirated,. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, then stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution for 20 min. Colonies containing ≥50 cells were counted manually under a light microscope.

Cell proliferation was also assessed using a Cell-Light EdU DNA Cell Proliferation Kit (RiboBio, C10310). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. EdU was then added to each well at a final concentration of 50 μM, and cells were incubated for an additional 2 h at 37°C. Then, cells were fixed with 50 μL of fixative and stained using Apollo Dye Solution. EdU-positive cells were imaged and counted.

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis was detected using flow cytometry with the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit I (Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Apoptosis was measured using the FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Cell cycle analysis

A cell cycle kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Haimen, China) was used for cell cycle analysis. HNSCC cells underwent three washes with cold PBS before being fixed in 70% ethanol at −20°C for 12 h. After fixation, the cells were resuspended in cold PBS with 200 mg/mL ribonuclease (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) and stained with 0.5 mL of propidium iodide (PI) staining buffer at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. A BD FACSVerse flow cytometer was used to analyze cell cycle progression. The FlowJo software (Tree Star, Inc., Ashland, OR, USA) was used to assess the distribution of cells in the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases.

Molecular dynamic simulation

The structural data for IRF9 was acquired from the Protein DataBank (www.rcsb.org). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of IRF9 were executed utilizing the Amber 16 software suite (http://ambermd.org/). These simulations were conducted under conditions of constant temperature and pressure, with periodic boundary conditions, using Gromacs version 2019.6. The AMBER14SB all-atom force field was employed alongside the TIP3P water model. Throughout the MD simulations, the LINCS algorithm was applied to constrain all bonds involving hydrogen atoms, with an integration time step of 2 fs. Electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle-Mesh Ewald (PME) method, with a non-bonded interaction cutoff of 10 Å, updated every 10 steps. The V-rescale thermostat was utilized to maintain the simulation temperature at 298.15 K, while the Parrinello-Rahman barostat was used to regulate pressure at 1 bar. Initially, energy minimization of the systems was achieved using the steepest descent method to prevent atomic overlap. This was followed by equilibration under NVT and NPT conditions for 1 ns each at 298.15 K. Finally, a 100-nanosecond MD simulation was performed, with system conformations recorded every 10 ps. The simulation results were visualized using the embedded Gromacs program and VMD.

Measurement of apatinib

LC-MS/MS was employed to determine the intracellular concentration of apatinib. Cell samples were homogenized in 200 μL of PBS using a Next Advance Bullet Blender. Apatinib was then extracted from 50 μL of the homogenate using 150 μL of acetonitrile containing 2.5 ng/mL of internal standard. After centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred to an autosampler vial for LC-MS/MS analysis. Quantification was performed using a standard curve prepared in PBS. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Waters Cortecs C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 2.7 μm) maintained at room temperature, with a gradient elution at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient program was as follows: initial hold at 10% B for 0.5 min, increase to 100% B over 0.5 min, hold at 100% B for 1 min, then return to 10% B followed by a 1 min re-equilibration period. The total run time was 3 min. Detection was carried out using a Sciex Triple Quad 5500 mass spectrometer. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) was used for quantitation. Calibration curves were constructed by plotting the peak area ratio of apatinib to the internal standard against nominal concentrations, using a quadratic regression model with 1/x2 weighting. The method was validated over a concentration range of 0.1–100 ng/mL, with appropriate dilution (up to 1:100, v/v) as needed.

ChIP-seq

First, the cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde. Subsequently, they were disrupted, leading to the release of cytoplasmic contents, and the nuclei were collected. The nuclei were lysed, and the DNA-protein complex was extracted and broken down into 200–1000-bp fragments. To obtain high-quality clean reads, the original sequencing data were filtered and compared with the reference genome. The input was used as the background, and MACS software was employed for peak-calling IP.

Prediction of potential sites of IRF9 and virtual screening

The IRF9 crystal structure was obtained from the RCSB PDB database. The protein was dewatered through hydrogenation using the Protein Preparation Wizard module, followed by energy optimization using the OPLS2005 force field and an RMSD of 0.30 Å. Using the Receptor Grid Generation module, a grid file was created from the processed proteins, centering on IRF9, with a box size of 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å. The GLIDE software (Schrodinger Maestro 11.4) was employed to virtually screen two commercial libraries with more than 62,600 compounds to target the predicted sites. The five compounds with the highest scores were sourced from the MCE and used in the experiments.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

Inhibitors targeting IRF9 were detected using the Biacore T200 SPR sensor and CM5 sensor chip, both from GE Healthcare, USA. The recombinant human IRF9 protein (custom-made, Cloud-Clone, cat#RPH780Hu01) was immobilized on the Fc2, 3, and 4 channels using the CM5 chip amino coupling method, with the Fc1 channel serving as the reference for activation and blocking. A series of concentrations was prepared based on solubility and core surface tests of the compounds. The conditions for compound determination were as follows: 120 s for compound injection and 180 s for dissociation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as means and their standard deviation. An unpaired Student’s t test or a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used for data analysis. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to conduct survival analysis. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software), and the significance was determined at p < 0.05.
