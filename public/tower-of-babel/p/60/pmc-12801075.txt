Medial Patellofemoral Complex Reconstruction Using the Rectus Femoris Tendon
Authors: Anshu Shekhar, Sachin Tapasvi, Camilo Helito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The medial proximal static stabilizers of the patella are the medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) and the medial quadriceps tendon femoral ligament. These are collectively referred to as the “medial patellofemoral complex” (MPFC). MPFL reconstruction is the workhorse of patellar stabilization surgery for recurrent lateral instability. We describe a technique for reconstructing the MPFC using the ipsilateral rectus tendon. The tendon is harvested via a curved superomedial incision using an open stripper, preserving its distal attachment. The tendon is prepared, flipped, and passed in the sub-vastus space toward the medial femoral epicondyle. A small longitudinal incision is used to retrieve the tendon. The anatomic attachment of the MPFC is identified using both palpatory and radiographic landmarks. A 25-mm-long loop of tendon is sutured and docked within a socket of the same diameter. Fixation is performed using a 7 × 25-mm interference screw with the knee in 20° of flexion and lateral patellar glide. This limb reconstructs the medial quadriceps tendon femoral ligament component. The rest of the tendon is shuttled back to the superomedial patella in the previous tissue plane and fixed at the junction of the proximal and middle thirds of the patella using a 2.8-mm suture anchor. This limb reconstructs the MPFL.

The patho-etiology of recurrent lateral patella dislocation (RLPD) is multifactorial and includes risk factors such as trochlear dysplasia, patella alta, increased distance between the tubercle tuberosity and trochlear groove, increased lateral patellar tilt, genu valgum, torsional abnormalities of the femur or tibia, and hyperlaxity.1 The medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) is the primary static soft-tissue restraint of the knee, preventing lateral tracking, and is frequently injured in patients with RLPD. However, another distinct structure, the medial quadriceps tendon femoral ligament (MQTFL), is also consistently present.2 Performing MPFL reconstruction (MPFLR) is currently the cornerstone of surgical management for any patient with RLPD.3 An isolated MPFLR can be safely and successfully used even in patients with lesser degrees of bony abnormalities or combined with other procedures when greater abnormalities are present.4,5

Reconstructing both the MPFL and the MQTFL, collectively referred to as the “medial patellofemoral complex” (MPFC), has been proposed to provide better patellar tracking by restoring anatomy more accurately.6 MQTFL reconstruction has been described using either the full thickness or the superficial lamina of the quadriceps tendon.7,8 Techniques for MPFC reconstruction using either hamstring autograft or allograft have shown promising results and are useful in both skeletally immature and older patients.9,10 The central issues in this reconstruction remain (1) placing the graft at the native insertions on the patella, quadriceps tendon, and femur to restore the anatomy and (2) preventing over-tensioning of the construct, which can increase patellofemoral pressure and predispose to patellofemoral arthrosis.3 We present our technique for MPFC reconstruction using the ipsilateral rectus femoris tendon, with a proximal limb reconstructing the MQTFL and a distal limb reconstructing the MPFL.

Surgical Technique

Patient Position and Preparation

The surgical procedure can be performed under either neuraxial or general anesthesia, with prophylactic antimicrobial coverage. The patient is positioned in the supine position with a high-thigh tourniquet, a lateral thigh support, and a foot stop at 90° of knee flexion. An image intensifier is required and must be brought in from the same side to obtain a true lateral view of the knee. The entire limb is draped and prepared in a standard manner.

Rectus Femoris Graft Harvest and Preparation

A 3- to 4-cm-long curved horizontal incision is made at the superomedial pole of the patella after infiltration with 2% lidocaine (Fig 1A). A full-thickness fasciocutaneous flap is raised circumferentially to visualize the vastus medialis obliquus muscle superiorly, the entire width of the quadriceps tendon laterally, the anterior surface of the superior half of the patella inferiorly, and the medial retinaculum medially. The quadriceps tendon is delineated, and a vertical incision is made in the rectus tendon with a No. 15 blade close to the lateral border, about 2.5 to 3 cm proximal to the superior pole of the patella. The rectus tendon is the anterior (superficial)–most layer of the quadriceps tendon. It is best differentiated from the rest of the tendon at this point and blends more distally.8 Another parallel incision is made 7 mm medial to the first incision, with care taken not to penetrate the intermediate or deep layer of the quadriceps tendon. The knee is extended, the skin flap is retracted proximally, and a plane is developed deep to the rectus tendon using curved Metzenbaum scissors. The parallel incisions in the tendon are carefully extended proximally for a distance of 4 to 5 cm or until the musculotendinous junction, using Metzenbaum scissors. A Mixter forceps is then inserted deep to the rectus tendon and slid proximally and distally to ensure the tendon is adequately free (Fig 1B). If an adhesion is still present, the dissection should be revised until there is smoother and freer excursion of the tendon. The distal (patellar) attachment is carefully preserved. An open tendon stripper (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is inserted around the tendon, ensuring that the entire tendon lies within the stripper loop. The knee is brought to approximately 30° of flexion, and the stripper is advanced in a gradual and firm direction slightly lateral to the midline. Harvesting the rectus in this manner is markedly different from other tendon-harvesting techniques because of its contiguous anatomy. The tendon needs to be separated not only from its muscle belly but also from the surrounding attachments of the quadriceps apparatus. Once a give way is felt, the stripper is left in situ and the graft is pulled out first to prevent entrapment (Fig 1C). Typically, a 250- to 280-mm length of tendon can be obtained in this manner, which is flat but quite broad mediolaterally (Fig 1D). The muscle is cleaned off the tendon and wrapped in gauze soaked with vancomycin.11

Flipping and Passing of Graft Medially

The distal 2.5 to 3 cm of the rectus is freed from the rest of the quadriceps tendon by sharp dissection using a No. 15 knife up to the superior pole of the patella. The lateral half of the tendon is separated over a longer distance over the anterior surface of the patella (Fig 2A). Attention is directed to identifying the sub-vastus plane at the superomedial corner of the patella (Fig 2B). The MPFC lies in layer 2 on the medial side of the knee, and the reconstruction must lie in this plane, outside the joint capsule.12 Correct identification of this plane is characterized by the smooth passage of Metzenbaum scissors medially toward the femoral epicondyle, creating a window. A second longitudinal incision is then made just posterior to the medial epicondyle. A hemostat is passed from the anterior window to emerge near the epicondyle, and a medial window is created in the sub-vastus plane at this location. A suture loop is passed from the medial to the anterior window (Fig 2C). The rectus tendon is flipped and fed into this suture loop such that the superficial surface lies deeper and the deeper surface lies superficially when the tendon is shuttled medially (Fig 2D). Thus, the rectus tendon is both rotated by almost 90° and flipped on itself. This maneuver, along with the liberal release of the lateral half of the tendon on the patella, prevents the formation of a dog ear. The distal attachment on the patella remains intact. While the surgeon pulls medially, the part of the tendon exiting the anterior window is sutured to the surrounding distal quadriceps tendon and patella using nonabsorbable suture (No. 2 BioFiber) (Fig 3A). Arthroscopy Before Reconstruction

Arthroscopy is performed using a 30° arthroscope from the anterolateral portal. The patella is inspected for any cartilage lesions. A superior portal is created, and viewing from here shows the lateral position of the patella and the integrity of the quadriceps tendon (Fig 4A). Femoral Insertion and Tensioning and Fixation of Tendon

The femoral insertion of the MPFC is located by palpation as a groove between the medial epicondyle and adductor tubercle. This is confirmed as lying at the Schöttle point with an image intensifier on an accurate lateral projection at 90° of knee flexion (Fig 3C). The combined palpatory and radiographic method of localization improves the accuracy of anatomic placement.13 A 2.4-mm drilled pin is passed and is directed proximally and anteriorly. This is over-drilled with a 4.5-mm cannulated drill bit. The tendon is marked at this point in 30° of knee flexion and then at a distance of 50 mm from the previous mark. It is folded at the midpoint to obtain a loop of 25 mm and sutured with a nonabsorbable suture (No. 2 BioFiber) (Fig 3B). The diameter of this tendon loop is measured, and a 25-mm-long socket of the same diameter (typically 7 mm) is drilled. The looped tendon is then inserted into the socket to dock. The knee is brought to 20° of flexion, and a 7 × 25-mm Softfix screw (Biotek) is inserted while the patella is translated laterally by 1 quadrant, with minimal tension of the tendon (Fig 3D). This segment reconstructs the MQTFL.

Shuttling of Graft to Patella and Fixation

Another window is created at the medial border of the patella, extending deep to layer 2, at the junction of the superior and middle thirds of the patella. A loop of suture is then passed from this window to the previous medial window. The tendon is shuttled from medial to anterior in this tissue plane (Fig 5A). A 2.8-mm single-loaded titanium anchor (Mini-Vim; Biotek) is inserted at the medial border of the patella after all soft tissue is removed from the bone (Fig 5B). The tendon is fixed at this point using the anchor sutures in 40° of knee flexion with 1 quadrant of lateral patellar translation (Fig 5C). Final Evaluation by Arthroscopy

The arthroscope is inserted from the superior portal to view the patella’s position. The reconstructed MPFC is visualized as a bulge on the capsule, which is broad and expansile at the patella and has a narrow point on the femur (Fig 4 A and B). Postoperative Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation progresses in a phased manner, with the initial focus being on achieving pain and edema control, as well as quadriceps activation, and regaining movement. Weight-bearing as tolerated and knee range of motion are started immediately, along with exercises such as quadriceps, hamstrings, and gluteal sets. A knee brace in full extension is applied for 3 weeks until good quadriceps control is achieved. Rehabilitation progresses based on clinical parameters and not in a strict time-bound manner.

Discussion

The essential steps in this technique, called REFITSS, are as follows: RE, which indicates rectus femoris harvest; F, flipping and rotating the graft medially and passing in layer 2; I, insertion of the tendon at its anatomic attachment in the femur in a socket; T, tensioning of the graft and femoral fixation; S, shuttling of the graft back to the patella; and S, stabilization of the graft at the patella. In this manner, the entire MPFC can be reconstructed using a single graft from the same site, resulting in lesser graft-related morbidity (Fig 6). It has been biomechanically shown that reconstructing the MPFC as a whole restores patellofemoral kinematics more effectively when tensioned correctly, as different parts of the complex behave differently.14,15Fig 6Left knee in 90° of flexion viewed from medial side, showing scheme of medial patellofemoral complex reconstruction using rectus tendon. The materials used to reconstruct the MPFL range from soft-tissue allografts or autografts to synthetic tapes and bio-inductive implants.16 Our technique of rectus graft harvest has been inspired by the work of Raman et al.,17 with distinct differences in incision site and preservation of the distal patellar insertion. The integrity of the quadriceps tendon is not compromised in any significant way, and no other autograft is required. The flipping, rotation, and fixation of the rectus graft medially occur at the site of the native MQTFL. The inferior limb is fixed to the medial patella at the anatomic attachment of the MPFL.6 The use of the rectus femoris tendon has grown in the current literature, and techniques for reconstruction of several structures around the knee have been described recently, including the anterior cruciate ligament and the anterolateral and anteromedial structures.18,19 The MPFC is another possible structure in which this graft can be used.

Several techniques of MPFC reconstruction have been described using allografts or hamstring autografts but requiring patellar drilling.14,20, 21, 22 The concept of rotating the graft and maintaining its insertion into the patella without the need for a tunnel or implant fixation has also been described for the patellar tendon.23 Our technique for MPFC reconstruction can be performed using a single tendon from the same site and without requiring patellar drilling. The complication rate of MPFLR has been reported to be as high as 26%, primarily because of improper tensioning and patellar tunnel–related issues.24,25 Tensioning of the graft needs to be titrated and controlled by the surgeon, and our technique allows the two limbs to be tensioned at different flexion angles to replicate the length change behavior of the MPFC. The superior fibers (MQTFL) provide stability at lower flexion angles, and the inferior fibers (MPFL) maintain tilt at higher flexion angles.15 Patellar drilling can be wholly avoided—as in our technique—by surface fixation of the graft to the patella with a small suture anchor.
