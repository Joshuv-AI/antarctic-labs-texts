Machine learning as an interpretive consistency approach for coupling geochemical and geophysical domains in data-limited landfills
Authors: Vincenzo Costanzo-Álvarez, Maria Jácome, Milagrosa Aldana, Rosario Trigo-Ferre, Melanie Jeffrey, Vincenzo Luciano Costanzo, Amaru Izarra-Jácome, Cristina H Amon, Yannis Yortsos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding landfill-related groundwater contamination is challenging in heterogeneous hydrogeological settings characterized by sparse monitoring networks and limited subsurface information, constraints common to many environmental and earth-science systems. Based on our previous investigations, we present an interpretive framework that synthesizes machine learning (ML), hydrogeochemistry, and subsurface geoelectrical imaging to characterize leachate plume dynamics and surface methane emissions. We examine two Canadian case studies: a closed, nonengineered municipal landfill and an industrial bark dump near an Indigenous community. At both sites, direct-current electrical resistivity (ER) and induced polarization (IP) surveys were conducted, and the resulting data were inverted to produce 2D and pseudo-3D tomographic images of leachate-related anomalies. In the municipal landfill, a supervised Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System employed geoelectrical proxies of waste stabilization to infer landfill-scale methane-emission trends, with localized misfits attributed to near-surface biogas attenuation and metallic-waste-related high-IP responses. Gaussian Mixture Modeling (GMM) and stacked pollution index mapping of hydrogeochemical data delineated contamination zones used to validate low-ER anomalies at well-screening depth. For the industrial landfill, hydrogeochemical facies were identified from water-quality parameters using unsupervised ML methods (GMM, Hierarchical Agglomerative Clustering, and Self-Organizing Maps + K-means), supporting the interpretation of ER and IP anomalies associated with plume migration, mixing interfaces, and matrix-controlled attenuation. These studies illustrate a methodological framework in which ML links geophysical proxies and environmental processes. By integrating geophysical and hydrogeochemical information and evaluating cross-domain consistency using multiple ML methods, this framework reduces interpretive ambiguity and strengthens process-based conceptual models in data-limited settings.Keywords: machine learning, hydrogeophysics, landfill leachate, geoelectrical imaging, hydrogeochemical facies

Landfill-related groundwater contamination inside heterogeneous subsurface settings with sparse monitoring networks poses a major challenge in hydrogeology. The integration of our recent work in this line of research provides a machine learning-based approach for cross-validating separate environmental domains within complex contaminated systems. Applied amid heterogeneous, data-limited conditions, this cross-domain analysis helps reduce interpretive ambiguity and supports process-based conceptual models, clarifying the relationships among hydrogeochemical facies, process domains, and methane-emission behavior. Beyond landfill applications, this convergence-based strategy offers a potentially transferable framework for integrating different observations in contaminated aquifers, subsurface energy systems, and other complex earth systems where uncertainty and sparse monitoring constrain decision-making.

Introduction

Groundwater contamination from landfills and historical waste-disposal practices poses a continual challenge in environmental hydrogeology and applied hydrogeophysics, reflecting broader difficulties in interpreting heterogeneous and data-limited earth systems. In areas where sites predate modern engineering standards or where containment systems fail, contaminant plumes migrate into shallow, lithologically complex aquifers that are often sparsely monitored and only partially constrained by direct measurements (1, 2).

Despite technical advances, the interpretation of plume geometry, composition, and migration pathways remains constrained by incomplete subsurface characterization and the indirect nature of many observational methods (3, 4). Site-specific studies have documented the persistence and spatial variability of landfill-derived contamination, underscoring the need for integrated approaches beyond single-method interpretations (1, 2, 5).

From a methodological standpoint, groundwater-quality sampling provides chemically explicit information on contaminant composition and concentration but is restricted to a few separate monitoring wells (MW). In contrast, geoelectrical methods provide spatial continuity but are subject to nonuniqueness and sensitive to multiple interacting controls such as lithology, clay content, water content or saturation, ionic strength, pore-fluid conductivity, and interfacial electrochemical processes (1, 2, 6). Thus, in landfill environments, electrical resistivity (ER) and induced polarization (IP) serve as proxies for contamination instead of direct measures of pollutant composition. Subsurface interpretation of geoelectrical data typically combines inverted ER and IP results with differing uncertainties and resolution (6–9).

Against this background of heterogeneous, data-limited aquifers, supervised, and unsupervised machine learning (ML) approaches have been increasingly adopted in hydrogeology and hydrogeophysics and, more broadly, across the earth sciences, where they play complementary roles in the study of complex subsurface systems. Rather than replacing physical and chemical understanding of nonlinear processes, ML methods support analyses where deterministic links between observations and mechanisms are difficult to establish. They organize heterogeneous datasets, relate geophysical proxies to subsurface domains, and test the consistency between multivariate geochemical patterns and geophysical responses under sparse sampling and uncertainty (9–12).

ML methods have been employed to model and predict methane generation and emissions in landfills, laboratory biodigesters, bioreactors, and both field- and model-derived datasets (13–16). These studies relate methane emissions to factors such as waste composition and geometry, bioreactor conditions, and physicochemical and biological controls on organic matter degradation via artificial neural networks, fuzzy logic, and deep-learning frameworks.

On the other hand, unsupervised clustering approaches, such as Gaussian mixture models (GMM), hierarchical agglomerative clustering (HAC), and self-organizing maps (SOM), are widely used in hydrogeological studies, including grouping multivariate groundwater-quality data into integrated facies reflecting subsurface heterogeneity (17–22).

A recurring challenge in interpreting landfill-impacted aquifers is combining results from supervised and unsupervised ML approaches with indirect geophysical signals, sparse geochemical observations, and process-based understanding of contaminated subsurface systems. Addressing this issue requires explicitly accounting for uncertainty and avoiding overfitting or conclusions not supported by the available data. Rather than seeking a unified predictive framework, it is therefore important to develop a coherent and transferable methodology for poorly engineered or nonengineered landfills. In this context, ML-derived outputs characterizing environmental domains (e.g. hydrogeochemical facies or empirical methane-emission patterns) are evaluated alongside geoelectrical observations as complementary and mutually constraining lines of evidence, with careful consideration of the scope, resolution, and limitations of each data type.

In recent years, our research team has explored these issues through a series of investigations at two landfill-impacted sites with limited monitoring-well coverage. An initial study at one site linked geoelectrical proxies and surface methane emissions to subsurface waste stabilization processes using supervised nonlinear inference (23). Subsequent studies integrated pseudo-3D ER models with groundwater-quality data to delineate the structure and evolution of the leachate plume (24). At the second landfill, unsupervised ML methods were employed to extract multivariate hydrogeochemical patterns and compare them with geophysical responses (25). Although focused on different site-specific objectives, these studies were designed to examine contaminated systems from complementary analytical perspectives.

Drawing from this body of work, we demonstrate how ML-assisted workflows can support different stages of landfill-site characterization, from exploratory proxy relationships to the identification of hydrogeochemical facies and their comparison with geophysical imaging. Considered together, these investigations define a transferable methodology that links ER and IP data, groundwater chemistry, and methane observations in landfill-impacted aquifers.

Rather than treating ML outputs as stand-alone classifications, we use them as structured mediators that systematically link indirect geophysical signals to direct geochemical observations, enabling more robust cross-domain assessment. The contribution here lies not in the use of well-established individual algorithms, but in their systematic combination and application to identify coherent relationships across hydrogeochemical facies and process domains. This shift, from conventional algorithm implementation to convergence-based synthesis grounded in internal consistency across environmental domains, constitutes the principal conceptual advance of this work.

Materials and methods

Hydrogeological settings

The first case study centers on a small municipal landfill in southern Ontario, Canada, which lacks liners or leachate/gas recovery systems and was closed in 2010 after operating for ∼30 years. Data acquisition and processing

In fall 2019, Jácome et al. (23) conducted 2D ER and IP surveys at the southern Ontario municipal landfill (first case study) and CAZ along four ∼300 m profiles (Fig. 1A), reaching depths of ∼50 m. In parallel, 147 surface methane measurements were collected along the same geoelectrical profiles at 5 to 10 m intervals using a handheld organic vapor analyzer–flame ionization detector operated in a walkover scheme aligned with the electrode layout. Measurements were acquired under consistent midday conditions using standardized timing, positioning, and wind-shielded sampling procedures.

In fall 2022, ER and IP surveys were carried out at the bark dump (second case study) to provide spatially continuous leachate plume coverage (28), using four 480 m lines: two parallel to the main landfill axis and local topography, and two perpendicular to them (Fig. 2).

At both sites, ER and IP data were acquired with a Syscal Pro Switch 96 multielectrode resistivity system (IRIS Instruments) using an automated switching unit and stainless-steel electrodes spaced at 5 m. A dipole–dipole array was selected for its ability to resolve lateral heterogeneities, shallow features, stratigraphic discontinuities, clay-rich layers, and preferential flow paths in landfill settings, outperforming Wenner-type arrays in laterally nonuniform environments (30, 31). Raw geoelectrical data were handled using smoothness-constrained 2D inversion (32, 33) with the models iteratively refined up to convergence after 4–5 iterations, yielding root-mean-square misfits <10%.

On the other hand, the hydrogeochemical dataset employed in the first case study consists of seasonal groundwater-quality measurements collected between 2008 and 2018 from 15 MW screened within the upper unconfined aquifer beneath the landfill and its CAZ (26). The screened interval, located at approximately 10 m depth, corresponds to the zone most directly influenced by leachate migration. Compiled from the municipality's annual groundwater-quality monitoring reports, this dataset captures spatial and seasonal variations in groundwater chemistry reflecting the evolution of the leachate plume and associated contaminant transport processes within the shallow aquifer.

Similarly, in the second case study, the hydrogeochemical dataset comprises annual late-summer groundwater-quality measurements gathered between 2000 and 2022 from 15 MW screened at ∼7 m depth within the upper aquifer (27). The resulting monitoring record captures the spatiotemporal evolution of a conductive contaminant plume associated with leachate migration, redox-controlled metal mobilization, and groundwater mixing.

In both case studies, the hydrogeochemical datasets include measurements of general groundwater chemistry, major ions, total dissolved solids (TDS), dissolved organic matter/carbon (DOC), nutrients, metals, pH and electrical conductivity (EC).

The raw data collected, generated, and analyzed in these two case studies are subject to confidentiality agreements with an Ontario municipal authority and to Indigenous data governance requirements in Canada under the OCAP (Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession) principles (34). Accordingly, the data are not publicly available, and any access, secondary use, or sharing requires prior authorization from the relevant municipal and Indigenous authorities. Requests for access may be submitted through the authors, who will facilitate communication with the appropriate authorities.

ML methods

The ML methods used in this study were selected to align with the structure and limitations of the available hydrogeochemical information, rather than to emphasize predictive complexity. Both case studies rely on sparse monitoring networks, relatively small water-quality datasets, indirect geophysical proxies, and a process-oriented framework linking groundwater chemistry, ER/IP anomalies, and methane observations. Under these conditions, adopting more computationally complex approaches such as deep-learning architectures, which typically involve multiple hidden layers, large numbers of neurons, and highly parameterized nonlinear structures designed primarily for large datasets, would be difficult to constrain robustly and could increase overfitting while reducing interpretability. Consequently, we employed ML approaches with less complex architectures and more interpretable clustering or rule-based behavior, allowing direct evaluation against hydrogeological, hydrogeochemical, and geophysical observations while maintaining stable performance under data-limited conditions (35–38).

An Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS), a supervised hybrid method combining fuzzy logic and neural networks, was applied to estimate surface methane concentrations at the municipal landfill using geoelectrical proxies for leachate accumulation. Minimum ER values and associated IP responses served as antecedent variables, with measured methane concentrations as the target (39). Because the relationship between geoelectrical proxies and surface methane emissions is influenced by multiple interacting controls associated with biogas generation, transport, and attenuation, we used ANFIS to represent the resulting nonlinear input–output relationships within an interpretable rule-based framework. The ANFIS training employed the standard hybrid-learning approach proposed by Jang (35), in which backpropagation (gradient descent) was used to optimize the premise parameters associated with the input membership functions, while least-squares estimation was applied to determine the consequent parameters associated with the output membership functions and fuzzy rules. In this context, the method reduced the risk of overparameterization under data-limited conditions while preserving interpretability (35, 40). The ANFIS implementation was performed using the Fuzzy Logic Toolbox for MATLAB (41).

To classify groundwater chemistry and characterize its multivariate structure, we applied unsupervised GMM, HAC, and Self-Organizing Maps + K-means (SOM + K-means). In case study 1, only GMM was used, whereas all three methods were implemented at the second landfill to provide complementary perspectives on hydrogeochemical organization and plume structure.

For hydrogeochemical facies identification, GMM models data as a mixture of multivariate Gaussians, estimating the mean, covariance, and mixing weights per component via the expectation–maximization algorithm to maximize the likelihood (42). Each observation is assigned a probabilistic membership, which is suitable for contaminated aquifers with mixing and transitional zones. In addition, GMM explicitly accounts for covariance among variables, which may contain important information on plume structure and attenuation processes (18, 20). GMM was implemented in Python using the GaussianMixture class from the Scikit-learn machine-learning library (43). GMM was conducted in Python using the GaussianMixture class from the Scikit-learn machine-learning library (43).

HAC builds a dendrogram by repeatedly merging similar groups, revealing nested and scale-specific structure. Clusters are defined by cutting the dendrogram at a selected level. In the industrial landfill site, HAC was applied in the same hydrogeochemical parameter space as GMM, providing an independent view of sample similarity patterns linked to plume heterogeneity and transport (17, 21, 38). The role of HAC was not to replace probabilistic clustering, but to evaluate the stability and consistency of hydrogeochemical groupings under sparse monitoring conditions. HAC analyses were performed in Python using the AgglomerativeClustering class from the Scikit-learn library (43).

At the bark dump, clustering at MW was extended to a spatially continuous framework using SOM, which projects high-dimensional datasets onto a low-dimensional lattice that preserves neighborhood relations, revealing multivariate hydrogeochemical patterns (37).

SOM training used synthetic spatial distributions of key water-quality parameters generated by ordinary kriging for each monitoring year between 2000 and 2022, on a uniform grid within the convex hull enclosing the well network shown in Fig. 2. Subsequent K-means clustering of the SOM-derived vectors from the multivariate grid-node inputs delineated dominant hydrogeochemical facies and their spatiotemporal shifts across the landfill and CAZ. This approach enabled spatial extension of borehole-based hydrogeochemical patterns while preserving multivariate structure for comparison with plume-scale geoelectrical anomalies (19, 22, 37). SOM computations were carried out in Python with the Somoclu library (44), while NumPy (45) and Matplotlib (46) were used for numerical processing and visualization. K-means partitioning was performed using the K-Means class from the Scikit-learn machine-learning library (43).

Case study implementation, results, and discussion

ANFIS-based geophysical–methane coupling and hydrogeochemical clustering: model implementation

In the first case study at a southern Ontario municipal landfill, we analyzed waste degradation and surface methane emissions by integrating geophysical, hydrogeochemical, and ML approaches (23, 24).

An ANFIS model was used to predict surface methane concentrations with measured methane concentrations as target variable. Inputs included minimum ER (highest conductivity) and the corresponding IP values extracted from inverted geoelectrical sections within the upper 15 m, directly underlying the surface methane measurement locations. These inputs were evaluated in raw, normalized, inverted, and logarithmic forms; the final model used log-transformed ER and IP values to reduce skewness and better capture nonlinear relationships.

The implemented ANFIS structure employed Gaussian membership functions and a grid-partition framework to define the fuzzy rule base from the ER/IP input space. Model calibration was performed using 70% of the dataset selected randomly across the surveyed lines; whereas the resulting fuzzy-rule system was subsequently applied to the complete dataset to compare inferred and observed methane concentrations (23).

To limit overparameterization under sparse data conditions, the final ANFIS implementation was restricted to 12 fuzzy IF–THEN rules derived from the [3 log(R), 4 log(IP)] partition structure (23). Several partition configurations were evaluated, and the selected architecture yielded the lowest methane inference root mean square error (RMSE) while avoiding excessive model complexity relative to the available sample size. Thus, the adopted configuration achieved a favorable trade-off between predictive performance, nonlinear representational capacity, model stability, and interpretability of the relationships between geoelectrical response domains and methane-emission behavior.

Model performance was assessed through RMSE, R2, and R, based on observed–predicted comparisons, yielding RMSE values of 12 to 30 ppm, R2 ≈ 0.46, R ≈ 0.74, and a regression slope of ∼0.67, indicating moderate predictive performance. The training error stabilized after ∼100 epochs during ANFIS optimization.

Although the ANFIS analysis focused on the relationship between ER/IP responses and surface methane emissions, the environmental significance of these geoelectrical anomalies may also be evaluated from a hydrogeochemical perspective. Accordingly, we integrated ER responses with groundwater-quality data (24), enabling hydrogeochemical characterization of the atypical low-resistivity features identified within the upper aquifer (∼10 m depth).

For clustering purposes, 12 groundwater-quality parameters from the Fall 2018 monitoring report (26) were retained after outlier filtering, based on their relatively stable behavior and representativeness as indicators of landfill leachate impacts within the upper aquifer. These parameters included major ions (Cl−, SO42−, Na), DOC, ammonia, trace elements (Fe, Mn, B), and bulk physicochemical indicators, including alkalinity (as CaCO3), hardness (Ca and Mg), TDS, and pH. Consequently, borehole–year observations were represented within a 12D hydrogeochemical feature space constructed from the selected groundwater-quality parameters.

To identify the optimal preprocessing strategy for GMM analysis, four alternatives were evaluated during model selection: untransformed (raw) data, standardized data, Min–Max-normalized data, and log1p-transformed data. Min–Max normalization, applied across the complete monitoring record (2008–2018), was identified as the most suitable preprocessing strategy and adopted for all subsequent analyses. The original variable distributions were generally nonnormal and right-skewed, supporting the use of probabilistic clustering approaches capable of representing partially overlapping hydrogeochemical populations.

GMM was implemented using a full covariance representation, allowing each hydrogeochemical population to be represented by an ellipsoidal distribution within multivariate space. Model selection using the Bayesian Information Criterion evaluated alternative preprocessing strategies, covariance structures (spherical, tied, diagonal, and full), and cluster dimensionality, identifying Min–Max normalization, a full covariance structure, and five clusters as the optimal configuration. A Davies–Bouldin analysis subsequently provided independent support for the same five-cluster partitioning scheme. A Davies–Bouldin analysis was subsequently performed as an independent validation of the selected partitioning scheme, with both criteria supporting a five-cluster subdivision of the hydrogeochemical system.

In parallel, contamination intensity was represented using the Stacked Pollution Index (SPI) defined in Ref. (24), which aggregates normalized hydrogeochemical variables into a single indicator of relative contaminant load. SPI values were computed using the same water-quality parameters employed in the clustering framework, with normalization performed across the complete monitoring record to ensure temporal comparability among observations. Rather than partitioning the dataset into discrete hydrochemical populations, the SPI assigned a continuous-valued contaminant-load score to each borehole–year observation. These scores provided the basis for spatial interpolation and plume-scale comparison between hydrogeochemical conditions and geoelectrical anomalies.

Geophysical–methane coupling and hydrogeochemical support for ER as a leachate proxy

Linking subsurface contamination and waste degradation to surface biogas release constitutes a key challenge in characterizing landfills lacking methane or leachate collection systems. This relationship inherently involves a scale-translation problem: methane generation occurs at depth, whereas surface measurements reflect signals already modified by transport and transformation processes within the cover soil and vadose zone, including interactions with the soil and rock matrix, biological oxidation, atmospheric and climatic variability, and vegetation effects (47, 48). Consequently, variations in subsurface degradation or gas generation do not translate linearly into surface methane emissions.

Deterministic or linear models are limited in their ability to represent the complex dynamics of methane generation and surface emissions in landfills. Conversely, nonlinear numerical and data-driven methods can account for multiple interacting controls and have therefore been widely used to simulate biogas production and release in landfills as functions of operational, climatic, hydrochemical, redox, and waste characteristics (13–16). Beyond strengthening predictive performance, these approaches also support a better understanding of the underlying methanogenic processes.

In this study, geophysical anomalies were treated not as direct predictors of gas emissions but as spatial indicators of subsurface process domains consistent with coupled biogeochemical and hydrological conditions associated with methane generation. Rather than attempting point-scale prediction of methane concentrations, we evaluated whether geophysical proxies of leachate accumulation captured the broader spatial patterns of emissions across the site. This approach reflects the fact that near-surface transport and methane oxidation introduce substantial local variability in surface measurements that is not fully represented by localized predictive relationships. Within this framework, ANFIS was used to evaluate landfill-scale spatial coherence between subsurface electrical properties and surface methane emissions.

The geoelectrical interpretation was constrained by site geology and stratigraphy. The combination of ER and IP supported by borehole lithological data, allowed differentiation of leachate-driven anomalies from background lithological signals. Indeed, ER alone cannot distinguish clays from leachates or saline waters, thereby masking true lithological and contamination contrasts. In a landfill context, low-ER values may indicate leachate-affected waste and sediments, while IP responses help discriminate among waste composition, clay content, and pore-fluid chemistry (49–51).

Based on this integrated geoelectrical interpretation, we defined a waste stabilization zone as a spatially coherent subsurface process domain characterized by biodegradation, leachate accumulation, and fluid–matrix exchanges associated with sustained biogas generation. At the municipal landfill, this zone corresponds to waste and downgradient sediments enriched in ion- and organic-rich leachate, appearing as laterally continuous low-ER features. IP data further distinguished these active degradation domains from lithological artifacts (23, 49–51).

The ANFIS model shown in Fig. 4 reproduces landfill-scale spatial patterns of methane concentrations along the surveyed profiles (Fig. 4A), showing consistent correspondence with ER and IP proxies of leachate accumulation (Fig. 4B), as reflected in the overall model performance metrics presented in Fig. 4C. The limited ability of the ANFIS to resolve fine-scale methane fluctuations likely signal localized features obscuring the correspondence between subsurface methane generation and surface emissions. Examples include point anomalies within the porous cover and vadose zone that selectively affect near-surface biogas transport, leading to local misfits in the inferred emissions. In addition, elevated IP values used as antecedent variables may be related to metal-rich, nonmethanogenic waste, introducing further mismatches in the inferred surface emissions, as indicated in Fig. 4A and B (23).

Hydrogeochemical clustering: model implementation

In the second case study, focused on a pulp and paper bark dump in northwestern Ontario, we applied GMM, HAC, and SOM + K-means to analyze leachate plume structure under sparse monitoring conditions. The resulting facies classifications were then integrated with geoelectrical (ER and IP) data to characterize groundwater conditions associated with anomalous ER/IP responses within the upper aquifer at approximately 7 m depth.

The water-quality parameters selected from the environmental site assessment reports (27) include EC, TDS, DOC, Cl−, SO42−, Al, As, Ba, Fe, Mn, and Na. These indicators of groundwater contamination were chosen because they exhibited fewer outliers, higher absolute values, and recurrent exceedances of the applicable Ontario regulatory guidelines. Together, they define an 11D hydrogeochemical space in which each borehole–year observation is represented as a multivariate vector. The complete 2000–2022 monitoring record, comprising annual observations from the 15 MW, was therefore represented by 345 vectors that served as the basis for subsequent data-driven analyses. Prior to clustering, all variables were normalized using Min–Max scaling computed over the full 2000–2022 monitoring record.

GMM was implemented using a full covariance structure, allowing each hydrogeochemical population to be represented by a multivariate Gaussian distribution that accounts for correlations among variables. The number of clusters was evaluated using the Calinski–Harabasz and Davies–Bouldin indices. While the two metrics exhibited different optimization profiles, both supported a four-group configuration, which was considered a stable and interpretable representation of the hydrogeochemical space (25).

HAC was applied to the same normalized dataset using Ward linkage and Euclidean distance. Cluster dimensionality was evaluated using the Calinski–Harabasz and Davies–Bouldin indices, both of which indicated a four-group solution for comparison with the GMM results (25). Contamination intensity was also quantified using SPI values derived from the same hydrogeochemical dataset, providing a continuous numerical variable that complements the categorical structure defined by the GMM and HAC groupings.

The multivariate partition obtained from the GMM and HAC analyses serves as a common reference framework for tracking plume evolution through time. Rather than generating separate clustering solutions for individual years, a single clustering structure was derived from the complete hydrogeochemical record (2000–2022). Consequently, all borehole–year observations were evaluated within the same 11D classification space. Temporal variability is therefore not treated as stationary hydrogeochemical facies, but rather as changes in cluster membership and contamination intensity. Each borehole–year observation is assigned to a cluster according to its position within the multivariate distribution of water-quality variables. Consequently, shifts in cluster membership at individual MW can be interpreted as changes in plume conditions through time. When annual cluster assignments are mapped onto borehole locations, these shifts reveal the spatiotemporal evolution of the leachate plume across the monitoring network.

To extend the borehole-based classifications spatially, ordinary kriging was applied independently to the selected hydrogeochemical parameters within the convex hull defined by the 15 MW considered in this study (Fig. 2). For each parameter, measured values were log-transformed to reduce right-skewness and improve variogram fitting. Kriging was then performed in the transformed domain, and the interpolated estimates were back-converted to the original scale for mapping and subsequent multivariate analysis. A spherical variogram model was used because it yielded the best cross-validation performance, with lower prediction error and stable residual behavior. Interpolation was carried out on a regular 25 m grid within the convex hull, generating a spatial mesh of 663 nodes for each monitoring year.

Kriging performance was assessed through leave-one-out cross-validation using the MW as validation points. For all selected water-quality parameters, observed and predicted values showed strong agreement, with R2 ∼ 0.9, normalized RMSEs generally below 17%, and regression slopes and intercepts close to 1 and 0, respectively, indicating low systematic bias and robust predictive performance. The interpolated values at the 663 grid nodes were then assembled into 11-component hydrogeochemical vectors. Across the 2000–2022 monitoring period, this procedure generated 15,249 vectors that served as inputs to the SOM-based clustering analyses.

The SOM framework used a hexagonal Kohonen lattice with an 8 × 8 configuration (64 neurons) to preserve topological relationships among the kriging-derived hydrogeochemical vectors while maintaining stable representation of plume-scale variability. This configuration was selected based on neuron activation patterns during training, as it provided adequate resolution while avoiding inactive neurons and maintaining the principal hydrogeochemical structure of the dataset. The network was trained for 250 epochs to achieve topological convergence.

K-means clustering was subsequently applied to the 64 neuron weight vectors, resulting in five hydrogeochemical groups. The number of clusters was determined from the SOM structure and internal consistency, following the distribution of neuron activations and the dimensionality of the lattice. This partition was consistent with the grouping tendencies observed in the GMM and HAC classifications.

Each hydrogeochemical data vector inherited the cluster label associated with its best matching unit. For every monitoring year, the classified multivariate vectors corresponding to the 663 interpolation nodes were projected onto their geolocations, generating a series of annual maps from 2000 to 2022 that captured changes in hydrogeochemical facies over time. These maps were analyzed alongside SPI distributions, whereas the 2022 classification was further evaluated against the ER/IP depth slice corresponding to the monitoring-well-screening interval, enabling an integrated interpretation of contamination intensity, hydrogeochemical structure, and geoelectrical anomalies within a unified spatial framework.

Because the SOM analysis was performed on kriging-interpolated hydrogeochemical fields derived from spatially sparse observations, interpolation-induced smoothing likely reduces fine-scale variability and sharp local gradients. Consequently, the resulting SOM clusters should be interpreted as representations of broader hydrogeochemical structure rather than localized chemical anomalies. In this context, the smoothing effect is consistent with the objective of the SOM analysis, which focuses on identifying stable multivariate plume-scale patterns under sparse monitoring conditions. However, the kriging interpolation preserved the principal spatial and multivariate structure of the hydrogeochemical dataset, as indicated by the strong cross-validation results. Accordingly, the SOM analysis was used to identify stable hydrogeochemical plume patterns and their correspondence with ER/IP anomalies, rather than small-scale variability at individual monitoring locations.

Integrated interpretation of machine-learning–derived hydrogeochemical facies and geoelectrical anomalies

Unlike the municipal solid waste landfill investigated in southern Ontario (23, 24), the pulp and paper bark dump in northern Ontario represents a different contamination setting shaped by distinct socioenvironmental conditions and waste characteristics. While the overarching objective of linking ML-derived groundwater pollution patterns to anomalous ER/IP responses at specific screening-depth intervals remained consistent with (24), the integration of HAC and SOM + K-means alongside GMM enabled a more detailed reconstruction of the internal hydrogeochemical organization of the leachate plume within the upper aquifer.

Unlike SPI, which quantifies contamination intensity as an additive scalar metric independent of interparameter relationships, ML-based clustering resolves how contamination signatures are distributed through the relational structure of the hydrochemical dataset. Consequently, differences between SPI distributions and cluster assignments reflect the complementary nature of magnitude-based and structure-based representations of contamination rather than direct one-to-one correspondence.

Additionally, SOM + K-means was applied to the kriging-expanded dataset to generate hydrogeochemical facies maps with greater spatial continuity than the GMM and HAC classifications restricted to borehole locations. Figure 6B shows the evolution of the SOM-derived facies for the same five representative years presented in Fig. 6A, together with the corresponding SPI distributions. The five SOM groups represent distinct hydrogeochemical facies spanning the range from less impacted to more contaminated groundwater conditions. The observed changes reflect both variations in contamination intensity (SPI) and reorganization of hydrogeochemical structure through time, consistent with plume dynamics driven by hydraulic redirection and lithological heterogeneity (28, 29). The overall similarities between the SOM-derived facies and the independently derived GMM and HAC classifications suggest that the principal hydrogeochemical patterns are reproduced across the different ML approaches, although the SOM approach provides additional spatial detail between MW.

Rather than establishing direct statistical correspondence, the comparison focuses on the spatial coherence and conceptual convergence between independent environmental indicators within a common hydrogeological context. Since SPI, ML facies, and geoelectrical responses characterize contamination through different representations and spatial resolutions, localized discrepancies are expected and are attributed to scale-dependent processes, lithological heterogeneity, and the inherent nonuniqueness of indirect geophysical interpretation.

Our hydrogeochemical–geophysical interpretation is constrained by lithostratigraphic boundaries derived from stratigraphic and geophysical information (Fig. 3), helping distinguish fluid-controlled anomalies from matrix-controlled responses. Hydrogeochemical facies are interpreted in the context of lithostratigraphy and geoelectrical responses, allowing cluster patterns to be linked to plausible contamination processes.

The geoelectrical anomalies analyzed here, together with groundwater-quality information, correspond to anomalies A and D previously identified by Jácome et al. (28). Anomaly A is characterized by low ER and relatively high-IP values, whereas anomaly D exhibits higher ER and intermediate IP responses.

In 2022, the year of the geoelectrical survey, the IP response of anomaly A at the screened depth divides into two subdomains (A1 and A2) with contrasting hydrogeochemical–geophysical relationships and distinct lithological controls (Fig. 7A). As shown in Fig. 3, A1 occurs within clay-rich units of the upper aquifer, where high groundwater EC values coincide with low ER and high IP. This signature is consistent with an active contaminant migration front within a heterogeneous leachate-impacted zone. More broadly, it represents a transitional setting in which contaminant accumulation, incomplete attenuation, and plume–matrix relations persist at significant levels, showing that electrical anomalies can be interpreted in terms of contamination processes when considered within their hydrogeochemical context.

The SOM + K-means analysis further improves this interpretation by revealing internal structure within A1 that is not captured by SPI alone. Two SOM + K-means groups (4 and 5) with similar SPI values display distinct multivariate geochemical characteristics (Fig. 7A), revealing a north–south subdivision of A1 across the French drain along line 2 (Figs. 2 and 7A). This distinction, which is not evident from SPI values alone, suggests plume segmentation and hydraulic reorganization within the CAZ and highlights the inability of SPI to resolve the internal hydrogeochemical organization of complex subsurface systems.

On the other hand, A2 exhibits hydrogeophysical behavior distinct from A1, with relatively low EC coexisting with low ER and elevated IP (Fig. 7A). Such a combined response suggests the presence of clay- or organic-rich sediments, where geoelectrical behavior is controlled by matrix effects through intrinsic surface conduction or residual contamination retained in low-permeability materials after partial flushing. In this sense, A2 would represent a transition zone between active contamination and background conditions (25), implying that similar electrical signatures may arise from distinct hydrogeochemical controls and highlighting the need for multiparameter integration in plume characterization. The spatial relationship between A2 and the two lobes of A1 suggests hydraulic redirection, possibly associated with the onlap of a coarser sand-and-gravel unit over a structural high (Figs. 3 and 7A). Although A2 broadly overlaps zones of low EC and low SPI, it is more clearly delineated by SOM + K-means group 1, indicating how multivariate classification identifies contamination patterns not clearly resolved by SPI.

Finally, ER anomaly D (Fig. 7B) corresponds to a hydrogeochemical–geophysical regime defined by relatively high ER and intermediate IP despite elevated groundwater EC and SPI. Stratigraphically (Fig. 3), D is associated with a coarser, more permeable domain in the CAZ where lithological conditions enable advective transport and reduced matrix influence. In this setting, pore-fluid conductivity contributes to bulk ER without fully controlling the geoelectrical response, allowing resistivity to remain comparatively high under elevated ionic strength while moderate IP reflects limited polarization capacity.

Figure 7B shows how anomaly D coincides with the zone of highest EC and SPI classified within SOM + K-means as group 4, the same contamination pattern identified in the southern lobe of A1. This agreement indicates that similar hydrogeochemical signatures can emerge under contrasting stratigraphic and transport conditions, demonstrating how comparable chemical domains can develop in different sedimentary environments.

Conclusions

In our recent work on ML-assisted hydrogeophysical and geochemical interpretation of leachate-affected aquifer systems, a central issue has been the integration of indirect geophysical information, spatially sparse geochemical data, and multiscale observations into a coherent conceptual model of leachate plume structure, composition, and controlling processes. This integration challenge represents a recurring problem in earth-science investigations involving heterogeneous subsurface systems.

One consistent lesson from this work is that in data-limited contaminated systems, interpretive improvement depends less on adding isolated methods than on applying a consistent integration approach that makes assumptions and limitations explicit. Our main contribution is therefore methodological rather than case-specific.

The ML-based approach synthesized here assigns distinct roles to each data type. Geoelectrical imaging provides 3D spatial continuity, groundwater chemistry provides compositional information, and surface methane emissions offer additional insight into subsurface processes. ML methods organize multivariate patterns and facilitate comparison between independent sources of information. None of these datasets is fully informative on its own; their value lies in their joint interpretation to evaluate consistency among hydrogeochemical facies and process domains. Where independent datasets converge, interpretations become more robust. Where they diverge, discrepancies become informative rather than errors, indicating lithological control, mixing processes, near-surface attenuation of migrating methane, or high-IP anomalies associated with metal-rich waste unrelated to methane generation. This systematic convergence-testing across environmental domains shifts the focus from isolated methods toward structured cross-validation, where ML serves as an integrative framework linking geophysical, hydrochemical, and gas-emission datasets within a common interpretive approach. Accordingly, the role of ML in this study is primarily exploratory rather than predictive. The objective was therefore not to benchmark algorithms in terms of predictive accuracy, but to evaluate whether different supervised and unsupervised approaches converged toward hydrogeochemically and geophysically consistent interpretations under data-limited conditions.

In practice, this convergence-based framework was implemented through both supervised and unsupervised analyses. In the supervised case, coupling geoelectrical proxies with surface methane observations was not intended to forecast point-scale emissions, but rather to evaluate whether geophysical signals associated with waste stabilization, waste type, and leachate accumulation domains carry coherent information about landfill-scale methane-emission behavior. In the unsupervised case, multivariate classification of groundwater chemistry preserved compositional structure that cumulative indices often obscure. When these hydrogeochemical facies, identified through unsupervised ML tools, are compared with ER and IP anomalies at consistent depths, cross-domain correspondence helps clarify whether plume behavior is fluid-controlled, matrix-influenced, or linked to legacy contamination. In this framework, ML methods act as structured mediators for evaluating consistency between chemical and geophysical observations.

To summarize, our interpretive approach has several useful implications. First, it reduces arbitrariness when interpolating between sparse wells by distinguishing what is inferred from proxies from what remains uncertain. Second, systematic comparison among hydrogeochemistry, geophysics, and stratigraphy strengthens conceptual models by avoiding single-method interpretations. Third, applying multiple ML approaches to the same datasets allows structural agreement to serve as an internal validation criterion under data-limited conditions. Fourth, the approach makes uncertainty explicit by grounding interpretations in the scale and resolution of each dataset rather than extending beyond their observational support.

This work presents a transferable framework in which ML is used not as a black box but as a structured means of linking independent environmental datasets to reduce conceptual nonuniqueness and strengthen process-based conceptual models for complex, data-limited contaminated systems.
