Beyond conventional polysomnography: advanced sleep feature engineering and machine learning for differentiating central disorders of hypersomnolence
Authors: Zhongxing Zhang, Ramin Khatami
Published: 2026

The diagnosis of central disorders of hypersomnolence (CDH) remains challenging because, at present, only narcolepsy type 1 (NT1) has established diagnostic hallmarks, namely low cerebrospinal fluid hypocretin-1 levels and/or cataplexy. In contrast, narcolepsy type 2 (NT2) and idiopathic hypersomnia (IH) frequently share overlapping clinical features, and their primary distinguishing criterion, sleep-onset rapid eye movement periods (SOREMPs) during the multiple sleep latency test (MSLT), has been criticized for poor reliability [1]. Consequently, the identification of objective biomarkers capable of reliably differentiating NT2 and IH has become a major unmet need in the field.

In recent years, nocturnal polysomnography (nPSG) has gained increasing importance in the diagnostic evaluation of CDH. SOREMPs identified on nPSG were incorporated into the ICSD-3 diagnostic criteria as equivalent to MSLT SOREMPs for NT1, NT2, and IH, and in the updated ICSD-3-TR criteria, the presence of a nPSG SOREMP together with cataplexy is sufficient to establish a diagnosis of NT1 [2]. A recent meta-analysis comparing nPSG parameters among NT1, NT2, and IH concluded that differences in classical nPSG metrics (e.g. sleep efficiency, sleep latency, wake time after sleep onset, arousal index, and leg movement index) were significant only between NT1 and IH. In contrast, NT2 and IH showed largely similar nPSG profiles, except for an increased REM sleep percentage and a decreased REM latency in NT2 [3]. These findings highlight the limitations of conventional nPSG measures and emphasize the need for more advanced analytic approaches to further elucidate differences in nPSG profiles among NT1, NT2, and IH.

In this context, the new study by Karas et al. [4] represents an important methodological advance. The authors performed extensive feature engineering, deriving 330 sleep features from raw nPSG data across six domains: whole-night hypnogram features, whole-night spindle features, quarter-night hypnogram features, quarter-night stage transition probability features, quarter-night quantitative electroencephalogram (qEEG) features, and quarter-night hypnodensity features. They applied a supervised machine learning (ML) algorithm (random forest) to classify well-defined NT1, NT2, IH, and clinical control (CC) groups using a nPSG database from the National Reference Centers for Narcolepsy in Montpellier, France.

Compared with prior ML studies using nPSG data [5–7], this study has several notable strengths. First, it directly addresses the clinically most challenging distinctions between hypocretin-competent hypersomnolences, i.e. NT2 versus IH and NT2/IH versus CC, rather than focusing primarily on NT1. Second, the extensive feature engineering performed by Karas et al. comprehensively covered both whole-night and quarter-night nPSG features, resulting in robust classifiers with areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUCs) approaching or exceeding 0.8 for distinguishing NT2, IH, and CC. Previous studies [5, 7] largely relied on nPSG features derived from hypnograms and/or hypnodensity graphs generated by automatic sleep staging algorithms, under the assumption that hypocertin deficiency in narcolepsy leads to an increased overlap among sleep stages in hypnodensity representations. However, this assumption is unlikely to effectively distinguish NT2 from IH, as these patients typically have normal hypocertin levels and more stable sleep architectures than patients with NT1.

Importantly, Karas et al. demonstrated that quarter-night qEEG features substantially enhanced model performance for NT2 versus IH classification; the omission of these features led to a marked reduction in AUC. These findings suggest that the temporal dynamics of brain activity across the night may contain diagnostically relevant information beyond conventional sleep staging. The pathophysiological mechanisms of spindle-related features and temporal qEEG measures (e.g. gamma power dynamics) in differentiating NT2 and IH remain poorly understood and deserve further investigation.

In addition to supervised classification, Karas et al. employed unsupervised clustering to examine nPSG features from a data-driven perspective. This analysis identified two subgroups within NT2, one showing greater similarity to NT1 and the other to IH. These results align with findings from a recent cluster analysis of the European Narcolepsy Network (EUNN) database, which identified clusters comprising a mixture of NT2 and IH patients [8]. Whereas the EUNN study primarily incorporated clinical symptoms and standard MSLT and nPSG variables, Karas et al. focused on a large number of features extracted directly from raw nPSG signals, without the inclusion of clinical symptoms. Together, these complementary approaches contribute to the ongoing debate regarding whether NT2 and IH represent distinct diagnostic entities or exist along a phenotypic continuum [9, 10]. The contrasting results between supervised (i.e. NT2 and IH can be well classified) and unsupervised analyses in the study by Karas et al. suggest that this longstanding controversy cannot yet be resolved, even with advanced ML techniques.

To conclude, the nPSG feature engineering work proposed by Karas et al. establishes an important benchmark for future nPSG-driven diagnosis in CDH, and independent replication using other datasets is warranted. Their results of unsupervised clustering analyses underscore the complexity of CDH classification and suggest that current diagnostic boundaries may not be fully captured by nPSG features alone. Emerging multimodal approaches integrating clinical symptoms, psychiatric comorbidities, electrophysiological measures, wearable device data, and molecular, immunological, and genetic markers may ultimately be required to more precisely define narcolepsy and related hypersomnolence disorders [11]. Such efforts hold promise for refining disease classification and advancing our understanding of pathological sleepiness.
