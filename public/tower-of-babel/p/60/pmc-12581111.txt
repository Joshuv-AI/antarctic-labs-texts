Optimized Kappa‐Index Test Protocol to Detect Intrathecal Immunoglobulin Synthesis in Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: Joel Björklund, Outi Itkonen, Pasi Nokelainen, Mika Siuko, Emilia Österlund‐Tauriala, Pentti J Tienari, Mikko Anttonen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundKappa free light chain (KFLC)‐based Kappa‐index (K‐index) can substitute oligoclonal banding (OCB) to detect intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis. Cutoffs for positive results vary; therefore, further comparison of different approaches is necessary to optimize K‐index for diagnostic utilization.MethodsWe analyzed 432 paired CSF/serum samples and selected 77 OCB‐positive patients with multiple sclerosis (MS), clinically isolated syndrome (CIS) that did not progress to MS in > 10 years, and other neuroinflammatory diseases (non‐MS). K‐index subtests were measured on the Optilite platform (The Binding Site) and OCB with isoelectric focusing on the Hydrasys platform (Sebia).ResultsOCB positive cases had a higher K‐index than OCB negative. The MS group had a higher K‐index than CIS (p = 0.0066) and non‐MS (p < 0.0001). A K‐index over 80.9 suggests MS over CIS, and 61.4 suggests MS over non‐MS. Different values to mimic undetectable CSF KFLC levels resulted in different cutoffs against positive OCB. Using the limit of detection (LOD) of the assay and fixing sensitivity and specificity to 95% to establish the gray area, the cutoffs were < 4.45 (negative) and > 12.92 (positive). When undetectable CSF KFLC was also considered negative, specificity against OCB was 99.7% and sensitivity 92.7%, leaving 6% for gray area OCB verification testing.ConclusionsPublished K‐index cutoffs vary due to different settings of CSF KFLC results below LOD. Using a reflex approach, where samples within a certain K‐index gray area are subjected to OCB verification testing, and where samples with CSF KFLC below LOD are considered negative, was the most efficient laboratory algorithm. K‐index may also have prognostic value in MS.Keywords: biomarker, kappa free light chain, K‐index, multiple sclerosis, oligoclonal band

Introduction

Immunoglobulin G (IgG) is synthesized intrathecally by B‐cell–derived plasma cells in both infectious and immune‐mediated central nervous system (CNS) inflammation [1]. The traditional method to detect intrathecal IgG synthesis, oligoclonal bands (OCB) by isoelectric focusing (IEF), is positive in 88%–95% of multiple sclerosis (MS) patients [2, 3] and it has been part of the 2017 McDonald criteria of MS diagnosis [4]. However, the 2024 revision of the McDonald criteria now includes kappa free light chains (KFLC) [5]. While OCB analysis is slow, labor intensive and subjective, the KFLC approach provides an efficient alternative to demonstrate intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis.

Immunoglobulins consist of heavy and light chains. The latter are produced excessively resulting in free light chains (FLC), either kappa (KFLC) or lambda (LFLC) [6]. Intrathecally synthesized KFLCs accumulate in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in case of inflammatory diseases of the CNS [7]. However, serum KFLCs may pass through the blood–brain barrier, particularly during its leakage, resulting in non‐CSF–based KFLC concentration [8]. These phenomena are taken into account in the Kappa‐index (K‐index), which includes both KFLC and albumin quotients in CSF and serum [9]. The K‐index provides an interpreter‐independent test for intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis, with similar accuracy as OCB [10, 11]. The K‐index is also able to detect increased IgA and IgM synthesis in MS when IgG OCBs are absent [12].

In the literature, there is variation in the suggested positive cutoffs for K‐index (2.4 to 20.0) [13] across different patient populations and depending on whether diagnostic sensitivity or specificity is maximized [14]. Currently, there is no consensus on the diagnostic use of K‐index, and the results between laboratories are not comparable. The companion paper of the 2024 McDonald criteria recommends a K‐index cutoff of ≥ 6.1 as an optimal threshold for diagnosing MS [15], but in general, each laboratory should verify the cutoff and testing protocol.

This study developed an accurate K‐index protocol in an accredited clinical laboratory using the reflex approach suggested and demonstrated by Hegen et al. [14, 16] In this approach, samples in the gray area, that is, between the negative and positive cutoffs, are tested for OCBs as a second step. We verified that assigning different values to the K‐index formula when the CSF KFLC concentration was below the limit of detection (LOD) had a significant impact on the cutoffs and identified which K‐index level suggests MS over other CNS inflammation. Our protocol provides a major reduction in OCB testing without losing diagnostic accuracy.

Methods and Materials

Samples

This study was approved by the Medical Research Committee of HUS (HUS/283/2025). The test protocol study was carried out during 2–8/2024 using 432 anonymous leftover diagnostic paired CSF and serum samples. The samples were utilized after reporting the results of the provider‐ordered OCB to the laboratory information system. Only sex and the OCB results of the samples were recorded. Some of the samples had been frozen before analysis. After OCB testing, samples were stored below +8°C to minimize the number of freeze/thaw cycles and analyzed with Optilite within 1 week.

We also utilized a cohort of 77 paired CSF and serum samples positive for OCB from a larger cohort of patients, collected between the years 2009 and 2014 for previous neuroimmunological studies [17, 18, 19] that were approved by The Ethics Committee of the Helsinki University Hospital (Dno 83/13/03/01/2013) in agreement with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. We included three groups, that is, MS patients fulfilling McDonald 2005 diagnostic criteria (n = 26), patients with clinically isolated syndrome, who did not develop MS after follow‐up for > 10 years (CIS, n = 24), and non‐MS patients, who had CSF oligoclonal bands due to other inflammatory diseases (n = 27). The details of these three patient groups are shown in Tables S1–S3. These samples were stored at −80°C except for 2 weeks before analysis at −20°C.

OCB Analysis

OCB was analyzed with Sebia Hydrasys 2 scan gel electrophoresis by immunofixation utilizing Hydrogel 9 CSF isofocusing kits (Sebia, Lisses, France). According to the international standard classification [20], the results were interpreted as follows: Type 1 polyclonal CSF IgG, Type 2 oligoclonal CSF IgG, Type 3 oligoclonal CSF IgG with some fractions common with serum, Type 4 oligoclonal IgG shared between serum and CSF, and Type 5 monoclonal IgG shared between serum and CSF. Detection of two or more CSF‐derived OCB fractions in Types 2 or 3 was considered a positive result, but the number of OCB fractions was not recorded.

K‐index Analysis

All CSF samples were allowed to reach room temperature, mixed, and centrifuged (2123 g, 10 min) prior to analysis. Blood contamination and turbidity interfere with both KFLC and albumin measurements, and suspected samples by visual inspection were excluded as a precaution. KFLCs and albumin concentrations were measured using Freelite Mx Kappa Free Kit and Low Level Albumin Kit (The Binding Site, Birmingham, UK), respectively, from paired CSF and serum samples with Optilite immunoturbidometric analyzer (The Binding Site) according to the manufacturer's protocols. The detection limit of the Kappa Mx assay is 0.33 mg/L for both serum and CSF, and of the Low Level Albumin assay is 2200 and 11 mg/L for serum and CSF, respectively. K‐index results in different categories were also compared to sex.

Statistical Analysis

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) with area under the curve (AUC), Youden's index, and boxplots were performed with Microsoft Excel utilizing Analyse‐It (4.81.1) add‐in. p values were calculated with Wilcoxon‐Mann–Whitney test, and the result was considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. In ROC analysis, OCB types 2 and 3 were considered as OCB positive (OCB+) and OCB types 1, 4, and 5 as OCB negative (OCB−) results. Similarly, in the OCB+ clinical cohort, MS was considered positive against CIS and non‐MS, and CIS was considered positive against non‐MS. For single cutoff approaches, the best point was defined by the highest Youden's index. For reflex approaches, the negative cutoff was the point where sensitivity reached 95% and the positive cutoff was the point where specificity reached 95% against OCB +/− classification.

Results

Kappa‐Index in OCB Classification Types 1–5

All five OCB classification types were represented in the 432 CSF/serum sample pairs: Type 1 (polyclonal CSF IgG) n = 205, Type 2 (oligoclonal CSF IgG) n = 73, Type 3 (oligoclonal CSF IgG with some fractions common with serum) n = 50, Type 4 (oligoclonal IgG shared between serum and CSF) n = 100, and Type 5 (monoclonal IgG shared between serum and CSF) n = 4 (Figure 1A, Table S4). We found 123 OCB+ (Types 2 and 3) and 309 OCB− (Types 1, 4, or 5) cases (Figure 1B, Table S5).

When the K‐index was calculated by setting the undetectable KFLC to LOD, we were able to differentiate between all except two types, namely, Types 2 and 3, and Types 4 and 5 (Figure 1A). Positive types 2 and 3 expressed higher median values than negative types 1, 4, and 5 (Table S4). As expected, OCB+ and OCB− categories differed from each other, and the OCB+ group yielded higher results than the OCB− group (AUC 0.93, Figures 1B and S1A, Tables S5 and S6).

Kappa‐Index in MS, CIS, and non‐MS

The CSF KFLC and K‐index results of the selected OCB+ patient samples (n = 77) are detailed in Tables S1–S3. The MS group had, on average, higher K‐index values than the non‐MS or CIS groups (Figure 1C and Table S7). K‐index differentiated the groups, that is, MS and non‐MS (p < 0.0001, Figure S2 and Table S8), MS and CIS (p = 0.0066, Figure S3, Table S8), as well as CIS and non‐MS (p = 0.0362, Figure S4 and Table S8); although the latter discrimination was poor (Table S8). Relying on the highest Youden's index in ROC analysis, an optimal single cutoff of 61.41 suggested MS over non‐MS, 80.92 suggested MS over CIS, and 38.65 suggested CIS over non‐MS (Figures S2–S4, Table S8). Within the MS group, the RMS and PPMS groups did not differ from each other (data not shown).

Different Approaches to Calculate Kappa‐Index Cutoffs

We compared how differently assigned values of CSF KFLC concentrations below LOD affect the single cutoff for positive K‐index results. All approaches distinguished OCB+ and OCB− cases effectively (Tables 1, S6 and Figure S1A–D), but the single cutoff for positive K‐index varied between 3.39 and 13.89. Using the LOD approach, the highest Youden's index indicated 13.89 as the most optimal positive cutoff with the highest specificity, while the other approaches were somewhat more efficient in perspective of AUC and sensitivity.

Optimization of Kappa‐Index Performance

In 269 of 432 (62%) cases (261 OCB−, 8 OCB+), CSF KFLC was below the LOD. With the LOD approach, K‐index was < 4.45, that is, negative, in 144 cases (139 OCB−, 5 OCB+), but in 111 cases, it was in the gray area (108 OCB−, 3 OCB+). Fourteen OCB− cases expressed a K‐index above 12.92, that is, positive, even though CSF KFLC was undetectable. In these samples, the elevated K‐index was due to a very low CSF/serum albumin ratio or serum KFLC.

Considering cases with CSF KFLC below LOD as a negative result, analyzing K‐index only for the rest of the samples and using the above optimized negative and positive cutoffs 4.45 and 12.92, only 6% of the cases remain in the gray area to be reflexed to OCB (10 OCB− and 15 OCB+) (Figure 2B). With this approach, specificity is 99.7% and sensitivity is 92.7%. One (0.3%) of the 309 OCB− cases had a positive K‐index, and nine (7.3%) of the 123 OCB+ cases had a negative K‐index. Out of curiosity, we took a second look at the original OCB gels: Most of these nine OCB+ cases had a borderline or faint positive result, some of which could alternatively be interpreted as negative, underlining the challenge of interpreting OCB gels. The single OCB− case was originally reported as Type 4, but it actually had 3–5 faint oligoclonal IgG bands in CSF and could also be interpreted as Type 3 (i.e., positive).

Of note, if the gray‐area cutoffs (fixing sensitivity and specificity to 95%) had been defined using only the samples with detectable CSF KFLC (n = 163), the cutoffs would be < 6.23 and > 11.02. With this approach, sensitivity drops to 87.9% and specificity remains at 99.3%.

Sex Effect in Kappa‐Index

Finally, results from the samples were compared by sex. In all OCB types 1–5, females expressed somewhat higher K‐index results compared to males (Tables S11 and S12). Although within Types 1 and 4 (OCB‐), the difference was significant (p = 0.004 and 0.0123, respectively), the absolute number of K‐index was practically identical between males and females in these types. When cutoffs were defined for both sexes alone by maximizing Youden's index (setting below LOD results to LOD), the single positive cutoffs were 13.89 for females and 10.94 for males. In‐depth results of sex‐dependent cutoffs are detailed in Table S13.

We further evaluated how the testing protocols (Figure 2) perform in the female versus male populations. The protocol in Figure 2A provides consistent sensitivities and specificities across both sexes but lacks efficiency due to the large number of samples in the gray area. The female population achieved 93.6% specificity and 96.4% sensitivity, while the male population achieved 97.1% specificity and 92.5% sensitivity. Instead, the protocol in Figure 2B is efficient for both sexes. The female population achieved 100% specificity, and 95.2% sensitivity, and the male population achieved 99.3% specificity and 87.5% sensitivity. In‐depth distribution of different OCB types within sex is detailed in Tables S14 and S15.

Discussion

In this study we propose a protocol to detect intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis primarily with the K‐index, which has been accepted in the 2024 revision of McDonald criteria as a substitute for OCB [5]. We have verified a streamlined two‐cutoff reflex approach for CSF analysis (Figure 2B). As different settings of undetectable KFLC levels in CSF affect cutoffs, our results primarily apply to the immunoturbidometric Freelite Mx assay on the Optilite platform. To the best of our knowledge the present study is the largest comparing all OCB classification types (n = 432), which was performed on the Sebia's Hydrasys platform. As expected, the K‐index was elevated when two or more CSF‐specific OCB were present (positive), whereas the K‐index was low when the OCB test indicated polyclonal immunoglobulins and/or serum‐derived OCB.

One of the challenges in utilizing the K‐index is the rather large variation in the published cutoffs. Moreover, there are various approaches to set CSF KFLC results below LOD in the literature [10, 11, 21, 22]. A systematic review suggested using the analytical LOD [13]. Previous studies with the Optilite Freelite Mx Kappa Free assay have evaluated K‐index cutoffs against OCB positivity and used different approaches in the setting undetectable CSF KFLC results (Table S16). In our data set, depending on which value was given to mimic undetectable CSF KFLC, the obtained cutoffs were remarkably different. All approaches efficiently distinguished OCB positive and negative cases, but assigning low artificial values may result in too low cutoffs, a concern previously raised by, for example, Levraut et al. [23].

The double‐cutoff reflex‐approach has recently been verified to be an effective protocol to detect intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis on the Siemens platform [16]. This approach defines a gray area with a K‐index between negative (3.5) and positive (20) cutoffs that should be verified by OCB. With our data on the Optilite platform, the cutoffs are 4.45 and 12.92 (Figure 2A), when setting sensitivity and specificity against OCB to 95% and samples with undetectable KFLC to 0.33 mg/L, that is, LOD. These cutoffs are similar to those by Hegen et al. [16] and compared to OCB, the differences in the positive cutoffs are marginal (Table S4 in that study [16]). At least one study with Optilite has verified the same single cutoff as our positive cutoff (12.8) [24]. Altogether, this approach (Figure 2A) provides a 69% reduction in OCB testing while maintaining the performance to identify CNS inflammation.

The number of undetectable CSF KFLC was high (62.2%), yet in the same magnitude (63.8%) as in a previous study using the same assay [11]. In our area of operation, it has been common to use the OCB test to exclude the possibility of any CNS‐based inflammation, not just as part of the MS diagnostic work‐up, in part explaining the high number of undetectable CSF KFLC (i.e., negative). The Siemens platforms (BN Prospec and BN II) coupled with the N Latex FLC kappa kit (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics Products GmbH) have a lower LOD (0.034 mg/L) [25, 26] compared to Optilite with the Freelite Mx Kappa Kit (0.33 mg/L) [11], suggesting that our study most probably has a higher number of cases with KFLC below the LOD compared to studies with the Siemens platforms.

The most efficient way to utilize the K‐index is presented in Figure 2B. Using the two cutoffs from the LOD approach (Table 2), but considering cases with undetectable CSF KFLC as negative, similar to Sanz Diaz et al. [11], leaves only 6% of the samples for OCB reflex (Figure 2B). This approach maintains great specificity and sensitivity, and minimizes the phenomenon where other subtests falsely increase the value of the K‐index. Although the number of undetectable CSF KFLC in our study may introduce bias to some extent, the absence of detectable KFLC in CSF still suggests that intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis is unlikely in these cases. The number of undetectable CSF KFLC and relatively high LOD of the Optilite can be considered a limitation of this study, alongside that we had only this platform to optimize the K‐index testing protocol. Another limitation is that our protocol purely relies on comparison against OCB. Taken together, we suggest that this approach (Figure 2B) should be further tested and verified also on the Siemens platform.

It has been suggested that accurate MS diagnostics should have sex‐specific K‐index cutoffs [27]. In our study, slightly higher K‐index results in the female population are most probably due to higher B‐cell activity (e.g., immunoglobulin synthesis) in females compared to males [28]. In general, humoral responses to, for example, vaccines have been reported to differentiate between sexes [29]. Notably, within non‐MS, CIS, and MS groups, there was no statistical difference between sexes. Regardless of sex, the protocol in Figure 2B yielded only one false‐positive case and nine false‐negative cases against OCB. Given that the K‐index is also able to detect non‐IgG–mediated CNS inflammation and intrathecal synthesis of IgA and IgM [12], the presence of IgA/IgM synthesis might explain the false positive result. Eight out of nine false‐negative cases had CSF KFLC results below LOD, even though OCB was positive. This was particularly the case with the male population, presenting somewhat lower sensitivity. Regarding these aspects, we believe utilizing sex‐specific cutoffs would not bring any additional clinical information.

The K‐index and OCB have comparable diagnostic accuracy in MS [14]. K‐index > 55 has been shown to have 100% specificity of MS against non‐MS CNS inflammation, whereas a slightly elevated K‐index suggested probability towards other neurological diseases [21]. Our smaller cohort supports this finding, as K‐index above 80.92 is suggestive of MS over CIS, and K‐index above 61.41 is suggestive of MS over non‐MS. Numerous studies have shown that the K‐index is a particularly diagnostic marker [30]. Some studies have also proposed short‐term prognostic value [31, 32, 33, 34]. A recent study by Berek et al. [35] demonstrated that K‐index over 100 predicts time to relapse and time to disability accrual in the long term. In our study, the patients in the CIS group did not develop MS during an extended 10‐year follow‐up. Significantly higher K‐index in the MS group further supports the long‐term prognostic value of K‐index. In line with this cutoff value of 80.92, one other study showed that patients with K‐index above 106 may have four‐fold risk of a new clinical episode [36].

In conclusion, K‐index is a labor‐efficient substitute for OCB testing to detect intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis in MS. We addressed the issue of significant variation in the published K‐index cutoffs in a single data set and found that the main reason is the different settings of CSF KFLC results below LOD. To this end, we present two reflex testing protocols, which can be implemented in clinical laboratories (Figure 2). If one chooses to calculate the K‐index in all cases (Figure 2A), undetectable CSF KFLC values should be assigned to the LOD of the assay to reach consistency across laboratories. For test production from May 2025 onwards, we selected the most efficient and accurate protocol (Figure 2B), in which undetectable CSF KFLC is considered a negative result, a K‐index above 12.92 is considered positive, and only samples with K‐index results in the gray area are reflexed to OCB. The reporting of results to the ordering clinician includes an automated interpretive statement indicating whether there are signs of intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis based on all the tests—in the spirit of good‐old OCB testing.
