Congenital anterior urethrocutaneous fistula
Authors: Yacobda Hamonangan Sigumonrong, Stefanus Purnomo, Muhammad Haritsyah Warli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital anterior urethrocutaneous fistula (CAUF) is an uncommon penile malformation characterized by the urethra’s connection to the skin on the ventral aspect of the penis. It can occur alone or with genitourinary or anorectal anomalies. Due to the scarcity of reported cases, treatment strategies vary, prompting a systematic review to evaluate their clinical presentation, surgical outcomes, and recurrence rates. A systematic review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, searching PubMed, ScienceDirect, EBSCO, Cochrane, and Google Scholar for studies published up to December 12, 2024. Eligible studies focused on CAUF, its variants, surgical interventions, complications, and recurrence rates. Data were extracted and analyzed, including fistula location, associated anomalies, surgical techniques, and patient outcomes. Seventy-eight patients from 46 studies were reviewed. The most common fistula sites were subcoronal (42.3%) and mid-penile (39.7%), followed by penoscrotal (8.9%), proximal penile to subcoronal (5.1%), and scrotal/perineal (3.8%). Chordee occurred in 11 cases, while 19.2% had associated genitourinary anomalies and 16.7% systemic anomalies, mainly anorectal malformations. Surgical repair, especially with a ventral dartos flap, showed high success. Recurrence was 8.9%, with 5.4% closing spontaneously. CAUF is a rare anomaly with variable presentations and frequent associations with other anomalies. Surgical repair generally achieves good outcomes with low recurrence, though spontaneous resolution is possible in select cases. Larger multicenter studies are needed to standardize management and clarify genetic and embryologic factors.

INTRODUCTION

Congenital anterior urethrocutaneous fistula (CAUF) is a rare defect affecting the epithelial lining of the ventral urethra, penis, subcoronal region, or scrotum. It is typically an isolated disorder but can also be associated with genitourinary and anorectal malformations.[1] This disorder is occasionally confused with hypospadias but is distinct from posterior urethrocutaneous fistula, which typically involves a duplication of the Y-type urethra with a proximal ventral limb and is linked to anorectal malformation.[23]

CAUF is less common than a posterior or perineal urethrocutaneous fistula.[4] Clinical results have identified two variants of CAUF: one exhibiting a normal foreskin, intact distal urethra, and normal spongiosum and the other presenting hypospadias-like features with spongiosal abnormalities.[45] The etiology remains unknown, and current embryological theories do not fully explain urethral duplication.[6]

The surgical approach to this anomaly is tailored based on clinical variables, including the diameter of the distal urethra, the position and dimensions of the fistula, and the existence of concomitant anomalies.[56] Due to the limited number of cases reported, most information on CAUF comes from original studies and case reports. This study aims to provide further insights into CAUF to address the limitations of previous research.

METHODS

In accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) criteria, we executed a systematic review of the literature, employing a comprehensive and meticulous methodology to identify pertinent research. We performed a search in the following databases: PubMed, ScienceDirect, EBSCO, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar for studies published up to December 2024. The search methodology incorporated a synthesis of keywords and Medical Subject Headings terms: “Urethral” AND “Fistula” AND “Congenital” OR “Congenital Anterior Urethrocutaneous Fistula” OR “CAUF” OR “Congenital Urethrocutaneous Fistula” OR (“Congenital AND Urethra AND Anomaly”) OR (“Urethrocutaneous Fistula AND Variant AND Anomaly”) OR “Congenital Genitourinary Malformation.” We assessed all pertinent papers to determine the most relevant literature concerning CAUF.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were considered acceptable for inclusion if they satisfied the following criteria: the study concentrated on CAUF, the most recent or largest study, was chosen in instances of numerous publications presenting the same or overlapping data, and the publication language was English. To ensure methodological consistency, only studies published up to December 2024 were considered, aligning with the standardized inclusion period across all sections of this paper. Studies were excluded if they focused on animal or cellular research, as well as nonoriginal works. Additionally, articles that did not provide relevant clinical data on CAUF or failed to report pertinent outcomes were excluded.

Study selection and data extraction

The research selection procedure was executed in two stages. In the preliminary stage, the titles and abstracts of selected papers were evaluated to ascertain their pertinence to CAUF and the study aims. During the second phase, full-text publications were evaluated according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria to confirm their eligibility for final analysis. Two independent evaluators conducted the selection process, and any disagreements were reconciled through dialog and consensus.

For data extraction, a structured approach was employed to ensure completeness and consistency. The extracted variables comprised the first author, year of publication, country of study, sample size, circumcision history, fistula location, associated genitourinary anomalies, additional anomalies, type of surgical repair executed, recurrence count, catheter usage duration, and auxiliary examinations performed. These key variables were systematically recorded to facilitate analysis across the included studies.

Ethical considerations

Ethical considerations were carefully addressed to ensure compliance with the highest standards of research integrity. As the assessment included secondary data from previously published investigations, direct ethical approval was unnecessary. Nevertheless, all studies included in this review had obtained the necessary ethical approvals and informed consent, where applicable, as documented in their respective publications. Our evaluation maintained strict confidentiality and anonymity of study data, adhering to ethical guidelines for handling data in systematic reviews. We prioritized transparency, preventing data manipulation, and ensuring the accuracy and reliability of our findings, thereby upholding the highest standards of ethical research conduct. No conflicts of interest were declared by the authors or the studies included in the review.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using standard risk-of-bias tools tailored for economic evaluations and healthcare cost studies. The quality of each study was rated based on criteria such as study design, sample size, cost measurement methods, and risk of bias in outcomes.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review included a total of 78 patients from 46 articles, providing a comprehensive overview of CAUF cases reported in the literature. The cases were identified across various countries, demonstrating the global but rare occurrence of this anomaly.[56] The most common fistula locations were subcoronal (42.3%) and mid-penile (39.7%), followed by penoscrotal (8.9%), proximal penile to subcoronal (5.1%), and scrotal/perineal (3.8%). While subcoronal fistulas were previously reported as the most frequent, this review suggests mid-penile CAUF may be more common, possibly due to selection bias or reporting variations.[78910111213141516171819202122232425]

Chordee was present in 11 cases, and 15 cases (19.2%) had associated genitourinary anomalies, including megalourethra (15%), undescended testes (26%), and Y-type urethral duplication (6%). Additionally, 16.7% had systemic anomalies such as anorectal malformations (66%), inguinal hernia (14%), and congenital heart disease (7%). These findings suggest that CAUF is often part of a broader congenital condition, necessitating comprehensive evaluation.[678910111213141516]

Various surgical techniques have been used to repair CAUF, with the ventral dartos flap showing high success rates, especially for subcoronal fistulas with sufficient surrounding tissue. This technique reinforces the repair site, promoting healing and reducing recurrence.[123456] Surgical management was tailored based on fistula location, urethral integrity, and associated anomalies.[8910111213141516] The recurrence rate was low (8.9%), with 5.4% of cases closing spontaneously, suggesting that conservative management may be viable in select cases. Notably, no persistent recurrences occurred after reoperation, indicating overall surgical success with minimal long-term complications.[7]

CAUF is classified into two types: (1) isolated CAUF with an intact distal urethra and normal foreskin, and (2) CAUF associated with chordee, dorsal hood, or spongiosal defects, resembling hypospadias.[26] While isolated CAUF is more common, its embryologic origin remains unclear. Theories suggest defects in urethral fold fusion due to transient androgen deficiency, impaired local testosterone effects, or defective corpus spongiosum leading to skin erosion. Genetic and androgenic dysfunctions have also been proposed. Another theory suggests that an isolated congenital penile urethrocutaneous fistula may result from the prevention of fusion of the urethral folds due to a focal developmental defect of the urethral plate. Cases reported by Ansari et al. have reported the absence of scarring in all cases, supporting this theory.[27] Campbell’s theory states that distal congenital obstruction can lead to embryonic urethral defects called fistulas.[528]

Batie notes that congenital CAUFs are rare compared to postprocedural fistulas. The urethral grooves normally fuse proximodistally, but unexplained embryologic “skip” lesions in healthy urethral spongiosum may contribute to CAUF unexplained.[7] The study conducted by Moza brings up an interesting element of the fact that early skin dehiscence can occur despite successful urethroplasty and tension-free closure. This can occur without any evidence of infection, inflammation, or trauma, which is still a question related to the wound healing of the skin adjacent to the urethral groove.[8] Ansari describes various surgical techniques for CAUF, including skin flaps, urethroplasty (Thiersch-Duplay and Denis Brown), buccal mucosa grafts, and dartos flap repair. The choice of procedure depends on fistula size and location.[27]

Long-term follow-up is also essential in monitoring surgical success and identifying potential complications at an early stage.[1] Routine postoperative assessments allow clinicians to evaluate urinary function, structural integrity, and any signs of recurrence, enabling timely intervention if needed.[4] As the patient grows, continuous monitoring ensures that any functional abnormalities are addressed, improving overall quality of life and long-term surgical outcomes.[6]

According to the aforementioned research, conducting a physical assessment on all individuals is preferable, as it may result in varied approaches to fistula closure. Fistulas measuring <0.5 cm can be effectively managed with straightforward closure, particularly following the excision of the margins and subsequent skin closure. Fistulas ranging from 0.5 cm to <1.0 cm may be occluded using a rotating flap. Fistulas above 1.0 cm can be occluded using tubular incision plate urethroplasty employing the Thiersch-Duplay method.[27] Two rare diseases with very few reported cases, namely scaphoid megalourethra associated with congenital urethrocutaneous fistula, have similar treatment measures. The treatment is surgical that is urethroplasty following the same basic technique as hypospadias.[28]

This systematic review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. As it is based primarily on case reports and small sample sizes, the generalizability of the findings is limited. Furthermore, missing or incomplete data, particularly regarding recurrence rates and long-term outcomes, restricts a comprehensive evaluation of postsurgical success. To address these gaps, larger, multicenter prospective studies are needed to establish evidence-based management strategies for CAUF. Future research should also explore genetic and embryological factors that may contribute to its development, providing a deeper understanding of its etiology. Additionally, there is a strong need for standardized reporting of surgical outcomes, including long-term follow-up, to facilitate meaningful comparisons across studies. Establishing a more rigorous classification system for CAUF, based on fistula location, severity, and associated anomalies, would further improve diagnosis and treatment strategies. By addressing these research gaps, future studies can enhance clinical decision-making and optimize patient outcomes.

CONCLUSION

This systematic review highlights the rare occurrence, clinical variability, and diverse surgical approaches for CAUF. Our findings confirm subcoronal fistulas as the most common subtype, often with an intact distal urethra, necessitating individualized surgical planning. While surgical success rates are high with low recurrence, cases with associated anomalies may require more complex interventions. Given the limited long-term outcome data, future multicenter studies are needed to establish standardized treatment protocols, explore genetic and embryological factors, and optimize long-term patient care and surgical success.
