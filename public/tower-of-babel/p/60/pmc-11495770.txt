Estimation of the graft failure by current value joint model, and extension to alternative parameterization structures: Cohort study
Authors: Alev Bakir, Zeynep Atli, Eda Kaya, Salih Pekmezci, Nurhan Seyahi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In clinical practice, individuals are followed up to predict the outcome event of interest, and their longitudinal measurements are collected on a regular or irregular basis. We aimed to examine the classical approach, joint model (JM), and alternative parameterization structures using data on the effect of time-varying longitudinal measurements on survival. The motivating cohort dataset included 158 consecutive kidney transplant recipients who had baseline and follow-up data. Although the longitudinal log-transformed estimated glomerular filtration rate (log[eGFR]) measurements and graft failure have an association clinically, the 2 processes are analyzed separately in the classical approach. In addition to the extended Cox model, the current value JM, the weighted cumulative effect JM, and dynamic predictions were performed in the study, by taking advantage of R codes. Of the 158 patients, 34.8% were males. The mean age was 29.8 ± 10.9 years, and the median age was 26 years at the time of transplantation. The hazard ratio for graft failure was 8.80 for a 1-unit decrease in log(eGFR) in the extended Cox model, 10.58 in the current value JM, and 3.65 in the weighted cumulative effect JM. The presence of coronary heart disease was also found to be associated with log(eGFR): 0.199 (P = .03) for the current value JM and 0.197 (P = .03) for the weighted cumulative effect JM. The current value JM was identified as a better model than the extended Cox model and the weighted cumulative effect JM based on parameter and standard error comparison and goodness of fit criteria. JMs should be preferred, as they facilitate better clinical decisions by accounting for the varying slopes and longitudinal variation of estimated glomerular filtration rate among patients. Suitable types of models should be practiced depending on baseline biomarker levels, their trends over time, the distribution of the biomarkers, and the number of longitudinal biomarkers.

1. Introduction

The end-stage renal disease represents a significant public health burden in the world.[1] With regard to renal replacement, there are several treatment strategies, including hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, and kidney transplantation. Compared to others, kidney transplant recipients are more advantageous in terms of better quality of life, lower health costs, and a longer survival time.[2] According to the European Renal Association Registry Annual Report 2021, 19,568 kidney transplantations were performed annually.[3] Fortunately, compared with previous reports, a decline was observed in all-cause graft failure rate.[4] With these studies notwithstanding, the graft trend failure rate was still reported to be a significant problem for kidney transplant recipients.[5] For the purpose of managing early treatment strategies based on outcome prediction, follow-up of graft functionality is of great importance for kidney transplant recipients.

In clinical studies, individuals are followed up, and measurements are repeated on a regular or irregular basis, especially for biomarkers of morbidity, mortality, diagnosis, prognosis, and recurrence. Researchers are particularly interested in treatment outcome estimation models that depend on measurements that change over time and cumulative effects. For this reason, longitudinal study designs have become conspicuously popular.

Generally, the Cox proportional-hazards model is used to estimate risk factors related to survival events. In the Cox proportional hazard model, the risk of an event depends only on fixed effects such as gender, treatment, and baseline age. However, the risk of an event may be associated with time-dependent covariates. Therefore, the Cox proportional hazard model may be extended to include time-dependent covariates.[6] When endogenous variables are included in the model, this approach can lead to a biased estimation of the parameter that describes the association between repeated measurements and the risk of an event. Recently, joint modeling was developed to render the estimation models more understandable by using both the “repeated measurement” and “their time-to-event” data together.[7,8] Researchers have recently shown a growing tendency toward the longitudinal data design with the joint model (JM) to yield smaller sample sizes and higher power and obtain higher efficiency from the study.[9–12] In addition, dynamic prediction for survival probabilities is more reliable in JM since biomarker measurements up to time t > s, which are affected by event at time s, which means that biomarkers are directly associated with failure time.[13–15]

The standard JM formulation uses current value parameterization. Current value parameterization in joint modeling directly captures the immediate effect of a biomarker on the hazard rate for an event. However, in some cases, past levels of biomarkers also relate to the risk of an event occurring. Alternative parameterization structures have been proposed to overcome these limitations and provide a more comprehensive evaluation. Additional insights into how changing rates and cumulative histories of biomarkers relate to the risk of an event are provided by time-dependent slopes and weighted cumulative effect parameterizations. By integrating these advanced modeling techniques, survival prediction can be improved while facilitating more informed clinical decisions.

Current value parameterization is easy to apply and implement. It reflects the current status of a biomarker, and it is associated with the risk of an event, making it comprehensible and intuitive. This approach is effective in capturing the immediate effect of a biomarker on an event risk, particularly in situations where the current state of a biomarker is strongly associated with the occurrence of an event. The current value parameterization does not consider the trajectory of biomarkers, which can be very important in understanding the overall risk profile. Ignoring past values of biomarkers may lead to biased parameter estimation especially if the historical state has a significant impact on the risk of an event. Alternative association structures examine all past biomarker developments, thereby enhancing the evaluation of events risks. Moreover, they appropriately incorporate time-dependent covariates and, thus, are suitable for modeling dynamic processes more accurately. Nevertheless, these models are more complex and computationally intensive, and these models need more frequent data, which may not always be available to collect in some cases.

In this study, we aimed to examine the current value JM, the weighted cumulative JM, and dynamic predictions, in conjunction with the extended Cox model, to improve the accuracy of risk estimation of the events of interest. Our motivation is to contribute to the medical literature by exploring the relationship between the longitudinal variation and trends of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) biomarkers and graft failure.

2. Methods

The real-world data were collected from a retrospective cohort. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul University of Medical Faculty (no. 45940) and protocols conformed to the ethical guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. Strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology checklist for the Cohort study was appraised.

Baseline and follow-up data of 158 consecutive kidney transplant recipients, whom we had analyzed previously, were included in the study.[16] Data were collected from adult (≥18 years of age) patient files and from the electronic database of the hospital. There was no missing data. Donors are spouses or family relatives up to third degree. Recipients who had undergone transplantation less than one year prior, as well as those with malignancies or active infections, were excluded from the study.

The standard immunosuppressive protocol consisted of induction therapy with rabbit antithymocyte globulin (in cadaveric transplantations) or basiliximab (in immunologically high-risk living transplantations), followed by maintenance therapy with a calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus or cyclosporine), mycophenolate mofetil or mycophenolate sodium, and low-dose prednisolone (5 mg/day). All recipients who were diagnosed with an acute rejection episode received bolus steroids. Further treatment was tailored according to the severity of rejection.

Patients’ follow-up files were reviewed and demographic (age, transplant age, and gender), clinical (date of transplantation, donor type, recipient’s body mass index, hypertension, antidiabetic medication use, and coronary heart disease), and previous renal replacement therapy (type and duration) data were recorded. The number of measurements varied from patient to patient because different follow-up schedules were planned for each patient according to their clinical conditions. In the analysis of the unbalanced data set, the median follow-up time was 14.58 (range, 3.69–31.13) years.

Serum creatinine (SCr) is a routinely measured biomarker to monitor graft functionality and the easiest method to maintain the follow-up.[17] eGFR based on serum creatinine measurement is the clinical standard for the evaluation of kidney function (1)[18]

Starting with the time of kidney transplantation, the eGFR values of the patients were measured during their routine controls for the follow-up of graft functionality. We did not include age and gender data in the statistical models because of the age- and gender-adjusted eGFR values of the recipients. We calculated the log-transformed eGFR [log(eGFR)] values to get better statistical estimations on linear relationships and homoscedasticity. This was considered the longitudinal data, which was correlated with the change in graft survival outcome over a period. Data regarding events of interest and dead or lost follow-up patients were recorded as right censored at the end of the study.[19]

2.1. Statistical analysis

2.1.1. Descriptive statistics

Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, median, and minimum-maximum, while categorical data were expressed as frequency and percentages. The Kaplan-Meier analysis was examined to estimate survival probability. The JM and Cox time-dependent covariate analyses were used to estimate the association between the changes in log(eGFR) and graft survival. The weighted cumulative effects were examined to show how strongly the graft survival is related to the cumulative history of log(eGFR). Dynamic estimates were obtained for patient-specific conditional kidney survival probabilities at each time point at which individual log(eGFR) measurements were made. The JM package in R language (version 4.0.2) was used for the implementation of the JM.[20] Dynamic predictions were obtained with the survfit.JM function available in JMpackage. A P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

2.1.2. Explanation of statistical models

The JM for longitudinal and survival data and the extended Cox model were used in this study. In the classical approach, the association between endogenous variables such as repeated biomarkers and the risk of an event (kidney failure) is usually examined with the extended Cox model. The disadvantage of this model is that longitudinal outcomes measured with error are included in the model directly, and the measurement error is ignored. This assumption leads to obtaining biased estimates of association parameters.[21] Another disadvantage is that the extended Cox model assumes that repeated measurements are constant between 2 follow-up time points. However, this assumption is not realistic, especially for endogenous variables such as biomarkers.[11,15,22] An alternative approach is a 2-stage approach in which the longitudinal and survival processes are modeled separately.[23] Since information from the survival process is not used to obtain parameter estimates for the longitudinal model, the 2-stage approach does not remove bias completely.[12] Wulfsohn and Tsisatis[21] and Henderson et al[24] suggested the JM approach in which model parameters are obtained simultaneously to assess the association between longitudinal measurement and the risk of an event.

JM consists of 2 submodels: the longitudinal and the survival submodel. The most commonly used longitudinal and survival submodels are the linear mixed effects model and the proportional Cox hazard model, respectively.[25,26]

2.1.3. Alternative parameterization structures

The standard JM formulation is given in equation (2). In this formulation, the association parameter is assumed to explain the association between the risk of an event at time t and the true level of the longitudinal biomarker mi(t) at the same time point, and this parameterization is often called the current value. The current value association has a simple and clear functional form. However, in some cases, the strength of the association between longitudinal and survival processes may need to be explained with complex forms. The association structure can be expanded into alternative forms of parameterization structures such as the current value, time-dependent slope parameterization, weighted effects, and weighted cumulative effect parameterization. The extension of parameterization structures was presented by Rizopoulos,[11] and for more details, we referred to Rizopoulos and Ghosh,[27] Rizopoulos et al,[28] and Mauff et al.[29] The weighted cumulative effects model was selected to assess whether graft survival is related to the cumulative history of eGFR.

2.1.3.1. Weighted cumulative effects

2.1.4. Dynamic survival probabilities prediction

Prediction of individualized dynamic survival probabilities plays a prominent role for clinicians to understand the disease process when a new longitudinal measurement for a patient is available. We focus on predicting survival probabilities for a new patient who has been known to survive up to time t with an available history of longitudinal measurement and a vector of baseline covariates.

To handle the computational complexity in obtaining the conditional probability of the standard error and confidence interval (CI), Rizopoulos[11] and Rizopoulos et al[30] suggested using Monte Carlo simulations.

For the survival submodels, we used the proportional Cox hazard model. We examined the association between the survival event and log(eGFR) values by fitting the JM with different association structures. The association between longitudinal measurement and the event outcome was found to be significant because the parameters α were strongly significant and negative: α1, −2.359 (−3.059 to −1.659) and α2, −1.296 (−1.983 to −0.566; P < .001 for both). The survival submodel of the current value JM (model 1) showed that the HR for graft survival was 10.58 (95% CI, 5.25–21.30) for a 1-unit decrease in log(eGFR), and it was 3.65 (95% CI, 1.76–7.26) in the weighted cumulative effect JM (model 2). The presence of coronary heart disease, standardized RRT time, donor type, and antidiabetic medication use parameters were not significant in either statistical model. Although the results of the current value JM and the weighted cumulative effect JM were similar, the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) values of the current value JM model were found to be lower (Table 3).

4. Discussion

In this study, we used different approaches to examine the relationship between time-varying longitudinal measurements and the risk of an event of interest. Besides the widely recognized extended Cox model, we examined 2 different functional forms for the association structure (current value and weighted cumulative effects), which have been proposed recently for clinical statistical studies.

In the extended Cox model, it was found that reduced log(eGFR) levels were associated with a higher risk of graft failure. In our study, for a 1-unit decrease in log(eGFR), the HR increased by 8.80 in the Cox model, whereas it was 10.58 in the current value JM. Panunzi[8] found the association parameter to be 2.32 with the extended Cox model and 2.75 with the current value JM. These results indicate that lower log(eGFR) values are strongly associated with an increased risk of graft failure. It once again emphasizes the importance of regularly monitoring this biomarker in kidney transplant patients. The utilization of the current JMs to predict the parameters provided a model better than the extended Cox model: AIC, 290.08 and BIC, 298.40 for the extended Cox model and AIC, 155.97 and BIC, 223.35 for the current JM. Moreover, JMs showed that graft failure was associated not only with reduced log(eGFR) levels but also with the magnitude of the decreased log(eGFR) values (P < .001). Although the findings from the models looked similar, a smaller estimate for log(eGFR) was found with the Cox model than with the current JM. In the extended Cox analysis, the association parameter was considerably underestimated in this study, as in the literature.[10,22]

We obtained similar comments from the studies within the limited literature where we used similar approaches. Asar et al[22] reported that the association between the decrease in log(eGFR) and the increased HR for RRT was underestimated by the Cox model compared to the JM. Fournier et al[31] demonstrated that the risk of graft failure increased as greater increases in the SCr level were observed using JM. Wang et al[32] found that albumin trajectories after peritoneal dialysis were better at predicting the mortality risk compared with initial serum albumin values with JM.

Piulachs et al[33] reported the relation between high cumulative demand and lower probabilities of survival based on the weighted cumulative effect JM. Mauff et al[29] focused on the weighted cumulative effects to examine the cumulative effect of the HbA1c marker on the risk of vision-threatening retinopathy. Motivated by these studies in the literature, we extended the scope of our study as a guide, by examining the weighted cumulative effect of log(eGFR) values on the risk of graft failure. Log(eGFR) was similarly estimated to decrease as the time increased in both the current value JM and the weighted cumulative effect JM. In addition, a patient with a history of coronary heart disease was more likely to have an increased log(eGFR) compared to the same patient without this history in both approaches, in contrast to the extended Cox model results. It may be identified as a weakness of the extended Cox model results. We found the current value JM is the best model according to AIC and BIC and standard errors (AIC, 155.97 and BIC, 223.35 for the current JM and AIC, 192.10 and BIC, 259.48 for the weighted cumulative effect JM). For a 1-unit decrease in log(eGFR), the HR was 10.58 in the current value JM and 3.65 in the weighted cumulative JM.

In nephrology, where trajectories in longitudinal data are valuable, individualized dynamic survival probabilities were predicted at each new measurement of the patient. This method, which creates dynamic and personalized risk estimations and patient-specific reports during any time of patient follow-up, is frequently found within the literature.[34–37]

In conclusion, examining the degradation of the biomarkers during a process is important for clinical follow-up studies. From this perspective, the JM was more advantageous compared with the commonly used models. The current JM, unlike the extended Cox model, provided a better model and parameter estimation. Also, we observed that other types of association structures advance the literature depending on the data structure. The dynamic predictions from JM were used throughout the history of biomarker measurement, and by updating survival probabilities with new biomarkers, it also strengthened clinical implications. In addition, JMs utilized shared random effects parameters and provided subject-specific information because patients have different slopes. This feature allows the clinician to make better decisions by using patient-specific predictions. Comprehending the longitudinal variation of eGFR enhances clinical evaluations of patient health and the risk of kidney failure.

4.1. Limitations

The number of events of interest was relatively small, and the data were collected retrospectively, but they were real-world data. Our study might not be representative of the general transplant population because the majority of the patients in our unit were living donor transplant recipients rather than not cadaveric donors.

4.2. Future directions

This framework can be expanded to incorporate multivariate joint modeling, utilizing longitudinal data such as proteinuria and blood pressure in conjunction with eGFR.
