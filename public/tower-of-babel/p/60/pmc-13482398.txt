Caloric restriction improves glycemic control via the adiponectin–ceramide axis in non-obese men and women: the CALERIE™ 2 randomized controlled trial
Authors: Moritz V. Warmbrunn, Raaj Kishore Biswas, Anthony S. Don, Maria Lastra Cagigas, Yi Li, Lin Yang, Stephen M. Twigg, Giovanni Fiorito, Luigi Fontana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Caloric restriction (CR) improves metabolic health across species, but the molecular mediators of its effects in humans remain incompletely defined. In a 24-month non-blinded randomized controlled trial (Clinicaltrial.gov: NCT00427193) of non-obese (BMI 22-27.9 kg/m2) men and premenopausal women aged 21 to 50 years, we assessed prespecified outcomes. Participants were randomized to an ad libitum or CR diet. We found that CR was associated with increased high-molecular-weight (HMW) adiponectin and reduced circulating ceramide species implicated in insulin resistance, including C16:0, C18:0, and C24:0. Mediation analysis indicated that reductions in ceramides were statistically compatible with partial mediation of the CR-associated improvements in insulin secretion, insulin sensitivity, and IGF-1 signaling markers. These effects were most pronounced at 12 months and attenuated by 24 months, suggesting partial metabolic adaptation over time. Overall, our findings are consistent with a model in which CR remodels bioactive lipid profiles and may enhance glucose metabolism in part through an adiponectin-ceramide-linked mechanism, highlighting a potential therapeutic axis for enhancing metabolic health.

Introduction

The global prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2D) is rapidly increasing, with cases projected to rise from 537 million in 2021 to 783 million by 20451. This surge is primarily driven by the obesity pandemic2. Central obesity, compounded by genetic factors, triggers harmful metabolic adaptations such as low-grade chronic inflammation and dysregulated adipokine secretion, which promote systemic insulin resistance3,4. Moreover, excess adiposity leads to ectopic fat accumulation in several key organs and increased fatty acid influx into muscles cells. This disrupts β-oxidation and intracellular signaling, impairs mitochondrial function, and promotes further lipid accumulation, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates insulin resistance5,6.

Metabolites are increasingly recognized as active signaling molecules that can drive metabolic dysfunction7. In particular, elevated levels of circulating lipid intermediates such as ceramides and diacylglycerols disrupt insulin receptor phosphorylation, impairing GLUT4 translocation and glucose uptake in muscle, and GLUT2-mediated glucose handling in the liver, thereby promoting gluconeogenesis and hyperglycemia5. Additionally, ceramides can also affect IGF-1 signaling1 and have been implicated in inflammatory processes8,9. Other metabolites, such as the phospholipids phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), which depend on diacylglycerol as a precursor, have also been linked to insulin resistance10,11. Aberrations in PC and PE metabolism can alter cell organelle and membrane fluidity, compromising mitochondrial respiration and exacerbating gluconeogenic pathways12.

Early intervention in the course of insulin resistance is critical for preventing progression to prediabetes and T2D and for lowering all-cause mortality13, as reversal becomes increasingly difficult once β-cell function is compromised. Among non-pharmacological strategies, caloric restriction (CR) with adequate nutrition has emerged as a powerful intervention that improves insulin resistance, modulates lipid metabolism, and reduces systemic inflammation while improving immune function, even in young, healthy, non-obese men and women without overt metabolic dysfunction14.

The Comprehensive Assessment of Long-term Effects of Reducing Intake of Energy (CALERIE) trial, a 2-year, multicentre, randomized controlled study, was designed to investigate the long-term physiological effects of sustained CR, including markers linked to healthy aging. While previous analyses have demonstrated favorable changes in weight, body composition, insulin sensitivity and cardiometabolic risk15, the metabolomic adaptations underlying these effects remain less explored. Here, we examine both short- and long-term changes in circulating metabolites following a 25% CR intervention, and explore their relationship to insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism in a metabolically healthy, non-obese adult population. To our knowledge, this is the first human intervention study to report findings consistent with CR improving glycemic control in part through changes in adiponectin and ceramide levels. These findings provide important mechanistic insights that could guide the development of more effective, targeted dietary interventions.

To identify metabolomic changes specifically attributable to CR, we applied a module preservation analysis within the framework of weighted metabolite co-expression network analysis. This approach examined within-individual changes from baseline to 12 and 24 months in both CR and AL groups. Module preservation analysis was then used to identify group-specific modules robustly maintained in the CR group but were not preserved in the AL group, indicating CR-specific effects18. The analysis was performed on modules detected at both time points, with only modules containing at least 20 metabolites included to ensure statistical robustness. In total, 12 modules were assessed for preservation at 12 months and 9 at 24 months. Among these, one module (referred to as the Blue module) was not preserved between groups at either 12 (Blue.12) and 24 (Blue.24) months, indicating that the metabolites within this module were specifically altered in response to CR at both time points (Fig. 3A, Figs. To explore how these CR-specific lipid clusters relate to metabolic health, we correlated the Blue modules from the weighted metabolite co-expression network analysis with relevant clinical outcomes. Interestingly, at 12 months, the Blue-12 module was positively associated with amino acid intake, AUC insulin, total cholesterol, and fat-free mass, suggesting a direct link between this lipid cluster and well-established metrics of metabolic health. Conversely, the Blue-12 module was inversely associated with insulin sensitivity, as assessed by the Matsuda index (Table S8). Similar patterns were observed at 24 months, suggesting a sustained relationship over time (Table S9). Specifically, the Blue-24 module was positively correlated at 24 months with total cholesterol and dietary intake of several amino acids, including methionine, all three BCAAs (isoleucine, leucine, and valine), lysine, and cystine.

Together, these findings suggest that CR drives coordinated shifts in complex lipid networks, particularly involving sphingolipids and phosphatidylcholines, that are closely associated with clinically meaningful improvements in metabolic health.

Lipids mediate improved insulin secretion and reduced inflammation via CR

Given the novel association between CR-specific lipid modules (Blue-12 and Blue-24) and key metabolic outcomes (Fig. 4), we hypothesized that these 164 lipids may partially mediate the beneficial effects of CR. To test this hypothesis, we performed causal mediation analysis to disentangle direct and indirect effects of CR on clinical outcomes at 12 months and 24 months. Based on Blue modules’ associations with AUC insulin, we hypothesized that changes in insulin sensitivity (AUC insulin) were mediated by these lipid species. At 12 months, 32 lipid metabolites significantly mediated improvements in AUC insulin within the CR group, with ceramides comprising the largest subclass (n = 12). As ceramides were the dominant mediators, they were the focus of subsequent analyses. Because ceramide species are biologically and statistically correlated, the estimated indirect effects for individual species should be interpreted as overlapping rather than mutually independent. Notably, no significant mediators were identified in the AL group during this interval (Tables S10–S11). At 24 months, 18 lipid species, including 11 ceramides, showed to mediate CR-induced improvements in AUC insulin, whereas only one ceramide (22:1) had a mediating effect in the AL group (Fig. 5). Specifically, reductions in ceramides 20:1, 18:0, and 22:1 were significant mediators at 12 months. While ceramide 22:1 also showed an indirect effect, the total effect was not statistically significant. In addition, reductions in total dihydroceramides modestly contributed ( ~ 5%) to improved AUC insulin. However, fasting insulin levels were unaffected by ceramides changes in both groups at either time point. Other lipid classes, such as phosphatidylethanolamines and triacylglycerols, were also found to mediate insulin-related changes at 12 months in the CR group.

Given ceramides’ known roles in promoting insulin resistance15, impairing pancreatic β-cell function, and inducing β-cell apoptosis in animal models20, we hypothesized that the observed improvement of AUC insulin may reflect enhanced beta-cell function or insulin sensitivity. To explore this, we examined whether reductions in ceramides mediated changes in C-peptide secretion. Notably, reductions in ceramide 18:0 mediated 24–33% of the observed decrease in C-peptide levels at both 12 and 24 months in the CR group. Ceramide 22:0 exerted a similar, albeit smaller (14-18%), effect. At 12 months, mediation analysis suggests that additional ceramides (16:0, 18:1, 20:0, 22:0, 22:1, 24:1) also contributed to reduced fasting C-peptide levels.

Given previous links between ceramides and inflammation8,9, we investigated their potential role in modulating inflammatory cytokines. Mediation analysis suggested that CR significantly reduced TNF-α levels at both time points via changes in triacylglycerols, but not ceramides, and only in the intervention group. CR also reduced C-reactive protein (CRP) at both time points, though this effect was not mediated by ceramides. Instead, other lipid species, including phosphatidylethanolamines, lysophosphatidylcholines and sphingolipids, mediated CRP changes in the CR group, while phosphatidylcholines and two ceramides (18:1, 22:1) were mediators in the AL group, although without a direct or total effect. Interestingly, while CR did not directly alter IL-8 levels, mediation analysis revealed that increases in specific lipid species, namely ceramides (18:1, 20:1, 24:0), hexosylceramide 22:1, and dihydroceramides (22:2, 24:0), were associated with elevated IL-8 concentrations at both timepoints in the CR group. Additionally, ceramide 20:0 contributed to IL-8 increases at 12 months. At 24 months, several triacylglycerols and phosphatidylethanolamines also emerged as significant IL-8 mediators (Tables S10–S11). These results suggest that, while CR does not directly modulate IL-8 expression, CR may affect lipidomic profiles directly and thereby contribute to low-grade inflammatory responses.

Because ceramides are known to impair insulin signaling by disrupting mitochondrial function21 and attenuating Akt phosphorylation downstream of insulin and IGF-1 receptors22, we tested the potential role of ceramides in regulating circulating IGF-1 levels. IGF-1 was increased in the CR group at 12 months but was comparable to the AL group at 24 months (Fig. S12). At 12 months, reductions in total ceramides, including 18:1, 20:1, 22:1, and 24:0, were associated with increased IGF-1 via significant negative indirect effects. Similar trends were observed at 24 months (Fig.5), involving additional ceramides (16:0, 22:1, 24:0) as well as total sphingomyelins, dihydroceramides and cholesterol esters (Tables S10–S11).

Together, these mediation analyses are statistically compatible with a partial indirect role of specific ceramides and related lipid species in CR-associated changes in insulin sensitivity, β-cell function, inflammation, and IGF-1 signaling.

Adiponectin mediates ceramide reduction under caloric restriction

Given the substantial ceramide reductions observed with CR and their potential role in mediating metabolic improvements, we next investigated upstream regulators of ceramide levels in this context. Previous research has suggested that adiponectin, an adipokine primarily secreted by adipose tissue, can lower ceramide concentrations through adiponectin receptors, which share sequence homology with ceramidases23–26. On this basis, we hypothesized that CR-induced increases in adiponectin may mediate reductions in plasma ceramides.

As anticipated, plasma adiponectin concentration rose significantly at both 12 and 24 months in the CR group (Fig. 1). At 12 months, mediation analysis revealed that adiponectin influenced 45 metabolites in the CR group, but none in the AL group. The most prominently affected classes included triacyclglycerols (n = 17), ceramides (n = 8), and dihydroceramides (n = 5). Most notably, CR-induced increases in adiponectin were associated with reductions in total ceramide levels, including specific species (16:0, 18:0, 20:0, 22:0, 22:1, 24:0, 24:1). Mediation analysis revealed that adiponectin levels accounted for 10-20% of the total effect of ceramide levels (Tables S12–S13), providing mechanistic insight into the link between elevated adiponectin and decreased ceramide burden under CR. At 24 months, no adiponectin-mediated effects were observed in the CR group. However, in the AL group, 11 metabolites, including one ceramide (24:0) and two dihydroceramides (22:0, 22:2), were identified as mediators, though their relevance remains unclear as no direct effect was observed.

Untargeted metabolites do not exhibit CR-specific changes

The untargeted metabolite panel identified 1118 metabolites, of which 864 were annotated and classified as belonging to a metabolic process. To assess CR-specific metabolic alterations, we performed weighted metabolite correlation network analysis on changes from baseline to 12 and 24 months. Module preservation analysis revealed that none of the identified metabolite modules were uniquely preserved in the CR group (Tables S14–S15), indicating that untargeted metabolites did not demonstrate CR-specific patterns over time.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses including sex and age as covariates in the mediation models showed that the ceramides identified as mediators of improved insulin sensitivity remained statistically significant after adjustment. Ceramides 18:0 (−0.04 AU [−0.15, −0.02]), 20:1 (−0.02 [−0.07, −0.00]), and 22:1 (−0.02 [−0.14, −0.01]) mediated reductions in AUC insulin between baseline and 12 months in the CR group, but not in the AL group. Similarly, between baseline and 24 months, ceramides 18:0 (−0.04 [−0.14, −0.01]), 20:1 (−0.03 [−0.12, −0.01]), and 22:1 (−0.02, [−0.14, −0.01]) mediated reductions in AUC insulin in the CR group but not in the AL group. These results were consistent after adjusting for age and sex (Table S16). When total energy intake was added as an additional covariate, the overall pattern remained unchanged at both 12 and 24 months (Table S16).

We additionally conducted multiple-mediator analysis grouping ceramides by acyl-chain length. Specifically, we examined long-chain (C16-20) and very long-chain (C22-24) ceramides present in the Blue-12 and Blue-24 modules. Long-chain ceramides (16:0, 18:0, 18:1, 20:0, 20:1) collectively mediated reductions in AUC insulin over time in the CR group (−0.02 [−0.03, −0.00]) but not in the AL group (−0.02 [−0.06, 0.03]). Very-long-chain ceramides (22:0, 22:1, 24:0, 24:1), showed a modest indirect effect in the CR group (−0.01 [−0.02, 0.00]), and no effect in the AL group (0.00 [−0.05, 0.05]). Only one medium-chain ceramide (14:0) and one ultra-long-chain ceramide (26:1) were present in both modules.

Discussion

Caloric restriction (CR) is one of the most robust non-pharmacological strategies for improving metabolic health, yet molecular processes underlying its benefits in humans remain incompletely understood. In this randomized controlled trial, CR was associated with improved postprandial glycemic control in non-obese, metabolically healthy individuals, and our findings are consistent with a lipid-mediated process involving the adiponectin–ceramide axis. Specifically, our mediation analyses suggest a potential pathway in which CR increases circulating HMW adiponectin, which is statistically associated with lower levels of circulating ceramide species—bioactive lipids linked to insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction. These ceramide reductions were statistically compatible with improvements in insulin secretion and sensitivity. Overall, these findings are consistent with a potential lipid-based process by which CR may contribute to better glycemic control.

Ceramides, synthesized endogenously from saturated fatty acids and amino acids19, are potent inhibitors of insulin signaling and accumulate in states of metabolic dysfunction, including obesity and type 2 diabetes27. Importantly, their accumulation appears more closely linked to insulin resistance than total lipid content, as demonstrated by the insulin sensitivity preserved in endurance-trained individuals despite high intramyocellular lipid levels28,29. Our findings corroborate prior evidence from animal and human studies indicating that diet and exercise interventions reduce ceramide levels in parallel with improved insulin sensitivity30.

Similar patterns have been observed in non-human primates, where prolonged exposure to high-fat diets led to weight gain and elevated plasma levels of ceramide and dihydroceramide31. These increases correlated with higher fasting glucose and insulin levels, reinforcing the strong association between ceramides and insulin resistance. This supports the hypothesis that reducing ceramide levels may improve glycemic control. Experimental data from rodent models further corroborate this idea. Inhibiting ceramide synthesis using agents such as the serine palmitoyltransferase inhibitor myriocin has been shown to prevent or reverse insulin resistance32–34, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis35, diabetes32, hypertension, atherosclerosis36,37, and even cardiomyopathy38,39. Similarly, inhibition of the final steps in ceramide biosynthesis using the dihydroceramide desaturase 1 inhibitor fenretinide dramatically decreases ceramide levels40 and has insulin-sensitizing effects comparable to myriocin40,41. Together, these findings support the view that ceramides may play a direct role in insulin resistance development, a conclusion supported by the findings of our study, where reduced ceramide levels mediated improvements in insulin sensitivity in the CR group, but not in the AL group (Fig. 6).

Ceramides with a C16 or C18 acyl chain have been strongly implicated in metabolic dysfunction42,43. Our results corroborate this, showing that CR-induced reduction of C16:0 or C18:0 ceramides statistically mediated improvements in AUC insulin and C-peptide. Decreases in other species such as 20:1, 22:1, and 24:0, were also associated with enhanced glycemic markers. While some studies suggest potential benefits of very-long-chain (C24) ceramides, the overall evidence, including our data, supports a detrimental role of multiple ceramide species in glycemic control44,45. Specifically, reductions in ceramide (18:0), abundant in skeletal muscle43,46,47, were associated with lower postprandial insulin levels, likely reflecting enhanced muscle glucose uptake. This ceramide did not mediate changes in fasting insulin, consistent with its limited role in hepatic insulin sensitivity, which remained unchanged during the study. Previous animal studies have already shown that CR can reduce ceramide levels and improves metabolic health. For example, CR suppresses hepatic ceramide synthesis in male mice more than in females, suggesting sex-specific effects48. However, adjusting for sex in our study did not change results importantly. Similarly, CR attenuates ceramide accumulation in models of cardiac myopathy49. However, a 16-week CR trial in individuals with obesity found improved insulin sensitivity without significant changes in skeletal muscle ceramide or diacylglycerols50, potentially due to the shorter intervention duration or lower CR intensity.

Mechanistically, we observed that CR-induced increases in HMW adiponectin preceded ceramide reductions and improvements in glycemic control. This is biologically plausible: HMW adiponectin preferentially binds AdipoR1, which possesses ceramidase activity and promotes intracellular ceramide hydrolysis51–53. Although adiponectin receptors share limited homology with canonical ceramidases23–26, their functional ability to activate ceramide breakdown supports a mechanistic link between adiponectin and ceramide turnover. Mediation effects were strongest at 12 months, when weight loss was greatest, and diminished by 24 months, possibly reflecting metabolic adaptation or a plateau in adiponectin response. Importantly, these effects were observed in a healthy, non-obese cohort, indicating that CR may exert metabolic benefits beyond traditionally high-risk populations. To examine indirect pathways, we used causal mediation analysis, which assumes correct specification of the underlying causal model, assumptions that cannot be empirically verified54. Thus, support from prior studies, biological plausibility, and the randomized design of our trial are critical for strengthening model robustness and mitigating the risk of type I error54. Consistent with our findings, previous work has shown that adiponectin receptors can activate ceramidases23–26, and that lowering ceramide levels improves insulin sensitivity31–34,40,41,45. Taken together, the randomized controlled design and the absence of a mediated effect in the AL group reinforce the specificity of the proposed pathway: CR increases adiponectin, which in turn reduces ceramides, likely via ceramidase activation, thereby contributing to improved glycemic control (Fig. 6).

Our findings also implicate ceramides in the regulation of IGF-1 signaling. Prior research has shown that ceramides can inhibit IGF-1-induced Akt phosphorylation22, by preventing Akt translocation to the plasma membrane and impairing GLUT4 mobilization via disruption of insulin receptor substrate (IRS)-1 and -2 pathways55,56. This inhibition likely reduces cellular sensitivity to both insulin and IGF-1. In our study, lower ceramide concentrations were associated with higher plasma IGF-1 levels, consistent with enhanced IGF-1 signaling capacity following CR. However, this effect was not observed at the 24-month follow-up, suggesting it may be transient or more pronounced during the earlier phase of intervention. Beyond CR, several factors, including dietary fat composition, fiber-rich and low-glycaemic diets, physical activity, and regular sleep patterns, have been shown to favorably modulate the adiponectin–ceramide axis57–60. These factors may partly contribute to the interindividual variability in metabolic responses to CR.

Our study has several notable strengths, including the randomized controlled design, long duration, and the use of high-resolution lipidomics to capture CR-specific metabolic changes. The identification of distinct lipid profiles associated with improvements in glycemic control strengthens the translational relevance of these findings. Furthermore, the inclusion of non-obese, metabolically healthy individuals underscores that metabolic improvements from CR are not restricted to those with overt metabolic dysfunction and supports the safe implementation of CR in a broader population. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. All measurements were performed in plasma, precluding tissue-specific interpretation. While we employed validated surrogate markers such as adiponectin, C-peptide, and oral glucose tolerance test results61–63, direct analyses of muscle or adipose tissue would strengthen mechanistic inferences. In addition, module preservation analysis detects differences in correlation structure rather than absolute metabolite abundance; thus, CR-induced shifts in metabolite concentrations that occur within stable correlation structures may not be captured by this approach. Accordingly, our use of module preservation was intended as a dimensionality-reduction and hypothesis-generation step rather than a comprehensive characterization of all CR-induced metabolomic changes.

While we focused on biologically hypothesized mediators identified through network-preservation analysis, future studies could incorporate multidimensional mediation approaches to more comprehensively identify key mediating metabolites. Because ceramide species are biologically and statistically correlated, the estimated indirect effects for individual ceramides should be interpreted as overlapping rather than mutually independent. We cannot exclude the possibility of false-positive findings arising from multiple testing, and therefore emphasize effect sizes, consistency across time points, and convergence with prior biological evidence rather than statistical significance alone. We also acknowledge that imputing missing values for metabolites can introduce bias. Additionally, generalizability to individuals with obesity or type 2 diabetes remains to be tested in future trials. Although we cannot entirely exclude the possibility of false-positive findings arising from multiple testing, our analytical strategy reduces this risk. Specifically, the metabolites were not selected in an untargeted, discovery-wide manner but were pre-specified from biologically preserved modules, thereby substantially limiting the number of statistical tests compared with a genome- or metabolome-wide scan.

In summary, this study provides human evidence consistent with CR being associated with beneficial lipid remodeling, particularly reductions in ceramides that are linked to improvements in insulin secretion, insulin sensitivity, and IGF-1 signaling. The observed associations are compatible with partial mediation by adiponectin, but the strength of evidence is modest and dependent on the assumptions of the mediation framework. Accordingly, our results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, pointing to the adiponectin–ceramide axis as a potential pathway for future experimental validation rather than establishing a confirmed mechanistic link. Future work should test these relationships in clinical populations and explore whether pharmacological or dietary strategies that mimic the lipid-modulatory effects of CR can deliver comparable benefits.

Methods

Study design

The CALERIE study was a multicenter, phase 2 randomized controlled trial conducted at three U.S. clinical sites: Washington University School of Medicine (St. Louis, MO), Pennington Biomedical Research Center (Baton Rouge, LA), and Tufts University (Boston, MA), with coordination by Duke University (Durham, NC). The trial evaluated the effects of sustained 25% CR, without malnutrition, over a 2-year period in healthy, non-obese (BMI 22–27.9 kg/m2) men and premenopausal women aged 21 to 50 years. The study protocol (NCT00427193) received institutional review board approval at all participating centers, and all study volunteers provided written informed consent. A CONSORT diagram detailing participant flow has been previously published15. Our study examined exploratory outcomes prespecified in the study protocol, including serum insulin, glucose, adiponectin, and lipids. The primary outcomes resting metabolic rate and core temperature64,65 as well as the secondary outcomes serum triiodothyronine, tumor necrosis factor-α, and adverse effects65 have been reported elsewhere.

A detailed account of the study design, interventions, and randomization procedures has been published previously15,65. In brief, 220 men and women were randomized in a 1:2 ratio to either an ‘ad libitum’ (AL) control group or a CR group targeting a 25% reduction in caloric intake, individualized based on total daily energy expenditure (TDEE) measured using doubly labeled water. Biological samples (blood and urine), anthropometric and body composition data were collected at baseline, 12 and 24 months. Cardiometabolic markers, glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, inflammatory parameters, and hormone profile were assessed as previously described15. Metabolomic and oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) data were available for 191 participants. As we used a subset of the original cohort, changes over time for some clinical outcomes may differ than previously published15,65. Deidentified participant-level data are available from the Aging Research Biobank (https://agingresearchbiobank.nia.nih.gov/studies/calerie/details).

Metabolites

Plasma EDTA samples were processed by Metabolon (Morrisville, United States). Sample preparation was performed using the automated MicroLab STAR® system (Hamilton Company, Reno, United States). Internal recovery standards were added prior to extraction for quality control. Proteins were microprecipitated with methanol under vigorous shaking (2 min, Glen Mills GenoGrinder 2000), followed by centrifugation. The resulting extract was split into five fractions for UPLC-MS/MS analysis: two for reverse phase (RP) with positive ESI, one for RP with negative ESI, one for HILIC with negative ESI, and one reserved for backup. Solvent was removed using a TurboVap® (Zymark), and extracts were stored under nitrogen overnight prior to analysis.

Samples were analyzed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system coupled to a Thermo Q-Exactive mass spectrometer with heated electrospray ionization (HESI-II) and Orbitrap detection at 35,000 resolution. Extracts were dried, reconstituted in method-specific solvents, and analyzed under four chromatographic conditions: two reverse phase (RP) with positive ion mode (targeting hydrophilic and hydrophobic compounds), one RP with negative ion mode, and one HILIC with negative ion mode. The mobile phases included combinations of water, methanol, acetonitrile, formic acid, perfluoropentanoic acid, and ammonium-based buffers. MS analysis alternated between MS and data-dependent MSn scans across a 70–1000 m/z range.

Lipids were extracted using methanol:dichloromethane with internal standards, dried under nitrogen, and reconstituted in ammonium acetate buffer. Infusion-MS was performed on a Sciex SelexIon-5500 QTRAP in both positive and negative mode using MRM scanning. Lipid species were quantified based on peak area ratios relative to internal standards, and lipid class concentrations and fatty acid compositions were calculated accordingly. Before analysis, each metabolite was rescaled so that its median equalled 1, and missing values were imputed with the lowest observed value for that metabolite.

Statistical analysis

Ordination, Individual comparison of clinical parameters and metabolites

All statistical analyses were performed in R Studio (V4.3). We assessed global differences in metabolite profiles using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities with scaled and log-transformed lipid metabolite data. The analysis included only participants with complete data for the complex lipids metabolite panel. Statistical significance of group, time, and interaction effects was evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), with significance assessed via 999 permutations.

To assess differential longitudinal trajectories between intervention groups, linear mixed-effects models were applied to each metabolite and clinical variable. This approach uses all available repeated measurements and can accommodate unequal numbers of observations per participant, under the assumption that data are missing at random and are estimated via maximum likelihood66,67. Models included fixed effects for timepoint, group, and their interaction, with a random intercept for each participant to account for within-subject correlation. Analyses were restricted to metabolites with sufficient non-missing data and variability. Fixed effect estimates and associated statistics were extracted to evaluate group-specific changes over time. No technical replicates were used, all measurements are derived from distinct samples.

Weighted gene network analysis and module preservation

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the ‘WGCNA’ package (V 1.73) in R, as previously described68, referred to as weighted metabolite co-expression network analysis throughout the manuscript to avoid ambiguity regarding the source data. This approach is particularly suited to exploring coordinated lipidomic responses to CR. While WGCNA assumes linear correlations and may miss nonlinear dependencies, it enables data-driven detection of functionally related modules69. Delta values, (representing change scores from baseline to 12 and 24 months) were calculated for each metabolite. A signed network was constructed for the CR group using a soft-thresholding power selected by scale-free topology criteria. Adjacency matrices were computed and transformed into topological overlap matrices, followed by hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cutting (with a minimum cluster size of 20) to define metabolite modules. Module eigengenes were calculated and correlated with clinical trait changes using Pearson correlation, and significance was visualized using annotated heatmaps. Module preservation was assessed using permutation-based statistics (1,000 permutations) with CR as the reference network and AL as the test. Modules with significant trait associations (p < 0.05) and low preservation (Zsummary <2, specificity for CR) were prioritized for analysis18.

Mediation analysis

Metabolites from low-preserved (CR-specific) modules were tested for mediation of relevant clinical outcomes. Causal mediation analysis assessed whether metabolite changes explained the effect of CR on clinical variables over time. Linear models were fit for each clinical-metabolite pair, with treatment group and time as predictors. Mediation models were run using the mediate function from the ‘mediation’, package (V 4.5.1) in ref. 70, with treatment as the exposure (CR vs. AL), the metabolite as the mediator, and the clinical variable as the outcome71. Estimates were generated using 1000 nonparametric bootstrap simulations. We used bootstrapped confidence intervals rather than p–based multiple-comparison corrections, as interval estimates are more robust and informative for assessing effect size and avoiding spurious type I errors in this high-dimensional setting72,73. For each model, the average causal mediation effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), total effect, and proportion mediated were reported with 95% confidence intervals and p-values. If clinical parameters had too many missing data ( > 30%), metabolites had insufficient variation (variance = 0) which can result in converging models, variables where excluded. For missing clinical parameters see Table S17. Directionality of mediation effects was interpreted using linear mixed-effects models (LME) with fixed effects for time, treatment group (CR vs. AL), and their interaction (time × group). For each metabolite, the estimated coefficient of the time × group interaction term was used as an empirical approximation of the a path (i.e., the effect of time on the mediator), stratified by group. This estimate guided the directional interpretation of mediation models54.

In mediation analysis, ACME quantified the indirect effect of time on the outcome via the mediator, while ADE represented the remaining direct effect of time not explained by the mediator. The total effect (c path) was defined as the sum of the ACME and ADE. The sign of the ACME was interpreted in conjunction with the inferred a coefficient from the LME model. Specifically, if a was negative (e.g., indicating that the intervention reduced the mediator over time) and ACME was also negative, this implied that lower mediator levels were associated with lower outcome values — suggesting a positive b path (mediator → outcome). The implied b coefficient was computed as: b = ACME/a. A positive inferred b indicated that higher mediator levels were associated with higher outcome values, while a negative b implied the opposite.

Multiple mediation analyses were conducted using structural equation modeling (SEM) within a latent variable framework. A latent metabolite factor was specified to represent the combined variation of selected metabolite groups, modeled separately for long-chain ceramides and very-long-chain ceramides.

For each model, the following pathways were estimated: (i) the effect of time on the latent metabolite factor (path a); (ii) the effect of the latent metabolite factor on insulin area under the curve (AUC) (path b); (iii) the direct effect of time on insulin AUC (c′); (iv) the indirect effect of time on insulin AUC mediated by the metabolite factor (a × b); and (v) the total effect (c′ + a × b).

All models were adjusted for age, sex, and energy expenditure measured by doubly labeled water. Mediation analysis was performed separately within the caloric restriction (CR) and ad libitum (AL) groups. Statistical inference was based on two-sided tests with a significance level of α = 0.05, and corresponding confidence intervals and p-values were reported.
