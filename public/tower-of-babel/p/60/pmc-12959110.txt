Educational Development for New GME Programs at Community Hospitals: Process, Outcomes and Recommendations

Introduction

Traditionally, academic medical centers (AMCs) – teaching hospitals associated with medical schools, and community medical centers (CMCs) – non-academic providers such as community hospitals, have played a crucial, yet distinct role in America’s health care system. AMCs are essential to the U.S. health care system, distinguished by their tripartite social mission: to educate the health care workforce, conduct innovative biomedical and clinical research, and deliver high-quality patient care.1 The graduate medical education (GME) enterprise at AMCs is key to providing the nation with its supply of high-quality physicians. Community hospitals account for nearly 80 % of all hospital admissions each year and play a crucial role as the main healthcare providers in most communities, focusing on patient care, rather than education and federally funded research.2

Health care reform initiatives, economic pressures, constraints of space, and increasing patient volume are incentivizing optimization of care coordination and driving health systems integration.3,4 This has resulted in a nationwide trend of consolidation, with AMCs using their resources to merge with, acquire, or partner with community hospitals. One noteworthy result of these consolidations is the expansion of GME into community hospitals.5 Many new residency training programs are now based in community hospitals, which have become more prevalent in the past decade.3,4 This has led to a unique situation in medicine where physicians who initially chose to focus solely on patient care are now being asked to participate in medical education. Supervising trainees can be quite different from their original path of direct patient care, requiring them to develop new skills in education and teaching.6

The purpose of this article is to describe how we, in our role as curriculum and educational specialists at an independent AMC, collaborated with GME program directors to conduct a pilot project involving structured support and systematic faculty development programming to assist in integrating direct-care community hospital physicians into trainee education.

Methods

Houston Methodist (HM) is comprised of a nine-hospital system with a flagship academic medical center, Houston Methodist Hospital (HMH), seven community-based hospitals and one long-term acute care hospital. Houston Methodist also has responsibility for education of over 400 residents and fellows in over 70 clinical programs. Over the past 3 years, HM has expanded its training programs beyond HMH to community-based hospitals, such as Houston Methodist – The Woodlands (HMTW) and Houston Methodist – Willowbrook (HMWB) – these two programs are our focus for this manuscript (Fig. 1).

Within HM, there are resources to support the educational mission of the academic medical center such as the Office of Curriculum and Educational Development (OCED) through which teaching faculty, training program administrators and trainees are equipped with evidence-based teaching skills and support for systematic curriculum development, implementation and evaluation.

OCED sought to support the program directors and faculty members of our newest GME programs at HMTW (Internal Medicine) and HMTW (OBGYN) by curating a faculty development series prior to the start of the programs. We met with key stakeholders in each program, reviewed seminal topics that would be most helpful to clinicians who are new to the role of an educator, and prioritized and organized workshops to prepare them to be effective clinician educators (Table 1). Workshops were facilitated at the respective hospital locations and recorded for future reference. We administered a survey after each session and interviewed each program director to evaluate the impact of the workshops.7

Results

The faculty development series consisted of 1-h workshops with a satisfaction survey given at the end of each session. Upon completion, the program directors were interviewed regarding the planning, implementation, and evaluation of the series. The faculty development series at HMWB consisted of five workshops and reached 48 physicians who reported a Net Promotor Score of 100 (Table 1). The faculty development series at HMTW consisted of nine workshops and reached 116 physicians who reported a Net Promotor Score ranging from 67 to 100 (Table 1). Net Promotor Score (NPS) is a way to see how happy customers are with the service provided. We asked the faculty to rate the likelihood of recommending our course on a scale of 1–10. Based on their response, the faculty were categorized into three groups: Promoters (9–10), Passives (7–8), and Detractors (0–6).8 NPS is calculated by subtracting the percentage of detractors from the percentage of promotors. NPS is considered an ideal metric because it is a simple and standardized way to calculate satisfaction.8

We held a 30-minute interview with each program director. Programs directors were asked about their experience and satisfaction in the areas of planning: rationale and how buy-in was ascertained, implementation: motivators and barriers to participation, and evaluation: impact on faculty/anecdotal account. In the area of planning, program directors expressed their desire to prepare faculty to confidently work with residents as both locations were launching new programs and many faculty members were new to teaching.

To promote buy-in from faculty, a collaborative approach was taken as faculty were asked what topics were most important to them. Additionally, both program directors noted the importance that presenters were willing to customize content to make it more meaningful for faculty. In the area of implementation, faculty have expressed their desire to build confidence and skills as a motivation factor in participating in these sessions with the most popular sessions including engaging components such as discussions and clinical examples. The most common barrier to participation in sessions was time. In the area of evaluation, both program directors received positive anecdotal feedback from their faculty and noted increased confidence in teaching due to the sessions. Overall, feedback about the sessions was positive. Program directors noted areas of improvement such as increased awareness and promotion of session offerings and the added value of clinician co-presenters to certain topics.

Discussion

By engaging new GME program directors at community hospitals for Internal Medicine (HMTW) and OBGYN (HMWB) in creating educational development programs for their faculty, OCED was able to provide the support necessary to build teaching capacity for direct-care physicians. The approach we took in developing these programs mirrors the recommendations Sweigart et al. suggest for overcoming barriers and gaining the benefits of utilizing these training sites.6 First, the larger Houston Methodist system recognized that its community hospitals needed educational support and prioritized staff resources within OCED for meeting this need. Second, we lowered the barriers to teaching by working with the local program directors to create teaching development series that took their unique context into account, rather than deploying a one-size-fits-all approach. This included holding the sessions in person, on-site at the community hospitals and limiting attendance to the program faculty to encourage group discussion and cohesion. Although we do not have any ACGME survey data regarding program citations or overall survey results yet because these two programs are new, our intention with offering our workshops is to prevent problems related to education from arising in the first place. The HMTW OBGYN program filled all its spots in its first year (2025–26) which is a testament to a strong start.

While these approaches were successful overall, we still have challenges related to faculty time and engagement. We plan to address this by curating a list of free, online on-demand teaching resources that all faculty can access to provide more just-in-time learning opportunities and fill in knowledge gaps as needed. The Just in Time Teaching (JiTT) Infographics App is an example of this modality, with a wide variety of clinical teaching topics all housed in a single app that are also eligible for continuing medical education credits.9 Engaging physician co-presenters as suggested by the program directors may also increase buy-in and perceived relevance. Our next steps for program improvement are to interview faculty participants from HMTW now that they have had residents for a year to get their perspective on program barriers and successes and repeat the process for HMWB once they reach the same milestone. Development needs for these programs will change as they become more established, requiring continued relationships between OCED and program leadership to address these needs as they arise. We will also monitor ACGME survey data and citations in the future to see if our preventive approach for education-related problems has been successful.

As we continue to add more GME programs at our community hospitals, the blueprint we developed with these two programs will provide guidance for those that follow and allow others to more quickly implement educational training for direct-care physicians. Our work here also provides a path for other independent academic medical centers with similar educational development offices who are seeking ways to support their community hospitals with new GME programs.
