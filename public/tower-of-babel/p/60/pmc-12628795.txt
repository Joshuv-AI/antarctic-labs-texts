Intra-plaque vascularization assessed in contrast-enhanced ultrasound predicts cardiovascular events in type 2 diabetes with no significant carotid atherosclerotic stenosis: a prospective study with therapeutic implications for SGLT2 inhibitor use
Authors: Raffaele Marfella, Ludovica Vittoria Marfella, Carlo Fumagalli, Luca Rinaldi, Ferdinando Carlo Sasso, Domenico Cozzolino, Francesco Nappo, Ausilia Sellitto, Ciro Romano, Caterina Carusone, Giuseppe Diodato, Pasquale Russo, Lorenza Marfella, Nicola Maria Tarantino, Gerardo Carpinella, Fulvio Furbatto, Sandro Gentile, Giuseppina Guarino, Ersilia Satta, Alessandro Bellis, Luca Marinelli, Isabella Donisi, Nunzia D’Onofrio, Ciro Mauro, Salvatore Cappabianca, Maria Luisa Balestrieri, Celestino Sardu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsPatients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and no significant carotid stenosis are often considered at moderate cardiovascular risk. However, some may harbour biologically active plaques. Intra-plaque vascularization (IPV), detectable in contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), reflects plaque vulnerability and may enhance risk stratification. We assessed the prognostic value of CEUS-derived IPV and the effects of SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) on cardiovascular outcomes and inflammatory markers.Methods and resultsIn this 6-year prospective cohort study, 251 asymptomatic T2DM patients with carotid atherosclerosis <50% stenosis were enrolled. IPV was quantified by CEUS and stratified by tertiles. The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE: CV death, non-fatal MI, non-fatal stroke, or heart failure hospitalization). Secondary endpoints included changes in VEGF, IL-6, and TNF-α levels. Patients were also stratified by chronic SGLT2i use. High IPV was associated with greater MACE incidence (32.5%) compared with low IPV (7.4%; HR 3.84, 95% CI 1.89–7.78; P < 0.001). SGLT2i-treated patients showed reduced MACE incidence (12.1% vs. 26.3%, P = 0.004), particularly in the high-IPV subgroup (23.1% vs. 38.9%; HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.25–0.91; P = 0.026). Treatment was also linked to significant VEGF (−52.4 vs. −18.6 pg/mL) and IL-6 (−1.9 vs. −0.6 pg/mL) reductions (P < 0.001 for both).ConclusionCEUS-detected IPV predicts cardiovascular events in T2DM patients without significant stenosis. SGLT2i may reduce risk by modulating plaque inflammation and angiogenesis. CEUS combined with biomarker profiling may support personalized prevention strategies in diabetes.Keywords: intra-plaque vascularization, contrast-enhanced ultrasound, type 2 diabetes, SGLT2 inhibitors, cardiovascular outcomes, VEGF, inflammation, carotid plaque

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a widely recognized risk factor for centres for disease control and prevention/atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and remains a significant contributor to global cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. An accumulating volume of evidence has demonstrated that T2DM does not only accelerate atherogenesis but also triggers plaque instability via metabolic and inflammatory adjustments, with an impact on major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including myocardial infarction (MI) and stroke.1,2 It is remarkable that even in the absence of apparent CVD, patients with T2DM and SC-CA—so in particular those with non-haemodynamically significant plaques—constitute a high- risk group with an under-recognized risk of CVD.3,4

Plaque vulnerability in these situations is being more often linked to IPV, hallmark of advanced and unstable atherosclerotic plaques. IPV leads to formation of brittle and leaky microvessels that foster intra-plaque haemorrhage, inflammation, and plaque rupture.5,6 The introduction of contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is now capable of identifying and grading IPV non-invasively, even in asymptomatic presence; thus, the identification of high risk plaques in asymptomatic patients is feasible.7 Prior studies have shown that the extent of CEUS-based intra-plaque vascularization (IPV) is strongly associated with cerebrovascular events, independently from traditional risk factors and degree of luminal stenosis.8,9

VEGF, which regulates both atherosclerosis and IPV and is one of the molecular drivers of IPV,46,52 significantly contributes to angiogenesis within the plaque microenvironment. VEGF is highly expressed in the vulnerable plaque and its expression is increased in response to hyperglycaemia, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation, all of which are characteristics of diabetes.10,11 Concomitantly, pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) also promote angiogenesis as well as extracellular matrices degradation to further increase the plaque vulnerability.12,13 As a result, the interventions in these pathways may be a potential therapeutic target for plaque stabilization.

Sodium–glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), anti-diabetic drugs known to exhibit cardiovascular protective benefits, may be modulators of atherosclerotic plaque biology.14 In addition to the glucose-lowering effect, SGLT2i are thought to have anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic effects, potentially by down-regulation of VEGF and pro-inflammatory cytokines.15,16 Studies indicate that SGLT2i could decrease neovascularization and inflammation burden in atherosclerotic plaques, potentially preventing plaque rupture and subsequent MACE.17,18

The purpose of the present study is to explore the prognostic value of CEUS-detected IPV for major cardiovascular events in T2DM patients free from previous cardiovascular events, without significant carotid atherosclerosis. The secondary objective is to investigate the potential impact of chronic SGLT2i treatment on IPV and intended molecular mechanisms, with particular attention given to VEGF and inflammatory cytokines. We speculate that aggressive IPV is an independent predictor of MACE in this population, and SGLT2 inhibitors mediate protective effects through stabilizing the plaque via modulation of the angiogenic and inflammatory pathways.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a prospective observational cohort study of 6 years duration at a tertiary cardiovascular-diabetes care centre. Two hundred and fifty adult patients with T2DM, aged 40–80 years, were recruited between January 2017 and December 2018, and were followed until December 2024. All of the participants were diagnosed with T2DM for at least 5 years and had no history of cardiovascular events including MI, stroke, coronary revascularization or peripheral arterial disease. Moderate atherosclerosis of carotid arteries was considered as the presence of any one or more atherosclerotic plaque with <50% stenosis according to the NASCET criteria19 as diagnosed with duplex carotid ultrasound.

Patients were required to be on stable glucose-lowering treatment for at least 6 months before randomization. The threshold was derived from earlier studies, which indicated a marked change in VEGF and pro-inflammatory cytokines after 12–24 weeks treatment.17 Patients with known malignancy, end-stage renal disease (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²), decompensated liver disease, active infection, autoimmune condition, or on anti-VEGF therapies were excluded. Patients were classified into two groups based on sodium–glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) treatment, with the SGLT2i group treated with empagliflozin, dapagliflozin or canagliflozin and the control group treated with other standard oral anti-diabetic agents and unexposed to SGLT2i. The treatment assignment was not randomized and it was influenced by the prescriber towards a therapy based on national guidelines. Patients were enrolled in the SGLT2i group only when continuously on gliflozins for 6 months or more at baseline of the study. The two cohorts were also well-matched at baseline for several confounding variables such as age, sex, diabetes duration, HbA1c, lipid profile, and carotid plaque features.

Evaluating of plaque vascularization

All participants underwent CEUS evaluation of IPV exhibit at baseline and during follow-up. All studies were conducted with a high-resolution ultrasound machine with contrast-specific imaging software (e.g. Philips EPIQ 7 or GE LOGIQ E9) and high-resolution linear-array transducer run in low mechanical index mode (mechanical index < 0.10). The most significant carotid plaque features such as echolucency, proximity to the bifurcation, or thickness were used. Following plaque localization using B-mode imaging, 2.4 mL of a sulphur hexafluoride-based microbubble contrast agent (sonovue®, Bracco Imaging, Milan, Italy) was injected into the antecubital vein, and flushed with 10 mL saline. CEUS recording was started immediately after starting injection and continued for at least 90 s, while the transducer was held still in order to minimize movements. Offline quantitative analysis was achieved with digitally stored cine loops. Measurement of IPV was conducted with contrast perfusion analysis software (QLAB®, Philips or VueBox®, Bracco). A region of interest (ROI) was drawn manually covering the entire plaque area, with the exclusion of the intra-vascular lumen and perivascular parenchyma. Software was used to produce time–intensity curves (TICs) showing contrast enhancement in the ROI over time. From these curves, the following calculated parameters were automatically stored: peak intensity (PI), defined as the maximum reached signal intensity during the contrast bolus; and area under the curve (AUC), which was considered to be the total contrast load in the plaque over time. These values were normalized (in a.u.) and indicate the level of microvascular perfusion in the plaque tissue.

TIC analyses were all conducted from three consecutive cardiac cycles, which were averaged to minimize motion artefacts and inter-beat variation. The values of IPV were also gathered and compared throughout the study population. According to PI distribution, patients were divided into three groups: low vascularization (PI < 6.5 a.u.), moderate vascularization (PI 6.5–8.0 a.u.), and high vascularization (PI > 8.0 a.u.). This categorization provided a means for comparing differing degrees of neovascularization and how they related to inflammatory biomarkers and cardiovascular end points. The CEUS values of the plaque were interpreted based on pathophysiology, and increased evasion of contrast enhanced neovessels was considered related to dense vasa vasorum network in the plaque. These neovessels are frail, leaky, and pro-inflammatory and thereby promote plaque destabilization and risk of rupture. Hence, increased PI and AUC was considered an expression of more active, vulnerable plaque.

This semi-quantitative and qualitative CEUS method was used in previous research and confirmed as an appropriate surrogate marker for plaque vulnerability.4,5,8 The analyses were done by two blinded and independent vascular sonographers to maximize reproducibility. Intra-group and inter-observer reproducibility was calculated in a random sample of 30 cases with a variability of <10% for PI measurements, which demonstrates the reliability of the method.

Laboratory and biochemical assessment

Venous blood collection was performed after overnight fasting at the baseline and on each follow-up visit. Angiogenic and inflammatory biomarkers including VEGF, IL-6, IL-18, and TNF-α were analysed in the serum. All assays were conducted in duplicate in duplicate using high-sensitivity enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (e.g. R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA; Lot numbers [available upon request]) as per the manufacturer’s instructions.20

Glycaemic control was assessed using glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), and measured with high-performance liquid chromatography on a Tosoh G8 Analyzer (Tosoh Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan). Lipid panel (total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglyceride tests) were measured enzymatically using an automated chemistry analyser (Roche Cobas 8000, Basel, Switzerland). Renal function as estimated by eGFR, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (CRP), and uric acid were also collected at every visit, in order to adjust for comorbid effects on plaque biology and biomarker kinetics.

All laboratory measurements were centralized at the core facility and accordingly, were under internal quality control and external accreditation. Blinding of the investigators during the analysis was maintained to avoid bias.

Follow-up and adjudication of outcomes

The prospective follow-up of patients was for 6-years, and ended in December 2024. Routine assessments took place every 6 months, consisting of clinical visits followed by structured telemedicine encounters in alternate cycles. History of symptoms, cardiovascular risk profile, adherence to medications, and occurrence of new clinical events were extensively reviewed at each follow-up. Major adverse cardiac events (MACE) were prospectively collected and consisted of cardiovascular death, non-fatal MI, non-fatal ischaemic stroke, and HF hospitalization. Events were adjudicated by an independent committee of cardiologists and neurologists who were blinded to treatment assignment and CEUS findings. Review of clinical data, imaging, and laboratory parameters was done in consensus meetings to validate event classification based on the pre-defined criteria modified from international guidelines.21 Retention was excellent, with over 90% of the original cohort having at least 6 years of follow-up. Patients who became lost to follow-up were censored at the time of their last recorded visit. Data were managed and the statistics were supervised by the institutions central clinical research unit guaranteeing complete data, quality control and ethical compliance.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the occurrence of MACE, which was a composite of cardiovascular death, non-fatal MI, non-fatal stroke, and hospitalization due to heart failure. All events were adjudicated by a clinical events committee which was both independent and blinded to provide for objective and standardized adjudication of outcomes. Secondary endpoints were the analysis of the changes in IPV evaluated by CEUS, and in the circulating markers of inflammation (IL-6, TNF-α) and angiogenesis (VEGF). These parameters were analysed globally and according to tertiles of vascularization to search for mechanistic associations between treatment, plaque biology, and cardiovascular risk.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics, including demographic, clinical, and biochemical variables, were compared between the SGLT2 inhibitor group and the control group using independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Similar comparisons were conducted across tertiles of IPV, as assessed by CEUS, to evaluate differences in biomarker levels and clinical outcomes. The association between vascularization grade and the incidence of MACE was examined using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, with log-rank tests used to assess differences in MACE-free survival across tertiles of vascularization intensity.22

Time-to-event analyses were performed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. Hazard ratios (HRs) for MACE were estimated for each vascularization tertile, with the lowest tertile serving as the reference category. Models were adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, HbA1c, LDL cholesterol, aspirin, sartan, and beta-blockers. Proportional hazard assumptions were tested using Schoenfeld residuals. A secondary analysis was performed to explore the impact of SGLT2 inhibitor therapy on MACE outcomes within each vascularization tertile. Interaction terms between vascularization tertile and treatment group were tested to evaluate potential effect modification. Sensitivity analyses were conducted excluding patients who initiated or discontinued SGLT2i therapy during follow-up. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess the incremental prognostic value of IPV over conventional risk factors, we computed the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). These metrics were calculated by comparing a base model including clinical and biochemical covariates (age, sex, hypertension, HbA1c, LDL-C, statin use) to a second model incorporating IPV tertiles. The statistical significance of the improvements was evaluated using bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals. Data were analysed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R statistical software version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Standardized mean differences (SMD) were calculated; values < 0.1 were considered well balanced. To assess baseline comparability between treatment groups, SMD were calculated for anthropometric and biochemical variables including age, BMI, diabetes duration, HbA1c, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, and triglycerides. These comparisons, reported in Supplementary data online, Table S1, allowed for an appraisal of group balance without the need for formal matching.

Results

In all, 251 patients with T2DM and non-significant carotid atherosclerosis were enrolled. We divided the patients in terciles of IPV according CEUS in the last cena-eCRF time point. Detail of the initial demographic, clinical, metabolic and biochemical characteristics were presented in Table 1. Among the three IPV tertiles, no significant differences were found with respect to age (mean 69.6 ± 2.9 years), BMI (27.2 ± 1.5 kg/m²), HbA1c (7.2 ± 0.16%), the duration of diabetes (11.5 ± 6.4 years), lipid profile, or renal function. Averages of LDL cholesterol were 94.2 ± 14.1 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol 50.1 ± 8.9 mg/dL, triglycerides 147.5 ± 24.6 mg/dL, creatinine 0.89 ± 0.13 mg/dL, and eGFR 78.4 ± 10.2 mL/min/1.73m². There were no differences in the utilization of anti-diabetic or cardiovascular drugs between tertiles. Nonetheless, participants in the greatest IPV tertile characterized significant high levels of inflammatory and angiogenic biomarkers at baseline. As demonstrated in Figure 1A, VEGF levels were higher in tertile 3 (227 ± 43 pg/mL) than in tertile 1 (162 ± 38 pg/mL, P < 0.001) and tertile 2 (191 ± 39 pg/mL, P < 0.001). The levels of IL-6 were 4.8 ± 1.3 pg/mL in tertile 3, whereas they were 2.9 ± 0.9 pg/mL in tertile 1 and 3.7 ± 1.1 pg/mL in tertile 2 (all P < 0.001). The same changes were found for TNF-α (5.6 ± 1.4 vs. 3.7 ± 1.0 vs. 4.5 ± 1.1 pg/mL, P < 0.001 with a significant difference between all the tertiles. Patients were stratified by IPV and were classified according to treatment with SGLT2 inhibitors. The final number of patients treated with SGLT2i was 132 and the number of patients not treated was 119. Remarkably, the distribution of treated patients and untreated controls differed across IPV tertiles, with a stepwise increased frequency of SGLT2i-treated patients in lower tertiles and of control in the highest tertile, suggesting an eventual correlation between treatment exposure and initial plaque biology (Table 1 and Figure 2). Characteristics of patients at baseline by treatment group are presented in Table 2. Both groups were comparable for age (69.44 ± 2.90 vs. 69.57 ± 2.86 years, P = 0.73), BMI (27.22 ± 1.45 vs. 27.28 ± 1.43 kg/m², P = 0.73), HbA1c (7.20 ± 0.16%), diabetes duration (12.1 ± 6.6 vs. 11.2 ± 6.2 years, P = 0.27), lipid profile, and renal function. The only point of statistical difference was more frequent use of beta blockade in the control group (58% vs. 44%, P = 0.03).

Indeed, the control group at baseline showed a statistically significant higher level of each inflammatory marker than SGLT2i-treated patients (Figure 1B). The mean VEGF was 212 ± 38 pg/mL in controls and 178 ± 42 pg/mL in SGLT2i treated patients (P < 0.001), the mean IL-6 was 4.7 ± 1.2 and 3.5 ± 1.1 pg/mL (P < 0.001), and the mean TNF-α was 5.5 ± 1.2 and 4.1 ± 1.0 pg/mL (P < 0.001). Controls also had higher IL-18 and CRP (165 ± 29 vs. 142 ± 31 pg/mL, and 4.2 ± 1.3 vs. 3.1 ± 1.1 mg/L, respectively; P < 0.001 for both).

Over 72 participants’ months post-baseline there were no significant changes in BMI, HbA1c, LDL, HDL, triglycerides, total cholesterol, or renal function in either treatment group or across IPV tertiles longitudinally. These trends are evident in Figure 3, which shows temporal stability of metabolic measurements. Time by treatment effects were not detected for these variables.

Temporal trends of metabolic parameters during the 72-month follow-up in SGLT2i-treated patients' vs. controls. Mean values (±SD) of HbA1c, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides are shown over time for the two treatment groups. No significant longitudinal changes were observed in either group, and no time-by-treatment interaction was detected.

Conversely, inflammatory marker levels at follow-up were significantly decreased in SGLT2i (as opposed to controls). VEGF was reduced of −52.4 ± 19.1 pg/mL among SGLT2i as compared with −18.6 ± 14.7 pg/mL in controls (P < 0.001). IL-6 fell −1.9 ± 0.7 vs. −0.6 ± 0.5 pg/mL (P < 0.001), and TNF-α fell −1.2 ± 0.6 vs. −0.4 ± 0.3 pg/mL (P < 0.001) (see Supplementary data online, Figure S1). Patients in the highest tertile of IPV had the greatest reduction, which might indicate an effect of SGLT2 inhibition modulation of inflammatory plaque biology. There were 47 MACEs through the follow-up period including 11 cardiovascular deaths, 18 non-fatal MI, 12 non-fatal stroke, and 6 hospitalizations for HF. Incidence of MACE differed substantially across IPV terciles (Figure 4). Patients of tertile 3 had the greatest event rate (32.5%) as compared with tertile 2 (16.0%) and tertile 1 (7.4%) (P < 0.001). The Kaplan–Meier survival analysis validated that the MACE-free survival rate was significantly lower in the high-IPV group (log-rank P < 0.001) (Figure 4A). Multivariable Cox regression analysis, high IPV was also the independent predictor of MACE (HR 3.84, 95% CI 1.89–7.78, P < 0.001) (Figure 4B). In models adjusted for conventional cardiovascular risk factors, the inclusion of IPV tertiles improved predictive accuracy for MACE (see Supplementary data online, Figure S2). The AUC increased from 0.71 (95% CI 0.65–0.78) in the base model to 0.79 (95% CI 0.74–0.84) with IPV (P = 0.002). NRI for the full model was 0.28 (P = 0.004), and IDI was 0.07 (P = 0.008), indicating a statistically significant improvement in both discrimination and classification performance (see Supplementary data online, Figure S3). There was a trend in this direction for the intermediate tertile (HR 2.24, 95% CI 0.99–5.05, P = 0.05). Stratified by treatment group, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that the incidence of MACE in patients treated with SGLT2i was significantly lower than that in controls (12.1% vs. 26.3%, log-rank P = 0.019) (Figure 4C). This advantage was particularly evident in patients with high IPV, of which MACE occurred in 23.1% of SGLT2i-treated patients vs. 38.9% of neutralizing controls. SGLT2i treatment was associated with a 51% relative risk reduction for MACE in adjusted Cox models (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.27–0.88, P = 0.018), with the largest effect observed in tertile 3 (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.25–0.91, P = 0.026) (Figure 4D). In a separate multivariate Cox regression analysis, elevated VEGF (HR 1.92, 95% CI 1.25–2.95, P = 0.003), IL-6 (HR 1.65, 95% CI 1.14–2.39, P = 0.009), and TNF-α (HR 1.49, 95% CI 1.06–2.11, P = 0.021) levels at baseline were independently associated with MACE risk after adjustment for age, sex, LDL-C, HbA1c, and SGLT2i use (see Supplementary data online, Figure S4). The association remained significant after adjusting for IPV tertile, suggesting an additive inflammatory and angiogenic risk component.

Panel C includes the adjusted Cox regression model for treatment effect, indicating that SGLT2i therapy was associated with a 51% reduction in MACE risk (HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.27–0.88, P = 0.018), most pronounced in the highest tertile (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.25–0.91, P = 0.026). No significant interaction was detected between treatment effects.

Discussion

This is a prospective cohort study that brings new information on the prognosis of IPV in type 2 diabetic mellitus (T2DM) patients with non-significant carotid atherosclerotic disease. Trends towards an independent association between an increased vascularization index (as assessed by CEUS) and MACE risk following 6 years of follow-up were found, with inflation of the confidence intervals after adjusting for CRP or FGF-23. Most importantly, SGLT2 inhibitors resulted in a clinically relevant reduction in MACE, with greatest benefit observed in association with high-grade plaque vascularization, indicating that the effect of these agents might be linked to additional vascular effects in patients with vulnerable plaque phenotypes.

The importance of plaque neovascularization in development and destabilization of atherosclerotic lesions has been broadly acknowledged during recent two decades. Adventitial neovessels invade the plaque centre and create an unorganized and immature NPC which lacks coherent wall structure.23 These vessels are particularly prone to leakage and develop intra-plaque haemorrhage, which accelerates the expansion of the lipid core and local inflammation and leads to plaque rupture.24,25 This is even more important in diabetic patients, as choric hyperglycaemia, oxidative stress, and low-grade inflammation condition endothelial dysfunction and enhance angiogenic signalling pathways, like those of VEGF and HIF-1α.26–28 Our results validate this pathophysiological concept, as we demonstrate that patients with the greatest degree of vascularization vs. those with low vascularization shared more than a three-fold greater risk for future MACE.

The significance of CEUS in this setting is also interesting prognostically. CEUS facilitates direct and non-invasive imaging of intra-plaque microvessels in living subjects and offers dynamic information about plaque activity, which cannot be obtained using conventional parameters like luminal stenosis. The application of CEUS in assessing neovascularization and predicting plaque instability has also been confirmed by several published studies.29,30 In our patients, vascularization tertiles on CEUS allowed a stratification in asymptomatic diabetic patients with subclinical carotid atherosclerosis. In line with this, the addition of IPV to a clinical model significantly improved risk stratification metrics. The observed increase in AUC, NRI, and IDI supports the added value of CEUS-derived vascularization grading beyond standard predictors. These data further highlight the potential of IPV as a biomarker of residual risk in asymptomatic diabetic patients. Furthermore, CEUS was a good tool to work as a research platform in investigation of anti-atherosclerotic features of SGLT2i in addition to a predictive tool. Those receiving SGLT2i used to have fewer MACE at an overall level, and it was more significant in the highest tertile of vascularization, with an absolute risk reduction of almost 16%. These findings are consistent with the cardiovascular findings of major trials, such as EMPA-REG OUTCOME and DECLARE-TIMI 58, which showed that the use of SGLT2i in high-risk cohorts led to reductions in heart failure hospitalization and cardiovascular death.31,32

Nevertheless, our data extend these observations by providing a potential mechanistic association between SGLT2 inhibition and plaque biology and not exclusively through systemic hemodynamic or metabolic effects.

Mechanically, SGLT2 inhibitors could inhibit plaque neovascularization by down-regulating VEGF expression. VEGF is a key regulator of angiogenesis and is induced by oxidative stress, inflammation, and hypoxia—all characteristics often observed in destable atherosclerotic plaques. Our data reveal that VEGF concentrations are highest in patients with severe vascularization, and that SGLT2-inhibitor treatment, decreases significantly the levels of VEGF, IL-6, and TNF-α over time. These modifications are probably due to an inhibition of the inflammatory-angiogenic axis in the plaque microenvironment. Consistent with this, laboratory data also exists that SGLT2 inhibitors decrease the activity of HIF-1α, block pro-angiogenic pathways and ameliorate endothelial function in diabetic and atherosclerotic animal models.15,33,34 And the anti-inflammatory properties of SGLT2i could break down the vicious circle of chronic vascular inflammation that drives angiogenesis as well as thrombosis. In addition to their effects on vascular inflammation, IL-6 and TNF-α act as stimulators to VEGF production and increase vascular permeability.35

The higher biomarker decreases found in the SGLT2i group of this study indicate a systemic and local anti-often carry silent yet destabilized plaques with strong inflammatory and angiogenetic such activities.

The clinical implication of our results is that personalized risk stratification is feasible. It is possible that CEUS imaging, in combination with biomarker profiles, could enable identification of diabetics most likely to benefit with regard to cardiovascular outcomes from SGLT2i treatment. Instead of a one-size-fits-all treatment strategy, next-generation care might involve vascular imaging and molecular signatures to identify patients who may benefit from a more aggressive treatment approach among those with subclinical—but biologically active—atherosclerosis.

However, this is a limitation of the study. First, the treatment allocation was not random, which may result in selection bias despite adjustment for baseline differences. Second, our study was performed at a single tertiary centre, possibly impeding its generalizability. Third, CEUS evaluations were conducted at yearly intervals, which may not reflect the dynamic changes in the vascularization of the plaque that may take place in shorter periods of time. Moreover, while CEUS targeted the most morphologically active carotid plaque, systemic burden was indirectly controlled by homogeneous baseline risk and inclusion criteria.

Nevertheless, the possibility of undetected coronary disease remains a limitation. Finally, although reduced VEGF and inflammatory cytokines suggest a mechanistic relationship, any cause-and-effect relationship cannot be conclusively determined in an observational design. Although no formal propensity score matching was performed, we evaluated the baseline comparability between groups using SMD. All evaluated variables showed SMD values < 0.1, indicating excellent balance. This supports the internal validity of the observed associations and strengthens the robustness of our results in the absence of randomized allocation.

In conclusion, our study shows that IPVD is an independent predictor of MACE in patients with type 2 diabetes and moderate carotid atherosclerosis. The risk of this may be reduced by SGLT2 inhibitors, and this effect is probably related to anti-angiogenic and anti-inflammatory effects. These results justify the integration of CEUS in the risk stratification of cardiovascular risk in diabetes and the putative repurposing of SGLT2 inhibitors in plaque stabilization. Future RCTs and mechanistic studies are needed to validate these findings and to investigate the therapeutic value of plaque vascularization targeting in the treatment of atherosclerosis.

He also serves as Head of the Internal Medicine Unit at the same University Hospital. His research focuses on the interplay between metabolic disorders, vascular disease, and inflammation, with particular interest in diabetes, atherosclerosis, and environmental risk factors such as micro- and nano-plastics. Prof. Marfella has authored more than 600 peer-reviewed publications in leading journals, including the New England Journal of Medicine, Circulation, and Cardiovascular Diabetology. His translational research has contributed to advancing the understanding of plaque vulnerability, cardiovascular prevention, and the clinical benefits of novel anti-diabetic therapies such as SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists. He is internationally recognized for his contributions to cardiovascular diabetology and for his leadership in multidisciplinary research projects funded at national and European levels.
