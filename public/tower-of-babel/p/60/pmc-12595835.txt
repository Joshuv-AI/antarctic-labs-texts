Morphological characteristics of the flexor hallucis longus-associated part of Kager’s fat pad
Authors: Kodai Sakamoto, Mutsuaki Edama, Tomonobu Ishigaki, Hirotake Yokota, Ryo Hirabayashi, Makoto Komiya, Junichi Kawakami, Taku Toriumi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe deep, flexor hallucis longus (FHL)-associated part of Kager’s fat pad (KFP-F) is adjacent to structures implicated in the pathophysiology of posterior ankle impingement syndrome (PAIS), and pathological changes within Kager’s fat pad itself are also observed in PAIS. However, the detailed topographical relationships of these structures in the deep ankle remain insufficiently understood. This study aimed to provide a comprehensive anatomical description of the KFP-F and its relationship with surrounding neurovascular and musculoskeletal structures.MethodsFifty legs from 28 Japanese cadavers (28 male, 22 female; mean age at death, 78 years) were examined. The presence or absence of direct contact and positional relationship between the KFP-F with FHL, tibial nerve, posterior tibial artery, posterior tibial vein, talocrural joint capsule, and subtalar joint capsule was systematically evaluated. The width of the contact interface of KFP-F with the talocrural and subtalar capsules were measured and compared.ResultsThe KFP-F demonstrated direct contact with the FHL and the talocrural and subtalar joint capsules in all specimens. In 45 ankles (90%), the KFP-F also demonstrated direct contact with the tibial nerve, posterior tibial artery, and posterior tibial vein. Notably, the KFP-F was observed to circumferentially envelop the superficial and deep aspects of the FHL and neurovascular structures. The width of the contact interface with the talocrural joint capsule was significantly greater than that with the subtalar joint capsule (p < 0.01).ConclusionThe KFP-F forms circumferentially enveloping relationship with the FHL and the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle. This detailed topographical description provides a crucial foundation for improving the interpretation of diagnostic imaging and refining surgical approaches for deep posterior ankle pain.

Introduction

Kager’s triangle is an anatomical space containing Kager’s fat pad (KFP) [1], which functions as a shock absorber [2, 3] and, due to nociceptive fibers, can be a direct source of pain [4]. The KFP is clinically implicated in various posterior ankle pain syndromes, with pathological changes within it serving as an important focus for understanding pathophysiology of these conditions [5]. Posterior ankle impingement syndrome (PAIS), one such posterior ankle pain syndrome, occurs in the deep aspect of the ankle and is known clinically as a condition where differentiating the causative tissue is difficult [6–9]. This is due to the complex anatomy of the deep posterior ankle, where various tissues—such as the flexor hallucis longus (FHL), joint capsule, and bony abnormalities—are involved in the pathology [6–9]. In recent years, pathological changes such as inflammation or fibrosis in the KFP posterior to the talus have also been reported [5, 6, 10], highlighting that evaluation of the KFP is an important focus in the diagnosis of PAIS [5]. Considering these points, a detailed anatomical understanding of the deep posterior ankle, particularly the KFP and its adjacent structures, is essential for providing an anatomical basis to refine diagnostic imaging and therapeutic strategies.

The KFP consists of two distinct parts: the superficial Achilles-associated part (KFP-A) and the deep FHL-associated part (KFP-F), separated by the fascia overlying the FHL [3, 11]. Understanding structures of the KFP-F could be particularly crural for the accurate pathophysiology and diagnosis of PAIS, as the KFP-F relates to structures known to cause the condition, such as the FHL and joint capsules [12]. Previous research using 12 fresh cadaveric ankles suggested that the KFP-F extended to the ankle joint capsule, and closely related to the FHL, and its fascia [12]. However, detailed investigations into the specific contact sites and the extent of the KFP-F’s relationship with these structures remain incomplete [12]. Another study examining nine cadaveric ankles reported that adipose tissue surrounds the FHL tendon at the level of the talar dome [13]. However, these previous studies were limited by small sample sizes, and their dissection procedures were often described only descriptively [3, 12, 13]. Moreover, the detailed topographical descriptions of the KFP-F’s relationship with the FHL and posterior joint capsules have not been sufficiently elucidated.

Furthermore, in the previous researches, the relationship between the KFP-F and another critical structure in the region has not been observed: the tibial nerve. Compression by structures adjacent to the tibial nerve can be one of the factors of the tarsal tunnel syndrome, which can also be causes of the posterior ankle pain [5, 8]. Adipose tissues can play a vital role in protecting peripheral nerves and enabling smooth gliding [14, 15], and its pathological changes, such as fibrosis or inflammation, can lead to neuropathy and pain. However, the anatomical relationships between the KFP-F and tibial nerve have not been investigated. Elucidating these relationships could be essential for enhancing the differential diagnosis of posterior ankle pain syndromes and providing an anatomical basis for safer surgical interventions like nerve decompression.

This study aims to elucidate the detailed topographical relationships between the KFP-F with the FHL, ankle joint capsule, and tibial nerve in the deep ankle, using a large number of specimens. By elucidating these relationships, this research seeks to enhance differentiation of posterior ankle pain syndromes.

Materials and methods

Specimen Preparation

This investigation examined 50 legs from 28 cadavers (mean age at death, 78 years [range: 63–96]; 28 male, 22 female; 25 right legs, 25 left legs). The sample included 25 right and 25 left legs, selected to ensure a balanced sample and prevent potential laterality bias. The specimens were fixed by perfusion with 10% formalin shortly after death and subsequently transferred to alcohol. None of the specimens exhibited macroscopic evidence of prior major surgeries in the foot and ankle regions. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional ethics committee (approval no. 19128–230824), and the research was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent for the use of cadavers in research was provided either by the donors themselves prior to death or by their families.

Dissection procedures

The legs were sectioned approximately 10 cm above the ankle joint.

Posterior ankle dissection, step 1: superficial dissection and exposure of the calcaneal tendon

The skin and subcutaneous tissues of the posterior ankle were removed (Fig. 1a). The crural fascia was then incised longitudinally along the central region and reflected medially and laterally to expose the paratenon and the enclosed calcaneal tendon (Fig. 1b). Following this, the paratenon was incised and reflected in a similar manner to fully expose the calcaneal tendon.

Posterior ankle dissection, Step 2: Deep dissection and exposure of the KFP-F.

The calcaneal tendon was subsequently removed to reveal the KFP-A (Fig. 1c). The KFP-A was separated from the posterior intermuscular septum of leg and reflected posteriorly. Finally, a longitudinal incision was made in the septum, which was then reflected medially and laterally to expose the KFP-F (Fig. 1e). .

Lateral ankle dissection, step 1: exposure of the peroneal tendons

On the lateral side, the posterior intermuscular septum of leg was dissected from the fibula, and the superior peroneal retinaculum was removed. The peroneus longus and brevis tendons were then identified and detached (Fig. 2a-c).

Gross anatomical evaluation

During gross dissection, the anatomical interfaces between the KFP-F and adjacent structures were characterized. The presence or absence of direct contact was assessed based on macroscopic observation during systematic dissection, an approach consistent with prior anatomical studies of infrapatellar fat pads [16]. To further characterize the nature of these macroscopically observed contacts, the following criteria were also applied. For the FHL and neurovascular bundle (tibial nerve, posterior tibial artery, and posterior tibial vein), direct contact was identified based on macroscopic observation, which was then confirmed by observable co-movement of the KFP-F upon gentle traction of the adjacent structure. The direct contact between the KFP-F and the neurovascular bundle and its branches within the tarsal tunnel region was also assessed using these same criteria. For the joint capsules (talocrural and subtalar), direct contact was identified where, in addition to macroscopic continuity, separation required persistent blunt dissection without disrupting tissue integrity. Following characterization of the interface, the mediolateral width of the contact interface between the KFP-F and both the talocrural and subtalar joint capsules was measured. To allow for measurement, the KFP-F was carefully separated from the tibia and fibula. For the talocrural joint capsule, the width was measured at the level of the talar dome using a digital caliper (resolution, 0.01 mm). The caliper was zeroed before each measurement. Subsequently, for the subtalar joint capsule, the width was measured at the level of the fibula-osseous tunnel. The measurements were repeated three times, and the mean values were recorded. All measurements were performed by a single examiner experienced in musculoskeletal gross anatomy. A preservation solution (78% water, 16% alcohol, 4% glycerin, 2% phenol) was applied every 10 min during the assessment. This standard holding solution was utilized to maintain the consistency of the already formalin-fixed tissue and prevent measurement artifacts, such as shrinkage or hardening, that would arise from desiccation during the lengthy assessment period. Specimens were also immersed in the solution for 10 min prior to assessment. To assess intra-rater reproducibility, 10 randomly selected ankles were used; measurements were performed twice by the same examiner with a one-hour interval between assessments. During this interval, the specimens were immersed in the preservation solution to prevent deformation due to desiccation, and reproducibility was verified.

Statistical analysis

The normality of variables was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the presence of direct contact of the KFP-F with the neurovascular bundle between sexes and sides. For Fisher’s exact test, the Phi coefficient was calculated, with values of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 indicating small, medium, and large effects, respectively. A post-hoc power analysis was performed for these comparisons using G*Power software (ver. 3.1.9.7). The independent t-test was used to compare the width of contact interface between the KFP-F and both talocrural and subtalar joint capsules between sexes and sides. Cohen’s d was used to assess the magnitude of any effect and interpreted as follows: trivial, < 0.2; small, 0.2 to 0.49; medium, 0.50 to 0.79; and large, >0.8. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed to compare the width of the contact interface between the KFP-F and both talocrural and subtalar joint capsules, as it is appropriate for paired data and non-parametric distributions. For Wilcoxon signed-rank test, the effect size of 0.1 indicated a small effect, a value of 0.3 indicated a moderate and a value of 0.5 a large effect.

Discussion

This study elucidated the topographic relationships of the KFP-F with the surrounding structures on a large-scale cadaveric sample. We demonstrated two key anatomical features: First, the KFP-F circumferentially enveloped the FHL and the neurovascular bundle, and this interface extended to the neurovascular branches within the tarsal tunnel. Second, deep to the FHL, the KFP-F showed a broad contact interface with the posteromedial to posterolateral regions of the talocrural joint capsule and the posterolateral region of the subtalar joint.

Regarding the first key finding, the observation that the KFP-F circumferentially envelops both the FHL and the neurovascular bundle is a novel contribution. Theobald et al. [3] described the KFP-F as being enclosed within the FHL tendon sheath, and Malagelada et al. [12] noted its continuity with the FHL and its fascia, but these remained descriptive reports. A report by Kawada et al. [13] described adipose tissue surrounding the FHL tendon, which was consistent with our findings. However, this relationship was not investigated in these prior studies, likely because their methods were limited to sectional observations or because the neurovascular bundle had been removed during the dissection process [3, 12, 13]. Our study clarifies that the KFP-F envelops the FHL and the neurovascular bundle together, creating a shared, fat-filled space where these structures are intimately associated. Furthermore, these prior studies were limited to a small number of specimens (fewer than 12 ankles), whereas our investigation of 50 legs provides a more robust and comprehensive validation of this anatomical relationship. Based on this anatomical arrangement, pathological changes such as FHL tenosynovitis [6, 8, 17] or inflammation and fibrosis of the KFP [5, 6, 10], which were pathologies observed in PAIS, could plausibly lead to mechanical compression of the adjacent tibial nerve. This suggests that the diagnostic imaging for suspected tibial neuropathy may need to include scrutiny of the entire KFP-F compartment for abnormalities, in addition to the nerve itself. This detailed anatomy also informs therapeutic strategies. Given that the KFP-F envelops the tibial nerve, this intimate relationship implies a protective role for the fat pad. Therefore, during surgical procedures such as posterior ankle arthroscopy, excessive resection of the KFP-F should be avoided to maintain a protective gliding surface for the tibial nerve.

Meanwhile, in 5 specimens, direct contact of the KFP-F with the neurovascular bundle was not observed. To investigate potential factors associated with this variation, we assessed for differences based on sex and side but found no statistically significant associations. Furthermore, a post-hoc power analysis revealed that the statistical power for these comparisons was low (57% for sex; 1.7% for side). This suggests that our study was underpowered to detect a true difference, and the precise reason for this anatomical variation remains unclear based on our data. It is possible, however, that this variation is attributable to a combination of previously reported morphological variations, such as the distance between tibial nerve and the FHL, and the tibial nerve branching height [18, 19]. In addition, a post-hoc power analysis for the width of the joint capsule contact interface demonstrated low power for both the talocrural (5.6% for sex; 19.1% for side) and subtalar (21.7% for sex; 10.9% for side) joint capsules. Future research with larger sample sizes is warranted to investigate such factors.

Our second key finding was the broad contact interface of the KFP-F located deep to the FHL, interposed between the FHL and the posterior joint capsules. Pinching of these joint capsuloligamentous structures in deep posterior ankle is known as one of the pathomechanisms of PAIS [20, 21]. Dynamic imaging studies have established that the KFP is a deformable structure that changes shape during ankle motion [3, 12]. Considering this known deformability alongside our anatomical finding of a broad attachment, it might be plausible that the pliability of the KFP-F directly influences the compliance and extensibility of the adjacent joint capsules and ligaments, specifically those of the entire talocrural joint capsule from its posteromedial to posterolateral aspects and of the posterolateral region of the subtalar joint capsule. Furthermore, this specific anatomical location has direct clinical relevance to the os trigonum. Our findings demonstrate that the KFP-F is located at the lateral aspect of the posterior talar process, which is the location where an os trigonum forms [22, 23]. Therefore, one might speculate that the presence of an os trigonum could apply mechanical stress to the KFP-F during plantar flexion, potentially involving the fat pad in the pathophysiology of PAIS.

This study has several limitations. First, this study used formalin-fixed cadavers. Direct contact was defined based on initial macroscopic observation, which was then further characterized by assessing for co-movement upon traction. This latter assessment could be influenced by fixation-induced tissue stiffening. However, a study [16] on the infrapatellar fat pad also revealed that the embalming technique is unlikely to affect a binary parameter such as the presence or absence of an attachment. Nevertheless, the fixation process does alter tissue properties, precluding any assessment of the KFP-F’s dynamic function and biomechanical properties. Building on the anatomical foundation provided by this study, future research using dynamic ultrasonography in vivo may elucidate the dynamic behavior of the KFP-F and its mechanical influence on surrounding structures. Second, our qualitative study lacked quantitative morphometric data, as we prioritized the characterization of gross anatomical relationships over the destructive sectioning techniques required for such metrics. Future research employing dynamic ultrasonography to assess the KFP-F’s biomechanical role and 3D reconstruction technic for morphometric analysis is needed to bridge our findings with clinical imaging. Third, the sample consisted of an elderly cohort, so the findings may have been influenced by age-related changes such as fat atrophy or fibrosis. The generalizability of these findings to younger or athletic populations, in whom PAIS is more prevalent, is therefore a limitation. Future studies on younger cohorts are needed to clarify these potential differences. Fourth, limitations of the measurement protocol include the absence of an inter-rater reliability analysis. While a single examiner performed all measurements to ensure consistency, the potential for measurement bias in this approach cannot be excluded. Finally, as our sample lacked specimens with an os trigonum, we could not investigate the influence of this variant on the KFP-F. Therefore, our discussion on the potential interaction between these structures remains hypothetical. Future investigation into this clinically significant relationship, particularly its potential mechanical influence on the KFP-F, is essential.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this gross anatomical study demonstrated that the KFP-F circumferentially enveloped the FHL, tibial nerve, and posterior tibial vessels. Furthermore, deep to the FHL, the KFP-F demonstrated a broad contact interface with the talocrural and subtalar joint capsules. This detailed anatomical knowledge could provide an anatomical basis for future clinical investigations aimed at refining the interpretation of imaging findings in PAIS and developing safer surgical approaches for the tibial nerve.
