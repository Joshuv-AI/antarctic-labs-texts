Versatile Applications of CRISPR‐Based Programmable T‐DNA Integration in Plants
Authors: Xiao‐Xia Lin, Ben‐Qiang Gong, Feng‐Zhu Wang, Jian‐Bo Wan, Xiangyu Xiong, Jian‐Feng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Agrobacterium‐mediated T‐DNA integration into plant genomes represents a cornerstone for transgenic expression in plant basic research and synthetic biology. However, random T‐DNA integration can disrupt essential endogenous genes or compromise transgene expression, stressing the need for targeted integration strategies. Here we explored CRISPR‐aided targeted T‐DNA integration (CRISTTIN) in Arabidopsis, leveraging CRISPR‐induced double‐strand breaks (DSBs) to facilitate precise T‐DNA insertion. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, conventional Cas9 outperformed a designed Cas9‐adaptor fusion nuclease that may recruit Agrobacterium VirD2/T‐DNA complexes to DSB sites via the adaptor‐VirD2 interaction. Using Cas9‐based CRISTTIN, we streamlined the parallel generation of FERONIA null alleles and in‐locus complementation alleles expressing a wild‐type or mutated gene. This enabled phenotypic comparisons under identical genomic contexts and significantly accelerated gene characterisation and critical residue identification. Additionally, CRISTTIN was employed to simultaneously knockout AGAMOUS and in‐locus integrate a RUBY reporter, yielding plants with pink double‐petaled flowers. CRISTTIN also enabled site‐specific insertion of 35S enhancers for transcriptional upregulation of adjacent genes or reporter constructs for promoter activity monitoring. CRISTTIN's effectiveness was further validated in rice. These results demonstrated CRISTTIN as a versatile tool for gene functional studies and precise control of transgene expression in plants.Keywords: Agrobacterium, CRISPR, double‐strand break, targeted T‐DNA integration, transcriptional upregulation

Introduction

Over a century ago, Agrobacterium tumefaciens was identified as the causal agent of crown gall disease in plants (Smith and Townsend 1907). Subsequent investigations uncovered this phytopathogen's remarkable capacity to transfer a segment of tumour‐inducing (Ti) plasmid DNA, termed transferred DNA (T‐DNA), into host cells through a type IV secretion system. In the prevailing working model, T‐DNA strands within plant cells feature a 5′‐terminal covalent attachment of Agrobacterium VirD2 protein and a coating of VirE2 protein, which together mediate nuclear import and protection against host nucleases prior to random genomic integration (Gelvin 2003, 2017; Kim and Gelvin 2007; Nester 2015). While native T‐DNA encodes tumorigenic factors and opine biosynthetic enzymes to facilitate bacterial colonisation, plant biologists harness Agrobacterium for efficient transgene delivery into plant genomes by replacing inter‐border sequences in T‐DNA with intended transgene expression cassettes, capitalising on the essential role of left and right border (LB/RB) sequences in T‐DNA transfer (Gelvin 2003).

Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation has nowadays become the predominant plant genetic engineering approach, mainly due to its broad host range and extraordinary capability for delivering large DNA fragments (> 100 kb) through biological mechanisms that obviate the requirement for specialised equipment (Gelvin 2003; Liu et al. 1999). These advantages enable the simultaneous introduction of multiple transgenes critical for either complex agronomic trait development in crop molecular breeding or sophisticated metabolic pathway engineering in plant synthetic biology (Baltes and Voytas 2015; Collier et al. 2018). However, the stochastic nature of T‐DNA integration poses significant limitations, including insertional mutagenesis risks (Alonso et al. 2003; Feldmann 1991) and position‐dependent transgene expression variability (de Lange et al. 2018; Kirchhoff et al. 2020). Therefore, it is highly desirable to develop precise T‐DNA integration technologies.

T‐DNA genomic integration preferentially occurs at DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs) via plant endogenous repair pathways (Kleinboelting et al. 2015; van Kregten et al. 2016). Pioneering studies have demonstrated that meganuclease‐induced DSBs could substantially enhance T‐DNA integration at specific cleavage sites (Chilton and Que 2003; Salomon and Puchta 1998; Tzfira et al. 2003). Subsequent advances in designer nucleases, including zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs) (Wright et al. 2005), transcription activator‐like effector nucleases (TALENs) (Zhang et al. 2013), and the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR‐associated (Cas) system, revolutionised plant genome engineering by enabling programmable DSB generation (Li et al. 2013; Shan et al. 2013). Among them, the CRISPR/Cas system offers particular advantages by using a programmable guide RNA (gRNA) to direct the Cas nuclease for DSB generation, providing unparalleled simplicity compared to ZFNs and TALENs that require demanding re‐engineering of proteinaceous DNA‐binding modules (Hassan et al. 2021). While CRISPR‐mediated targeted T‐DNA integration has been demonstrated in plants (Kamoen et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2018), systematic evaluation of its efficiency across different genomic loci and prospective applications remains underexplored.

Notably, Cas9 exhibits prolonged association (~6 h) with DSB sites post‐cleavage (Clarke et al. 2018; Richardson et al. 2016), suggesting its potential as a DSB molecular anchor for VirD2/T‐DNA complexes. In this study, we identified a 53‐amino acid (aa) VirD2‐interacting motif in Arabidopsis importin α‐3. This peptide was subsequently used as a Cas9 fusion adaptor to tether VirD2/T‐DNA complexes at CRISPR‐induced DSBs (Ali et al. 2020). Comparative analysis revealed that this tethering strategy resulted in reduced T‐DNA integration efficiency compared to co‐delivery of Cas9 and unbound VirD2/T‐DNA (non‐tethering strategy). We further demonstrated versatile applications of targeted T‐DNA integration: (1) concurrent gene knockout and in‐locus complementation or transgene expression, (2) targeted insertion of transcriptional enhancers and (3) precise reporter integration for promoter activity profiling. Our results highlight the utility of programmable T‐DNA integration in plant gene functional studies and synthetic biology applications.

Results

Fusion With a 53‐aa VirD2‐Interacting Peptide Enables Cas9 to Interact With VirD2

Agrobacterium VirD2 mediates T‐DNA transfer by covalently attaching to its 5′ terminus (Gelvin 2017; Nester 2015). To promote targeted T‐DNA integration through DSB‐tethered VirD2/T‐DNA complexes (Figure 1a), we sought to engineer a physical linker between Cas9 and VirD2. Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis) importin α‐3 (IMPα), a nuclear transport protein, has been shown to interact with VirD2 (Lee et al. 2012). IMPα is composed of an N‐terminal IMPβ‐binding (IBB) domain, eight tandem armadillo (ARM) repeats and a C‐terminal acidic domain (C_domain) (Figure S1a). Using the Arabidopsis protoplast‐based bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay, we validated the interaction between IMPα and VirD2 in the nucleus (Figure S1a). Systematic truncation analysis localised the VirD2‐binding motif within the N‐terminal IBB domain (IMPα1–105, Figure S1a). Further dissection revealed that a minimal 53‐aa segment (IMPα1–53) reproducibly retained full interaction capacity, while its adjacent region IMPα54–105 failed to associate with VirD2 (Figure S1a).

We next evaluated whether the fusion of IMPα1–53 may impair the nuclease activity of Cas9. We constructed N‐terminal or C‐terminal fusions of IMPα1–53 to the Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9) and compared their performance with conventional SpCas9 in editing the BRI1 ASSOCIATED RECEPTOR KINASE 1 (AtBAK1) locus in Arabidopsis protoplasts. Successful editing of the target sequence would abolish a PstI restriction site, rendering 507‐bp target PCR amplicons resistant to PstI digestion (Figure S1b). This assay indicated that, in the absence or presence of co‐expressed VirD2, both IMPα1–53‐SpCas9 and SpCas9‐IMPα1–53 were equally active as SpCas9 (Figure S1b). We further assessed whether IMPα1–53 fusion could bridge the interaction between SpCas9 and VirD2. Co‐immunoprecipitation (co‐IP) assay in Arabidopsis protoplasts revealed that immunoprecipitation of SpCas9‐IMPα1–53 led to co‐precipitation of VirD2, whereas neither IMPα1–53‐SpCas9 nor SpCas9 showed such interaction (Figure S1c). Based on these findings, we selected the C‐terminal fusion of IMPα1–53 to Cas9 for subsequent transgenic evaluation.

Cas9 Outperforms Cas9‐IMPα1 –53 in Mediating Targeted T‐DNA Integration

In transgenic evaluation, we selected Staphylococcus aureus Cas9 (SaCas9) over SpCas9 based on three critical advantages: (1) the compact size of SaCas9 (Ran et al. 2015) facilitates vector construction and protein expression; (2) the extended NNGRRT PAM (protospacer adjacent motif) of SaCas9 enhances editing specificity compared to SpCas9's NGG PAM (Kaya et al. 2016; Yang et al. 2023); (3) SaCas9 exhibits comparable or even superior editing efficiency in Arabidopsis relative to SpCas9 (Wolter et al. 2018; Xiong et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2018).

To assess IMPα1–53‐mediated tethering effects on targeted T‐DNA integration, we compared SaCas9 and SaCas9‐IMPα1–53 at two genomic loci in Arabidopsis, namely ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE 3 (AtABI3) and ENHANCER OF TRY AND CPC2 (AtETC2) (Figure 1b,c). SaCas9 and SaCas9‐IMPα1–53 were expressed by the egg cell‐specific EC1 promoter (Wang et al. 2015), while the gRNA expression was driven by the Arabidopsis U6‐26 promoter (Li et al. 2017). A control with SaCas9's start codon disrupted was used to indicate baseline integration rates without DSB induction.

At the AtABI3 locus, PCR screening and amplicon sequencing of 381 transgenic T1 plants revealed 221 mutant alleles containing indels and/or T‐DNA insertional mutagenesis when the tethering strategy was adopted. Targeted T‐DNA integration occurred in 3.67% (14/381) of plants (Figure 1d). Remarkably, the non‐tethering strategy (i.e., without the adaptor) achieved a targeted integration efficiency of 7.33% (14/191 in T1 plants) (Figure 1d). The DSB‐free control group showed a complete absence of targeted T‐DNA integration in 288 transgenic T1 plants (Figure 1d). Parallel experiments at the AtETC2 locus confirmed this pattern: 0% (0/389) without DSB, 4.47% (17/380) for the tethering strategy and 10.53% (10/95) for the non‐tethering strategy (Figure 1d). These results suggested that DSB generation alone enables site‐directed T‐DNA integration, while Cas9‐IMPα1–53‐mediated T‐DNA tethering perplexingly reduces integration efficiency. Hereafter, we designated this DSB‐dependent integration strategy as CRISPR‐aided targeted T‐DNA integration (CRISTTIN).

Notably, T‐DNA insertions at both target sites exhibited multiple patterns, including bidirectional orientations (LB‐RB or RB‐LB) (Figure 2a) and unconventional head‐to‐head (RB‐RB) or tail‐to‐tail (LB‐LB) configurations (Figure S2). Also, indels were frequently detected at T‐DNA integration junctions (Figures 2a and S2), consistent with earlier findings that T‐DNA integration is mediated by plant endogenous DSB repair machinery (Kleinboelting et al. 2015).

Homozygous T‐DNA Insertional Mutagenesis Abolishes Target Gene Expression

Since the T‐DNA was inserted into the second exon of AtETC2 (Figure 1c), we predicted that targeted T‐DNA insertion by CRISTTIN should be able to disrupt AtETC2 expression. To confirm this, we performed PCR‐based genotyping on the T2 progeny from randomly selected T1 lines (i.e., #32 and #38) using insertion site‐flanking primers. We identified many homozygous T‐DNA insertion alleles that yielded no PCR products (Figure S3). Subsequently, we analysed the transcript levels of AtETC2 in four homozygous lines (i.e., #32‐4, #32‐6, #38‐11 and #38‐17) by reverse transcriptase quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) using a primer pair targeting either the upstream (qETC2‐F1/R1) or downstream (qETC2‐F2/R2) sequence of the insertion site (Figure 2b). As expected, all four homozygous lines exhibited undetectable or dramatically reduced AtETC2 transcripts compared to Col‐0 plants (Figure 2c). In contrast, RT‐PCR amplicons of the housekeeping gene AtACTIN2 in these lines were comparable to those in Col‐0 plants (Figure 2c), confirming that the reduced AtETC2 transcript levels in the homozygous lines are not due to poor RNA quality. These findings demonstrated that CRISTTIN‐mediated exonic T‐DNA insertion can effectively cause target gene knockout.

CRISTTIN Enables Simultaneous Target Gene Knockout and In‐Locus Complementation

While CRISPR‐Cas9 has revolutionised gene editing, CRISTTIN offers unique advantages by concurrently mediating target gene knockout and precise in‐locus complementation with wild‐type or mutated gene versions. This dual functionality is achieved through two distinct CRISTTIN products: (1) targeted T‐DNA insertions carrying complementation cassettes and (2) non‐insertion mutant alleles that enable isolation of loss‐of‐function mutants through genetic segregation. This parallel generation of null and complementation alleles significantly accelerates gene functional studies and critical residue characterisation.

To provide a proof of concept, we selected Arabidopsis FERONIA (AtFER), which encodes a CrRLK1L‐type receptor kinase essential for plant growth and stress responses (Cheung 2024). Building on previous findings that AtFER's kinase activity is indispensable for rosette development but dispensable for ovule fertilisation (Chakravorty et al. 2018), we designed a 13‐kb T‐DNA construct co‐expressing SaCas9, an AtFER‐targeting gRNA and an AtFER or AtFER K565R (encoding kinase‐dead AtFER) transgene (Figure 3a). Both AtFER and AtFER K565R transgenes harboured synonymous mutations at the gRNA target sequence to avoid Cas9 cleavage and were expressed using the AtFER native promoter.

PCR screening and amplicon sequencing revealed that targeted integration of the AtFER‐encoding T‐DNA occurred in 3.64% (2/55) of T1 transformants, while the AtFERK565R‐encoding T‐DNA achieved a targeted integration efficiency of 17.71% (17/96) (Figure S4). Meanwhile, we identified many alleles with edited AtFER but no T‐DNA insertion at the AtFER locus among these two batches of T1 transformants. In the T2 generation, we successfully identified CRISTTIN‐generated homozygous T‐DNA insertion alleles and fer loss‐of‐function alleles. Indeed, during the vegetative phase, the AtFER K565R ‐complemented lines phenocopied fer null alleles (by‐products of CRISTTIN) showing small rosettes, whereas the AtFER‐complemented lines resembled Col‐0 plants (Figure 3b). During the reproductive phase, both AtFER‐ and AtFER K565R ‐complemented plants showed normal fertilisation, whereas the fer null alleles displayed obvious fertilisation defects (Figure 3c). These phenotypes precisely recapitulated previous findings (Chakravorty et al. 2018). Our results highlighted the advantage of CRISTTIN in gene functional studies, which enable simultaneous generation of null mutants and in‐locus complementation lines to simplify gene characterisation.

CRISTTIN Enables Coordinated Control of Endogenous Gene and Transgene Expression

Plant synthetic biology applications, particularly metabolic engineering, demand concurrent integration of exogenous biosynthetic enzymes and rewiring of endogenous metabolic pathways (Liu and Stewart 2015). Building upon our success in simultaneous gene knockout and complementation, we leveraged Arabidopsis as a foundational plant chassis (Holland and Jez 2018; Stewart et al. 2018) to develop a floral enhancement scheme. Since the dysfunction of Arabidopsis AGAMOUS (AtAG) can cause double‐petaled phenotypes (Lenhard et al. 2001), we designed a 13.7‐kb T‐DNA to co‐express SaCas9, an AtAG‐targeting gRNA and a RUBY reporter that encodes betalain biosynthetic enzymes (He et al. 2020) (Figure 3d). The tissue‐specific Arabidopsis APETALA 1 (AtAP1) promoter (Bowman et al. 2012) was employed to drive RUBY expression, ensuring red pigment production primarily in petals.

Using CRISTTIN, we achieved a targeted T‐DNA integration rate of 22.37% (17/76) at the AtAG locus in transgenic T1 plants (Figure S4). A few T1 lines appeared to carry biallelic mutations, with T‐DNA inserted in one chromosome and an indel mutation in the other homologous chromosome. These lines exhibited distinctive pink double‐petaled flowers, while Col‐0 plants displayed white cruciform blossoms (Figure 3e). The engineered plants also showed extended floral longevity (7–10 days versus wild‐type 2–4 days) with delayed wilting, substantially enhancing ornamental value. While these modified plants suffered severe fertilisation defects and produced no siliques, this proof of concept showcased CRISTTIN's capacity for simultaneous endogenous gene regulation and transgene expression control, which is highly desired for advanced plant synthetic biology applications.

CRISTTIN Enables Site‐Specific Activation Tagging for Target Transcriptional Upregulation

Activation tagging, a forward‐genetic approach for gene functional characterisation in plants, typically employs random T‐DNA integration of four tandem copies of cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S enhancers to boost transcription of adjacent genes (Weigel et al. 2000). Building on this concept, we developed CRISTTIN‐mediated precise activation tagging by strategically positioning four tandem 35S enhancers at target gene loci. We purposely placed tandem enhancers proximal to the RB of T‐DNA (Figure 4a), mirroring conventional activation tagging architecture. In a preliminary test, we targeted SaCas9/gRNA to the promoter of Arabidopsis TARGET OF RAPAMYCIN (AtTOR) (Figure 4a). In transgenic T1 plants, CRISTTIN‐mediated T‐DNA integration achieved a targeted insertion efficiency of 9.04% (17/188) (Figure S4). By PCR‐based genotyping, we selected two T1 lines, #33 and #106, with T‐DNA integrated in an LB‐RB orientation, which placed 35S enhancers adjacent to the gene body of AtTOR (Figure 4b). RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that the transcript levels of AtTOR were increased by up to 3‐fold in these lines compared to those in Col‐0 plants (Figure 4c).

To further validate the generality of the above strategy, we selected a gene cluster composed of two face‐to‐face genes, namely PURPLE ACID PHOSPHATASE 1 (AtPAP1) and ABI FIVE BINDING PROTEIN 2 (AtAFP2) (Figure 4d). We targeted SaCas9/gRNA to the promoter of either AtPAP1 or AtAFP2, giving rise to a targeted T‐DNA integration rate of 8.85% (17/192) for the former and 19.8% (19/96) for the latter in T1 transformants (Figure S4). In lines with a proper T‐DNA insertion orientation to position tandem enhancers close to the target genes (Figure 4d,e), RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that the transcript levels of both genes were co‐upregulated by several folds relative to those in Col‐0 plants, regardless of whether the T‐DNA was inserted in the promoter of AtPAP1 or AtAFP2 (Figure 4f,g).

It has been reported that a single 35S enhancer might be sufficient for transcriptional activation during random activation tagging (Weigel et al. 2000). Therefore, we tested the insertion of two tandem 35S enhancers into the promoter of Arabidopsis CAM‐BINDING PROTEIN 60‐LIKE G (AtCBP60g) using CRISTTIN. In the T1 generation, we observed a targeted T‐DNA integration efficiency of 10.95% (30/274) (Figure S4). RT‐qPCR analysis of a transformant containing 35S enhancers aligned with the transcriptional direction of AtCBP60g (Figure S5a) showed that its transcript levels were significantly increased when compared to wild‐type controls (Figure S5b). Collectively, these results demonstrated CRISTTIN's capacity to convert random activation tagging into a programmable transcriptional upregulation tool for a specific gene or multiple clustered genes, enabling both promoter and 3′ region‐mediated transcription enhancement through site‐specific enhancer deployment.

CRISTTIN Facilitates Promoter Trapping for Monitoring Target Promoter Activity

Histochemical analysis of transgenic plants expressing the promoter::GUS reporter (Jefferson 1989) is a frequently used method for mapping gene expression patterns in plants. However, positional effects from random T‐DNA integration can compromise GUS transgene expression fidelity (Peach and Velten 1991), potentially confounding the interpretation of gene expression patterns. To address this limitation, we developed a targeted promoter trap methodology by integrating CRISTTIN with conventional promoter trapping that relies on by‐chance integration of a promoter‐less reporter behind a promoter via stochastic T‐DNA insertion (Springer 2000). By CRISTTIN‐mediated deployment of a promoter‐less reporter at the 5′ end of target genes, this strategy would allow the reporter to mirror endogenous gene expression patterns with enhanced spatial–temporal accuracy. Notably, conventional promoter trapping requires precise in‐frame fusion between the reporter and endogenous gene, substantially reducing the promoter trapping efficiency (Springer 2000). To circumvent this constraint, we incorporated an internal ribosome entry site (IRES) upstream of the reporter sequence, enabling cap‐independent translation initiation (Dorokhov et al. 2002) and eliminating the need for reading frame alignment. For high‐resolution expression profiling, we designed a dual‐reporter system containing firefly luciferase (LUC) and GFP linked by a T2A ribosomal skipping peptide reported previously (He et al. 2020). Validation experiments in Arabidopsis protoplasts demonstrated that the UBQ10 promoter (UBQ10p)‐driven IRES‐LUC‐T2A‐GFP‐HA cassette produced GFP‐HA proteins in a similar molecular weight and comparable levels compared to those from a conventional UBQ10p::GFP‐HA construct (Figure S6), confirming the functionality of both IRES‐mediated translation initiation and T2A‐mediated ribosomal skipping in plant cells.

To demonstrate the capacity of the targeted promoter trap analysis, we selected Arabidopsis CIRCADIAN CLOCK‐ASSOCIATED 1 (AtCCA1) as a target gene, which plays a central role in regulating the circadian clock and photoperiodic responses. Previous studies have established the circadian oscillation of AtCCA1 expression using AtCCA1::LUC reporter lines (Alabadí et al. 2001). We engineered a 10.5‐kb T‐DNA construct containing a promoter‐less IRES‐LUC‐T2A‐GFP‐HA reporter cassette coupled with SaCas9/gRNA cassettes targeting the 5′ coding region of AtCCA1 (Figure 5a). Among 109 transgenic T1 plants, 12 lines (11.01%) exhibited targeted T‐DNA insertion at the AtCCA1 locus (Figure S4). The line #13 was selected for further analysis due to its precise T‐DNA integration in an LB‐RB orientation (Figure 5b). To ensure single‐copy T‐DNA integration, critical for promoter trapping, we performed qPCR‐based copy number estimation in three T2 lines (i.e., #13‐6, #13‐30 and #13‐42) using AtTOR and AtACTIN1 (AtACT1) as single‐copy reference genes. While the lines #13‐6 and #13‐30 showed ~4 copies of T‐DNA in their genomes, the line #13‐42 displayed a single‐copy integration (Figure 5c). Whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) of #13‐42 further confirmed targeted T‐DNA insertion at the AtCCA1 locus, according to a distinct breakpoint of sequencing reads matching the gRNA target site (Figure S7). Subsequent T‐LOC analysis (Li et al. 2022) verified complete T‐DNA integration exclusively at AtCCA1, though two non‐functional T‐DNA fragments were detected on chromosomes #3 and #5, respectively (Figures 5d and S8).

To characterise rhythmic patterns of AtCCA1 expression, #13‐42 seedlings entrained under a 12‐h light/dark photocycle for 14 days were sampled at 4‐h intervals over 24 h. RT‐qPCR analysis revealed circadian oscillation of LUC transcripts peaking at dawn (Figure 5e), reminiscent of the reported AtCCA1 expression patterns (Alabadí et al. 2001). In vivo luminescence imaging of seven‐day‐old #13‐42 seedlings further demonstrated circadian oscillation of LUC activity synchronising with that of AtCCA1::LUC control seedlings (Yuan et al. 2021) (Figure 5f). These results validated CRISTTIN as a robust platform to combine precise T‐DNA integration with sensitive reporter readouts, empowering spatial–temporal gene expression analysis.

CRISTTIN Enables Targeted T‐DNA Integration in Oryza sativa (Rice)

Finally, we evaluated the performance of CRISTTIN in rice. Our previous work has shown that phosphorylation of the juxtamembrane (JM) region in Arabidopsis CHITIN ELICITOR RECEPTOR KINASE 1 (AtCERK1) enhances chitin perception and improves plant survival under fungal infection (Gong et al. 2019). Inspired by this discovery, our recent work demonstrated that replacing the native JM region of rice CERK1 (OsCERK1) with the AtCERK1 JM sequence (AtJM) confers enhanced fungal resistance in rice (Li et al. 2024). To test CRISTTIN's efficacy in such genetic engineering, we used the same T‐DNA construct (Li et al. 2024) co‐expressing SpCas9, an OsCERK1‐targeting gRNA and a chimeric OsCERK1 AtJM transgene driven by the OsCERK1 native promoter (Figure 6a). Using CRISTTIN, we obtained eight out of 216 (3.7%) transgenic calli with targeted T‐DNA insertion (Figure 6b). Sanger sequencing confirmed precise T‐DNA integration at the OsCERK1 locus (Figure 6c). These results demonstrated that CRISTTIN also enables simultaneous gene knockout and in‐locus complementation in a monocot species. A caveat is that, owing to the chimeric nature of callus cells, a large proportion of transgenic rice plants regenerated from calli identified by PCR to contain targeted T‐DNA insertions may not actually carry a targeted insertion. Consequently, the efficiency of targeted T‐DNA insertion determined using rice calli is an overestimate.

Discussion

Targeted T‐DNA insertion into plant genomes is highly desirable as it ensures efficient and stable transgene expression while avoiding perturbations of endogenous gene expression. In this study, we explored CRISTTIN to achieve programmable T‐DNA integration in plants and further demonstrated its versatility through multiple applications: (1) simultaneous generation of null alleles and complementation lines expressing either wild‐type or modified target genes (Figures 3a–c and 6); (2) coordinated inactivation of endogenous genes with controlled transgene expression (Figure 3d,e); (3) locus‐specific enhancer insertion for transcriptional activation of target genes (Figure 4); and (4) targeted integration of reporter constructs to accurately monitor promoter activity (Figure 5). CRISTTIN‐mediated precise T‐DNA integration addresses critical challenges in plant functional genomics. While traditional complementation analyses comparing wild‐type and mutant alleles (e.g., catalytically inactive kinases or phosphorylation‐deficient variants) are often confounded by positional effects of random T‐DNA integration (de Lange et al. 2018; Kirchhoff et al. 2020), CRISTTIN streamlines the generation of null alleles and complementation lines expressing different allelic variants and enables direct phenotypic comparisons under identical genomic contexts. This technology may also advance plant synthetic biology by enabling targeted insertion of transgene cassettes into characterised genomic safe harbour sites (Dong et al. 2020). Such site‐specific integration ensures predictable transgene expression while maintaining genomic stability, a crucial requirement for both plant basic research and biotechnological applications.

During the preparation of this manuscript, Xu and colleagues (Xu et al. 2025) reported a CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated targeted T‐DNA integration approach in Arabidopsis that employs sequential transformation (i.e., introducing gRNAs into pre‐existing Cas9 transgenic lines). Their method achieved a targeted insertion efficiency of up to 30%, comparable to our CRISTTIN approach that requires only a single transformation step. Their approach enables gene activation tagging by inserting a CaMV35S promoter upstream of target genes, resulting in enhanced expression levels. Additionally, they demonstrated male germline‐specific tagging by integrating a kanamycin‐resistance gene and an mCherry reporter into candidate male germline‐specific genes, thereby facilitating mutant analysis and sperm cell tracking. These two independent studies complement each other, collectively validating the utility of targeted T‐DNA integration for diverse applications in plant research.

When comparing CRISTTIN with CRISPR‐mediated gene targeting via homology‐directed repair (HDR) (Dong and Ronald 2021), each methodology has advantages and limitations. CRISTTIN enables the integration of larger DNA fragments compared to the HDR‐mediated gene targeting (Liu et al. 1999), offering substantial advantages for transgene stacking in crop molecular breeding and metabolic pathway engineering. This capability also facilitates the physical linkage of transgenes, preventing genetic segregation while reducing labor‐intensive multi‐trait integration processes. The limitations of CRISTTIN are its inability to achieve precise and seamless insertion of target gene sequences and the relatively low efficiency of ideal targeted T‐DNA insertions in the intended orientation. Our experimental data from ten Arabidopsis genomic loci demonstrated a targeted T‐DNA insertion efficiency of up to 22.37% (Figure S4), exceeding reported HDR efficiencies in Arabidopsis (Miki et al. 2018; Steinert et al. 2016; Wolter et al. 2018; Zhao et al. 2016). However, only a proportion of these targeted T‐DNA insertions consists of single RB‐LB or LB‐RB insertions. Given their respective strengths and limitations, CRISTTIN and HDR‐mediated gene targeting each hold distinct application scenarios in plant genetic engineering.

Post‐cleavage retention of Cas9 at DSB sites (Richardson et al. 2016) has been exploited to improve HDR efficiency through VirD2‐Cas9 fusion (Ali et al. 2020). However, we found that Cas9‐IMPα1–53 reduced T‐DNA integration efficiency when recruiting VirD2/T‐DNA complexes to DSB sites (Figures 1d and S1). This discrepancy is probably attributed to fundamental mechanistic differences. While T‐DNA can serve as an HDR template (Zhang et al. 2022), its genomic integration primarily depends on polymerase‐θ‐mediated DNA repair (van Kregten et al. 2016), a pathway independent of HDR mechanisms. Previous studies suggested that Cas9 associates with the protospacer region after DSB generation (Clarke et al. 2018). Therefore, by sequestrating VirD2/T‐DNA at the C‐terminus, Cas9‐IMPα1–53 might sterically hinder the integration of T‐DNA at DSB sites. The failure of our tethering strategy could also be attributed to other possibilities: (1) Upon entry of VirD2/T‐DNA complexes into plant cells, VirD2 may be bound by multiple host factors, which may prevent the Cas9‐adaptor (lately expressed from the T‐DNA) from binding to VirD2; (2) After the T‐DNA complexes are imported into the nucleus, the T‐DNA strands are likely converted to double‐stranded molecules prior to genomic integration. During this process, VirD2 may have been released from the T‐DNA complexes (Tzfira and Citovsky 2002); (3) Although fusing IMPα1–53 to Cas9 in the absence or presence of VirD2 exerts no adverse effects on Cas9's nuclease activity (Figure S1b), the potential perturbation of Cas9's activity by T‐DNA within the Cas9‐IMPα1–53/VirD2/T‐DNA complex cannot be ruled out. Notably, when comparing mutation efficiencies between SaCas9 and SaCas9‐adaptor at the AtABI3 and AtETC2 loci, SaCas9 appeared to outperform SaCas9‐adaptor (Figure 1d). This observation suggests that the presence of T‐DNA may impair the functionality of the Cas9‐adaptor in planta.

Targeted transcriptional activation represents an important gain‐of‐function strategy for interrogating gene functions, which can circumvent genetic redundancy and gene knockout‐induced lethality. We developed a novel transcriptional activation strategy leveraging CRISTTIN to convert random activation tagging into targeted transcriptional upregulation. This approach enables deployment of tandem 35S enhancers adjacent to target genes using a promoter‐ or 3′ region‐targeting Cas9/gRNA pair, effectively upregulating a specific gene (e.g., AtTOR) or multiple clustered genes (e.g., AtPAP1 and AtAFP2) (Figure 4c,f,g). This is particularly valuable for upregulating the biosynthesis of plant secondary metabolites, where biosynthetic genes often form gene clusters with genomic proximity (Zhan et al. 2022). Moreover, this cis‐acting method preserves endogenous expression patterns of target genes unlike using constitutive promoters (e.g., CaMV 35S promoter) or trans‐acting gene activators, such as dCas9‐TV (Li et al. 2017) and CRISPR‐Act3.0 (Pan et al. 2021). By maintaining physiological regulatory contexts, this strategy provides more authentic functional insights compared to overexpression systems in plant gene functional studies.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Arabidopsis ecotype Columbia‐0 (Col‐0) and rice cultivar Zhonghua 11 (ZH11) were used as wild‐type plants in this study. Arabidopsis seeds were subjected to stratification in the dark at 4°C for 2–4 days. The transgenic T1 plants were germinated on soil (Jiffy Group, Netherlands) containing 0.3% Basta and grown in a growth chamber (Ningbo Saifu, China) under a photoperiod of 16 h light (75 μmol m−2 s−1) at 23°C and 8 h dark at 21°C unless otherwise specified. In two weeks, the Basta‐resistant seedlings were transferred to Jiffy soil to continue growing without Basta treatment. For in vivo bioluminescence assays, plants were grown on 1/2 × Murashige and Skoog medium supplemented with 0.5% sucrose in a plant growth chamber under a photoperiod of 12 h of light at 23°C and 12 h of darkness at 21°C.

Plasmid Construction

All single‐guide RNAs (sgRNAs) in this study were designed using the CRISPR‐GE platform (http://skl.scau.edu.cn) with optimised parameters to minimise off‐target potential (Xie et al. 2017). Target sequences are catalogued in Table S1. The gRNA cassettes were assembled through oligonucleotide annealing and directional cloning. For transgenic expression in Arabidopsis, the binary plasmids were constructed to contain the following cassettes between T‐DNA borders: gRNA expressed by the AtU6‐26 promoter (Li et al. 2017), SaCas9 expressed by the egg cell‐specific EC1 promoter (Wang et al. 2015) and different transgene or genetic element cassettes inserted at the AvrII or MfeI site. The expression cassette of AtFER, AtFER K565R or RUBY was inserted into the AvrII site for in‐locus complementation. To prevent SaCas9‐mediated cleavage of AtFER and AtFER K565R complementation cassettes, we introduced synonymous mutations into the target sequence (5′‐AGAGTCTTCAGAGGAAGATGATAAGAAT‐3′) to generate a modified coding sequence (5′‐AGAGTCTTCtGAaGAtGAgGAgAgaAAc‐3′). A quadruple 35S enhancer sequence was inserted into the AvrII site for transcriptional activation. A promoter‐less luciferase coding sequence was inserted into the MfeI site for promoter trapping. Molecular cloning was carried out in Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells using the ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme Biotech, China).

Generation of Transgenic Plants or Calli

The binary plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 for rice transformation or strain GV3101 for Arabidopsis transformation via electroporation. Correct A. tumefaciens cells were used to transform Arabidopsis floral tissues and rice embryogenic calli according to detailed protocols described previously (Clough and Bent 1998; Nishimura et al. 2006). Transgenic Arabidopsis T1 plants and rice calli were selected on the medium containing 0.3% Basta or 50 mg/L hygromycin.

Genomic DNA Extraction

Arabidopsis genomic DNA was extracted by homogenising 10 mg of tissues from 2 to 3 leaves in 15 μL of lysis buffer containing 100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 9.5, 1 M KCl and 10 mM EDTA. The lysate was incubated at 70°C for 30 min and subsequently diluted 1:10 for target PCR amplification. The genomic DNA of transgenic rice calli was extracted using the cethyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method (Allen et al. 2006). Whole‐Genome Sequencing (WGS) analysis using Next‐generation sequencing (NGS) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by the Biomarker Technologies company (Beijing, China).

Genotyping of Transgenic Plants

PCR‐based genotyping was performed using Green Taq Mix (Vazyme Biotech, China). To detect T‐DNA integration events in plants, a quadruple‐primer amplification strategy was employed: two genomic primers flanking the target site (250 bp upstream/downstream of the Cas9 cleavage site) and two T‐DNA primers complementary to the T‐DNA borders (250 bp from left/right borders). Each genomic primer was combined with a T‐DNA primer in a pairwise manner, generating four possible amplicons. Successful T‐DNA integration at the target locus was confirmed by the amplification of 500‐bp products from two primer pairs. PCR products were gel‐purified and validated by Sanger sequencing. Mutations in edited plants were identified by analysing the deep sequencing data of target amplicons using the Hi‐TOM platform (Liu et al. 2019). The PCR primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

T‐DNA Copy Number Quantification

The copy number of LUC transgene was determined by qPCR using genomic DNA as templates and SYBR Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio, Japan) as the reaction mixture. Transgene copy number was calculated via the 2−∆∆CT method and estimated by referring to single‐copy genes AtACT1 and AtTOR. The qPCR primers are listed in Table S3.

RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from 50 mg of two‐week‐old seedlings harvested at four‐hour intervals over 24 h using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara Bio, Japan). Residual genomic DNA was removed by DNase I treatment, followed by cDNA synthesis using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio, Japan). RT‐qPCR was conducted using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio, Japan) on a CFX96 Real‐Time PCR System (Bio‐Rad, US) using 50 ng cDNA per reaction. A two‐step amplification protocol described by the manufacturer was adopted. The qPCR primers are listed in Table S3.

In Vivo Bioluminescence Imaging

Bioluminescence in ten‐day‐old #13‐42 and AtCCA1::LUC control seedlings was monitored over four days using a NightSHADE LB 985N imaging system (Berthold Technologies, UK). Seedlings were supplemented with 3 mM luciferin and imaged at 20°C under simulated daylight conditions (500 nm white light, 100 μmol m−2 s−1). Image acquisition and analysis were performed using Indigo software v2.1. Circadian luminescence was quantified with a NightOwl CCD camera (600 s exposure, 1 × 1 binning, high gain) after 120 s dark adaptation. Data represent mean ± SEM of ten biological replicates.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Sample sizes were estimated based on established protocols, with no statistical method used for predetermination. Model fitting to curves was conducted using GraphPad Prism v8.0. All the gene structures were generated using the IBS2.0 (Xie et al. 2022).
