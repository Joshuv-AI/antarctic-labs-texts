Standard: human breast cancer organoids derived from diverse clinical sample sources
Authors: Zhigang Chen, Ningning Xu, Ming Chen, Jianming Hu, Zhuohang Yu, Sangsang Zhu, Yi Zhang, Ronghao Zhang, Kun Wang, Shu Wang, Dong Gao, Fei Ma, Jin Zhang, Jiong Wu, Binghe Xu, Erwei Song, Jian Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, driven by therapeutic resistance, recurrence, and metastasis. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) have emerged as a robust platform that preserves histopathological, genetic, and functional features of parental tumors, enabling translational research and precision oncology. However, the lack of standardized procedures across laboratories limits reproducibility and clinical applicability. Here, we present a consensus-oriented standard for the establishment, characterization, quality control and application of human breast cancer organoids derived from diverse clinical sources, including surgical tissues, biopsies, pleural effusion, ascites, and circulating tumor cells (CTCs). This document defines key terminologies, outlines standardized workflows, and introduces quantitative validation criteria for successful organoid establishment. In addition, it provides structured guidance for advanced models, including immune–tumor, neuro–tumor, and microbiota−tumor organoid systems, with defined functional validation endpoints. Standardized protocols for drug sensitivity testing are also proposed, including seeding density, exposure duration, and response metrics such as IC50 and AUC. Representative validation strategies, including histological, molecular and functional concordance with parental tumors, are incorporated to enhance reproducibility and translational relevance. This standard aims to harmonize methodologies, improve cross−study comparability, and accelerate the clinical implementation of breast cancer organoid-based precision medicine.Keywords: Standard, Breast cancer, Organoids, Drug sensitivity, Personalized treatment

Background

Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed malignancy and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide. Despite major advances in early detection and targeted therapies, therapeutic resistance, recurrence and metastasis remain significant clinical challenges. The marked heterogeneity of breast cancer underscores the need for experimental models that faithfully recapitulate patient-specific tumor biology and enable functional evaluation of therapeutic responses.

Three-dimensional (3D) patient-derived organoids have emerged as a promising platform for cancer modeling and precision medicine. Compared with conventional two−dimensional cell lines and patient-derived xenograft models, tumor organoids more faithfully preserve the histopathological architecture, genetic alterations, and molecular characteristics of the original tumors. Increasing evidence suggests that organoid-based drug testing can recapitulate patient-specific treatment responses, supporting their potential role in translational research and personalized therapy.

Recent advances have enabled the successful derivation of breast cancer organoids from multiple clinically accessible specimen types, including surgical tumor tissues, core needle biopsies, and malignant body fluids such as pleural effusion and ascites. These diverse sources greatly broaden the applicability of organoid technology, especially for patients with advanced disease for whom surgical specimens are often unavailable. Organoids derived from different specimen types, however, exhibit distinct biological and technical characteristics, due to inherent differences in cellular composition, tumor cell abundance, sample processing, and culture requirements. Consequently, protocols optimized for surgical tissues are often not directly applicable to biopsy- or fluid-derived samples.

To date, no unified guideline has systematically addressed the establishment and standardization of breast cancer organoids derived from diverse clinical sample sources. To fill this gap, the present standard provides operational recommendations for sample processing, organoid culture, characterization, and quality control across multiple specimen types, including surgical tissues, biopsies, and malignant body fluids. By defining standardized workflows tailored to different clinical sources, this guideline aims to improve reproducibility and facilitate the broader implementation of breast cancer organoid technology in translational research and precision oncology.

Scope

This document specifies the general principles, operational procedures, quality control requirements, and storage/transport protocols for the establishment and application of human breast cancer organoids from multiple sources of clinical specimens (e.g., tumor tissues and body fluids).

It applies to the culture, passaging, cryopreservation, thawing, characterization, and personalized drug testing of human breast cancer organoids.

Terms and definitions

The following terms and definitions apply to this document.

Organoids

A three-dimensional (3D) in vitro cellular structure derived from primary tissues, stem cells, or circulating tumor cells (CTCs), capable of self-organization and long-term expansion, and can partially recapitulate the structural, cellular, genetic, and functional characteristics of their origin tissues (Ballard et al. 2019; Clevers 2016; Dutta et al. 2017; Zhao et al. 2022).

Breast cancer organoids

Organoids derived from human breast adult stem cells of cancer/normal tissue or pluripotent stem cells, recapitulating tumor pathologic features, genetic characteristics, and treatment response, and capable of stable passaging in vitro (Dekkers et al. 2021).

Surgical-specimen-derived breast cancer organoids

Organoids established from surgically resected primary or metastatic breast cancer tissues using a 3D culture system, retaining essential biological characteristics of the parental tumors (Dekkers et al. 2021; Sachs et al. 2018; Sato et al. 2009).

Biopsy-sample-derived breast cancer organoids

Organoids derived from core-needle or fine-needle biopsy specimens of pathologically confirmed breast cancer (Dekkers et al. 2021; Sachs et al. 2018; Sato et al. 2009).

Pleural/ascitic-fluid–derived breast cancer organoids

Organoids established using tumor cells isolated from pleural or peritoneal effusions of metastatic breast cancer patients, high-fidelity stimulate the tumor spatial structures, cellular composition and interactions, tumor cell characteristics, and drug responses, among others (Dekkers et al. 2021; Sachs et al. 2018; Sato et al. 2009).

CTCs–derived breast cancer organoids

Breast cancer organoids established from CTCs isolated from peripheral blood samples, enabling minimally invasive modeling of tumor biology (Pan et al. 2025).

iPSCs-derived breast cancer organoids

Organoids generated through directed differentiation of induced pluripotent stem cells under 3D culture conditions (Dutta et al. 2017; Rowe and Daley 2019).

Organoid culture medium

A defined culture medium optimized to support long-term expansion and self-organization of breast cancer organoids and preserve their original tissue phenotypes and functions.

3D cell culture matrices

A biomimetic scaffold that provides structural support and extracellular matrix cues to enable 3D cell growth and organization.

Tumor cell viability

The proportion of live tumor cells within a total organoid sample, reflecting cell survival and overall viability.

Tumor cell purity

The proportion of tumor cells relative to non-tumor cells within an organoid culture, reflecting sample composition and quality.

Complex organoids

Advanced organoids that incorporate multiple cellular components and structural complexity, enabling the recapitulation of native tissue architecture and interactions.

These models are characterized by:

Multi-cellular components (e.g., tumor, immune, neuronal, or microbial components);

Multi-cellular components (e.g., tumor, immune, neuronal, or microbial components);

Structural organization or spatial architecture;

Structural organization or spatial architecture;

Functional interactions such as immune cytotoxicity, neural signaling, or host–microbiota crosstalk;

Functional interactions such as immune cytotoxicity, neural signaling, or host–microbiota crosstalk;

Optional integration with bioengineering platforms (e.g., microfluidics, biomaterials).

Optional integration with bioengineering platforms (e.g., microfluidics, biomaterials).

Immune-tumor organoid co-culture systems

Co-culture systems integrating tumor organoids with immune cells, like T cells, NK cells, DC cells, macrophages and TILs, to effectively mimic the complex critical interactions between immune cells and tumor cells, providing a platform to study the mechanisms of tumor recognition and immune evasion (Dao et al. 2022; Neal et al. 2018; Polak et al. 2024).

Neuro-tumor organoid co-culture systems

Co-culturing neurons with tumor organoids to mimic interactions between tumor and neurons to study how neurons contribute to the TME and tumor pathogenesis, furthermore, drug response (Chan et al. 2025).

Microbiota–organoid co-culture systems

Co-culture systems integrating tumor organoids with associated microbiota in tumor microenvironment to replicate interactions between tumor cells and microbes (Cao et al. 2024; Fu et al. 2023, 2022).

Organoid-on-a-chip

A mammary microphysiological system that can reflect the key structures and biofunctions (such as metabolism, regeneration and secretion) of the human breast by seeding multiple cells (e.g., breast epithelial cells, stromal cells and vascular endothelial cells) and reconstructing microenvironmental elements (e.g., extracellular matrix and fluids) (Park et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2024, 2025).

Multi-organoid co-culture systems

Combining organoid-on-a-chip systems for multiple organs to investigate inter-organ interactions (Gabbin et al. 2023; Tao et al. 2022).

Drug sensitivity

The degree to which tumor cells respond to specific therapeutic drugs.

Personalized drug testing

Individualized drug response evaluation to guide tailored treatment decisions.

Organoid passage

Process of dissociating existing organoids into smaller fragments, or single cell via physical, chemical, or biological methods, and keeping them growing in vitro under the same culture conditions (Dekkers et al. 2021).

Organoid cryopreservation

Freezing process by which organoids are maintained at low temperature in an inactive state for maintaining cellular composition, gene expression, and functional properties.

Organoid thawing

Process of bringing frozen organoids from an inactive to an actively growing state.

Ethics review committee

Institutional committee overseeing the ethical compliance of studies involving human biological samples.

Informed consent

Voluntary agreement by donors after full disclosure of sample usage in accordance with GB/T 38736—2020.

General requirements

Tissue and fluid sample collection must receive prior ethics review committee approval.

Written informed consent must be obtained before sample collection.

Tissue processing procedures shall be standardized to ensure operational feasibility and reproducibility.

Sample handling personnel must receive professional training in biosafety and organoid technology.

Personal information of donors must be handled according to Sect. 6 of GB/T 38736—2020.

Procedures and methods

Processing of different sample sources (Fig. 1)

Overall pipeline for PDO establishment and preclinical drug testing. The resulting PDOs undergo phenotypic and molecular validation, including morphological tracking, H&E/IHC staining, and WES, to ensure consistency with the original tumors. Validated PDOs are subsequently utilized for high-throughput in vitro drug testing. The representative graph demonstrates the differential viabilities of PDOs from three patients following doxorubicin treatment, identifying drug-sensitive and resistant profiles

Surgical tissue samples

Surgical sample selection

Select surgical samples in which the primary tumor mass shall be no less than 1.0 cm and metastatic tumor mass shall be no less than 0.5 cm.

Surgical sample collection

Make an incision along the longest diameter of the tumor with a scalpel, avoiding necrotic tissue, and take fresh tumor tissue about 2–3 mm thick.

Sample preprocessing

Rinse twice with pre-chilled PBS buffer. Using sterile ophthalmic forceps and ophthalmic scissors, remove adipose tissue (yellow), necrotic tissue (brownish), and blood vessels/clots (red).

Biopsy samples

Biopsy sample collection

Obtain biopsy specimens using a 15G biopsy needle, selecting two tissue samples (each specimen must be at least 1 × 10 × 10 mm3).

Body fluid sample (Pleural/ascitic fluid)

Clinical patient evaluation

For patients with a history of breast cancer presenting with pleural effusion or ascites, collect exfoliated cells for pathological diagnosis.

Body fluid sample collection

Collect 100–500 ml of pleural/ascitic fluid retained within the past 3 h. Observe its characteristics to exclude cloudy inflammatory fluid. Transfer the sample to a 500 ml sterile specimen bottle.

Sample preprocessing

Filter to remove suspended fibers, then centrifuge to obtain cell sediment. If a large number of red blood cells are present, treat with red blood cell lysis buffer and then centrifuge.

Circulating tumor cells (CTCs)

Clinical patient evaluation

For patients with locally advanced breast cancer or metastatic breast cancer, a comprehensive assessment and documentation of their medical history, imaging studies, and pathological findings should be conducted.

Peripheral blood sample collection

Using a 10 ml anticoagulant tube, collect 10–20 ml of peripheral blood and label it with the relevant patient information.

Sample processing

Due to the low abundance of CTCs, enrichment strategies may include immunoaffinity capture, microfluidic sorting, or in vivo expansion using xenograft models (CTC-Derived eXplant). The latter approach is optional and should be justified based on experimental objectives, as it is not yet a universally standardized method.

Tumor organoids construction

Organoids from surgical tissue samples

Use ophthalmic forceps and scissors to cut the tumor tissue into 0.3 mm3 blocks.

Add collagenase and use a tissue dissociator to digest the tissue (digestion for 60 min, not exceeding 90 min).

Filter and centrifuge the digested cell suspension.

Resuspend the cell sediments with matrix gel and inoculate them into pre-warmed 24-well plates, taking care to avoid forming bubbles.

After the matrix gel has solidified, add organoid culture medium.

Cultured in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Change the culture medium every 3 days. Each generation of tumor organoids has a growth cycle of approximately 5–7 days.

Organoids from biopsy samples

Refer to the section “Organoids from surgical tissue samples”.

Note: Digest biopsy tissues in the tissue dissociator for 15–30 min. After the organoid was successfully constructed, change the culture medium every 3 days. Each generation of tumor organoids has a growth cycle of approximately 7–14 days.

Organoids from body fluid sample

Resuspend the preprocessed cell sediment in a small volume of organoid medium. Then add 2–3 times the volume of matrix gel.

Resuspend the cell sediments in matrix gel and inoculate them into pre-warmed 24-well plates, taking care to avoid forming bubbles.

After the matrix gel has solidified, add organoid culture medium.

Cultured in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Change the culture medium every 2–3 days. Each generation of tumor organoids has a growth cycle of approximately 7–14 days.

Note: In some cases, fibroblast cells in pleural and peritoneal fluid samples exhibit greater growth advantage than tumor cells. If a large number of fibroblast cells adhere and proliferate, timely passage is required.

Successful establishment of breast cancer organoids shall meet the following minimum criteria:

Formation of stable 3D structures within 3–7 days post-seeding;

Capability for at least three serial passages;

Capability for at least three serial passages;

Histological similarity to parental tumor confirmed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining;

Concordant expression of at least one clinically relevant marker (ER, PR, HER2);

Test methods

Morphology

Observe organoid morphology using an inverted phase contrast microscope. After passage, most breast cancer organoids form 3D cell spheres with diameters of 100–150 μm, exhibiting either a solid structure or a hollow structure (Fig. 2).

Morphological heterogeneity of PDOs. Representative bright-field microscopy images illustrating the diverse morphologies of four independent PDO lines. Scale bars: 50 μm

Identity

The organoids were characterized across multiple dimensions, including morphology, histopathology and molecular genetics. This assessment aimed to confirm the consistency of the organoids with their parental tumors, which is a prerequisite for subsequent drug screening.

The method can be found in Appendix C.

Organoid viability assessment

After thawing, the number of viable breast cancer organoids should be no less than 50% of the number before cryopreservation, and the surviving organoids should be capable of passage culture in vitro.

The method can be found in Appendix A.

Complex organoid models

Human-iPSC-derived neuro-breast cancer organoids

Using a combination of multiple small molecules, human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neural progenitor cells were differentiated into midbrain neurons and cultured to maturity.

Mix dissociated organoids with mature neuronal cells in equal parts of volume (tumor cells 5000–10000: neuronal cells 10000–50000 per well).

Inoculate into ultra-low-attachment 96-well plates and incubate at 37 °C, changing the medium every 2–3 days.

Validation indicators should include the following: axonal infiltration into the target region, synaptogenesis, the presence of neuronal markers such as βIII-tubulin and MAP2, evidence of physical interaction between neurons and tumor cells, as well as functional synchronous electrical activity and neurite infiltration.

Immune-tumor organoids

For cancer patients receiving immunotherapy, accurately document case information, including record number, name, and medical history.

Collect paired peripheral blood and biopsy samples from tumor patients undergoing immunotherapy.

Lymphocytes were isolated from peripheral blood using a lymphocyte isolation solution and cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 150 U/ml of IL-2 to promote expansion.

Co-culture isolated immune cells with tumor organoids with an effect-to-price ratio as 10:1–100:1 based on different immune cells, mixing organoid and immune cell culture media in a 1:1 ratio.

During co-culture of immune cells and tumor organoids, regularly evaluate the proliferation, activation, and killing functions of immune cells.

Validation indicators should include the following: the presence of viable immune cells (≥ 30% of initial input after 72 h), expression of immune activation markers such as CD69 and IFN-γ, evidence of tumor cell killing (≥ 20% reduction in viability compared to the control), and the recruitment and infiltration of immune cells into organoids, with a significant increase in infiltration level as co-culture time is extended (Fig. 3).

Microbiota–organoids

Select patients with locally advanced or metastatic breast cancer. Avoid inflammatory conditions such as superficial abscesses and no antibiotic use within the past 2 weeks.

Mechanically dissociate freshly collected tumor tissue under aseptic conditions to release potential intratumoral microbiota.

Inoculate the processed tissue suspension into the selective culture medium, then culture it under the appropriate conditions.

Preliminary identification of bacteria through observation of colony morphology and staining characteristics.

Co-culture the isolated and identified Intratumoral microbiota with organoids.

Validation indicators should include the following: organoids maintaining intact morphology and viability, bacteria expanding steadily without short-term overgrowth, identification of microbial species through sequencing or staining, stable co-culture for ≥48 h, and evidence of functional interaction, such as cytokine modulation or growth alteration (Fig. 4).

Microbiota-organoid co-culture system. Upper panel: Bacteria labeled with a green fluorescent probe and organoids stained with calcein blue (AM) were co-cultured to visualize bacterial invasion. After 24–48 h of co-culture, the inflammatory response of organoids was evaluated by measuring IFNα, IL-1β, and other inflammatory factors to characterize the functional features of the bacteria–organoid co-culture system

Drug sensitivity testing standard

Conduct clinical drug sensitivity testing using tumor organoids to create drug sensitivity curves for chemotherapy drugs, small-molecule inhibitors, non-immune checkpoint inhibitors, targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors.

To evaluate in vitro drug responses under standardized conditions, PDOs at passages 3 to 5 were harvested and dissociated into single-cell suspensions. Initially, a base layer was prepared by mixing complete PDO culture medium and basement membrane matrix at a 1:1 ratio on ice. This 50% matrix mixture was dispensed into each well of a 384-well culture plate (20 μL/well) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to ensure complete solidification. Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in complete medium supplemented with 5% basement membrane matrix and seeded onto the base layer at a standardized density of 1,000 cells per well in a 20 μL volume. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Prior to drug administration, representative wells were microscopically evaluated as a quality control step to confirm that the micro-organoids had successfully formed and reached a standardized target diameter of 50 to 150 μm. For each tested drug, a 7-point concentration gradient was prepared via a threefold serial dilution (meeting the requirement of at least 5 concentration gradients), utilizing the previously reported clinical plasma concentration as the median value. The diluted drugs were added to the experimental wells in triplicate, while control wells received equivalent volumes of the corresponding solvent. Blank wells containing only matrix and medium were prepared for background subtraction. Following a standardized drug exposure period of 96 h, therapeutic responses were evaluated using a dual-readout approach adapted for the high-throughput format. First, basic morphological assessments were conducted on representative wells to visually confirm drug-induced structural disruption or growth arrest. Second, for the primary quantitative viability-based assay, an ATP-based 3D cell viability detection reagent was added at a volume equal to the existing liquid in each well. The plates were vigorously shaken in the dark for 15 min to lyse the cells and disrupt the 3D matrix, after which luminescence signals were measured using a multi-mode microplate reader. Relative cell viability was calculated using the formula: Relative Viability (%) = [(experimental well reading − average blank well reading)/(average control well reading − average blank well reading)] × 100%. Finally, dose–response curves were generated, and half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were calculated via a non-linear regression model using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (Fig. 5).

C Dose–response curve of doxorubicin-sensitive PDOs, showing a marked decrease in viability starting at sub-micromolar concentrations. Transportation and storage

Transportation

Place fresh tumor tissue or biopsy specimens into a 15 ml centrifuge tube containing pre-cooled commercial tissue preservation solution (preservation time should be less than 24 h); or collect 100–500 ml of pleural or ascites fluid retained for no more than 3 h, observe its characteristics and transfer it to a 500 ml sterile specimen bottle. Record the transportation process in accordance with clauses A.3 and B.3 of GB/T 37864—2019. The record shall include the mode of transport, temperatures during transport and upon receipt, start and end times and dates of transport, and the names of the transport personnel and receiving personnel.

Appendix A

Organoid viability assessment

A.1 Live/dead cell staining

During organoid culture, assess organoid proliferation and apoptosis status by fluorescent staining of live and dead cells followed by fluorescence microscopy imaging.

A.1.1 Reagent preparation

Prepare Calcein-AM stock solution (1 mM in anhydrous DMSO) and propidium iodide (PI) stock solution (1 mg/mL in water). Store both at −20 °C (Calcein-AM) or 4 °C (PI) protected from light. Prepare working solutions immediately before use: Calcein-AM at 2 μM and PI at 4 μM in PBS.

A.1.2 Staining procedure

Harvest organoids and wash twice with pre-warmed PBS. Incubate with combined staining solution (2 μM Calcein-AM + 4 μM PI) for 15–30 minutes at 37 °C in a humidified incubator (5% CO2), protected from light. Wash once with PBS and transfer to glass-bottom imaging dish for microscopy.

A.1.3 Fluorescence microscopy

Image stained organoids using an inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate filter sets:

Calcein (live cells): Excitation 490 nm, emission 515 nm (green fluorescence)

PI (dead cells): Excitation 535 nm, emission 617 nm (red fluorescence)

Acquire images using a 10× or 20× objective with consistent exposure times. For dual-color visualization, use a FITC/TRITC dual-band filter set (excitation ~490 nm). For dead cells only, use TRITC filter set (excitation ~545 nm).

A.1.4 Image analysis

Analyze fluorescence images using ImageJ or equivalent software. Subtract background using rolling ball algorithm (radius = 50 pixels). Segment individual organoids and measure mean fluorescence intensity for Calcein (green channel) and PI (red channel). Calculate viability as a percentage of live cell area:

A.1.5 Controls

Include fully viable organoids (uniform Calcein staining) and heat-killed organoids (70% ethanol, 10 minutes; uniform PI staining) as positive controls. Include unstained organoids and single-stain controls to assess autofluorescence and spectral bleed-through.

A.2 Fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM) (optional)

By analyzing fluorescence lifetime components, cellular oxidative phosphorylation activity and energy metabolism status can be reflected. FLIM measures the fluorescence lifetimes of NAD(P)H and FAD to dynamically assess organoid metabolic activity.

A.3 Inducible cell division counter (iCOUNT) (optional)

A.3.1 Design and construct reporting systems based on fluorescent proteins, DNA barcodes or mitosis-dependent gene expression. Introduce these reporting systems into the progenitor cells of organoids.

A.3.2 Cultivate organoids from cells that express the reporting system to ensure stable expression of the reporter throughout organoid development. Optimize culture conditions to encourage the growth and maturation of the organoids.

A.3.3 Regularly monitor fluorescent signals using fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry, or analyze barcode changes through sequencing techniques to record cell division events.

A.3.4 Use image analysis software (Image J) or sequencing data analysis tools (Python or R) to process data and extract information such as cell division counts, proliferation rates and lineage relationships.

Appendix B

Drug sensitivity testing

B.1 Key technical points for clinical chemotherapy and targeted drug sensitivity testing in breast cancer

B.1.1 Evaluation of organoid cell viability prior to drug treatment

Cell viability is quantitatively and visually evaluated using the cell viability assay kit and Annexin V first.

B.1.2 Uniform inoculation

Use TrypLE™ Express enzyme to gently dissociate passaged organoids stably for 30 minutes. Monitor the progress of the dissociation closely under a microscope and dissociate the organoids into single cells. Filter the cell suspension through a 70 μm filter membrane to remove cell clumps. Finally, use a pipetting device to dispense samples uniformly, ensuring consistent organoid numbers in each well.

B.1.3 Drug treatment

After single cells have been cultured for 2 days to form organoids, a gradient of 7–9 drug concentrations is applied to treat the organoids, with 3–5 replicate wells per concentration. Cell viability is assessed after 96 hours of treatment.

B.2 Key technical points for clinical immunotherapy drug sensitivity testing in breast cancer

B.2.1 Immune-organoid co-culture system

Before conducting immunotherapy drug-sensitivity testing, it is essential to clarify the mechanism of the drug and identify the types of immune cells that exhibit synergistic effects with it. Based on this understanding, corresponding immune-organoid models should be constructed.

B.2.2 Immune cell viability test

Using cell viability assay kits to assess the viability of organoids and immune cells. Additionally, IL-2 or response factors are added to the co-culture system to maintain the activity of immune cells, ensuring their proper function during the assay.

B.2.3 Drug sensitivity testing

The presence of immune cells makes it difficult for cell viability-based assays to detect the effects of tumor killing directly and accurately. Therefore, probe imaging technology was employed to conduct qualitative and quantitative analyses of drug sensitivity. Furthermore, flow cytometry was utilized to further characterize the functions of immune cells, thereby enabling a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of sensitivity to immunotherapy agents.

Appendix C

Quality assessment of breast cancer organoids

C.1 Sample quality evaluation and control

C.1.1 Comprehensive documentation of the donor's basic information, pathological details, medical history overview and the detailed circumstances of sample collection and transportation is required for newly acquired samples. Samples that were damaged during treatment or collection should be clearly labelled, and quality assessment and control procedures should be implemented.

C.1.2 Observe the organoid under a microscope on the same day or the following day after construction. There are distinct differences between apoptotic tissue clusters and cells and normal ones: Apoptotic cells fail to form spherical organoids; organoids or cells may exhibit darkening and shrinkage, and may even display apoptotic bodies.

C.2 Organoid quality evaluation and control

C.2.1 3D structural evaluation of organoids for morphological and histological characterization.

C.2.2 H&E staining validation

Breast cancer organoids should exhibit high consistency with parental tumor tissue in histological features such as nuclear morphology, nucleoplasmic ratio, and luminal architecture.

C.2.3 Molecular marker consistency testing

Immunohistochemistry serves as a key diagnostic method, with breast cancer organoids and their parental tumor tissues exhibiting consistent expression of key markers such as ER, PR, HER2, and Ki67.

C.2.4 Genetic mutation spectrum analysis

Using whole-exome sequencing or targeted sequencing technology, confirm that organoids carry the driver gene mutations of the original tumor and screen for de novo mutations arising during the culture process.

C.2.5 Heterogeneity assessment

Using single-cell sequencing or spatial transcriptomics, verify whether organoids retain the cellular subpopulation diversity and heterogeneity of the parental tumor, thereby preventing clonal selection bias caused by culture.
