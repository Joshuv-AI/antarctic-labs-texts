Health Literacy Promotion and Digital Interventions for Depressive Disorders
Authors: Jacqueline Posselt, Jonas Lander, Marie-Luise Dierks
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe lack of therapeutic treatment options for depressive disorders increases the relevance of digital interventions for patients and general practitioners (GPs), particularly to bridge waiting times. However, patients may require adequate (digital) health literacy (HL) to partake in such interventions.ObjectiveThis study contrasts expected benefits and risks of digital mental health interventions, specifically digital therapeutics on prescription (DTx), among GPs and patients with depression according to HL dimensions (access, understanding, appraisal, and application) and patients' personal determinants.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews with purposively sampled GPs (n = 17) and patients with mild or moderate depression (n = 17) in Germany. The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and coded. Data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis.Key ResultsDTx include benefits to access health information and promote HL as DTx can improve understanding of health content and foster self-management skills. But DTx also implies risks for routine care distribution and creates new media barriers. During waiting times patients appraise DTx for themselves as more positive than GPs expect. However, digital- and disease-specific challenges such as non-commitment, listlessness or lethargy appear to hinder the use of DTx in practical situations. While GPs were not convinced about the digital skills of their patients, patients raised concerns that depressive symptoms may inhibit them from actual use.ConclusionIn the context of DTx for depressive disorders, patients' mental health needs are seldom noticed and rarely promoted. To foster (digital) HL, DTx should be comprehensively embedded in supply situations. [HLRP: Health Literacy Research and Practice. 2024;8(4):e236–e245.]

Digital health interventions (DHI) for depressive disorders is an umbrella term for delivering therapeutic content through electronic services to prevent, treat or promote self-management for people with depressive disorders (Hegerl & Oehler, 2020). Due to the growing number of offers and the rising prevalence of depression (World Health Organization, 2017), digital health services are relevant for public mental health (Bucci et al., 2019; Murray et al., 2016). DHI for people with depression can contribute to overcoming access challenges to mental health content (Emerson et al., 2021), e.g. during waiting times or for people with fewer access to in-person health care (Hafner et al., 2022). They may bridge times between general practitioners' (GPs') primary psychosomatic care and specialized treatment (Lin & Baumeister, 2015; Marciniak et al., 2020) by providing therapeutic content. DHI can also foster patients' empowerment (Heidel & Hagist, 2020). Meta-analyses show that internet-based services for depressive disorders tend to produce better effects than waiting times without intervention (Donker et al., 2013; Furukawa et al., 2021); however, more robust study designs are required (Serrano-Ripoll et al., 2022).

Furthermore, people with depressive disorders increasingly used DHI during the COVID-19 (coronavirus disease 2019) pandemic (Czaplicki et al., 2022), because infection control measures reduced in-person appointments (Reich et al., 2022). However, central challenges lack of awareness in routine care, especially according to patients' adherence (Dahlhausen et al., 2022; Kernebeck et al., 2021; Oehler, Scholze, et al., 2021) and the risk of creating a digital divide and increasing health inequalities (Cornejo Müller et al., 2020; Mackert et al., 2016).

In Germany, costs for specifically approved DHI (“Digitale Gesundheitsanwendungen” or digital therapeutics [DTx]) are covered by statutory health insurances. DTx are low-risk medical devices and require a prescription by a health professional (BfArM, 2020; Rämsch-Günther et al., 2018). In contrast to ordinary DHI, DTx have passed a review process by the Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM [Bundesinstitut für Arzneimittel und Medizinprodukte]). DTx for people with depression mainly include educational content and are based on cognitive behavioral therapy (BfArM, 2023). These web-based applications provide reliable health information via text, video and audio formats and include practical tasks for patients.

To use DHI such as DTx, participate in educational programs and benefit from potential advantages, patients require self-management skills and health literacy (HL) (Mantell et al., 2020). HL is a complex and multidimensional construct, focusing on cognitive, behavioral, affective, or conative dimensions (Bröder et al., 2017) to access, understand, appraise, and apply health information to promote and maintain health (Sørensen et al., 2012). According to Sørensen et al. (2012), HL includes individuals' knowledge, motivation, and competence to make judgments concerning health care, disease prevention, and health promotion. In a digitalized health care system, people require additional competencies, which are referred to as digital HL (Samerski & Müller, 2019; Schmidt-Kaehler et al., 2021; Sørensen, 2023; Sørensen et al., 2021). Norman and Skinner were the first to define electronic Health Literacy (eHL) in 2006 as the ability to seek, find, understand, and appraise health information from electronic sources and expanded the concept of HL to digital health information. Although, there is an abundance of internet-based information, an adapted definition of digital HL has not been established yet (Griebel et al., 2018; Huhta et al., 2018; Norman, 2011; Wang et al., 2021).

While HL is considered a comprehensive and context-specific approach that varies according to the topic and situation (El Benny et al., 2021), digital HL includes the individuals' digital abilities (Griebel et al., 2018; Levin-Zamir & Bertschi, 2018; Samerski & Müller, 2019). However, both literacies are closely interrelated. Digital HL or eHL is a growing research area and is referred to as a prerequisite for patients to successfully participate in digital health information and interventions (Dunn & Hazzard, 2019; Gerlach et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). Within the growing amount of health information or care options delivered digitally, eHL or digital HL has become more relevant for patients, where higher (digital) HL enables individuals to participate in a digitalised health care system, use and benefit from digital resources to improve or maintain health (Dratva et al., 2024; Kayser et al., 2018). Instead, people with lower HL are less likely to use health information tools (Mackert et al., 2016). Insufficient digital HL makes it difficult for individuals to navigate online platforms, use mobile health apps or evaluate online health information, while higher digital HL is associated with higher user engagement on digital mental health tools (Borghouts et al., 2021). Digital HL can be promoted by offering educational opportunities or reducing structural and organizational barriers (Busse et al., 2022). To enhance the current status, mental DHI should ease the use and fit users' needs and skills (Connolly et al., 2021). However, research in this field rarely focuses on (digital) HL needs of (potential) users (Emerson et al., 2021). Currently, it is unclear how (digital) HL can be promoted in practical situations from a providers' and recipients' perspective. Services which are not user-orientated can lead to poor use of DTx in routine care, even though DTx may be needed. To identify potential barriers and facilitators for considering DTx, this study aimed to contrast GPs' and patients' perspectives on DTx for depressive disorders according to the core HL dimensions.

Materials and Methods

Research Design

We chose a two-armed qualitative study design to explore GPs' (Study I) and patients' (Study II) perspectives, as little is known about digital health interventions and HL in primary care. To report the study design and the methods for data collection and analysis, we followed the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ-Guidelines).

Sample

We used purposeful sampling to achieve two heterogeneous participant groups of GPs and patients with variation of sex, gender, and geographic location. Further, the present study purposively sought a variety of participants according to experience and medical specialist education (GPs) as well as age and educational status (GPs, patients).

Recruitment

We invited GPs for general or internal medicine practicing for German statutory health insurance by email. To recruit patients with mild or moderate depression, we distributed posters and leaflets in GP offices, counselling centers, and self-help facilities. For both groups, the invitation to participate contained the topic of the study and information about the estimated interview duration. It introduced the research project as part of a research program on chronic diseases and HL (ChEG).

Data Collection

Semi-structured interviews were chosen as they allow for some degree of flexibility to capture new themes during interviews. The authors developed the interview questions, and both interview guides were pre-tested by three participants (patients and physicians). The interview guides comprised of the main topics: (1) Experience with digital health interventions; (2) Attitude toward digital health approaches for depressive disorders and (3a) Challenges of depressive disorders in ambulatory care (GPs) or (3b) Depression-specific health information seeking-behavior (patients); as well as (4) Expected benefits and risks of DTx. All interviews were conducted by the first author and carried out in German between January and June 2022 via video conference call. Data were transcribed verbatim according to Dresing and Pehl (2018). All participants declared informed consent to audio recording and scientific use of the interviews before participation. Patients received a compensation of 15 €. All identifying information is anonymized. Interviews lasted between 21 and 41 minutes.

Data Analysis

Qualitative thematic content analysis was carried out according to Kuckartz and Rädiker (2022): Based on the interview guide, a system of categories was built deductively according to HL dimensions of health information processing access, understanding, appraisal, and application and personal determinants. We contrasted patients´ and GPs' perspectives focusing on perceived benefits and risks (Table 1). The coding process resulted in a set of five main- and 21 subcategories, which were then analyzed via thematic content analysis. We used MAXQDA 2022 to technically support the coding and analysis process. The procedure and research results were regularly discussed with PhD students in a qualitative research workshop. One coder coded the material three times with at least one month between each cycle according to Kuckartz & Rädiker (2022). The results were reported according to the coding scheme (Table 2).

Results

Sample Characteristics

GPs (n = 17) and patients (n = 17) participated, of which 9 GPs and 11 patients were women. GPs had specialist training in general medicine (n = 10) or internal medicine (n = 4), were in training (n = 2) or absolved a combined specialist training (n = 1). Professional experience varied between 6 months and 27 years of clinical practice. Patients' educational backgrounds ranged from basic undergraduate school degrees (n = 4) to PhD (n = 1). Most patients reported suffering from depression for over ten years (n = 11). Other patients lived with depressive symptoms from 4 to 5 years (n = 3) or less than a year (n = 3). Findings: Comparison of GPs' and Patients' Perspectives on DTx Concerning Health Literacy Dimensions

Access. Firstly, participants discussed aspects according to access to mental health content. Overall, both groups experienced DHI as low-threshold opportunities and perceived this as a benefit: GPs saw a chance to reach target groups that rarely visit GP offices and to have a treatment offer for people with different native languages. Further advantages for patients were anonymity, flexibility, and no additional costs. Considering the lack of therapeutic treatment, GPs and patients perceived a bridging function of DTx as important to provide motivation during waiting times, to give access to health information, and support self-management skills.

“[To receive] opportunities really bridging the acute shortage we have [in mental health care]. That the patients are not completely helpless. That they have something to hold on to.” (GP17: 63)

Therefore, participants described access to (verified) health content as a chance to find professional help for specific complaints due to depression.

“I can just deal with my disease. I can look up my complaints, and then I can find help for these complaints.” (P9: 41)

Additionally, GPs found it helpful to “have something in your hand” (GP14:56) to prescribe to patients to fulfil their need for a treatment intervention.

Further, in the specific context of depression, both groups emphasized an ideal component of accessing mental health content on a low threshold due to DTx. Patients appreciate knowing there was an available tool if acute care became necessary, even if they did not currently intent to use DTx: “And this knowledge would have given me much support, and I would have been pleased about that.” (P6:56)

A further theme was access to DTx and responsibilities in routine care: Patients were worried about GPs lack of awareness on available DTx. They feared that GPs may restrict prescriptions due to a lack of knowledge or rejection of services. Hence, patients demanded greater involvement of health care organizations and pro-active recommendations, especially from physicians.

“Well, I think that doctors should also offer this, because you don't learn anything about it yourself. It should also be addressed in the context of mental problems.” (P4:70)

At the same time, GPs commented that their patients rarely ask about available offers. According to them, patients should directly address the topic during a medical consultation to receive a prescription. Further, GPs with fewer DTx experiences tended to perceive health insurers as responsible for promoting DTx to affected patients. Therefore, patients and GPs differed in understanding who is responsible for instigating DTx access.

Overall, access included various opportunities for HL promotion given as design or availability. Further DTx-access may be inhibited by a lack of knowledge or interest of health care providers.

Understanding. Both interviewed groups did not prioritize aspects related to the dimension understanding. Patients cited an appealing design as a relevant indirect factor that facilitates or hinders understanding. They emphasized that illustrations help them express their mental condition.

“Sometimes you see a picture and say: that's how I feel, exactly like that. I think that's good.” (P5:75)

GPs welcomed an opportunity to care for patients with limited German language skills in their native language. Accordingly, DTx could contribute to overcome language barriers for therapeutic content.

“Because if there is a language barrier, I don't need to recommend it [health information] to patients.” (GP15: 50)

GPs highlighted the difficult and time-consuming process to create awareness for mental health issues at an early stage of depression “to understand what's going on” (GP3:54).

“Sometimes it takes a long time to realize what's wrong. Patients realize that they're not doing well but can't see that it might be depression.” (GP16:18)

Consequently, both groups stated the advantages for understanding of DTx to achieve some basic knowledge of depression, its symptoms and self-management skills. While patients focused on visualizations to illustrate health conditions, GPs perceived opportunities for vulnerable and underserved patients to improve their understanding of mental and therapeutic content.

Appraisal. The dimension includes relevant aspects for patients and GPs to appraise DTx. Both groups perceive the quality of DTx differently: patients expect information from DTx prescribed by health professionals to be especially trustworthy. Contrastingly, GPs were skeptical about the evidence and evaluation methods and criticized DTx. Additional risks observed by GPs were the high costs for DTx and potential negative effects on the GP-patient relationship by suggesting inadequate interventions.

“If I prescribe things the patient doesn't benefit from, that's not conducive to adherence and so on. If I recommend an app that doesn't work, I'll get that back, and then [the patient] won't like the other things I offer. You must be careful about that.” (GP2:69)

General criticisms such as data safety concerns and insufficient technical equipment were mentioned by patients but referring to potential other patients rather than themselves. Moreover, both groups were concerned about further dysfunctional effects: GPs mentioned risks such as over-supply, addictive use and social isolation. Patients classified potential technical problems or malfunctions as problematic as they disturb therapy sessions. They also worried about loneliness in unstable circumstances during therapy sessions.

“It was then quite good to leave the house, especially if you were not so stable. It is a small aim, but you leave the house. With such a diagnosis, people often withdraw, isolate themselves, and break off contact with the outside.” (P10: 33)

Due to experience with long waiting times for in-person treatment, patients perceive digital interventions as an additional and available therapeutic service. They expressed interest in DTx and additional guidance during those periods. Patients' interest tended to be higher than GPs expected. Contrastingly, GPs considered that they “don't need to come up with digital interventions” (GP1:27) as their experience was that patients were not interested in them. GPs tended to compare DTx with in-person treatment provided by psychotherapists or themselves, while patients mainly assessed DTx as first contact with depression, for unguided waiting times or after specialized treatment.

“I wouldn't see this as a substitute for a real conversation with a psychologist. But to bridge the time until you have a therapy place, I imagine it would be helpful.” (P9:33)

According to the intervention and its effect on patients' health, both groups expected DTx to be less effective than face-to-face care. Appraisal of DTx seems to be linked to the available alternative offers. For patients and GPs, personal contact with a health professional or therapist is precious and generally preferred to DTx. Further GPs specified that “it needs personal accompaniment to endure such an illness” (GP9:82). Neither patients nor GPs perceived DTx as a universal solution to substitute for in-person care. GPs appraised further DTx as “one brick within a concept” (GP1:39). Still, due to a lack of current mental health care, patients appraised DTx as a potential support.

In general, patients tended to be less critical about DTx than GPs and appraised the services more positively and expected benefits from DTx. At the same time, patients were wary of the risk DTx may pose to the recovery or health status of depression.

Application. This dimension includes patients' or GPs' expectations of DTx-application in routine care for depression and its challenging or beneficial factors.

The actual (non-) use of DTx is a major issue for both groups: GPs tended to be skeptical about patients' interest in the use of DTx or whether prescriptions are suitable for patients' needs:

“It's not my future. And it's not the future of the patients either. I'm more and more sure of that. I sit with my two eyes, facing them with both eyes, and listen, and that's just not what the app does.” (GP6:30)

In general, participants expressed that the intention to use and actual use of DTx diverge. Different depression-specific circumstances such as disease awareness, disease stages, and symptoms are considered major risks for a missing application of DTx or patients “don't continue” (P16:46) with its use after registration.

“The intention is already there. The intent is in the head, but there is usually a lot of difficulty to realize it.” (P18:55)

Further, participants stressed the difficulty of making use of a DTx intervention during a phase of depression.

“When you're in a real phase, you can hardly structure your everyday life. So, I think an app like that is something you don't think about right away because you don't think about many other things either.” (P14:50)

Consequently, patients and GPs identified an unspecified usage of DTx as challenging. While patients experienced treatment sessions as emotionally challenging and felt more secure in having an in-person HP at their side, GPs were worried that patients felt overwhelmed by their symptoms while using the application:

“If the patients are without guidance in these apps, that may cause symptom worsening, if the depressed patient digs more and more into its depression. Without a doctor or an expert to talk to, that that worsens the symptoms.” (GP15:60).

Personal capacities and determinants. Further personal capacities and determinants were discussed by patients and GPs, which influence the core HL-dimensions:

GPs and patients mentioned certain cognitive skills and self-recognition as a precondition for using DTx which does not “overwhelm” (P12:54) patients. Especially for behavioral therapy approaches, a greater understanding of content seemed essential from the health professional perspective:

“You also need a certain intellect. If you want to participate in this, you need be able to read and understand a whole text, not just a few letters.” (GP7:11)

Also, a “certain degree of affinity for technology” (GP2:41) seemed necessary for patients to use DTx. Additionally, participants needed to be comfortable with digital technology and have interest in applying them. Even a patient with good technical skills was wary of technical procedures when they disrupt therapy sessions: “I have an affinity for IT and programming, but it really annoys me that you always have to make some updates, and it's never like that works properly. And that just gets me in trouble that I'd like to avoid during this therapy process.” (P7:39)

The need for basic digital skills was discussed as another central personal capacity. Even though digital skills were described as central, patients and GPs noted that many patients have adequate basal skills or that GPs may “expect a worse internet capability than patients demonstrate” (GP5:39).

From a GPs' perspective, it is also important to consider patients' condition and their social environment when recommending DTx. For example, DTx are not considered for “patients who have depression because of loneliness” (GP17:24).

According to the participants, personal determinants include cognitive and digital skills and patients' health status and needs. Prescribers and patients assess those determinants differently, which could be challenging in practical situations.

Discussion

Summary of the Main Findings

This study contrasted the views of GPs and patients regarding barriers and facilitators of DTx for people with depression according to four HL dimensions. Patients and GPs expected similar improvements on current intervention due to DTx and opportunities to promote depression-specific knowledge and competencies. The results demonstrate that physicians tend to compare DTx with in-person treatment. Conversely, the patients' perspective focuses on the challenges of receiving mental health care and experiencing waiting times. Therefore, while they were interested in finding support for unmet health care needs and improve self-management skills. However, GPs were skeptical about patients' interests and their abilities to handle (digital) health information. Media- and disease-specific factors influenced the four main HL dimensions, specifically in terms of access and understanding health information. The need for different literacies to use DTx substantially distinguished between GPs and patients: digital literacy was an essential precursor for GPs to prescribe DTx, while patients named motivational reasons (e.g., lack of adherence, skepticism about self-efficacy) and disease-specific conditions (e.g., lethargy, social isolation) relevant to themselves.

Comparison With Existing Literature

Access to health care such as DTx is an important HL-related pre-condition to manage chronic or long-term diseases like depression (Visscher et al., 2018). DHI in general and DTx specifically create new access barriers due to media use (Azzopardi-Muscat & Sørensen, 2019). Even though the participants in the present study considered their own (digital) skills as sufficient, those barriers may still cause new inequalities due to a digital divide (Cornejo Müller et al., 2020; Neter et al., 2018; Weiss et al., 2018). The results revealed different perceptions of patients' interest in DTx, especially for certain groups of patients and GPs such as non-digital natives, which causes inequalities for DTx-access. At the same time, it is known that elderly patients use DHI more intensely, e.g., by spending more time completing more sessions (Oehler, Scholze, et al., 2021) and therefore may profit from those services.

The results reveal multiple approaches of DTx to promote patients' HL due to an increased understanding of depression-specific health information. Language and its understanding are the main tools of psychotherapeutic care; therefore, limited language skills are a major barrier for many underprovided groups (Eller & Berg, 2023). Whether and in which situations DTx may be (temporary) approach to bridge deficits in routine must be examined in further research. Furthermore, an interplay between competencies and perceived improvements due to DTx use and the current situation is necessary. In our study, the participants distinguished between DTx and a current lack in standard care for depression. Especially patients as end-users appraise advantages from DTx compared to their actual situation, including elements to promote HL, e.g., promoting verified health information, fostering understanding of content, and increasing patient self-management skills. But also risks and concerns on dysfunctional effects can be contextualized by preliminary research showing that the adverse effects of web-based interventions are comparable to face-to-face psychotherapy (Oehler, Görges, et al., 2021). Our findings are in line with other results, demonstrating that physicians tend to be more critical than patients toward mHealth applications and digital innovations (Hennemann et al., 2017; Hofer & Haluza, 2019; Radić et al., 2021).

A major challenge is to apply DTx: In routine care, DHI have been rarely prescribed or recommended (Löbner et al., 2022; Weitzel et al., 2022). The interaction of responsibilities of access and appraisal of DTx can lead to challenges in practical situations. GPs tend to be cautious to prescribe DTx. Other research and concerns about DHI and their incompatibility with difficult depressive episodes also identified disease-specific challenges (Hafner et al., 2022; Weitzel et al., 2022). Lethargy and a lack of motivation are symptoms and barriers to the actual use of DTx and a general openness toward technology for mental issues is a prerequisite to apply DTx. The results indicate that depression symptoms represent a challenge for DTx-application, and hence (digital) HL may not be promoted by DTx as intended. In conclusion, this means that there is a need for strategies to counter depression-specific barriers by patients and practitioners.

Study Strengths and Limitations

Firstly, interviews were conducted in German and quotes were translated into English after data analysis. This could impact the tone of participants' quotes. In addition, the participants were informed about the study as part of a PhD project, which may have affected their responses. Secondly, participants mostly reported a long disease course and have visited a therapist within this time. Accordingly, they may not constitute the main target group for DTx. Nevertheless, these long-time experiences enabled them to assess their attitude retrospectively by challenges or experiences from their disease progression. Furthermore, people who have visited a therapist are more critical towards digital health interventions compared to those without therapeutic experience (Dunbar et al., 2018), which may have affected the sample. Thirdly, instead of member checking, the results were validated by a qualitative peer-research group during workshops. Still, this does not replace the need for more involvement of patients in planning and conducting research e.g. by collaboration during interpretation of the findings. Fourthly, due to COVID-19 restrictions, interviews were conducted via video meetings, which may disqualify participants with limited equipment or digital skills. The main strength of this study is that it is one of the first to contrast patients and GPs on the use of DTx during waiting times for further treatment. Moreover, the study contributes to the knowledge of GPs' and patients' needs to implement DTx and promote health literacy in GP facilitations.

Conclusions

DTx supply approved health information and approaches, which can foster patients' self-management skills when in-person treatment is not available. This contribution is a major benefit of DTx for HL promotion for patients with depressive disorders. Patients and GPs consider DTx differently, e.g., for trustworthiness, interest and use in routine care. In addition to digital literacy, disease-specific determinants such as depressive symptoms should be considered in practical situations to overcome expected challenges on DTx use.
