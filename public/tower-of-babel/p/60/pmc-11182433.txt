Brucellosis in Omani children: a multicenter experience over 15 years
Authors: Lamees Al Sawafi, Amal Al Tai, Mohammed Al Reesi, Mahmood Al Subhi, Muna Al Busaidi, Sanaa Al Abri, Badria Al Waili
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND: Brucellosis, a common zoonotic disease worldwide, can lead to serious complications in humans. In Oman, the disease occurs most often in the south, but is increasing in the north.OBJECTIVES: Describe brucellosis in children in the Northern Governorate in Oman.DESIGN: Retrospective, observationalSETTINGS: Hospitals in the Northern Governorate in Oman.PATIENTS AND METHODS: We collected data on the demographics, distribution, common clinical presentations, risk factors, laboratory findings, therapy, and complications of confirmed cases of brucellosis. We included all brucellosis cases up to the age of 13 years at the Child Health Department in Royal, Suhar, Al Rustaq and Nizwa Hospitals over a 15-year period.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Descriptive findings of brucellosisSAMPLE SIZE: 57 confirmed casesRESULTS: The 57 confirmed cases of Brucella had a mean age of 6 years. Most of the cases were from Dhakhiliya 21 (36.8%) and Batinah 18 (31.6%). Consumption of raw milk was documented in 75% and 41% had animal contact. From 2010, there was a marked rise of the cases with a maximum rise was noticed in 2019 with a total of 10 cases. The main clinical manifestations were fever (92%, n=52), arthritis (44%, n=25), which involved mainly knees and hips. Forty-two had bacteremia, 41% anemia, 10% osteomyelitis/septic arthritis, one case with congenital brucellosis and one case neurobrucellosis (1.8%). Forty cases had positive serology results of which 19 had positive cultures. Most cases had received cotrimoxazole and rifampin as the primary treatment regimen 22 (40%). Four patients relapsed (7%) after treatment completion.CONCLUSION: This study showed the distribution and clinical characteristics of brucellosis in different regions in the Northern Governorate in Oman. Although the numbers of cases remained low, the gradual increase is concerning for public health and preventive strategies. Further studies are needed to compare this data with the Southern region.LIMITATION: Retrospective study with small sample size.

INTRODUCTION

Human brucellosis is a zoonotic disease that is caused by the gram-negative coccobacilli bacteria, Brucella spp. The most pathogenic species to humans are Brucella melitensis, which commonly found in goats, sheep, and camels; Brucella abortus; Brucella suis and Brucella canis.1 Brucella melitensis spreads widely in Oman and is the leading cause of human brucellosis in the country.2 Consumption of unpasteurized milk or ‘raw’ milk from one of these animals is a common means of infection.

In humans, brucellosis has a wide clinical presentation, with multiorgan involvement. As a result, it can be easily misdiagnosed as another multisystemic disease. This can lead to delays in management and increases the risk of complications. Brucella spp. are intracellular bacteria that may have more tendency to relapse that other bacterial infections.3,4 Brucellosis can lead to serious complications such as meningitis, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, and rarely, pneumonitis and aortic involvement. Prolonged antibiotic therapy is required to treat brucellosis. Even though this disease is seen all over the world, the most endemic areas are the Mediterranean basin, Middle East, Central Asia, China, the Indian subcontinent, sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of Mexico and Central and South America.5

Oman has a high incidence of brucellosis, especially in Dhofar region (Southern governate).6 Human cases are mainly restricted to the Dhofar Governorate since the human brucellosis surveillance program began in 1991. The rest of the governorates of Oman have shown a consistently low incidence of the disease, but a marginal increase has been observed in recent years, peaking during the year 2016.7 In this study we highlighted the burden of the disease in children in the northern governorates, which has not been studied before to our knowledge.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Confirmed cases of pediatric brucellosis at the Child Health Department in Royal, Suhar, Al Rustaq, and Nizwa Hospitals were analysed to determine the demographic data, common clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, complications, treatment regimens and the rate of recurrence. We included all brucellosis cases up to the age of 13 years that were registered and followed up in the period between January 2006 and December 2020.

The Royal Hospital is a tertiary care institution where patients are referred from all of Oman. Suhar and Al-Rustaq Hospitals are secondary care hospitals in the Al Batinah region. The Al Batinah region has a large population, and most of the uncomplicated cases of brucellosis are managed in the regional hospitals. Nizwa Hospital is a secondary care hospital covering the Al Dakhliya region.

The diagnosis of brucellosis is based on positive cultures of Brucella spp in blood, bone marrow, cerebrospinal fluid and other body fluids or tissue samples; and/or clinical findings consistent with brucellosis with positive serology test results.

Serological testing was performed using Remel agglutinating sera for both Babortus and B melitensis. A titre of ≥1 in 80 is considered positive. Blood samples were cultured and incubated aerobically for five days using a BD BACTECTM blood culture system (Becton, Dickinson and Co., Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA). Blood cultures were further incubated further for fourteen days if requested or if brucellosis was suspected and mentioned clearly in the request form.

Microscopic examination and gram staining revealed characteristic gram-negative cocci or cocco-bacilli. Blood was inoculated onto 5% horse blood and chocolate agar plates, sealed and incubated under CO2 at 37°C. After 24 hours of incubation, the plates were examined under biosafety level three precautionary measures. Suspected colonies were reactive to catalase, oxidase, and urease tests.

The study was approved by the Centre of Studies and Research in the Ministry of Health. Confidentiality and privacy were ensured during data collection. The Epidata program was used for data entry, and statistical analysis was performed using the IBM SPSS program version 29.0. Data are presented as frequencies and percentages, while the mean and standard deviation were used for continuous variables. Relapse was defined as the presence of a positive blood culture or a recurrence of symptoms after completion of treatment.3 Congenital brucellosis was defined as a positive blood culture of Brucella spp from both the mother and the neonate, or a positive serology test in both.8

RESULTS

Of the 57 laboratory-confirmed cases of Brucella in the four included centers, 34 patients were male and 23 were female with a mean (SD) age of 6.0 (3.8) years (Table 1). Most of the cases included in this study were from Dhakhiliya (n=21, 36.8%) and Batinah (n=18, 31.6%). Most patients had a history of raw milk consumption (75%) and/or animal contacts (41%). Two cases had reported stillbirth or abortion in the animals and 19 cases (33.3%) report similar illness in the family. From 2010, there was a marked rise of the cases with a maximum rise in 2019 with a total of 10 cases (Figure 1). Fifty-two cases presented with fever and most had arthritis (43.9%) with involvement of knee or hip joint as the commonest joints involved (Table 1). Anemia was a common laboratory finding (n=20, 4.7%), whereas most of the cases had normal white cell counts in their presentation. Forty cases had positive serology tests of which 19 cases had positive blood culture. Twelve patients (21%) presented with septic arthritis and/or osteomyelitis and almost half of them had positive blood cultures for Brucella species on presentation (n=6, 10.5%). Most cases had received cotrimoxazole and rifampin as the primary treatment regimen (n=22, 40%). Combinations of cotrimoxazole, rifampin and gentamicin were administered to 13 (23.6%). Doxycycline and rifampin were used in 13 cases (24.1%) and 2 used combined therapy with gentamicin. Ciprofloxacin was added in 7 cases (12.5%). The mean duration of therapy was 78 days. One patient was diagnosed as having congenital brucellosis. The mother was symptomatic with fever and her blood culture was positive for Brucella species. She had a history of consuming homemade cheese prior to delivery. The baby was born preterm at 25 weeks of gestation. After 8 days, the baby was septic and reintubated, and the blood culture showed Brucella melitensis. The patient developed signs of necrotizing enterocolitis. He was treated with rifampin, cotrimoxazole and ciprofloxacin for 3 weeks and then continued oral cotrimoxazole and rifampin for another 4 weeks, with full recovery. Four patients experienced relapse of brucellosis after treatment with relapse rate of 7%. One case had positive blood culture on relapse and one case presented with pyomyositis on the same knee joint on relapse with negative tissue culture. In one case the relapse was attributed to noncompliance to treatment and there was no documentation of animal contact or consumption of raw milk on relapse. DISCUSSION

Approximately 500 000 cases of Brucella infection are reported worldwide yearly.8 According to the 2020 annual Ministry of Health report, 130 cases were reported in Oman, 50 cases were in age groups less than 19 years of age with over 65% <9 yearsof age.9 The majority of cases were reported in Dhofar region (85%). Between 2013 and 2017, the disease, age-specific rate per 100 000 was 42, 44, 55, 59, 31, 27, 23, and 26 for ages 0–4, 5–9, 10–14, 15–19, 20–24, 25–34, 35–44, and >45 years, respectively.10

In this study, 57 cases of laboratory confirmed brucellosis were reported in the northern region, with 56% occurring in children <6 years of age and 60% were male. These findings are comparable with Saudi Arabia reports of brucellosis in children between 6 and 12 years of age.11,12 Consumption of raw milk and animals contact remain the main risk factors in children.12 The Ministry of Health and Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries (MAF) raised the concern of increased brucellosis in human and animals in the northern governorate. MAF implemented a test and slaughter policy in some of the northern areas in 2016.7 Another report confirmed the increase of brucellosis in children throughout the same period.6 The main clinical manifestations were fever (92%) and arthritis (36%), which involved mainly the knees and hips like the findings by Pourakbari.13 Less frequent manifestations were lymph-adenopathy, hepatomegaly, and splenomegaly. In one report from Saudi Arabia, excessive sweating, bone aches, joint pain, and swelling were the main symptoms encountered.12

Congenital brucellosis is rare, and complicates approximately 2% of brucellosis in pregnancy, and is mostly associated with preterm delivery.8,14,15 It results from transplacental transfer of Brucella species from infected pregnant women to the fetus.14 It can be very difficult to diagnose this condition because it mimics other common conditions in preterm infants, for example, respiratory distress syndrome, meconium aspiration syndrome, enterocolitis-like syndrome and sepsis.14,16 If not recognised and treated promptly, congenital brucellosis carries high morbidity and mortality rates.8,14 Favorable outcomes from combined use of rifampicin and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole for the treatment of congenital brucellosis have been observed in previous case reports.8,17 In this cohort, we had one case of congenital brucellosis in a preterm baby. Although his disease course was complicated by necrotising enterocolitis, the patient recovered fully after antibiotic therapy. Gastrointestinal involvement is a manifestation of Brucella infection. However, a direct association between necrotizing enterocolitis and congenital Brucella could not be strongly hypothesised and has not been previously reported.

A diagnosis of brucellosis is made if a patient with a potential exposure and clinical features has a positive serological test with or without a positive blood culture.11,17 Although blood culture is considered the standard for diagnosis, it has a low sensitivity. Serology has a sensitivity of 65–95% and is the most frequently used method for the diagnosis.11 Most cases in our study were diagnosed by positive serology, and less than half had positive blood cultures. This finding agrees with previous reports. For example, in a retrospective study of 153 children with brucellosis, almost all patients (99.35%) showed positive serology. However, only 34% had a positive blood culture.11

Patients with brucellosis may manifest several laboratory features, but they are neither specific nor useful in making the diagnosis.11 These include anemia, leukopenia, pancytopenia, and elevated inflammatory markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP).11,17 Anemia and elevated inflammatory markers were common in our cohort. These findings are similar to the one revealed by another regional study conducted in Saudi Arabia, where leukocytosis was observed in 11.11% patients, leukopenia in 4.57%, and thrombocytopenia in 8.49%. The ESR was elevated in 66% and the CRP in 28.1%. The aspartate transaminase (AST) and alanine transaminase (ALT) levels were high in 43.79% and 9.8% of patients, respectively.11

Different antibiotic regimens are used for brucellosis in children depending on the patient's age and the presence of a focal disease. Oral doxycycline (4 mg/kg/day) and rifampicin (20 mg/kg/day) were used in those >8 years of age, while oral trimethoprim/sulfa-methoxazole (6–8 mg/kg/day) and rifampicin were used in those <8 years of age.17–20 For complicated and relapsed cases, a third agent, usually an aminoglycoside, was added.17 Doxycycline is not used in those below 8 years due to its side effects, especially tooth discoloration.17 Forty percent of our patients received rifampicin and co-trimoxazole regimens, while 24.1% were administered rifampicin with doxycycline. These findings are consistent with those one described by Qasim et al, who reported that the former regimen was the most frequently used (52.9%), followed by latter regimen (35.3%).17 Gentamicin was used (23.6%) as a third agent for children who required admission or relapse cases. The recommended duration of antibiotic therapy is 6–8 weeks.18,19 In one study, 64.7% of patients received six weeks of therapy, while only 13.8% required more than six weeks.17

The most common complications in our cohort were bacteremia (42%), anemia (41%), osteomyelitis/arthritis (10%), and one case of neurobrucellosis (1.8%). In contrast to one of the largest cohort studies in Saudi Arabia (153 cases), they reported bacteremia (34%) and neurobrucellosis (1.9%).17

Neurobrucellosis is a case of astrocytoma on a ventriculoperitoneal shunt. Blood culture showed positive results for Brucella species and serology was positive. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) results of the abdominal fluid were positive for Brucella. The patient was treated with doxycycline and rifampicin for six months. The patient's condition improved without relapse.

The relapse rate of 7% was almost the same as the rate reported from Saudi and Turkey.16–21 However, a higher relapse rate of 21% was reported from Iran.13 Four cases in our study had relapse of brucellosis after treatment. Diagnosis of relapse is challenging with brucellosis with non-specific symptoms with seropositivity of these cases. However, one patient had presented with a positive blood culture of Brucella spp. The other cases presented with septic arthritis of the same knee joint and the tissue culture was negative. The other two cases were labelled by the treating physician as a relapse with positive serology and response to treatment.

The total number of goats, sheep, cattle, and camels examined for brucellosis in the Sultanate of Oman during the years 2015 to 2019 was 10 492 animals, of which 1161 (11%) animals were serologically positive for brucellosis. Cattle, camels, sheep, and goats showed a high incidence rate (between 6.6% to 23.5%) during the years 2015 to 2019 in different Governorates of Oman. Reported cases of brucellosis among people and animals in Oman are high.²² In Oman, despite vaccination campaigns for sheep and goats with a reduced dose of Rev-1 strain, containing 1×103 colony- forming units (CFU) of B melitensis, brucellosis remains the principal cause of human and animal brucellosis with a high incidence rate. However, some results of experimental studies clearly indicated the low efficiency of the reduced-dose Rev-1 vaccine in protecting sheep and goats against brucellosis. Due to these results, it was recommended to stop vaccinating sexually mature animals with the reduced Rev-1 vaccine.22,23 Thus, it is highly recommended to use the full dose Rev-1 vaccine that contains 1×109 CFU for all female sheep and goats at 3–6 months old and female camels at 6 months of age. For cattle, it is recommended to use a full dose of the S-19 B abortus vaccine for female calves at 3–6 months age. Vaccines for humans against brucellosis are not yet available. Vaccination of goats, sheep, and camels with a full dose of the Rev-1 B melitensis vaccine will help in controlling the disease in animals, and this will lead to control of human brucellosis in Oman.22,23 However, applying hygienic measures to limit human exposure to infected animals, consuming only pasteurized milk and milk products, vaccinating animals, and the detection of brucellosis by serological tests are highly recommended as principal approaches to control brucellosis in Oman.

The major limitation in this study was the small sample size, which did not include the all the different types of livestock with the exposed cases. In addition, serological values were not included in those with positive blood culture. Relapse cases can be misdiagnosed due to non-specific symptoms and persistence of seropositivity.

Brucellosis in Oman is present in different regions with different manifestations and complications that mimic other diseases. The burden of the disease in the Northern regions remained low but is increasing with time. Continuous surveillance in these regions is needed and future study to compare the prevalence of the disease with the southern region is required.
