Comparison of the nasal complex in individuals with and without cleft lip and palate using cone-beam computed tomography
Authors: Panchanok Kaiyasuan, Rajda Chaichit, Saowaluck Limmonthol, Pipop Sutthiprapaporn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study compared nasal septum deviation (NSD), nasal deformities, and anterior nasal spine deviation (ANSD) and evaluated their relationships in patients with unilateral cleft lip and palate (UCLP), bilateral cleft lip and palate (BCLP), and no cleft, using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT).Materials and MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted in 98 individuals (43 with UCLP, 33 with BCLP, and 22 non-cleft individuals; aged 8–36 years). CBCT measurements of NSD, nasal morphology, and ANSD were obtained. Group differences and correlations among NSD, nasal deformities, and ANSD were analyzed using 1-way analysis of variance, paired t-tests, chi-square tests, and multinomial logistic regression (P<0.05).ResultsNSD was highly prevalent, affecting approximately 97% of patients with cleft lip and palate and 86% of non-cleft individuals. Cleft type was significantly related to the severity and shape classification of NSD; UCLP was associated with greater severity than BCLP, which was linked to greater severity than in non-cleft individuals. Conversely, age and sex displayed no significant relationships with NSD occurrence. Patients with UCLP exhibited the most pronounced nasal asymmetry and significantly greater ANSD, with deviation toward the non-cleft side consistently observed. In patients with BCLP, NSD and ANSD demonstrated a moderate to substantial positive correlation (r=0.47), although their deviations often occurred in opposite directions.ConclusionPatients with BCLP exhibit a comparatively symmetrical nasal appearance; however, the nasolabial area remains affected. Both NSD and ANSD contribute substantially to nasal deformities in patients with cleft lip and palate, particularly those with UCLP.Keywords: Nasal Septum, Nose Deformities, Nasal Bone, Cleft Lip, Cleft Palate

Introduction

Cleft lip and cleft palate are common craniofacial anomalies caused by incomplete fusion of the facial processes during early development, resulting in a cleft of the upper lip, alveolus, and/or palate.1 The global prevalence of cleft palate or cleft lip and palate ranges from 3.4 to 22.9 per 10,000 births.2 Cleft lip and palate can affect midfacial development, producing both skeletal and soft tissue deformities. Previous studies have evaluated key outcomes to improve treatment techniques, especially nasolabial appearance, which influences parent and patient satisfaction.3,4,5,6 In a 2015 study by Thittiwong et al.,5 patients reported the nose as having the least satisfactory appearance, followed by the lip. The nasal deformity associated with cleft lip and palate remains one of the greatest challenges for orthodontists and surgeons, causing significant functional and cosmetic problems.4 After surgery, residual anomalies usually persist in both soft and hard tissues of the nose, including depression of the nasal dome, shortening of the columella, deviation of the alar base, nasal asymmetry, and nasal septum deviation.7

Nasal septum deviation is common in individuals with clefts who present with distortions of the nasomaxillary complex.8,9,10,11 According to the Scott cartilage theory, the nasal septal cartilage functions as a growth center for the anterior skull and midface, especially during the first year of life. This cartilage influences the growth of surrounding bone and facial tissues, including the nasal floor and palatal region. Nasal septum deviation occurs when the septum is displaced from the midline.12 Deviation can affect the nasal framework and appearance; smaller nostrils and changes within the nasal cavity alter airflow, resulting in nasal obstruction, sleep apnea, frequent sneezing, nosebleeds, facial pain, and sinusitis.9,13,14 Previous studies indicate that reconstructing the nasal septum is critical for establishing a stable nasal framework when correcting nasal deformities during rhinoplasty.15,16 Moreover, substantial research has shown that nasal septum deviation can significantly affect nasal symmetry in patients with unilateral cleft lip and palate (UCLP).17

According to a literature review, nasal septum deviation affects not only the soft tissue nose and nasal framework but also the anterior nasal spine (ANS).18 The ANS is a small, prominent bony projection located at the midline between the alveolar bone and the nasal pyriform aperture.19 It serves as an attachment point for the caudal nasal septum and enhances projection of the nasal tip and upper lip.20,21 In patients with cleft lip and palate, the ANS is often displaced, hypoplastic, or absent, contributing to nasolabial asymmetry and aesthetic concerns.22 Previous studies have demonstrated that caudal septal deviation can be significantly corrected (that is, straightened and repositioned) through ANS relocation, both in non-cleft individuals23 and in patients with UCLP undergoing orthognathic surgery.18

Many studies have documented a high prevalence of nasal septum deviation among patients with cleft lip and palate, with greater deviations in those with UCLP due to the asymmetric nature of the cleft. While several investigations have evaluated the structure of the nasomaxillary region in patients with UCLP,3,5,18,24,25,29 limited evidence has specifically linked septal deviation to external nasal deformities and ANS deviation (ANSD) in patients with bilateral cleft lip and palate (BCLP). This gap restricts the evidence base for current rhinoplasty strategies and orthognathic surgery planning in this group. Understanding this relationship is important for clarifying how nasal septum deviation differs among patients with UCLP, BCLP, and no cleft, along with how nasal septum deviation and ANSD contribute to nasal deformities—critical factors in planning surgical and orthodontic interventions.8,10 The present study aimed to use cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) to compare nasal septum deviation, nasal deformities, and ANSD, as well as to evaluate the relationships among these variables, in patients with UCLP, those with BCLP, and non-cleft individuals.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study used existing CBCT images recorded at the Oral Radiology Clinic, Faculty of Dentistry, Khon Kaen University, between 2017 and 2022. The study protocol was approved by the Khon Kaen University Ethics Committee for Human Research (No. HE 662130).

The sample comprised patients with CLP (UCLP or BCLP) who underwent CBCT for orthodontic treatment, as well as individuals without clefts who received CBCT scans for various indications. Consecutive sampling was used, and only patients who met all eligibility criteria were included. For inclusion, all participants were at least 8 years old and had no syndromic conditions. Patients with cleft lip and palate had undergone primary surgical repair (cheiloplasty and palatoplasty without primary rhinoplasty) in accordance with standard cleft treatment protocols. The included non-cleft individuals had no history of orofacial clefts or other craniofacial deformities. Patients were excluded if they had undergone alveolar cleft closure, received previous orthodontic treatment, had syndromic diseases or other craniofacial deformities, or had a history of trauma, surgery, or pathology (such as cysts or tumors) in the nasomaxillary region. To protect patient privacy, all personal information was anonymized using numerical codes.

All CBCT images were acquired using a single scanner (WhiteFox; Acteon Group, Merignac, France) with the following exposure settings: field of view, 200 mm × 170 mm; tube current, 9 mA; tube voltage, 105 kV; and voxel size, 0.3 mm3. Three-dimensional (3D) reconstructions were generated using WhiteFox version 3.0. Before measurement, each image volume was oriented using 3 reference planes to standardize head position: the Frankfort horizontal plane was set parallel to the floor, the midsagittal plane was aligned vertically, and the coronal plane was set perpendicular to the midsagittal plane, thus minimizing measurement error. For soft tissue nasal measurements, the head was additionally pitched upward by 45° in the 3D rendering to clearly visualize the nasal anatomy from below.

The nasomaxillary parameters assessed included nasal septum deviation in the coronal section, ANSD in the axial section, nasal cavity width (NCW) in the coronal section, nasal bone thickness in the axial section, and soft tissue parameters. Each nasal septum was also morphologically classified using Mladina’s 7-type system (Fig. 1), as follows: type I, mild nasal septum deviation in both horizontal and vertical planes; type II: moderate deviation in the anterior vertical plane throughout its length; type III, C-shaped deviation in the posterior vertical plane at the middle turbinate level; type IV, S-shaped deviation with the anterior and posterior portions deviating to opposite sides; type V, deviation with a spur that may or may not contact the contralateral nasal wall; type VI, similar to type V but with a deep groove on the concave surface; and type VII, a combination of types II through VI.26 The severity of nasal septum deviation was categorized according to Akbay et al.27 as mild (<5.08 mm), moderate (5.09–7.36 mm), or severe (>7.38 mm).

The 11 linear and 3 angular measurements were adapted from Akay et al.,8,27 Mladina et al.,26 Veloso et al.,13 Kumjan et al.,25 You et al.,28 de Almeida et al.,29 Serifoglu et al.,30 Kayalar et al.,31 and Cao et al.17 (Figs. 2, 3, 4). All linear and angular measurements were recorded in millimeters and degrees, respectively. For bilateral features (e.g., nostril width and height), measurements were taken on both sides. All measurements were performed by a single trained examiner. To minimize measurement error and assess intra-examiner reliability, 20% of samples were randomly selected, calibrated with an expert, and re-measured after a 2-week interval. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) for continuous variables and Cohen kappa values for categorical variables demonstrated excellent reliability (ICCs: 0.85–0.99; kappa values: 0.87–0.94). Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution, with a 2-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study groups. Categorical variables (e.g., sex, cleft type, age group, presence of nasal septum deviation, deviation severity category, and deviation type) were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations. Correlation analyses were performed to explore relationships between nasal septum deviation and other nasal morphology within each group. To assess correlations between nasal septum deviation measures—that is, distance of greatest deviation (MND) and angle (NDA)—and other nasal deformity parameters, Pearson correlation coefficients were used for normally distributed data, and Spearman rank correlation coefficients were applied otherwise. The chi-square test or the Fisher exact test was used to evaluate associations between the prevalence of nasal septum deviation and binary categorical variables (e.g., age group and sex). Multinomial logistic regression was used to examine associations between nasal septum deviation and factors such as cleft type, nasal septum deviation severity, and deviation classification for dependent variables with more than 2 categories. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare continuous outcome variables across the 3 groups. If the assumption of normality was violated, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni adjustment to identify specific group differences.

In patients with UCLP, paired t-tests (or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed data) were used to compare measurements between the cleft and non-cleft sides. In patients with BCLP and in non-cleft individuals, paired comparisons between the left and right sides were performed to assess nasal asymmetry.

Results

Comparative and correlative analyses were conducted among the 98 patients who met the eligibility criteria. All patients had undergone CBCT scanning. Participants were categorized into 2 age groups: 8–17 years and ≥18 years. Cohen kappa values ranged from 0.87 to 0.94, and ICCs ranged from 0.85 to 0.99, indicating very high inter-rater agreement. A sample size calculation using G*Power 3.1.8.4 (University of Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany; α=0.05, effect size w=0.408, df=4) confirmed sufficient statistical power of 0.82.

The study population comprised 98 patients—57 male (58.2%) and 41 female (41.8%)—with a mean age of 12.85 years (standard deviation: 5.168, range: 8–36 years). Most participants (n=88, 89.8%) were under 18 years old. Based on clinical diagnosis, participants were categorized into 3 groups: 43 UCLP (43.9%), 33 BCLP (33.7%), and 22 non-cleft individuals (22.4%).

The prevalence of nasal septum deviation was 97.7% among patients with UCLP, 97.0% among those with BCLP, and 86.4% among non-cleft individuals. Age, sex, and cleft type were not significantly associated with the presence of nasal septum deviation. When deviations were categorized by severity, the largest group was mild deviation (47 of 98 participants; 47.96%). Among shape classifications, type II and III deviations were the most common (17 of 98 participants each; 17.35%). However, cleft type was significantly associated with both the severity and the shape classification of nasal septum deviation (chi-square: 49.21 and 51.48, respectively; P<0.05). Patients with UCLP were more likely to exhibit moderate or severe septal deviation and type II deviation compared to those with BCLP and non-cleft individuals (Table 2).

In general, patients with UCLP and BCLP exhibited significantly different nasal dimensions compared to non-cleft individuals (ANOVA, P<0.05 for most parameters). These parameters were categorized into unilateral and bilateral measurements.

Bilateral measurements, including MND, NDA, ANSD, AW, ABW, NTL, CV, and NA, were compared across the 3 groups. All parameters displayed significant differences in at least 1 pairwise comparison (P<0.05). Patients with BCLP had the largest values for AW and ABW, whereas non-cleft individuals showed the greatest NTL. NTL did not differ significantly between patients with UCLP and BCLP, and AW was not significantly different between patients with BCLP and non-cleft individuals. Notably, the UCLP group exhibited the greatest deviations in all bilateral measurement parameters (MND, NDA, ANSD, and CV), exceeding those of patients with BCLP and non-cleft individuals (P<0.05) (Table 3).

Patients with UCLP, who exhibit intrinsic asymmetry, displayed notable side-to-side differences in nasal morphology. The findings revealed no significant differences in nasal measurements on the cleft side between UCLP and non-cleft individuals. On the non-cleft side, however, NCW (P<0.05) and CL (P<0.05) were significantly narrower than in non-cleft individuals. Additionally, lateral nasal bone thickness (LNT) (P<0.05) was significantly greater on both cleft and non-cleft sides in patients with UCLP compared with non-cleft individuals (Table 4). When comparing cleft types, patients with UCLP had significantly narrower NCW and CL on the cleft side than patients with BCLP (P<0.05), while no significant differences were found for other measurements (Table 5). Among patients with UCLP, the non-cleft side tended to have greater values than the cleft side for certain parameters. However, NCW (P<0.05), CL (P<0.05), and NH (P<0.05) were significantly greater on the cleft side. Other parameters—such as LNT and INT—were slightly higher on the cleft side, but these differences were not statistically significant (Table 6).

Among patients with BCLP, no significant differences were found between the right and left sides, indicating a more symmetric nasal morphology (data not shown). However, compared with non-cleft individuals, patients with BCLP had a significantly narrower NCW on the right side, shorter CL, and greater LNT (P<0.05). Although patients with BCLP exhibited wider NW and shorter NH than non-cleft individuals, these differences were not statistically significant (Table 5). These findings reflect the classic nasal features of BCLP—a broad, flat nose with a short columella.

In non-cleft individuals, most nasal measurements were relatively symmetrical between the left and right sides. A small but significant difference was noted in NW, with the left nostril being slightly wider than the right on average (P<0.05)—a minor asymmetry commonly observed in the general population (data not shown).

Among patients with UCLP, significant positive correlations were found between MND and various nasal deformity parameters, including NCW (r=0.428), AW (r=0.510), ABW (r=0.476), NTL (r=0.508), CL on the cleft side (r=0.382), and LNT on the non-cleft side (r=0.421) (P<0.05). This suggests that greater nasal septum deviation in UCLP is associated with more pronounced external nasal asymmetries, such as a wider nasal base and a more protrusive or deviated nasal tip. Conversely, a significant negative correlation was observed between MND and NH on the cleft side (r=−0.403) (P<0.05) (Table 7). These findings align with clinical observations indicating that the nasal septum can influence the external nasal morphology in individuals with cleft conditions.

Discussion

Recent advances in 3D imaging have enabled more precise assessments of nasal septum deviation in individuals with and without cleft lip and palate. Although several studies have attempted to identify factors associated with nasal septum deviation in the general population, considerable controversy remains regarding its true prevalence and contributing risk factors—particularly among individuals with different cleft conditions. The present study sought to address these unresolved issues by analyzing the prevalence, severity, and associated risk factors of nasal septum deviation across relevant demographic groups.

Although nasal septum deviation is common in the general population,24,32 cleft-related distortions of the nasomaxillary complex may exacerbate the degree or complexity of septal deviation.8,9,10,11,18 In the present study, the prevalence of nasal septum deviation was extremely high across all groups. Nearly all patients with clefts exhibited a deviated septum, and even among non-cleft individuals, a large majority showed deviation. This prevalence is notably higher than many earlier reports, which vary widely, with rates ranging from 28.9% to 89.2% in the general population. It also exceeds the prevalence reported in studies of patients with cleft lip and palate, which often ranges between 60% and 70%, including 65% in Middle Eastern Asians,33 89% in Japanese, 68% in Europeans, and 34% in Americans.8 This discrepancy is likely attributable to the present study’s use of advanced 3D imaging, which enables detection of even mild deviations. Notably, the present results demonstrated that cleft type, sex, and age were not significantly associated with the presence of nasal septum deviation. The prevalence of deviation was similarly high in the UCLP and BCLP groups (approximately 97%). In other words, the type of cleft did not make a person more or less likely to have nasal septum deviation—it was nearly ubiquitous regardless of such factors. The findings align with several prior studies (e.g., Akay et al.8 and Romoozi et al.34), which likewise reported no association of septal deviation with sex or age in cleft cohorts. Nonetheless, the study by Akay et al.8 assessed nasal septum deviation exclusively in patients with UCLP and was limited by a small sample size. Because participants were still undergoing nasal development and the study lacked age stratification, its conclusions were constrained.9 In contrast, the present analysis provides more comprehensive and reliable results. Even so, no significant differences were observed between the younger (<18 years) and older (≥18 years) subgroups. This age categorization was based on the typical developmental timeline of nasal bone and soft tissue growth, which is generally completed by around 11–12 years in females and 16–17 years in males.10,35

While previous studies have suggested that nasal septum deviation tends to increase with age in patients with UCLP and BCLP11,37 and in non-cleft individuals,36 they have not shown a significant correlation between age and the severity or characteristics of the deviation. Moreover, in those studies, more than half of the sample had previously undergone alveolar bone grafting, potentially limiting the ability to detect statistically significant effects.37 In the present study, patients with a history of alveolar bone grafting were excluded, thereby reducing potential confounding and facilitating a clearer assessment of the relationship between age and nasal septum deviation in this population. These results showed that age was not significantly associated with the severity of nasal septum deviation. This suggests that a deviated septum is established early in life and remains relatively stable throughout adolescence, particularly following primary surgical repair for clefts. Hence, it may be reasonable to address severe deviation earlier rather than later, especially if it contributes to nasal obstruction or deformity, instead of assuming it will worsen over time. Consideration of septal deviation correction during primary rhinoplasty may help achieve nasal symmetry in early childhood.

Regarding cleft anatomy—particularly differences among patients with UCLP, BCLP, and non-cleft individuals—the present study found that cleft type significantly influences the severity and characteristics of nasal septum deviation. Patients with UCLP exhibited greater deviation and more complex septal morphology than the other groups, whereas patients with BCLP and non-cleft individuals generally showed mild deviation. This pattern suggests that the cleft may induce an asymmetrical growth environment that pushes or pulls the septum off-center. This finding is consistent with Starbuck et al. (2014),37 who reported that deviation toward the cleft side is typically more severe in UCLP than in BCLP. Notably, the present study included a control group (non-cleft individuals), who may exhibit different patterns of nasal appearance and septal deviation, thus addressing limitations of previous research.37,38 Overall, these data support a multifactorial explanation for nasal septum deviation in cleft lip and palate, implicating both intrinsic and extrinsic factors. A general predisposition to septal deviation may exist that is compounded by the cleft’s disruption of normal facial development. The anatomical and developmental differences inherent to UCLP and BCLP potentially contribute to variation in deviation characteristics. Clinically, these findings underscore that patients with UCLP almost universally have substantial septal deviation; surgeons should anticipate the need for septoplasty in these patients as part of secondary cleft rhinoplasty or nasal airway correction.

In this study, sex was significantly associated with nasal characteristics. Male patients with cleft lip and palate had significantly larger nasal dimensions (e.g., AW, LNT, INT, NTL) than female participants. This finding reflects normal sexual dimorphism in craniofacial features.39 It suggests that treatment plans should consider sex-specific norms for nasal shape, as the ideal aesthetic outcome may differ between male and female patients due to inherent anatomical differences. Sex did not affect the likelihood or severity of nasal septum deviation, a result consistent with several prior studies. The present results align with those of Akay et al.,8 who found no significant difference in the maximum septum deviation angle between sexes in patients with UCLP. This contrasts with the study conducted by Jiang et al.,9 which reported a significant sex difference in septal deviation angle. Although this issue remains a topic of debate, the present study is strengthened by its large sample size and the inclusion of patients with BCLP, providing more reliable and generalizable conclusions in this population.

A primary concern in patients with cleft lip and palate is nasal deformity, which can impair nasal breathing and is often a significant source of dissatisfaction with appearance.4,5 Patients with UCLP exhibited significant nasal asymmetry, whereas patients with BCLP had more symmetric nasal features (nasal septum deviation [distance and angle], ANSD, and CV). Furthermore, while bilateral measurements highlight the main effect in the nasolabial area, a significant reduction in NTL and an increase in NA were detected in patients with BCLP compared with UCLP and non-cleft individuals. When the present study compared the cleft with the non-cleft side in the same participant, asymmetry was found, including a significantly broader NCW and shorter nostril height on the cleft side than on the non-cleft side in patients with UCLP. This suggests that the non-cleft side often appears overdeveloped or displaced relative to both the cleft side and non-cleft individuals; for example, the non-cleft side typically had a wider nasal cavity and longer columella. This may indicate compensatory growth or positional changes on the side opposite the cleft. In contrast, patients with BCLP and non-cleft individuals generally displayed symmetry between sides, with both sides being similarly affected by the cleft. Notably, even individuals without clefts can exhibit minor nasal asymmetries,12 but the asymmetry in patients with UCLP far exceeds normal variation. These findings underscore the importance of addressing not only the cleft-side deformity but also compensatory changes on the non-cleft side to achieve overall facial balance.

Another key finding of the present study is the correlation between internal septal deviation and external nasal deformities. In patients with UCLP, greater septal deviation was associated with more pronounced external deformities, such as a wider alar base and a more deviated nasal tip. In contrast, no significant correlation was found between septal deviation and external nasal deformities in patients with BCLP, who largely exhibited only mild septal deviation. This supports the observation by Cao et al.17 that the degree of CV is linked to nasal septum deviation in patients with UCLP. The nasal septum and external nasal structures appear to be biomechanically connected; a deviated septum can exert force on the nasal tip and alar cartilages, contributing to nasal asymmetry. Therefore, straightening the septum may help improve certain external nasal deformities.17 Notably, a similar correlation was observed in non-cleft individuals: even mild septal deviation was associated with a tilted columella, supporting the concept proposed by Kim et al.40 that a deviated septum can contribute to a crooked nose.

The nasal septum plays a crucial role in maintaining nasal symmetry and function, while the ANS serves as skeletal support for the nasal base.4 Previous studies have shown that in patients with UCLP, the ANS typically deviates toward the non-cleft side, along with the caudal septum.18,41 These abnormalities affect midfacial projection, columellar support, and overall facial harmony. Importantly, in patients with BCLP, this study revealed a correlation between septal deviation and ANSD, confirming that both are key contributors to nasal deformities. Greater septal deviation was associated with increased ANSD, suggesting coordinated displacement within the midfacial skeleton. Notably, septal deviation tended to occur in the opposite direction to ANSD. Additionally, a strong correlation between septal deviation and ANSD was identified, suggesting a developmental linkage between septal alignment and maxillary skeletal positioning. In patients with UCLP, asymmetry was more pronounced, with both septal deviation and ANSD typically deviating toward the non-cleft side. These findings align with previous reports indicating that cleft-related deviations affect not only soft tissue but also skeletal structures.10,38 ANSD plays a crucial role in nasal base stability and may influence the outcomes of secondary surgical procedures in patients with cleft. As demonstrated in this study, greater ANSD was associated with increased nasal asymmetry, highlighting its importance in treatment planning.18 Because the ANS is a component of the maxilla, this association may reflect how septal position is influenced by the underlying skeletal base, particularly in patients with BCLP. Overall, the correlation analysis emphasizes that septal deviation is not merely an incidental finding; rather, it has meaningful relationships with surrounding nasal anatomy and should be evaluated within the broader context of the nasomaxillary complex.

For the assessment of nasal septum deviation and nasal deformities, the present use of CBCT enabled a comprehensive evaluation of both hard and soft tissue components of the nose. Traditional methods for assessing nasal deformities in patients with cleft often separate skeletal and soft tissue analyses. Direct anthropometry and 2-dimensional photography are useful tools but are limited by issues of consistency, depth perception, and reliance on participant cooperation.39,42,43 Modern 3D surface imaging (such as stereophotogrammetry or structured light scanning) offers noninvasive, accurate measurements with short scanning times; however, these modalities cannot visualize internal structures and may miss critical details in deeper areas such as the nasal septum or nasal floor.25,44,45,46 Using CBCT, it was possible to measure septal deviation, bony landmarks, and external soft tissue landmarks within a single dataset, eliminating the need to merge data from different imaging modalities. This streamlined approach reduces alignment errors and improves analytical efficiency.47 Previous studies have also demonstrated the effectiveness of CBCT for comprehensive assessment of both soft and hard tissues in the nasal region.48,49,50 Notably, however, CBCT involves radiation exposure. In the present study, all scans were clinically indicated for orthodontic diagnosis, and these existing images were utilized for the research to avoid exposing the patients to additional radiation.

This study has several limitations. First, with its retrospective cross-sectional design, it enables the identification of associations at a single time point but cannot establish causal relationships. Second, there was limited access to detailed clinical information that could influence outcomes, such as cleft severity, the timing and type of surgical interventions, and the quality of postoperative care following primary surgery. Third, the sample size was relatively small for both non-cleft individuals and the subset of adult patients with clefts. Although the sample provided adequate statistical power, a larger cohort would help further validate these findings. Future research would benefit from longitudinal studies to track the progression of nasal asymmetry and septal deviation over time, as well as to evaluate the effects of different surgical techniques on septal alignment and nasal morphology.

In conclusion, both patients with clefts and non-cleft individuals frequently exhibit nasal septum deviation. Cleft type significantly influences the severity and characteristics of this deviation, with impacts on various nasal measurements. Patients with UCLP display more pronounced deviations of the nasal septum, ANS, and columella compared to patients with BCLP and non-cleft individuals. Individuals with BCLP have a more symmetric overall nasal appearance; however, the nasolabial area is affected, including reduced NTL and an increased NA. Additionally, sex influences overall nasal structure, with male patients showing larger measurements, particularly INT, AW, and NTL.

Moreover, nasal septum deviation is significantly correlated with several external nasal measurements in patients with cleft lip and palate. In non-cleft individuals, despite milder deformities, septal deviation still correlates with specific nasal parameters, suggesting that even subtle deviations can influence nasal anatomy. Both septal deviation and ANSD are key contributors to nasal deformities in patients with cleft lip and palate, with septal and skeletal asymmetries strongly associated, particularly in patients with UCLP.
