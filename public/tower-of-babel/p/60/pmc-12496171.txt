Integrative multi-omics analysis decodes HOXC9-driven malignant transformation and metastasis in OSCC
Authors: Mengyu Xu, Zewen Sun, Dandan Wang, Guoxin Li, Wei Feng, Chunyan Qiao, Ce Shi, Qilin Liu, Peng Chen, Zhengwen An
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is a highly prevalent head and neck malignancy with a poor prognosis, often exhibiting resistance to conventional therapies. This highlights an urgent need for reliable biomarkers to facilitate early detection and effective management of recurrent or metastatic cases. Leveraging multi-omics analysis, we identified the HOX gene family as significantly overexpressed and closely associated with OSCC progression. Among these, HOXC9 was prioritized as a key regulator using machine learning algorithms. Mechanistic investigations revealed a strong correlation between HOXC9 expression and DNA hypomethylation at the CDX1 motif, which play a crucial role in regulating MMP13 expression. Single-cell RNA sequencing further elucidated the role of HOXC9 in driving OSCC malignant transformation. Clinical evidence demonstrates that HOXC9 promotes OSCC invasion and metastasis through the ITGA6/PI3K/Akt/MMP13 signaling axis. Additionally, HOXC9 expression appears to be modulated by miR-196, presenting a potential target for therapeutic intervention in OSCC.Subject areas: Integrative aspects of cell biology, Bioinformatics, Cancer, Transcriptomics

Introduction

Tumor metastasis is the most critical risk factor for cancer-related mortality, contributing significantly to reduced survival rate.1 Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is a prevalent and potentially life-threatening form of oral cancer, affecting millions of individuals worldwide.2,3 OSCC is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, with lymph node metastasis occurring in 30–60% of cases, resulting in poor prognosis and decreased survival. Despite significant advancements in OSCC research over the past few decades the underlying mechanisms driving metastasis remain inadequately explored.

The homeobox (HOX) genes encodes an evolutionarily highly conserved family of homeodomain-containing transcriptional factors essential for embryogenesis and morphogenesis.4 In humans, 39 HOX genes are organized into four clusters: HOXA, HOXB, HOXC, and HOXD.5 These clusters are located on chromosomes 7p15, 17p21, 12q13, and 2q31,6 respectively. Recent research highlights that dysregulation of HOX genes is implicated in cancer development through various mechanisms.7,8 HOX genes influence tumor progression by modulating processes such as angiogenesis, differentiation, apoptosis, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis.9,10 Aberrant expression of HOX genes has been shown to promote cancer progression through specific signaling pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin,11,12 TGF-β7, and PI3K/Akt13,14 signaling pathway which collectively affect cancer cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, metastasis, and motility.15 Notably, the PI3K/Akt pathway has been long associated with HOX gene-mediated tumor growth regulation. For instance, overexpression of HOXB7 enhances the growth and proliferation of colorectal cancer (CRC) cells through the overactivation of the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways,15 although the mechanism remain unclear. Similarly, HOXC10 promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) by activating the PI3K pathway and inducing the expression of phosphorylated PI3K and Akt.16 Overexpression of HOXB5 drives cell migration and metastasis in hepatocellular carcinoma via PI3K/Akt pathway.13 In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), HOXB5 acts as an oncogene by directly binding to the promoter region of EGFR and regulating the Akt/Wnt/β-catenin synergistic signaling axis. This regulation enhances cell proliferation, cell migration, invasion, and EMT,17 underscoring the critical role of HOX genes in cancer progression.

HOXC9 has been reported to act as oncogene and contribute to tumor progression and promote multi-chemoresistance in several types of cancer.18,19,20 Upregulation of HOXC9 positively correlated with tumor invasiveness, and immune response in lung adenocarcinoma and gastric cancer.20,21 Elevated expression of HOXC9 has been associated with OSCC22 but the mechanism remains unclear.

In this study, we leveraged multi-omics analysis to elucidate the crucial gene regulatory network involving the ITGA6/PI3K-Akt/NF-κB/HOXC9/MMP13 pathways that drives OSCC progression and metastasis. These findings provide novel insights into potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets, offering promising avenues for improving clinical intervention and advancing OSCC treatment strategies.

Results

Transcriptomics and genome-wide chromatin accessibility analysis reveals HOXC9 as a signature molecular in OSCC

To gain a comprehensive view of gene expression pattern in OSCC, we analyzed a total of 252 patient samples, including 234 tumor tissues and 18 adjacent normal tissues (ANTs), selected based on the clinical data from the TCGA-HNSCC project (Table S1). The associated RNA-seq data were retrieved using R package “TCGAbiolinks”23 for further analysis. Our results identified 1307 of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising 774 upregulated and 533 downregulated genes in OSCC (FDR p-value ≤0.05). Notably, HOX gene families emerged as the most significantly upregulated DEGs (Figures 1A and 1B). Integration with the ATAC-seq data (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/ATACseq-AWG) derived from three matched OSCC patient samples revealed that HOX clusters were enriched in accessible chromatin region, as indicated by peak calling analysis, suggesting activation of HOX genes in OSCC (Figure 1C). Using a random forest model,24 we applied a machine learning algorithm to identify HOXC9 as the most important gene among all DEGs. This finding was consistent with the comparative analysis of all HOX family genes in OSCC (Figure 1D). Immunohistochemical staining of tissues from 87 archived OSCC patient samples in our tissue biobank (Table S2), further confirmed a positive correlation between HOXC9 expression and the malignant progression of OSCC (Figure 1E). These results highlight HOXC9 as a signature molecular differentially expressed in OSCC, with potential implications for its role in disease progression.

HOXC9 binds to CDX1 motif to regulate MMP13 expression

To elucidate the molecular function of HOXC9, a highly conserved transcription factors from the homeobox family (TFs),25 we sought to identify its downstream targets. By leveraging DNA methylation datasets and matched RNA-seq data from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-HNSC), we investigated cancer-specific promoter region by integrating DNA methylation and gene expression data from tumor and ANT samples to infer multi-level cis-regulatory networks in OSCC. Firstly, we compared overall methylation levels in OSCC and ANT using the HM450K DNA methylation dataset from TCGA, revealing significant alterations in methylation levels in OSCC compared to ANT (Figure 2A). Our analysis revealed the 30,008 differentially methylated CpG sites in promoter region, including 8,577 hypermethylated and 21,451 hypomethylated sites (Figure S1). To identify target genes regulated by OSCC-specific promoters, we used the ELMER pipeline,26 linking the gene expression to promoter methylation levels. This analysis revealed 322 hypomethylated probe-gene pairs, which we expanded by considering the nearest genes (10 genes upstream/downstream) around these probes. Gene Ontology and pathway enrichment analysis indicated significant enrichment in processes such as “Integrin-binding”, “ECM-receptor interaction”, and “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway” (Figure 2B). To identify TFs regulating these target genes, we analyzed promoter probes sequences (+/− 250bp around each probe) for TF motifs enrichment. From 243 identified TFs, CDX1 emerged as the top of enriched motif (Figure 2C). Interestingly, a strong correlation was observed between HOXC9 expression and CDX1 hypomethylation levels, rather than with CDX1 gene expression itself (Figure 2D). Using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/), we found that the DNA-binding motifs of CDX1 and HOXC9 share very similar sequences (Figure S2). Molecular interaction modeling25 confirmed a strong binding affinity of HOXC9 to the CDX1 motif (Figure 2E). To experimentally validate this interaction, we performed a dual-luciferase reporter assay, confirming that HOXC9 can bind to CDX1 motif (Figure 2F). Correlating methylation levels of probes enriched in CDX1 motif with nearby gene expression identified MMP13 as the top target gene (Figure 2G and Table S3). This finding aligns with our earlier results identifying MMP13 as a key molecule in OSCC progression through machine learning algorithms (Figure 1D). MMP13 expression was further validated in clinical tissue samples, showing a positive correlation with OSCC progression (Figure 2H). Immunofluorescent staining revealed co-localization of HOXC9 and MMP13 in malignant epithelial cells of OSCC (Figure 2I), suggesting a direct regulatory role of HOXC9 in modulating MMP13 expression during OSCC progression.

HOXC9 drives normal epitheliums to malignant transformation in OSCC

Recent advances in single-cell transcriptomics evolutionally provides gene expression at the single-cell level, enabling the study of heterogeneous cell populations and reconstruct cellular development pathway.27 Leveraging this technology, we reanalyzed single-cell datasets from a previous published study28 (GSE164690), focusing specifically on HPV-negative OSCC samples. Using UMAP dimensionality reduction analysis, we identified 22 distinct clusters, including six classified as epithelial cells (Figures 3A and 3B). These epithelial populations were further categorized into malignant and non-malignant cells based on copy number variations (CNV) heatmap generated using the R package “inferCNV” (https://github.com/broadinstitute/inferCNV) (Figures 3C and 3D). Intriguingly, HOXC9 expression was found to be specifically enriched in malignant epithelial cells compared to the non-malignant cells (Figure 3E). Similarly, HOXC9 target gene MMP13 was also found highly upregulated in malignant epithelial cells, exhibiting a consistent expression pattern with HOXC9 (Figure 3E). Using pseudotime analysis,29 we observed a trajectory suggesting a transition from normal epithelial cells to malignant transformation, driven by HOXC9 (Figures 3F and 3G). Additionally, analysis of TCGA-HNSCC data revealed a correlation between HOXC9 expression and advanced stages of OSCC (Figure 3H). These findings suggest a functional role for HOXC9 in driving the malignant transformation of normal epithelial cells, contributing to OSCC development and progression.

Regulating HOXC9 expression via ITGA6/PI3K/Akt/NF-κB pathway activation

To unravel the molecular mechanism underlying aberrant regulation in OSCC, we carried out Gene Ontology (GO) analysis on the TCGA-HNSCC RNA-seq dataset (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-HNSC) to identify key biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions in development of OSCC. The analysis revealed that “extracellular matrix organization” and “extracellular structure organization” were among the top enriched processes (Figure 4A), highlighting the pivotal role of ECM remodeling in OSCC. Pathway analysis further identified the “PI3K/Akt signaling pathway” as upregulated in both KEGG (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) and WiKi (https://www.wikipathways.org/) pathway database. The “Integrin signaling pathway” was enriched in PANTHER pathway database (https://www.pantherdb.org/pathway/) (Figure 4B). These findings align with previous studies30,31 demonstrating that ECM interactions drive PI3K/Akt pathway activation in cancer. To pinpoint key genes involved in ECM interactions and PI3K/Akt pathway regulation in OSCC, we analyzed genes overlapping across these pathways and found the integrins were the most enriched. Scrutinized the expression of all the identified integrins in single-cell dataset, we found that integrin α6 (ITGA6) was highly expressed in the same epithelial cell population as HOXC9 and MMP13 (Figure 4C). Validation using tissues microarray (TMA) samples from clinical patients confirmed the upregulation of both phosphorylated Akt and ITGA6 in OSCC (Figures 4D, 4E, and S3). Immunofluorescent staining further demonstrated co-localization of phosphorylated Akt and ITGA6 in malignant epithelial cells of OSCC (Figure 4F). Notably, PI3K/Akt signaling has previously been shown to mediate HOX gene expression.13,32 To determine whether the upregulation of HOXC9 is driven by PI3K/Akt pathway activation, we treated Cal27 and SCC25 cells with the Akt phosphorylation inhibitor MK2206.33 This treatment significantly reduced phosphorylated Akt levels and led to decreased HOXC9 and MMP13 expression compared to controls (Figures 4G and S4). These findings indicate that HOXC9 expression is directly regulated by the PI3K/Akt pathway, which in turn controls MMP13 expression. Additionally, NF-κB, a downstream effector of PI3K-Akt pathway, is known to play a pivotal role in cancer progression.34 To evaluate whether NF-κB regulates HOXC9 and MMP13 expression in OSCC, we treated Cal27 and SCC25 cells with the NF-κB pathway inhibitors (BAY 11–7082).35,36 Inhibition of the NF-κB pathway significantly reduced the expression levels of HOXC9 and MMP13 (Figures 4H and S5). Together, these findings demonstrate that HOXC9 expression is regulated via the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB pathway, which, in turn controls MMP13 expression, suggesting a key role for this signaling axis in OSCC progression.

Preferentially targeting of HOXC9 by miR-196 defines a co-signature in OSCC progression

Transcriptional dysregulation drives cancer development, with its progression heavily reliant on key regulators of gene expression. Post-transcriptional regulation by microRNAs represents a critical mechanism contributing to aberrant gene expression and function. In many species, miRNAs are expressed from intergenic regions within HOX gene clusters, and these HOX cluster-embedded miRNAs are known to preferentially target HOX mRNAs.37 Our ATAC-seq data analysis (Figure 1C) revealed that miR-196 is embedded in the HOX gene cluster, particularly near the promoter region of HOXC9, suggesting a potential regulatory mechanism between miR-196 and HOXC9 expression. Further scrutiny of microRNA data from TCGA database, comprising 151 OSCC samples and 11 ANT, identified 99 differentially expressed microRNAs, of which 48 were upregulated and 51 downregulated. Among these, miR-196b and miR-196a-1 emerged as the most significantly up-regulated microRNAs (Figure 5A). These findings align with previous studies linking elevated miR-196 to poor prognosis in other squamous carcinomas.38,39 Using a random forest regression algorithm,24 we confirmed that miR-196 is the most important differentially expressed miRNA in OSCC (Figure 5B). To explore the relationship between differentially expressed miRNAs and HOXC9 mRNA levels, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated. The results revealed that a strong positive correlation between miR-196a-2, miR-196a-1, and miR-196b with HOXC9 among the top 10 identified miRNAs (Figures 5C and 5D). The expression levels of miR-196 in OSCC was further verified through a miRNAScope in situ hybridization assay (Figure 5E). To investigate the function role of miR-196 in regulating HOXC9, we employed miRNA mimics in a dual luciferase reporter assay system. Our results demonstrated that both miR-196a and miR-196b tend to upregulate HOXC9 expression (Figure 5F). However, statistical significance was observed only for miR-196b (Figure 5F), suggesting its exclusive role in modulating HOXC9 expression. Overall, our findings highlight the potential of miR-196b and HOXC9 as co-signatures for malignant transformation and prognosis of OSCC. These results suggest that the high expression of miR-196b and HOXC9 could serve as novel biomarkers and provide a basis for therapeutic strategies targeting OSCC progression.

Discussion

Over the past decade, advances in molecular profiling have significantly expanded our understanding of OSCC, paving the way for identifying predictive biomarkers and targeted therapeutic interventions. Despite these strides, the precise mechanisms underpinning OSCC progression remain unclear. HOXC9 has been implicated in OSCC,22 but its regulatory role and molecular mechanism remains underexplored. Recent developments in multi-omics technologies,40 which integrate high-throughput molecular techniques, offer an unprecedented opportunity to unravel the intricate interactions and regulatory networks that drive cancer development and progression. Through an unbiased analysis of OSCC multi-omics data from TCGA database, we uncovered a previously unrecognized gene regulatory network centered on HOXC9. Our findings suggest that HOXC9 operates as a master regulator, orchestrating epithelial malignant transformation and modulating MMP13 expression via the ITGA6/PI3K/Akt/NF-κB signaling pathway.

While HOX genes were originally recognized for their roles in embryogenesis and morphogenesis, mounting evidence links them to tumor development.41 This connection reinforces the lineage-dependency theory, which posits that processes governing cellular lineage and adaptability during development may also underpin tumorigenesis.42 HOX can function as transcriptional activators18,19 or repressors43,44 depending on tumor microenvironment. In OSCC, we identified HOXC9 as a transcriptional activator, indicating that the specific OSCC microenvironment facilities HOXC9 upregulation.

Our DNA methylation analysis revealed that CDX1 motif is highly enriched among hypomethylated regions in OSCC. While identification does not directly confirm TF binding, correlation analysis using ELMER pipeline,45,46 suggested a binding interaction between HOXC9 and CDX1. As the binding motif of CDX1 and HOXC9 have the very similar DNA sequence, we speculated that HOXC9, instead of CDX1 binds to the perceived CDX1 motif region and controls MMP13 expression. This was validated through a dual luciferase assay and immunofluorescent staining of clinical TMA samples. Using scRNA-seq technology, we mapped the precise expression landscape of HOXC9 in OSCC heterogenetic cellular components and elucidated the trajectory dynamics during malignancy transformation. Pathway analysis further revealed that the ITGA6/PI3K/Akt signaling pathway regulates HOXC9 expression, which subsequently drives MMP13 upregulation. These findings align with established evidence that ECM interaction activates PI3K/Akt pathway,47,48 contributing to HOX gene regulation in cancer.15 MMP13 plays a critical role in cancer invasion,49 angiogenesis and metastases.50,51 Its upregulation promotes tumor angiogenesis, facilitating extravasation andmetastases.52 In our cohort TMA, MMP13 expression correlated strongly with tumor grade and poor prognosis, particularly in cases where HOXC9 and MMP13 co-expressed.

Therapeutic strategies targeting HOX genes, including microRNA-based approaches53 are gaining attraction. For instance, in gastrointestinal stromal tumors, miR-196a is associated with higher tumor grade, recurrence rate, and poor survival.54 Similarly, miR-193a-3p regulates HOXC9 in bladder cancer chemoresistance.19 In OSCC, the proximity of HOXC9 and miR-196 loci and their correlated expression suggest a cooperative regulatory mechanism. Our findings propose HOXC9 and miR-196 as potential co-signature for prognosis marker and therapeutic targeting. However, the precise mechanisms governing their interaction require further investigation.

Beyond its role in transcriptional regulation, HOXC9 exerts its oncogenic effects through a complex protein interaction network. This underscores the importance of holistic approaches in designing HOX-targeted therapies.53 Machine learning further reinforced HOXC9 as a master regulator in OSCC, orchestrating a previously unknown gene network critical for OSCC progression and metastasis. Our study highlights the power of using multi-omics analysis in decoding cancer biology, offering new perspective for precision medicine in OSCC. By elucidating the HOXC9-centered regulatory network, we highlight novel avenues for therapeutic intervention, which could transform the clinical management of OSCC.

Limitations of the study

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. Although multi-omics analyses suggest that HOXC9 may promote OSCC progression through the PI3K-Akt/MMP13 axis, this represents a potential pathological association rather than a confirmed direct regulatory mechanism. Additionally, we observed a positive correlation between HOXC9 and miR-196, and preliminary dual-luciferase reporter assays indicated that miR-196b might upregulate HOXC9 expression. However, the underlying regulatory mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Currently, our findings are supported by a limited cohort of clinical samples and in vitro experiments. Future work will focus on expanding the clinical cohort and performing comprehensive in vitro and in vivo functional experiments to validate the regulatory networks involved in OSCC progression.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

Cal27, SCC25, and HEK 293T cell lines maintained in the laboratory were re-authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling and matched the expected profiles. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely tested using the MycoBlue Mycoplasma Detector (Vazyme, Cat. No. D101-01), and all results were negative.

The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium-high glucose (DMEM) (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. D5796) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (CLARK Bioscience, Cat No. FB15015) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) (HyClone, Cat No. SV30010) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. MK-2206 and BAY 11–7082 dissolved in DMSO were added to Cal27 and SCC25 cells, with MK-2206 at a final concentration of 10 μM and BAY 11–7082 at a final concentration of 5 μM. After 24h of treatment, cell protein samples were extracted for subsequent experiments. Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No. L3000015) was used for the transfection of mimics and plasmid into HEK 293T cells, according to the manufacturer’s protocol and approximately 48 h after the transfection, cells were harvested for further analysis.

Human tissue samples biobank

Investigations involving human subjects were conducted following approval from the Ethics Review Committee for Human Life Sciences and Medical Research at Jilin University Stomatological Hospital (JDKQ202366), in compliance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent has been obtained from all patients. A total of 87 clinical patient tissue sections were collected from the existing tissue bank archived at the Department of Oral Pathology, Jilin University Stomatological Hospital, and were grouped based on their pathological TNM stage. The pathological grading of the tissues was independently diagnosed by three specialist pathologists. The potential influence of sex or gender was not evaluated in this study, and thus their role remains undetermined. Patient details are listed in Table S2.

Method details

High-throughput tissue microarray (TMA)

Two TMAs of paraffin-embedded tumor specimens were constructed from 49 patient samples, consisting of 27 archived samples and 22 freshly collected samples from 2023 at the Department of Oral Pathology, School and Hospital of Stomatology, Jilin University. The archived samples included 25 cancerous and 15 ANT, while the freshly collected samples included 23 cancerous and 17 ANT. Briefly, 3mm-diameter punches were taken from a series of paraffin-embedded (donor) tissue blocks and transferred into a positionally encoded array (5 columns × 8 rows) in a recipient paraffin block. Tissue sections were sliced to a thickness of 5μm and stored at −20°C for immunostaining.

Multi-omics datasets information

Bulk data, including RNA-seq, DNA methylation and miRNA-seq on OSCC primary tumors and normal solid tissues was downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) using R package ‘TCGAbiolinks’.23 252 samples (234 oral squamous cell carcinomas and 18 normal solid tissues) in TCGA-HNSCC project were retrieved for further analysis based on clinical information ‘tissue/organ of origin’ (Table S1). The classification of clinical stage of each sample was according to American Joint Committee of Cancer (AJCC, 7th edition). Downloaded RNA-seq data included Fragments per Kilobase Million (FPKM) matrix, raw counts matrix and Transcripts Per Million (TPM) format. DNA methylation level was evaluated by Illumina Human Methylation 450 BeadChip. Downloaded DNA methylation matrix contains beta values, representing the ratio of intensity of the methylated bead type to the combined locus intensity. Samples without RNA-seq data were excluded in integration analysis of methylation and expression. For miRNA-seq data, all reads aligned to known miRNAs in miRbase V21 and UCSC were quantified as raw read counts of microRNAs. The raw was downloaded. ATAC-seq data for OSCC samples in HNSC cohort was downloaded from GDC portal (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data/publications/ATACseq-AWG). Provided bigWig files containing chromatin accessibility information were normalized by the total insertions in peaks and binned into 100-bp bins. R package ‘karyoploteR’55 was used to visualize the ATAC-seq data. Single cell dataset of OSCC was obtained from GSE164690. We selected 8 OSCC samples without HPV infection for further analysis.

DEGs analysis and machine learning algorithm

DEGs between cancers and normal tissues were analyzed by ‘TCGAbiolinks’ pipeline.23 Genes with an absolute value of log2FoldChange >2 and adjusted P-value (FDR p) < 0.05 were identified as significant DEGs. The expression levels of DEGs were first converted to log2(FPKM+1) and normalized to Z score. Clustered heatmap was drew to visualize the Z score of DEGs in each sample using R package ‘pheatmap’ (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/index.html). To judge the importance of DEGs, random forest model was constructed with normalized DEGs expression matrix using R package ‘randomForest’.24 Gene importance plot was established based on mean decrease in GINI index, a criterion used to evaluate the quality of a split in a decision tree. When constructing a decision tree, at each node, the Gini index is used to determine the best split among all possible splits based on different features. The Gini index measures the impurity of a node by calculating the probability of misclassifying a randomly chosen element in that node. In brief, a higher decrease in GINI index reveals the DEG has more importance in judging cancers from normal tissues.

Single cell analysis for OSCC

Single cell RNA sequencing data for 8 OSCC samples was normalized by sctransform56 and performed non-linear dimensional reduction (UMAP).57 Epithelial cell clusters were identified using well-established markers. The malignancy of epithelial cells was calculated based on copy number variations using R package ‘inferCNV’ (https://github.com/broadinstitute/inferCNV). To confirm the accuracy of the judgment for malignancy, cell pseudo-time trajectory was constructed by R package ‘monocle’.29 Dot plot for visualizing the distribution of HOXC9 was drew by R package ‘Seurat’.58

Integration analysis of DNA methylation and expression for OSCC

ELMER pipeline26 was conducted to combine DNA methylation and gene expression from OSCC/normal control samples to infer multi-level cis-regulatory networks. Differential Methylated CpGs (DMC) analysis was performed to identify differential methylated probes for further analysis. Probes with absolute value of beta difference between tumors and normal tissues >0.3 and adjusted p values (FDR p) < 0.05 were regarded as hyper/hypo-methylated probes. Putative target genes for these probes were identified using methylation and expression correlation. These significant probes-genes pair were then enriched to motifs. Finally, the master regulatory Transcription Factors (TF) for these motifs were identified based on the correlation between DNA methylation changes on probes and expression.

Analysis for microRNA

Differential expression analysis for microRNA was conducted with downloaded raw microRNAs count matrix using R package ‘DESeq2’.59 Differential expressed microRNA importance was ranked according to the method used for DEGs. The correlation between normalized differential expressed microRNA counts and HOXC9 count was measured by Pearson correlation coefficient (R). Top 10 absolute value of R (|R|) were considered as potential microRNA-HOXC9 regulating pairs.

Molecular docking model

The three-dimensional structure of the HOXC9 (Homo sapiens) protein was obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Using ChimeraX software, the three-dimensional model of the CDX1 motif (ACCATAAAA) was constructed. Both the HOXC9 protein and CDX1 motif structures were then uploaded to the HDOCK server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) for prediction and analysis of the protein-motif binding affinity. The model with the highest binding affinity was selected for presentation.

RNAscope for miRNA detection

The miRNA probe (sequence: 25- uagguaguuucauguuguuggg −46) was synthesized by Advanced Cell Diagnostics, ACD Bio-Techne, USA. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues were post-fixed after sample preparation according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ACD Bio-Techne, USA. Cat No. 324500). Sections were placed in an oven at 60°C for 60 min, followed by deparaffinization with xylene and 100% ethanol, and then placed in a 10% neutral formalin solution for 16 h. Subsequently, the sections were treated with hydrogen peroxide and subjected to target retrieval. Proteinase III was incubated at 40°C for 30 min miRNA probe detection was performed in a HybEZ Oven (ACD Bio-Techne) at 40°C for 2 h, following the manufacturer’s instructions (ACD Bio-Techne).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was performed on tumor sections from 87 OSCC patients. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues were sectioned at 5 μm and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval. The following primary antibodies were used in IHC:HOXC9 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No. PA5-41125, 1:100), MMP13 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No. MA5-14238, 1:100), and p-Akt (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat No. sc-514032, 1:200). All slides were scanned using Motic DSAssistant software.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, Cat No. P0096) for 25min and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Biosharp, Cat No. BS114) in PBS. After a washing step, the tissues were incubated with primary antibodies diluted with 5% BSA at 4°C overnight, followed by the secondary antibodies’ incubation at RT for one hour. The following primary and secondary antibodies were used in IF: HOXC9 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat No. sc-81100, 1:100), MMP13 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No. MA5-14238, 1:100), ITGA6 (Proteintech, Cat No. 27189-1-AP, 1:300), p-Akt (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat No. sc-514032, 1:200), anti-Cytokeratin (pan reactive) (BioLegend, Cat No. 628606, 1:300), Fluorescein (FITC)–conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) (Proteintech, Cat No. SA00003-2, 1:300), Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L/HRP antibody (Bioss, Cat No. bs-0296G-APC, 1:300), CoraLite594 – conjugated Goat Anti Mouse IgG(H + L) (Proteintech, Cat No. SA00013-3, 1:300). The tissues nuclei were stained with DAPI (Solarbio, Cat No. C0065). Fluorescent images were captured by a confocal microscope (Nikon AXR).

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Cellular proteins were extracted using lysis buffer. Samples were centrifuged at 16,000g for 20 min at 4°C and collected the supernatant. Then, protein samples were boiled at 95°C for 10 min, separated by electrophoresis on 10% polyacrylamide gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. The following primary and secondary antibodies were used in western blot:HOXC9 (Abcam, Cat No.ab50839, 1:100), MMP13 (Thermo Fisher, Cat No. MA5-14238, 1:200), p-Akt (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat No. 4060S, 1:2000), Phospho-p65 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat No. 3033S, 1:1000), β-actin (Proteintech, Cat No. 20536-1-AP, 1:2500), HRP-conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Mouse (Proteintech, Cat No. SA00001-1, 1:10000), HRP-conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Rabbit (Proteintech, Cat No. SA00001-2, 1:10000). The membranes were developed using ECL solution, followed by autoradiography. All experiments were performed independently at least three times and typical results are shown. In each sample, protein expression shown in each band was quantified after normalization to β-actin expression level. Error bars shown in the correlogram represent the standard deviation of quantified results across all experiments.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

The CDX1 motif (ACCATAAAA) was inserted into pGL3-Basic vector using the KpnI and HindIII restriction enzyme sites. The 3′UTR sequence of HOXC9 was inserted into the pmirGLO vector using the NheI and XhoI restriction enzyme sites. The miR196a/b mimics (miRNA196a: UAGGUAGUUUCAUGUUGUUGGG/miRNA196b: UAGGUAGUUUCCUGUUGUUGGG). All the plasmids and mimics were synthesized by Sangon Biotech, Shanghai. Briefly, HEK293T cells were seeded into a 24-well plate at a density of 50,000 cells per well, reaching approximately 70% confluence. 450ng HOXC9 and 50ng motif plasmids or 500ng of HOXC9 plasmid and 50nM of miR196a/b were added per well, together with blank and negative control groups, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Lipofectamine 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No. L3000015). Six hours post-transfection, the medium was replaced with P/S-free medium (90% DMEM +10% FBS). Samples were collected 72 or 48 hours post-transfection, and cells were lysed and transferred into an all-white 96-well microplate. Luciferase assay reagent was added to each well to measure luciferase luminescence, followed by the addition of Renilla luciferase assay reagent as an internal control (Yeasen, Cat No. 11402ES60).

Quantification and statistical analysis

R was used for statistical analysis. A t-test was used to compare the mean methylation level on probes between tumors and normal tissues, p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. Gene and microRNA counts used for correlation test were normalized using inverse normal transformation. For the multiclass logistic regression analysis examining the association between HOXC9 expression levels and pathological staging, we employed the ‘nnet’ package (https://www.stats.ox.ac.uk/pub/MASS4/) to compute the odds ratios (ORs) and P-values. Experimental statistical analyses were conducted with Prism v8.0 (GraphPad). All experimental statistical analysis results can be found in supplementary data.
