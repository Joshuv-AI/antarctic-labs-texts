Potential effect of cerium oxide nanoparticles against boldenone-induced oxidative stress, inflammation, and reproductive dysfunction in male albino rats
Authors: Hanaa M Hassan, Honyda S Mohammed, Nuha Anajirih, Safaa S Soliman, Fatma S Mohammed, Ahmed A Aldarmahi, Ahmed Abdeen, Shrouk Amer, Eman E ELwakeel, Heba M Abdelkareem, Ahmad F Rawan, Mustafa Shukry, Samah F Ibrahim, Majed Aldehri, Areej M Alshehri, Mostafa F Abdel-Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnabolic–androgenic steroids such as boldenone (BOL) are used in veterinary medicine to improve growth, food conversion in food-producing animals, and recently utilized by bodybuilders to increase muscle mass and enhance physical performance. Additionally, they have serious adverse effects on various organs, mostly the testicular tissues. In this context, the purpose of this study was to assess BOL-induced testicular damage and the potential protective benefits of cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeNP). Twenty-four adult male rats were randomly allocated into 4 equal groups: Control, BOL (5 mg/kg), CeNP (100 μg/kg), and a combination of BOL and CeNP-treated groups. All of the treatments were administered intraperitoneally to the rats, twice a week for 8 consecutive weeks. Hematological parameters, oxidative stress markers, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and hormonal profiles were measured using standard biochemical and immunoassay techniques, as well as histopathological changes in the testicular tissue. The results showed that BOL markedly increased testicular oxidative damage as demonstrated by a drastic elevation of lipid peroxidation marker (MDA), NO levels, with a notable decline in GSH concentration, as well as SOD and CAT enzyme activity. Additionally, inflammation has also been elicited, evidenced by elevated levels of testicular proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6, along with a marked decrease in serum levels of LH, FSH, and testosterone. Interestingly, CeNP diminished the detrimental effects of BOL on testicular tissue by reducing lipid peroxidation, inflammation, and enhancing antioxidant activity. The present findings indicated that CeNP may serve as a promising protective agent against BOL-induced reproductive damage in male rats.Keywords: Boldenone, Cerium oxide nanoparticles, Reproductive toxicity, Oxidative stress, Inflammatory cytokinesSubject terms: Biochemistry, Medical research, Physiology, Zoology

Introduction

Boldenone (BOL; 1,4-androstadiene-17b-ol-3-one) is a well-known anabolic–androgenic steroid (AAS) synthesized through the dehydrogenation of testosterone. It is predominantly produced for veterinary usage, notably for horses, to improve feed conversion efficiency and promote growth in fattening animals1. Despite being prohibited for human consumption, BOL remains illegally accessible and is widely abused by bodybuilders, athletes, and non-athletes for fitness2–5. Its anabolic effects are mediated through stimulation of protein synthesis, decreasing protein breakdown, and increasing water retention, nitrogen, and electrolytes, which consequently improve the positive nitrogen balance6,7. However, its performance-enhancing properties, accumulating evidence highlights the serious health hazards associated with BOL exposure. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has classified BOL as a potential human carcinogen, having a higher carcinogenic potential compared with other anabolic steroids8. In addition, BOL misuse has been implicated in a variety of pathological conditions, including liver impairment9, cardiovascular abnormalities1, renal damage10, and testicular disorders11. Humans may be negatively impacted by BOL directly through muscle injections or indirectly through the consumption of meat from animals treated with BOL11. Experimental investigations have demonstrated that oxidative stress is a key mechanism underlying BOL-induced toxicity. BOL exposure has been shown to increase the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and promote lipid peroxidation, leading to disruption of endogenous antioxidant defense systems in several organs, altering the antioxidant defense systems of various tissue organs in rat models11,12.

In recent years, advances in nanotechnology have introduced innovative therapeutic strategies for the diagnosis and treatment of numerous health issues13. Notably, nanoparticles exhibit distinctive physicochemical properties that enable them to mitigate cellular damage induced by free radicals14. Among these nanomaterials, cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeNP) have garnered a lot of interest for use in biological applications due to their remarkable redox-based antioxidant activity13,15,16. CeNP exert their protective effects by scavenging ROS, enhancing cellular antioxidant defenses, and mimicking the enzymatic activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) through reversible switching between reduced (Ce+3) and oxidized (Ce+4) states, depending on their double redox status17. Additionally, CeNP has been reported to improve reproductive function by increasing serum luteinizing hormone (LH) and testosterone levels, as well as enhancing sperm quality and quantity, largely attributed to their ROS-scavenging capability18.

Despite these promising biological properties, the potential chemoprotective effects of CeNP against anabolic–androgenic steroid-induced testicular damage, particularly that caused by BOL, remain largely unexplored. Thus, the current study intends to investigate the protective effect of CeNP against BOL-induced testicular toxicity in a rat model. The study evaluates hematological parameters, oxidative stress biomarkers, hormonal profiles, inflammatory responses, and histopathological alterations in testicular tissue to elucidate the potential role of CeNP in mitigating BOL-induced testicular toxicity.

Materials and methods

Molecular docking assessment

The three-dimensional structures of rats’ superoxide dismutase-1 (SOD1; P07632), SOD2 (P07895), SOD3 (Q08420), and catalase (CAT; P04762) were sourced from the UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/) database. The boldenone (13308) three-dimensional structure was retrieved from the PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database. Ligand and target proteins were prepared for the docking process using the Chimera 1.18 software, while molecular docking was done by AutoDock Vina software executed in Chimera 1.18. Finally, the molecular interactions of boldenone and target proteins were visualized using the Discovery Studio 2016 Client software.

Chemicals

The commercial form of BOL, Equigan® vial (Laboratorios Tornel Co., Mexico), was acquired. Each vial contains an oily solution (50 mg BOL/mL vehicle). Ce oxide (CeO2) nanoparticles (CeNP) were acquired from (Sigma-Aldrich in St. Louis, USA).

Characterization of Ce oxide nanoparticles

The structural properties of the synthesized CeO2 nanoparticles were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis performed on a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurements were carried out using an Alpha II Bruker FTIR spectrometer to identify the functional groups and Ce–O bonding vibrations. The thermal stability and decomposition behavior were evaluated using a NEXTA thermal analyzer through thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and differential thermal analysis (DTA). The morphology and surface characteristics of the nanoparticles were examined using a JEOL JCM-7000 scanning electron microscope (SEM).

Animals and experimental design

Twenty-four adult male albino rats (150–165 g, 8-weeks-old) were acquired from the Experimental Animal Care Center, Faculty of Medicine, Minia University, Egypt. Two weeks before the trial’s commencement, the rats were housed in a well-controlled environment with a temperature of 25 °C, a light/dark cycle of 12:12, and a humidity of 60 ± 5%, fed a standard pellet diet, and allowed free access to water.

Following acclimation, all rats were sorted into four equal groups (6 rats each); Control group, rats were given saline intraperitoneally; BOL group, animals received BOL intraperitoneally, at a dose of 5 mg/kg b.wt, twice a week19, CeNP group, rats were treated with CeNP, given intraperitoneally (100 μg/kg b.wt, twice a week)20, and the BOL + CeNP group, rats were treated with the abovementioned dose of BOL and CeNP.

All rats received treatment for 8 weeks, and within 24 h of the final treatment, Pentobarbital (100 mg/kg i.p.) was used to anesthetize each group; thereby, two separate blood samples were gathered from the inferior vena cava. The first blood sample was collected in heparinized tubes for hemogram evaluation. The second blood sample was allowed to coagulate at room temperature before being centrifuged at 2000g for 15 min. Serums were then gathered and stored at − 20 °C for further biochemical examinations. Moreover, each rat’s testicles were taken after scarification and perfused with ice-cold phosphate buffer saline (PBS). One testicle was collected and processed for oxidative stress biomarkers and proinflammatory cytokines evaluation. The other testicle was preserved in a 10% buffered formalin for further histological assessments. All animals were humanely euthanized at the study endpoint by cervical dislocation, in accordance with the approved protocol.

Hematological examination

The hematological examination, including (RBCs and WBCs count, Hb concentration, PCV %) were estimated using an automatic cell counter (H.A-Vet Clindiage, Belgium) according to Thrall et al.21.

Tissue oxidative and antioxidant biomarker assays

Testicular tissue levels of oxidative stress and antioxidant biomarkers, including malondialdehyde (MDA), nitric oxide (NO), reduced glutathione (GSH), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT), were determined using commercially available diagnostic kits (Laboratory Bio-diagnostic Co., Giza, Egypt) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. MDA was measured using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) method (Cat. No. MD2529), based on the reaction between MDA and thiobarbituric acid under acidic conditions to form a pink chromogen, which was quantified spectrophotometrically at 534 nm. Results were expressed as nmol/g tissue. NO levels were determined indirectly by measuring nitrite, a stable end product of nitric oxide metabolism, using Griess reagent (Cat. No. NO2533). The resulting azo dye was measured at 540 nm, and the results were expressed as µmol/g tissue. GSH content was assessed using the DTNB method (Cat. No. GR2511), where GSH reacts with 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) to form a yellow-colored chromophore measured at 412 nm. Results were expressed as µmol/g tissue. SOD activity was determined based on its ability to inhibit superoxide-mediated reactions (Cat. No. SD2521), and results were expressed as U/g tissue. CAT activity was measured by monitoring the decomposition rate of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at 240 nm (Cat. No. CA2517), and results were expressed as U/g tissue.

Proinflammatory cytokine and hormonal assays

Levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), were measured in testicular tissue homogenates using commercial ELISA kits (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Wuhan, China; Cat. No. E-EL-R0019 for TNF-α and E-EL-R0015 for IL-6) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Cytokine levels were expressed as pg/mg protein. In addition, serum levels of reproductive hormones, including luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and testosterone, were determined using ELISA kits (Cusabio Biotech Co., Wuhan, China; Cat. No. CSB-E12654r for LH, CSB-E06869r for FSH, and CSB-E05100r for testosterone) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Hormonal levels were expressed as mIU/mL for LH and FSH, and ng/mL for testosterone. All ELISA assays were based on the sandwich ELISA principle, in which the target analyte binds to a specific capture antibody pre-coated onto the microplate wells. A biotinylated detection antibody is subsequently added, followed by enzyme conjugate and chromogenic substrate, producing a colorimetric reaction proportional to the analyte concentration, which was measured at 450 nm. All samples were analyzed in duplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Histological investigation

Testicular tissues from all experimental groups were preserved in 10% phosphate buffered formalin solution for 24 h. Subsequently, tissues were thoroughly washed under running tap water for 10 min, then dehydrated in ascending grades of ethyl alcohol. The specimens were cut into 5-μm paraffin slices using a rotary microtome, after being cleaned in xylene and fixed in paraffin. For histological examination, serial sections that had been de-waxed were stained with hematoxylin and eosin22. The stained sections were examined under a light microscope (Olympus CX 31 microscope, Tokyo, Japan), and photomicrographs were captured using a digital camera system.

For the evaluation of histopathological alterations, the severity of lesions was scored as follows: (−) absence of the lesion = 0%, (+) mild > 0–25%, (++) moderate > 25–50%, and (+++) severe > 50–100% of the examined tissue sections23.

Statistical analyses

The variables’ normality was tested whether they were normally distributed at P > 0.05 using Shapiro–Wilk’s test. Data are presented as box-and-whisker plots with individual data points. Homogeneity of variances among groups was evaluated using Levene’s test. One-way ANOVA was used to statistically compare the various groups using GraphPad Prism software, version 10, USA. To compare groups, Duncan’s test was used as a post hoc test. P values below 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant. Multivariate analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), correlation heatmap, clustering analysis, and variable importance in projection (VIP), were conducted using RStudio software under R version 4.0.2 (specifically version 2025.05.1) on Windows.

Characterization of CeNP

Figure 2 presents the comprehensive physicochemical characterization of bare CeNP using XRD, FTIR, thermal analysis (TGA/DSC/DTA), and SEM techniques. The XRD pattern shown in Fig. 2A confirms the crystalline nature of CeO2 with characteristic diffraction peaks centered approximately at 2θ = 28.5°, 33.1°, 47.5°, 56.3°, 59.1°, and 69.4°, corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), (311), (222), and (400) crystal planes of fluorite-type cubic CeO2, respectively. The sharp diffraction peaks and the absence of secondary impurity phases indicate the successful formation of highly crystalline CeNP with phase purity. The dominant (111) reflection suggests preferential crystal orientation and agrees well with the standard fluorite cubic structure of ceria (JCPDS No. 34-0394)24. The relatively broadened diffraction peaks also imply the nanoscale crystallite dimensions of the prepared particles.

Physicochemical characterization of bare CeO2 nanoparticles: (A) X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern confirming the crystalline fluorite cubic structure of CeO2, (B) Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum showing characteristic Ce–O stretching (C) thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) profile illustrating thermal stability and weight loss behavior, (D) differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) curve, (e) differential thermal analysis (DTA) curve, and (F) scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs revealing the agglomerated nanoscale morphology of CeO2 nanoparticles at × 4000, and × 7000.

The FTIR spectrum in Fig. 2B further verifies the chemical structure of CeNP. A broad absorption band observed around 3400 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of surface hydroxyl groups and adsorbed water molecules25. The weak band near 1630 cm−1 corresponds to the bending vibration of molecular water26. More importantly, the strong absorption bands located below 700 cm−1, particularly around 500–600 cm−127, are assigned to the Ce–O stretching vibration28, confirming the formation of cerium oxide lattice bonds. The presence of surface hydroxyl groups may enhance the redox activity and surface reactivity of CeNP through the Ce3+/Ce4+ transition mechanism.

Moreover, the thermal behavior of CeNP was evaluated by TGA, DSC, and DTA analyses, as displayed in Fig. 2C–E. The TGA profile reveals a very small overall weight loss (~ 6–7%) up to 1000 °C, indicating the excellent thermal stability of the synthesized CeNP. The initial weight loss below approximately 200 °C can be attributed to the evaporation of physically adsorbed moisture and residual volatile species. The gradual weight reduction at higher temperatures may arise from the removal of surface hydroxyl groups and decomposition of trace residual organic species. The DSC and DTA curves exhibit broad thermal events without sharp decomposition peaks, further confirming the structural stability and absence of significant phase transformation within the investigated temperature range. The broad endothermic behavior observed at lower temperatures is associated with dehydration and desorption processes, whereas the relatively stable thermal response at elevated temperatures reflects the high thermal resistance of CeNP, which is an important feature for biomedical and antioxidant applications under physiological and oxidative environments.

Furthermore, the SEM micrographs shown in Fig. 2F demonstrate that the CeNP possess an agglomerated nanostructured morphology composed of irregularly shaped fine particles with porous-like clustered architecture. The observed aggregation behavior is commonly associated with the high surface energy and nanoscale dimensions of CeNP. Despite partial agglomeration, the particles remain within the nanoscale range (~ 28 nm) and provide a relatively high surface area, which can facilitate enhanced surface redox reactions and reactive oxygen species scavenging activity. Such morphological characteristics are advantageous for biological and antioxidant applications because they increase the density of active surface oxygen vacancies and improve the reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycling capability.

Histopathological alteration of the testicular tissue after treatment with BOL and/or CeNP

The histological changes in testicular tissues after BOL and/or CeNP treatment were evaluated to validate the previously acquired results. The control and CeNP-treated rats displayed a normal structure of the seminiferous tubules (normal arrangement of germ cells and Sertoli cells, the spermatogonia line the periphery of the seminiferous tubules, the primary spermatocytes are situated above the spermatogonia, and the spermatids are situated higher in the epithelium. Additionally, some Sertoli cells were observed near a normal basement membrane in seminiferous tubules. Sperms were plentiful in the lumen of the seminiferous tubules. There were several Leydig cells in the interstitial tissue, as exhibited in Fig. 6. Contrariwise, BOL-intoxicated rats exhibited abnormalities in the organization of the germ cells and a reduction in the quantity of germ cells with black nuclei and vacuolated cytoplasm. These alterations also caused the tubules to lack sperm in their lumen. There were a few Leydig cells and dilated interstitial tissue. Additionally, there were dilated, congested blood vessels and inflammatory cell infiltration. Alternatively, the concurrent exposure to BOL + CeNP showed an improvement in their epithelium. Furthermore, the interstitial tissue between the tubules was enhanced, and sperms were detected in the lumen, with the exception of a few vacuolation in the cytoplasm of some germ cells. Interestingly, the semiquantitative lesion scoring shown in Table 2 affirming that the BOL-intoxicated group exhibited the highest injury severity along all detected lesions in comparison to other treated groups. While, the CeNP cotreated BOL-injured rats indicated lesser damage than those treated with BOL alone.

Discussion

Anabolic androgenic steroids (AAS) represent a broad class of synthetic compounds that mimic the actions of male sex hormones, like testosterone and dihydrotestosterone. Despite a well-documented adverse health effect, particularly those of BOL, the abuse of AAS by athletes to improve physical performance has significantly expanded globally in recent years. In parallel, nanotechnology has become a viable method for disease investigation and treatment. In this context, the present study provides comprehensive evidence that BOL-induced testicular damage is triggered by a complicated pathogenic cascade involving oxidative stress, inflammatory reactions, and disruption of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis. Crucially, the current findings demonstrate that CeNP exerts a marked protective effect against these deleterious alterations, emphasizing its potential as a promising nanotherapeutic approach for reducing the reproductive harm caused by anabolic steroids.

The hematological disturbances associated with BOL exposure can be attributed to the erythropoietic effects of BOL. Previous studies have demonstrated that BOL enhances erythropoietin production and activates bone marrow function, resulting in increased erythrocyte mass, hemoglobin concentration, and packed cell volume29–31. Such hematological alterations may increase blood viscosity and alter tissue oxygenation dynamics, potentially contributing to systemic complications associated with prolonged AAS misuse32. In parallel, leukocytosis observed after BOL administration may represent an inflammatory or immune-mediated response to steroid-induced tissue injury, as reported previously by Urhausen et al.33 and Neamat-Allah30, who revealed a sharp rise in the total leucocytic count in response to BOL.

Accumulating evidence indicates that oxidative stress represents a major toxic mechanism underlying BOL-induced tissue damage6,34–39. While the primary cause of male infertility is still unknown, between 30 and 80% of infertility cases were shown to be caused by excessive levels of ROS40. The testis’s high lipid content and rapid O2 consumption make it a vulnerable organ to lipid peroxidation41. Overproduction of ROS and nitrogen species disrupts redox homeostasis, resulting in lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage, which collectively compromise cellular integrity and function42–44. In line with this concept, the present study demonstrated marked oxidative damage in the testicular tissue following BOL exposure, characterized by enhanced lipid peroxidation and NO, along with substantial reductions in GSH concentration, as well as enzymatic activities of SOD and CAT in the testicular tissues. These results are consistent with previously published studies6,8,11.

GSH is the main endogenous antioxidant that is widely present in all biological systems because it scavenges ROS directly, acting as a nonenzymatic antioxidant45,46. The first line of defense against free radicals is provided by endogenous enzymes SOD and CAT. The superoxide radical anion is dismutated to H2O2 and O2 by SOD, whereas H2O2 is broken down to O2 and H2O by CAT47. In addition to the harmful effects of ROS, MDA itself can cause substantial cellular damage by drastically changing the DNA integrity, cellular proteins, and mitochondrial membrane potential46. Moreover, free radicals like nitric oxide can combine with superoxide to generate peroxynitrite (ONOO−), which can seriously harm the tissues6. Surprisingly, iNOS increases the production of NO and toxic peroxy-nitrite species, which intensifies necroptosis and inflammation46,48. Collectively, these oxidative mechanisms may contribute to impaired testicular function and infertility49.

Oxidative stress is also closely linked to inflammatory activation. Increased intracellular signaling cascades that boost proinflammatory gene expression and cytokine release can be triggered by elevated ROS levels, resulting in long-lasting inflammatory reactions50. In the current investigation, elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-6 suggest activation of inflammatory pathways following BOL exposure. In the pathophysiology of inflammation, TNF-α is the most significant and early inflammatory mediator51. TNF-α and NF-κB/p65 pathways activate proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, and adhesion molecules, which promote leukocyte recruitment52. Histopathological evidence of inflammatory cell infiltration further supports the involvement of inflammation in BOL-induced testicular injury. Consistent with these findings, several studies have previously reported an increase in the expression of several inflammation-related genes following BOL exposure35,53,54.

Disruption of the HPG axis represents another critical consequence of BOL exposure. Remarkably, it is well known that any disruption in the HPG axis, which is primarily associated with male and female reproductive efficiency, may result in decreased or total infertility34,55. The observed suppression of LH, FSH, and testosterone reflects impaired endocrine regulation of testicular function. Testosterone is essential for the ultimate maturation of sperm and is also controlled by LH, which is secreted from the anterior pituitary gland. Meanwhile, the preservation of testicular gametogenic activity depends on FSH. The reduction of reproductive hormones in our study may be explained by the severe damage caused to Leydig and Sertoli cells by higher production of free radicals and oxidative stress caused by BOL, as one of the potential pathways for the damage of Leydig cells triggered by anabolic–androgenic steroids. Lipid peroxidation of Leydig cell mitochondrial membranes, which are the main location for testosterone biosynthesis, prevents cholesterol from moving to the inner mitochondrial membrane, hence suppressing the steroidogenesis process56,57. Additionally, excessive ROS may disrupt the hypothalamic release of gonadotrophic hormones, such as LH and FSH, by crossing the blood–brain barrier58. These mechanisms are supported by previous reports demonstrating significant reductions in LH, FSH, and testosterone following BOL exposure6,11,59. In the same data frame, these results are consistent with those of Mohammed et al.60 and Oda and El‐Ashmawy61, who revealed that BOL injections significantly decreased the level of testosterone hormone. Histological alterations observed in the present study further corroborate the association between endocrine disruption and testicular structural damage.

Notably, CeNP treatment markedly attenuated BOL-induced testicular injury by modulating hematological parameters, restoring redox balance, suppressing inflammatory responses, and improving reproductive hormone levels. The antioxidant activity of CeNP is mainly attributed to its unique ability to undergo reversible redox cycling between Ce3+ and Ce4⁺ oxidation states15. This dynamic surface chemistry allows CeNP to function as a regenerative antioxidant62. Specifically, Ce3+ sites can donate electrons to neutralize superoxide radicals (O·−), mimicking superoxide dismutase activity, whereas Ce4+ facilitates the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), resembling catalase-like activity62. Importantly, this redox cycling is self-renewing, enabling sustained antioxidant activity without rapid depletion, unlike conventional antioxidants15. These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting improved antioxidant status and reproductive hormone levels following CeNP supplementation18,63. Restoration of redox balance by antioxidative agents, such as CeNP, may therefore indirectly preserve HPG axis integrity by protecting hypothalamic and pituitary cells from ROS-mediated dysfunction. In addition to their antioxidant effects, CeNP exhibited pronounced anti-inflammatory properties, as evidenced by reduced levels of TNF-α and IL-6 and diminished inflammatory cell infiltration in testicular tissue. These observations align with previous reports showing that CeNP may reduce apoptotic cell death, monocyte/macrophage infiltration, and proinflammatory cytokine production, including TNF-α and IL-664. Therefore, collectively, the protective effects of CeNP on testicular tissue may be attributed to their dual role in restoring redox balance and suppressing inflammation, which in turn preserves the integrity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis and improves hormonal regulation.

Interestingly, considering all of the previously mentioned findings, multivariate statistical analysis was conducted using PCA, correlation and clustering heatmaps, volcano plots, and VIP scores to evaluate the degree of testicular damage brought on by BOL exposure. All of the previously obtained data were supported by the correlation heatmap, which verified a significant appropriate correlation between all parameters under study. The clustering heatmap of the same data demonstrated the differences in each variable’s concentration levels across the various treatment groups. Rats exposed to BOL had a higher probability of exhibiting more testicular damage, according to the heatmap. Furthermore, the VIP scores demonstrated that the main modulators of BOL-induced testicular damage were oxidative stress and inflammation. According to the VIP scores, the most important variables in response to BOL and/or CeNP treatments were NO, MDA, WBCs, PCV, Hb, TNF-α, and IL-6. Conclusions

Based on the current findings, BOL-induced testicular injury is driven by interconnected oxidative, inflammatory, and endocrine disruption mechanisms. Pretreatment with CeNP appears to attenuate these pathological mechanisms, highlighting their potential protective role against anabolic steroid-induced reproductive toxicity. Despite these promising findings, the current study is limited by the use of a single BOL dose and treatment duration in a preclinical animal model. In addition, direct fertility-related endpoints, such as sperm parameters and reproductive performance, were not evaluated. Future research is advised to study the dose–response relationships, long-term fertility outcomes to further elucidate CeNP-mediated protection and its potential translational relevance.
