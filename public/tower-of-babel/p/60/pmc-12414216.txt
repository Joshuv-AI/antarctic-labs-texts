Conference Proceedings for the 2024 Design for Implementation: The Future of Trauma Research and Clinical Guidance Conference Series
Authors: Danielle J Wilson, Jaclyn A Gellings, Gabriela Zavala, Andrew Steiner, Bethany M Kwan, Pamela J Bixby, Michelle A Price, Cynthia Lizette Villarreal, Ashley N Moreno, Lacey N LaGrone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic injury is the leading cause of death for individuals aged 1–45 in the USA. Variations in patient management based on geographic locations, community resources, and provider characteristics contribute to disparities in patient outcomes. It is estimated that 20,000 Americans lives could be saved yearly if all trauma centers performed as well as the highest-performing center, which is achievable, in part, through the reduction of inappropriate practice variation. Trauma clinical guidance currently lacks standardization, is redundant, and remains difficult to access and implement at the bedside. To explore and address these issues, the Design for Implementation: The Future of Trauma Research and Clinical Guidance (DFI) Conference Series was developed. This conference series and complementary research aims to redesign the development, dissemination, and implementation of trauma clinical guidance with a focus on more effective and equitable systems. To do so, key community partners were convened to present clinical guidance best practices, including digital integration, resource stratification, and patient-centeredness. Conference attendees, which included partners from various provider groups, patients, dissemination and implementation scientists, public health experts, government agencies, and software developers, worked together to create a “minimum viable product” which specified key objectives for an ideal future state of trauma clinical guidance, as well as the “risk”, “audience”, and “key performance indicators”. The next conference in the series will take place in February 2025, with a focus on translating the conceptualized priorities into tangible solutions. This paper serves to share the events from the 2024 conference proceedings.Keywords: Research, practice guideline, Emergency Medical Services, patient-centered care

Introduction

The first Design for Implementation: The Future of Trauma Research and Clinical Guidance (DFI) Conference Series was held at the American College of Surgeons (ACS) in Chicago, Illinois, on February 21–22, 2024. This conference series aims to improve the development and dissemination of trauma clinical guidance (including guidelines, algorithms, protocols, etc). It was designed in collaboration with the Coalition for National Trauma Research (CNTR) and supported in part by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). Currently, trauma clinical guidance is siloed within different organizations and lacks standardization, creating barriers for providers to enact evidence-based care. This ultimately contributes to disparities in patient outcomes and preventable deaths. Multidisciplinary community partners united to define the problem, formulate solutions, and work toward creating a framework for efficient and equitable development, dissemination, and implementation of clinical guidance to improve outcomes.

To accomplish these goals, 72 in-person and 35 virtual attendees were convened, including 36 presenters ranging from trauma surgeons and emergency physicians, nursing and anesthesia providers, patients, dissemination and implementation (D&I) scientists, public health experts, software developers, and others with unique expertise to develop innovative solutions. This diverse group also included providers from urban, rural, global, and military practice settings.

The DFI series cultivated a shared understanding on day 1 by exploring rural physician and patient perspectives, understanding best practices, and proposing future directions (table 1). After introductory lectures, breakout sessions allowed participants to describe barriers to guidance implementation for various end-user groups and envision ideal solutions. Day 2 focused on D&I science and technological advancements to facilitate broad implementation (table 2). The conference culminated in a “minimum viable product” (MVP), which defined key objectives, audience, risk, and key performance indicators of an envisioned end-product. Session recordings and presenter slides are available at the conference website (bit.ly/DesignImplement).

Day 2 sessions—February 22, 2024

Defining the problem

The conference opened with Dr Eileen Bulger, Medical Director for Trauma Programs at the ACS, introducing the problem attendees were gathered to address. Traumatic injury, the number one cause of death for individuals aged 1–45 years in the USA, has risen to 99% between 1992 and 2021.1 Treatment of injured patients varies widely depending on geographic location, community resources, and provider characteristics. Additionally, outcome disparities exist for uninsured, non-white, rural, and low-income urban patients, who are disproportionately affected by trauma.2 Mortality and disparities can be decreased if all hospitals perform at the level of the highest-performing centers, and clinical guidance can help achieve this.3 4 Dr Elliott Haut, past-president of the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma, echoed this, noting that guidance reduces inappropriate practice variation, speeds bench-to-bedside research translation, improves patient care, reduces disparities, and decreases cost.5

The problem, Dr Bulger described, is that “a lot of societies have created guidelines, algorithms, best practices, and protocols […] but are we working together?” She noted several other guidance challenges, including variable update processes, poor bedside readiness, and absent active dissemination. She proposed we BRIDGE the gap by Building collaboration, Regularly updating guidelines, including Implementation research and DEI (diversity, equity, and inclusion) best practices, and focusing on Guideline accessibility and the End-user experience.

Dr Lacey LaGrone, conference principal investigator, then gave an overview of the DFI series, describing how to tackle the “redesign of an ideal future state for trauma clinical guidance” to limit human suffering. This first conference serves to identify needs and functions unmet by current guidance, allowing for the incorporation of solutions into the end-product. Future conferences will focus on designing a new system and, ultimately, on sustainability. She challenged attendees to voice their opinions to create a system prioritizing user needs and patient outcomes.

The opening session concluded with the keynote by Arnold Wynn, a trauma survivor and patient advocacy representative, who relayed how to keep humanity in the business of medicine. He described unique aspects of the patient-physician relationship that foster comfort and reported the importance of his care team embracing his culture, which he later found was supported in the trauma literature.6 Despite being a black man cared for in a hospital that served mainly white patients, his providers acknowledged his culture through music, which he credits, in part, to his favorable recovery. He urged participants to keep in mind the humanity of medicine as these new tools are developed.

Diversity, equity, and inclusion sessions

Dr Vanessa Ho, Chair of the Equity, Diversity, and Inclusion Committee at CNTR, kicked-off the first DEI session by detailing how disadvantaged individuals experience barriers to accessing care, inferior treatment, and greater financial burdens.2 7 To promote equitable improvement in clinical guidance, Dr Ho presented a roadmap developed at the Summit on the Advancement for Focused Equity Research in Trauma (SAFER-Trauma) which emphasized studying all injury care phases, utilizing standard methodologies, including diverse study populations and researchers, and leveraging an economic argument for equitable system improvement.8 Dr Rochelle Dicker, Founding Director of the University of California San Francisco Center for Global Surgical Studies, then introduced the concept of trauma-informed care which improves patient experiences and outcomes through empathetic, trustworthy, and transparent communication, peer support, mutual respect, survivor empowerment, and heightened awareness of biases.9 Utilizing these patient-centric concepts when developing and implementing clinical guidance will improve the equitable delivery of care.

Dr Cherisse Berry, ACS Diversity Pillar Lead, described how historical policies, structural racism, and health inequity intersect and negatively impact health outcomes.10–12 To address this, health-equity standards must be identified and integrated into all aspects of patient care. Utilizing the Joint Commission’s certification domains, specific goals were developed including financial resource allocation to reduce disparities, program-level accountability for community collaboration, collection of self-reported data on race, sexual orientation, and preferred language, promoting workforce diversity, and capturing health-equity metrics.13 Dr Berry called for trauma-specific health-equity standards integration into clinical guidance, research, and ACS verification standards.

The final DEI sessions included panels of two key groups that were the focus for increased inclusivity: rural providers and trauma patients. Rural providers, for whom trauma represents a small proportion of their overall practice, have unique insights into guidance availability and usability. They proposed new guidance dissemination through existing statewide trauma networks or standardized trainings such as Advanced Trauma Life Support and emphasized the importance of guidance standardization for ease-of-use. Additionally, consideration of resource limitations and support for patient transfer to a higher level of care were deemed necessary for implementation. The Injury Research Engagement Project (I-REP) patient panel expressed that compassionate engagement and open communication are paramount. The role of law enforcement in the hospital after a traumatic injury and patient engagement in physical and emotional support during recovery were considered valuable patient-centered topics. The panelists encouraged continued involvement of patients and their families in research, clinical guidance, and improvement programs.

Clinical guidance best practices from leaders in the field

Dr Halinder Mangat, Brain Trauma Foundation (BTF) Research Director, opened by reporting that only 20% of high-level research evidence becomes practice, with a 17-year temporal delay.14 Furthermore, one study concluded: “The deficits we have identified in adherence to recommended processes for basic care pose serious threats to the health of the American Public,” capturing how poor implementation impacts patients.15 The BTF Implementation of Evidence Model combats these issues through planning, monitoring, and evaluation phases, centered around collaborator needs. Additionally, a consensus-based Severe Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) Algorithm, which succinctly outlines tiered interventions, has been downloaded over 75,000 times in 4 years, highlighting the effectiveness of user-friendly tools.16

Key lessons from our civilian and military partners were shared by Dr Kathryn Collins, Senior Physician Editor of UpToDate, and Col Stacy Shackelford, MD, Trauma Medical Director for the US Defense Health Agency. UpToDate is a leader in clinical guidance due to the comprehensive breadth of topics available that undergo rigorous and continuous review. The easy-to-navigate resource uses focused clinical questions and graphic aids to enhance readability and clearly references evidence for each recommendation. UpToDate is available online or as a download, increasing global accessibility, and diverse viewpoints on each topic enhance implementability. Col Shackelford described the “learning trauma system” whereby a single system can document and analyze patient care, implement improvements, and create guidelines based on the results. When crafting guidelines, the military focuses on care roles to ensure applicability to first responders, definitive care units, and providers in between. Additionally, partnerships with data and implementation scientists are vital to automate electronic health record (EHR) data collection and subsequently translate this data into a user-friendly interface.

After many engaging sessions, a clinical interlude by Sabrina Vogler, Certified Professional Coach, allowed attendees to pause and identify their own needs. Burnout, which produces emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, affects 61% of trauma surgeons, and caring for injured patients often creates moral distress.17 Improving the efficacy and efficiency of current systems will reduce patient suffering and concurrently improve provider distress; however, achieving this goal requires diverse groups to work together. Employing compassion for one another’s experiences increases motivation for change and promotes success.

Mary Nix, Deputy Director in the Division of Practice Improvement at AHRQ, provided the session capstone by sharing the National Guideline Clearinghouse (NGC) storyline. The NGC, developed by AHRQ, served as an online repository for evidence-based guidelines and provided an excellent example of respect for the guideline developers’ intellectual property, adherence to strict scientific standards, and open access. However, the 4–6 month delay for processing and dissemination made the goal of living guidelines, which are continuously updated, unattainable. A new system focusing on end-user needs and program utilization metrics is being developed. Additionally, Ms Nix envisions that clinical guidance “implementability” will be a key metric.18 This speaker’s vast experience engaged attendees in a robust discussion on guideline requirements and barriers after her talk.

Digital integration sessions

Maria Michaels, Public Health Advisor at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, described the “Adapting Clinical Guidelines for the Digital Age” initiative, which aims to narrow the gap between current and optimal healthcare.19 “Waterfall” guideline development, which follows a linear path through research, development, and dissemination, is prohibitively slow, needlessly redundant, and susceptible to misinterpretation. Computable guidelines provide consistent implementation by integrating guidance into EHR workflows or clinical measures. Additionally, Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) is an international standard representing knowledge in a computable form.20 These advancements from written to computable guidelines are akin to progressing from print to executable digital maps and provide striking improvements in guidance implementation.

Further demonstrating digital integration benefits, Dr Christopher Tignanelli, Associate Dean for Data Science at the University of Minnesota, outlined his research in artificial intelligence (AI) decision support. Employing AI in learning health systems creates an evidence-to-guidance process that is more efficient, computational, and interoperable by providing real-time, personalized clinical recommendations based on patient data. Additionally, the system collects and aggregates this data to generate predictive care models and executable recommendations that can be expeditiously implemented. This model can ultimately integrate computational decisional support throughout medicine.

Dr Andres Rubiano, Neurosurgery Professor at La Universidad El Bosque in Colombia, closed the session by describing how guidance recommendations are prohibitively selective and fail to adapt to diverse clinical environments. He demonstrated this by listing numerous consensus algorithms for TBI management, none of which apply to all Colombian hospitals. Therefore, a multilayer TBI clinical guideline, BootStrap, was created, which gives tiered recommendations based on resource availability and phases of care: prehospital, emergency room, surgery, and postoperative.21 This allows providers to tailor the recommendations to their clinical situation and supports wide dissemination. BootStrap demonstrates the feasibility and necessity of including resource context in clinical guidance.

Applying dissemination and implementation science to clinical guidance

Successful implementation of research findings requires integration into clinical policy, clinician buy-in, and satisfactory delivery systems. Without these considerations, the research impact is markedly diminished. Dr Brian Mittman, Senior Scientist at the US Department of Veterans Affairs Center for Healthcare Innovation, Implementation, and Policy, defined implementation science as “the study of methods to promote the systematic uptake of research findings and other evidence-based practices into routine practice […] to improve the quality and effectiveness of health services”.22 Healthcare interventions require numerous perspectives from researchers, patients, health professionals, and external partners as each offers unique insights into implementation barriers. Moreover, effective implementation requires research on policy, care delivery, and practice improvement to ensure practice change.

Next, Dr Bethany Kwan, Director of D&I Research for the Colorado Clinical & Translational Science Institute, described barriers to dissemination, including poor fit between the innovation and context for use and lack of incentivization for active dissemination. To mitigate these, Dr Kwan shared a four-phase fit-to-context framework to design for dissemination and sustainability, which ensures culturally appropriate, feasible, equitable, and impactful research products.23 The conceptualization phase establishes demand for innovation while evaluating the capacity for change. The design phase follows, where research products are co-designed with key partners to be feasible, equitable, and acceptable in real-world contexts. The dissemination phase, which enhances the capacity for adoption and sustainment, aims to provide broad awareness and equitable access to innovation among the intended audience. The final phase demonstrates the impact of a research product by measuring adoption and health-equity impact. Dr Kwan encourages researchers to engage all involved parties to ensure the product design, dissemination, and sustainability plan aligns with the audience’s needs and perspectives.

Dr Rinad Beidas, Chair of Medical Social Sciences at Northwestern University, described how her interests in behavioral economics and implementation science culminated in an observational study highlighting the time required to implement clinical change.24 Historically, increased education was believed to drive behavioral change, but behavioral economics, which designs environments to elicit predictable, desired behavior, refutes this. The EAST framework for behavioral economics considers ideal interventions Easy for participants, Attractive and eye-catching, Social through peer-networks, and Timely. Examples of these principles include brightly painting a staircase to encourage use or changing EHR default settings. Developing trauma clinical guidance with dissemination, implementation, and behavioral economic concepts is imperative to drive evidence-based care.

The session capstone was provided by Dr Jonathan Purtle, Director of Policy Research at New York University’s Global Center for Implementation Science, who described dissemination simply as widespread communication. He outlined a three-stage approach for dissemination research, starting with formative audience research, which studies the attitudes, awareness, and adoption of an intervention, along with preferences for receiving intervention information.25 Audience segmentation research then identifies subgroups within the target cohort to tailor dissemination efforts. Lastly, dissemination effectiveness research determines the most practical dissemination strategy for a specified outcome (eg, changes in attitudes, behaviors, etc). Utilizing these data-driven techniques ultimately improves the communication of evidence.

Utilizing big data and partnerships to improve implementation

Dr Scott Sagraves, Chair of the ACS Verification Review and Consultation Program (VRC), explained the role of quality programs such as the VRC and Trauma Quality Improvement Program (TQIP) to improve outcomes through benchmarking. High-performing centers have less variability in the care of injured patients, in part through utilization of trauma clinical guidance. During the verification process, centers must now provide the clinical guidance they implement and the VRC identifies adherence to it.26 These efforts promote high-quality and consistent care for injured patients.

A novel approach for clinical guidance development was shared by Dr Nate Jacobs, CEO of Flashpub.io. Dr Jacobs has expertise in rapid research communication workflows through micro-publications, which communicate the smallest possible unit of research while maintaining quality standards. The challenges inherent to this form of research include an increased volume of publications and loss of the conventional research narrative. To address this, Dr Jacobs developed a Quorum framework that collects and displays data as a ‘dashboard.’ These concepts can be applied to clinical guidance to identify gaps in primary research and collect preliminary data from multiple sources when formal trials are unavailable. Additionally, this framework promotes the collaboration of all experts on a topic to ensure diverse applicability.

Ashley Farley, Program Officer of Knowledge & Research Services at the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, spearheads the foundation’s Open Access Policy. Research is traditionally funded by taxpayers or private initiatives, not by for-profit publishers who also do not pay authors or reviewers. This results in “needless gatekeeping”. Additionally, current systems hinder the sharing of diverse viewpoints and create barriers to reading, publishing, and engaging in research. Ms Farley emphasized that open access does not equate to expensive publishing, low-quality, or lack of peer-review. The foundational concepts of open access to improve research dissemination and DEI efforts align with the mission to improve trauma clinical guidance.

Closing the conference, Dr Erik Van Eaton, National Institutes of Health (NIH) award recipient for biomedical health informatics, and Mark Buchner and Neil Francoeur, co-founders of Tactuum, shared industry and institutional success stories. Dr Van Eaton described his clinical experiences trying to access guidance for patient care which uncovered knowledge gaps, difficult-to-navigate resources, and lack of peer-review or standardization. Through a pilot study, he determined that guidelines must be easily accessible, clinically actionable, and delivered through a robust IT platform. Mr Buchner then explained how Tactuum developed the digital platform Quris, which makes guidelines, policies, and protocols actionable at the bedside. Clinical decision support ranges from narrative guidelines to decision support integrated into patient-care workflows. Mr Francoeur described that moving from narrative to computable guidance requires time, effort, and resources but is achievable with robust technology, standardization to allow uniform guideline display and integration, multinational collaboration, and open data sharing. Digital platforms such as Tactuum improve provider productivity and ensure key resources are always available, concepts vital for the future of trauma clinical guidance.

Conclusion

The 2024 Design for Implementation conference series shared clinical guidance best practices from experts and demonstrated how novel technological and strategic innovations can advance guidance development and implementation. Attendees shared their unique and valuable experiences to develop a minimum viable product (MVP) with objectives including guidance stratified by resource environment, patient characteristics, and community context emphasizing compassionate patient experiences. This MVP will guide the mission to limit human suffering through clinical guidance enhancement. The next series in the conference will be held in February 2025 and focus on designing the solution.
