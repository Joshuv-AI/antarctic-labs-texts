A pH-responsive mesoporous Zn-MOF nanocarrier for efficient cisplatin delivery and inhibition of A549 cancer cells
Authors: Xiaomei Ye, Shuzhen Lu, Hao Peng, Zhirong Huang, Chunyu Pan, Yayan Huang, Kangrui Yuan, Youfa Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mesoporous metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have the advantages of high specific surface area, tunable pore size, and favorable biocompatibility, making them promising candidates for drug delivery in cancer therapy. In this study, a series of mesoporous zinc-based MOF, constructed using cyclopentane dicarboxylic acid as the organic linker, was successfully synthesized via a surfactant-assisted solvothermal method. Surface characterization revealed that MOF-2 exhibited high surface area (1325 m2/g), pore volume (0.273 cm3/g), and average pore diameter (5.69 nm). UV-Vis analysis showed that MOF-2 demonstrated improved drug loading (18% w/w), encapsulation efficiency (86%), and cumulative release (96%), along with both pH-responsive degradation and drug release properties. Furthermore, cisplatin-loaded MOF-2 exhibited potent antitumor activity against A549 cells and effectively inhibited cell migration and invasion, while maintaining minimal cytotoxicity toward LO2 normal hepatic cells. These findings suggest MOF-2 as a promising nanocarrier for pH-responsive anticancer drug delivery.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health challenge and one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Despite notable advances in treatment, the 5-year survival rates for certain malignancies such as lung and pancreatic cancers remain still very low. Conventional modalities, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy, are frequently limited by severe systemic toxicity [3–5]. Hence, the development of innovative anticancer strategies—particularly drug delivery systems with high selectivity, substantial drug-loading capacity, and reduced toxicity—has become a critical focus of current research. Among various carriers such as porous materials, nanoparticles, micro spheres, and liposomes, metal­organic frameworks (MOFs) have emerged as especially promising.

With their exceptional biocompatibility, high surface area and porosity, and tunable pore architecture, MOFs have gained increasing attention as versatile platforms for targeted cancer therapy [6–8]. However, most reported MOFs (such as ZIF-8 and HKUST-1) are microporous (pore size <2 nm), rather than mesoporous (2–50 nm) or macroporous (>50 nm). This pore limitation restricts the access of larger drug molecules to the pores or active sites of MOFs, impairs diffusion and mass transfer, and significantly hinders their biomedical applications [9, 10]. Although MIL-101(Cr) and related analogs provide larger pore cages (2.9–3.4 nm), their synthesis often requires harsh hydrothermal conditions, toxic chromium precursors, or exhibits limited degradability in physiological environments, which compromises their biomedical applicability [11, 12].

These constraints have stimulated increasing interest in the development of mesoporous MOFs (mesoMOFs) with pore sizes in the 2–50 nm range to enable improved drug accommodation, enhanced diffusion kinetics, and responsive disintegration under tumor-specific stimuli. Template-assisted strategies represent an effective route to mesoporosity. Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), a cationic surfactant, is frequently employed as a templating agent in the formation of diverse mesoporous materials. However, the weak interactions between CTAB and MOF precursors limit its effectiveness in mesoMOF construction. In comparison, citric acid acts as a bridging ligand or mesopore-directing modulator, contributing to the establishment of necessary interactions between CTAB and MOF ligands during synthesis [13]. This cooperative templating strategy provides a rational basis for constructing structurally stable mesoMOFs.

MesoMOFs have shown great potential in anticancer drug delivery. For example, Abedi et al. [14] synthesized hierarchically porous imidazole-zinc MOFs with significantly higher cisplatin loading capacity than microporous MOFs. These mesoMOFs also exhibited stronger inhibition of A2780CP ovarian cancer cell proliferation and enabled rapid cisplatin release under mildly acidic conditions (pH 5.5) due to pH-responsive degradation. Similarly, Wei et al. [15] developed hydrolytically stable mesoporous Gd(III)-MOF under solvothermal conditions. After loading with 5-fluorouracil (5-Fu), mesoMOF enabled sustained drug release in phosphate-buffered saline and effectively suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion of A549 non-small cell lung cancer cells in vitro, while also demonstrating promising antitumor efficacy in vivo using A549 xenograft models. Nevertheless, despite these encouraging advances, studies on mesoMOFs for anticancer drug delivery remain relatively limited, and, to the best of our knowledge, no reports are available on mesoporous zinc MOFs based on cyclopentane-1,2-dicarboxylic acid.

Cyclopentane-1,2-dicarboxylic acid (CPDA), a non-aromatic alicyclic linker, provides distinct structural advantages: its rotational flexibility enables diversified coordination geometries, while its alicyclic backbone affords higher hydrophobicity and conformational rigidity compared to linear aliphatic ligands. These features facilitate the formation of stable yet degradable Zn-carboxylate frameworks with enlarged pore domains, offering a structural space not attainable in ZIF-8 (microporous, imidazolate-based) or MIL-101 (rigid aromatic terephthalate-based). Accordingly, CPDA represents an attractive ligand candidate for the construction of functional mesoporous Zn-MOFs [16].

Based on these characteristics, we hypothesize that the intrinsic conformational flexibility and moderate hydrophobicity of CPDA can facilitate the formation of a loosely packed yet structurally stable Zn-carboxylate network, thereby promoting the generation of enlarged mesoporous domains under dual-template regulation. Furthermore, such a framework is expected to exhibit enhanced drug accommodation capacity and accelerated mass diffusion, while remaining susceptible to acid-triggered degradation, which is beneficial for pH-responsive drug release in tumor microenvironments.

In this study, mesoporous zinc MOFs were synthesized via a solvothermal method using CTAB as the template agent and citric acid as the bridging agent, with CPDA employed as the organic linker. The impact of adjusting the ratio between CTAB and citric acid on particle size and porosity was investigated, and the structure and morphology of mesoMOFs were systematically characterized. The optimally structured mesoMOF was then selected for further evaluation of pH-responsive degradation, drug loading, and release behavior. Finally, its antitumor efficacy and anti-metastatic potential were assessed to provide a foundation for further in vitro and mechanistic studies.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagent

N, N-Dimethylformamide (DMF), ethanol, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) and citric acid was provided by Sigma-Aldrich. Cisplatin (Pt) was acquired from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. 1,2-cyclopentanedicarboxylic acid (CPDA) was obtained from Beijing Huawei Ruike Chemical Co., Ltd. Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O) was sourced from Xilong Science Co., Ltd. The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was obtained from Solarbio Life Sciences.

Synthesis of mesoMOFs

Mesoporous zinc-based MOFs were synthesized via a solvothermal method. CTAB (0, 0.1, 0.2, or 0.3 g) and citric acid (0, 0.05, 0.1, or 0.15 g) were sequentially dissolved in 30 mL of DMF. The solution was then supplemented with 3 mmol of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O and an equivalent amount of CPDA, followed by continuous stirring for 30 min. The obtained mixture was placed in a stainless-steel reactor equipped with a Teflon liner and subjected to heating at 150 °C for 48 h. The resulting solids were separated via centrifugation at 4400 rpm, rinsed three times with deionized water, ethanol, and diethyl ether, respectively, and subsequently suspended in 50 mL of a 1:1 (v/v) ethanol-ammonium nitrate mixture for reflux treatment at 70 °C for 48 h. After further centrifugation and washing, the samples were dried at 70 °C for 3 days. Residual CTAB was removed by refluxing the solids in 60 mL of ethanol containing ammonium nitrate (6 g/L) at 60 °C for 12 h. For clarity, the sample synthesized in the absence of CTAB and citric acid was designated as MOF-1 [17], and the one prepared with 0.3 g CTAB and 0.1 g citric acid was labeled as MOF-2.

Characterization of mesoMOFs

The surface morphology of the materials was observed by a Hitachi S-4700 cold field-emission scanning electron microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was conducted using a JEOL instrument (JEM-2100F, Japan) operated at 200 kV. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were recorded using a Bruker D8 diffractometer (USA). Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms, along with measurements of specific surface area, pore diameter, and total pore volume, were obtained using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 M + C analyzer. The particle size distribution was assessed with a Malvern Nano ZS Zetasizer (Malvern Instruments, UK). Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded on a Nicolet Magna 550II spectrometer (USA) across the 500–4000 cm⁻¹ range with a resolution of 4 cm–1. Thermal stability was evaluated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) on a PerkinElmer instrument (USA), performed from 35 to 800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere. The percentage of Zn2+ was determined using an ELAN DRC-e inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS, USA).

In vitro degradation

To evaluate the degradation behavior of MOF-2 under different pH conditions, 50 mg of MOF-2 was first dried at 100 °C for 48 h and then suspended in 5 mL of buffer solution. The buffers used included 20 mM phosphate-buffered saline at pH 6.5 or 7.4, and 20 mM sodium acetate buffer at pH 5.5, all supplemented with 140 mM NaCl. The dispersions were maintained at 37 °C, and weight loss was recorded at predetermined time intervals: 0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h.

To assess serum stability under protein-rich conditions, MOF-2 was additionally incubated in fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C for 48 h. At selected time points (0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h), the samples were collected, separated by centrifugation, washed with deionized water to remove residual serum proteins, and dried to constant weight before measurement. The residual mass was used to evaluate the stability profile in FBS.

Cisplatin adsorption

MOF-1 and MOF-2 (0.2 g) were added separately into 20 mL of cisplatin solution (1.0 g/L) and maintained under dark conditions at room temperature for incubation. At designated time intervals (0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h), the absorbance of the collected supernatant was determined at 301 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. Cisplatin (Pt) concentrations were quantified based on the standard calibration curve: Y = 0.0033X+ 0.0578, Y: Absorbance; X: Concentration.

Cisplatin release

Drug release properties of MOF-1 and MOF-2 were investigated. The Pt@MOFs suspension was enclosed in dialysis membranes (MWCO: 3500 Da) and immersed in 30 mL of buffer at 37 °C under mild shaking. At specific intervals (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 12, and 24 h), 2 mL of the medium was collected and replenished with an equivalent volume of fresh buffer. The concentration of Pt in the release medium was determined by UV-visible spectrophotometry. Additionally, to assess the pH-responsive release behavior, the drug release profiles of Pt@MOFs in media with pH values of 1 to 7 over 3 h were measured using UV-visible spectrophotometry.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of samples against A549 non-small cell lung cancer cells and LO2 normal human hepatocytes was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 100 μL of culture medium. A549 cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum, while LO2 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2, LO2 cells were treated with CPDA (20 mg/mL), Zn2+ (20 mg/mL), or MOF-2 (10, 20, 30 mg/mL) for another 24 h. In parallel, A549 cells were treated with cisplatin at concentrations of 10, 20, and 40 μmol/L and with Pt@MOF-2 at equivalent cisplatin concentrations of 1, 5, 10, 20, or 40 μmol/L. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by a 2-h incubation under dark conditions. Absorbance was then recorded at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer.

Live-dead staining assay

A549 cells were plated in 6-well culture plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The cells were subsequently exposed to CPDA (20 mg/mL), Zn2+ (20 mg/mL), or MOF-2 at concentrations of 10, 20, and 30 mg/mL. Following 24 h of treatment, the cells were rinsed twice with PBS and stained with 2 mL of a Calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) working solution (5 μg/mL). After incubation for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark, the cells were washed twice with PBS and immediately observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Cell migration and invasion assays

A wound healing assay was conducted to evaluate the anti-migratory effect of Pt@MOF-2 on A549 cells. Cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 4 × 104 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. A linear scratch was made across the cell monolayer using a sterile pipette tip. After gently rinsing with phosphate-buffered saline to remove detached cells, the wells were incubated in serum-free medium containing Pt@MOF-2 (equivalent to 40 μmol/L cisplatin) for 24 h. Wound closure was imaged using an inverted optical microscope (Leica DMi1, Wetzlar, Germany).

An invasion assay was performed to assess the anti-invasive effect of the samples on A549 cells. A volume of 100 μL A549 cell suspension (1 × 105 cells) was seeded into the upper chamber of Matrigel-coated Transwell inserts (Corning, NY, USA). The lower chamber was filled with 750 μL DMEM supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum. After 2 h of serum starvation, the cells were treated with serum-free medium containing MOF-2 or Pt@MOF-2 (cisplatin concentration: 40 μmol/L) and incubated for 24 h. Non-invading cells were gently removed with a sterile cotton swab, and the invading cells were imaged and calculated under an inverted microscope.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. All tests had been repeated for three times under the same setting conditions. The release profiles of MOFs were mathematically fitted using Origin software (version 2025). Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the independent samples t test were employed to determine statistically significant differences among groups, with P < 0.05 considered indicative of statistical significance.

FTIR analysis

The FTIR spectra of CTAB, citric acid (CA), MOF-1, and MOF-2 are displayed in Fig. 2. The FT-IR spectrum of MOF-1 exhibited prominent absorption peaks at 1702 cm–1 and 1658 cm–1, which were assigned to the stretching vibrations of C = O bonds in carboxylate groups. These results coincided with previously reported FTIR data of Zn-MOF, supporting the successful coordination of carboxyl groups with Zn2+ ions [15]. In the FTIR spectrum of citric acid, the peak at 3497 cm–1 was associated with the O-H stretching vibration of hydroxyl groups. In the spectrum of MOF-2, the O-H stretching band was absent, indicating that citric acid was effectively removed during the post-synthetic treatment. Thermogravimetric analysis

The thermal behavior of MOF-2 was investigated using thermogravimetric analysis (Fig. 3). An initial weight loss of 3.01% (theoretical: 3.95%) occurred within the temperature range of 38 –114 °C, corresponding to the release of physically adsorbed water. Between 204 °C and 549 °C, a pronounced exothermic transition was detected, accompanied by a weight reduction of 66.23% (theoretical: 67.54%), indicative of the thermal decomposition of the MOF-2 framework. The sharp mass loss between 38 °C and 114 °C might reflect the degradation of coordinated carboxyl groups, while the gradual loss from 114 °C to 549 °C might be associated with the breakdown of the cyclopentane backbone. pH-Responsive degradation

As shown in Fig. 4, MOF-2 exhibited a pH-dependent degradation profile. A significant increase in weight loss was observed as the pH decreased from 7.4 to 5.5. Nearly complete degradation occurred after 24 h of MOF-2 incubation at pH 7.4 and 6.8, whereas complete degradation was achieved within 12 h at pH 5.5. This accelerated degradation under acidic conditions was attributed to the protonation of CPDA ligands, which weakened their coordination with Zn2+ ions and thereby promoted pH-responsive disassembly of the MOF framework [20, 21]. Cisplatin loading and release capacity

The drug loading capacity and encapsulation efficiency were quantitatively assessed via UV-visible spectrophotometry [Fig. 5a, b]. The respective drug loading efficiencies were found to be 11.52 ± 0.65% for MOF-1 and 18.92 ± 0.73% for MOF-2, with platinum loading reaching near saturation within 48 h. The corresponding encapsulation efficiencies were 70.39 ± 2.37% for MOF-1 and 86.47 ± 3.77% for MOF-2. As shown in Fig. 5c, the 3-h release profile of cisplatin under varying pH 1–7 conditions exhibited a clear pH-dependent trend, with the release increasing as the pH decreased. Consistently, MOF-2 demonstrated higher cisplatin release than MOF-1 across all tested pH values. The drug release profiles of MOF-1 and MOF-2 are presented in Fig. 5d. At 24 h, MOF-2 exhibited a cumulative release of 96.38 ± 2.52%, significantly higher than that of MOF-1 (62.28 ± 2.82%). Both materials reached release plateaus by 24 h, indicating completion of the release process. During the initial 6 h, MOF-1 and MOF-2 released 59.66 ± 4.37% and 90.66 ± 4.88% of the encapsulated drug, respectively, suggesting a relatively faster initial release phase followed by a slower release phase.

The linear fitting curves of drug release kinetics and their corresponding equations are presented in Fig. 5e. The results demonstrated that the drug release profiles of both MOF-1 and MOF-2 fit well with the first-order kinetic model, with adjusted R2 values of 0.9789 and 0.9808, respectively. These findings indicated that physical adsorption played a dominant role in the drug release process.

The release of Zn2+ under different pH conditions and at various time points was quantified by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), and the results are presented in Fig. 5f. The cumulative release percentage of Zn2+ increased progressively with time, indicating continuous framework degradation. Notably, consistent with the cisplatin release behavior, Zn2+ release exhibited distinct pH responsiveness, with a significantly higher release observed under acidic conditions. This result further confirms that the acid-triggered protonation of carboxylate ligands weakens the Zn-O coordination bonds, thereby accelerating the collapse of the MOF framework and promoting both metal ion and drug liberation in a tumor-mimicking microenvironment.

These results suggest that mesoporous MOFs function as pH-responsive drug delivery systems, characterized by a slow and stable release pattern. Such a release profile meets the requirements for anticancer drug delivery carriers.

Cytotoxicity assays

The viability of LO2 cells was assessed by CCK-8 assay after 24 h exposure to CPDA, Zn2+, and MOF-2 at concentrations of 10, 20, and 30 mg/mL [Fig. 6a]. A concentration-dependent decline in cell viability was observed with increasing MOF-2 levels. Moreover, LO2 cells treated with MOF-2 (10, 20 mg/mL) maintained over 80% viability, exceeding that of the Zn2+ and CPDA groups, thereby highlighting the superior biocompatibility of MOF-2. According to ISO standards, samples that maintain cell viability above 70% are considered non-cytotoxic [23]. Similarly, the live/dead assay results showed high cell viability in all groups, with live cells predominating (green) [Fig. 6d], indicating excellent cytocompatibility of MOF-2. The results revealed that Pt@MOF-2 showed a dose-dependent inhibitory effect, with an IC50 value of 14.65 μmol/L, which was lower than that of cisplatin (17.90 μmol/L). Discussion

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), also known as coordination polymers, are crystalline materials with porous structures, composed of organic linkers and metal ions or metal clusters that are connected and stabilized by coordination bonds [10]. Due to their structural diversity, well-defined pore architecture, and high surface area, MOFs have found a broad range of applications in drug delivery, catalysis, gas separation, and electrodes. However, MOFs with ordered mesoporous structures (ranging from 2 to 50 nm) remain relatively rare in the literature. Compared to microporous materials, the larger pore sizes of mesoporous MOFs enable the expression of novel physical and chemical properties.

In this study, mesoporous zinc-based MOFs were synthesized using a template-assisted solvothermal method. The pH-responsive degradation, drug loading capacity, and release profiles of the material were evaluated. Additionally, the anticancer efficacy and the inhibition of metastasis and invasion were investigated, providing a solid basis for further in vitro and mechanistic studies.

The degradation results showed accelerated structural collapse under mildly acidic environments, which can be attributed to protonation of the carboxylate ligands. Protonation weakens Zn-O coordination bonds by reducing the electron-donating ability of the ligand, thereby destabilizing the secondary building units and promoting stepwise framework disassembly. This acid-triggered weakening of Zn-carboxylate connectivity is well documented for zinc-based MOFs and provides a mechanistic explanation for the faster degradation observed in tumor-mimicking acidic conditions compared with physiological pH 7.4 [27, 28].

A good first-order fit suggests that the release rate depends on the remaining drug concentration (i.e., a concentration-gradient-driven process), which is commonly used to describe diffusion-governed release from porous/matrix-based carriers [29]. However, it should be noted that the first-order model describes the overall release kinetics and does not explicitly distinguish between diffusion from intact pores and drug liberation caused by framework degradation. Notably, the burst release within the first 6 h (90.66 ± 4.88% for MOF-2) suggests that a considerable fraction of cisplatin is likely surface-adsorbed or shallowly confined rather than deeply embedded within mesoporous channels, which can account for the rapid initial release [30]. Therefore, in this work, the release process is conceptually decoupled as an initial diffusion-dominated (surface/shallow) release followed by a degradation-assisted release under acidic conditions.

Surfactants such as cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) have attracted considerable attention as templates for mesoporous MOFs due to their simple preparation, easy availability, and effective control over pore size and morphology [31]. However, when used alone, they may not fully satisfy the requirements for mesoporous MOFs because of their weak interactions with the MOF precursors. The incorporation of chelating agents can enhance the interaction between metal ions and surfactant molecules via Coulombic attraction and coordination. The surfactant molecules self-assemble into micelles, while the chelating agent bridges the interaction between the metal-organic framework and the micelles, facilitating the self-assembly process and promoting crystal growth through a synergistic templating effect. However, neither the surfactant nor the chelating agent alone is sufficient to induce mesopore formation [32].

Wu et al. [33] reported the preparation of hierarchical mesoporous ZIF-8 under aqueous conditions at room temperature, using histidine and CTAB as surfactant-directed agents. In this approach, histidine was selected as a chelating agent for zinc ions (II), which stabilizes the interactions between the metal ions and the micelles. Since histidine can pre-chelate divalent zinc ions, it plays a pivotal role in stabilizing the CTAB micellar structure and preventing the formation of by-products. This characteristic significantly accelerates the synthesis of pure-phase ZIF-8, leading to improved crystallinity and optimized pore sizes at both micro- and meso-scales.

Ardila-Suárez et al. [31] systematically investigated the effect of defect engineering on the physicochemical properties and surface characteristics of the MOF-808 series of microporous/macroporous materials, employing both modulator and surfactant strategies. The results demonstrated that microporous/mesoporous MOFs with missing linker defects could be synthesized by using modulators with varying chain lengths. However, compared to materials synthesized with formic acid and propionic acid as modulators, the use of acetic acid facilitated the formation of MOFs with a broader mesopore size distribution. On the other hand, ordered microporous/macroporous materials with crystalline structures were successfully synthesized by incorporating surfactants. Notably, the addition of CTAB did not lead to significant changes in the surface chemical composition compared to the surfactant-free materials.

Citric acid, used as a linker or mesopore directing agent, establishes the necessary interactions between the MOF crystals and the CTAB template during the synthesis of mesoporous MOFs. Citric acid acts as a weak multidentate modulator that transiently coordinates with Zn2+, thereby slowing metal-ligand association and regulating nucleation density. This competitive coordination lowers the free-energy barrier for organized crystal growth and allows Zn2+ to distribute more uniformly around CTAB micelles. Meanwhile, CTAB provides positively charged micellar domains that electrostatically attract partially deprotonated citrate-Zn complexes, stabilizing the soft template during framework assembly [34–37]. The optimal weight ratio of CTAB to citric acid should be between 1:1 and 3:1. Deviations from this optimal range result in a decrease in specific surface area and pore volume. At lower citric acid concentrations, the lack of a linking agent to bind the MOF to the CTAB template leads to the formation of microporous MOFs during the crystal growth stage. Under conditions of high citric acid concentration, the competition between free citric acid and citric acid precursor complexes for interaction with the template intensifies, ultimately resulting in defective crystals with lower specific surface area and pore volume [14].

In this study, MOF-2 exhibited a BET surface area of 1325 m2/g, a pore volume of 0.273 cm3/g, and an average pore diameter of 5.69 nm, which falls within the mesoporous range. These textural parameters are comparable to those of recently reported mesoporous MOFs such as MIL-101(Fe) (surface area: 732 m2/g), pore diameter 2.68–3.1 nm [38] and hierarchical mesoporous MUV-2 (surface area: 1200 m2/g, pore diameter: 1–3 nm) [39]. This comparison suggests that the dual-templating strategy based on CTAB and citric acid is an effective approach for constructing mesoporous architectures with high specific surface areas, which are favorable for drug accommodation.

Most MOF-based nanoplatforms exhibit tunable microporosity and spatial steric hindrance, which can be precisely adjusted to achieve different drug encapsulation efficiencies and personalized release profiles. Furthermore, both the metal ions and organic ligands in the MOF composition can serve as therapeutic agents and exhibit antitumor activity. The components of the MOF platform can synergistically enhance antitumor efficacy through simple design principles [40]. Mehdi et al. reported a hierarchical mesoporous Zn-based MOF with a high surface area of 1859 m2/g, large pore size of 14.13 nm, and pore volume of 0.314 cm3/g, enabling a high cisplatin loading of 28% (w/w). The cisplatin-loaded Zn-MOF exhibited a significantly enhanced anticancer efficacy against A2780cp cells, with an IC50 of 6.3 μg/mL, compared with 13.8 μg/mL for free cisplatin, demonstrating the advantage of mesoporous MOF carriers in improving platinum-based chemotherapy[14].

In this study, MOF-2 demonstrated enhanced drug loading (18% w/w), encapsulation efficiency (86%), and cumulative release (96%), alongside pH-responsive degradation and drug release properties. Consistent with previous findings, MOF-2 exhibited excellent biocompatibility, and upon loading with cisplatin, the drug delivery system showed potent anticancer activity (IC50: 14.65umol/L). These results highlight MOF-2 as a promising pH-responsive nanocarrier for anticancer drug delivery.

This study has several limitations. We chose the widely recognized surfactant CTAB in combination with citric acid for pore tuning of the MOFs, without testing other template agents or linker materials, nor did we further functionalize the materials with surface modifications, which limits the scope of material selection. Although Zn2+ release contributes to the pH-responsive degradation and may enhance antitumor efficacy, excessive Zn2+ accumulation could also raise potential systemic toxicity concerns, which require careful evaluation. In particular, the Zn2+ cytotoxicity threshold toward normal LO2 cells under prolonged exposure was not determined in this study, and thus, the long-term biosafety window remains to be established. Future studies should therefore focus on improving the colloidal stability and biosafety of the nanocarrier, for example, through surface PEGylation to prolong blood circulation and reduce nonspecific uptake, as well as ligand modification to enhance tumor-targeting specificity. Future experiments could explore validation using other materials. Additionally, due to time and budget constraints, in vivo animal studies were not performed to validate the experimental results. These will be addressed in subsequent experiments through in vivo validation.

Conclusion

In this study, mesoporous Zn-MOF nanoparticles (MOF-2) were successfully synthesized via a surfactant-assisted solvothermal method for cisplatin delivery and assessment of antitumor efficacy. The formation of a mesoporous framework significantly improved the specific surface area, pore size, and total pore volume compared with Zn-MOF (MOF-1), thereby enhancing drug loading capacity and enabling both pH-responsive degradation and drug release. The CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that the MOF-2 exhibited low cytotoxicity toward LO2 normal hepatic cells. Upon cisplatin loading, Pt@MOF-2 displayed potent antitumor efficacy and effectively restrained the migration and invasion of A549 cells. Given its excellent biocompatibility, tunable pore structure, and dual pH-responsive behavior, MOF-2 holds great promise as an efficient nanocarrier for anticancer drug delivery.
