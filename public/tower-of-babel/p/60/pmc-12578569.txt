Beyond the Pulselessness: A Multifaceted Case of Takayasu Arteritis With Aneurysmal Complications and Extensive Upper Limb Vascular Occlusion in a 40‐Year‐Old Female
Authors: Sakib Abrar, Ibrahim Khalil, Shah Tanvir Ahmed, Fabiha Rahman Spriha, Md Imran Hossain, Md Abu Sayed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTakayasu arteritis, a rare chronic large‐vessel vasculitis primarily affecting the aorta and its major branches, poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to its heterogeneous manifestations and potential for severe vascular complications. This case report details a 40‐year‐old female presenting with a 12‐month history of right arm pain and restricted movement, ultimately diagnosed with Takayasu arteritis. The patient exhibited hallmark features, including a diminished right radial pulse, blood pressure asymmetry (150/92 mmHg left, 125/80 mmHg right), and mild right arm weakness (4/5 strength). Physical examination of the shoulders revealed limited active range of motion in the right shoulder, with abduction restricted to 90° and external rotation to 30°, likely due to ischemic changes, while the left shoulder showed full range of motion. Laboratory findings confirmed active inflammation, with C‐reactive protein (CRP) at 18.4 mg/L and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) at 45 mm/h, alongside mild anemia (11.5 g/dL) and reactive thrombocytosis (360–410 × 103/μL). CT angiography revealed thrombotic occlusion of the left vertebral artery (60%–70%), significant narrowing with thin flow in the left common carotid and subclavian arteries, moderate occlusion with reconstituted flow in the left mid and distal brachial and radial arteries, and narrowing in the right ulnar and interosseous arteries. Additionally, a 4.0 cm infrarenal aortic aneurysm was identified, a potentially life‐threatening complication. Treatment was initiated with intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g daily for 3 days), transitioning to oral prednisone (50 mg/day), combined with ibuprofen for pain (400 mg as needed, up to 1200 mg daily), amlodipine and nifedipine for hypertension, and esomeprazole for gastric protection. A multidisciplinary approach was emphasized, integrating rheumatology and vascular surgery, with plans for steroid‐sparing agents (methotrexate or azathioprine) and serial aneurysm surveillance. This case underscores the complexity of Takayasu arteritis, highlighting the need for comprehensive vascular assessments, tailored immunosuppression, and vigilant monitoring to prevent complications and optimize outcomes.Keywords: aortic aneurysm, large‐vessel vasculitis, multidisciplinary care, Takayasu arteritis, upper limb ischemia

Introduction

Takayasu arteritis (TA) is a rare chronic granulomatous vasculitis that predominantly affects large vessels, including the aorta and its major branches, often leading to significant morbidity due to vascular stenosis, occlusion, or aneurysm formation [1]. With an estimated incidence of 1–3 per million annually in Western populations and higher rates in Asia, TA is a critical condition in rheumatologic and vascular medicine, primarily affecting young females, particularly those of Asian descent, though it is observed globally [2]. The disease typically presents in individuals under 40 years, but cases in older patients are not uncommon, especially in Western populations [3]. The hallmark of TA is immune‐mediated inflammation of arterial walls, resulting in intimal hyperplasia, medial degeneration, and adventitial fibrosis, which disrupt arterial integrity and lead to clinical manifestations such as pulselessness, claudication, or organ ischemia [4].

The pathophysiology of TA involves a T‐cell‐driven inflammatory process, with granulomatous infiltrates causing progressive arterial damage [5]. This can manifest as stenosis or occlusion in 85%–90% of cases, often affecting the subclavian arteries first due to their proximity to the aortic arch, or, less commonly, aneurysm formation in 10%–30% of patients [6]. Early symptoms are often nonspecific, including fatigue, fever, and arthralgia, which can delay diagnosis and allow vascular damage to progress [7]. The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 1990 criteria provide a diagnostic framework, emphasizing features such as age under 40 years, limb claudication, pulse deficits, and angiographic abnormalities [8]. Laboratory markers like CRP and ESR are elevated in active disease but lack specificity, necessitating advanced imaging for confirmation [9]. CT angiography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are essential for visualizing luminal changes, vessel wall inflammation, and complications such as aneurysms [10].

Management of TA focuses on suppressing inflammation and preventing vascular complications. Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of therapy, achieving initial remission in up to 80% of patients, though relapses are common, affecting 60%–80% within 5 years [11]. Steroid‐sparing agents, such as methotrexate, azathioprine, or biologics like tocilizumab, are increasingly utilized to minimize long‐term corticosteroid toxicity [12]. Vascular interventions, such as angioplasty or bypass grafting, are reserved for severe stenosis or aneurysms but carry heightened risks in inflamed vessels [13]. This case report describes a 40‐year‐old female diagnosed with TA after a 12‐month history of right arm pain and restricted movement, notable for vascular occlusion and a potentially life‐threatening aortic aneurysm. By detailing the clinical workup, imaging findings, and management strategy, this report aims to provide actionable insights for clinicians managing similar cases, emphasizing the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to optimize patient care.

Case Presentation

History

A 40‐year‐old female presented to our tertiary care rheumatology clinic with a 12‐month history of persistent right arm pain and restricted movement. The pain was described as a deep, aching sensation localized to the proximal arm and shoulder, worsened by physical activity such as lifting or prolonged use, and unrelieved by rest. She reported no associated swelling, erythema, or trauma and denied systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, fatigue, night sweats, or visual disturbances. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no prior diagnoses of autoimmune diseases, hypertension, diabetes, or cardiovascular conditions. She was a non‐smoker, had no family history of vasculitis or connective tissue diseases, and reported no recent infections, travel, or medication use.

Physical Examination

Physical examination revealed a significantly diminished right radial pulse compared to the left, suggestive of vascular compromise, a finding often termed the “pulseless” phase of TA. No bruits were audible over the carotid, subclavian, or abdominal arteries. Blood pressure measurements showed asymmetry: 150/92 mmHg in the left arm and 125/80 mmHg in the right, raising suspicion of arterial stenosis affecting the right upper limb. Motor strength in the right arm was mildly reduced (4/5), particularly in shoulder abduction, external rotation, and elbow flexion, with no sensory deficits or reflex abnormalities. The right shoulder exhibited limited active range of motion, with abduction restricted to 90° (normal 180°) and external rotation to 30° (normal 90°), likely due to ischemic changes from vascular occlusion, while the left shoulder showed full range of motion with no restrictions. No synovitis, joint deformities, or systemic signs (e.g., rash, lymphadenopathy, or organomegaly) were observed. The cardiovascular examination revealed no murmurs or irregular rhythms, and respiratory and abdominal examinations were normal.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included other vasculitides (e.g., giant cell arteritis, polyarteritis nodosa), atherosclerotic vascular disease, and non‐inflammatory causes such as thoracic outlet syndrome or cervical radiculopathy. Giant cell arteritis was unlikely due to the patient's age, absence of temporal artery tenderness, and negative autoimmune markers. Polyarteritis nodosa was ruled out due to the lack of microaneurysms and organ‐specific involvement. Atherosclerotic disease was less likely given the absence of cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., smoking, diabetes, dyslipidemia) and the presence of inflammatory markers. Non‐inflammatory causes were excluded due to the vascular findings on imaging and the inflammatory profile.

Investigations

Laboratory tests confirmed active inflammation. CRP was elevated at 18.4 mg/L (normal < 5.0 mg/L), and ESR was 45 mm/h (normal < 20 mm/h). A prior CRP measurement, taken 6 months earlier during a routine health check, was 6.2 mg/L, indicating fluctuating inflammatory activity over time. Complete blood count revealed hemoglobin of 11.5 g/dL, suggestive of anemia of chronic disease, supported by normal iron studies (serum ferritin 120 ng/mL, TIBC 300 μg/dL), absence of hemolysis (normal haptoglobin, no schistocytes on peripheral smear), and negative autoimmune and infectious workup. White blood cell count ranged from 9.8 to 11.7 × 103/μL, and platelet count was 360–410 × 103/μL, consistent with reactive thrombocytosis. Neutrophil percentages varied from 52% to 60%, and lymphocytes ranged from 22.5% to 35.0%. Renal function was preserved, with creatinine levels of 0.7–0.85 mg/dL, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was 95 mL/min/1.73 m2. Liver function tests (ALT, AST, bilirubin) were within normal limits. Autoimmune screening, including antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti‐double‐stranded DNA, rheumatoid factor, and anti‐neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), was negative, ruling out systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, or other vasculitides. Complement levels (C3, C4) were normal, and infectious workup (hepatitis B/C, HIV, and QuantiFERON‐TB Gold for tuberculosis) was negative, excluding secondary causes of vasculitis.

A comprehensive imaging workup was undertaken to evaluate vascular pathology. The chest X‐ray was normal, showing clear lung fields, a normal cardiac silhouette, and no evidence of rib fractures, pleural effusion, or pulmonary infiltrates. CT angiography of the neck vessels and upper limb vessels, performed with sequential filming by MDCT 128 slice (Figures 1, 2, 3, 4), revealed critical findings. The left‐sided aortic arch was visualized, showing a brachiocephalic trunk, right subclavian artery, axillary artery, and brachial artery with moderate flow. However, significant narrowing with thin flow was observed in the right ulnar and interosseous arteries, contributing to ischemic symptoms in the right arm. The right common carotid and right vertebral arteries appeared normal, reducing the risk of cerebrovascular complications. In contrast, the left common carotid and subclavian arteries exhibited significant narrowing with thin flow, while the left vertebral artery showed thrombotic occlusion (60%–70%). Thrombotic occlusion was also identified in the left subclavian, axillary, and proximal brachial arteries, with reconstituted moderate flow in the left mid and distal brachial and radial arteries. Narrowing with significant thin flow was noted in the left ulnar and interosseous arteries. Additionally, a separate CT angiogram of the lower limbs identified a dilated infrarenal abdominal aorta, measuring 4.0 cm in diameter, suggestive of an aneurysm, with no significant stenosis in the iliac, femoral, popliteal, or distal arteries. Vessel wall thickening and enhancement were noted on CT, suggesting active inflammation.

Treatment

Immediate treatment was initiated to address the inflammatory flare and alleviate symptoms. The patient received intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g daily for 3 days) to suppress acute inflammation, followed by a transition to oral prednisone (0.8 mg/kg/day, approximately 50 mg daily), to be tapered over 6–8 weeks based on clinical response. Oral ibuprofen (400 mg as needed, up to 1200 mg daily) was prescribed alongside corticosteroids for pain management, with instructions to monitor for gastrointestinal side effects. To address hypertension, likely secondary to vascular involvement, amlodipine (10 mg daily) was started, with nifedipine extended‐release (30 mg daily) added for additional control. Esomeprazole (40 mg daily) was included for gastric protection against corticosteroid and NSAID‐related complications. The patient was counseled on the importance of daily blood pressure monitoring using a home device and maintaining a log for review. A follow‐up visit was scheduled in 10 days to assess symptom response, inflammatory markers, and blood pressure control, with plans to introduce a steroid‐sparing agent (e.g., methotrexate or azathioprine) within 4–6 weeks to minimize long‐term corticosteroid exposure.

Conclusion and Results

The patient met the ACR 1990 criteria for TA, fulfilling four of six criteria: claudication of the right upper extremity, decreased right brachial artery pulse, blood pressure discrepancy (> 10 mmHg between arms), and angiographic evidence of left common carotid, subclavian, and vertebral artery involvement [8]. Although the patient's age (40 years) is at the upper limit of the typical range, TA can occur in older individuals, particularly in Western populations [2]. The elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 18.4 mg/L, ESR 45 mm/h) and imaging findings corroborated active disease.

Discussion

This case exemplifies the diagnostic and therapeutic complexity of Takayasu arteritis, where widespread vascular pathology produces a multifaceted clinical syndrome. The patient's primary complaint of right arm pain and restricted movement, persisting for 12 months, was driven by ischemic changes from significant narrowing in the left common carotid and subclavian arteries, thrombotic occlusion of the left vertebral artery (60%–70%), and additional vascular involvement in the upper limbs. The significantly diminished right radial pulse and blood pressure discrepancy (25 mmHg systolic difference between arms) are hallmark features of the “pulseless” phase, reflecting advanced vascular involvement observed in up to 80% of patients with TA [14]. These findings align with the natural history of the disease, where chronic inflammation leads to progressive arterial stenosis or occlusion, often affecting the subclavian arteries first due to their anatomical proximity to the aortic arch [15].

The CT angiogram findings provide a detailed map of the patient's vascular pathology. The significant narrowing with thin flow in the left common carotid and subclavian arteries, coupled with thrombotic occlusion of the left vertebral artery, underscores the disease's predilection for the aortic arch and its branches. The reconstituted moderate flow in the left mid and distal brachial and radial arteries suggests some collateral circulation, which may have mitigated more severe ischemic symptoms, though the narrowing in the left ulnar and interosseous arteries likely contributed to the patient's functional limitations. On the right side, the narrowing with thin flow in the ulnar and interosseous arteries explains the diminished radial pulse and mild weakness (4/5 strength) in the right arm, while the normal right common carotid and vertebral arteries indicate a lower risk of cerebrovascular events, which affect 5%–10% of TA patients [16]. The presence of vessel wall thickening and enhancement on CT suggests ongoing inflammation, a feature that may precede luminal changes and guide therapeutic intensity [17].

The identification of a 4.0 cm infrarenal aortic aneurysm is a critical finding, as aneurysms occur in 10%–30% of TA patients and carry a rupture risk with a mortality rate of up to 50% if untreated [18]. Although the aneurysm's current size is below the typical intervention threshold (5–6 cm), its presence in the context of vasculitis warrants close surveillance, as inflamed arterial walls may be more susceptible to rapid expansion or rupture [19]. The aneurysm's larger diameter compared to typical early aneurysms in TA (often < 3 cm) suggests a more advanced lesion, potentially driven by prolonged untreated inflammation, highlighting the need for aggressive disease control [20]. The absence of significant stenosis in the lower limb arteries (iliac, femoral, popliteal, distal) is reassuring, but the aneurysm's location in the infrarenal aorta necessitates serial imaging to monitor for growth or complications.

The elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 18.4 mg/L, ESR 45 mm/h) confirmed active disease, consistent with flares reported in 60%–80% of TA patients, even with treatment [21]. The variability in CRP levels (previously 6.2 mg/L) underscores the relapsing–remitting nature of TA, necessitating serial monitoring to guide therapeutic adjustments [22]. The mild anemia (hemoglobin 11.5 g/dL) is consistent with anemia of chronic disease, supported by normal iron studies (serum ferritin 120 ng/mL, TIBC 300 μg/dL), absence of hemolysis (normal haptoglobin, no schistocytes on peripheral smear), and negative autoimmune and infectious workup, reflecting a systemic inflammatory state commonly seen in active vasculitis [23]. The negative autoimmune and infectious workup was critical to exclude mimics, such as giant cell arteritis, polyarteritis nodosa, or tuberculous aortitis, which can present with similar vascular findings [24]. The preserved renal function (creatinine 0.7–0.85 mg/dL, eGFR 95 mL/min/1.73 m2) was reassuring, as renal artery stenosis, a complication in 20%–60% of TA cases, can lead to renovascular hypertension requiring targeted interventions [25].

Therapeutically, the initiation of high‐dose intravenous corticosteroids (methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days) was appropriate to control the acute inflammatory flare, with studies showing a 70%–80% response rate in active TA [26]. The transition to oral prednisone (0.8 mg/kg/day) aims to maintain remission, but long‐term corticosteroid use is associated with significant adverse effects, including osteoporosis, diabetes, weight gain, and infection, necessitating the early introduction of steroid‐sparing agents [27]. Methotrexate (15–25 mg weekly) or azathioprine (2 mg/kg/day) is commonly used, with response rates of 50%–60% in maintaining remission [28]. Biologics, such as tocilizumab, an interleukin‐6 inhibitor, have shown efficacy in refractory cases, achieving sustained remission in up to 50% of patients and reducing steroid dependence [29]. The treatment plan combines corticosteroids with ibuprofen for pain management, with esomeprazole to mitigate gastrointestinal risks from both medications. The patient's hypertension, likely secondary to aortic arch irregularities or subclinical renal artery involvement, was appropriately managed with amlodipine and nifedipine, though regular renal artery imaging (e.g., Doppler ultrasound or MR angiography) is essential to detect stenosis, which may require angioplasty or stenting in 10%–20% of cases [30].

The infrarenal aortic aneurysm poses a significant long‐term challenge. Current guidelines recommend serial imaging (CT or MRI) every 6–12 months to monitor for growth, with intervention considered for aneurysms exceeding 5 cm, rapidly expanding lesions (e.g., > 0.5 cm/year), or those causing symptoms like pain or organ compression [31]. In TA, surgical or endovascular interventions are complicated by active inflammation, which increases the risk of restenosis, graft failure, or anastomotic complications, with failure rates as high as 30% in inflamed vessels [32]. A multidisciplinary approach involving vascular surgery, rheumatology, and interventional radiology is critical to balance disease control and procedural risks. The patient's aneurysm, at 4.0 cm, requires regular follow‐up, with patient education on recognizing symptoms of rupture (e.g., sudden abdominal or back pain, hypotension) to ensure timely intervention [33].

The prolonged symptom duration (12 months) before diagnosis highlights a common challenge in TA: delayed recognition due to nonspecific symptoms. Primary care providers may initially attribute arm pain to other causes, delaying referral to rheumatology [34]. This case underscores the need for heightened awareness of TA in patients with atypical vascular symptoms, even those at the upper age limit of the typical demographic. Educational initiatives targeting general practitioners and emergency physicians could reduce diagnostic delays, potentially preventing complications like aneurysms or end‐organ ischemia [35].

Looking forward, the patient's prognosis depends on effective control of inflammation, prevention of vascular progression, and management of the aneurysm. TA follows a relapsing–remitting course, with 50%–60% of patients experiencing flares within 5 years of diagnosis, often triggered by stress, infection, or medication non‐adherence [36]. Regular monitoring of inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) and imaging (CT/MRI every 6–12 months) is essential to detect disease activity and vascular changes early [37]. Lifestyle modifications, including smoking cessation (though not applicable here), weight management, low‐sodium diet, and regular aerobic exercise (as tolerated), are critical to reduce vascular stress and improve cardiovascular health [38]. Patient education on disease chronicity, treatment adherence, and symptom recognition (e.g., worsening arm pain or abdominal pain) is paramount to enhance quality of life and reduce morbidity.

Future research should focus on improving outcomes in TA. Novel biomarkers, such as pentraxin‐3 or matrix metalloproteinases, may offer greater specificity than CRP and ESR for detecting disease activity, enabling earlier intervention [39]. Advanced imaging techniques, such as positron emission tomography (PET) with 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose, could enhance the detection of vessel wall inflammation, particularly in early or subclinical disease, with sensitivity exceeding 80% in some studies [40]. Randomized controlled trials evaluating biologics (e.g., tocilizumab, ustekinumab) and small‐molecule inhibitors (e.g., JAK inhibitors) are needed to establish optimal second‐line therapies, as current evidence is largely observational [41]. Longitudinal studies on aneurysm progression in TA could inform risk stratification and intervention thresholds, addressing a critical gap in current guidelines [42].
