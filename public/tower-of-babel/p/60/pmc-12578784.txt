IMT504 accelerates post-hepatectomy liver regeneration in mice with involvement of GLAST + Wnt1 + bone marrow stromal progenitors
Authors: Maximiliano Borda, Lucía Cafaro, Romina Sierra, Mercedes Díaz Pedraza, Camila Becerra, Agustina Ábalo, María José Karolewski, María José Cantero, Máximo Schiavone, Guillermo D Mazzolini, Alejandro Montaner, Esteban J Fiore, Jorge B Aquino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe immunomodulatory oligodeoxynucleotide (ODN) IMT504 exhibits antifibrotic and pro-regenerative properties in the context of liver fibrosis. It also enhances the contribution of GLAST+ Wnt1+ bone marrow stromal progenitors (BMSPs) to liver regeneration in a mouse model of chronic liver disease. In this study, partial hepatectomy (PHx) was performed in CD1 mice. In parallel, Cre-loxP mice were used to identify GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs and assess their contribution to liver cells populations. A combination of in vivo and in vitro assays was conducted. IMT504 significantly enhanced liver mass recovery following PHx by promoting both early and late induction of hepatocyte proliferation. The expression dynamics of genes associated with proliferation, liver function, and liver progenitor identity were analyzed. Moreover, IMT504 stimulated the pro-regenerative activity of GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs by promoting their differentiation into liver cells. In summary, our findings identify IMT504 as a promising therapeutic candidate with strong pro-regenerative potential. Introduction

The liver exhibits a remarkable intrinsic ability to regenerate following injury or partial resection, primarily through hepatocytes that re-enter the cell cycle, and, in some cases, through progenitor cells that are activated to proliferate and differentiate into hepatic cells1.

Both liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) currently lack effective clinical solutions. Liver transplantation and surgical resection remain the preferred curative strategies for early-stage primary o metastatic liver tumors2. However, in patients with large tumor masses and insufficient healthy liver tissue, major resection is not feasible due to the high risk of postoperative liver failure resulting from inadequate regeneration. Additionally, transplanted livers are often undersized, fail to grow sufficiently, or cannot meet the body’s metabolic demands3. Consequently, there is urgent need for new therapeutic approaches to enhance liver regeneration.

IMT504 is a synthetic oligodeoxynucleotide (ODN) with immunomodulatory properties that can stimulate the mobilization of bone marrow stromal progenitors (BMSPs)4. We previously demonstrated that IMT504 exerts potent antifibrotic and pro-regenerative effects in mice5. Moreover, IMT504 was also shown to directly upregulate hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), which promotes hepatocyte proliferation, and insulin-like growth factor I (IGF-I), a hormone secreted by hepatocytes and considered a marker of hepatocyte function, in primary cultured hepatocytes.

Furthermore, we demonstrated that IMT504 induces the proliferation and mobilization of a subpopulation of BMSPs that co-express the glutamate-aspartate transporter GLAST and Wnt1, a ligand involved in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling5,6. These cells, which we term GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs, migrate from the bone marrow to the injured liver, where they contribute to endothelial-like cells (ELCs) and hepatocyte-like cells (HLCs)5,6. In addition, we found that factors released by IMT504-pretreated GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs upregulate the expression of HGF and IGF-I in primary cultured hepatocytes, suggesting that these cells may also play a significant role in IMT504-induced liver repair5.

In this study, we investigate the potential role of IMT504 in accelerating liver mass recovery in a mouse model of partial hepatectomy (PHx), with a particular focus on its effects on GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs in the context of hepatic resection. Additionally, we examine whether this cellular subpopulation possesses hepatogenic potential and assess the impact of IMT504 on this process.

Results

IMT504 increases liver regeneration rate after partial hepatectomy

After PHx, the liver initiates a tightly regulated regenerative program to restore its mass and function7. Given our previous findings that IMT504 promotes hepatocyte proliferation in the context of liver fibrosis5, we investigated whether this oligonucleotide could also enhance liver mass recovery following PHx.

The effect of IMT504 on liver regeneration was initially evaluated by administering the compound either one day before or one day after PHx in CD1 mice, to identify the optimal treatment protocol. Mice were treated with IMT504 (0.4 mg/ml; 6 mg/kg/animal) or saline, and liver mass recovery was assessed 6 days post-hepatectomy. As shown in Figure S1, both treatment regimens significantly increased liver mass recovery compared to control. Although postoperative administration resulted in a slightly greater effect, the difference was not statistically significant. Therefore, IMT504 was administered one day after surgery in subsequent experiments.

Then, to analyze the kinetics of liver regeneration following IMT504 treatment, liver mass restoration and liver regeneration rate (LRR) were assessed at days 2, 6 and 10 after PHx. As shown in Fig. 1A, IMT504 treatment accelerated liver regrowth at days 2 and 6 post-surgery compared to the hepatectomized control group. Strikingly, IMT504-treated mice achieved nearly 100% of their original liver mass by day 6, whereas the vehicle-treated group reached full recovery between days 7 and 10, as previously reported8,9.

In addition, the LRR was calculated using a previously published formula10. LRR values were significantly higher on days 2 and 6 post-surgery in the IMT504-treated group compared to control (Fig. 1A, right). Interestingly, the control group exhibited a significant decrease in LRR by day 6 post-PHx (p < 0.001; compared to day 2 post-injury; Tukey’s multiple comparisons test), whereas LRR remained stable over the same period in the IMT504-treated group (p > 0.5). By day 10 post-resection, LRR values converged, and no significant differences were observed between the groups. These findings indicate that IMT504 enhances the natural regenerative process without impairing body’s ability to regulate liver mass recovery.

Induction of hepatocyte proliferation and enhanced expression of liver regeneration markers by IMT504

Hepatocytes are the primary cells responsible for proliferating and restoring most of the parenchymal cells after PHx due to their ability to self-replicate1,11. In these mouse models, hepatocytes rapidly initiate cell division, reaching their proliferation peak between days 1 and 2 post-surgery12. Histological analyses were conducted to investigate the mechanisms underlying the effect of IMT504 on the enhancement of liver regeneration. Two or six days after surgery, IMT504- and vehicle-treated hepatectomized CD1 mice were sacrificed to analyse cell proliferation on liver tissue by PCNA immunostaining. As shown in Fig. 1B, IMT504 treatment significant increased hepatocyte proliferation at both days 2 and 6 post-hepatectomy, as evidenced by a higher number of PCNA-positive cells. These findings suggest that IMT504 accelerates liver mass recovery by enhancing hepatocyte proliferative activity during both early and late phases of liver regeneration. These findings were confirmed by mitotic index analysis performed at day 2 post-surgery (Fig. 1C).

To assess the effects of IMT504 on the expression of genes involved in liver regeneration, qPCR analyses were performed on liver tissue collected two days post-surgery (Fig. 1D). Several proliferation markers were significantly upregulated in the IMT504-treated group, including Ccnd1, a positive regulator of cell cycle progression and a downstream target of Wnt/β-catenin signaling1; Pcna, a marker of cell proliferation5,13; Hgf, a key mitogen for hepatocytes13; and Notch2, which is involved in progenitor cell differentiation toward the hepatocyte lineage and promotes proliferation of hepatocyte precursors14,15. Furthermore, IMT504 treatment upregulated genes associated with DNA synthesis and repair, including Bcra2, Myb and Abl116–18, while downregulating Gadd45α, a known inhibitor of cell growth19. Interestingly, Igf1, a marker of hepatocyte function, and two liver progenitor cell markers, Prom1 (CD133) and Tnfsf12 (TWEAK), were also significantly overexpressed in response to IMT504 treatment20,21. A strikingly similar gene expression profile was observed in samples collected six days post-PHx (Figure S2).

These results suggest that a single dose of IMT504 accelerates liver regeneration by exerting a sustained positive effect on hepatocyte proliferation and potentially activating hepatic progenitors.

Dynamic changes in the expression levels of liver regeneration markers after IMT504 treatment

Based on our previous identification of liver regeneration genes modulated by IMT504 during both the early and late phases of repair following PHx, we further examined the expression dynamics of selected genes involved in cell proliferation, liver function, and hepatic progenitor activity. For this analysis, liver samples were collected at the time of PHx (day 0) and at 2-, 6- and 10-days post-surgery. Day 0 served as the baseline control; day 2 corresponded to the peak of hepatocyte proliferation; day 6 reflected a more advanced stage of regeneration; and day 10 marked the completion of liver regrowth and the end of the regenerative process9,22.

We first analyzed the temporal expression patterns of Pcna and Hgf, markers of cell proliferation and pro-regenerative signaling, respectively (Fig. 2). Two days after PHx, Pcna and Hgf levels were elevated approximately 2- to 4-fold in vehicle-treated mice, while IMT504-treated mice exhibited a more pronounced upregulation of about 5- to 7-fold. By day 6, expression levels in the vehicle-treated group had returned to baseline. In contrast, Pcna and Hgf expression remained elevated in the IMT504 group, maintaining a ~ 3- to 4-fold increase relative to baseline. By day 10, expression levels were comparable across both groups and no longer significantly different from baseline values.

We next assessed the expression of Igf1 mRNA, a marker of liver function, during the course of regeneration. At 2-days post-PHx, Igf1 was downregulated by approximately threefold in the saline-treated group compared to day 0. In contrast, IMT504-treated animals showed a significant upregulation of Igf1, both relative to baseline and to the saline-treated group. By day 6, Igf1 expression had increased substantially in both groups, with significantly higher levels detected in IMT504-treated mice. At day 10, transcript levels remained elevated compared to baseline, although no significant differences were observed between the two treatment groups.

Finally, we examined the expression patterns of two hepatic progenitor cell markers, Prom1 (CD133) and Tnfsf12 (TWEAK). At 2 days post-PHx, both genes were upregulated approximately 2- to 3-fold in vehicle-treated mice, whereas IMT504-treated mice showed a more robust increase of about 6- to 7-fold. By day 6, expression levels in the vehicle group had returned to baseline or showed no significant difference compared to day 0. In contrast, expression remained elevated in the IMT504 group, with a ~ 3- to 4-fold increase relative to baseline. By day 10, expression levels of both genes had returned to baseline or was slightly lower than day 0 levels.

IMT504 enhances the contribution of GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs to HLCs and ELCs after partial hepatectomy

As previously demonstrated using an in vivo genetic lineage tracing model, IMT504 promotes the proliferation and mobilization of GLAST+ Wnt1+ bone marrow stromal progenitors (GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs) during liver fibrogenesis, enhancing their migration into peripheral blood and their contribution to endothelial- and hepatocyte-like cells (ELCs and HLCs)5. Moreover, our findings suggest that GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs play a role in the antifibrotic and proregenerative effects mediated by IMT504.

To determine whether IMT504 enhances recruitment of GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs to the liver following PHx, we performed the surgery in GLASTCreERT2;Rosa26Tom (TxP60) mice to trace Tom+ cells in hepatic tissue. Mice received IMT504 or saline (control) treatment five days post-surgery and were sacrificed five days later (10 days post-PHx) for histological and flow cytometry analyses.

Histological analyses revealed a significant increase in the number of Tom+ cells in the livers of IMT504-treated animals compared to vehicle-treated controls following PHx. Notably, many Tom+ cells exhibited morphological features characteristic of endothelial cells and were localized within hepatic sinusoids (Figure S3A and data not shown). The predominant Tom+ phenotype, observed at a frequency at least four-times greater than that of ELCs, corresponded to hepatocytes (Fig. 3A-E). Flow cytometry further confirmed a significant increase in CD31+ Tom+ ELCs within the non-parenchymal cell-enriched fraction (Figure S3B), as well as Albumin+ Tom+ HLCs within the parenchymal cell-enriched fraction (Fig. 3F) in IMT504-treated animals. Remarkably, IMT504 also promoted the proliferation of Albumin+ Tom+ HLCs even at 10 days post-PHx (Fig. 3F) -a stage typically associated with the completion of liver regeneration and cessation of cell proliferation-. These findings indicate that IMT504 enhances the contribution of GLAST+ Tom+ BMSPs to both ELCs and HLCs, thereby supporting liver regeneration after PHx.

We previously demonstrated in vivo that GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs contribute to CD31+ Tom+ ELCs and Albumin+ Tom+ HLCs following PHx5. This was shown by transplanting bone marrow mononuclear cells from Wnt1Cre;Rosa26Tom donor mice into athymic recipients five days after PHx. In the non-parenchymal fraction, all Fibronectin+ Tom+ cells expressed CD31 at both 1- and 5-days post-transplant. Within the parenchymal fraction, approximately half of the Fibronectin+ Tom+ cells expressed albumin at 1 day post-transplant, and by day 5, all had acquired this hepatocyte marker5.

We employed a similar experimental approach to determine whether IMT504 enhances the contribution of GLAST + Wnt1 + BMSPs to ELCs or HLCs. IMT504 was administered to Wnt1Cre;Rosa26Tom mice 6 h prior to isolating bone marrow mononuclear cells, which were then injected via tail vein into PHx athymic mice 5 days after surgery. One day post-transplantation, IMT504 treatment resulted in a significant increase in the proportion of CD31+ Tom+ ELCs in the non-parenchymal fraction, as well as Fibronectin+ Tom+ cells in the parenchymal fraction, likely transitioning toward Albumin expression (Figures S4 and S5). These results indicate that IMT504 significantly enhances the contribution of GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs to Tom+ ELCs and Tom+ HLCs in the regenerating liver and promotes sustained proliferation of Tom+ HLCs. Collectively, these findings suggest that GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs support liver regeneration after IMT504 treatment.

IMT504 enhances the hepatogenic potential of GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs

As previously demonstrated, in vivo IMT504 administration following PHx increases the proportion of Tom+ cells expressing hepatocyte and endothelial markers in the liver. To determine whether IMT504 pretreatment influences the acquisition of these phenotypes in GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs -and to assess whether Tom− BMSPs lack this potential- we performed an in vitro priming assay. Tom+ and Tom− cells at passage 8, isolated from GLASTCreERT2;Rosa26Tom, were treated with IMT504 or DMEM (control) for 2 h, followed by a 4-hour incubation without IMT504. Subsequently, cells were cultured for 7 days in conditioned medium (CM) derived from regenerating liver tissue collected 3 days post-Hx.

Prior to this, we determined the optimal CM dilution for inducing endothelial and hepatocyte marker expression. Tom+ cells were exposed to 1/3, 1/4, 1/8 dilutions of CM, as well as to DMEM alone. Of these, only the 1/8 dilution significantly increased transcript levels associated with the targeted phenotypes5. Based on these results, the 1/8 dilution was used in the current assay. Expression of several endothelial and hepatocyte markers was subsequently evaluated by qRT-PCR and flow cytometry.

Remarkably, only Tom+ cells incubated with CM derived from PHx mouse livers consistently upregulated endothelial cell markers such as CD31, von Willebrand factor (vWF) and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) (Fig. 4), as well as hepatocyte markers including alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), albumin, glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase) and hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha (HNF4-alpha) (Fig. 5). Importantly, priming with IMT504 further enhanced transcript levels of both endothelial and hepatocyte markers and increased the proportion of cells exhibiting these phenotypes in culture. Additionally, Tom+ cells exposed to PHx-CM showed improved glycogen storage capacity (Fig. 6). Given that albumin is a functional protein secreted by hepatocytes, these results indicate that GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs are not only the sole subpopulation capable of acquiring endothelial and hepatocyte marker expression under these in vitro conditions but also have the potential to differentiate into functional hepatocyte-like cells in response to hepatogenic cues.

Discussion

Chronic liver disease affects over 844 million people worldwide and is a leading cause of fibrosis, cirrhosis, and more than 2 million deaths annually23–25. Currently, liver transplantation remains the only curative treatment for advanced cirrhosis or HCC. In addition, among patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) liver metastases, curative hepatic resection is feasible in only about 30% of cases, as extensive surgeries often result in inadequate functional liver remnants26. Our previous studies demonstrated that IMT504 exerts potent antifibrotic effects in the liver by directly targeting resident macrophages, myofibroblasts and hepatocytes5. In the present study, a single dose of IMT504 significantly accelerated liver mass recovery following partial hepatectomy, achieving near-complete regeneration by day 6, 1 to 2 days earlier than in controls. Similar regenerative effects have been reported with Fas receptor stimulation27 or treatment with IL-6/soluble receptor fusion proteins28.

Our data indicate that the pro-regenerative effects of IMT504 are primarily driven by enhanced hepatocyte proliferation during both early and late phases of liver mass recovery. Additionally, transcripts associated with hepatic progenitor cells -Notch2, CD133 and TWEAK- were upregulated at 1 and 5 days following IMT504 administration. Whether IMT504 directly activates liver progenitor cells, however, remains to be determined. Importantly, in PHx animals, IMT504 did not disrupt the physiological regulation of liver size once functional demands relative to body mass were fulfilled. Further studies are needed to explore whether IMT504 influences other aspects of liver regeneration -such as tissue remodelling or vascularization- to clarify whether its regenerative benefits extend beyond hepatocyte proliferation and partial restoration of liver function.

Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are a heterogeneous population known to exert beneficial effects in experimental models of liver injury, mainly via paracrine mechanisms. In this study, using in vivo transplantation and genetic lineage tracing, we demonstrate that GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs -a mesenchymal subset accounting for approximately 15% of all bone marrow CFU-Fs- contribute to tissue regeneration after PHx. Moreover, IMT504 enhances this contribution and promotes the proliferation of Tom+ HLCs, likely derived from GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs, even after liver regeneration is otherwise complete. Previously, we showed that GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs exhibit significantly greater motility than other BMSP subpopulations, potentially due to elevated Wnt signaling5. These cells are located near bone marrow sinusoids within hypoxic niches, which may increase their responsiveness to injury-associated cues, mechanisms also observed in other tissue repair models29–31.

Other types of bone marrow cells have been reported to contribute to the endothelial cells of sinusoidal capillaries32,33. In our transgenic mice, bone marrow- and peripheral blood-derived Tom+ cells were previously shown to exhibit stromal phenotypes and did not express markers of hematopoietic stem cells (CD45 and Sca-1), endothelial progenitors (CD31 and CD11b), granulocyte monocyte progenitors (CD11b and c-Kit) or myeloid/lymphoid lineages (Sca-1)34. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that some Tom+ hepatocytes or endothelial-like cells could result from fusion of GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs with liver cells.

Nevertheless, notably we demonstrated that GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs -even at passage 8 in vitro- were likely the only population capable of acquiring endothelial characteristics after just 7 days of incubation with CM from PHx liver. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that MSCs can differentiate into endothelial-like cells in response to specific microenvironmental cues. For example, bone marrow stromal cells cultured with VEGF and other angiogenic factors upregulated endothelial markers and displayed functional properties such as tube formation and improved blood perfusion in murine models of hindlimb ischemia35,36. Although we did not perform functional endothelial assays in this study, our data suggest that GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs may represent the only bone marrow stromal subpopulation capable of acquiring endothelial traits at least under the short-term conditions of our protocol.

Furthermore, GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs appear to be the only BMSP subset capable of acquiring hepatocyte markers in vitro following 7 days of exposure to PHx-derived conditioned medium. These differentiated cells expressed albumin and accumulated cytoplasmic glycogen, hallmarks of functional hepatocytes. Collectively, our in vivo and in vitro findings support the view that the acquisition of hepatocyte-like phenotypes by certain MSCs, as reported by multiple independent studies37, is not merely an artifact of culture conditions or a reflection of induced plasticity. Rather, this capacity represents an intrinsic property of a distinct BMSP subpopulation co-expressing GLAST and Wnt1, which is detectable as early as postnatal day 2 in mice.

In addition, IMT504 upregulated endothelial and hepatocyte-specific markers and increased the proportion of CD31+ and albumin+ cells in primary cultures incubated with PHx CM. This is particularly noteworthy given that IMT504 induces an approximately 4-fold increase in Wnt1, β-catenin, and several Wnt/β-catenin target genes in GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs5. While sustained Wnt activation could theoretically promote hepatogenic specification while impairing terminal differentiation, our data do not support this concern, as late hepatic markers, such as glucose-6-phosphatase38, were also upregulated in IMT504-pretreated GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs. Nonetheless, additional studies are needed to fully assess the functional capacity of BMSP-derived HLCs and to compare their phenotype and performance directly with primary cultured hepatocytes.

Our findings support several key conclusions. First, IMT504 directly stimulates hepatocyte proliferation throughout the course of liver mass recovery in the PHx mouse model and may also activate liver progenitor cells. Second, IMT504 promotes the proliferation and mobilization of GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs in response to liver injury and enhances their acquisition of ELC and HLC phenotypes. Notably, BMSP-derived HLCs also proliferate, thereby expanding the bone marrow´s contribution to liver regeneration. In this study, we have not addressed whether bone marrow-derived stromal cells could promote or enhance liver regeneration. With this regard, we have previously shown that conditioned medium from IMT504-treated GLAST⁺ Wnt1⁺ BMSPs induces the expression levels of HGF and IGF in primary cultured hepatocytes, suggesting that Tom⁺ cells may exert pro-regenerative effects5.

Small-for-size syndrome (SFSS) is a serious complication that can occur after living donor liver transplantation or major hepatic resection, characterized by functional graft insufficiency due to inadequate liver volume. In murine models, SFSS typically arises after resections of 80% or more, which are often associated with high mortality39 and delayed hepatocyte proliferation40. Our data show that IMT504 promotes liver regeneration in a 2/3 hepatectomy model, suggesting a potential proregenerative effect that could be relevant in more severe scenarios. However, further studies are required to confirm its efficacy in SFSS.

Clinically, SFSS presents with cholestasis, coagulopathy, and ascites within the first postoperative week41–43. Its pathogenesis is multifactorial, involving insufficient graft size, portal hyperperfusion, venous outflow impairment, sepsis, and donor/recipient-specific variables41,44. IMT504 may offer therapeutic benefit in this setting by stimulating liver regeneration, enhancing hepatic function, reducing fibrosis, and attenuating inflammation43. Additionally, our findings may support the future application of adult bone marrow-derived cells in tissue replacement therapies and in vitro organoid models for pharmacological and disease research.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that IMT504 significantly enhances liver regeneration following partial hepatectomy. Future clinical studies are essential to evaluate its efficacy, safety, and optimal dosing. Additionally, further research is needed to identify whether a human equivalent of murine GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs exists and contributes similarly to liver regeneration.

Methods

Oligodeoxynucleotide

ODN IMT504 with a phosphorothioate backbone was purchased from Biosynthesis (Lewisville, TX), purified by high-performance liquid chromatography, and assayed by MALDI. Lyophilized ODN IMT504 was diluted in depyrogenated water, tested for lipopolysaccharide contamination, and stored at -20 °C until use. The sequence of ODN IMT504 is 5′ TCATCATTTTGTCATTTTGTCATT 3′.

Animal models

Wnt1 Cre mice (Jackson stock Nº 022501) were bred with Rosa26td − Tomato (Rosa26Tom; Jackson stock Nº 007909) mice. GLASTCreERT2 mice were bred with Rosa26td − Tomato (Rosa26Tom) mouse on a CD1 genetic background5. GLASTCreERT2;Rosa26Tom mice were s.c. injected with tamoxifen (Tx; Sigma-aldrich; T-5648; 20 mg/mL in corn oil; 50 µl/animal) at postnatal day 2 (P2) or P60. Both male and female mice were used in the experiments. Animal care was conducted in accordance with NIH ethical guidelines, and the experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care Committee of the School of Biomedical Sciences, Universidad Austral (CICUAL IIMT CONICET-Universidad Austral protocols: 2019-03, 2021-06, and 2022-09). All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Partial hepatectomy model

Partial hepatectomy of 2/3 of liver mass was performed following previously published protocols22. In brief, to establish this model, mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane at an oxygen flow rate of 0.5–1.0 l/min. An incision was made in the peritoneal cavity and held open with a pediatric blepharostat. Using swabs and forceps, the left lobe of the liver was mobilized to expose its underside, where a Vicryl Plus suture thread (Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc.) was placed. The lobe was repositioned, and two knots were tied around its base using both ends of the thread. After securing the knots, most of the left lobe was excised, taking care not to disturb the suture. The same procedure was then performed on the right lobe, leaving only the quadrate and caudate lobes. Following surgery, the abdominal incision was closed with sutures, and the animals were allowed to recover on a heating pad until the effects of anesthesia wore off.

To evaluate liver regeneration and the underlying mechanisms, two-month-old CD1 mice were administered with IMT504 (0.4 mg/ml; 6 mg/kg/animal; s.c.) or vehicle, either one day before or one day after hepatectomy and were sacrificed at 2-, 4-, 6-, and 10-days post-surgery. Liver samples were analyzed by morphometry (liver mass measurements), gene expression (qPCR), and histology.

To identify cells derived from GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs, two-month-old GLASTCreERT2;Rosa26Tom mice injected with Tx at P60 were treated with IMT504 five days post-hepatectomy and were sacrificed five days later. Bone marrow, blood, and liver samples were collected for flow cytometry and histological analysis.

Liver weight restoration was expressed as the percentage of regenerated liver mass relative to the original liver weight. It was also expressed as the liver-to-body weight ratio.

Tissue regeneration analyses

Immunohistochemistry for proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) was performed to analyse regeneration in hepatic tissue. Liver samples were fixed in 10% formalin and processed for paraffin embedding. Sections of 8 μm were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 in ethanol, and biotin/avidin blocking was performed using a commercial kit (Vector Laboratories). Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against PCNA (PC10, sc-56, 1:100; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), followed by incubation with biotinylated secondary antibodies and an avidin-biotin complex. The signal was developed using DAB, nickel ammonium sulphate, and H2O2. Negative controls exhibited only a weak background. Images were acquired with a Leica ICC50 E microscope at 100x magnification and analyzed with ImageJ software. PCNA expression was quantified by counting positive nuclei in 80 images (100x) using a colour threshold detection system in ImageJ. In addition, we performed mitotic index analyses, following previously published protocols45.

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis by qPCR

For in vivo experiments, 30–50 mg of liver tissue was homogenized using a Polytron, and total RNA was extracted with TRIzol Reagent® (Invitrogen). For in vitro experiments, RNA was extracted directly from cultured cells (6 × 105) by adding TRIzol Reagent® and incubating for 2–3 min. RNA integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer.

RNA was treated with DNase I (Thermo Scientific) to remove genomic DNA. Briefly, 2 µg of RNA was incubated with DNase I for 30 min at 37 °C. Reverse transcription was then performed using SuperScript II reverse transcriptase (Life Technologies), Oligo(dT) primers, dNTP mix, and RiboLock RNase inhibitor (Life Technologies).

Gene expression was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) using an Agilent Aria thermal cycler with a SYBR Green-based master mix (Productos Bio-Lógicos, Argentina). The amplification program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 54–60 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min. A dissociation curve was included to verify product specificity. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH using the ΔΔCt method. All assays were performed in triplicate across two or three independent experiments. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Cell morphology in vivo analyses of Tom+ cells

Samples from the regenerated hepatic lobes were processed for cryostat sectioning34. Sections were stained with Hoechst 33258 or DAPI for 15 min, followed by additional washes. Finally, they were mounted with coverslips in an 80% glycerol solution in 1X PBS. Images were acquired using Zeiss LSM700, Zeiss LSM780, and Olympus IX-83-DSU confocal fluorescence microscopes. The number of Tom+ cells with different phenotypes was quantified by direct observation under a fluorescence microscope at 400×, considering only cells with visible nuclei in the analysis. For the morphological assessment of Tom⁺ hepatocyte-like cells, only those with a rounded nucleus and abundant cytoplasm, usually arranged within hepatocyte fascicles—features characteristic of hepatocytes—were included in the quantifications. For morphology assessments of Tom+ endothelial-like cells, small and flattened nuclei of cells surrounding vessel lumen were counted. A similar approach was also followed in previous publications5,34.

Transplantation of Wnt1-traced bone marrow mononuclear cells

In the transplantation experiments, BM-MNCs isolated from Wnt1Cre; Rosa26Tom mice were injected (1 × 10⁵ cells per animal) into the tail vein of NLAE: NIH(S)-Fox1nu/nu athymic mice that had undergone partial hepatectomy 6 days earlier. One day after cell injection, the animals were sacrificed under anesthesia by collagenase perfusion, and liver samples were collected for subsequent flow cytometry analysis.

Flow cytometry

Livers from hepatectomized double-transgenic mice were dissected and incubated in 1% collagenase (C5138, Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 37 °C. The tissue was then minced with scissors and incubated in the same solution for an additional 30 min. Cells were mechanically disaggregated using a micropipette, and the resulting suspension was filtered through 40 μm mesh. 5 × 105 cells were used per sample. Cells were stained for anti-albumin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-37467), anti-CD31 (BD Pharmingen, 553370) and/or anti-Ki67 (abcam ab15580). Autofluorescence and single-label controls were included in all analyses. During staining procedures, cells were washed three times with FACS buffer (1xPBS, 1% BSA) by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 minutes and then resuspended. Cells were then incubated for 45 min with unconjugated primary antibodies diluted 1/100 in FACS buffer containing 0.1% saponin. Following two washes with FACS buffer, cells were incubated for 45 min with secondary antibodies conjugated to AlexaFluor 488® and/or AlexaFluor 647® (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 1:100 dilution in FACS buffer with 0.1% saponin. After two additional washes, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stored in 200 µL of 1x PBS until flow cytometry analysis. Flow cytometry was performed using a Becton–Dickinson FACSAria Fusion (Leloir Institute Foundation), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software, version v10.0.8 (FlowJo, Ashland, OR, USA). The same protocol was applied to cell culture experiments.

Bone marrow stromal progenitor cell (BMSP) cultures

Two-month-old GLASTCreERT2;Rosa26Tom (TxP2), weighing between 20 and 25 g, were sacrificed by cervical dislocation by a trained person. Femurs and tibias were dissected, and bone marrow was flushed using low-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco). The resulting cell suspension was seeded in 145-mm Petri dishes (Greiner Bio-One) at a density of 5 × 105 cells/cm2. Cells were maintained in the same culture medium, which was replaced every 72 h, until reaching 90% confluence. Tom+ cell colonies were identified by fluorescence microscopy (Nikon Eclipse NiE, Melville, NY, USA). The margins of the Tom+ colonies were delineated on the lower surface of the Petri dishes. The Tom+ cell colonies were harvested first, followed by the Tom− colonies. They were trypsinized (0.25% trypsin with 1 mM EDTA), dissociated, and reseeded at 5 × 103 c/cm2 in fresh medium. This procedure was repeated for the first three passages, resulting in cultures enriched in GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs and others composed by Tom− cells. In both Tom+ and Tom− cultures, trypsinization and reseeding were continued at 80–90% confluence until passage 8 (P8). This protocol yielded populations enriched in GLAST+ Wnt1+ BMSPs (~ 85%) and Tom− BMSPs (~ 100%), which were used in subsequent analyses.

Incubation of BMSPs with CM from hepatectomized livers

GLAST+ Wnt1+ (Tom+) BMSPs or GLAST− Wnt1− (Tom−) BMSPs at passage 8 (P8) were incubated in semi-confluent cultures with conditioned medium (CM) from hepatectomized livers, diluted to 1/8, or with standard medium composed of 2% FBS (Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich), and 0.2% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco; 15070063) in low-glucose DMEM (Elessar Científica) for 7 days. The medium was replaced every 36 h. In some assays, cells were pre-incubated with IMT504 (0,5 µg/ml) in serum-free medium prior to the addition of DMEM or CM: cells were treated with IMT504 for 2 h, followed by a 4-hour recovery period without ODN.

For CM preparation, three adult CD1 mice underwent PHx and were sacrificed three days later. Livers were dissected and sectioned into 4 × 4 × 4 mm pieces. Tissue pieces were selected from each lobe, avoiding necrotic regions, and incubated overnight in 6-well plates with 1 mL of serum-free low-glucose DMEM at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was then collected, centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was aliquoted into 1,5 mL tubes for subsequent use.

For periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining, cells cultured in P6-well plates were incubated with 1% periodic acid (Biopur Diagnostics, cat. # 158) for 10 min. After washing with distilled water, cells were incubated in Schiff`s reagent for 15 min. Cells containing glycogen deposits exhibited a pink coloration after 5 min in tap water.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise specified, each experiment was performed at least twice, with a minimum sample size of four. Data from independent experiments were pooled for analysis. Unless otherwise specified, results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using appropriate tests based on the number of groups and data distribution. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using Prism software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA).
