Predictors of rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease in anti-MDA5 dermatomyositis: a meta-analysis
Authors: Ting Liu, Xiaojian Ji, Yujie Wei, Yi Zhong, Jian Zhu, Chi Wang, Mianyang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAnti-MDA5 antibody-positive DM (MDA5+DM) is frequently complicated by rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease (RP-ILD). The current study aims to identify predictors for the development of RP-ILD in MDA5+DM, thereby providing an objective basis for early intervention strategies.MethodsWe searched the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and Cochrane Library databases and extracted studies published before December 2024. Two reviewers extracted data and assessed risk of bias. Population characteristics, clinical features and laboratory indicators were systematically summarized. For the overall main effects, results were presented as standardized mean differences (SMDs), odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% CIs.ResultsTwenty-seven cohorts with 2579 patients were included in this meta-analysis. The results demonstrated that clinical characteristics predictive of RP-ILD risk factors included advanced age, male sex, fever, skin ulcers and arthritis. Specifically, the RP-ILD group exhibited significantly elevated levels of laboratory parameters including CRP, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), ferritin, ESR, leukocytes, Krebs von den Lungen-6 (KL-6), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), cartilage glycoprotein 39, positive anti-Ro52 antibodies and high-titre anti-MDA5 antibodies. Conversely, the RP-ILD group demonstrated significantly reduced levels of lymphocytes, albumin, PaO2/FiO2 ratio and CD3+CD4+ T cell count.ConclusionThis meta-analysis identified multiple clinical and laboratory parameters as significant predictors of RP-ILD progression in MDA5+DM patients. High-quality prospective studies are essential to validate these associations and identify additional biomarkers for the early prediction of RP-ILD onset in MDA5+DM patients, which will facilitate timely therapeutic interventions aimed at reducing mortality.

Introduction

Anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 (MDA5) antibody-positive DM (MDA5+DM) represents a distinct clinical subtype of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM) characterized by marked phenotypic heterogeneity, with its core defining feature being the presence of antibodies targeting MDA5. MDA5+DM demonstrates a particularly high prevalence within Asian populations, accounting for ∼20–30% of all DM cases [1]. Patients typically present with cutaneous manifestations as the initial symptom, often exhibiting relatively mild or absent muscle involvement. Characteristic skin lesions include Gottron’s sign, heliotrope rash, periungual capillary dilation and cutaneous ulcerations [2].

MDA5+DM is frequently complicated by rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease (RP-ILD), which serves as the primary life-threatening complication and results in a remarkably poor prognosis [1, 3, 4]. RP-ILD constitutes the pivotal pathological process driving disability and mortality in patients with MDA5+DM. Over 90% of patients develop interstitial lung disease (ILD) during the disease course, with 38–70% progressing to RP-ILD [5]. RP-ILD is characterized by acutely worsening dyspnoea, hypoxaemia and rapidly evolving radiological manifestations, typically including bilateral diffuse ground-glass opacities, consolidations and traction bronchiectasis [6, 7]. Timely identification of high-risk individuals, followed by intensive immunosuppressive treatment has been shown to improve survival rates to >60% [8]. Currently, the clinical risk stratification for RP-ILD primarily relies on empirical criteria, which highlights the need for more precise, multidimensional data-driven tools to improve diagnostic accuracy and patient outcomes.

Meta-analysis that systematically integrate cohort data from MDA5+DM patients could not only quantitatively evaluate weights of risk factors, but also establish foundations for prediction models. However, most available meta-analysis focus on generalized DM or fail to specifically distinguish the MDA5+ subtype, resulting in potential confounding effects from other DM subcategories [9]. More critically, existing studies predominantly focus on end-stage outcomes such as overall mortality, with limited attention directed towards the crucial intermediate end point—RP-ILD. Although mortality prediction facilitates general risk stratification, it fails to inform timely intervention during the subclinical phase of ILD. In contrast, the dynamic progression of RP-ILD offers a theoretical basis for identifying optimal therapeutic windows. Notably, the predictive potential of emerging biomarkers in RP-ILD remains insufficiently explored and the quantitative relationship between this antibody and RP-ILD has yet to be fully established.

In the current study, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of predictors associated with the development of RP-ILD in MDA5+DM patients, providing a quantitative overview of patient characteristics and plasma-derived biomarkers, which may contribute to the early intervention of RP-ILD.

Methods

Data sources and inclusion/exclusion criteria

This study systematically searched four major English databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and Cochrane Library) for literature published before December 2024. The search strategy combined subject headings and free-text terms using Boolean operators, with core terms including ‘anti-MDA5’, ‘MDA5’, ‘dermatomyositis’ and ‘interstitial lung disease’ (detailed search syntax provided in Supplementary Materials). To ensure comprehensiveness, reference lists of included articles were manually screened. Studies were included based on the following criteria: (1) original research (e.g. cohort studies, case-control studies) focusing on anti-MDA5-positive DM with ILD; (2) provision of complete raw data enabling differentiation between [AQ]MDA5+DM patients with and without RP-ILD to allow for effect size calculation; (3) clear stratification of MDA5+DM patients into RP-ILD and non-RP-ILD cohorts. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) studies not specifically addressing RP-ILD in MDA5+DM; (2) studies lacking appropriate population or group definitions as required by the inclusion criteria; (3) publications with incomplete or inaccessible raw data; (4) reviews, case reports, case series, commentaries, editorials, theoretical discussions and duplicate publications; (5) studies with a Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) score ≤4 (high risk of bias) and (6) studies published >10 years ago. The study protocol rigorously adhered to the PRISMA reporting guidelines [10].

Study selection and data extraction

Two investigators independently performed literature selection, data extraction and risk of bias assessment. Discrepancies were resolved through cross-verification and discussion, with unresolved issues referred to a third researcher for adjudication until consensus was achieved. The screening process strictly followed predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The extracted data encompassed: (1) study characteristics (first author, publication year, country, study design); (2) population profiles (sample size, RP-ILD incidence, age and gender); (3) original clinical data from MDA5+DM patients, such as clinical manifestations, laboratory parameters, to ensure comprehensive capture of disease-specific metrics. Following data extraction, a uniform data transformation was performed using the mean variance estimation online tool [11, 12]. All extracted data were converted into the format of ‘mean ± standard deviation’ to ensure consistency for subsequent meta-analyses.

Literature quality assessment

Cohort and case-control studies were evaluated using the NOS [13], a validated tool comprising three domains and eight items, with a maximum of 9 points. Studies scoring ≤4 were classified as high risk of bias (low quality), those scoring 5–6 were classified as moderate risk of bias (medium quality) and those scoring ≥7 were deemed low risk of bias (high quality). Only moderate- and high-quality studies were included in this meta-analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK) and Stata 12.0 software (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA). For continuous variables, the mean difference (MD) with 95% CI served as the effect measure, while odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI was applied for categorical variables.

Heterogeneity among included studies was assessed using the Q-test and I2 statistic. A fixed-effects model was employed when P ≥ 0.1 and I2 ≤ 50%, indicating nonsignificant heterogeneity; conversely, a random-effects model was utilized when P < 0.1 or I2 > 50%, indicating significant heterogeneity. To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analyses and meta-regression.

Sensitivity analysis was conducted by recalculating pooled effect sizes using both fixed- and random-effects models, with sequential exclusion of individual studies to assess the robustness of the results. Stability plots were generated with individual studies on the vertical axis and pooled effect sizes with 95% CI on the horizontal axis; robustness was confirmed if the recalculated estimates remained within the original 95% CI after any study’s exclusion. The robustness of the findings was subsequently assessed through sensitivity analyses, which included recalculating the pooled estimates using alternative statistical models and by omitting each study individually.

Publication bias was evaluated via funnel plots supplemented by Egger’s regression test for risk factors reported in at least 10 studies, including LDH, ESR, anti-Ro-52 antibody, age, gender, myasthenia, arthritis, mechanic’s hands, Gottron’s sign and skin ulcers. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Graphical presentation of the results was generated using RevMan 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK) and the R programming language (version 4.1.1) for analysis.

Results

Literature search

Initial database retrieval yielded 2736 records, from which 1099 were removed as duplicates. Among the remaining 1637 publications, preliminary screening of titles and abstracts excluded 731 studies with insufficient relevance to MDA5+DM. Additionally, 848 non-research publications, including case reports, reviews and commentaries, were eliminated during initial screening phase. Following full-text quality assessment, 31 studies were excluded due to incomplete data or failure to meet predefined inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 27 cohort studies were included in the analysis after quality assessment using the NOS to evaluate the methodological rigour of observational studies. The screening workflow, along with specific exclusion rationales is comprehensively documented in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1).

Characteristics and quality assessment of included studies

The 27 included publications comprised seven Chinese and 20 English manuscripts [9, 14–39], with demographic characteristics detailed in Table 1. Collectively, these studies enrolled 2579 patients, all published within the last 5 years. Studies were carried out in Asia, with two studies in Japan and 25 in China, with two prospective and 25 retrospective studies, respectively. All investigations employed uniform diagnostic criteria for RP-ILD, defined as individuals developing progressive dyspnoea, hypoxaemia and radiographic interstitial abnormalities within three months of respiratory symptom onset, per established radiological parameters [40]. Patients were stratified into RP-ILD (n = 620) and non-RP-ILD (n = 1959) cohorts, with reported RP-ILD incidence ranging from 22.1% to 77.8% (median 47.3%). Methodological quality assessment revealed a median NOS score of 8.2 (Interquartile Range [IQR]: 7.5–8.7), with 23 studies meeting high-quality thresholds (≥7 points). The methodological quality of the included studies was generally high according to the NOS (Supplementary Table S1).

Risk factors for RP-ILD in MDA5+DM patients

This study systematically evaluated clinical and laboratory parameters through pooled analysis of MDs or odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs derived from at least two independent cohorts. The indicators were ultimately analysed across two domains: (1) clinical characteristics encompassing age, gender, infection, myasthenia, fever, mechanic’s hands, Gottron’s sign, V sign, shawl sign, skin ulcers and arthritis (Supplementary Table S2, Fig. 2); (2) laboratory biomarkers comprising CRP, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum ferritin, anti-Ro-52 antibody positivity, high-titre anti-MDA5 antibodies (+++), ESR, chitinase-3-like protein 1 (YKL-40), serum albumin, lymphocyte count, alanine peripheral blood CD3+CD4+ T cell subsets and oxygenation index (PaO2/FiO2 ratio). Baseline data from all cohorts were collected during the initial admission assessment, prior to therapeutic interventions to minimize confounding effects from subsequent treatments (Supplementary Table S3, Fig. 3).

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Pooled analysis of 19 cohort studies (n = 1436) identified advanced age (MD = 5.33, 95% CI 3.63–7.04; ROM [ratio of means [41]] = 1.11, 95% CI 1.07–1.15, P < 0.00001) and male gender (OR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.04–1.64, P = 0.02) as significant risk factors for RP-ILD progression in MDA5+DM patients (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B). Among immunological biomarkers, both anti-Ro-52 antibody positivity (OR = 2.87, 95% CI 1.52–5.40, P = 0.001) and high-titre anti-MDA5 antibodies (+++) (OR = 1.53, 95% CI 1.09–2.15, P = 0.01) were also significantly linked to higher RP-ILD risk (Supplementary Fig. S1C and D). Direct inflammatory manifestations, specifically fever (OR = 3.16; 95% CI 2.12–4.72; P < 0.00001) and cutaneous ulcers (OR = 1.71; 95% CI 1.11–2.61; P = 0.01), showed significant associations with RP-ILD (Supplementary Fig. S1E and F). Conversely, the presence of arthritis was associated with a reduced risk of RP-ILD (OR = 0.64; 95% CI 0.46–0.89; P = 0.008; Supplementary Fig. S1G). Characteristic skin manifestations such as mechanic’s hands, Gottron’s sign, V sign and shawl sign (all P > 0.05), along with myasthenia (P = 0.22) and infections (P = 0.71), showed no significant associations (Supplementary Fig. S2A–F).

Laboratory data

A systematic evaluation of predictive biomarkers for RP-ILD in MDA5+DM was conducted using data from 27 cohort studies, assessing 14 biomarkers across three categories: systemic inflammatory markers, biomakers of tissue damage and immune dysregulation indicators. Complete results for all laboratory indicators are summarized in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S3.

Elevated systemic inflammatory markers including CRP (MD = 6.18, 95% CI 3.71–8.64, P < 0.00001), ESR (MD = 8.39, 95% CI 6.74–10.05, P < 0.00001) and leucocyte count (MD = 1.19, 95% CI 0.39–1.98, P = 0.003)—were significantly associated with RP-ILD risk (Supplementary Fig. S3A–C).

Markers of tissue injury (Supplementary Fig. S3D–H), including LDH (MD = 75.94, 95% CI 64.20–87.67, P < 0.00001), AST (MD = 24.35, 95% CI 8.74–39.96, P = 0.002) and ALT (MD = 26.20, 95% CI 10.04–42.36, P = 0.001), showed strong positive correlations with RP-ILD. KL-6 exhibited a borderline association (MD = 342.31, 95% CI −0.82 to 685.43, P = 0.05) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 80%), while CK showed a nonsignificant increasing trend (MD = 37.57, 95% CI −8.29 to 83.44, P = 0.11).

Key immune dysregulation biomarkers positively linked to RP-ILD risk included ferritin (MD = 703.75, 95% CI 338.66–1068.83, P = 0.0002), YKL-40 (MD = 24.80, 95% CI 11.34–38.25, P = 0.0003) and reduced CD3+CD4+ T cells (MD = −166.65, 95% CI −220.99 to −112.31, P < 0.00001) and lymphocyte counts (MD = −0.26, 95% CI −0.34 to −0.18, P < 0.00001) (Supplementary Fig. S4A–D). In contrast, serum albumin (MD = −2.83, 95% CI −5.32 to −0.34, P = 0.03) and oxygenation index (MD = −50.21, 95% CI −79.80 to −20.62, P = 0.0009) were inversely correlated with RP-ILD risk (Supplementary Fig. S4E and F).

Sensitivity analysis and heterogeneity analysis

Sensitivity analysis performed using Stata 12.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA) indicated that the meta-analysis results for most clinical and laboratory indicators remained stable upon sequential exclusion of individual studies. However, exclusion of specific studies—such as that by Qiu et al. for CK and Han et al. for concurrent infection—significantly altered effect estimates and reduced heterogeneity, suggesting that these studies contributed notably to outcome variability. Similar influences were observed in skin ulcer and ALT analyses after omitting studies by Guo et al. and Mao et al. respectively.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across cohorts evaluating oxygenation index, infection, ferritin, KL-6, serum albumin, anti-Ro-52 antibody and age (Fig. 4A). Meta-regression revealed that heterogeneity in ferritin and KL-6 analyses was not significantly associated with age, sex ratio or study quality (Fig. 4C and E; P > 0.05), nor was it explained by clinical factors in oxygenation index studies (Fig. 4B; P > 0.05). In contrast, heterogeneity in anti-Ro-52 antibody analysis was potentially linked to sex differences (Fig. 4D; P < 0.05). Sensitivity analyses excluding outliers did not resolve heterogeneity, indicating sources beyond extreme values. Analysis of infection-related heterogeneity was limited by the small number of available cohorts (≤3).

Publication bias analysis

Funnel plots were employed to assess potential publication bias for indicators included in over 10 studies. Symmetric scatter distribution was observed across funnel plots for age, gender, arthritis, ESR, Gottron’s sign, heliotrope rash, LDH, mechanic’s hands, myasthenia, anti-Ro-52 antibody and skin ulcers (Supplementary Fig. S5A–K). Begg’s and Egger’s regression tests were performed for indicators with ≥3 pooled studies (Fig. 5). For male gender, Begg’s test indicated marginal no significance (P = 0.059), while Egger’s test showed significance (P = 0.004). In such discordant cases, Begg’s test took precedence due to its superior robustness to distributional assumptions. All remaining biomarkers demonstrated nonsignificant publication bias in both tests (P > 0.05), suggesting minimal bias susceptibility and supporting overall data reliability.

Discussion

Through systematic integration of contemporary cohort data on MDA5+DM, our meta-analysis identified 18 factors significantly associated with RP-ILD progression: inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR), tissue damage indicators (LDH, AST, ALT, ferritin, KL-6, YKL-40), immune parameters (lymphocyte count, CD3+CD4+T cell count, anti-Ro-52 seropositivity, high-titre anti-MDA5 antibodies) and clinical features (advanced age, male gender, fever, cutaneous ulcers, hypoalbuminemia, reduced PaO2/FiO2 ratio). Notably, not all MDA5-seropositive patients develop RP-ILD, as some exhibit chronic ILD progression [42]. Consequently, aggressive combined immunosuppressive therapy administered universally to seropositive patients may elevate risks of treatment-related adverse events [42]. This clinical dilemma underscores the imperative to identify serological biomarkers predictive of RP-ILD development to guide personalized therapeutic strategies. Epidemiological evidence reveals precipitous decline in survival curves within 6 months post-RP-ILD diagnosis, followed by stabilization thereafter [43], indicating this initial period constitutes the critical therapeutic window for early intervention. Current clinical guidelines, however, rely on empirical judgement rather than multidimensional evidence-based risk assessment systems for RP-ILD management.

Cutaneous ulcers and low muscle involvement: mechanism-guided predictive markers for high-risk RP-ILD progression in MDA5+DM

The MDA5 antibody drives the inflammatory process by recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns, thereby activating the downstream IFN expression cascade. Molecular studies confirm that skin lesions in MDA5+DM patients are significantly associated with local immune cell activation, infiltration and the overexpression of type I IFN-signature molecules. Notably, IFN-κ, specifically secreted by keratinocytes, displays a distinct and abnormally elevated expression exclusively within the lesional skin of MDA5+DM patients, suggesting a potentially key role in the pathogenesis of skin damage [44]. In contrast, the IFN signature profile observed in muscle tissue from MDA5+DM patients closely resembles that of healthy individuals, indicating minimal impact from this pathway activation within muscle. This tissue-specific IFN response pattern demonstrates that MDA5-mediated IFN pathway activation primarily targets the skin and lungs, while eliciting only a limited inflammatory response in muscle tissue; consequently, when the immune response driven by MDA5 antibodies focuses on the skin and lungs, muscle tissue involvement becomes inherently less likely. Further, our study observed that MDA5+DM patients presenting with cutaneous ulcers demonstrated a significantly increased risk of progression to RP-ILD compared with those without ulcers (OR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.11–2.61), a clinical finding highly consistent with its unique tissue tropism. In summary, less severe muscle involvement in MDA5+DM correlates with more severe skin and pulmonary manifestations and predicts a correspondingly increased risk of progression to RP-ILD.

Cytokine storm as an early warning for RP-ILD development in MDA5+DM

An exaggerated inflammatory response is a key pathological feature of MDA5+DM. Elevated serum inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-1β and IL-12 are closely associated with MDA5+DM complicated by RP-ILD [45]. This meta-analysis further confirms that multiple inflammatory biomarkers predict progression to RP-ILD in MDA5+DM.

CRP exhibits elevated levels (MD = 6.18, 95% CI: 3.71–8.64), which reflects systemic inflammation intensity and promote neutrophil alveolar infiltration through complement C5a pathway activation, and thereby accelerating epithelial damage. Multiple studies establish an independent association between elevated CRP and RP-ILD development: Xie et al. [4] and Wu et al. [46] identified CRP as a mortality risk factor in MDA5+DM, while heightened CRP significantly increases RP-ILD risk and correlates with elevated mortality in MDA5+ clinically amyopathic DM (CADM) patients [47]. Patients with high CRP levels warrant heightened vigilance for acute deterioration [48].

ESR demonstrates significant predictive value for RP-ILD in multivariate models. Gono et al. [43] associated ESR elevation with RP-ILD risk (OR = 1.8), noting improved prediction accuracy when combined with ferritin (AUC = 0.82); Li et al. [49] validated ESR’s independent predictive role (OR = 1.6), with KL-6 co-analysis optimizing sensitivity to 88%; Xie et al. [4] confirmed ESR as an independent predictor (MD = 8.39) via Bayesian modelling, showing enhanced high-risk cohort identification when used with CRP (NRI = 0.32). Although ESR’s predictive efficacy varies geographically and methodologically (higher AUC in Asian cohorts), synergistic application with ferritin and KL-6 remains a core strategy, provided infections or anaemia are excluded.

MDA5 (encoded by IFIH1) a cytosolic dsRNA sensor in the RIG-I-like receptor family is activated by picornaviruses and drives type I IFN and proinflammatory cytokine production. Overactivation may trigger autoinflammatory disorders [50], suggesting viral infections as potential initiating factors for MDA5+DM. Our analysis revealed a higher infection rate in the RP-ILD group (40.68%) vs non-RP-ILD (30.28%), though statistically insignificant; exclusion of Hanyuan Yuan et al.'s data yielded significant differences, but the limited cohort inclusion (three studies) and low sensitivity of pooled effect estimates necessitate validation with expanded cohorts.

Fever, a core manifestation of infection, often reflects disease activity in autoimmune conditions. Niu et al. [51] established fever as an independent predictor of poor prognosis in MDA5+DM-ILD, while our study confirmed significant association between fever and RP-ILD progression, indicating potential underlying infection.

Elevated biomarkers of organ damage herald progression to RP-ILD in MDA5+DM

Multiple studies have confirmed the predictive value of hypoxaemia indices and partial pressure of oxygen for patient mortality [32, 52]. This study further demonstrates that the oxygenation index (PaO2/FiO2 ratio) holds prognostic value for predicting progression to RP-ILD. Among MDA5+DM patients, the mean oxygenation index was significantly lower in the RP-ILD progression group compared with the non-progression group. A decline in this index indicates impaired gas exchange due to alveolar-capillary membrane damage, which is significantly associated with pathological features of RP-ILD such as diffuse alveolar damage and organizing pneumonia. An oxygenation index below 300 mmHg is one diagnostic criterion for adult respiratory distress syndrome [48]. In this meta-analysis, the oxygenation index range (67.7–180.6 mmHg) was substantially below this threshold, indicating more severe interstitial inflammation and fibrosis in MDA5+DM patients.

The serum concentration of KL-6, a specific product of alveolar type II epithelial cell injury, often signifies structural damage to the alveolar-capillary barrier when elevated (>500 U/ml). It shows significant correlations with reticular opacities (OR = 4.2) and honeycombing (OR = 6.7) on high-resolution CT (HRCT) [1]. In contrast, AST is a non-specific enzyme with broad tissue distribution, with the highest concentrations found in cardiac muscle, followed by liver and skeletal muscle, and only minimal presence in lung tissue. Clinically, AST is most commonly used as a quantitative marker of myocardial injury and is frequently applied in the diagnosis of conditions such as myocardial infarction and myocarditis. Previous studies have also reported associations between AST levels and disease severity or prognosis in patients with pneumonia, suggesting that AST may increase in the context of systemic inflammation or severe pulmonary disease [53, 54]. In our study, AST levels were significantly elevated in patients with RP-ILD and showed a positive correlation with KL-6 [1, 49], suggesting a relationship between AST elevation and more severe pulmonary involvement. However, given the relatively low abundance of AST in lung tissue, isolated pulmonary parenchymal injury is unlikely to fully explain marked increases in circulating AST. Therefore, in patients with MDA5+DM complicated by RP-ILD, elevated AST more likely reflects the combined effects of severe hypoxaemia, systemic inflammatory response and multi-organ stress rather than lung-specific damage alone. Potential contributing mechanisms include hypoxic hepatic injury secondary to respiratory failure, subclinical skeletal muscle inflammation and possible myocardial involvement. In inflammatory myopathies, AST may also rise in association with muscle injury, further underscoring its limited organ specificity. Importantly, previous studies have shown that an insufficient decline in AST levels (<30%) during early treatment (e.g. from baseline to 3 months) is associated with poor therapeutic response and persistent disease activity, highlighting its potential value in dynamic monitoring [9]. Nevertheless, the predictive utility of AST is constrained by its susceptibility to non-pulmonary confounders, including hepatic dysfunction, haemolysis and muscle injury. Accordingly, interpretation of AST elevation in clinical practice should be integrated with creatine kinase levels, cardiac biomarkers, comprehensive liver function tests and imaging findings to better define its source. Collectively, MDA5+DM may induce severe organic damage and elevated clinical biomarkers indicative of such damage warrant vigilance for progression to RP-ILD.

Immune dysregulation markers predict progression of RP-ILD in MDA5+DM

As an autoimmune disease, the assessment of immune status in MDA5+DM plays a crucial role in monitoring disease progression [55]. In MDA5+DM patients with RP-ILD, decreased CD3+CD4+T lymphocytes, anti-Ro-52 antibody positivity and high-titre anti-MDA5 antibodies serve as key immunological indicators reflecting disease activity and prognosis. Reduced peripheral CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in RP-ILD suggest T cell exhaustion and migration to lung tissue, contributing to local injury and peripheral lymphopenia [45]. Furthermore, given the potential involvement of infectious triggers like viral infections or other pathogens in disease initiation, these factors may also contribute to lymphocyte exhaustion. Previous research has developed a predictive model based on parameters including lymphocyte count, CD3+CD4+T cell count and anti-Ro-52 antibody status to assess the mortality risk in anti-MDA5-positive DM patients with ILD before treatment initiation [56]. It is noteworthy that the anti-Ro-52 antibody is not specific to this condition; it is frequently observed in primary SS (pSS), SLE, SSc and other diseases, and represents the most prevalent serological marker in IIMs [57]. Nevertheless, our analysis revealed significant differences in its presence when distinguishing MDA5+DM with or without RP-ILD. A study involving 265 MDA5+DM patients identified distinct subgroups using unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis. One subgroup (n = 85), characterized by a high risk of RP-ILD and a cumulative incidence of non-RP-ILD of only 32.9%, exhibited a higher prevalence of anti-Ro-52 antibodies and high-titre anti-MDA5 antibodies compared with other subgroups, with a mortality rate as high as 60% [58]. Additionally, dynamic monitoring of anti-MDA5 antibody titre changes holds potential value for assessing clinical treatment response. As such, the combined monitoring of these indicators signifying immune dysregulation in MDA5+DM holds promise for predicting the development of RP-ILD.

Potential impact of therapeutic interventions on baseline indicators and risk assessment

Although most of the studies included in this analysis defined laboratory measurements obtained at the time of initial hospitalization or diagnosis of MDA5+DM as baseline indicators, in real-world clinical practice some patients may have already received varying degrees of treatment before these tests were performed. This situation may introduce confounding when interpreting the association between so-called ‘baseline’ variables and the risk of RP-ILD, and therefore the potential influence of therapeutic interventions on these parameters should be carefully considered. Glucocorticoids, which represent a cornerstone of treatment for MDA5+DM and its associated ILD, can induce neutrophil demargination, suppress lymphocyte proliferation and alter T cell subset distribution, leading to increased peripheral leucocyte counts, lymphopenia and reductions in CD3+CD4+ T cell levels after corticosteroid or combined immunosuppressive therapy [59]. These treatment-related changes may cause systematic shifts in inflammatory and immunological markers and potentially exaggerate the apparent differences in inflammatory burden or immune suppression between patient groups. At the same time, glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressive agents can rapidly suppress inflammatory responses and reduce acute-phase reactants such as CRP and ESR, which may obscure the true contribution of disease activity to RP-ILD progression [60]. Similarly, muscle and liver enzyme levels—including CK, LDH, AST and ALT—often decline following effective control of inflammation and may reflect treatment response rather than intrinsic disease severity if measured after therapy initiation [61]. Furthermore, previous studies have suggested that biomarkers such as anti-MDA5 antibody titres and serum ferritin can change dynamically during treatment and correlate with disease activity and therapeutic response [62], indicating that prior or ongoing immunosuppressive therapy may modify their measured ‘baseline’ levels. In addition, rescue interventions such as plasma exchange or intensified immunosuppressive regimens may transiently reduce circulating inflammatory mediators or autoantibody levels, thereby influencing the stability and reproducibility of these markers as predictive indicators. Taken together, treatment exposure should be considered an important potential confounder when interpreting the risk factors identified in this study and their predictive value. Future investigations should aim to obtain strictly pre-treatment baseline data whenever possible or carefully account for treatment regimens, timing and intensity in study design and statistical analyses in order to improve the reliability and clinical applicability of these indicators for predicting RP-ILD risk in patients with MDA5+DM.

However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The included literature suggests that biomarkers such as BAFF [16], γ-GT, sCD206 [9, 26], galectin-9 [35], CYFRA 21–1 [32], IL-15, IL-1RA, IL-6, CXCL10, VCAM-1, CCL22 [37], CEA [14], SPP1, SAA1, KNG1 [17] and macrophage percentage [34] might have predictive value for RP-ILD. However, due to limitations including insufficient cohort size and heterogeneity between studies, it was not feasible to perform a pooled effect size analysis of these biomarkers in the present meta-analysis. Heterogeneity was observed in the analyses of oxygenation index, infection, ferritin, KL-6, serum albumin, anti-Ro-52 antibody and age. Meta-regression indicated that the heterogeneity in ferritin and KL-6 was not associated with age, sex ratio or study quality, and the heterogeneity in oxygenation index remained unexplained by the included clinical variables, whereas that in anti-Ro-52 antibody appeared partly related to sex distribution. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the pooled estimates, suggesting that the remaining heterogeneity may mainly reflect differences in study design and baseline population characteristics. As most studies reported univariate or unadjusted estimates, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Furthermore, as the included studies were predominantly conducted in East Asian populations and racial information was largely inferred from the country of study, the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups may be limited. Therefore, these conclusions require further validation in large-scale, multicentre, prospective cohorts encompassing diverse populations to more comprehensively assess their independent predictive value and broader applicability.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identifies older age, male sex, fever, skin ulcers, as well as elevated levels of ferritin, CRP, CK, LDH, AST, ALT, ESR, leukocytes, KL-6, anti-Ro-52 antibody positivity and anti-MDA5 antibody titres, plus reduced levels of lymphocytes, serum albumin, PaO2/FiO2 ratio and CD3+CD4+T cells as significant predictors of progression to RP-ILD in patients with MDA5+DM. Notably, arthritis demonstrated a protective effect against RP-ILD progression. These indicators not only reflect the underlying biological basis of disease activity but also provide quantifiable, objective evidence to support early intervention strategies. In the future study, high-quality prospective studies are essential to validate these associations and identify additional biomarkers for the early prediction of RP-ILD onset in MDA5+DM patients, which will facilitate timely therapeutic interventions aimed at reducing mortality.
