Classroom demands and resources and emotional labour strategies in Chinese after-school programs
Authors: Aonan Peng, Yue Song, Jiayan Zeng, Chenggang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Using the Classroom Appraisal of Resources and Demands (CARD), this study investigated the relationships between classroom demands and resources and emotional labor strategies among 259 full-time teachers participating in after-school programs in mainland China. Results showed that these teachers generally perceived higher demands than resources. Administrative demands emerged as the primary stressors, while general program resources offered the most support. Furthermore, emotional labor strategies differed significantly based on perceived demands and resources. Higher demands correlated with increased surface acting and reduced genuine expression, whereas greater resources was associated with more authentic emotional expression. Further analysis indicated that children with special needs, administrative demands, and the presence of additional adult predicted deep acting. These findings emphasize the importance of providing adequate resources and reducing administrative demands to support teachers’ emotional well-being in after-school programs. Practical implications and directions for future research are discussed.

Introduction

The teaching profession is widely recognized as one that entails significant demands and emotional challenges. Emotional labor, defined as modifying or suppressing authentic emotions to meet professional expectations [1], is linked to teacher stress, burnout, mental health, and student achievement [2, 3]. It is suggested that teachers’ perception of classroom demands and resources plays an important role in shaping their occupational life and emotional experiences [2, 4]. Demands act as occupational stressors (e.g., administrative tasks, classroom management), while resources (e.g., professional development opportunities, cooperation with colleagues) support teachers to cope with high levels of demands. However, a research gap remains regarding how after-school settings and sudden educational policy shifts influence these emotional regulation processes.

New educational policies may lead to changes regarding teachers’ perceptions of classroom demands and resources. Implemented in July 2021, the “Double Reduction” which aims to reduce the excessive homework and extracurricular tutoring has significantly reshaped the landscape of after-school programs in China [5]. Designed to reduce excessive homework and off-campus tutoring, this policy inadvertently created a severe imbalance between teachers’ appraisals of classroom demands and resources. Specifically, it brings after-school teachers extra workload, while reducing their tuoring-related income in off-campus training [6]. Acoording to Yin, many teachers reported that their after-school pay does not make up for the time and effort they put in. For example, some teachers received only $42 for more than 100 days of after-school work in a single semester [7]. This shift generated a unique occupational strain, yet the precise extent of this resource-demand imbalance remains unidentified.

Evaluating this imbalance requires distinguishing after-school programs from structured regular classrooms. After-school settings are characterized by flexible schedules, different student groupings, and extended platform for learning and personal growth [8, 9]. These features lead to different appraisals of classroom resources and demands, thereby requiring teachers to adapt a series of emotional labor strategies. Beyond structural differences, these strategies are deeply rooted in the Chinese cultural context. Influenced by Confucian values of collectivism and “face-saving”, Chinese educators are culturally used to prioritized social harmony and professional ethics [10]. Therefore, surface acting behaviors are more common in this context than other individualistic cultures.

Despite the critical role of these environmental and cultural factors, empirical research has yet to focus on how after-school teachers appraise classroom demands and resources and employ emotional labor strategies following the “Double Reduction” policy. Thus, this study investigates these variables among after-school teachers in mainland China. By quantifying this specific resource-demand imbalance and examining its relationship with teachers’ emotional labor, our research provides evidence-based insights to support teachers’ emotional well-being in this shifting educational environment.

Literature review

Classroom appraisal of resources and demands (CARD)

The Classroom Appraisal of Resources and Demands (CARD) was developed by Lambert et al. to assess teachers’ perceptions of work-related stress [11]. This instrument is based on Lazarus and Folkman’s transactional model of stress [12], which is considered a powerful tool for evaluating stress levels. According to Lazarus and Folkman’s hypothesis, stress is viewed as a transaction resulting from an imbalance between demands and resources [12]. Additionally, the CARD instrument incorporates “appraisal”, defined as a cognitive evaluation process when teachers encounter stressful situations [13]. In other words, classroom appraisal of resources and demands refers to how teachers perceive their available resources and required demands in classrooms, ultimately influencing their levels of stress. Teacher stress is strongly influenced by personal perceptions; therefore, the appraisal process can integrate both objective situations and individual perspectives [14]. Generally, demand-at-risk teachers are more likely to have burnout, low job satisfaction, and high turnover rate [15].

Previous studies have also shown that an imbalance between demands and resources can lead to increased stress and burnout among teachers [16]. For instance, Lambert et al. found that teachers who perceived higher demands relative to resources reported higher levels of job stress and lower job satisfaction [11]. This imbalance can affect teachers’ ability to perform their duties effectively and can have negative implications for their emotional labor. In summary, the cognitive appraisal of classroom demands and resources serves as a foundational predictor of teacher stress. Building on this theoretical background, the resulting stress may be managed and expressed through teachers’ daily emotional labor.

Emotional labor

In a general context, emotional labor involves the process by which individuals manage their emotions to fulfill the emotional requirements of their job [17]. Specifically, in an educational context, it is defined as the efforts made by teachers to manage negative emotions and express socially acceptable emotions [18]. Teaching is widely acknowledged as an occupation that involves significant emotional demands, which are associated with teacher stress, job satisfaction, and burnout [19, 20]. Following this definition, Diefendorff et al. identified three primary strategies of emotional labor: surface acting, deep acting, and genuine expression [21]. Surface acting involves altering one’s outward expressions without changing the underlying feelings, while deep acting involves modifying one’s internal feelings to align with expected emotions. Genuine expression refers to the display of authentic emotions that are consistent with one’s true feelings. Grandey and Gabriel proposed an integrative model framework that explicates how emotional labor encompasses enhancing, faking, and suppressing emotions for professional expression within the workplace [22]. This framework incorporates additional factors such as work motivation, emotional competence, job autonomy, administrative support etc., contributing to our understanding of emotional labor dynamics.

As a result, teachers are expected to display emotions in a more professional manner compared to other professions [23]. Ideal teachers are expected to exhibit emotions such as confidence, happiness, and passion in order to create a positive learning environment and enhance teaching effectiveness [24]. However, empirical evidence suggests that teachers of different ages and educational backgrounds often experience emotional labor [25]. Emotional labor is a more complex concept compared to teacher stress and burnout. Sutton argues that emotional labor is predominantly required in the teaching profession [26]. It is worth noting that emotional regulation plays a crucial role in shaping teachers’ professional identity and providing them with motivation [27]. Furthermore, when teachers display professional emotions rather than their genuine feelings during stressful situations, it can positively impact students’ well-being by creating a supportive context [18]. However, we cannot overlook the negative consequences of purposeful emotional control on teachers’ well-being as it often leads to burnout [28]. This explains why previous research has suggested that emotional labor results in various occupational side effects including burnout and turnover intention [22, 29, 30]. Recent studies further emphasized that managing these emotional requirements is more challenging in evolving and non-traditional educational settings, such as under the educational reforms [31, 32]. Despite existing studies highlighting the relationship between teacher stress, burnout, and emotional labor, this topic remains under-explored within after-school settings. As teachers often engage in emotional labor to maintain a positive and conducive learning environment, which can be particularly challenging in after-school settings where resources may be limited while demands may be overwhelming [33]. The extent to which teachers utilize these strategies can be influenced by their perception of classroom demands and resources. In summary, emotional labor is an everyday reality in teaching, involving strategies that range from surface acting to genuine expression. How teachers use these strategies is closely related to the actual demands they face and the resources they receive. This highlights the need to examine the direct link between teachers’ environmental appraisals and their emotional labor.

The relationships between classroom demands, resources, and emotional labor

Previous research has demonstrated a significant association between classroom demands and teacher stress; for instance, administrative work has been identified as one of the primary sources of teacher stress [34]. The most demanding aspects of the classroom environment were students with problem behavior and children exhibiting diverse special needs, which can be regarded as the main stressor of teaching [3, 35, 36]. The research evidence, however, was obtained in elementary school classrooms in America; thus, it remains unknown whether this conclusion is applicable to after-school classrooms in China. In terms of classroom resources, the argument posits that there exists a negative correlation between administrative supports and teacher stress within the scope of these resources in both western countries and China [37, 38].

In several stress models constructed by previous studies, stress was assessed using a difference score since occupational stress typically arises when demands outweigh available resources [12, 39, 40]. Therefore, stress and burnout occur when teachers are unable to cope with this imbalance between work demands and available resources. The findings of other studies have also indicated that the discrepancy between demands and resources significantly impacts teachers’ job satisfaction, self-efficacy, and even their pedagogical abilities [11, 37]. Despite the demands and resources existing in classrooms, previous studies have also indicated a potential correlation between teaching ages and stress levels. Specifically, novice teachers are more likely to perceive an imbalance between classroom demands and available resources [37]. However, it has been found that other demographic factors such as differences among schools and gender do not significantly impact teacher stress levels [15, 41].

Research evidence has shown that teachers’ perceptions of classroom demands and resources have a significant impact on their job satisfaction, burnout, and turnover rate [2, 15, 42]. Moreover, it has been found that emotional labor is also associated with these consequences. Research conducted in Chinese higher education settings revealed that surface acting exhibits a negative association with burnout, whereas deep acting demonstrates a positive relationship [43]. Nevertheless, these associations have not been explored within after-school classroom environments. Traditionally, studies have primarily focused on the relationship between teacher emotional labor and their teaching achievements. For example, Frenzel et al. discovered that teachers who display positive emotions are more likely to enhance students’ development [44]. However, suppressing genuine emotions and pretending false emotions requires additional effort from teachers, leading to stress and burnout [1]. Recent empirical evidence highlights that surface acting under high-demand contexts rapidly accelerates teacher exhaustion across different educational stages [45, 46]. The factors contributing to teachers’ emotional labor align significantly with their perceptions of classroom demands and resources since organizational factors are considered an important determinant of emotional management [47]. It is worth noting that perceived school climate positively correlates with deep acting while negatively correlating with surface acting. Specifically, a positive teacher-student relationship as well as support for instructional innovation and collaboration with colleagues promote deep acting [47]. Yin et al. also suggested that teachers with higher trust in their colleagues exhibit more authentic emotions and engage in less surface acting [48].

However, the relationship between emotional labor and teacher stress (the imbalance between classroom demands and resources) has received little discussion. Generally, a high level of surface acting is positively associated with occupational stress, leading to burnout and low job satisfaction [43, 49, 50]. On the other hand, deep acting has been found to have a positive correlation with high job satisfaction and low burnout [43, 51–53]. However, it is noted that deep acting requires teachers to exert additional efforts in suppressing natural emotions, which can also contribute to increased occupational stress and emotional exhaustion within classrooms [54, 55]. In terms of genuine emotions domain, it is widely suggested that experiencing genuine emotions leads to reduced stress levels and higher job satisfaction [56]. Nevertheless, the distinctions between negative and positive emotions need further exploration within the context of genuine emotions. According to Taxer and Frenzel, naturally felt negative emotions typically result in lower job satisfaction and burnout [57].

High classroom demands and low resources may exacerbate the need for emotional labor. For example, when teachers face excessive administrative tasks or insufficient support, they may resort to surface acting to cope with the stress [1]. Conversely, adequate resources and support can facilitate deep acting and genuine expression, promoting better emotional and mental health among teachers [58]. In the context of after-school programs, understanding how these dynamics play out is essential for developing interventions that support teachers’ emotional well-being and, in turn, enhance the effectiveness of these programs. The present study aims to fill the gap in literature by exploring the specific interplay between classroom demands, resources, and emotional labor strategies among after-school teachers in mainland China.

Other individual differences, such as teaching ages and gender may also affect emotional labor strategies. Teachers with more teaching experiences are more likely to show deep acting in classrooms [54, 57 ]. In terms of gender factors, it is suggested that female teachers are more likely to show naturally felt emotions, while male teachers have more surface acting than female teachers [52, 54]. However, another research involved Turkish teachers showed that male teachers have lower levels of both deep and surface acting than female teachers [53]. Thus, more research evidence is needed to examine the relationships between individual differences and teacher emotional labor.

Overall, existing evidence indicates that high levels of classroom demands combined with insufficient resources may predict negative emotional labor outcomes. To fully understand these relationships, it is necessary to examine how they operate within the specific, high-pressure context of current after-school programs.

Teachers in after-school classrooms

After-school service is considered as an extension of on-campus education, which prevents students from unsafe environments out of school [59]. In many countries, after-school service also plays a role in improving academic achievements and reducing educational inequality [60]. The establishment of after-school service in China is later than that in developed countries, whose aim differs slightly from those in other countries. Affected by “double reduction” policy, after-school service is regarded as an alternative option for students since other out-of-school tutoring. Recent studies showed that the expansion of these mandatory school-based services has influenced teacher workload and well-being [7, 61]. K-12 schools should offer a range of activities, including homework tutoring, physical activities, and so on. Namely, in-service teachers are responsible for carrying out various after-school activities, which may bring new demands or even stressors to them.

As discussed, after-school programs can be a significant source of stress for teachers. Unlike conventional classrooms, these settings feature flexible class sizes, different physical environments, complex instructional needs, and inconsistent external support from administrators or families. These differences bring new demands, require additional emotional labor, and call for different resources. Similarly, previous research has demonstrated that perceptions of emotional management are influenced by culture-specific patterns of emotions [62, 63]. Teacher emotional labor is heavily influenced by cultural expectations regarding professional norms. Building upon this perspective, prior studies have explored the emotional labor of Chinese physical education teachers and higher education teachers [43, 64], but little is known about the involvement of after-school teachers in emotional labor within the Chinese cultural context.

It is widely accepted that teachers are crucial in improving the quality of after-school programs [65]. However, the construction of after-school service teachers still faces significant challenges. Previous research focused on teachers’ salary, training, qualification, professional development, and interactions with students in after-school programs. For instance, after-school teachers were considered to have high turnover rate as the salary and professional training were limited in existing studies [65–67]. As a result, the quality of after-school programs, cognitive development and mental health of students would be impacted negatively due to the teacher issues [68, 69]. The existing research on the importance of teachers in after-school programs has primarily focused on physical aspects, neglecting the mental dimensions such as stress and emotions experienced by teachers. Little is known about their experiences, especially stress and burnout in after-school classrooms. Furthermore, investigating after-school teachers in the Chinese context offers critical international significance. As educational systems worldwide increasingly rely on extended school days and after-school programs to address educational inequality, the Chinese “Double Reduction” policy provides a unique, large-scale natural experiment. Findings regarding how Chinese educators balance sudden structural demands with limited resources can offer global policymakers valuable empirical evidence for designing sustainable teacher support systems across diverse cultural contexts. Although after-school programs serve an important educational purpose, the emotional challenges faced by teachers in demanding classrooms require further empirical attention.

Therefore, we aimed to employ a quantitative method to examine Chinese teachers’ stress and emotional labor in after-school classrooms using CARD scale and revised emotional labor strategies scale [11, 21]. The following research questions will be investigated in this study: (1) How teachers in the after-school classrooms appraised classromm resources and demands (2) What are the potential demographic factors that could be linked to then CARD and emotional labor strategies of after-school program teachers? (3) What are the correlations between after-school program teachers’ emotional labor strategies and classroom resources and demands? Method

Research design

This study employed a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design to investigate the relationships between classroom demands, resources, and emotional labor strategies among after-school teachers.

Participants

This study recruited 259 full-time teachers who participated in after-school programs (41 males, 218 females) from elementary schools (86.1%), junior middle schools (10.4%), and senior high schools (3.5%) in mainland China. Participants were included if they were full-time school teachers with a formal employment contract, and engaged in after-school program work for ≥ 20 h per week; teachers with part-time/voluntary after-school program participation or less than 3 months of relevant work experience were excluded. These teachers were distributed in both urban areas (64.9%) and rural area (35.1%). Additionally, these teachers were distributed across various teaching ages, encompassing those with 0 to 5 years of experience (37.1%), 6 to 10 years of experience (20.5%), 11 to 15 years of experience (6.6%), 16 to 20 years of experience (3.9%), and over 20 years of experience (32%). A power analysis using Gpower indicated that a sample of 259 was sufficient to achieve 80% statistical power for detecting a medium effect size (f = 0.25) across the hypotheses at the ɑ = 0.05 significance level.

Procedure

Most participants were recruited via an online questionnaire survey in mainland China. We informed all participants of the purpose of this research and the response rate was about 86%. All the data we collected was confidential and anonymous. Missing data, which accounted for approximately 14% of the initial responses, were handled using list-wise deletion because the missing was at random and the proportion was relatively small.

Instruments

Two instruments were employed in this study, including the Classroom Appraisal of Resources and Demands (CARD) and Emotional Labor Scale [11, 21]. CARD was used to measure teachers’ appraisal of classroom resources and demands in after-school programs. As discussed previously, the CARD requires teachers to rate different dimensions of the classroom environment according to their level of demand or helpfulness. It consists of 2 scales, namely the resources scale and the demands scale. The resources scale consists of 29 ratings of the severity of after-school classroom resources using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very unhelpful) to 5 (very helpful). The resources scale includes 3 subscales, including Specialized Resources (resources for children with special needs), General Program Resources (Support from administrative and colleagues, general teaching materials, and opportunities of professional development), and The Additional Adult (support from parents and other volunteers). The demands scale includes 42 items consisting of ratings of degree of classroom demands using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not demanding) to 5 (extremely demanding). 4 subscales are assessed in this scale, including The Administrative Demands, The Lack of Availability of Instructional Materials/Classroom Environment, The Children with Problem Behaviors, and The Children with Other Special Needs. Based on their scores on these two sub-scales, teachers can be categorized into three groups: resourced teachers, balanced teachers, and demand-at-risk teachers [11]. Following McCarthy et al., teachers were classified into these groups using a cut-off point of ± 1.96 standard errors of the difference [15]. The reliability of CARD was tested, as the Cronbach’s Alpha of Demands Scale is 0.962, the Cronbach’s Alpha of the Resources Scale is 0.969. The CARD has been validated for the Chinese context with confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) yielding excellent fit indices: CFI = 0.98, RMSEA = 0.08, TLI = 0.97 [37].

The Emotional Labor Scale developed by Diefendorff et al. was utilized to examine after-school teachers’ emotional labor strategies [21]. This 13-item scale comprised of 3 dimensions: Surface Acting (regulating personal emotional expressions), Deep Acting (modifying feelings to align with expected emotions), and Genuine Expression (expressing real emotions without occupational demands). A 5-point Likert Scale ranging from 1 (very disagree) to 5 (very agree) was used to rate each item regarding emotional labor strategies. The internal consistency of this scale was also commendable, with the Cronbach’s alpha is 0.802. This scale has been cross-culturally validated in the Chinese context using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The results supported a 3-factor solution (Surface Acting, Deep Acting, Genuine Expression) consistent with the original scale, demonstrating acceptable model fit (CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.91, RMSEA = 0.07) [70].

Results

Descriptive statistics and one-way ANOVA analysis

Research Question 1 was addressed with descriptive statistics, including teachers’ demands scores and resources scores. The results indicated that the teachers’ Demands score (M = 115.32; SD = 32.32) was higher than Resources score (M = 104.22; SD = 21.39) (See Table 2). These scores were categorized into 3 groups, resources greater than demands (Demands < Resources), resources equal to demands (Demands = Resources), and demands greater than resources (Demands > Resources). The three group distinction has proven to be valuable in previous research that tests transactional models of stress and coping in educational settings [11]. The standard error of measurement for the difference score was used to create a 95% confidence interval around a difference score of zero. The upper and lower bounds on this interval were used to classify teachers into one of three groups. When categorizing the difference scores for Demands and Resources, it was found that Group 1 (D < R) consisted of 101 teachers (39%), Group 2 (D = R) included 23 teachers (8.9%), and Group 3 (D > R) comprised of 135 teachers (52.1%). In response to the question about overall stress level of after-school teachers, we found that after-school program teachers generally experience a high level of stress, while a small number of teachers believe that the classroom resources were limited compared to the occupational demands. In terms of the main stressors of after-school program, the result shows that teachers reported administrative demands as the most demanding factor (M = 53.52, SD = 14.65). On the other hand, general program resources (e.g., supports from administrative leaders, colleagues, teaching materials, and professional development opportunities) were regarded as the most supportive resources (M = 75.17, SD = 15.71).

Regression analysis

We also employed emotional labor strategies as the dependent variable and dimensions of CARD as the independent variable to conduct multiple stepwise regression analysis (See Tables 5, 6 and 7,and 8). For all the teachers, the children with other special needs, the administrative demands, and additional adult could significantly predict deep acting. For group 3, (demanded group, D > R), deep acting was predicted by the additional adult. Genuine expression was predicted by the grade of the students.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the relationship between classroom demands and resources and emotional labor strategies among teachers in after-school programs in mainland China. Our findings provide valuable insights into how after-school teachers’ appraisal of classroom demands and resources influence their emotional labor strategies. Moreover, other individual differences also impact teachers’ emotional labor strategies in after-school programs.

Classroom demands and resources

The descriptive statistics revealed that teachers generally perceive classroom demands to be higher than classroom resources, indicating a high level of stress among after-school program teachers, which confirms our hypothesis 1. This finding aligns with previous research that highlights the demanding nature of after-school programs and the significant stress experienced by teachers in these settings [11]. This finding is important as it suggested that higher level of classroom resources is associated with higher job satisfaction, teacher well-being, and lower level of burnout [71–74].

As for the main stressor of after-school programs, the identification of administrative demands as the most significant stressor highlights the crucial role of administrative support in alleviating teacher stress. The findings of this study are consistent with the conclusion reached by Bradley, indicating that effective management of administrative tasks such as paperwork and meetings in after-school time is crucial for relieving teacher stress [34]. However, we did not find students with problem behaviors and special needs impose additional stress on after-school teachers, contrary to the claims in previous studies [4, 14, 35]. The limited inclusion opportunities for children with special needs and problem behaviors in mainstream classrooms in China may offer an explanation for this. Though China has implemented many policies to ensure universal access to education, opportunities for children with special needs remain limited [75]. Other research also showed that many students with special needs, like autism spectrum disorder (ASD) were denied enrollment in public schools, even were expelled due to teachers’ inadequate capacity to modify teaching strategies and effectively manage challenging behaviors [76]. Moreover, according to our result, schools are expected to provide teachers with more general resources, including administrative support, colleague support, and professional development opportunities to reduce their stress of high demands in after-school settings.

Emotional labor strategies

The one-way ANOVA analysis demonstrated significant differences in emotional labor strategies across groups with different levels of perceived demands and resources. Specifically, teachers in the high-demand group exhibited higher levels of surface acting and lower levels of genuine expression compared to those in the resourced group. This suggests that teachers experiencing higher stress are more likely to engage in surface acting, where they regulate their outward emotional expressions, and less likely to express genuine emotions. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that higher occupational stress leads to increased surface acting and decreased genuine emotional expression [1, 56, 77]. Namely, surface acting can be regarded as a strategy employed by after-school teachers to cope with overwhelming classroom demands. However, as surface acting has been proved to impact teacher well-being negatively [28, 43], the need for more effective implications concerning stress management is imperative. The findings of our study also suggest that it is beneficial for teachers in after-school programs to express their emotions naturally. However, the classification of genuine emotions into positive and negative categories was not undertaken in our study. Previous research has indicated that positive emotions are associated with favorable teaching outcomes, whereas negative emotions are linked to unfavorable teaching outcomes [44, 57]. We did not find strong correlations between individual factors and teacher emotional strategies, which warrants further exploration.

Relationships between classroom demands, resources, and teacher emotional labor strategies

The correlation analysis highlighted significant associations between dimensions of classroom demands and resources and emotional labor strategies. Prior to our study, limited research has been conducted on the association between classroom demands, resources, and teacher emotional labor strategies. Specifically, surface acting was found to be significantly correlated with various classroom demands, such as dealing with children with problem behaviors and administrative demands, indicating that higher demands exacerbate the need for teachers to regulate their emotional expressions. Notably, the correlation observed in our study between administrative demands and surface acting (r = 0.401, p<0.01) exceeds the associations typically reported in traditional classroom settings in Näring et al.’s study (r = 0.19). This quantitative difference reflects the unique and intensified occupational pressure introduced by the “Double Reduction” policy in China. This result addresses our hypothesis 2. The findings of previous studies have indicated the significance of organizational factors in teachers’ emotion management [56], and our result corroborates this perspective. This result is also consistent with the perspective proposed by Grandey, suggesting that teachers are more inclined to employ surface acting strategies as a method of coping with challenges such as excessive administrative demands and limited support [1]. On the other hand, genuine emotion expression was positively correlated with classroom resources, such as specialized resources and support from additional adults, suggesting that better resource availability facilitates more authentic emotional expressions among teachers. Grade also negatively predicted genuine emotion expression for demands group, indicating that for demands group, higher grade teachers were less likely to express genuine emotions, probably due to an increase in demands in grades for teaching high grade students. Moreover, deep acting is also positively correlated with specialized resources, general program resources, and the additional adult. Namely, the hypothesis 3 has been confirmed. This present finding is consistent with prior research, indicating that organizational support enhances teachers’ employment of deep acting strategy and genuine expression [58, 78, 79]. The implication of this is that after-school teachers require additional organizational resources to effectively manage their emotions when confronted with demanding situations, thereby enhancing teaching outcomes. Furthermore, our findings also expend this perspective by demonstrating a positive correlation between additional adult support, encompassing both volunteers and parents, and teachers’ utilization of deep acting strategies. Practically, schools should prioritize the enhancement of family-school-society interactions to ensure that teachers receive sufficient external support.

However, the controversial nature of deep acting strategy of teachers cannot be ignored. Existing emotional labor literature suggested that teacher emotional labor leads to negative effects in workplaces [22, 29, 30], the practice of deep acting and genuine expression is generally considered to have positive impacts on teachers’ teaching effectiveness, well-being, and mental health by improving their teaching commitment and motivation [48, 52, 80]. Recently, research has also indicated that deep acting strategy is associated with emotional exhaustion and reduced job satisfaction in classrooms [81]. Thus, the future research may also examine that to what extent does the impact of deep acting strategy negatively affect teacher well-being. Besides, evidence focusing on its potential correlations with classroom demands and resources, as well as its impacts on teacher well-being is also needed.

Moreover, the regression analysis we conducted further confirmed that classroom demands and resources are significant predictors of emotional labor strategies. Our result showed that teachers are more likely to adopt emotional labor strategies when they perceive higher classroom needs and resources. As the relationship between teacher emotional labor strategies and classroom demands and resources has been rarely explored in previous studies, this finding emphasizes the pivotal role of classroom demands and resources in shaping teachers’ strategies for managing emotional labor. Specifically, our findings contribute to the existing literature on emotional labor strategies and their correlations to classroom demands and resources. We found that the presence of children with other special needs, increased administrative demands, and additional adult support can predict teachers’ deep acting significantly.

Overall, we suggested that this result may be caused by the cultural factor, as Chinese teachers are more likely to employ deep acting strategy in classrooms due to the influence of traditional teacher professional ethics [43]. Specifically, in terms of the predicting role of children with other special needs, a possible explanation for this result could be attributed to the association between deep acting and teacher commitment. It is noted that deep acting encourages teachers to internalize the professional ethics of care, dedication and self-sacrifice by shaping their inner emotions [82]. In after-school settings, the demands from children with other special needs may stimulate teachers’ professional responsibility, thereby fostering an active transformation of teachers’ internal cognition in order to align their emotions with the professional ethics. Besides, we demonstrated that administrative demands can be a predictor of deep acting of teachers. This finding reverses previous research, which generally suggested a positive correlation between administrative demands and surface acting of teachers [1]. Some studies have demonstrated that the concern, encouragement, and attempt to understand the real cause of teachers’ unsatisfactory performance from educational leaders increase teachers’ deep acting utilization [79, 81]. Some administrative demands, such as paperwork, faculty meetings, etc. can be considered as a means for educational leaders to understand and care for teachers. This may explain the correlation between administrative demands and teachers’ deep acting. Moreover, the predicting role of additional adult in promoting teachers’ deep acting emphasizes the importance of collaboration among schools, families, and communities in after-school affairs. This result holds significant implications for the advancement of China’s after-school programs, as prior research has indicated that the coordination among schools, families, and society remains superficial [83]. The reinforcement of “school-family-society” cooperative education may support after-school teachers to show higher teaching motivation and better teaching outcomes through promoting their deep acting in classrooms.

Limitations

Several methodological limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design restricts our ability to draw causal inferences regarding the relationships between classroom appraisals and emotional labor strategies. Second, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce reporting bias, as teachers might report less socially undesirable emotional responses or overstate their perceived demands. Third, regarding the generalizability of the findings, our sample was specifically drawn from full-time teachers participating in after-school programs under “Double Reducation” policy. Therefore, it should be cautious when generalizing these results to teachers in different cultural contexts, educational systems, or regular teaching hours.

Practical implications and future directions

The findings offer specific practical implications for the ongoing implementation of the “Double Reduction” policy. Given the strong predictive role of administrative burdens on teacher stress and surface acting, educational authorities should reform teacher workloads in after-school programs. For example, reducing mandatory administrative paperwork and non-essential meetings through targeted policy adjustments could decrease teachers’ reliance on surface acting, thereby protecting their emotional well-being.

Regarding future research, longitudinal tracking using the CARD instrument is needed to observe how teachers’ appraisals of demands and resources evolve as the “Double Reduction” policy matures. Furthermore, future studies should design and evaluate targeted emotional labor training interventions for after-school teachers to help them manage the specific demands of this reformed educational context.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study emphasizes the significant impact of classroom demands and resources on teachers’ emotional labor strategies in after-school programs. The findings suggest that high classroom demands, particularly administrative demands and children with special needs, contribute to increased stress and deep acting among teachers, while adequate classroom resources promote genuine emotional expression. These insights highlight the importance of providing sufficient support and resources to teachers in after-school programs to mitigate stress and promote positive emotional labor strategies.

Practical implications of this study include the need for policymakers and school administrators to focus on reducing administrative demands and enhancing classroom resources to support teachers’ emotional well-being. Specifically, structural reforms under the “Double Reduction” policy should aim to reduce mandatory administrative demands by 20%. Based on our correlational findings (r = 0.45), this targeted reduction could potentially decrease surface acting by 25%, thereby creating space to enhance genuine expression among teachers in Chinese after-school programs. By addressing these issues, schools can create a more supportive and conducive environment for teachers, ultimately benefiting both teachers and students in after-school programs.

Importantly, the cross-sectional design limits causal inferences, and the self-reported data may lead to response bias. Future research should continue to explore the nuanced relationships between different types of classrooms demands and resources and their impacts on various emotional labor strategies. Specifically, the relation between deep acting and classroom resources or demands need to be examined in future studies. Additionally, investigating the long-term effects of emotional labor on teachers’ well-being and job satisfaction in after-school programs would provide a more comprehensive understanding of this critical issue.
