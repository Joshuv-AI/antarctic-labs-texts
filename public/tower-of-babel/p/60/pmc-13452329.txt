Return to School After Posterior Spinal Fusion in Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Anne Boeckmann, Erica Olfson, David Thornberg, Chan-Hee Jo, Megan Johnson, Amy L. McIntosh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPosterior spinal fusion (PSF) is the standard surgical treatment for progressive adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS). While return to sport after PSF has been described, return to school (RTS) remains poorly defined despite its educational and psychosocial importance. This study aimed to quantify RTS timelines, compare anticipated versus observed absences, and evaluate demographic, surgical, psychosocial, and socioeconomic predictorsMethodsConsecutively enrolled English-speaking AIS and juvenile idiopathic scoliosis (JIS) patients undergoing PSF at a single institution between 2022 and 2025 were prospectively studied. Preoperative surveys captured demographics, school type, anticipated RTS, and awareness of accommodations available through a patient’s school setting. Postoperative surveys documented actual RTS, attendance, and barriers. RTS was defined as the first day the patient resumed full-day, in-person school attendance. Partial day attendance, hybrid instruction, and virtual learning were not separately analyzed. The Pain Catastrophizing Scale assessed psychological status, and the Area Deprivation Index (ADI) measured socioeconomic context. Group comparisons used chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests and t-tests/Mann-Whitney U; regression identified predictorsResultsA total of 118 patients (84% female; mean age 14.5 ± 1.9 years) were included. Families anticipated 31.4 days of absence, while 71 postoperative respondents reported a mean absence of 46.8 ± 18.6 days (P = .022). Homeschool and private school students demonstrated numerically shorter RTS intervals than public school students (26.3, 32.8, and 43.1 days, respectively); however, these differences did not reach statistical significance (P = .160). Fusion length, sex, ADI, and race were not independent predictors of RTSConclusionsAIS/JIS patients undergoing PSF returned to school at a mean of 6.7 weeks, approximately 2 weeks later than anticipated. Exploratory analyses suggested possible differences by school type and psychosocial factors, which should be investigated further in future, larger studies.Key Concepts(1)There may be an educational gap, as families tend to underestimate the time required for their child to return to school (RTS).(2)Surgical and demographic factors, including length of fusion, sex, Area Deprivation Index, and race, were not associated with time to RTS.Level of EvidenceII, Prospective cohort study

Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is the most common form of scoliosis, representing ∼90% of pediatric cases [1]. Defined by a lateral curvature >10° with rotation, scoliosis most often presents during the adolescent growth spurt and affects 2-4% of adolescents, with a female predominance [1,2]. Surgical management is indicated for progressive deformities, typically thoracic curves >45-50°. While PSF outcomes related to radiographic correction, safety, and return to sport are well described, return to school (RTS) remains underexplored. Most athletes resume sport within 3-12 months depending on fusion length, but comparable data on school reentry are limited [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Prolonged school absence is associated with poorer academic performance, impaired psychosocial development, and long-term socioeconomic disadvantage [7,8]. Despite these risks, no standardized guidelines exist for RTS, and published data to inform counseling are sparse.

Surgical magnitude, pain, psychosocial resilience, and school flexibility have been proposed as factors in RTS, but findings are inconsistent [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Pain catastrophizing, a construct linked to delayed recovery in pediatric pain populations, has not been systematically evaluated in scoliosis patients [10].

We therefore aimed to (1) define the mean RTS timeline after PSF for AIS and juvenile idiopathic scoliosis (JIS); (2) compare anticipated vs actual absences; and (3) assess demographic, surgical, psychosocial, and socioeconomic predictors. We hypothesized that actual absences would exceed expectations, with psychosocial and school-related factors more influential than surgical characteristics.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

We conducted a prospective survey of consecutively enrolled English-speaking AIS/JIS patients undergoing PSF at a single center between March 2022 and March 2025 (Fig. 1). Eligible patients were enrolled in school and expected to RTS postoperatively; patients whose postoperative recovery occurred primarily during scheduled school breaks (i.e., summer vacation or winter break exceeding 2 consecutive weeks) were excluded because postoperative school absence could not be accurately assessed. Homebound program

Federal law requires public schools to provide a form of educational continuation when a student’s medical condition prevents school attendance; however, implementation varies by state. Homebound instruction is an educational service offered by public schools within the state that this study was conducted. It is intended to be temporary for students who cannot attend school due to a medical condition. Within the study’s home state, a student must (1) be confined to the home or a hospital bed for at least 4 weeks (weeks do not need to be consecutive), (2) provide documentation from a licensed physician, and (3) receive approval from a school committee. Eligible students receive at least 4 hours of face-to-face instruction per week. Although awareness of the homebound program was assessed, utilization of homebound services was not prospectively collected.

Data capture and surveys

Data were collected in Research Electronic Data Capture [13,14]. Preoperative surveys recorded demographics, school type, anticipated RTS, awareness of accommodations, and Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) scores. Postoperative surveys were distributed at 4 weeks and repeated every 2 weeks until RTS was confirmed, documenting return dates, attendance format, and barriers.

Psychosocial and socioeconomic measures

The PCS is a validated, 13-item patient-reported questionnaire that is used to quantify maladaptive pain-related cognitions (rumination, magnification, and helplessness) [15]. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“all the time”), with total scores ranging from 0 to 52 and higher scores indicating greater catastrophizing for this patient. For analyses, we calculated total PCS scores preoperatively and categorized patients as “pain catastrophizers” (PCS score ≥30) versus non–pain catastrophizers (PCS score <30).

Socioeconomic status was assessed using the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), a neighborhood-level measure of socioeconomic disadvantage derived from U.S. Census indicators [16]. Patients were assigned ADI values based on residence (zip code-level linkage) and both state-level decile rankings (10-10) and national percentile rankings (1-100) were recorded when available. Higher ADI values represent greater neighborhood deprivation (indicating more socioeconomic disadvantage), whereas lower ADI values represent lower neighborhood deprivation.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R (Advanced statistics graphics) and SPSS (IBM: Descriptive statistics, t-tests, chi-squared, Fisher's, regrerssion). Chi-squared/Fisher’s exact tests compared categorical variables, and t-tests/Mann-Whitney U-tests compared continuous variables. Regression models evaluated predictors of RTS (demographics, surgical factors, PCS, and ADI). Two-tailed significance was set at a P value < .05.

Results

Baseline demographics and social characteristics

A total of 118 patients and families completed the preoperative survey. The majority of participants were female (84.0%, n = 99), with males comprising 16.0% (n = 19) of the cohort. Most respondents identified as “non-Hispanic or Latino” (84.0%, n = 99), while 16.0% (n = 19) identified as “Hispanic or Latino.” The racial distribution was predominantly White (68.6%, n = 81), followed by other/unknown (17.0%, n = 20), Black or African American (10.2%, n = 12), Asian (2.5%, n = 3), and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander (1.7%, n = 2). Fig. 2 depicts the distribution of national ADI scores within the cohort. The mean state ADI score was 3.9 (1-10), and the mean national ADI score was 45.9 (1-98). The mean age at the time of surgery was 14.5 (10.3–18.3) years. Preoperative survey results

Of the 118 patients, 105 (89.0%) were enrolled in public school, 7 (5.9%) in private school, and 6 (5.1%) were homeschooled. Respondents anticipated being absent for a mean of 31.4 days of school (approximately 4.5 weeks), with an expected range of 0–90 days. When stratified by quartiles, expected return times were ≤21 days (Q1), 21-28 days (Q2), 28-42 days (Q3), and >42 days (Q4). Half of the patients expected to RTS by 28 days. Of the public school attendees (n = 105), the majority of respondents were aware of the Homebound program, with 78 (74%) of families having knowledge of the offering, 24 (23.0%) unsure if their school offered it, and 3 (3.0%) believing their school did not offer it.

Eighty-seven (74%) of respondents reported existing absences with the majority totaling less than 1 week (71%). Doctor appointments/illness was the primary reason for pre-existing absences (86%) followed by unspecified reasons (8%); however, 2 of the 87 respondents reported absences due to pain from their curve.

Attrition analysis

Of the 118 participants who completed the preoperative survey, 71 (60%) completed the postoperative survey and reported returning to a full day of school. Baseline comparison between responders and nonresponders demonstrated significant differences in preoperative SRS (Scoliosis Research Society) pain scores (3.57 vs 3.81, P = .038), age at surgery (14.9 vs 14.2 years, P = .024), length of stay (72.0 vs 70.5 h, P < .01), and the proportion of patients meeting criteria for pain catastrophizing (0% vs 15%, P = .005). These differences suggest the potential for response bias and should be considered when interpreting postoperative RTS outcomes.

Return to school outcomes—postoperative survey

For the 71 respondents who completed the postoperative survey and reported a final RTS date, the mean duration of school absence was 46.8 ± 18.6 days.

Univariate findings

There were no significant differences between actual return times when stratified by sex, racial group, school type, pain catastrophizing status, fusion type, or surgical time (P ≥ .05). However, there was a significant difference in RTS duration when comparing patients who underwent PSF in the spring (February–May) vs fall (August–November) (37.9 ± 17.9 vs 44.2 ± 18.3 days, P = .038).

Correlation findings Patients undergoing surgery during the spring returned to school significantly earlier than those undergoing surgery during the fall (37.9 ± 17.9 vs 44.4 ± 18.3 days, P = .038). The expected and actual absence duration were weakly and positively correlated (Pearson’s r = 0.270, P = .022). Conversely, the actual absence duration did not correlate with SRS domain scores, ADI, age at surgery, preoperative major curve cobb, the length of stay, or the number of levels fused using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation except for the SRS Pain domain, which was weakly and negatively correlated (Spearman's r = −0.276, P = .021).

Predictive modeling results and regressions

After controlling for race, ADI, number of levels fused, gender, surgery season, school type, race, and PCS total, multivariable linear regression found that no variables were associated with RTS absences (all β estimates nonsignificant; 95% confidence intervals crossed 0; P > .10).

Postoperative complications

Postoperative complications were uncommon in this cohort. Two patients had an extended length of stay (>5 days) due to evaluation for suspected cerebrospinal fluid leak; however, neither completed the postoperative RTS survey. Overall, 8 of 118 (6.7%) patients underwent at least one re-peration; four occurred >6 months postoperatively and were unlikely to influence RTS timing, while four occurred within 2 months. Among the 4 patients with early reoperations, 2 returned to school around 49-50 days after their index procedures, whereas the other two patients did not go on to complete their postoperative RTS survey.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study demonstrated that adolescents undergoing PSF for idiopathic scoliosis returned to full-day, in-person school at a mean of 6.7 weeks postoperatively, approximately 2 weeks longer than anticipated by families. This finding exceeds the commonly cited 4- to 6-week estimate for RTS after PSF [17]. While return to sport after scoliosis surgery has been increasingly investigated, with systematic reviews reporting return to sport between 8 and 12 months [18], [19], RTS remains an equally important functional milestone with significant educational and psychosocial implications. These findings suggest that current preoperative expectations may underestimate the recovery period required for RTS and support the need for more evidence-based counseling.

We found no significant associations between RTS timing and surgical or demographic factors, including fusion length, sex, race, or socioeconomic deprivation. Notably, fusion length has not been shown to influence return to sport timing, paralleling the current study’s finding that fusion length did not predict RTS timing [20]. Rather, this study’s findings suggest that educational reintegration may be influenced by factors beyond operative magnitude alone. Although homeschool and private school students demonstrated numerically shorter RTS intervals than public school students, these differences were not statistically significant and should be interpreted cautiously, given the small subgroup sizes. Rather than reflecting faster physiologic recovery, these findings may represent greater educational flexibility and individualized pacing. Similarly, while patients with higher pain catastrophizing scores demonstrated trends toward delayed return, our study was not powered to determine whether catastrophizing independently influences RTS timing, and these findings should be considered exploratory and in the context of their limitations. This current study’s data, in addition to prior work showing that pain catastrophizing is a risk factor for poorer long-term recovery 1 year post fusion, supports the potential value of presurgical psychosocial screening [21,22].

Patients undergoing surgery during the spring returned to school earlier than those undergoing surgery during the fall. This finding may reflect overlap between postoperative recovery and scheduled school breaks rather than true differences in recovery and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Although scheduling surgery around school vacations may appear advantageous from an educational perspective, delaying clinically indicated deformity correction should be balanced against the potential for curve progression and other patient-specific clinical considerations. Likewise, RTS in this study was defined as full-day, in-person attendance and did not account for gradual return schedules, hybrid instruction, or virtual learning, all of which have become increasingly common following the COVID-19 pandemic. Although awareness of the state homebound program was assessed, utilization of these services was not collected and may also influence RTS timing by allowing continued academic participation while recovering at home.

The clinical implications of these findings extend beyond the timing of school reentry. Surgeons play an important role in establishing realistic preoperative expectations for patients and families. Prior work has shown that pain is the greatest preoperative concern for both patients and parents, while return to activities is the second most common patient concern, yet patient and parent concerns rarely overlap with the surgeon concerns [23]. Furthermore, patients and guardians understand only approximately 60% of the surgical consent for AIS surgery, with postoperative mobility being particularly poorly understood [24]. Our findings suggest that counseling families to anticipate approximately 6 to 7 weeks before return to full day school attendance may better align expectations with observed recovery and facilitate planning for academic accommodations when needed.

Of note, RTS in the present study was defined as full-day, in-person attendance and therefore did not account for gradual return schedules, hybrid instruction, or virtual learning, all of which have become increasingly common following the COVID-19 pandemic. Future studies should evaluate how these educational models influence postoperative reintegration.

This study’s strengths include its prospective design and incorporation of psychosocial and socioeconomic measures. Limitations include its single-center design, inclusion of only English-speaking participants, and a 60% postoperative survey response rate, introducing the potential for response bias. Although responder and nonresponder characteristics were compared, significant baseline differences in pain scores, age, length of stay, and pain catastrophizing were observed and should be considered when interpreting the findings. In addition, subgroup analyses involving homeschool and private school students were underpowered, and important contextual factors, including parental employment, caregiver availability, household and family structures, grade level, homebound utilization, and hybrid or virtual educational participation, were not collected.

Overall, adolescents undergoing PSF returned to school approximately 2 weeks later than families expected. While exploratory trends suggest that educational environment and psychosocial factors may contribute to RTS timing, larger multicenter studies are needed before definitive conclusions can be drawn. Future work should further evaluate the influence of school accommodations, family characteristics, and evolving educational models on postoperative reintegration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, adolescents undergoing PSF returned to school approximately 2 weeks later than families anticipated. Surgical and demographic characteristics were not associated with RTS timing. Although exploratory analyses suggest that educational environment and psychosocial factors may contribute to postoperative reintegration, larger multicenter studies are needed before definitive conclusions can be drawn. These findings support improved evidence-based preoperative counseling and further investigation into educational accommodations that may facilitate RTS.
