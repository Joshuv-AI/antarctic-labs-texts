Real‐World Treatment Effectiveness of Disease‐Modifying Antirheumatic Drugs by Serostatus Among Patients With Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Yinzhu Jin, Jun Liu, Rishi J. Desai, Seoyoung C. Kim
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe objective of this study was to compare the clinical effectiveness of biologic disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) or Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) among seropositive versus seronegative patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) in a real‐world setting.MethodsWe used Optum's deidentified Clinformatics Data Mart Database (January 1, 2004, to March 31, 2021) linked with outpatient laboratory test results. The study population was adult patients with RA who initiated a bDMARD or JAKi. The index date was the dispensing of the first‐ever study drug. At least 1‐year continuous enrollment before and after the index date was required. Disenrollment due to death after the index date was allowed. Serostatus was defined using laboratory test results or the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision code M05x or M06.0x any time prior to the index date. Treatment effectiveness was measured based on a claims‐based composite endpoint at 1‐year post index, including nonoccurrence of any of the following: addition of conventional synthetic DMARDs, addition of or switching to new bDMARDs/JAKi, initiation of glucocorticoids, increased glucocorticoid dose, or death. Log‐binomial regression models were constructed to estimate the risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) comparing seropositive patients with seronegative patients, adjusting for more than 60 baseline covariates.ResultsWe identified a total of 7813 seropositive patients and 4202 seronegative patients. The mean (±SD) age was 56.7 (±14.0) years; 77.9% were female. The risk of 1‐year treatment effectiveness was 70.2% among seropositive patients and 69.8% among seronegative patients. The adjusted RR (95% CI) was 1.00 (0.98‐1.02).ConclusionIn this real‐world cohort study, seropositive and seronegative patients with RA had similar 1‐year treatment effectiveness after initiating a bDMARD/JAKi.

SIGNIFICANCE & INNOVATIONS Serostatus may be associated with different responses to disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA). INTRODUCTION

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic autoimmune disease characterized by highly heterogenous immune dysregulation. It is estimated that about 1.3 million adults are affected by RA in the United States (1). Although the exact pathogenesis of RA is not fully known, several autoantibodies have been identified as distinctive features of the disease. Rheumatoid factor (RF) and anticitrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs) are the most prominent autoantibodies, and the presence of RF or ACPAs is currently used as a biomarker for RA diagnosis. Historically, RF was the primary biomarker for RA diagnosis and prognosis (2). However, with an evolving understanding of disease‐associated autoantibodies, the 2010 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) criteria included ACPAs alongside RF and redefined seropositive RA as the presence of either RF or ACPAs. The absence of both RF and ACPAs is defined as seronegative RA (3).

Growing evidence suggests that seropositive and seronegative RA are different regarding their underlying genetic risk profiles and cellular pathology and might potentially differ in responses to disease‐modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) (4, 5). Earlier studies indicated that patients with the presence of RF or ACPA have higher disease activity and worse radiographic progression (6, 7, 8). However, some studies have reported conflicting conclusions in which seronegativity is associated with more severe inflammatory activity (9, 10). The current ACR and EULAR treatment guidelines do not differentiate the treatment strategies for the management of two subtypes of RA (11, 12). Whether a different treatment strategy is needed for seropositive versus seronegative RA subsets remains unclear because of the limited evidence. To date, several studies have shown heterogeneous treatment effectiveness among seropositive and seronegative patients with RA (4, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18). However, the evidence from different studies is not uniform, possibly because of different study populations, designs, and outcome definitions. Furthermore, current evidence of the association between serostatus and treatment effectiveness is mostly focused on tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) and abatacept, and little is known for newer agents such as interleukin 6 (IL‐6) inhibitors or targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs), for example, Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi). An improved understanding of the real‐world effectiveness of different treatments by serostatus is essential to inform treatment decisions. Therefore, the current study aimed to examine how seropositive and seronegative patients with RA are managed in a real‐world setting and provide data on the effectiveness of different biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs)/JAKi for RA by serostatus.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

We used Optum's deidentified Clinformatics Data Mart Database (January 1, 2004, to March 31, 2021) linked with outpatient laboratory test results, which contains administrative health claims from a large national managed care company in the United States. The population of the database comprises commercial health plan and Medicare Advantage plan members from all 50 states. Comprehensive longitudinal information, including demographics, inpatient and outpatient diagnoses and procedures, outpatient laboratory test results, and pharmacy dispensing, were recorded in the database for the insurance enrollees. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Brigham and Women's Hospital Institutional Review Board.

Study population and exposure

The study population was bDMARD/JAKi‐naive adult patients who initiated one of the following drugs: TNFi (eg, adalimumab, certolizumab pegol, etanercept, golimumab, infliximab), abatacept, IL‐6 inhibitors (eg, sarilumab, tocilizumab), or JAKi (eg, baricitinib, tofacitinib, upadacitinib). Dispensing of the first‐ever bDMARD or JAKi was defined as the index date. Eligible study participants with RA were required to have two or more RA diagnostic codes separated by 7 to 365 days before the index date (19). At least 365 days of continuous enrollment in the insurance was required before the index date. bDMARD/JAKi‐naive patients were defined as those not having any of the study drugs any time before the index date. To assess patients’ serostatus, we restricted the cohort to patients who had RF or anti–cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti‐CCP) antibody laboratory test result values or International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) diagnosis codes of M05x/M06.0x any time prior to the index date (20, 21). Lastly, 395 days or more of continuous enrollment after the index date was required for outcome assessment. However, patients who disenrolled from the insurance owing to death were not excluded because death is one of the outcome components.

Seropositive and seronegative RA

RA serostatus was defined based on laboratory test results or ICD‐10 diagnosis codes of RA identified any time prior to the index date. Patients were defined as having seropositive RA if the RF laboratory test result was greater than 13 IU/ml or the anti‐CCP test result was greater than 19 Units or if they had an ICD‐10 code of M05x. Otherwise, patients were defined as having seronegative RA. Patients who had missing laboratory test results for both RF and anti‐CCP tests and did not have records of an ICD‐10 diagnosis code of M05x or M06.0x were excluded from the study cohort. Using M05/M06 as the proxy to determine seropositivity had a positive predictive value of about 80% based on a recent validation study using the US registry and insurance claims databases (20, 21).

Outcome and follow‐up

The clinical effectiveness measure was adapted from a previously validated algorithm based on claims data as the proxy for clinical improvement in RA disease activity (22). The algorithm had a positive predictive value (PPV) of 76% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 71%‐81%), a negative predictive value (NPV) of 90% (95% CI: 88%‐92%), sensitivity of 72% (95% CI: 67%‐77%), and specificity of 91% (95% CI: 89%‐93%). To measure this surrogate clinical effectiveness measure, we first measured the following end points that are indicative of treatment failure: 1) all‐cause death within 395 days after the index date, 2) discontinuation of the index drug allowing for a 60‐day gap period, 3) initiation of a new conventional synthetic DMARD (csDMARD) (eg, methotrexate, sulfasalazine, leflunomide, or hydroxychloroquine), 4) initiation or addition of a new bDMARD or JAKi that is different from the index drug, 5) initiation of oral glucocorticoids among those without glucocorticoid dispensing before 90 days within the index date, and 6) increased dose of glucocorticoids among those with glucocorticoid dispensing before 90 days within the index date. The increased dose was defined as at least a 10‐mg increase in the mean prednisone equivalent daily dose for at least 14 days during the outcome assessment period compared with those 90 days prior to the index date. Follow‐up started 1 day after the index date and ended 395 days after the index date; however, each outcome component was evaluated at 365 ± 30 days after the index date, except the death component. Death was measured any time between 1 and 395 days after the index date. Finally, the study outcome of 1‐year treatment effectiveness was defined as the nonoccurrence of any of the six component end points mentioned previously. Baseline patient characteristics

Baseline patient characteristics were measured within 365 days prior to the index date. These included demographics (age, sex, and US census region), calendar year of the index date, serostatus, comorbidities—combined comorbidity score (23, 24), frailty index (25), hospitalized infection, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke, transient ischemic stroke, venous thromboembolism, coronary artery disease, heart failure, inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease, liver disease, chronic kidney disease, hepatitis, cancer, human immunodeficiency virus infection, alcohol abuse, and smoking—and medication use (angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, beta‐blockers, calcium channel blockers, statins, nonstatin lipid‐lowering drugs, insulin, noninsulin antidiabetics, anticoagulants, antidepressants, benzodiazepines, opioids, nonsteroid antiinflammatory drugs, selective cyclooxygenase 2 inhibitors, and recent oral glucocorticoids). We also assessed health care use patterns (hospitalizations within 30 days, number of primary physician visits, number of rheumatologist visits, number of unique generic drug prescriptions, herpes zoster vaccination, pneumonia vaccination, and influenza vaccination) and laboratory tests ordered (C‐reactive protein [CRP], erythrocyte sedimentation rate, hemoglobin A1c, and lipid panel).

Statistical analysis

Patient baseline characteristics were assessed in the overall cohort and were compared between the seropositive and seronegative groups. The risk ratio (RR) and its 95% CI of the primary outcome (1‐year treatment effectiveness) comparing seropositive versus seronegative groups was estimated using a generalized linear model with log‐link function and binomial distribution. To adjust for the baseline confounding, we conducted a multivariable linear regression model by including baseline patient characteristics mentioned previously as covariates. We also included an interaction term of serostatus and index drug to test whether the RR varied across individual index drugs. Finally, we conducted a subgroup analysis to estimate the effect of serostatus on the treatment effectiveness of each study DMARD. The crude and adjusted RRs (95% CI) were estimated stratified by index drug groups: TNFi, abatacept, IL‐6 inhibitors, and JAKi. Because of smaller sample sizes of some subgroups, particularly IL‐6 inhibitors and JAKi, the adjusted RRs of subgroups were estimated by adding age, sex, index calendar year, US census region, Charlson comorbidity score, and use of methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine, and leflunomide at baseline. All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding patients with indications for bDMARDs/tsDMARDs other than RA, such as inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, or systemic lupus erythematosus, at baseline. The same outcome definition and multivariable log‐binomial regression model was applied. Additionally, among this subset of patients without other indications for bDMARDs/tsDMARDs, we repeated the same analysis by restricting the cohort to those with laboratory data to determine their serostatus. Furthermore, among those with laboratory data, we compared patients’ characteristics between RF‐positive versus anti‐CCP‐positive patients.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

A total of 12,015 patients with RA were identified after applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The mean age (±SD) was 56.7 (±14.0), and 77.9% were female. The study population comprised 81.1% TNFi initiators, 8.2% abatacept initiators, 3.4% IL‐6 inhibitor initiators, and 7.23% JAKi initiators (Table 1). Concomitant use of csDMARDs was common: 35.0% used methotrexate and 16.8% used hydroxychloroquine on the index date.

There were 7813 (65%) seropositive and 4202 (35%) seronegative patients with RA. The seropositive group was 5 years older on average than the seronegative group. More TNFi initiators were identified in the seronegative group (86.7%) versus the seropositive group (78.1%). In contrast, there were fewer non‐TNFi bDMARD and JAKi initiators in the seronegative versus the seropositive group (Table 1). Seropositive patients had a slightly higher mean Charlson comorbidity score than seronegative patients. Overall, the prevalence of baseline comorbid conditions was similar between the two groups. Among those who had a CRP laboratory test result, the mean (±SD) value of CRP was 72.5 (±98.1) mg/l in the seropositive group and 62.6 (±88.7) mg/l in the seronegative group.

One‐year treatment effectiveness end point

The risk of the composite end point of treatment effectiveness more than 1 year after the treatment initiation was 70.2% in the seropositive group and 69.8% in the seronegative group. The occurrence of each component of the composite endpoint is shown in Supplementary Table 1. The crude RR of treatment effectiveness (95% CI) was 1.01 (0.98‐1.03) for the seropositive group versus the seronegative group (Table 2). The risk of treatment effectiveness was slightly lower in TNFi initiators compared with other bDMARD/JAKi initiators in both the seropositive and seronegative groups (Table 2).

After adjusting for baseline confounding, the adjusted RR (95% CI) was 1.00 (0.95‐1.06) (Figure 2). The adjusted RR with an interaction term of index drug and serostatus was 1.00 (0.98‐1.02) (Figure 2), and there was no trend in RR across individual index drugs (P value for interaction term was 0.945). The subgroup analysis showed no significant difference in treatment failure between seropositive and seronegative RA among initiators of different bDMARDs/JAKi. A sensitivity analysis excluding patients with other indications for bDMARDs/tsDMARDs (inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis, or systemic lupus erythematosus) at baseline yielded a similar result, in which adjusted RR (95% CI) was 1.00 (0.97‐1.03). When we restricted patients furthermore to those with laboratory data for serostatus, the RR remained the same. Patients’ characteristics also remained similar to those of the main cohort in both sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). For those with laboratory data, we did not find substantial differences in patients’ characteristics at baseline between RF‐positive and anti‐CCP‐positive patients (Supplementary Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Heterogeneity of treatment effectiveness still exists in a considerable proportion of patients with RA despite the drastic improvement in the pharmacological management of RA in recent years with the approval of bDMARDs and JAKi (26, 27). Seropositive and seronegative RA have been shown to have different radiographic progression disease severity (28), which may potentially contribute to the differences in treatment selection and treatment response among patients with RA. Therefore, the study hypothesis was that the treatment effectiveness among patients with RA may differ among seropositive versus seronegative subsets. In this large observational study of the adult RA population who initiated bDMARDs/JAKi, we observed a similar clinical effectiveness of bDMARDs/JAKi among seropositive versus seronegative patients with RA. Such association was not affected by the selection of initial treatment for RA.

The latest ACR and EULAR guidelines recommend similar treatment strategies for the management of RA regardless of serostatus (11, 12). We found that in this RA population who initiated bDMARDs/JAKi, seronegative patients were more likely to receive adalimumab, etanercept, and infliximab compared with seropositive patients. In contrast, receiving non‐TNFi bDMARDs and JAKi was more common in seropositive patients. This suggests that despite the nondifferential therapeutic recommendations, in the real‐world setting, seropositive patients, who are considered to have a worse prognostic disease course than seronegative patients, would be more likely to be treated with newer bDMARDs/JAKi and that seronegative patients would be more likely prescribed with conservative options. However, the difference in the treatment selection was modest, with a standardized mean difference less than 0.1 for most of the agents.

Real‐world evidence based on the US population is limited because large administrative claims data frequently used in comparative effectiveness research do not have disease activity or functional score records. The current study has adapted a previously validated algorithm of treatment effectiveness among patients with RA. The algorithm is composed of several components that are highly related to treatment effectiveness, such as discontinuation, switching, and change in glucocorticoid use and/or dose, and have been shown to have a positive predictive value of 76% (95% CI: 71%‐81%) and a negative predictive value of 90% (95% CI: 88%‐92%). Although we have used an adapted outcome algorithm, we would expect that the performance of the adapted outcome algorithm would remain similar to that of the original algorithm because most of the components remained the same (high adherence, switching or adding a new biologic, addition of a new non‐bDMARD, adding, or increased dose of glucocorticoids). With the use of this previously validated effectiveness outcome definition, our main finding from large US administrative data is consistent with the previous two large observational studies from European and US registry data in terms of the similar treatment effectiveness between two serotypes in TNFi users (adjusted RR: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.98‐1.02). A recent collaborative analysis based on data in 16 European registries examined the association between seropositivity and clinical endpoints of drug retention rate, disease remission, and low disease activity at 1 year after bDMARD treatment (13). The authors found that seropositivity (RF and/or ACPA positive) was associated with a lower drug discontinuation rate and response rate in abatacept and tocilizumab users, but no association was observed in TNFi users. Another observational study that used the Corrona RA registry defined treatment response based on remission, low disease activity, and modified ACR20/50/70 and found that being ACPA positive was associated with a greater treatment response in abatacept users but not in TNFi users (17). However, a Japanese cohort study of patients with RA showed a contradictory result that treatment discontinuation of TNFi due to insufficient response was higher among the RF‐positive versus RF‐negative group, whereas ACPA was not predictive of discontinuation of TNFi due to insufficient response (15). These different findings could be due to variations in region, study design, confounding variables adjusted for, and statistical analysis methods. The current study used a large US health insurance claims database that covers all regions of the United States and conducted a comprehensive confounding adjustment in the statistical analysis to minimize any residual confounding in the association between seropositivity and treatment effectiveness.

It is noteworthy that multiple studies support the finding that abatacept is associated with a better treatment response/retention rate among the seropositive RA group (13, 17, 18, 29). Such association is biologically plausible given the mechanism of action of abatacept. Abatacept is a CD80/86‐CD28 T cell costimulation modulator that intervenes in the production of autoantibody B cells. Therefore, it could be possible that abatacept would show a better effectiveness in seropositive patients with RA than seronegative patients.

In addition to TNFi and abatacept, our study also examined the role of serostatus in treatment effectiveness among newer agents, including IL‐6 inhibitors as well as JAKi. These agents were less studied than TNFi and abatacept possibly because of insufficient data. To date, only little conflicting real‐world evidence is available for comparative effectiveness of tocilizumab in seropositive versus seronegative RA subtypes (29, 30). Our study filled in the knowledge gap by including currently available IL‐6 inhibitors (tocilizumab and sarilumab) as well as JAKi (tofacitinib, baricitinib, and upadacitinib). No association between serostatus and treatment effectiveness was observed in either IL‐6 inhibitor or JAKi initiators. Overall, the present study did not find a difference in treatment effectiveness between seropositive and seronegative RA groups, and the result did not differ by the initial choice of bDMARD/JAKi. Nevertheless, treatment heterogeneity among patients with RA cannot be neglected because seronegative RA could still be a complex entity with undetermined biomarkers (16, 31). Further research is necessary to continue to put pieces together to provide comprehensive evidence to help personalized choice of the treatment strategy for patients with RA.

Our study has several limitations. First, the insurance claims data do not have clinical measures, such as disease severity and activity. Therefore, the outcome in this study was a surrogate clinical effectiveness outcome, which is different from the endpoints used in registry data or randomized controlled trails. For the same reason, we were not able to adjust for any baseline RA activity differences between the two groups. However, the outcome definition used in the study was adapted from a previously validated algorithm for RA treatment effectiveness based on claims data, which showed sufficient PPV and NPV. Also, we adjusted for several baseline covariates that are proxies of disease activity or severity in the absence of some clinical variables. Second, nondifferential misclassification of outcome could be possible, which may potentially bias our estimate toward the null. Third, despite the rigorous adjustment for baseline covariates, as an observational study, it is still prone to unmeasured confounding. Fourth, coexisting noninflammatory pain conditions cannot be completely ruled out, which might have affected the treatment escalation pattern in the outcome components. For example, increased dose of glucocorticoids might be due to coexisting conditions, such as fibromyalgia, which may be underrepresented based on diagnosis codes in claims data. Last, some of the newer agents had insufficient statistical power to yield a significant association between serostatus and treatment effectiveness, if any. Further investigations should be conducted to continue to fill in such a knowledge gap.

In this large real‐world cohort study of a biologic‐naive RA population, we demonstrated that serostatus was not associated with treatment effectiveness at 1 year after the initiation of bDMARDs/JAKi and that the association was not observed across the individual drug. Future research is needed to elucidate the role of RF and/or ACPA and other potential biomarkers in personalizing therapeutic strategies among patients with RA.
