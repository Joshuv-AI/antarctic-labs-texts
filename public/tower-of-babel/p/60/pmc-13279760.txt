Intellectual property (IP) rights governance and related value mechanisms in Common European Data Spaces
Authors: Richard Rak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Common European Data Spaces (CEDS) are EU initiatives aimed at establishing secure, trustworthy, and multi-stakeholder data ecosystems in strategic sectors and domains to enable controlled access, sharing, and re-use of data. This article adopts a doctrinal and policy-oriented approach to examine the legal challenges of operationalising data sovereignty in CEDS through the governance of intellectual property (IP) rights. It analyses the intersections between relevant international and EU IP regimes—on copyright, the sui generis database right, trade secrets, and patents—and the EU data acquis, in particular the Data Act and the European Health Data Space Regulation. The article posits that adequate and effective protection of IP rights are crucial in CEDS, which require legal and governance frameworks that ensure fair balance between facilitating legitimate data access and re-use by downstream data users, while safeguarding the IP rights and incentives of data holders. The argument critiques the introduction of mandatory access regimes in the EU data acquis, which provide calibrated exceptions, limitations, or delegations of control to public authorities, thereby adjusting the exercise of IP rights enshrined in other international and EU legal instruments. It highlights concerns that the lack of clarity regarding the interplay between various IP regimes risks legal uncertainty and trust erosion. In addition to legislative fine-tuning, the article recommends the adoption of IP frameworks that incorporate FRAND terms, NDAs, advanced (AI and distributed ledger) technologies, and that build on multi-stakeholder governance structures to facilitate clear, incentivising and socioeconomically fair IP governance and related value mechanisms in CEDS.

Introduction

Common European data spaces (CEDS) are initiatives led by the European Union (EU) to create multi-stakeholder data ecosystems in strategic economic sectors and domains of public interest, such as health, energy, manufacturing, mobility, agriculture, defence, public administration, research and innovation, and smart cities. EU policy on CEDS has been shaped by the European strategy for data (2020) [1], two Commission Staff Working Documents on CEDS [SWD(2022) 31 final; SWD(2024) 28 final] [2,3] and, most recently, the Data Union Strategy (2025) [4]. According to these policy documents and the Blueprint for Data Spaces provided by the Data Spaces Support Centre (DSSC) project [5], CEDS are conceptualised as federated networks and interoperable data-sharing ecosystems built on secure infrastructures designed to enable controlled access to and sharing of data between public and private actors within and across sectors. CEDS also aim to enhance the wider pooling and re-use of data under fair access rules and trustworthy governance mechanisms. The overarching policy goal of CEDS is to stimulate innovation, growth and competitiveness in the EU’s data economy and to generate socioeconomic benefits through improved data-driven public services and sustainability efforts. The development of CEDS aims to support the EU’s vision of establishing a single market for data (European Data Union) and to enable the widespread deployment of data-driven technologies, particularly artificial intelligence (AI), while upholding European values including personal data protection, consumer protection, fair competition, and data sovereignty. In the long-run, the Data Union Strategy plans to scale up CEDS by linking them to AI infrastructure through data labs and AI factories.

This perspective article examines the legal challenges of operationalising data sovereignty through intellectual property (IP) rights governance in CEDS. Data sovereignty in CEDS refers to the principle that data holders—individuals, organisations, and public sector bodies—should retain control over the access, use, storage, and sharing of data they create/generate [6]. Such control derives primarily from IP rights and/or data protection rules and/or supplementary contractual and non-legal mechanisms, ensuring data holders assert value while permitting lawful re-use. The implementation of this principle is critical to the successful functioning of federated and interconnected data ecosystems in CEDS. One key aspect of data sovereignty in CEDS is reconciling data holders’ exclusive rights with downstream users’ legitimate data access needs. In other words, CEDS promotes broad data access and re-use by downstream data users while guaranteeing appropriate protection for IP rights holders. By definition, IP rights refer to the exclusive right granted by a jurisdiction that permits its holder (in the present case, the data holder or other rights holder) to exclude or restrict certain third-parties from specific actions, such as using or exploiting the subject matter (e.g., datasets) covered by these rights [7]. This paper argues that a “fair” balance is required between enabling data users (data consumers) to access data for legitimate (“secondary”) re-use purposes under specific conditions, while safeguarding the IP rights and related legitimate interests and incentives of data holders (data providers) and other relevant rights holders. Achieving this requires robust (adequate and effective) legal frameworks that ensure predictability in the governance (protection and management) of IP rights in CEDS. Without clear, coherent, and consistent IP rules, fragmented IP practices may risk undermining trust, interoperability, and equitable value creation in CEDS.

Against this background, the analysis addresses two key legal-policy questions:

(a) How does the differential regulation—i.e., the varying scope of the protected subject matter, duration, terms of protection, exceptions, and enforcement mechanisms—of IP rights affect their application in CEDS? This problem is grounded in literature on overlapping IP rights and multi-rights data governance frameworks, which shows that datasets and algorithms often sit (debatably) at the intersection of several IP regimes, each with distinct rules and policy goals [8,9].

(b) Under what principles, terms, and safeguards should access to IP-protected data in CEDS be permitted, and how might this affect the (re)distribution of value generated from such data re-use? These elements operationalise IP governance in CEDS: principles (e.g., fairness, proportionality) shape the normative framework, terms (e.g., licensing conditions, non-disclosure agreements) define contractual obligations, while safeguards (e.g., technical protection measures, secure processing environments) ensure compliance and protect legitimate interests. In addition to these, value mechanisms encompass the rules and practices governing fee calculations and benefit redistribution from reusing IP-protected datasets; unlike the direct legal protection offered by IP rights, they address the economic and distributive aspects of data re-use.

Methodologically, this article adopts a doctrinal and policy‑oriented approach to analyse IP governance in CEDS by focusing on IP–data acquis intersections. The research question is addressed through a comparative reading of IP rules set forth in international and EU legal instruments. The analysis focuses on four principal IP rights relevant to CEDS: copyright, the sui generis database right, trade secrets, and patents (and utility models). The selection of these rights is based on their established role in protecting data‑related assets in EU law. The article also examines how the most relevant horizontal and sectoral EU data acquis instruments governing access, (re)use, and sharing of data—the Open Data Directive [10], the Data Governance Act [11], the Data Act [12] (with consideration to the Digital Omnibus Regulation proposal [13]), and the European Health Data Space [EHDS] Regulation [14]—intersect with IP rights in CEDS. It considers how mandatory or incentivising IP governance approaches set forth in these EU data acquis instruments (and in other instruments of IP law) influence the governance of IP rights in CEDS. The article also draws on EU policy documents and scholarly literature to situate its arguments in existing debates. Stakeholder position papers are used to illustrate practical concerns about IP governance in specific contexts. Regarding the use of terminology, although the article uses the legally established notion of ‘data’, there is a plausible argument that the term ‘data product’ could more accurately describe the standardised, consumable unit of data (e.g., a dataset, database, or data service) that is packaged with metadata, licensing terms, and other use conditions for sharing and re-use in CEDS. This alternative notion is inspired by emerging data space architectures (e.g., the Data Spaces Support Centre’s building blocks [5]) and is distinct from the legal definition of ‘data’ under Article 2(1) of the Data Act, which covers “any digital representation of acts, facts, or information”. The term ‘data product’ could add a governance and economic layer to raw ‘data’, as it specifies who can access it, under what conditions, and how value is (re)distributed, making it a potentially useful analytical unit for studying IP governance in CEDS in the future.

The analysis proceeds in four steps: (1) outlining the significance of IP governance in CEDS; (2) mapping the relevance of specific IP rights in CEDS; (3) examining how the abovementioned EU data acquis instruments interact with IP rights in CEDS; and (4) discussing key principles, adequate and effective terms, and state-of-the-art safeguards for IP governance and value (re)distribution in CEDS.

The Significance of IP Governance in Common European Data Spaces

The significance of the research questions lies in recognising that mutually incentivising IP governance solutions—combined with supplementary value mechanisms—can foster trust among stakeholders, guarantee fair competition, and ensure equitable participation in CEDS. IP governance solutions are “mutually incentivising” when governance arrangements align the rights and interests of data holders, data users, and other stakeholders so that all parties have a clear incentive to participate and share IP-protected data assets. These governance arrangements need to be coupled with appropriate ‘value mechanisms’, such as remuneration schemes, revenue or cost-sharing models [15]. For example, a manufacturer of connected medical devices may be incentivised to share product data in the EHDS for health research purposes insofar as it retains control over its IP-protected algorithms through strict legal, technical and organisational measures and receives fair compensation (based on market value) for the disclosure of its IP-protected data [16], including data preparation (e.g. compilation, pseudonymisation, anonymisation) costs associated with making available electronic health data for secondary use purposes. Conversely, unbalanced or poorly implemented measures risk hindering the competitiveness of rights holders and may undermine the prosperity of data-driven research and innovation (R&I) in the EU.

According to the utilitarian and reward theories of IP law (typical of modern Anglo-American legal systems), IP rights aim to strengthen incentives to invest resources in the development, dissemination, and utilisation of protected innovations or creations. According to the personality-based and moral rights theories (typical of continental European legal systems), the objective of IP rights is to protect the creator’s personality and ensure control over the externalisation of their creative will [17]. By providing temporary market power (a limited monopoly) and/or inalienable moral rights alongside economic exclusivity, the protection of IP rights serves as a vital incentive and safeguards mechanism that enables the recovery of investments and compensates researchers and innovators for their costly trial-and-error efforts over time. Such IP-related mechanisms are particularly important for making the lifecycle of new data-driven solutions and digital technologies viable and sustainable, given the considerable financial, technical, and knowledge-based resources required in the form of upfront investments [18]. IP rights (in addition to data protection rules and/or supplementary contractual and non-legal mechanisms) form the basis upon which data holders and other rights holders can assert control and derive value, while also setting certain parameters for data access and (re)use by data users provided that, and to the extent that, the law provides such possibilities. IP rights not only provide legal protection to creators and innovators but also play a fundamental role in shaping incentives, upholding moral rights, safeguarding investments, and fostering collaboration among relevant actors. The protection of IP rights also plays a crucial role in corporate valuation, especially for start-ups seeking funding through venture capital or private equity investments, which in turn can facilitate further R&I [19]. In terms of regulations, a robust legislative framework on IP governance can boost competitiveness by directly incentivising or safeguarding creative autonomy to incentivise mutually beneficial R&I collaborations and by attracting international investments.

In international IP law, Article 7 of the Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS) [20] states that “[t]he protection and enforcement of intellectual property rights should contribute to the promotion of technological innovation and to the transfer and dissemination of technology, to the mutual advantage of producers and users of technological knowledge and in a manner conducive to social and economic welfare, and to a balance of rights and obligations.” This principle is mirrored in the Commission’s IP action plan (2020), which recognises specifically with regard to data sharing that IP rules must be well-calibrated in order to balance rights, obligations, legitimate interests, and the public interest in a positive-sum manner [21]. Balancing legal and economic incentives with the prospect of achieving mutually beneficial value creation is inherently important in the design and functioning of CEDS [22]. However, achieving this fine balance requires delicate trade-offs in legislation and its implementation. This problem has been discussed in recently adopted EU data acquis instruments (e.g., Article 8 of the Data Act, Article 52 of the EHDS Regulation). As IP rights operate through the right to exclude others, their potentially extensive scope may inhibit competition and progress in R&I [23] In such cases, access to and sharing of socioeconomically valuable datasets could be constrained by overly broad exclusive rights. Conversely, an inadequately constructed value mechanism may risk under-compensation or other forms of exploitation, which could undermine trust and the willingness of stakeholders to share valuable datasets.

The Relevance of Specific IP Rights in Common European Data Spaces

The complexities of CEDS require a precise assessment of how the protection and management of specific IP rights (and supplementary value mechanisms) govern IP-protected data assets in relation to specific data processing operations. This poses challenges, because each IP right is subject to specific rules with regard to its particular legal nature and underlying policy objectives. Furthermore, the rules on IP rights are governed at different levels: international, EU, and national. With regard to these dimensions, the following section maps the relevance of four IP rights to CEDS, emphasising their pro-innovation role while analysing data acquis intersections for analytical clarity.

Copyright protection

Copyright (and related rights) refer to legal rights granted to creators over their original intellectual (e.g., literary, artistic or scientific) works, enabling them to control and authorise the use and dissemination of those works. With reference to the Berne Convention [24], the “cornerstone” international treaty on copyright, copyright protection includes economic rights (permitting rights holders to exclusively control the use of their works and be remunerated for their use, by selling them or licensing them to others) and moral rights (empowering authors with the right to claim authorship of the work and the right to object to any derogatory action affecting the work). Copyright may be granted to authors (authors' rights), while ‘related rights’ may be granted to performers, producers, and broadcasters. (While copyright may have relevance in all sectoral or domain-specific CEDS, the protection of ‘related rights’ is only relevant, in principle, in the common European media data space.)

In principle, copyright is territorial and national in scope. However, international treaties, such as the WIPO Copyright Treaty (WCT) [25], adopted under the Berne Convention, guarantee global protection of “expressions” (including computer programs and databases) and the rights of their authors in contracting states, including EU Member States. At EU level, copyright law is largely harmonised, although differences between Member States still exist. The relevant EU legislative instruments are: the Information Society (InfoSoc) Directive [26], the Copyright Term Directive [27], and the Directive on Copyright in the Digital Single Market (CDSM Directive) [28]. While the first two provide for general rules to harmonise the terms of protection and duration of copyright and related rights, the Directive on Copyright in the Digital Single Market introduces certain exceptions and limitations to copyright and related rights in digital and cross-border environments, as well as measures to facilitate certain licensing practices. Notably, the Directive includes exceptions for text and data mining (TDM) activities: Article 3 offers a mandatory, non-overridable exception for research organisations and cultural heritage institutions for scientific purposes, while Article 4 provides a broader opt-out exception for anyone unless rights holders expressly reserve rights.

With relevance to CEDS, databases (compilations of data) and computer programs (software and algorithms, including source code, object code, and graphical user interfaces) may qualify as copyright-protected works insofar as their structure, selection or arrangement reflects sufficient originality and intellectual creativity (regardless of the effort and labour) [29]. However, it is important to point out that copyright protection only safeguards the creative expression in how the contents are organised but does not cover the actual content (data) itself (see Article 5 of WCT). For this reason, individual raw data points and other non-edited data are typically considered to be factual and thus not copyrightable [30]. However, a database combining information with contextual data may enjoy copyright protection if its organisation reflects substantial creative effort. Copyright protection may also extend to the training dataset of an AI system, if humans actively compile the data in a sophisticated manner that, for example, integrates previously overlooked or specially curated data sources [31].

In principle, by granting creators exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, and control access to original databases and software, copyright protection aims to incentivise investment in data collection and primary data analyses. However, this “exclusivity” may lead to restrictive licensing that limits access to databases and software [32]. In addition, the problem of determining “originality” in databases is becoming an increasingly complex question in the AI era, which complicates the application and enforcement of copyright-based governance and licensing mechanisms [33]. Furthermore, the lack of interaction between EU digital and copyright acquis is hindering the availability of copyright-protected content and the transferring of digital assets in CEDS [34]. These problems together pose challenges to the open data sharing and interoperability goals of CEDS. Therefore, striking a balance between different considerations through standardised rights and licensing information, and reconsidering copyright exceptions—such as those allowing TDM for certain entities— would be crucial to facilitate data re-use purposes [35,36]. Technical solutions, such as machine-readable licenses and verifiable credentials, could also help to ensure equitable and clear access regimes without compromising copyright exclusivity.

Sui generis database protection

The sui generis database right is a quasi-property right, unique to EU law, provided under Article 7 of the Database Directive [37]. While copyright protects the original and creative organisation of database contents, the sui generis database right protects non-original databases, which are nevertheless valuable and have required qualitatively and/or quantitatively substantial financial, material, or human investment by the maker of a database in either obtaining, verifying, or presenting the contents to prevent extraction and/or reutilisation of the whole or a substantial part of the contents of that database. The two rights may coexist and apply cumulatively to the same database if conditions for each are met. However, when the contents of a database lack sufficient originality but their compilation was made through substantial investment, the sui generis database right may apply solely, regardless of the existence of copyright protection. For example, this may be the case when raw data from connected (Internet of Things, IoT) devices are automatically collected and aggregated into a large unstructured database.

It is important to point out that the sui generis database right faces challenges regarding its consistent application in increasingly complex data environments, including CEDS. With regard to the case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) (in the British Horseracing [38] and the Fixtures Marketing [39] cases) and the 2018 Evaluation of the Database Directive [40], the database right should be interpreted narrowly, limiting the legal protection only to investments in “obtaining” data rather than in “creating” it. This interpretation rules out the protection of machine- or sensor-generated raw data [41]. However, a narrow interpretation of the right causes a certain degree of legal uncertainty about whether certain big data sets may qualify for sui generis database protection, despite the significant resources devoted to collecting, verifying, and structuring such datasets [42]. Developments in AI and the integration of smart, connected (IoT) devices further complicate the distinction between data collection and data creation, making it harder to determine investment attribution for legal protection [43]. Moreover, distributed data networks, typical of CEDS, could challenge the accurate identification of database makers and investors, which may undermine the potential enforcement of the sui generis database right [42].

Article 43 of the Data Act [12] seeks to clarify and adjust the scope of the sui generis database right in light of recent technological shifts. This provision directly engages with the aforementioned narrow CJEU interpretation by codifying the exclusion of databases where data is obtained from or generated by a connected (IoT) product or related service in order to safeguard users’ rights to access and share such data without restriction. However, given that Article 43 places “obtained” and “generated” data on the same level, it may lead to ambiguities with regard to the semantic closeness of creation and generation [44]. This may raise complex questions about the legal status of mixed databases in CEDS which contain both IoT-generated and externally obtained data. Furthermore, the Data Act does not exclude derived or processed data—such as aggregated, analysed, or transformed outputs—, which may still benefit from sui generis database protection if the maker of the database demonstrates substantial investment in obtaining, verifying, or presenting, as per the Database Directive’s criteria.

Generally, the Data Act’s policy objective of enhancing data sharing via contractual mechanisms places greater emphasis on contractual rights rather than exclusive IP rights. This not only diminishes reliance on the sui generis database right, but it indicates a developing legal framework in which database protection is intended to be supplemented by negotiated data-sharing arrangements designed to facilitate fair access regimes [45]. However, with regard to the abovementioned ambiguities, further regulatory refinements will be needed to reconcile the sui generis database right with the realities of large-scale, machine- or sensor-driven data environments, ensuring that CEDS remain open and commercially viable with due respect to the rights and legitimate interests of database makers.

Trade secrets protection

Article 2(1) of the Trade Secrets Directive [46] defines ‘trade secrets’, in a manner consistent with Article 39(2) of the TRIPS Agreement [20], as “information which meets all of the following requirements: (a) it is secret in the sense that it is not, as a body or in the precise configuration and assembly of its components, generally known among or readily accessible to persons within the circles that normally deal with the kind of information in question; (b) it has commercial value because it is secret; (c) it has been subject to reasonable steps under the circumstances, by the person lawfully in control of the information, to keep it secret.” Recital (14) of the Trade Secrets Directive clarifies that the “definition should [.] cover know-how, business information and technological information where there is both a legitimate interest in keeping them confidential and a legitimate expectation that such confidentiality will be preserved. Furthermore, such know-how or information should have a commercial value, whether actual or potential. Such know-how or information should be considered to have a commercial value, for example, where its unlawful acquisition, use or disclosure is likely to harm the interests of the person lawfully controlling it, in that it undermines that person’s scientific and technical potential, business or financial interests, strategic positions or ability to compete.” A trade secret holder can be any natural or legal person (e.g., company, university or research institute).

While trade secrets are distinguished sometimes as non-registered and contract-dependent rather than true “exclusive rights”, EU documents and related doctrinal analysis often list them among IP rights. The legal protection of trade secrets does not require any additional formalities, but reasonable steps to ensure secrecy generally include the secure storage of confidential information or the conclusion of a non-disclosure agreement (NDA) or confidentiality clauses. Trade secrets protection is crucial in CEDS for safeguarding rapidly evolving digital assets (e.g., sensitive datasets or proprietary algorithms) that underpin competitive advantages. Certain assets, such as research data, consumer behaviour insights data or AI algorithms, often contain commercially (including scientifically or technically) valuable sensitive information that does not qualify per se as an invention eligible for patent protection. However, the protection of trade secret law encourages investments in such data-driven activities, while enabling selective sharing of data and digital solutions. Although it seems odd for the law to encourage secrets, trade secret law paradoxically encourages disclosure rather than secrecy by substituting legal protection for the excessive physical and contractual restrictions that relevant persons would otherwise impose to prevent competitors from acquiring their information [47]. For example, a manufacturer may find it incentivising to share its process data within the common European manufacturing data space under an NDA, physical access restrictions, firewalls, encryption and/or secure processing environment (and a fair compensation scheme) in order to participate in a joint R&D project, while keeping its valuable core algorithms confidential [48].

At the same time, the protection of trade secrets presents significant challenges in ensuring that data and algorithms become available and interoperable in CEDS. In some cases, it can be difficult to determine whether specific information meets the threshold for secrecy. In general, neither data generated by sensors nor real-time streaming data are considered trade secrets, but the insights derived from sensor data, the algorithms used to process it or the specific configurations of sensor networks could potentially be deemed trade secrets if they meet the criteria for trade secret protection (e.g., a company collects sensor data that is suitable to develop a highly accurate prediction model for traffic and other mobility patterns that could give that company a competitive advantage) [49].

Other major challenges of trade secret protection concern the fear of losing control over confidential data (and the inherent competitive edge), as well as the difficulties of enforcement [50]. For this reason, there could be potentially excessive or overly broad claims of trade secrets by data holders in CEDS, which could impede the (re)use of data or algorithms for societally beneficial data-driven R&I activities and impede the functioning of open collaborative data ecosystems. With consideration to these concerns, the EU data acquis and CEDS governance frameworks must carefully balance the protection of trade secrets with mandatory or incentivised data-sharing obligations.

Patent and utility model protection

With regard to Article 52 of the European Patent Convention (EPC) [51], a patent can be granted for any invention, in all fields of technology, provided that they are new (i.e., not part of the state-of-the-art), involve an inventive (i.e., non-obvious) step, and are susceptible of industrial application. A patent holder enjoys the exclusive right to prevent third parties from commercially exploiting their invention for a limited time, but in return, the patent holder must disclose the invention to the public in its patent application. In the EU, patents can be obtained as: (a) national patents granted by national offices, offering protection only within that country; (b) European patents, filed via the European Patent Office, that create a “bundle” of national patents for designated EPC states, with validation required in each country; (c) European patents with unitary effect that provide single, uniform protection across participating EU Member States without separate national validation under the Regulation implementing enhanced cooperation in the area of the creation of unitary patent protection [52]; and (d) international patents, filed under the Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT) [53] at WIPO, that enables applications in multiple countries and may result in a bundle of national or regional patents. In 18 EU Member States, inventors can also obtain a utility model (often referred to as a “petty patent”), which has less stringent requirements than a standard patent. While novelty remains a mandatory criterion for a utility model, the invention only needs to demonstrate a lower level of inventive step compared to patent applications [54].

In the digital context, a patent may protect a novel and inventive product (e.g., a new physical hardware component), as well as novel and inventive processes. Products or processes implemented using computer technology are referred to as ‘computer-implemented inventions’ (CIIs). Patent eligibility generally requires that the CIIs solve a technical problem in a novel and non-obvious manner; otherwise software-based solutions are typically not patentable. However, a process comprising a series of technical steps designed to solve a technical problem may be eligible for patent protection, even if executed through software [55]. In addition to this, it is important to point out that while software code qualifies for copyright protection as a literary work upon fixation in a tangible medium—without needing registration or novelty—, it only safeguards the specific expression, not the underlying ideas or functionality, which limits its scope. Thus, patents, copyright and trade secrets may serve as complementary forms of IP protection, enabling inventors of CIIs to safeguard their innovations and establish competitive advantages. However, in practice [56].

Patent protection is essential for encouraging R&I and maintaining trust within CEDS, yet it faces practical challenges when applied to data-driven or AI-related inventions. One difficulty is defining the boundaries of patentable subject matter, as inventions based on data processing, machine learning models or AI algorithms often blur the line between abstract ideas and technical solutions. In those cases, patent offices must carefully assess whether such inventions solve a concrete technical problem in a novel and non-obvious way, which can be complex due to the intangible and evolving nature of data-driven technologies [57]. Furthermore, the sheer volume and interconnectivity of data-driven innovations can lead to overlapping patent claims (or “patent thickets”), which risk stifling collaboration and increasing transaction costs within CEDS. Licensing practices tied to patented AI or other data-processing methods may also hinder interoperability and limit the re-use of data between different systems and stakeholders.

In the context of the EHDS Regulation, these challenges are particularly relevant, as the regulation seeks to facilitate the secondary use of electronic health data to advance research, innovation and patient care, among other goals. Ensuring robust protection of patents related to medicinal products and medical devices—such as those incorporating advanced AI technologies for diagnostics or treatment monitoring—is essential. At the same time, the implementation of the IP governance framework established by the regulation must strike a balance by ensuring that such patented technologies and related electronic health datasets remain accessible for legitimate (and socioeconomically beneficial) secondary use purposes, while respecting the essence of IP rights.

IP Governance and Related Value Mechanisms in Common European Data Spaces

Horizontal and sector-specific legislative perspectives

The European Strategy for Data [1] set the path to developing a genuine single market for data, aiming to establish a data-driven economy in which both personal and non-personal data—including sensitive information—can flow seamlessly across borders and sectors under regulated, safe and secure conditions. The strategy provided the initial foundation for a package of legislative initiatives to create the necessary overarching governance framework for a data-agile economy, and to address common / similar data sharing issues in key sectors and domains. Among the EU legislative instruments adopted to achieve these policy objectives (to date), the Open Data Directive [10], the Data Governance Act [11], the Data Act [12], and the European Health Data Space [EHDS] Regulation [14] play pivotal roles in shaping the EU data acquis and its intersections with IP governance rules in CEDS. The first three regulations address horizontal aspects of data access, sharing, and (re)use across sectors, establishing foundational principles and mechanisms for interoperable data transmissions. (Note: the Digital Omnibus Regulation proposal [13] plans to simplify the regulatory landscape by integrating the rules on the re-use of protected data under the Open Directive and the Data Governance Act into an expanded and modified Data Act.) The EU data acquis is highly relevant for CEDS because it operationalises policy goals from the European Strategy for Data through concrete rules on data governance and related safeguards regarding the technical and organisational aspects of CEDS infrastructures and services. In addition to the aforementioned cross-sectoral legislative framework, sectoral data-sharing legislation as well as other legislation that ensures availability and interoperability of data can also be an important enabler for the development of CEDS [2,3]. In this regard, the EHDS Regulation is the first overarching sector-specific legislative framework addressing CEDS, which regulates the primary and secondary use of electronic health data, supporting the distinct regulatory needs of the health data ecosystem.

Regarding the intersections between the EU data acquis, CEDS and relevant IP regimes, the following needs to be considered with regard to the research questions. First, the EU data acquis aims to provide essential legal foundations for building interoperable, trustworthy data ecosystems in CEDS by mandating or facilitating data flows under IP-compliant conditions. Second, the EU data acquis aims to introduce mandatory data access rules, calibrated exceptions, limitations, and delegations of control that adjust the exercise of IP rights while purporting to respect its core protections. These objectives may affect the scope and enforceability of IP protection, especially within the context of the dynamic and multi-party ecosystems of CEDS. Given the plethora of factors, the following sections analyse how IP rights (and related value mechanisms) are regulated under specific EU data acquis instruments with consideration to their potential relevance and application in the context of CEDS.

Open data directive

The Open Data Directive (ODD) lays down the legal framework for the re-use of documents held by public sector bodies or public undertakings, and of publicly funded research data. The ODD’s regulatory approach to IP governance, as set forth under Article 1(5) of the Directive [correlating with Article 32i(6) of the Data Act under the Digital Omnibus Regulation proposal], ensures alignment with international agreements on the protection of IP rights. Recital (54) explains that the Directive does not affect the IP rights (“copyright and related rights only, including sui generis forms of protection”) of third parties, or “documents covered by industrial property rights, such as patents and registered designs and trademarks”. Under the ODD, public sector bodies retain their IP rights but must exercise those rights in a manner that actively facilitates re-use, for example, by licensing their own IP-protected documents on open terms. To this end, public sector bodies can leverage CEDS as an effective infrastructure to enable the re-use of documents held by them—through federated networks offering secure, standardised data-sharing mechanisms—while respecting relevant IP rights (either their own or those of third parties). At the same time, the ODD grants public sector bodies legal flexibility to fulfil their re-use obligations through alternative means, such as APIs or open data portals. In addition to the foregoing, Article 10 of the ODD requires Member States to adopt open access policies for publicly funded research data on an ‘open by default’ basis, compatible with FAIR principles, while taking IP rights into account per ‘as open as possible, as closed as necessary’. In CEDS that incorporate data access regimes for research purposes, this can support public funding bodies in making IP-protected research data available under calibrated licensing terms, while balancing openness with IP rights protection.

Data Governance Act

The Data Governance Act (DGA) aims to support the development of CEDS by establishing trusted, neutral data intermediation services (e.g., data marketplace platforms, data ecosystem orchestrators, collaborative data pools) and introducing conditions for the re-use, within the Union, of certain categories of data held by public sector bodies. Its IP-related provisions are directly relevant to CEDS as they regulate the governance of IP-protected data in relation to data re-use and data intermediation services within the scope of the DGA. Data intermediation services can act as neutral hubs in CEDS by matching data holders with data users under compliant IP terms, ensuring trusted data access and sharing mechanisms without direct peer-to-peer exposure of sensitive assets. Article 5(7) (complemented by Recitals (17) et seq.) of the DGA [correlating with Article 32i(7) of the Data Act under the Digital Omnibus Regulation proposal] mandates that re-use of data shall be allowed only in compliance with IP rights and insofar as they are compatible with international agreements on the protection of IP rights. Furthermore, data subject to IP rights as well as trade secrets may be transmitted to a third party only where such transmission is lawful by virtue of Union or national law or with the agreement of the rights holder. As a limitation, public sector bodies may not exercise the sui generis right of the maker of a database to prevent the re-use of data or to restrict re-use beyond the limits set by the DGA. Public sector bodies must also ensure that additional safeguards (e.g., data modification, aggregation or treatment by any method of disclosure control) are put in place for situations in which the re-use of such public sector data takes place on the basis of a processing of the data outside the public sector, including with regard to commercially sensitive data and IP rights. To support the implementation of those obligations, on the basis of Article 30(d) of the DGA [correlating with Article 42 of the Data Act under the Digital Omnibus Regulation proposal], the European Data Innovation Board advises and assists the Commission in developing consistent guidelines on how to best protect, in this framework, commercially sensitive non-personal data, in particular trade secrets, but also non-personal data representing content protected by IP rights from unlawful access that risks intellectual property theft or industrial espionage. Such guidance is important to foster trust and legal certainty essential for private sector participation in CEDS.

Data Act

The Data Act plays a pivotal role in enabling the development and deployment of CEDS, including through the governance of IP rights. Regarding rules addressing specifically CEDS, Article 33 sets essential requirements regarding interoperability of data, of data sharing mechanisms and services, as well as of CEDS. It mandates data holders to describe datasets—including use restrictions and licenses—in machine-readable formats to enable lawful access and use (without infringing IP rights). The provision supports IP protection by requiring transparency on applicable licenses and restrictions. Similarly to this, the Data Act’s switching rules (Chapter VI) prevent vendor lock-in in cloud services, facilitating data portability and provider changes, which can enhance data mobility essential for dynamic CEDS ecosystems. Both set of rules are crucial for CEDS, as they ensure seamless data flows across interconnected ecosystems while respecting IP boundaries, thereby directly supporting the operational goals of CEDS by promoting trust and participation among stakeholders.

For CEDS, another important set of provisions concern the Data Act’s harmonised framework regulating access to and use of data generated by connected (IoT) products and related services across the Union, as IoT data is a key component in most CEDS (such as in the manufacturing, mobility, and energy sectors), where large-scale data collection and real-time data analytics are crucial for innovation and improved operations. In principle, the Data Act recognises users (natural or legal persons) as entitled to access ‘data from connected products and related services’. Meanwhile, data holders—typically manufacturers or service providers—must make ‘readily available data’, including metadata, accessible free of charge in structured, machine-readable formats without undue delay. Recital (15) of the Data Act excludes from this obligation “information inferred or derived from such data, which is the outcome of additional investments into assigning values or insights from the data, in particular by means of proprietary, complex algorithms, including those that are part of proprietary software.” Regarding its provisions on IP governance, Recital (13) of the Data Act clarifies that it is “without prejudice to Union and national legal acts providing for the protection of [IP] rights”. However (in contrast to the ODD and the DGA), the Data Act does not clarify its intersection with international IP instruments, which may lead to legal uncertainties, if a participant of a CEDS were to base its IP-related claims on those.

The Data Act makes a dogmatically debatable distinction between the protection of IP rights and trade secrets by providing for separate rules for the latter. Recital (31) explains that the Data Act “requires data holders to disclose certain data to users, or third parties of a user’s choice, even when such data qualifies for protection as trade secrets, [but this obligation] should be interpreted in such a manner as to preserve the protection afforded to trade secrets under [the Trade Secrets Directive] [46]. These rules are particularly pertinent to CEDS, where trade secret-protected data (e.g., proprietary algorithms or insights) must flow securely to foster collaboration without eroding competitive advantages. For instance, in the common European mobility data space, an automotive mobility service provider could share proprietary traffic prediction algorithms with transport authorities under strict confidentiality protocols, facilitating collaborative urban planning initiatives while safeguarding its competitive models from rival providers. It is important to point out that the Data Act’s measures for trade secret protection are more detailed than the Trade Secrets Directive’s open concepts. In particular, the requirement to notify a competent authority for an initial validity check of trade secret claims and provisions on technical protection measures provide enhanced remedies beyond standard trade secret protection [58]. While this regulatory solution may, to a certain degree, build confidence in trade secret protection, there are potential challenges concerning the regulatory (counter)balancing of the power position of data (trade secret) holders that may hinder seamless data flows in CEDS and prevent organisations from identifying “mutually beneficial” data-driven relationships [59].

With reference to Articles 4(6) and 5(9) of the Data Act, data holders are required to identify trade secrets prior to the disclosure, and have the possibility to agree with users, or third parties of a user’s choice, on necessary measures to preserve their confidentiality, including by the use of model contractual terms, confidentiality agreements, strict access protocols, technical standards and the application of codes of conduct. In CEDS, these supplementary instruments can play an important role in enabling controlled sharing of trade secret data across multi-stakeholder ecosystems. However, the cited requirement may generate a risk of unjustified restrictions on data access through “overclaiming” [60]. To counter this, the Data Act sets forth that data holders cannot, in principle, refuse a data access request solely on the basis that certain data is considered to be a trade secret. Only, in exceptional circumstances, as defined by Articles 4(8) and 5(11) of the Data Act, a data holder who is a trade secret holder “may refuse on a case-by-case basis a request for access to the specific data in question” if it is able to demonstrate to the user or to the third party that, despite the technical and organisational measures taken by the user or by the third party, “it is highly likely to suffer serious economic damage from the disclosure of trade secrets”.

Although the Data Act aims to address imbalances in cases where data value is concentrated with data holders, trade secret holders may face significant challenges in demonstrating that the disclosure of certain data would “highly likely cause serious economic damage”—a high threshold that requires clear, objective evidence of potential harm. Proving such “highly likely” risk entails pre-emptively substantiating scenarios where data disclosure could lead to irreparable loss or competitive disadvantage, which is a complex task in a fast-evolving data market landscape. This evidentiary burden could be especially challenging to apply in the dynamic multi-participant ecosystems of CEDS, which may potentially deter data holders from participating in such data ecosystems, when participation is voluntary. For instance, in a common European data space for manufacturing, a machine tool producer could refuse a user’s request for proprietary sensor algorithms by demonstrating that disclosure to a competing firm in the space would likely enable reverse-engineering, causing serious economic damage through lost market share. This burden of proof (and associated legal costs) is compounded by the requirement to identify and document trade secrets proactively before data requests arise, ensuring they are legally and technically protected. Similarly, the technical complexity of implementing robust confidentiality standards and continuous monitoring to prevent data misuse may pose operational challenges. These issues highlight the potential practical difficulties of ensuring large-scale data accessibility within CEDS, while safeguarding proprietary innovations and commercial interests in accordance with the Data Act [60].

European Health Data Space (EHDS) regulation

Regarding sector-specific IP governance issues in the EU data acquis, it is important to highlight the relevant peculiarities of the EHDS Regulation, the first legislative framework supporting the development of CEDS in a specific sector. The EHDS Regulation has introduced a notably different IP governance framework compared to the legal regime that has previously governed IP rights in health data ecosystems within the EU. One of the main objectives of the EHDS Regulation is to scale up the processing of both personal and non-personal electronic health data for secondary use purposes (i.e., for purposes other than the initial purposes for which they were collected or produced). Article 53 of the EHDS Regulation stipulates legitimate secondary use purposes for the processing of electronic health data, including for: public interest in the areas of public or occupational health; policymaking or regulatory activities; official statistics; or scientific research related to the health or care sectors, including development and innovation activities. In this data governance framework, Article 52(1) of the EHDS Regulation requires that “electronic health data protected by intellectual property rights, trade secrets or covered by the regulatory data protection right [.] shall be made available for secondary use”. Article 52(2) of the EHDS Regulation calls on health data holders to inform the designated health data access bodies (HDABs)—the competent public sector body/bodies designated in each Member State—about any IP rights, trade secrets or regulatory data protection right within the electronic health datasets at their disposal and justify the need for their protection. Article 52(3) delegates the responsibility to HDABs to determine and implement appropriate (i.e., necessary and proportionate) legal, organisational and technical measures to protect those rights when granting data access to health data users. However, under Article 52(5), if access to electronic health data entails a “serious risk” of infringing IP rights, trade secrets or the regulatory data protection right, “which cannot be addressed in a satisfactory manner”, the competent HDAB must refuse access to the health data applicant.

The IP governance scheme in the EHDS Regulation is arguably at odds with other international and EU legal instruments governing IP rights. Article 52 of the EHDS Regulation essentially trims the power of health data holders in managing their IP rights (either their own rights or when acting on behalf of another rights holder) and delegates that responsibility (including decision over its justification and implementation of suitable safeguards) to the HDABs (national authorities). At the same time, the EHDS Regulation makes no reference to international agreements on IP rights, nor to the Database Directive, and it claims to be “without prejudice to” the Data Governance Act and the Data Act. With regard to this (lack of) legal interplay, it is debatable how the IP governance scheme under the EHDS Regulation interacts with the protection of IP rights guaranteed under international treaties (e.g., Article 39 of the TRIPS Agreement [20], Article 10bis of the Paris Convention for the Protection of Industrial Property [61], or Article 5 of the WIPO Copyright Treaty [25]), under Articles 16 and 17 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union [62], or in the constitutions of Member States.

The IP governance scheme under the EHDS Regulation may also lead to uncertainties about its proper interaction with relevant rules under EU secondary law, such as the sui generis database right under the Database Directive or the protection of trade secrets according to the Trade Secrets Directive. Furthermore, HDABs may not always be in a position to accurately assess the likelihood and severity of economic or scientific risks related to the disclosure of datasets that are claimed to be “secret”, and risks may be amplified by the possibility that different HDABs may have different legal interpretations. In addition, there is uncertainty regarding the proper interaction between the EHDS and the trade secrets provisions under the Data Act in cases where access is requested to data from connected health products [63]. While the Data Act introduces the ‘right to refuse data sharing’ on specific grounds, the EHDS Regulation does not provide a similar control mechanism. While Article 6(2) of the Data Act prohibits the use of data to develop competing products; to derive insights about the economic situation, assets and production methods of or use by the data holder; or to use data in a manner that adversely impacts the security of the product or related service(s), the EHDS Regulation does not prohibit such secondary use purposes.

The secondary data use scheme of the EHDS Regulation has been criticised by health stakeholders (including medical associations, universities, industry associations, and even some patient groups) for not granting adequate and effective control and safeguards to health data holders (rights holders) and for potentially undermining existing legal protections and incentives that are particularly important for researchers and innovators in the health ecosystem [[64], [65], [66], [67]]. The critics have warned that legal uncertainties, inadequate safeguards to IP rights, and a potentially fragmented implementation of relevant rules in the EHDS Regulation could slow health R&I, weaken the global competitiveness of the sector, and limit patients’ access to state-of-the-art healthcare solutions in the EU. This negative perception may increase development costs, risk IP erosion due to loss of ultimate control over data-derived assets, delay innovation, and place relevant actors in the European healthcare domain (e.g., research institutes and universities, medical device companies, pharmaceutical companies) at a competitive disadvantage compared to regions with clearer data commercialisation frameworks.

Regulatory trend

Overall, the legislative trend in the EU data acquis is one of “flexibilisation”, which is described here as the introduction of mandatory access regimes with calibrated exceptions, limitations, or delegations of control to public authorities, thereby adjusting the exercise (rather than the core scope) of IP rights enshrined in existing international and EU law, while purporting to respect IP exclusivity, though with varying degrees of override or deference depending on the context and right at issue. This trend manifests selectively. As discussed, the Data Governance Act permits re-use of public data if it is compatible with IP rights and with rights holder agreement, while limiting the enforcement of the sui generis database right. The Data Act defers to IP legal instruments but excludes inferred or derived data from access duties and allows trade secret refusals only on proof of “highly likely serious economic damage” regardless of safeguards. The EHDS Regulation mandates secondary use of IP-protected electronic health data unless HDABs deem infringement risks unmitigable, delegating safeguard decisions to authorities. With regard to these emerging legislative frameworks on horizontal and sector-specific IP governance, clarity is essential on the proper application of IP rights in order to balance R&I incentives with data access goals. Without precise delineation of exceptions and limitations, rights holders may face uncertainty that could stifle investment in data-driven technologies and pose legal barriers to the further development and implementation of CEDS. Therefore, policymakers must prioritise the fine-tuning of legislations and deliver clear implementing acts and other supplementary guidance to ensure consistent protection and enforcement of IP rights across Member States.

‘Fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory’ (FRAND) data sharing terms and non-disclosure agreements (NDAs)

The establishment of a mutually incentivising data governance scheme that respects data sovereignty in an open and collaborative data ecosystem requires finding an appropriate balance between data access and data confidentiality. By combining legally mandated ‘FRAND’ (fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory) data-sharing terms and standardised non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) with robust technical and organisational measures—supported by regulatory guidance and effective dispute resolution mechanisms—, CEDS can facilitate data sharing in ways that respect the protection of IP rights while promoting R&I and competition. The two legal measures operate in a complementary way: while FRAND licensing opens data access on equitable terms, NDAs secure the confidentiality of data that should remain protected.

The principles for calculating fees and compensating for costs associated with providing access to data subject to IP rights in CEDS are grounded in the concept of FRAND (and related) terms, as outlined in the Data Governance Act, the Data Act and the EHDS Regulation. According to Article 6(1) of the Data Governance Act [correlating with Article 32y of the Data Act under the Digital Omnibus Regulation proposal], public sector bodies which allow re-use of data held by public sector bodies may charge fees for allowing such re-use. However, with reference to Article 6(2) of the Data Governance Act, any fees charged must be transparent, non-discriminatory, proportionate, and objectively justified and shall not restrict competition. Public sector bodies may offer discounted or free access in specific cases—such as for non-commercial scientific research, SMEs, start-ups, civil society, and educational institutions—to support entities that struggle to collect data, in line with state aid rules. This approach intends to safeguard IP rights by ensuring that fee structures under the Data Governance Act prevent unauthorised exploitation of IP-protected data held by public sector bodies data while maintaining fair access conditions.

In business-to-business (B2B) relations, Article 8(1) of the Data Act states that where a data holder is obliged to make data available to a data recipient (in order to grant the user the right to share data from connected products and related services with third parties), or under other applicable Union law or national legislation adopted in accordance with Union law, it has to agree with the data recipient the arrangements for making the data available and must do so “under fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms and conditions and in a transparent manner”. According to Articles 8(2) and 8(3) of the Data Act, the data holder shall not discriminate regarding the arrangements for making data available between comparable categories of data recipients, and contracts made under this obligation must not include unfair contractual terms. While these provisions aim to create a level playing field in the data economy by emphasising equitable compensation and ensuring affordable access to data across all business sizes and sectors, it is important to point out the inherent risk for data holders (and other rights holders) of unintentionally disclosing sensitive or confidential business information during this mandated sharing process, which could harm competitive positions or pose security risks. Furthermore, given that, in the context of standard-essential patents (SEPs), parties have regularly failed to reach a licensing agreement on FRAND terms, there is also uncertainty about the very meaning of the FRAND terms [68]. This is expected to be specifiable to individual circumstances only through practice in negotiations, contracting, reasoning, and litigations over time [69].

In the health data ecosystem, Article 62 of the EHDS Regulation ensures that fees charged for access to electronic health data for secondary use must be “transparent and non-discriminatory”, “in proportion to the cost of making the data available”, and “shall not restrict competition”. These requirements align with the FRAND principles by promoting fair compensation while preventing unjustified or discriminatory charges. The value mechanism scheme also aims to balance the IP rights of data holders (or other rights holders) with the wider public interest of facilitating an equitable data governance framework for the legitimate secondary use of electronic health data.

Generally, ‘FRAND’ terms intend to set a balanced framework that enhances data access and innovation while preventing anti-competitive practices or unjustified preferential treatment in licensing or data-sharing contracts [69]. ‘Fair’ terms relate to the licensing or contractual conditions that must not be anti-competitive or unlawful. An example of “unfair terms” would be a bundling license that obliges the data user to pay for an unwanted software product. ‘Reasonable’ terms mainly concern fees charged, which should not be excessive. ‘Non-discriminatory’ terms may cover licenses or data sharing contracts that are offered without unjustified differences between similarly situated economic actors. This requires that data users in comparable market positions receive similar terms unless objective justifications exist. Objective justifications and efficiencies linked to particular differences in terms must be clearly demonstrated.

To complement FRAND terms (and with regard to the abovementioned regulatory uncertainties), NDAs are expected to play a critical role in protecting IP rights, especially trade secrets, within CEDS [70]. The purpose of NDAs is to define what constitutes confidential information and to set out strict confidentiality obligations. In general, NDAs specify the scope and nature of protected data, detail the responsibilities of receiving parties not to disclose or misuse the information, and impose limitations on data re-use strictly to agreed purposes. NDAs may also outline exceptions, such as disclosures required by law, and enforce security measures like encryption and access controls to safeguard protected data. Depending on the context and parties, different types of NDAs can be employed in CEDS. Mutual NDAs protect both parties when sensitive information is shared bilaterally, while one-way NDAs focus on safeguarding the information of the disclosing party only. In addition, standardised NDA templates developed by the Commission could promote legal certainty and ease of adoption across sectors, while sector- or project-specific NDAs could address tailored confidentiality needs in CEDS.

By establishing clear durations for confidentiality and providing remedies and penalties for breaches, NDAs can create a legal framework to uphold trust and cooperation in data sharing, ensuring that parties can share valuable information without risking unauthorised exposure. The use of model contractual terms and confidentiality agreements are authorised by Article 4(6) of the Data Act with respect to the protection of trade secrets. Similarly, Article 52(4) of the EHDS Regulation mandates the Commission to develop and recommend non-binding models of contractual terms for contractual arrangements between health data holders and health data users for the sharing of data containing information or content protected by IP rights or trade secrets.

Ensuring socioeconomically ‘fair’ redistribution of value created/generated from the re-use of data subject to the protection of IP rights

CEDS seek to ensure socioeconomically fair redistribution of value generated from the re-use of data by implementing an integrated approach grounded in legal, governance, economic, and societal principles [71]. Central to this is the enforcement of data access, data sharing and related IP governance rules that adhere to FRAND principles. This framework needs to balance the interests of diverse stakeholders, ranging from large corporations to SMEs, and potentially even individual data subject contributors (e.g., users, consumers, passengers, patients). By doing so, the framework must prevent monopolistic/oligopolistic control of data and promote healthy, competitive data ecosystems where all participants may benefit proportionately from access to and re-use of data. Inclusive governance structures that involve real multi-stakeholder representation could ensure that value (re)distribution is not solely driven by market mechanisms but also respects societal norms and values, fostering public trust and transparency in the governance of CEDS.

In addition to ensuring FRAND terms, CEDS should provide adequate incentives and compensation for data holders, encouraging active participation and investment in data generation and sharing. These incentives may take the form of monetary remuneration or reciprocal benefits, such as access to additional datasets or analytical insights, which could further fuel innovation. Hybrid governance models combining “data commons” with market-based approaches could recognise that the value of data extends beyond pure economics to social and cultural dimensions—such as community building and knowledge co-creation [72]. Such models could help to promote redistributive outcomes that reward collective contributions while safeguarding societal benefits. Moreover, the support of SMEs and research organisations through transparent fee structures that cap charges at marginal costs could safeguard equitable access and ensure that smaller players remain competitive contributors within CEDS.

Despite promising conceptual ideas, significant challenges persist in establishing fair and incentivising schemes for the redistribution of value from access to and re-use of data subject to IP rights in CEDS. For instance, in the common European manufacturing data space, balancing proprietary IP rights of industrial innovators with fair data access regimes presents challenges to the enforcement of FRAND terms with respect to the policy goal of ensuring that SMEs and start-ups can participate in the data ecosystem without disproportionate cost barriers [48]. In the common European energy data space (CEEDS), the challenge of integrating data from multiple sources while maintaining fair access and value (re)distribution demands the adoption of socioeconomically just regulatory frameworks that could promote inclusivity of vulnerable consumers and prevent dominance by large utilities [73]. Similarly, in the common European mobility data space (EMDS), the highly dispersed data sources across different systems, organisations, and platforms and the diverse stakeholders—from multiple private operators to public service providers—necessitate harmonised IP governance models and licensing schemes to ensure fair sharing of value without privileging dominant players and marginalising smaller participants. While recognising that some data should be freely available in the EMDS to a certain extent, it will be also essential to determine how data covered by licensing should be priced and structured [49].

Secure processing environments and the potential deployment of advanced technologies for the management of IP rights

Secure processing environments (SPEs) (sometimes referred to as ‘trusted research environments’ or ‘data safe havens’) provide secure infrastructure where sensitive data subject to IP rights can be processed without risk of unauthorised exposure. With reference to Article 2(20) of the Data Governance Act, SPEs provide physical or virtual environment and organisational means to ensure compliance with Union law, as well as with applicable national law, and allow the entity providing the SPE to determine and supervise all data processing actions. SPEs are intended to enable stakeholders to collaborate and share protected datasets while maintaining robust safeguards, which is essential to building trust in multi-party data ecosystems. The concept of a SPE has been invoked in the EHDS Regulation, in which Article 73 requires that HDABs provide access to electronic health data pursuant to a data permit for secondary use purposes in a SPE [74,75].

Alongside secure processing environments, advanced technologies such as AI and distributed ledger technologies (DLTs)—including blockchain and smart contracts—together with complementary tools like digital watermarking and content fingerprinting, hold significant transformative potential for IP governance in CEDS. For example, AI could improve compliance verification of IP rights by enabling continuous tracking and automated infringement detection [76]. Concurrently, DLTs could provide enhanced security and trust by maintaining decentralised, tamper-proof records of IP-protected data assets [77]. Smart contracts built on DLTs could automate IP license management, fee payments, value (re)distribution, and facilitate self-executing agreements that help minimise disputes and delays. Article 11 of the Data Act explicitly authorises the data holder to apply appropriate technical protection measures, including smart contracts and encryption, to prevent unauthorised access to data. Overall, the promise of these technologies lies in their deployment as a comprehensive set of (semi)automated safeguards that could ensure authorised use, traceability, and effective enforcement of IP rights in CEDS.

Conclusion

Data sovereignty is one of the central innovative and transformative concepts catalysing the development of data spaces [78,79]. However, this analysis reveals that the operationalisation of data sovereignty in CEDS through the governance of IP rights faces profound legal challenges stemming from fragmented intersections between international/EU IP regimes and the EU data acquis. The protection and management of IP rights within CEDS face challenges due to the multi-layered legal frameworks governing IP rights, including copyright (InfoSoc Directive and CDSM Directive with TDM exceptions), the sui generis database right (Database Directive, narrowed by CJEU case law and the Data Act’s exclusion for IoT data), trade secrets (Trade Secrets Directive, with the Data Act’s high threshold for trade secret holders to refuse data sharing), and patents (EPC with patentability challenges relating to computer-implemented inventions). Although EU data legislation (especially the Open Data Directive, the Data Governance Act, the Data Act, and the EHDS Regulation) seeks to promote controlled access, sharing, and re-use of data, the calibrated exceptions, limitations, or delegations of control to public authorities—especially in the Data Act and the EHDS Regulation—regarding the protection of IP rights, and the lack of clear references to other IP regimes risk generating negative effects due to significant legal uncertainties. For this reason, this analysis argues that the EU data acquis should explicitly respect international IP regimes, and guarantee smooth interplay between IP provisions in EU secondary legislation to ensure inter-legislative consistency.

Looking forward, this article recommends harmonising the relevant EU data acquis with international/EU IP baselines by clarifying exceptions, limitations, and other open questions through targeted amendments, additional recitals, implementing acts and/or guidance (as appropriate). In addition, there are several ways in which the implementation of the EU data acquis can help to clarify and strengthen IP governance schemes when applied in CEDS. For instance, the Commission is already empowered to provide guidance on FRAND model terms and NDAs (pursuant to Article 41 of the Data Act or Article 52(4) of the EHDS Regulation). Furthermore, it can support competent national authorities in developing secure processing environments (Article 20 of the Data Governance Act or Article 73 of the EHDS Regulation), and could even promote the use of smart contracts for automated licensing (Article 11 of the Data Act). However, legal and technological solutions alone are insufficient to resolve the delicate balance between openness and protection regarding IP governance in CEDS.

Data sovereignty implies participation in the governance of CEDS [80]. To support the creation and implementation of adequate and effective IP governance and accompanying value mechanisms, it is important that policymakers, industrial actors, researchers, and other interested parties can regularly engage in a structured dialogue. Multi-stakeholder governance mechanisms (such as the European Data Innovation Board, the European Health Data Space Board and its stakeholder forum, and other data space governance authorities) could help to foster the development of mutually incentivising value mechanisms in CEDS (e.g., proportionate fees, reciprocal access, SME discounts), ensuring socioeconomically fair value (re)distribution and without undermining IP incentives critical for R&I and competitiveness. A multi-level, multi-stakeholder (public–private) effort could address existing legal inconsistencies, regulatory gaps, and implementation challenges in the context of CEDS [81]. Institutionalised collaboration is vital not only to refine and enhance the coherence of the existing frameworks but also to build mutual trust and understanding among all actors involved. Unlocking the potential opportunities of CEDS requires the resolution of interrelated technical, organisational, business, legal, and sector- or domain specific challenges. Only through inclusive and sustained engagement can the EU realise its vision of developing CEDS that promote innovation, respect IP rights, and foster value creation that benefits the broader common good.
