Is zero-profile fixation safe in consecutive two-level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion with partial uncinate resection? A finite element analysis
Authors: Ning Wu, Jiarui Zhang, Zhilin Ge, Rueishiuan Jiang, Lingxi Cai, Siqi Zhang, Jiying Wei, Kai Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe necessity and extent of uncinate process resection (UPR) during anterior cervical discectomy and fusion remains controversial. The Zero-Profile device offers a low-profile alternative to traditional plate-cage systems but its biomechanical stability following various degrees of UPR, particularly in two-level ACDF, has not been clearly established.MethodsThis computational finite element analysis used a validated three-dimensional C2–T1 cervical spine model to simulate Two-Level ACDF (C4–C6) with a Zero-Profile construct under three unilateral uncinate process resection (UPR) conditions (0%, 30%, 50%). No clinical or cadaveric experiments were performed. Under six physiological motions (flexion, extension, left/right lateral bending, left/right axial rotation), we quantified global and segmental range of motion (ROM), adjacent-segment ROM, von Mises stress in the implant, vertebral body and endplate stresses, intradiscal pressure (IDP), and maximum displacement.ResultsUnilateral UPR led to a dose-dependent deterioration in biomechanical stability. In the 50% UPR model, the ROM of the C4/5 segment increased by 29.4% in flexion and 16.7% in lateral bending, while adjacent levels (C3/4 and C6/7) also exhibited elevated ROMs, indicating reduced segmental rigidity and increased compensatory motion. Stress on the fixation components rose significantly, with peak screw stress increasing by 47.1% and cage stress by 90.0% under flexion. Endplate stresses also increased, particularly in bending and rotation, with the C5 superior and C6 inferior endplates showing stress increments of 41.5% and 20.3%, respectively. Moreover, IDP at adjacent segments increased with greater UPR extent, reflecting enhanced load transmission to non-fused levels. The maximum construct displacement rose by 50.0% in flexion and 48.8% in axial rotation in the 50% UPR model, underscoring the progressive compromise of structural integrity. In contrast, the 30% UPR model demonstrated only mild changes in motion, stress, and displacement, suggesting that biomechanical performance remained within acceptable limits.ConclusionsExtensive UPR (≥ 50%) substantially compromises biomechanical stability in two-level ACDF using the ZP fixation, increasing mechanical load on implants and adjacent segments. ZP fixation appears acceptable when UPR is limited to 30%, but caution is warranted when higher resection is required. Surgeons should consider supplemental fixation strategies or alternative systems when extensive UPR is anticipated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09226-2.

Introduction

The uncovertebral joint is a distinctive component of the cervical spine, formed by the articulation between the uncinate process along the superolateral margin of C3–C7 vertebral bodies and the adjacent inferior vertebral margin. Degenerative hypertrophy, fracture, or osteophyte formation of the uncinate process can narrow the foramen and compress adjacent neurovascular structures—most notably the nerve roots, but also the vertebral artery and sympathetic chain—thereby contributing to cervical spondylosis, particularly radiculopathy [1, 2]. Among proposed mechanisms, nerve root compression is widely regarded as a principal driver of chronic neck pain and upper-limb radicular symptoms [3].

For patients with radiculopathy refractory to conservative care, anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) remains a standard intervention, providing decompression and segmental stabilization [4]. When foraminal stenosis is primarily due to hypertrophic uncovertebral pathology, simple discectomy may be inadequate; in such cases, partial or complete uncinate process resection (UPR) is often required to restore foraminal patency and achieve thorough neural decompression.

Despite its anatomical rationale, the effect of UPR on postoperative biomechanical stability remains debated. The uncovertebral joint restrains lateral bending and axial rotation; resecting the uncinate process can increase segmental range of motion (ROM), potentially elevating risks of endplate subsidence, pseudarthrosis, and adjacent-segment degeneration. Cadaveric work (e.g., SCHMIEDER et al., 2007) demonstrated that even partial UPR diminishes stability, particularly in lateral bending [5]. These considerations underscore the need to quantify how the extent of UPR influences cervical biomechanics, providing the basis for the analyses that follow.

With advances in digital modeling techniques, finite element analysis (FEA) has emerged as a powerful tool for investigating the biomechanical consequences of UPR. Several studies have highlighted that the extent of resection is a key determinant of postoperative mechanical behavior [6–8]. Compared with 30% resection, unilateral UPR exceeding 50% has been shown to significantly increase ROM, stress concentration at the bone–implant interface, and stress within the screw channels, thereby elevating the risk of screw loosening and implant subsidence [7, 9, 10]. These findings suggest that UPR should be limited to a reasonable extent to avoid compromising construct stability, even if more extensive resection offers better decompression.

Traditionally, ACDF procedures have utilized interbody cages combined with anterior cervical plates to maintain spinal alignment and promote fusion. More recently, however, the emergence of minimally invasive techniques has led to increased use of zero-profile (ZP) devices. A ZP device is an anchored interbody spacer with integrated screws that sits flush with the anterior vertebral body, eliminating the need for a protruding anterior plate. These integrated implants combine a cage and anterior screws into a low-profile design that avoids plate prominence and may reduce plate-related dysphagia [11–14]. Although ZP fixations have demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes in single-level ACDF, with or without UPR, their use in multilevel procedures involving UPR remains controversial [15–17]. On the one hand, multilevel fusions inherently demand greater construct stability; on the other, UPR further weakens the anterior column. Compared with ZP fixations, anterior plating constructs have been reported to provide greater mechanical support in such scenarios, with several studies noting reduced implant subsidence and enhanced segmental stability in two-level ACDF. Consequently, many recent clinical and biomechanical investigations of multilevel ACDF with UPR continue to rely on traditional plating constructs.

In clinical practice, extensive UPR may be required in consecutive two-level ACDF when severe foraminal stenosis is driven by uncovertebral osteophytes. Despite the growing adoption of ZP devices, evidence beyond single-level ACDF—particularly when substantial UPR is performed in two-level constructs—remains limited [18]. As a result, surgeons often favor plate-based constructs when large foraminal resections are anticipated; whether ZP remains acceptable when UPR is limited versus extensive has not been clearly defined [19].

Taken together, although UPR facilitates decompression, its potential to compromise postoperative stability—especially in multilevel constructs—requires careful biomechanical appraisal when ZP devices are considered. To address this gap, we constructed and validated a high-fidelity three-dimensional finite element model of consecutive two-level ACDF (C4/5–C5/6) incorporating unilateral UPR at C4/5 of approximately 30% and 50%, and evaluated segmental ROM, implant and endplate stresses and adjacent-segment kinematics under multiple loading conditions. We therefore hypothesized that minor UPR (~ 30%) would maintain the biomechanical performance of ZP fixation within acceptable ranges, whereas extensive UPR (~ 50%) would produce materially greater motion and stress responses indicative of reduced stability.

Materials and methods

Models

All experimental content has been approved by the Human Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (NO. K-2022-125-1). Written informed consent was obtained from individual or guardian participants.

The three-dimensional cervical spine finite element model used in this study was based on our previously developed and validated C2–T1 segment model [20]. This model was validated in prior work by comparing its ROM under flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation with published in vitro experimental data. The results demonstrated good agreement between the model and the experimental measurements. Furthermore, a mesh sensitivity analysis was conducted in our previous study to assess the numerical stability and convergence of the model. The findings confirmed that mesh density had minimal influence on key biomechanical parameters, indicating that the model was both robust and reliable [20].

In the present study, structural modifications were made to this validated base model to meet the objectives of this research. Specifically, a two-level ACDF procedure (C4/5 and C5/6) was simulated, in which the C4/5 segment underwent unilateral UPR at two levels: 30% and 50% of the uncinate process. The choice of UPR thresholds brackets a clinically relevant spectrum: ~30% reflects a conservative decompression commonly used to restore foraminal patency, whereas ~ 50% approximates an aggressive upper bound reported in prior cadaveric/finite-element literature, enabling a dose–response appraisal of stability across conditions [5–10]. UPR extent was defined volumetrically as removal of 30% or 50% of the ipsilateral uncinate process at C4/5. The baseline uncinate volume (V0) was obtained from the segmented cortical shell and enclosed cancellous core bounded by the superior endplate footprint and lateral cortical margin. Resection proceeded from the tip toward the base within a prism aligned to local AP/SI axes, preserving the medial/posterior buttress and the endplate cortex; targeted removal volumes were 0.3V0 and 0.5V0. The contralateral uncinate was left intact. Zero-profile (ZP) fixation was implanted at both levels. The interbody fixation was represented by a generic zero-profile anchored spacer (integrated cage with anterior screws); the geometry was constructed for analysis and does not replicate any single commercial system, with key dimensions and screw trajectories selected within ranges reported for widely used devices, and the authors have no relationships with device manufacturers [11–14]. Material properties

The definitions of the materials of the model are all based on the previous literature, as shown in Table 1. The cortical, cancellous and bony endplates of the vertebral body are all defined as an isotropic elastoplastic material [21–23]. Among them, the nucleus pulposus model was modeled by elastic fluid property parameters, while the annulus fibrosus matrix and the annulus fibrosus fibers had hyperelastic and orthogonal anisotropic nonlinear material parameter characteristics, respectively [24]. For the cervical ligaments, different anatomical thicknesses were used according to the results of previous studies and were represented by orthogonal anisotropic nonlinear properties. The articular process cartilage was expressed by the linear elastic material with an elastic modulus of 10 MPa. See Table 1 for specific material properties.

Boundary, contact, and loading conditions

In the model, the bottom surface of the T1 vertebra was completely constrained. An axial compression load of 73.6 N was used to simulate the head weight, which was applied to the tip of the C2 vertebral body. At the same time, the models of the three planes (flexion, extension, lateral bending and axial rotation) of the natural motion of the cervical vertebra were analyzed under the working condition that a torque of ± 2 N·m was applied on the top of the C2 vertebral body. Contacts between the cage screws and the endplate were modeled using surface-to-surface contact elements whose coefficient of friction was 0.5 [25]. There was a frictionless nonlinear contact relationship between the joints [26], and all other contacts were set as bonded.

Results

Model validation

The intact cervical C2–T1 model established in this study was compared with previously published in vitro experimental results to evaluate its effectiveness. As shown in Fig. 2, all intact segmental ROM values (flexion–extension, lateral bending, axial rotation) fall within those ranges and previously reported FE studies [27–31]. For transparency, Supplementary Table S1 lists, for each segment and motion, the literature corridor (min–max), our FE ROM, and the percent deviation from the corridor midline; deviations range from approximately − 45.5% to + 25.0% while remaining within the experimental corridors. Minor level-specific differences are attributable to variations in follower-load magnitude and facet-friction assumptions across studies and do not affect the study’s conclusions.

Range of motion

ROMs are illustrated in Fig. 3 and listed in Table 2. At the adjacent level C3/4, ROMs for ACDF, ACDF + 30% UPR, and ACDF + 50% UPR measured 5.8°, 5.9°, and 6.2° during flexion and 6.1°, 6.2°, and 6.4° during extension, with lateral bending and axial rotation showing similar small stepwise increases (Table 2). At the fused level C4/5, ROMs remained low but rose with the extent of resection: 1.7°, 1.9°, and 2.2° during flexion, and 1.5°, 1.6°, and 1.8° during extension; comparable patterns were observed at C5/6 across loading modes (Table 2). At the adjacent level C6/7, right rotation measured 4.3°, 4.5°, and 4.6° for ACDF, 30% UPR, and 50% UPR, respectively, with other load cases showing similarly modest increases (Table 2). Overall, ROMs were smallest with ACDF, increased slightly with 30% UPR, and were consistently higher with 50% UPR, with the separation more evident at the fused levels.

Von mises stress

Comparison of superior endplate stress (MPa)

Table 3; Figs. 4 and 5 summarize superior endplate stresses at C5 and C6 for ACDF, ACDF + 30% UPR, and ACDF + 50% UPR across flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation.

At C5, stresses increased with the extent of resection. Relative to ACDF, the 30% UPR model showed increases of 6.6% (extension), 9.1% (flexion), 27.0% (left bending), 9.4% (right bending), 1.2% (left rotation), and 0.4% (right rotation). In the 50% UPR model, the corresponding increases were 20.8% (extension; 38.73 → 46.79 MPa), 31.2% (flexion), 41.5% (left bending), 22.1% (right bending), 2.4% (left rotation), and 2.5% (right rotation).

At C6, the pattern was similar. With 30% UPR, stresses changed by 6.8% (extension), 11.0% (flexion), 21.3% (left bending), 16.2% (right bending), − 0.4% (left rotation), and + 0.9% (right rotation). With 50% UPR, changes were 15.5% (extension), 13.4% (flexion), 49.0% (left bending), 20.3% (right bending), 0.2% (left rotation), and 3.1% (right rotation).

Overall, superior endplate stresses at the operated segments rose progressively from ACDF to 30% UPR to 50% UPR, with the largest elevations during lateral bending (both levels) and notable increases during flexion at C5. Full numerical values are provided in Table 3; spatial distributions are shown in Figs. 4 and 5.

Comparison of intradiscal pressure (MPa)

Table 7 summarizes intradiscal pressure (IDP) at the cranial (C3/4) and caudal (C6/7) adjacent segments under flexion, extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation after two-level ACDF. In the ACDF model, IDP at C3/4 was highest during extension (5.83 MPa), followed by right rotation (4.67 MPa) and left rotation (4.45 MPa); C6/7 showed lower values overall, peaking during right rotation (3.76 MPa).

Relative to ACDF, the 30% UPR model showed modest changes at C3/4, ranging from − 1.4% (extension) to + 6.3% (right rotation). At C6/7, IDP decreased in flexion (− 12.4%) and extension (− 12.7%), with small increases in bending and rotation (e.g., right bending + 10.3%).

With 50% UPR, C3/4 exhibited larger increases, notably + 2.4% (extension), + 25.5% (flexion), + 22.9% (left bending), and + 5.1% (right rotation). At C6/7, IDP increased under all motions, with the largest elevations in extension (+ 90.3%) and right bending (+ 21.2%).

Across load cases, IDP generally followed the pattern ACDF < 30% UPR < 50% UPR at C3/4, while at C6/7 the largest relative change occurred in extension for 50% UPR. Full numerical values are provided in Table 7.

Discussions

In this study, we constructed a three-dimensional finite-element model of consecutive two-level ACDF (C4/5–C5/6) to evaluate the biomechanical performance of a zero-profile (ZP) construct under varying degrees of unilateral UPR. The major finding is that limited UPR (≈ 30%) produced only modest changes in segmental ROM and implant/endplate stresses, consistent with acceptable construct stability, whereas extensive UPR (≈ 50%) generated materially greater motions and stresses, indicating reduced stability. Beyond this principal result, ROM increases with UPR were most pronounced at the operated levels and during flexion and lateral bending, while adjacent-segment changes were comparatively small; cage and screw stresses and superior endplate stresses increased with resection extent, consistent with load redistribution from the uncinate process to the implant–bone interface; and adjacent-segment intradiscal pressure showed a modest upward trend with extensive UPR. Taken together, these patterns suggest that ZP fixation may remain acceptable when only limited UPR is required, whereas anticipated extensive UPR should prompt consideration of supplemental fixation or alternative constructs.

The uncinate process is a distinctive structure of the cervical spine that contributes to segmental stability and guides coupled motion [2, 32, 33]. Whether it should be routinely resected during anterior cervical surgery remains debated. Some authors discourage routine removal of uncovertebral osteophytes—e.g., Schelfaut et al. [34] found intervention for hypertrophy unnecessary, and Bayley et al. [35] reported possible postoperative resorption—while others have argued that adequate foraminal decompression may be achieved through intervertebral distraction alone if stability is preserved [36]. However, distraction height lacks consensus: excessive distraction may disturb lordosis or provoke root traction and axial symptoms, whereas insufficient distraction risks residual compression or recurrence [37]. Residual osteophytes may also re-compress the root if cage subsidence occurs. Consequently, some researchers advocate complete removal of compressive structures, including uncovertebral osteophytes, to ensure thorough decompression and early symptom relief [38]. Prior work indicates that the uncinate process contributes to coupled motion, segmental stability, and neurovascular protection within the foramen; thus resection may compromise stability [16, 39]. Our current findings are consistent with this view.

Building on this framework, we then examined how increasing UPR altered the mechanical environment of the fused and adjacent segments. Compared to the intact model, the 30% UPR group exhibited only a slight increase in C4/5 ROM (maximum 0.2°), no notable change at C5/6, and less than 5% change at adjacent segments (C3/4 and C6/7), indicating that segmental stability was generally preserved.

Moreover, in the 30% UPR model, endplate stress increased by less than 10%, without obvious stress concentration. Both cage and screw stresses showed only minor elevations, cancellous bone stress at C5 remained evenly distributed, and IDP at adjacent segments remained stable. These findings suggest that the ZP fixation maintains favorable biomechanical behavior within this range of UPR and may effectively limit abnormal stress transmission. Accordingly, ZP fixation appears biomechanically acceptable in bi-level ACDF when UPR is limited.

Indeed, retrospective studies [16] have shown that the ZP fixation can achieve favorable clinical outcomes and acceptable safety in single-level ACDF with concomitant UPR. Nevertheless, several reports associate more extensive UPR with a higher risk of cage subsidence, for which plate–cage constructs have been recommended to enhance postoperative stability [40]. This trend was also observed in the study by Abudouaini et al. [16], although the intergroup difference did not reach statistical significance. In fact, cage subsidence may result from multiple factors, including cage design, insertion position, distraction force, endplate preparation, and postoperative micromotion [41–45]. Among these, the stability reduction caused by UPR may exacerbate micromotion and thereby contribute to subsidence. Some previous studies [10, 17] reported that when UPR exceeded 38%, the risk of subsidence after ACDF significantly increased.

At present, no consensus exists regarding the safe threshold of UPR. Guo et al. [9] based on finite element analysis, suggested that resecting less than 40% of the uncinate process unilaterally or less than 30% bilaterally exerts minimal influence on cervical stability. Others have indicated that resection below 25% has negligible biomechanical impact [7]. Synthesizing our current FEA results with prior FE thresholds (~ 25–40% unilateral resection), although the ZP fixation may offer inferior stability compared to traditional plate constructs in multilevel fusion, our results support the acceptability of ZP fixation when UPR is kept around ~ 30%, while caution is warranted as resection approaches one-half of the uncinate.

Overall ROM increased with the extent of UPR under flexion and lateral bending, indicating a decline in segmental stability. In the 50% UPR model, the flexion ROM at the C4/5 segment increased from 1.7° to 2.2° (an increase of 29.4%), while lateral bending ROM rose from 1.8° to 2.1° (16.7%). For adjacent segments, the flexion ROM at C3/4 increased from 5.8° to 6.2° (6.9%), and right axial rotation ROM at C6/7 increased from 4.3° to 4.6° (7.0%). This trend is consistent with previous cadaveric and finite element studies. The uncinate process plays a vital biomechanical role in limiting cervical flexion and lateral motion, and that its anatomical integrity is essential for maintaining segmental stiffness [46]. Although preserving a certain degree of postoperative mobility is beneficial for functional recovery, exceeding physiological motion thresholds may compromise stability of the implanted construct.

Implant stresses also increased with higher UPR percentages. In the 50% UPR model, the peak stress on C4/5 screws rose from 438.6 MPa to 492.7 MPa (an increase of 12.3%), with a flexion-related increase of 47.1%. The cage stress during flexion increased from 72.2 MPa to 137.2 MPa, representing a 90.0% increase. The observed peak stress elevation in both the screws and the cage suggests that the load previously borne by the uncinate process may be redistributed to the implant after resection, imposing greater mechanical demands on the ZP fixation [47]. Excessive stress concentration is a known contributing factor to implant-related complications such as screw loosening, screw pull-out, and cage subsidence [40].

Endplate stress also showed an upward trend. The superior endplate of C5 experienced an increase from 39.10 MPa to 55.36 MPa (41.5%) during left lateral bending, and the superior endplate of C6 increased from 31.64 MPa to 38.06 MPa (20.3%) during right lateral bending, suggesting a reduction in compressive resistance of the supporting bony structures. This may further increase the risk of endplate collapse and fusion failure [5, 44]. As the extent of UPR expanded, IDP at C3/4 and C6/7 increased correspondingly, indicating a greater load burden shifted to adjacent segments, which could raise the risk of distal degeneration. These findings imply that each stabilizing structure removed during anterior cervical decompression may lead to a cascade of kinematic and mechanical changes.

Maximum displacement is another key index of postoperative stability, reflecting the relative stability of ACDF under different amounts of uncinate resection. In the 50% UPR model, maximum displacement increased from 12.0 mm to 18.0 mm during flexion (a 50.0% increase), and from 4.1 mm to 6.1 mm during right axial rotation (a 48.8% increase). These changes intuitively demonstrate a reduction in construct stiffness and overall mechanical stability. UPR extended to 50% substantially alters the biomechanical condition of both fused and adjacent segments, increases mechanical load on the internal fixation system, and weakens the overall stability of the fusion construct. Given the low-profile design of a ZP fixation, it may be insufficient to withstand such elevated mechanical demands. Therefore, its indication in such cases should be carefully considered in clinical settings.

This deterministic finite-element analysis has several limitations. First, although the three-dimensional model was benchmarked against published in-vitro ROM corridors, it represents an idealized, single-specimen cervical anatomy with homogeneous material properties; inter-patient variability in morphology, bone-mineral density, and endplate integrity was not modeled. Second, only quasi-static pure-moment load cases were applied without active musculature or follower load; repetitive/fatigue loading, impact, and time-dependent biological healing/remodeling were not simulated. Third, immediate, perfect fusion was assumed with fully bonded interfaces, so micromotion and frictional slip at the cage–endplate and screw–bone interfaces were not captured. Fourth, implant positioning and surgical variability (e.g., cage footprint and endplate preparation, construct offset, screw trajectory/angle) were not parametrically explored. Consequently, absolute magnitudes should be interpreted as comparative surrogates rather than predictors of fatigue life or long-term clinical outcomes; no inferential statistics were performed. Because our aim was to delineate an acceptability window for ZP under UPR, we did not include a plate–cage comparator; a matched FE analysis with identical UPR levels and loading protocols, together with targeted bench/cadaveric testing and prospective follow-up, is planned to corroborate these model-based patterns.

Conclusions

Using a validated three-dimensional finite element model of consecutive two-level ACDF (C4/5–C5/6), we found that limited unilateral UPR (~ 30%) yields only modest increases in segmental ROM and implant/endplate stresses, supporting the acceptability of stand-alone zero-profile (ZP) fixation in this context. In contrast, extensive UPR (~ 50%) produced materially larger motion and stress responses consistent with reduced construct stability. Clinically, ZP may be reasonable when UPR is restricted (~ 30%); however, for planned resections exceeding ~ 30–40% (or when bilateral UPR is contemplated), surgeons should consider constructs with greater mechanical reserves—including anterior plate-augmented ACDF spanning C4–C6 with appropriately sized/angled screws, posterior supplemental fixation (e.g., lateral mass screw–rod instrumentation at the fused levels), or alternative anterior systems that provide enhanced load-sharing relative to stand-alone ZP. Augmentation is further favored in patients with osteopenia/osteoporosis, multilevel kyphosis, high BMI, or heavy smoking. Independently of construct choice, maximize endplate coverage with a large-footprint cage and avoid over-distraction to mitigate endplate stress. These inferences are model-based and do not account for biological healing, inter-patient variability, muscle activation beyond boundary conditions, or long-term fatigue; targeted bench testing and prospective clinical follow-up are warranted to confirm these thresholds and recommendations.
