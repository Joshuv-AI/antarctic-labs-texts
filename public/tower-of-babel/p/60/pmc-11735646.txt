Thromboelastography in dogs with idiopathic epilepsy treated with phenobarbital monotherapy
Authors: R García, J Pastor, C de la Fuente, S Añor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThromboelastography (TEG) is an effective technique to assess the efficiency of coagulation. Phenobarbital (PB) can induce hematological and coagulation disorders in both animals and humans, but its effects on hemostasis have been little investigated and are poorly understood in dogs.ObjectivesThe aim of this article was to assess coagulation using TEG in a population of dogs with idiopathic epilepsy treated with PB.Material and MethodsProspective observational study. TEG was performed in blood samples from dogs with idiopathic epilepsy that were divided in three groups: Two groups of treated dogs that were on phenobarbital treatment for less or more than 6 months, and a control group of healthy dogs. Duration of treatment, dose, phenobarbital serum concentration and selected hematological and biochemical parameters were evaluated and correlated with the TEG results.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were found between groups. None of the animals appeared to be in a hypo‐ or hypercoagulable state, however 9/19 (47,4%) dogs were classified as hyper‐fibrinolytic. A statistically significant negative relationship between MA and G values and increased fibrinolytic activity (LY30) were found. No statistically significant relationship was found between PB dose or PB blood levels and TEG parameters in either group. No bleeding complications were observed.DiscussionThe rise in fibrinolysis might be due to hepatic damage from PB, as indicated by elevated liver enzymes in many dogs with abnormal fibrinolytic patterns. Although TEG showed hyperfibrinolysis in some dogs, the presence of primary or secondary hyperfibrinolysis could not be confirmed due to the lack of D‐dimer measurements and liver biopsy. TEG’s sensitivity compared to other fibrinolysis biomarkers like PAP might also affect results.ConclusionsTThe cause of hyperfibrinolysis in epileptic dogs treated with phenobarbital remains unclear, with potential links to hepatic effects or handling, and further research is needed to assess its clinical significance.Keywords: antiepileptic drug, coagulation, dog, fibrinolysis, thromboelastographic hemostasis analyzer

INTRODUCTION

Phenobarbital (PB) is an antiepileptic drug (AED) widely used in veterinary medicine as a first‐choice treatment due to its high efficacy in controlling seizure activity compared with other anticonvulsants, availability of oral and injectable formulations, and affordable cost. Dose‐dependent side effects include polyuria/polydipsia, polyphagia, sedation, and ataxia.1, 2 Some idiosyncratic adverse effects include hematologic abnormalities such as anemia, thrombocytopenia, and neutropenia.3, 4 Treatment with PB has also been associated with liver damage in dogs. At normal doses, elevated alanine ALT and alkaline phosphatase (ALKP) activities have been observed in a high percentage of patients, but it is not yet clear whether this is due solely to enzyme induction or whether it represents subclinical hepatic injury. Hepatic cirrhosis can appear at high doses sustained over time.5, 6 Conversely, there are idiosyncratic reactions that do not depend on phenobarbital concentration or duration that can result in acute liver failure. 7 All hemostatic factors (except for von Willebrand factor) are synthesized in the liver as well as most of the regulatory proteins of the coagulation and fibrinolytic systems. Alterations in coagulation in patients with hepatic disease are due to thrombocytopenia and platelet functional abnormalities, abnormal fibrinogen synthesis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), hyperfibrinolysis, and defects in the production of vitamin K‐dependent factors. 8

Thromboelastography tests the efficiency of coagulation in blood by assessing its viscoelastic properties and providing global information about a patient's hemostasis. It overcomes certain limitations of traditional hemostasis testing based on monitoring independent and static parameters (such as platelet number, activated partial thromboplastin time [aPTT], PT, thrombin time, fibrinogen levels, and fibrinogen degradation products) and provides a better understanding of the hemostatic process as a whole, including clot strength, fibrin build‐up, fibrinolysis, and platelet function. 9 Thromboelastography has been validated in veterinary medicine and has been used to assess hemostasis in hepatic disease, ischemic stroke, and other conditions.10, 11, 12, 13, 14

Hemorrhagic disorders and coagulopathies associated with PB treatment have also been reported in animals and humans, although to a lesser degree. In cats, rats, and rabbits, prolonged exposure to high doses of PB has been observed to induce liver structural alterations and influence the coagulation cascade, particularly affecting vitamin K‐dependent factors and coagulation times (PT and aPTT), although the exact mechanisms are not fully understood. However, the effects of PB short‐ and long‐term treatment on the coagulation system of dogs are unknown.15, 16, 17

The aim of this article was to assess coagulation using TEG in a population of dogs with idiopathic epilepsy treated with PB. We hypothesized that dogs with higher PB blood concentrations and longer periods of treatment might have hypocoagulative TEG tracings compared with healthy dogs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

In this prospective observational study, we included dogs diagnosed with idiopathic epilepsy with a tier 2 confidence level according to the International Veterinary Epilepsy Task Force and treated with PB monotherapy. The database of the Neurology and Neurosurgery Service of our hospital was reviewed to identify potential subjects. Dogs newly diagnosed with idiopathic epilepsy were enrolled in the study between March 2020 and December 2021. Dogs were excluded from the study if they were on antiepileptic drug polytherapy if they were receiving any other treatment besides PB, if there was any suspicion of liver or platelet disease before the onset of treatment (ultrasound findings, bile acid testing, elevation of serum liver enzymes, automated platelet count or blood smear review), or if the serum samples were not handled and processed properly. Written consent was obtained from owners of all dogs enrolled in the study.

To assess the effects of PB in the short and long term, we divided the epileptic dogs into two different groups: dogs receiving PB for less than (Group A) and more than 6 months (Group B). Additionally, a group of healthy dogs with normal bloodwork, without any medical treatment or supplementation, and unremarkable neurologic examination was used as a control.

Written consent was obtained from owners of all dogs enrolled in the study.

Sampling

Blood samples from fasted dogs were collected at medical rechecks. Whole blood was obtained by venipuncture of the jugular vein with a 21G needle attached to a 5 mL syringe. Blood samples were collected into 1 each a citrated, lithium‐heparin, EDTA, and plastic serum tube. All except the serum tube were carefully inverted to ensure mixing; the serum tube was left to rest in a vertical position to allow clot formation. After the first blood draw, the patient was fed a commercial regular diet. Then, a second venipuncture in the contralateral jugular vein was performed after 2 h to collect an additional serum sample for postprandial bile acids determination.

The EDTA tube was used to perform a CBC count and a blood smear examination. The lithium‐heparin tube was centrifuged, and plasma was then separated for a biochemistry profile that included albumin, GGT, ALKP, ALT, bilirubin, and glucose. The serum tubes were centrifuged after the clot was formed. One milliliter of serum was frozen and sent to an external laboratory to determine the PB serum concentration, and 2 mL was separated into two different aliquots and labeled as pre‐ and postprandial for bile acids determination. The citrate tube was immediately taken to our in‐house hematology laboratory for TEG analysis.

TEG

Thromboelastography analyses were performed 30 min after blood sampling at our hematology laboratory by the same technician. Thromboelastography was performed according to the manufacturers' recommendations with recalcified citrated whole blood using a TEG 5000 (Haemonetics, Ferrer Farma Hospital, Barcelona, Spain). Briefly, the TEG 5000 was prewarmed at 37°C, and two levels of internal quality control materials (normal and abnormal) were assayed prior to each run. An electrical internal quality control (e‐test) was also performed. When the instrument was ready, 1 mL of citrated whole blood was placed in a silicate vial containing kaolin, buffered stabilizers, and a blend of phospholipids (Kaolin, Haemonetics, Ferrer Farma Hospital, Barcelona, Spain). Mixing was ensured by gentle inversion of the kaolin‐carrying vials five times. New pins and cups were placed in the TEG analyzer in accordance with the standard procedure recommended by the manufacturer. Then, 340 μL of kaolin‐activated citrated whole blood was added to the cup, followed by 20 L of calcium chloride (0.2 M) for a total volume of 360 L, and TEG times were recorded. Thromboelastography analysis was run for 120 min.

Clotting time (K), clot strength (G), reaction time (R), angle (A), maximum amplitude (MA), and LY30 (clot lysis at 30 min after maximum clot strength) were recorded. Normal values for interpretation were compared with previously published reference intervals. 10 Once the results were obtained, the patient was classified as normo‐ (if values were within reference interval), hyper‐ (G value >8.5 kd/s, MA > 64.1 mm and R < 1.5 min), or hypo‐coagulative (G value <3.2 kd/s, MA < 46 mm and R > 9 min) in accordance with previous studies.11, 12 The degree of fibrinolysis was also evaluated, and patients were classified as hyperfibrinolytic when LY30 was over 2.49% based on our laboratory reference range.

Statistical analysis

A Shapiro–Wilk test was used to test normality. All TEG parameters followed a normal distribution. ANOVA was used to study if there were differences between control and treated animals for selected variables.

One‐way ANOVA with Tuckey's multiple comparison test was used when more than two groups were compared. Pearson's linear correlations were used to study the linear correlation between different parameters. Graph Pad (version 8.0.1) statistical software was used for the statistical study. Statistically significant differences were identified when the p‐value was <0.05.

RESULTS

Study population

Twenty‐four dogs were included in the study. Dogs were divided into three different groups as follows.

Control group

Five dogs (1 Golden Retriever, 1 Bernese Mountain Dog, 1 German Shepherd, 1 Andalusian Hound, and 1 mixed breed) were included in the control group. There were 3 neutered males, 1 spayed female, and 1 intact female. Median age was 4 years (range: 1–11 years). Median weight was 25 kg (range: 8–38 kg).

Group A

Nine dogs were included in Group A (2 mixed‐breed dogs, 1 German Shepherd, 1 Labrador Retriever, 1 Golden Retriever, 1 Border Collie, 1 Shih‐Tzu, 1 American Staffordshire Terrier, and 1 Dogue de Bordeaux). Sex was evenly distributed with 4 spayed females, 3 neutered males, 1 male, and 1 female. Median age was 4 years (range: 2–7 years). Median weight was 29 kg (range: 8–52 kg). The median duration of treatment was 7 weeks (range: 3–9 weeks). Median dose of PB was 3.4 mg/kg every 12 hours (range: 2.5–6 mg/kg). Median serum PB concentration was 24.5 μg/mL (range: 17.2–37.5 μg/mL).

Group B

Ten dogs were included in Group B (2 American Staffordshire Terriers, 1 German Shepherd, 1 Dachshund, 1 mixed‐breed, 1 Saint Bernard, 1 Weimaraner, 1 Dobermann Pinscher, 1 Miniature Schnauzer, and 1 Poodle). There were 3 males, 4 neutered males, and 3 spayed females. Median age was 7 years (range: 4–10 years). Median weight was 26 kg (range: 5–75 kg). The median duration of treatment was 2.5 years (range 1–5 years). Median dose of PB was 3.75 mg/kg every 12 h (range: 3–5 mg/kg). Median serum PB concentration was 23.7 μg/mL (range: 15.1–29 μg/mL).

No statistically significant differences were found between age, breed, sex, or weight.

Hematology and biochemistry results

All dogs in the control group had hematologic and biochemical parameters within reference intervals (RIs). No significant alteration in hematocrit or total platelet count was detected, nor were significant differences in these variables found between any of the groups (Figure 1). One dog (11%) in Group A had an increased ALKP concentration (179 U/I; RI 20–156 U/L). Four dogs (40%) in Group B had increased ALT values (107 U/L, 131 U/L, 211 U/L, 110 U/L; RI 21–102 U/L), five dogs (50%) had elevated concentrations of ALKP (483.4 U/L, 2340 U/L, 1364 U/L, 1226 U/L, 422 U/L), and one dog (10%) had increased GGT concentrations (8.2 U/L; RI 1.2–6.4 U/L). The remainder of the biochemistry analytes, including bile acids, were normal for all dogs in the study. No statistically significant differences were found between groups in any of the measured variables. No statistically significant differences were found between Groups A and B for PB dose, PB serum concentration, and blood test results (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

The most common finding in our study population was an increase in fibrinolytic activity without being significantly related to platelet count, serum laboratory test results, PB treatment duration, PB dose, PB serum concentration, or clinical signs. Our data suggest that a high percentage of dogs with idiopathic epilepsy under PB treatment show hyperfibrinolytic tracings using kaolin‐activated TEG. None of the dogs in our study demonstrated bleeding complications.

One of the possible hypotheses to explain why dogs treated with PB developed increased fibrinolytic TEG changes is hepatic damage caused by PB, given the fact that all proteins involved in fibrinolysis are synthesized in the liver except for t‐PA and PAI‐1. Additionally, t‐PA clearance is mainly carried out in the liver, which is a cause of hyperfibrinolysis and secondary hemorrhage in human patients with cirrhosis who have impaired production of these inhibitors and decreased t‐PA clearance. 18 In veterinary medicine, two studies evaluating TEG in acute and chronic liver disease showed hyperfibrinolysis in 38% and 25% of dogs, respectively, which was correlated with greater disease severity.11, 12

Most dogs (80%) in Group B had increased serum hepatic enzyme activities. Of those, all dogs with hyperfibrinolytic TEG changes had increased ALT, ALKP, or GGT activities, which is in partial agreement with the findings of another study that showed increased ALT activities in dogs with hyperfibrinolysis on TEG compared with dogs without changes. 12 It is possible that these changes are not secondary to liver injury but solely due to PB drug metabolism, as demonstrated in previous studies. However, other studies showed greater evidence of hepatocellular damage on histology (swollen hepatocytes, fibrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, increased amounts of endoplasmic reticulum, etc.) in PB‐treated dogs compared with controls.5, 6, 7, 19

Table 2 shows increased fibrinolytic activities (LY30) in half of the dogs in Group A and 40% of the dogs in Group B. Although TEG kinetics (MA and G) were within the normal range, a significant negative relationship was found between these measurements and LY30 percentages. In human medicine, cases with low and normal or high MA values with prolonged LY30 values are seen with primary and secondary hyperfibrinolysis, respectively. Primary hyperfibrinolysis is common in cases of liver disease and neoplasia without increases in d‐dimer concentrations, whereas secondary hyperfibrinolysis is associated with conditions like disseminated intravascular coagulation or trauma, which are characterized by elevated d‐dimer concentrations. Since we did not measure D‐dimers or fibrin degradation products (FDPs) and a liver biopsy was not performed, primary or secondary hyperfibrinolysis could not be determined. 20

In human medicine, there are limitations to the use of TEG over other conventional plasmatic biomarkers of fibrinolysis activation, such as the plasmin‐alpha2‐antiplasmin complex (PAP). Certain studies reveal that the use of PAP as a marker of fibrinolysis is much more sensitive than TEG in trauma patients, and this is because TEG only detects increased states of fibrinolysis when t‐PA is five times higher than normal. 21 Therefore, hyperfibrinolysis could have been present in some of our patients even if they had normal TEG results.

One of the limitations of our study is that there was no “gold standard” to assess fibrinolysis, and even if TEG is one of the most sensitive techniques available, some studies report false negative results or hyperfibrinolysis in apparently healthy patients. Therefore, an intrinsic lack of sensitivity and specificity of TEG could have yielded equivocal results in our population. 22 Additionally, the reference values of certain TEG parameters were selected following previous publications and were not the exact values of our institution, although they were very similar.

The small sample size might have affected our statistical analysis. We did not measure FDPs, like D‐dimers, which could have provided more insights into the type of hyperfibrinolysis seen. D‐dimers are sensitive indicators of various diseases in veterinary and human medicine, but they lack specificity.23, 24 Clotting times (PT, aPTT) and fibrinogen levels could have been measured to evaluate the clinical relevance of TEG better. We plan to include these measurements in future studies. Finally, to enhance our understanding of fibrinolysis in patients treated with PB, we could have also measured PAP and t‐PA levels.

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, none of the dogs included in our study were hypocoagulative on TEG. All dogs were treated with the recommended PB doses, and none had toxic blood concentrations. Even though there were no statistical differences in values between groups, hyperfibrinolysis was seen in nearly half of the dogs in our study population diagnosed with idiopathic epilepsy and undergoing PB monotherapy, with no apparent correlation to dose or blood concentration. The cause of this hyperfibrinolytic tracing remains unknown, although hepatic origin or the possible effect of stress and sample handling on the results cannot be ruled out completely. 25 Likewise, whether phenobarbital has a direct effect on any of the proteins that activate or inhibit fibrinolysis has not been studied. Our results show that epileptic dogs receiving PB monotherapy have evidence of hyperfibrinolysis on kaolin‐activated TEG. Further studies are needed to determine the clinical relevance of this finding.
