Climate resilience mechanisms of vernacular dwellings in China’s Yangtze River delta region: a study based on a multi-level analytical framework
Authors: Tianyi Min, Tong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTaking vernacular dwellings in China’s Yangtze River Delta, which is located in the “hot-summer and cold-winter” climate zone, as the subject, this study systematically explores their coupled adaptation and climate resilience in a complex climatic scenario involving summer heat, winter cold, high humidity, abundant rainfall, and frequent calm winds. Based on the bioclimatic theory, it combines physical measurement with computational simulation to quantitatively validate and extract parameters related to the climate regulation performance of typical vernacular dwellings in the region. Subsequently, the study constructs a multi-level analytical framework for climate resilience, which involves five progressive dimensions (i.e., climatological situation, configuring form, gradient space, regulatory interface, and performance-oriented construction). At last, it proposes a concept of “Thermal Fabric” to represent the dynamic, holistic, and breathable spatial structure of vernacular dwellings in the Yangtze River Delta, and refines this concept as the Climate-space Configuring Model inherent in these traditional dwellings. In summary, the study not only reveals the underlying mechanism through which these dwellings coordinate various architectural elements to respond to contradictory climatic demands but also translates traditional wisdom from empirical cognition into regional knowledge for contemporary building design. It provides a referable paradigm and practical basis for green buildings in hot-summer and cold-winter climate zones to improve performance and reduce energy consumption, as well as for human settlements to achieve sustainability.Keywords: Sustainability, Vernacular dwellings, Climate adaptation, Environment regulation, Resilience mechanism, Yangtze River deltaSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Throughout history, every kind of vernacular dwelling, since the primitive nest and cave dwellings, symbolizes a unique regional climate, which together underpin the profound diversity of architectural forms1. Vernacular dwellings manifest as an organic ecosystem that maintains a dynamic balance with the local climate, representing the physical carrier of human lifestyles, conventions, and customs2,3. In essence, they exemplify the construction wisdom of people adapting to specific climatic and natural conditions over a long stretch. Coupled with unique climate-responsive strategies, the morphology of vernacular dwellings embodies an idea of “architecture-environment synergism”, which has been the focus of sustainability-centered architectural theories and discussions since the last century4. Within the rich and diverse spectrum of Chinese vernacular dwellings, those in the Yangtze River Delta, resonating with the unique network of waterways, the hot-summer and cold-winter climate, and the profound Jiangnan culture5, constitute a distinctive architectural landscape that represents a full set of climate resilience mechanisms and green construction strategies. They showcase the exceptional wisdom of traditional Chinese architecture in mediating the relationship among humans, buildings, and nature6, and offer an important perspective for contemplating and addressing the global ecocrisis7.

Drawing on the concept of “deep structure” from linguistics, Kenneth Frampton emphasized that climate has a decisive and fundamental influence on culture, and cultural characteristics, in turn, are expressed through architecture as a unique regional architectural language8. The regional climate adaptability of vernacular dwellings has become a research focus in interdisciplinary fields such as architecture, environmental science, sustainable development, and architectural heritage9–12. Earlier, Victor Olgyay proposed the “Bioclimatic Approach to Architectural Regionalism”, laying a foundation for the correlation analysis between architectural forms and climatic elements13. He posited that an ideal building should be using natural forces to achieve a “Climate Balanced” state to provide comfort while minimizing environmental intervention13. This pioneering theory of “environmental architecture” sparked numerous correlation studies, such as the Building Bioclimatic Design Chart14 and the Comfort Triangles Graph15. The manner in which the Olgyays established connections between building design and the science of climate also laid the foundation for much of the building simulation software in use today16.

In recent years, with the deepening of discussions on sustainable development and climate resilience, the research paradigm has shifted from qualitative description to quantitative analysis, and the perspective has moved from static summarization of building experience to dynamic exploration of climate adaptation mechanisms11,17. Compared with climate adaptation, climate resilience places greater emphasis on the capacity to buffer, adapt, and recover from significant climatic disturbances or seasonal variations18,19. Researchers have increasingly begun to investigate vernacular dwellings for their holistic climate-responsive capacity20, treating them as complex systems closely associated with climatic and energy flows21. Since then, the research focus has expanded to include the quantitative efficacy of vernacular dwellings’ passive regulatory strategies and their application potential in contemporary low-energy design, with environmental monitoring and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) used for scientific validation22,23. At the same time, global research exhibits distinct regional and empirical characteristics, as vernacular dwellings across climate zones have diverse typologies24–27. For instance, an empirical study investigated the earth-sheltered buildings in southern Italy, which has a climate characterized by dry, hot summers and mild, humid winters, and demonstrated their good thermal performance28. A year-round field survey of vernacular dwellings in three climate zones (i.e., cold-humid, hot-humid, and cold) in northeastern India revealed that these dwellings adopt envelopes with different thermal properties to respond to different climatic conditions29. Research on traditional dwellings in Malaysia’s hot-humid climate confirms that efficient cross-ventilation is key to achieving adaptive thermal comfort30. Based on field measurements and simulations, a study found that vernacular dwellings in dry-hot Kashgar, Xinjiang, use shading, greening, optimized envelopes, and solar energy as climate adaptation strategies31. The bioclimatic model was employed to assess the wind and thermal regulation patterns of vernacular dwellings in Jiangnan (south of the Yangtze River) 32. Studies on the thermal behavior of courtyards and sky-wells have demonstrated their critical role as thermal buffer spaces in microclimate regulation33. Meanwhile, the development of adaptive thermal comfort theory has provided important insights into the dynamic relationships among occupants, behavior, and the environment in vernacular dwellings34. Furthermore, the multi-method validation approach combining field measurements with simulations has gained increasing attention in vernacular architecture research35, laying a foundation for improving the reliability and generalizability of research findings. However, existing studies have largely focused on the performance validation of individual elements or isolated techniques. Therefore, how to systematically reveal the inherent logic of climate adaptation in vernacular dwellings under the synergistic effects of multi-scale and multi-level architectural elements, and to conduct integrated and correlational research on these elements, remains a direction worthy of further exploration. Building on existing research, this study aims to further explore the underlying logic of how vernacular dwellings employ a series of architectural elements across multiple scales and levels in a synergistic manner to achieve regional climate adaptation, and to conduct an integrated and correlational investigation into these elements.

Taking vernacular dwellings in the Yangtze River Delta, which is located in China’s “hot-summer and cold-winter” climate zone, as the subject, this study analyzes their coupled adaptation to the region’s complex climatic landscape of summer heat, winter cold, high humidity, abundant rainfall, and frequent calm wind, in an attempt to understand the fundamental mechanism. Based on the “Bioclimatic Approach to Architectural Regionalism”, it evaluates the climate of the Yangtze River Delta and identifies key climatic demands. Methodologically, the study employs physical measurement and computational simulation (e.g., Climate Consultant, Starccm + , and Phonics) to comprehensively interpret the climate adaptation mechanisms and green construction strategies of typical dwellings in the region from five progressive dimensions (i.e., climatological situation, configuring form, gradient space, regulatory interface, and performance-oriented construction). The study encompasses the quantitative validation of the dwellings’ microclimate, the spatial parameters and synergetic mechanisms of various architectural elements, and the empirical wisdom and local cultural expression embedded in architectural practices. It seeks to construct a clear, quantifiable, and applicable framework to explore the climate resilience mechanism of vernacular dwellings, thereby contributing to the sustainable development of human settlements. Also, it expects to transform the traditional empirical wisdom into an instructional knowledge system for modern building design (Fig. 1).

The uniqueness and contribution of this framework lie in: (1) deconstructing architectural elements into five progressively inward layers within the climate regulation process and synthesizing them into an organic “Thermal Fabric” system, thereby providing, methodologically, an analytical paradigm transferable to vernacular architecture studies in other climate zones; (2) introducing quantitative indicators such as the configuring-form coefficient (Cf) and the interface opening coefficient (Io) to achieve a transition from qualitative description to statistical inference; and, through multi-validation based on field measurements and simulations, systematically quantifying the climate regulation performance and parametric thresholds of key elements—sky-wells, cavity spaces, interfaces—in Yangtze River Delta vernacular dwellings, while extracting transferable design parameters.

Study area

Data source

Located in eastern China, the Yangtze River Delta encompasses parts of Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Anhui Provinces, as well as the entire Shanghai Municipality. Historically referred to as “Jiangnan”, this region has long served as the economic, social, and cultural center and possesses an abundant architectural heritage36. Since the Tang and Song Dynasties, the economic gravity has shifted southward, and Jiangnan has rapidly developed into a financial and cultural hub. By the Ming and Qing Dynasties, a considerable cluster of small towns and prefectures had emerged37. The dense hydrological structure (water surface ratio: 20.3%) and the unique human-land relationship due to high population density and limited land resources have shaped a poetic settlement landscape typified by “small bridges, flowing water, and houses”, as well as a composite “dual-chessboard” traffic system interweaving waterways and streets38. While the topographic texture constitutes the material foundation of the vernacular dwellings, it facilitates a structural synergy among architecture, water, and land in this densely-populated region.

Climate

The Yangtze River Delta is subject to China’s hot-summer and cold-winter climate zone39. The average maximum temperature in July is 33.2 °C, with extreme highs exceeding 42 °C; the average minimum temperature in January is − 1 °C, with extreme lows of − 10 °C40. Southeasterly winds prevail in spring and summer, while northwesterly and northeasterly winds account for over 50% in autumn and winter. Precipitation occurs during nearly 40% of the year, with an average annual rainfall of 1181 mm. Almost 60% of the rainfall occurs between May and September, and more importantly, there is a Meiyu (plum rain) season that lasts for approximately 40 days from June to July, which is characterized by continuous rainfall and predominant calm winds40. Additionally, the average annual relative humidity is as high as 80%. In summary, hot-humid summer and cold-humid winter are two typical climatic features in the Yangtze River Delta and represent the primary climatic challenges for vernacular dwellings in this region41.

Vernacular dwellings

Vernacular dwellings in the Yangtze River Delta (Fig. 2) are exemplary within the Chinese dwelling spectrum for regional climate adaptability. In response to the crisscrossing network of waterways, they adopt a highly compact spatial form centered around the sky-well and a layout that enables the buildings to gracefully align with the rivers and lanes42, forming a recognizable Jiangnan cultural landscape. Meanwhile, these dwellings exploit various configuring forms, gradient spaces, regulatory interfaces, and performance-oriented constructions to adaptively cope with the challenging climate characterized by summer heat, winter cold, high humidity, abundant rainfall, and frequent calm winds, using multi-dimensional elements to neutralize contradictory climatic demands (e.g., shading versus solar gain, ventilation versus wind protection, and heat dissipation versus thermal insulation). They represent an exceptional paradigm for climate regulation.

Methods

Field survey

This study selects 8 representative subregions within the Yangtze River Delta, including Nanjing, Suzhou, Wuxi, Changzhou, Shanghai, Hangzhou, Huzhou, and Huangshan. Between 2014 and 2025, the research team conducted field surveys on 705 vernacular dwellings across 35 historic towns, with architectural surveys and physical measurements performed on 158 of the 705 samples. The selection of measured samples was based on covering the eight representative subregions, with inclusion criteria requiring structural integrity, clearly defined spatial layouts, intact key spatial elements such as sky-wells and eaves corridors, and homeowner cooperation for field measurements. Dwellings that had undergone extensive modern renovation or improper restoration were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 158 dwellings. Statistical analysis shows that each subregion includes no fewer than 12 samples, meeting the basic requirements for cross-group comparison. During the measurement phase, each sample was monitored continuously for over 72 h in both summer and winter seasons, yielding over 150,000 valid data points. The cumulative mean curves of key parameters stabilized after 120 samples, indicating that the sample size supports region-scale generalizable conclusions.

Environmental measurement

The measurement instruments, their accuracy, and the sampling intervals used in this study are presented in Table 2. The deployment of the instruments followed a stratified principle. Measurement points in sky-wells and crab-eye sky-wells were located at the center and arranged at three heights: 1.5 m, 3.5 m, and 6 m above ground level. Measurement points in cooling alleys, sheltered lanes, and eaves spaces were set at a height of 1.5 m. Indoor measurement points were placed at the center of main halls, bedrooms, and window openings, at a height of 1.0 m. Each dwelling was equipped with no fewer than six synchronized indoor and outdoor measurement points. Data were recorded at 10 min intervals for a continuous period of at least 72 h. Real-time weather conditions (temperature, humidity, wind speed, cloud cover) were documented during the measurement period, and periods with extreme weather events were avoided. Quality control was conducted through instrument cross-calibration and outlier removal; missing data were filled using linear interpolation to ensure a data completeness rate of ≥ 95%. The above measurement protocol guarantees the systematic and comparable nature of the microclimate data, providing a reliable empirical foundation for subsequent analyses.

Bioclimatic evaluations

Quantitative simulation

This study employs climate analysis software (i.e., Climate Consultant 6.0 https://www.sbse.org/resources/climate-consultant, Solar Tools 3.2 https://www.sunearthtools.com/solar/index.php) and wind-thermal environment simulation software (i.e., Star-CCM + 2302 https://www.siemens.com/en-us/products/simcenter/fluids-thermal-simulation/star-ccm/, PHOENICS 2024 https://www.cham.co.uk/phoenics.php) to conduct environmental simulations and quantitative analyses of typical vernacular dwellings in the Yangtze River Delta under varying radiation, temperature, humidity, and wind speed conditions in summer and winter. Climate Consultant and Solar Tool are used for regional bioclimatic assessment, calculating the potential increase in comfort hours through passive strategies based on typical meteorological year data, while also providing boundary condition references for subsequent CFD simulations. Star-CCM + and PHOENICS are used for detailed thermal and wind environment simulations of typical dwellings, and cross-validation is performed between the two to ensure consistency.

Simulation setup. The computational domain is established based on field-surveyed dwelling geometries. Three-dimensional models are constructed for each dwelling type (Belt Form, Sanjian-Liangxiang Form, Duihe Form) and imported into the simulation environment. The models include key architectural features such as sky-wells, eaves corridors, cooling alleys, and full-height windows. The computational domain extends 5–8 times the building dimension in each direction to ensure full flow development.

Calibration and validation. To establish a clear traceable link between measured data and simulation inputs, the geometry, material properties, and boundary conditions (measured wind speed, wind direction, temperature, and humidity) of each measured dwelling were directly used as input parameters for the corresponding simulation model. The validation covered all three spatial types; for each type, 15–20 prototype dwellings were randomly selected from the measured sample, and their measured data (air temperature, surface temperature, wind speed) were compared point-by-point with the corresponding simulation outputs. Validation metrics included the coefficient of determination (R2) from linear regression, root mean square error (RMSE), and mean bias error (MBE). The results show that for temperature, R2 = 0.91, RMSE = 0.8 °C, and MBE = + 0.1 °C (simulated values slightly higher than measured—in practical terms, this is a very small bias that is almost negligible for thermal comfort assessment). For wind speed, R2 = 0.84, RMSE = 0.12 m/s, and MBE = − 0.02 m/s (meaning that the model predicts wind speed with an error smaller than the resolution of most handheld anemometers; hence the simulated airflow is reliable for evaluating natural ventilation). Taking the Sanjian-Liangxiang Form as an example, the RMSE between simulated and measured indoor temperatures is 0.7 °C in summer and 0.9 °C in winter. These metrics indicate that the simulation model adequately captures the actual physical performance of the measured dwellings, and the validation samples cover the main dwelling types, enhancing the generalizability of the conclusions.

Statistical assumptions and uncertainty. All 158 measured samples were taken from different villages and households, ensuring spatial independence and satisfying the independence assumption for regression analysis. In the linear regression analyses, we tested the normality of residuals (Shapiro–Wilk test, p > 0.05) and independence (Durbin–Watson statistic close to 2), and reported 95% confidence intervals along with standard errors for key regression relationships. For example, the regression coefficient between Cf (the configuring-form coefficient) and the summer temperature difference is − 3.2 (95% CI: − 4.1 to − 2.3, SE = 0.45). Sensitivity analysis indicates that when key parameters vary within a range of ± 10– ± 15%, the resulting changes in simulation outcomes remain within ± 7%, demonstrating the robustness of the conclusions.

Sensitivity analysis indicates that when key parameters vary within a range of ± 10– ± 15%, the resulting changes in simulation outcomes remain within ± 7%, demonstrating the robustness of the conclusions.

The above setup ensures clear logic among the simulation tools, traceable parameters, and verifiable results, providing strong support for the quantitative conclusions regarding ventilation efficiency, temperature gradients, and effective morphological parameters presented in this paper.

Analytical framework

This study proposes a five-dimensional framework to systematically analyze the climate resilience mechanisms of vernacular dwellings in the Yangtze River Delta. The dimensions follow a sequential progression from outdoors to indoors, while allowing for feedback interactions: the climatological situation (D1) shapes the configuring form (D2), which in turn determines the configuration of climate gradient spaces (D3) and the performance of regulatory interfaces (D4); finally, the interfaces achieve refined regulation through performance-oriented construction details (D5).

D1: Climatological situation. Refers to the regional climatic context that fundamentally constrains the layout and design of dwellings. Empirical indicators include typical meteorological year data (temperature, humidity, solar radiation, wind speed, and direction) and bioclimatic charts. From these, local climatic demands and the significant increase in comfortable hours achievable through passive climate regulation strategies are derived, and the optimal Sol–Air orientation range that balances summer and winter climate requirements in the region is determined.

Selection mechanism of climatological situation

Regional bioclimatic evaluations

The Yangtze River Delta belongs to the monsoon climate in northern subtropical Asia47. According to the psychrometric chart (Fig. 3), the annual relative comfort time ratio for a natural year is only 8.8% (32 days), and passive climate regulation strategies can supplement this ratio48. Among them, passive solar heating has the best performance and achieves a 44.1% increase in the annual comfort time ratio. In hot-humid summer (from June to September), enhancing natural ventilation can boost the comfort time ratio to over 80%. Evaporative cooling and exposed mass and night-purge ventilation together can provide another 14.8% of comfort time in summer. Overall, passive climate regulation strategies are expected to supplement 261 comfort days, equivalent to a 71.5% increase in the annual relative comfort time ratio (Table 3).

Selection of thermal and wind orientations

Victor Olgyay’s theory of “Sol–Air Orientation”49 suggests that the building thermal environment is a result of both solar radiation and air temperature. Based on the annual total solar radiation and the Sol–Air index in the Yangtze River Delta (Figs. 4 and 5), the thermal azimuth is approximately 12.5° east of south. During the hot-humid summer that lasts almost four months from June to September, vernacular dwellings in this region should make the most of the monsoon for ventilation. By superimposing the dominant wind direction during this period onto the thermal azimuth (Fig. 6), the calculation indicates that the optimal Sol–Air orientation that can balance the conflicting seasonal climatic demands is 12.5° to 16.1° east of south. Field survey statistics show that the average orientation of vernacular dwellings in this region is about 13.73° east of south. (Table 4), which aligns with the theoretical data. Generally, these dwellings strike a perfect balance between heat protection and solar gain via reasonable Sol–Air Orientation selection, not only making themselves a system that efficiently captures and uses natural wind, light, and thermal energy but also integrating perfectly with the Jiangnan village landscape to form a holistic ventilation network.

Buffer mechanism of configuring-form

In the Yangtze River Delta, there are three typical spatial types of vernacular dwellings (i.e., the Belt Form, the Sanjian-Liangxiang Form, and the Duihe Form), all of which exhibit excellent performance in ventilation and dehumidification in response to summer heat and winter cold (Fig. 7). In this regard, sky-wells serve as the negative space and the key component of the traditional Chinese dwelling paradigm centered on climatic energy. While these dwellings are externally enclosed with quaint walls, they use sky-wells to capture both wind in summer and sunlight in winter to balance seasonal climatic demands.

Morphological parameters

To balance summer shading and winter heating, this study comprehensively analyzes the morphological parameters of sky-wells50. According to statistics, the length-to-width ratio (L/W) of sky-wells in the region mainly falls into three categories: < 1, 1–2, and 2–3, while the height-to-width ratio (H/W) is concentrated in three ranges: 1–1.5, 1.5–2, and > 2. Based on these categorized statistics, sunshading diagrams were plotted for Major Heat and Major Cold (Fig. 10). The winter analysis reveals that solar heating efficiency is more significantly influenced by the H/W ratio: when H/W ranges from 1 to 1.5, its impact on ground-floor heating is minimal, whereas exceeding 1.5 leads to a sharp reduction in effective heating duration. The summer analysis indicates that the shading range of the sky-well is inversely proportional to L/W and positively proportional to H/W. Infrared thermography measurements in Fig. 11 show that the maximum temperature difference between shaded and sun-exposed areas on the sky-well walls reaches 16 °C, with a vertical temperature difference of 11.2 °C, demonstrating significant self-shading and temperature damping effects of sky-well morphology.

Based on the above analysis, combined with the statistical distribution of sky-well morphological parameters in the region (width: 6–13 m; depth: 3–7 m; height: 1–2 storeys), the study concludes that under the climatic conditions of the Yangtze River Delta, a sky-well with a L/W ratio of approximately 2 and a H/W ratio of approximately 1–1.5 can achieve a good performance balance among summer ventilation, solar shading and winter solar gain. It should be noted that this parameter range reflects the typical characteristics of sky-well morphology in this region; in practical applications, appropriate adjustments can be made according to specific orientation, surrounding environment, and functional requirements. For example, based on measured data, in the relatively hotter southern part of the Yangtze River Delta, the H/W ratio of the sky-well can be appropriately increased to 1.5–1.8 to enhance summer self-shading; for single-courtyard dwellings, a lower H/W ratio (1.0–1.3) is recommended, while for larger courtyard houses or the rear part of multi-courtyard dwellings, it can be moderately raised to 1.2–1.6.

Transition mechanism of gradient-space

Cavity spaces

Cavity spaces are primarily responsible for ventilation and heat dissipation. While outdoor wind is too calm to induce wind-driven ventilation, vertical cavities (e.g., crab-eye sky-wells) and horizontal cavities (e.g., cooling alleys and sheltered lanes) together construct a three-dimensional ventilation system via the thermal buoyancy effect 51, which invokes both daytime self-shading and nighttime cooling storage in walls and ground. In hot-humid summer, cavity ventilation lowers indoor temperatures and significantly promotes dehumidification during the Meiyu season39.

Taking Huihetang, a typical vernacular dwelling in the Yangtze River Delta, as an example, infrared thermometry on Major Heat demonstrates excellent self-shading and radiation protection of cavity spaces (Fig. 13). The slim crab-eye sky-wells utilize the temperature difference between the upper and lower openings to induce stack-driven ventilation, while the cooling alleys and sheltered lanes follow the dwelling’s north–south temperature gradient to facilitate air circulation. The crab-eye sky-well beside a sheltered lane (Fig. 13b) has a width of only 0.9 m yet provides a shading rate of 80%, exhibiting a significant gradient effect. The crab-eye sky-well behind the main hall (Fig. 13c) provides a shading rate of 65–75% during the hottest hours, and its vertical temperature difference (6–11 °C) can induce an upward airflow even in calm winds. At night, as the temperature difference decreases, the three-dimensional ventilation system formed by vertical and horizontal cavities benefits from the gradual release of thermal energy stored in walls and the ground. This not only drives nighttime thermal buoyancy ventilation within the cavities but also enables the heat-storing masses to function as auxiliary cooling sources during the day, thereby continuously regulating the microclimate.

Buffer layer spaces

For buffer layer spaces under eaves, their height and depth affect winter solar gain and summer shading, respectively. According to the Principles of Architecture (Yingzao Fayuan), the eaves height is often determined as 80% of the main bay’s width. Juzhe and Qiangjiao techniques can further elevate the eaves height52, and measurements show that a 10% increase is possible on Winter Solstice.

The eaves depth depends on the morphological parameters of interface sunshading components (e.g., overhanging eaves, Quesu eaves, and overhanging floor) and spatial sunshading components (e.g., eaves corridor and straddling corridor). Thermal envelope interface

Thermal envelope interfaces (e.g., external walls and roof) affect the indoor thermal environment via light-to-heat conversion, and the heat transfer depends on the material’s thermal conductivity. In this case, the representative white-lime walls and grey-tile roofs have low solar radiation absorptivity (0.48 and 0.52, respectively) and high thermal inertia (5.11 and 3.4, respectively). This indicates that thermal envelope interfaces are primarily responsible for summer thermal insulation and winter thermal preservation.

Therefore, thermal regulatory interfaces facing the courtyard generally adopt a “maximally permeable” principle, contrary to that of thermal envelope interfaces. Common implementations include installing full-height windows across the three south-facing bays, or placing a full-height window in the central bay with half-height windows or removable windows in the two flanking bays.

Despite large opening coefficients facilitating ventilation, they could bring about overheating in summer and high heat loss in winter. To achieve a balance, summer ventilation and heat dissipation must be coupled with shading and heat prevention to form a coordinated control mechanism, which involves determining the appropriate window opening range based on the effective shading coverage. For instance, interface sunshading components with a short overhang and an H/W ratio of ≥ 3.5, such as overhanging eaves and Quesu eaves (Fig. 17a), provide shading for the upper 44% of the regulatory interface area. Accordingly, installing half-height windows on the regulatory interface can block over 90% of direct radiation. Besides, spatial sunshading components with a H/W ratio of ≤ 2.4, such as eaves corridor and straddling corridor (Fig. 17b), allow of all-day shading and pre-cooling. Thus, full-height windows are ideal as they provide better ventilation without significantly increasing indoor heat load.

Winter solar gain is defined by the solar heat-receiving area index (As/At), which represents the interface’s capacity to receive and utilize solar radiation heat. Statistics show that the As/At ratio of vernacular dwellings in the Yangtze River Delta is between 64 and 81%. Roughly, 75% of the climatic interface area receives solar radiation. Thus, sky-wells with appropriate morphological parameters not only increase the heat-receiving area on the south, east, and west sides but also utilize strong solar radiation to compensate for heat loss in winter, which demonstrates the wisdom of passive solar energy utilization across seasons14.

Adjustment mechanism of performance-oriented construction

Static adjustment

When closed, the opening components (windows) should fit the thermal envelope interfaces (external walls) smoothly to form an integral barrier. Meanwhile, different opening components should be chosen for specific orientations. For instance, inward-opening wooden window sashes are suitable for the south wall as they, when closed, can block summer solar radiation. Window sashes on the east, west, and north walls often have grey square bricks riveted to the exterior of their wooden panels. Coupled with sunshading lattices, they effectively resist direct radiation in summer and contribute to thermal preservation in winter.

Bafeng (stack-driven ventilation) and Toufeng (wind-driven ventilation) are the most common strategies for thermal convection in the Yangtze River Delta. The Bafeng system is deployed at the gable walls on both sides of the roof (Fig. 18) to exhaust heat and humidity accumulated in the top thermal cell, enhance insulation, and prevent condensation. The Toufeng system is at the junction of walls and floors (Fig. 19). Indoors, raised wooden floors create an air layer of 25–30 cm. Outdoors, ventilation holes (diameter: 10 cm) are set along the external walls at intervals of about 2 m to guide airflow and mitigate ground humidity.

Full-height windows on the thermal regulatory interface are adjustable by changing the ratio of the upper lattice section to the lower solid panel section (Table 6), and this ratio is associated with the morphology of the sky-well. When the sky-well has a H/W ratio of ≤ 1, the “60% lattice and 40% panel” configuration is often appropriate, as it blocks heat reflected from the ground in summer and cold winds near the ground in winter. When the sky-well has a H/W ratio of 1–1.5 and has good performance of thermal protection, “80% lattice and 20% panel” is preferred for better ventilation. When the H/W ratio is ≥ 1.5, “100% lattice” full-height windows or removable sill wall windows are recommended to maximize ventilation and dehumidification.

Flexible adjustment

The indoor-outdoor exchange of climatic elements can be flexibly regulated by changing the interface opening mode. For instance, the dual-window design uses an outer grille window and an inner flat-sash window to satisfy summer shading and winter solar gain, respectively, and the removable window has a middle removable panel, which can be propped up in summer to serve as a shading platform. More importantly, full-height windows, accounting for over 85% of the regulatory interface area, play the roles of wall, door, and window, conforming to seasonal changes via the flexible change of opening modes (i.e., fully open, half-open, and fully closed), continuously regulating the climate.

In summer and transition seasons, full-height windows, when fully open, provide vertical shading and guide airflow. On the windward side, opening full-height windows can create a “wing wall” effect, which is expected to increase the indoor average wind speed by approximately 0.45 m/s (Fig. 20), particularly for single-sided ventilation. In winter, north full-height windows can be replaced with solid plank doors to enhance airtightness; this also helps create an air layer behind the central screen wall to improve insulation, thereby inhibiting heat convection. When half-open, full-height windows satisfy both daytime solar gain and wind protection; when fully closed, they effectively block damp-cold air during nighttime or extreme weather, achieving a temperature compensation of 7–8 °C (Fig. 21). Additionally, heating methods such as braziers work properly with these regulatory structures to guarantee indoor thermal comfort in winter.

Discussion

The thermal fabric with regional climate resilience

Based on the comprehensive analysis above, climatic conditions are the dominant factor shaping the spatial layout and architectural organization of vernacular dwellings in the Yangtze River Delta. Grounded in measured and simulated data, this study systematically reveals the climate-responsive logic of these dwellings through five progressive and synergistic dimensions: the selection mechanism of the climatological situation, the buffering mechanism of the configuring form, the transitional mechanism of the climate gradient, the exchange mechanism of the regulatory interface, and the regulating mechanism of the performance-oriented construction. These five dimensions are not abstract categories but directly correspond to quantifiable empirical indicators, collectively constituting an organic “Thermal Fabric” (Fig. 22), which manifests as a breathable, multi-level coupled spatial structure that promotes cross-ventilation and moisture exchange under the hot-humid summer and cold-humid winter climate.

The breathable Thermal Fabric from the general village layout to individual buildings.

Specifically, this thermal fabric exhibits differentiated synergistic mechanisms across seasons. In summer, self-shading of the sky-well (wall surface temperature difference up to 16 °C) and stack-driven ventilation in cavity spaces (vertical temperature difference of 6–11 °C in crab-eye sky-wells) dominate heat dissipation, while buffer layer spaces such as eaves corridors provide pre-cooling buffering (shading rate of 70–100%). The regulatory interface achieves coupled wind-thermal regulation through half-open and fully open full-height windows, supplemented by Bafeng and Toufeng constructions for ventilation and dehumidification. In winter, the appropriate height-to-width ratio of the sky-well (1–1.5) ensures ground-floor solar gain; buffer layer spaces create a temperature damping effect (temperature difference between the damping zone and the interior is only 1.5 °C); the regulatory interface reduces heat loss by closing or half-opening full-height windows, while maximizing solar radiation capture through the solar heat-receiving area index (As/At, 64–81%). The above empirical data directly support the conceptualization of the thermal fabric as a dynamic regulation system.

In essence, from the perspective of a synergistic “climate-architecture-human” relationship, this thermal fabric represents the Climate-space Configuring Model developed and refined through the long-term evolution of local vernacular dwellings, embodying regional climatic rationality. The core of this model lies in transforming climate adaptation from a singular, passive resistance into an active regulation system centered on the storage, release, and transfer of energy. It fully maps the flow, transformation, and utilization processes of natural energies such as wind, light, and heat both inside and outside the building, ultimately achieving the translation of bioclimatic needs into architectural form through the formal consolidation and orderly expression of architectural space.

Contemporary value of traditional construction wisdom

The value of the climate resilience wisdom inherent in vernacular dwellings in the Yangtze River Delta far exceeds the value of themselves as historical heritage53. From the conscious utilization of local climatic conditions and appropriate construction techniques to the accomplishment of a human settlement that maintains a harmonious balance with nature, along with the underlying climate regulation philosophies and strategies, they provide an important knowledge foundation and practical reference for contemporary architecture in addressing regional climatic challenges and promoting sustainable development54.

The core value of this construction wisdom lies in its methodological approach to resolving climatic contradictions systematically. Faced with opposing climatic demands across seasons, such as shading versus solar gain, ventilation versus wind protection, and heat dissipation versus thermal preservation, vernacular dwellings in this region achieve time-sequential guidance and precise regulation of climatic elements through the synergy of various spaces, interfaces, and constructions. They provide important evidence for achieving high thermal comfort with limited resources. In terms of spatial organization, an outward-inward gradient spatial sequence of “sky-well–cavity–buffer layer” can be adopted to form a climatic buffer zone from outdoors to indoors. In terms of interface regulation, appropriate opening coefficients can be established to enable time-sharing switching between the thermal envelope interface and the thermal regulatory interface for summer and winter performance. In terms of construction design, adjustable full-height windows, Bafeng and Toufeng systems can be utilized to achieve dynamic responses in wind and thermal environments.

Research limitations and future directions

This study focuses on typical types of vernacular dwellings in the Yangtze River Delta and pays little attention to intra-regional climatic variations and dwelling variants55. Future research could further: (1) expand the sampling range for comparative research, and carry out comparative analyses across climatic zones and cultures, and explore whether the thermal fabric concept is applicable in other climate zones; (2) conduct empirical research on applying traditional construction wisdom to modern green buildings, exploring its potential and pathways for reducing carbon emissions and enhancing the climatic resilience of buildings throughout their lifecycle.

Conclusion

Based on the regional bioclimatic evaluation and local climatic demands, this study measures and quantitatively simulates various climatic factors such as solar radiation, temperature, and wind speed, so as to systematically analyze the resilience mechanism and construction wisdom inherent in vernacular dwellings in the Yangtze River Delta facing summer heat, winter cold, high humidity, abundant rainfall, and frequent calm winds.

Firstly, the study establishes a five-dimensional analytical framework for climate resilience. It reveals how local people, through a multi-level configuring strategy, endow passive shelters with spontaneity to respond to the regional climate, making them a dynamic, breathable Thermal Fabric.

Secondly, as shown in Table 7, the study quantitatively analyzes the climate regulation performance of key spatial elements in these dwellings and reveals their recommended morphological parameters. For sky-wells, the optimal L/W ratio is 2, and the optimal H/W ratio is 1–1.5. For cavity and buffer layer spaces, the optimal H/W ratio is ≤ 2.4. For thermal envelope and thermal regulatory interfaces, which are responsible for balancing indoor-outdoor climatic elements and improving thermal comfort, the optimal opening coefficients are 0.02–0.06 and 0.5–0.65, respectively. Also, it determines the optimal opening modes for static and flexible constructions in different scenarios. Overall, various spaces, interfaces, and constructions constitute a natural energy-driven time-sequential regulation paradigm for contradictory climatic demands.

The terms in the article

Sky-well: A small-scale courtyard commonly found in traditional dwellings in southern China, serving functions such as lighting, ventilation, and rainwater collection.

Crab-eye sky-well: A distinctive type of sky-well in vernacular dwellings of the Yangtze River Delta. It is named for its resemblance to the two eyes of a crab when viewed from above, due to its small spatial scale (typically 1.2–1.8 m in depth) and its usual symmetrical arrangement. In practice, however, it may also appear as a single unit or in an elongated form.

Bafeng: In vernacular dwellings of the Yangtze River Delta, a suspended ceiling (Xuan) creates an enclosed air layer between the ceiling and the roof. An opening of approximately 20 cm, referred to as “Bafeng,” is often installed on the gable walls on both sides of this air layer to facilitate ventilation, thereby extracting hot air and moisture from the interlayer.

Toufeng: Located at the base of walls. In vernacular dwellings of the Yangtze River Delta, the wooden floors of ground-floor bedrooms are typically raised 25–30 cm above the hall floor or external ground level, forming an air layer. Ventilation openings of approximately 10 cm in diameter are created every 2 m or so in the baseboards facing the hall and in the wall bases facing the exterior corridor to promote airflow and mitigate moisture.
