Magnetic resonance imaging characteristics of ependymal tuberculosis in patients with intracranial tuberculosis
Authors: Le Zhang, Xu-wen Fu, Jia-lu Wei, Min Qi, Wei Gan, Ying Pu, Ling-jun Shen, Xiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to characterize the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features of ependymal tuberculosis in patients with intracranial tuberculosis.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 21 patients diagnosed with ependymal tuberculosis, defined as direct ventricular involvement caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, between July 2019 and May 2024. Craniocerebral MRI findings were reviewed, and the imaging characteristics and their temporal progression were systematically evaluated.ResultsTwo distinct MRI patterns of ependymal tuberculosis were identified: nodular and mixed types. The nodular type was characterized by intraventricular nodular lesions demonstrating isointensity on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), iso- to hypointensity on T2-weighted imaging, mild hyperintensity on T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery imaging, and absence of diffusion restriction on diffusion-weighted imaging. On contrast-enhanced T1WI, the nodules demonstrated either homogeneous or ring enhancement. The mixed type was characterized by intraventricular nodules associated with linear thickening of the ventricular wall. Both the nodules and the thickened ependymal lining demonstrated homogeneous or ring enhancement on contrast-enhanced T1WI. Leptomeningeal tuberculosis was identified as linear or nodular meningeal thickening involving the basal cisterns, Sylvian fissures, or cerebral convexities. In patients with concomitant parenchymal tuberculosis, nodular or ring-enhancing lesions were observed within the cerebral, cerebellar, or brainstem parenchyma and were occasionally associated with surrounding edema. Following anti-tuberculosis therapy, progression of ependymal tuberculosis was observed in 13 of 21 patients, whereas improvement was observed in 8 patients. Disease progression was characterized by enlargement of pre-existing intraventricular nodules, which in some cases resulted in localized hydrocephalus. Improvement was characterized by reduction in nodule size.ConclusionMRI findings of ependymal tuberculosis include homogeneously or ring-enhancing intraventricular nodules, with or without associated linear ependymal thickening, and possible development of hydrocephalus. Despite anti-tuberculosis therapy, a substantial proportion of patients may exhibit paradoxical worsening characterized by enlargement of pre-existing lesions.Clinical trialNot applicable.

Introduction

In 2024, an estimated 10.7 million new cases of tuberculosis and approximately 1.23 million tuberculosis-related deaths were reported worldwide [1]. Intracranial tuberculosis represents a severe manifestation of extrapulmonary tuberculosis, accounting for approximately 1% of all tuberculosis cases and carrying a mortality rate ranging from 15% to 60% [2, 3]. Among survivors, more than 50% experience long-term neurological sequelae of varying severity [2, 3]. In developing countries, intracranial tuberculosis has been reported to account for up to 50% of tuberculosis-related mortality [4]. In 2015, the Chinese Medical Association published the Expert Consensus on Imaging Classification of Intracranial Tuberculosis, which standardized the imaging classification and diagnostic criteria for intracranial tuberculosis [5]. Intracranial tuberculosis is classified into meningeal tuberculosis, cerebral parenchymal tuberculosis, and mixed-type intracranial tuberculosis. Meningeal tuberculosis may involve the dura mater, pia mater, basal cistern meninges, and ependymal lining. However, ependymal tuberculosis has rarely been reported, and its incidence remains unclear [6]. Furthermore, descriptions of its MRI features remain limited, particularly regarding imaging changes following anti-tuberculosis treatment for ependymal. Therefore, a retrospective analysis of 21 patients diagnosed with ependymal tuberculosis was performed, with detailed evaluation of imaging findings and post-treatment imaging changes performed to characterize the MRI features of ependymal tuberculosis.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This single-center retrospective study included 21 patients diagnosed with ependymal tuberculosis between July 2019 and May 2024.

The inclusion criterion was as follows: patients diagnosed with intracranial tuberculosis based on pathological examination, etiological analysis, and clinical improvement following anti-tuberculosis treatment (a total of 852 patients were diagnosed with intracranial tuberculosis during the study period).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with incomplete imaging or clinical data (46 cases excluded); patients without ependymal lesions (784 patients excluded); and patients with other ependymal lesions (1 patient diagnosed with xanthogranuloma of the choroid plexus).

Finally, 21 patients were included in the study. The cohort comprised 8 male patients (38.1%) and 13 female patients (61.9%), aged 16 to 67 years, with a median age of 28 years (interquartile range, 21–41 years). Pulmonary tuberculosis was present in 18 patients (85.7%), including 15 cases of acute or subacute hematogenous disseminated pulmonary tuberculosis and 3 cases of secondary pulmonary tuberculosis. Additionally, 8 patients (38.1%) had concomitant extrapulmonary tuberculosis, including 3 cases of spinal tuberculosis, 2 cases of spinal cord tuberculosis, 1 case of cervical lymph node tuberculosis, 1 case of peritoneal tuberculosis, and 1 case of testicular tuberculosis. Positive cerebrospinal fluid culture or GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay results for Mycobacterium tuberculosis were identified in 7 patients (33.3%). The cohort also included 1 patient with HIV co-infection, 1 patient with systemic lupus erythematosus, and 1 pregnant patient.

Laboratory methods

Medical records were reviewed to extract relevant whole blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) data. Whole blood and CSF samples were obtained from the initial examination performed within 48 h of hospital admission. Hematological analyses were performed using the Sysmex XN400 analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Japan), with 3 to 5 mL of whole blood collected to assess peripheral blood white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count (NEU), neutrophil percentage (NEU%), lymphocyte count (LYM), lymphocyte percentage (LYM%), monocyte count (MON), and monocyte percentage (MON%). Biochemical analyses of CSF were performed using the AU5400 automated biochemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter, USA). CSF biochemical parameters included protein (PRO), chloride (CL), glucose (GLU), and cell count (CELL), assessed using 3 to 5 mL of CSF. CSF cultures for M. tuberculosis were performed using 1 to 3 mL of CSF, either cultured on Lowenstein–Jensen medium (Zhuhai Encode Medical Engineering-like Co., Ltd.) or processed using the BACTEC MGIT 960 liquid culture system (BD, Becton, Dickinson and Company). In addition, 1 mL of CSF was used for the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay (Cepheid, USA) to detect M. tuberculosis and evaluate rifampicin resistance.

Imaging examination and analysis

Craniocerebral MRI examinations were performed using a United Imaging 3T uMR 780 scanner (United Imaging, China) equipped with a 24-channel head and neck coil. Both non-contrast and contrast-enhanced craniocerebral MRI examinations were conducted. The non-contrast imaging protocol included axial T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences, all acquired with a slice thickness of 5 mm. T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) was performed using a 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm isotropic voxel acquisition in the sagittal plane, with axial and coronal reconstructions generated at a slice thickness of 1 mm. The contrast-enhanced imaging protocol included axial T2 FLAIR sequences with a slice thickness of 5 mm and T1WI sequences acquired using the same 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm isotropic voxel parameters, with axial and coronal reconstructions at a slice thickness of 1 mm. Gadoterate meglumine (Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd., China) was administered as the contrast agent at a dose of 0.2 mL/kg body weight. Contrast-enhanced T2 FLAIR imaging was initiated approximately 30–40 s after contrast administration and required approximately 2.5 min for acquisition. Contrast-enhanced T1WI was initiated approximately 5 min after contrast administration and required approximately 3.5 min for acquisition.

The MRI images were independently reviewed by two experienced radiologists, each holding intermediate or higher professional qualifications and extensive experience in tuberculosis imaging diagnosis. In cases of disagreement, a third experienced radiologist reviewed the images to establish a consensus interpretation.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were reported as cases (percentage, %), and comparisons between groups were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were initially assessed for normality. Non-normally distributed data were reported as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Location of ependymal tuberculosis

Ependymal tuberculosis lesions were identified in the lateral ventricles in 14 of 21 patients (66.7%), in the third ventricle in 4 of 21 patients (19.0%), and in the fourth ventricle in 8 of 21 patients (38.1%).

MRI imaging features of ependymal tuberculosis

Ependymal tuberculosis was categorized into two types based on MRI features: nodular and mixed. Comparisons of whole blood and CSF parameters between patients with two types are presented in Table 1. The baseline demographic, imaging, and clinical characteristics of the 21 patients are presented in Table 2.

Nodular type

This pattern was observed in 14 patients (66.7%) and presented as intraventricular nodular lesions. The lesions demonstrated isointense signals on T1WI, iso- to hypointense signals on T2WI, and mildly hyperintense signals on T2 FLAIR imaging. No diffusion restriction was observed on DWI. Contrast-enhanced T1WI demonstrated homogeneous enhancement in 12 patients and ring enhancement in 2 patients (Figs. 1 and 2).

Mixed type

This pattern was observed in 7 patients (33.3%) and was characterized by intraventricular nodular lesions with MRI features similar to those of the nodular type. On contrast-enhanced T1WI, the intraventricular nodules demonstrated either homogeneous or ring enhancement, whereas the thickened ventricular wall demonstrated marked enhancement. Among these patients, homogeneous enhancement was observed in 4 patients and ring enhancement in 3 patients (Fig. 3).

Other intracranial tuberculosis manifestations

All 21 patients (100%) demonstrated imaging findings consistent with leptomeningeal tuberculosis, presenting as linear or nodular meningeal thickening involving the basal cisterns, Sylvian fissures, or cerebral convexities. Additionally, 17 patients (81.0%) had concomitant cerebral parenchymal tuberculosis, manifested as nodular or ring-enhancing lesions within the cerebral parenchyma, cerebellar parenchyma, or brainstem, with or without surrounding edema. Hydrocephalus was observed in 6 patients (28.6%), and cerebral infarction was identified in 5 patients (23.8%).

MRI follow-up

Following anti-tuberculosis treatment, 13 of 21 patients (61.9%) demonstrated progression of ependymal tuberculosis, characterized by enlargement of pre-existing intraventricular nodules, with localized hydrocephalus developing in 3 patients (Fig. 4). In contrast, 8 of 21 patients (38.1%) demonstrated improvement, as evidenced by reduction in the size of pre-existing intraventricular nodules (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This retrospective study characterized the MRI features of ependymal tuberculosis in patients with intracranial tuberculosis. Through analysis of MRI findings in 21 patients with ependymal tuberculosis, two distinct imaging patterns were identified: nodular and mixed types. Ependymal tuberculosis was frequently associated with pulmonary tuberculosis, leptomeningeal tuberculosis, and cerebral parenchymal tuberculosis. Despite anti-tuberculosis treatment, many patients demonstrated paradoxical worsening characterized by enlargement of pre-existing lesions.

The ventricular system consists of the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles, all of which contain CSF. The lateral ventricles communicate with the third ventricle through the interventricular foramina, whereas the third ventricle communicates with the fourth ventricle through the cerebral aqueduct. The fourth ventricle communicates with the subarachnoid space through the median and lateral apertures. The ventricular system is lined by ependymal cells, including specialized ependymal cells that form the choroid plexus. Together with adjacent capillaries, the choroid plexus plays a critical role in CSF production [7]. CSF circulates through the ventricular system, cerebral cisterns, and subarachnoid space.

The mechanism by which M. tuberculosis enters the ventricular system and causes ependymal tuberculosis remains unclear. Some studies have proposed that M. tuberculosis may reach the ventricles through hematogenous dissemination via the choroid plexus [8, 9]. In the present study, 71.4% of patients had concomitant acute or subacute hematogenous disseminated pulmonary tuberculosis, indicating a possible association between ependymal tuberculosis and dissemination through the choroid plexus. The classical route of dissemination is hematogenous spread, in which tuberculous bacilli reach the central nervous system through the bloodstream and establish foci within the subpial and subependymal regions of the brain. Subsequent rupture of these foci may result in dissemination of bacilli into the subarachnoid space, leading to inflammatory involvement of the leptomeningeal compartment, brain parenchyma, and less commonly, the pachymeningeal compartment [10, 11].

Furthermore, recent studies investigating CSF hydrodynamics have demonstrated bidirectional flow between the ventricular system and the subarachnoid space [12]. These findings indicate that CSF may circulate from the ventricles to the subarachnoid space and in the reverse direction. Notably, all patients included in this study had concomitant leptomeningeal tuberculosis. This bidirectional circulation raises the possibility that M. tuberculosis may migrate from the subarachnoid space into the ventricular system, thereby contributing to the development of ependymal tuberculosis. Nevertheless, further studies are required to clarify the risk factors associated with ependymal tuberculosis.

Previous case series have described MRI findings of ependymal tuberculosis as ventricular wall tuberculomas with or without associated ependymal thickening and enhancement. These ventricular wall tuberculomas have been reported to demonstrate homogeneous, heterogeneous, or ring enhancement [6, 8, 13]. The findings of the present study are consistent with previous reports; however, isolated ependymal thickening and enhancement, which have been described previously, were not identified in the current cohort. This discrepancy may be attributable to diagnostic challenges encountered in clinical practice. In the present study, contrast-enhanced volumetric T1WI was more effective for detecting ependymal thickening than contrast-enhanced T2 FLAIR imaging, which may be affected by high signal intensity from periventricular white matter, potentially resulting in diagnostic misinterpretation. Furthermore, volumetric T1WI acquisition enables multiplanar reconstruction, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy.

Hydrocephalus is a common complication of intracranial tuberculosis and may be classified as obstructive or communicating hydrocephalus. Ventricular wall tuberculomas located along CSF circulation pathways may obstruct CSF flow [14]. In cases involving a single ventricle, localized hydrocephalus may occur secondary to ventricular adhesions. In the present study, hydrocephalus developed in 6 patients, including 4 patients in which ependymal tuberculosis within the fourth ventricle obstructed the median or lateral apertures and 2 patient in which lesions within the third ventricle obstructed the interventricular foramina. Further investigation is required to determine optimal management strategies and treatment timing for hydrocephalus associated with ependymal tuberculosis.

A paradoxical reaction during anti-tuberculosis treatment refers to the appearance of new lesions or enlargement of pre-existing lesions during therapy and is relatively common in central nervous system tuberculosis [15, 16]. In the present study, 61.9% of patients (13/21) demonstrated enlargement of pre-existing ependymal tuberculosis lesions on follow-up imaging after initiation of anti-tuberculosis therapy. Among these patients, 3 developed localized hydrocephalus within the lateral ventricles secondary to intraventricular adhesions, resulting in epileptic seizures. Previous studies have reported paradoxical reaction rates ranging from 10% to 25% in HIV-negative patients [16]. The higher rate observed in the present study underscores the importance of careful imaging follow-up and further investigation into management strategies for ependymal tuberculosis. Although enlargement of ventricular wall tuberculomas may gradually regress with continued treatment, the optimal management of localized hydrocephalus remains uncertain.

This study has several limitations. First, the incidence of ependymal tuberculosis is low, and the condition is prone to misdiagnosis, resulting in a limited sample size. Second, long-term follow-up data were unavailable. Furthermore, previous studies have not proposed an imaging classification system for ependymal tuberculosis. To the best of current knowledge, this study is the first to classify the imaging features of ependymal tuberculosis based on a limited case series. Further validation of this classification will require larger studies with greater numbers of cases.

Conclusion

The presence of enhancing ventricular wall nodules and linear ependymal thickening on contrast-enhanced T1W1 should raise suspicion for ependymal tuberculosis. Follow-up contrast-enhanced MRI should be performed within 2 to 3 months after initiation of anti-tuberculosis therapy to assess treatment response and potential paradoxical progression. However, this study was limited by its single-center retrospective design and small sample size; therefore, the findings require validation in larger multicenter prospective studies.
