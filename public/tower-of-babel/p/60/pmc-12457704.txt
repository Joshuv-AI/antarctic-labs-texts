A Functional Photorespiratory Cycle Is Essential for Light‐Dependent Stomata Opening in Epidermal Peels of Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Azhin Mortezazadeh, Michael Hodges, Mathieu Jossier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTraditionally viewed as a wasteful pathway, photorespiration plays essential roles in cellular metabolism and stress responses. While its role in leaf mesophyll cells is well characterized, its functioning in leaf epidermal guard cells and its involvement in stomatal opening remain poorly understood. Using data mining, all photorespiratory genes were expressed, and their respective proteins were present in Arabidopsis thaliana guard cells, thus indicating the presence of a complete photorespiratory cycle. The inhibition of glycolate oxidase (GOX) by hydroxy‐pyridyl‐methane‐sulfonic acid (HPMS) or the glycine decarboxylase complex (GDC) by amino‐aceto‐nitrile (AAN) reduced light‐induced stomatal opening in Arabidopsis thaliana leaf epidermal peels. The exogenous supply of serine, 3‐phosphoglycerate, or malate alleviated this inhibition. It is proposed that guard cell photorespiratory metabolism contributes to light‐induced stomatal opening by supporting metabolite fluxes crucial for the regulation of guard cell turgor. These findings highlight the importance of a functional guard cell photorespiratory cycle in stomatal dynamics that could play a role in plant responses to environmental stimuli.Keywords: Arabidopsis thaliana, epidermal peels, guard cells, photorespiration, stomata

Introduction

Photorespiration is a fundamental primary metabolic pathway that begins with the oxygenase activity of ribulose‐1,5‐bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco), resulting in the production of one molecule of 2‐phosphoglycolate (2‐PG) and one molecule of 3‐phosphoglycerate (3‐PGA) (Bauwe 2023). However, 2‐PG has been shown to inhibit several key enzymes: triose‐phosphate isomerase and sedoheptulose 1,7‐bisphosphate phosphatase, enzymes of the Calvin–Benson–Bassham (CBB) cycle (Anderson 1971; Flügel et al. 2017), and phosphofructokinase, a glycolytic enzyme (Kelly and Latzko 1976). Consequently, the photorespiratory cycle plays a crucial role in eliminating “toxic” 2‐PG and recycling its carbon to “useful” 3‐PGA. The photorespiratory cycle involves five subcellular compartments: the cytosol, chloroplasts, peroxisomes, mitochondria, and the vacuole, and requires eight core enzymes as well as several transporters. It can convert two molecules of 2‐PG into a molecule of 3‐PGA, consuming one molecule of NADH and ATP while producing one molecule each of CO2, NH3, NADH, and ADP (Bauwe 2023; Timm et al. 2024) (see Figure 1). The photorespiratory cycle interacts with various metabolic pathways and processes, including photosynthesis in chloroplasts, respiration and C1 metabolism in mitochondria, cell redox homeostasis in peroxisomes, and amino acid metabolism (Hodges et al. 2016). Photorespiration also impacts pathogen defense (Sørhagen et al. 2013; Rojas et al. 2012; Rojas and Mysore 2012), salt stress resistance (Liu et al. 2019; Ziotti et al. 2019), and thermotolerance (Timm et al. 2019; Cavanagh et al. 2022; Fu et al. 2024).

Since the 1960s, the impact of photorespiratory metabolism on light‐induced stomatal opening has been investigated. Stomata are made up of two guard cells forming a stomatal pore in the leaf epidermis, thereby allowing gas exchange between the plant and the outside environment. Adjustments in stomatal aperture respond to both environmental conditions and internal signals and control the CO2 entering the leaf and water loss through transpiration. The regulation of gas fluxes is crucial for controlling mesophyll CO2 demand, maintaining optimal leaf temperature, and preserving plant water balance (Lawson and Vialet‐chabrand 2019). The first studies linking photorespiratory metabolism and stomatal opening focused on pharmacological approaches using specific inhibitors of two photorespiratory enzymes: glycolate oxidase (GOX) and the glycine decarboxylase complex (GDC) (Figure 1) (Zelitch 1957; Usuda et al. 1980). GOX is a peroxisomal enzyme catalyzing the conversion of glycolate to glyoxylate and producing hydrogen peroxide (Dellero et al. 2016). Inhibition of GOX by hydroxy‐pyridyl‐methane‐sulfonic acid (HPMS) (Zelitch 1957) in tobacco leaf disks (Zelitch 1961; Zelitch and Walker 1964), excised tobacco and Xanthium pennsylvanicum leaves (Meidner and Mansfield 1966), and tomato leaves (Khavari‐Nejad 1977) led to stomata closure in the light. This was associated with glycolate accumulation (Zelitch and Walker 1964) and a decreased 14C‐labeling of downstream photorespiratory metabolites such as glycine and serine when fed with 14CO2 (Hess and Tolbert 1966; Servaites and Ogren 1977). On the other hand, HPMS‐treated tomato leaves did not inhibit stomatal opening in O2‐free air when photorespiration is negligible (Khavari‐Nejad 1977). GDC is a four‐protein complex made up of three enzymes (P‐protein, T‐protein, and L‐protein) and a small lipoylated protein (H‐protein) and is located in mitochondria. It decarboxylates and deaminates glycine, leading to the production of CO2, NH3, NADH, and 5,10‐methylene‐THF (Bauwe and Kolukisaoglu 2003; Douce et al. 2001). Amino‐aceto‐nitrile (AAN) is structurally related to glycine, and it is a specific inhibitor of GDC, inhibiting GDC activity in isolated wheat leaf mitochondria (Usuda et al. 1980). It has been shown to decrease transpiration in detached rice leaves. However, AAN did not affect leaf transpiration when photorespiration is low, as in maize leaves (a C4‐plant) and rice leaves under 2% O2 (Cho et al. 1987).

After several decades of neglect, recent studies using Arabidopsis transgenic lines have reinforced the idea that photorespiratory cycle functioning impacts stomatal behavior (see the opinion of Hodges in Eckardt et al. 2024). When transferred to air from high CO2 conditions required for mutant growth, leaf transpiration was lower in several Arabidopsis photorespiratory mutants (pglp1, shmt1, and glyk1) (Eisenhut et al. 2017), the pglp1 mutant (Flügel et al. 2017) and rice containing lower amounts of GOX (Lu et al. 2014) both had a reduced gs when compared to wild‐type plants. In agreement with the pharmacological approach, such observations strongly suggested that an inhibited photorespiratory metabolism reduces stomatal opening. On the other hand, Arabidopsis overexpressing PGLP1 (Timm et al. 2019), rice overexpressing GOX (Cui et al. 2016), and tobacco overexpressing the GDC‐H subunit (López‐Calcagno et al. 2019) showed higher gs, while rice overexpressing SHMT1 had higher transpiration rates (Wu et al. 2015). Leaf gs was also modified in plants expressing an artificial chloroplastic photorespiratory bypass; Arabidopsis had a lower gs (Maier et al. 2012), while cucumber (Chen et al. 2019) and rice (Nayak et al. 2022) had higher gs values when compared to wild‐type plants.

Until very recently, the impact of photorespiratory metabolism on stomatal opening had been studied using leaf disks, detached or attached leaves, and its role in guard cells had not been addressed until the report of an altered leaf gs in Arabidopsis with a guard cell‐specific modulation of GDC‐H subunit expression (Sun et al. 2025).

Here, we highlight the presence of a complete photorespiratory cycle in Arabidopsis guard cells from literature‐available transcriptomics (Yang et al. 2008) and proteomics (Wang et al. 2023) data. Then, to investigate whether guard cell photorespiratory metabolism alone can impact stomatal opening in the light, a pharmacological approach was revisited using Arabidopsis rosette leaf epidermal peels to study the effect of HPMS and AAN on light‐induced stomatal opening in the absence of mesophyll cells. Finally, to better understand the role of guard cell photorespiratory metabolism on light‐dependent stomatal opening, selected metabolites were tested in the presence of the inhibitors.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material and Growth Conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Columbia (Col‐0) seeds were germinated on potting soil for 10 days in short days (8/16 h light/dark, 200 μmol of photons m−2 s−1, 65% humidity, 20/18°C day/night). Subsequently, seedlings were transplanted to individual pots, and plants were grown in short‐day conditions until they were 4 weeks old.

Purification of Recombinant AtGOX2 Protein and GOX Activity Measurements

Photorespiratory AtGOX2 protein was purified from broken, transformed E. coli cells by affinity chromatography as described in Dellero et al. (2015). GOX activity measurements were carried out by spectrophotometry at 440 nm using an enzyme‐coupled reaction by following the oxidation of o‐dianisidine as described in Jossier et al. (2020) with different HPMS (from 0.05 to 10 μM) and glycolate (from 0.1 to 5 mM) concentrations. AtGOX2 kinetic parameters were calculated from three independent AtGOX2 purifications using the Michaelis–Menton equation of Sigma‐Plot “Kinetic Enzyme Wizard” (Systat Software).

Stomatal Aperture Assays

Protocols were adapted from Merlot et al. (2007) with modifications. Leaves of 4‐week‐old plants were harvested in darkness at the end of the night period, and the abaxial side of the leaves was fixed onto microscope coverslips using medical adhesive (Hollister Medical Adhesif 7730, NEWPHARMA). To ensure stomatal closure, leaves were incubated in the dark for 1 h in a calcium‐containing solution (10 mM CaCl2 and 10 mM MES, pH 6). After incubation, the epidermis was gently peeled to obtain isolated epidermal cells, and stomatal closure was verified by microscopy. Coverslips with attached epidermal cells were then transferred to 4.5‐cm‐diameter Petri dishes containing opening buffer (100 mM KCl and 10 mM MES, pH 6). To test the effect of photorespiratory inhibitors on light‐dependent stomatal opening, increasing concentrations (1 to 20 mM) of either HPMS (Merck 856,169) or AAN (Merck R288616) were added to the opening buffer. Then the epidermal peels were either given a light treatment (200 μmol of photons m−2 s−1 from Osram HO 54W/830 Lumilex Warm White neon tubes) or left in the dark (for 2 and 4 h) in the controlled growth room used to grow the Arabidopsis plants. In experiments with selected metabolites, opening buffer with or without inhibitor was supplemented with various concentrations (5, 10, 20 mM) of glyoxylate, glycine, serine, PEP, malate, or 3‐PGA and incubated for 4 h as described above. For each tested condition, images of treated epidermal peels were recorded under an AxioImager Z2 microscope (ZEISS). Stomatal aperture was quantified using stomatal pore areas and total stomatal surface areas and reported as follows: (stomatal pore area/total stomatal surface area) × 100 (giving a value with no units). Area measurements from the recorded images were made using a graphic tablet associated with ZEN software. All experiments were repeated three times on three leaves from three different plants for a total number of at least 300 measured stomata.

Transcriptome and Proteome Data From Guard Cell and Mesophyll Cell Protoplasts

Transcriptomic and proteomic data from guard cell protoplasts were taken from Yang et al. (2008) and Wang et al. (2023).

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using XLSTAT software. Mann–Whitney tests were applied to calculate p‐values between two samples. For comparing more than two samples, one‐way ANOVA with a multiple comparisons test was used. Box‐plots were realized using R software: Center lines show the medians; box limits indicate the 25th and 75th percentiles; whiskers extend 1.5 times the interquartile range from the 25th and 75th percentiles, circles represent outliers, and crosses represent sample means.

Results

All Photorespiratory Enzyme Genes Are Expressed and Their Corresponding Proteins Are Present in Arabidopsis Guard Cells

To check whether guard cells have the potential to carry out a complete photorespiratory cycle, published transcriptomic and proteomic data from guard cells were used. Transcripts encoding photorespiratory enzymes in guard cell protoplasts made from 16 h long‐day, 5‐week‐old Arabidopsis ecotype Col‐0 leaves were retrieved from the Affymetrix Arabidopsis ATH1 microarray data of Yang et al. (2008). Photorespiratory protein abundances of 3‐week‐old Arabidopsis leaf guard cell protoplasts enriched after enzymatic isolation and purified by flow cytometry were extracted from Wang et al. (2023) (Figure 2). It can be seen that the 16 genes encoding core photorespiratory enzymes were expressed (Figure 2A) and their corresponding proteins were detected (Figure 2B). Based on these data, it was concluded that Arabidopsis guard cells have the potential to carry out a full photorespiratory cycle. It was therefore decided to evaluate the importance of guard cell photorespiratory metabolism on light‐dependent stomatal opening. This was achieved using a pharmacological approach with previously reported photorespiratory inhibitors (HPMS and AAN), and Arabidopsis rosette leaf epidermal peels instead of detached leaves or leaf disks. From the literature, it can be seen that epidermal peels are a viable method to successfully study stomatal dynamics and functioning, with examples including ABA and pathogen‐triggered closure (Rodrigues et al. 2017), in response to light and CO2 (Santos et al. 2021), vacuolar control of opening (Mirasole et al. 2023), and circadian rhythms (Gorton et al. 1989).

HPMS Inhibits the Activity of Recombinant AtGOX2

While AAN‐dependent inhibition of glycine decarboxylation by GDC has been verified using isolated mitochondria by (Usuda et al. 1980), the inhibitory action of HPMS on GOX activity had only been observed indirectly through an increase in glycolate levels and the reduced 14C‐labeling of downstream photorespiratory metabolites in leaves (see Section 1). Therefore, it was decided to test the effect of HPMS on the kinetic parameters of purified recombinant N‐terminal His‐tagged AtGOX2 protein (see Section 2; Dellero et al. 2015; Jossier et al. 2020). We had previously demonstrated that this recombinant, His‐tagged GOX exhibited similar enzymatic properties to plant‐purified GOX (see Dellero et al. 2015). AtGOX2 activity measurements were undertaken in the presence of increasing concentrations of HPMS (from 0.05 to 10 μM) at varying glycolate concentrations (from 0.1 mM to 5 mM), and KM glycolate and VMAX values were calculated (see Table 1). Even at concentrations as low as 0.05 and 0.1 μM of HPMS, VMAX was strongly decreased by 70% and 87% and KM glycolate increased 3‐ and 15‐fold, respectively, when compared to recombinant AtGOX2 in the absence of HPMS (Table 1). In the presence of either 1 or 10 μM of HPMS, AtGOX2 activity was no longer detectable (Table 1). Therefore, it was concluded that HPMS directly and efficiently inhibits plant GOX activity.

Photorespiratory Enzyme Inhibitors Reduce Light‐Dependent Stomatal Opening in Arabidopsis Epidermal Peels

To study the role of guard cell photorespiratory metabolism in light‐dependent stomatal opening, epidermal peels were made from Arabidopsis leaves collected at the end‐of‐the‐night period and treated for 1 h with calcium‐containing closing buffer. They were then transferred into the opening buffer in the absence or presence of increasing concentrations of either HPMS or AAN (Figure 3A,B) and given either a light treatment or left in the dark for up to 4 h. For each tested condition, images of the epidermal peels were recorded under a microscope, and stomatal aperture was estimated as described in the Materials and methods. It can be seen from Figure 3 that epidermal peel stomata exhibited a variable light‐induced stomatal opening, with some stomata staying relatively closed while others became widely open (with calculated stomatal opening values ranging from 0 to 15.5). In the absence of an inhibitor, stomatal opening after 4 h of light gave average values of 7.81 and 6.96 (Figure 3A,B) thus showing a certain variability of average light‐dependent stomatal opening between Arabidopsis plants grown at different moments. In the presence of either HPMS or AAN (Figure 3A,B), stomatal opening in the light was always significantly reduced, with values ranging from 45%–72% (HPMS) and 26%–48% (AAN) when compared to epidermal peels treated without inhibitor. During a 4 h dark period in the opening buffer, stomata of epidermal peels remained closed in the presence or absence of 10 mM inhibitor (Figure 3C), thus indicating that the observed stomatal opening was light‐dependent and that the inhibitors did not lead to stomatal opening in the dark. Taken together, it was concluded that epidermal peels contain viable cells, and in the absence of mesophyll cells, light‐dependent stomatal opening is negatively impacted by an impaired guard cell photorespiratory metabolism.

The Effect of Selected Metabolites on Light‐Dependent Stomatal Opening in the Absence and Presence of Photorespiratory Inhibitors

In an attempt to better understand the role of guard cell photorespiratory metabolism in light‐dependent stomatal opening, selected metabolites were tested to see if they could counteract the observed reduced light‐dependent stomatal opening in the presence of either HPMS or AAN. It was decided to test metabolites produced by the photorespiratory cycle (glyoxylate, glycine, serine, and 3‐PGA) and metabolites linked to malate production either by an eventual re‐assimilation of photorespiratory CO2 or via glycolysis (phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), 3‐PGA, and malate). This was done by adding a selected metabolite (5, 10, 20 mM) to the opening buffers in the presence or absence of an inhibitor (10 mM) and measuring stomatal opening in the dark and after 4 h of light treatment (see Figures 4 and 5; Tables S1 and S2). In the dark, stomata remained relatively closed when tested metabolites were added, whether in the presence or absence of the inhibitor. However, some significant differences were observed compared to control peels with no added metabolite (Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2). In the absence of an inhibitor, metabolite addition often reduced light‐dependent stomatal opening by differing degrees compared to metabolite‐free controls (Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2). This was most prominent with glyoxylate (up to 30%), glycine (up to 38%), and PEP (up to 43%) (Tables S1 and S2).

When treated with HPMS, the addition of glyoxylate did not restore stomatal opening in response to light as might be expected (Figure 4, Table S1) since HPMS inhibits GOX activity (Table 1). Surprisingly, the addition of glyoxylate actually led to a concentration‐dependent reduction in stomatal opening when HPMS was present, resulting in a value of only 1.92 for stomatal opening in the presence of 20 mM glyoxylate and 10 mM HPMS (Figure 4, Table S1). In the presence of glycine, HPMS still led to a 23%–27% inhibition of stomatal opening. On the other hand, either serine or 3‐PGA reduced quite significantly the HPMS inhibition of stomatal opening (Figure 4, Table S1). For instance, the addition of 10 mM serine or 10 mM 3‐PGA led to only a 7% and 2% reduction in stomatal opening by HPMS when compared to their respective controls without HPMS addition (see Table S1). PEP reduced stomatal opening in the light, even in the absence of HPMS, and led to similar reduced stomatal opening with or without HPMS (see Table S1). Malate also appeared to affect stomatal opening both in the absence and presence of HPMS (Figure 4, Table S1). The addition of 20 mM malate increased stomatal opening in the presence of HPMS by 59% compared to the control value in the absence of malate. A similar effect was also seen with 20 mM serine (54%) and 20 mM 3‐PGA (72%) (see Table S1).

The effect of tested metabolites on AAN inhibition of light‐dependent stomatal opening of epidermal peels was globally similar to those observed in the presence of HPMS, except for 3‐PGA (compare Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2). In the presence of glycine, stomatal opening was reduced and gave similar low values with or without AAN addition, as observed with HPMS (compare Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2). Serine addition alleviated the inhibitory effect of AAN, with only a residual inhibition of 1% in the presence of 20 mM serine (Figure 5, Table S2). PEP reduced stomatal opening even in the absence of AAN and led to similar low stomatal opening values (between 5.2 and 5.6) with or without AAN (Figure 5, Table S2). In the presence of AAN, only 20 mM malate completely restored stomatal opening to the level without AAN in the presence of malate (Figure 5, Table S2). However, contrary to what was seen with HPMS, the addition of 10 mM 3‐PGA to AAN‐treated epidermal peels only partially restored stomatal opening to 5.32 (Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2).

Discussion

Pharmacological studies (Zelitch and Walker 1964; Zelitch 1961; Meidner and Mansfield 1966; Cho et al. 1987) and observations made using transgenic plants with altered photorespiratory gene expression (Lu et al. 2014; Wu et al. 2015; Cui et al. 2016; Flügel et al. 2017; Duminil 2019; Liu et al. 2019; López‐Calcagno et al. 2019) and chloroplastic photorespiratory bypasses (Maier et al. 2012; Chen et al. 2019; Nayak et al. 2022) suggested that photorespiratory metabolism modifies stomatal opening, thereby impacting leaf transpiration and gs in the light. Data recovered from the literature (Yang et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2023) indicated the expression of all photorespiratory enzyme genes as well as the presence of their corresponding proteins in guard cells, thereby supporting the existence of a complete photorespiratory cycle in these specialized epidermal cells (see Figure 2). Until now, experiments evaluating the impact of photorespiratory metabolism on stomatal behavior had been conducted on leaves (in the form of disks, detached, or whole leaves). It remained to be shown whether guard cell photorespiratory metabolism influences stomatal opening. This was investigated using a pharmacological approach involving epidermal peels from Arabidopsis leaves and previously well‐studied photorespiratory enzyme inhibitors, HPMS and AAN.

It should be noted that when studying GC opening using epidermal peels, reproducibility is hampered by stomatal heterogeneity within the leaf. Indeed, stomata are not the same size, and even in a small area of leaf epidermis, it was observed that stomata could be either almost closed, or widely open, or everything in between. This can be seen by the wide dispersion of opening values for the 300 stomata that were analyzed for each treatment (with calculated stomatal opening values ranging from 0 to 15.5) (Figure 3) and the values obtained in the absence of inhibitor after 4 h of light in the HPMS (Figure 3A) and AAN (Figure 3B) experiments of 7.81 and 6.96, respectively. Furthermore, even though 300 or more stomata were measured for each treatment since the examined epidermal peels represent only a very small part of the examined rosette leaves, a certain degree of disparity between peels taken from different leaves grown at different times is to be expected (as seen in Tables S1 and S2, for instance). That said, since both inhibitors significantly reduced light‐dependent stomatal opening in Arabidopsis epidermal peels (Figure 3) and since metabolite additions had rather similar effects (Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2), it is more than likely that the same metabolic route was affected: photorespiration rather than off‐target processes. Therefore, a dysfunctional guard cell photorespiratory cycle alone can modify stomatal opening in the light. This agrees with the recent work of (Sun et al. 2025), who studied the stomatal behavior of Arabidopsis leaves with altered guard cell‐specific GDC‐H expression (Sun et al. 2025). They found that a lower guard cell GDC activity reduced leaf gs, whereas the opposite occurred when guard cell GDC activity was increased.

So why does photorespiratory metabolism impact stomatal opening? Metabolically active guard cells are necessary for the proper functioning of stomatal movements (see Lemonnier and Lawson 2023 for a review), and the modulation of the guard cell photorespiratory cycle could influence metabolic fluxes that determine guard cell turgor (see Figure 1 and the opinion of Hodges in Eckardt et al. 2024). Indeed, blocking photorespiration metabolism with either HPMS or AAN will lead to the build‐up of 2‐PG, a known inhibitor of certain CBB cycle and glycolytic enzymes. The tested inhibitors will also reduce photorespiratory CO2, NADH, and 3‐PGA production. Photorespiratory 3‐PGA can be converted to triose‐phosphates and used to make sugars and to produce malate (via glycolysis and the TCA cycle). Photorespiratory CO2 can be assimilated by Rubisco to make 3‐PGA or by PEP carboxylase (PEPC) to produce oxaloacetate that can be used to make malate (Figure 1). Studies have shown that PEPC is important during stomatal opening (see reviews by Santelia and Lawson 2016; Lemonnier and Lawson 2023). This includes guard cell PEPC (Asai et al. 2000). HPMS and AAN lead to an inhibition of leaf photosynthetic CO2 assimilation (Meidner and Mansfield 1966; Servaites and Ogren 1977; Créach and Stewart 1982; Cho et al. 1987), and carbon flow to sucrose (Hess and Tolbert 1966; Usuda et al. 1980). Photorespiratory mutants also show a reduction in leaf photosynthetic CO2 assimilation (e.g., Dellero et al. 2015, 2016) and modifications of sugar and malate content (Flügel et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2019). Based on these observations, selected metabolites were tested to help decipher why a blocked guard cell photorespiratory cycle reduced light‐dependent stomatal opening. First, to see if circumventing the photorespiratory block (with glyoxylate, glycine, serine, or 3‐PGA) restored plant opening in the presence of the inhibitor. Second, to see if the addition of malate or C‐skeletons required for malate biosynthesis (3‐PGA or PEP) could alleviate the observed reduced stomatal opening (see Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2). Globally, the effect of each metabolite appeared to be similar irrespective of the inhibitor (Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2), perhaps since both inhibitors block the photorespiratory cycle and reduce photosynthetic CO2 assimilation (see Section 1 for details). Glyoxylate was expected to restore a partial photorespiratory cycle in the presence of HPMS‐inhibited GOX activity and, therefore, alleviate the HPMS‐related lowering of stomatal opening in the light. However, this was not the case (Figure 4, Table S1), perhaps due to glyoxylate inhibiting GDC activity (Peterson 1982). Glyoxylate also inhibits PGLP activity (Vérin‐Vergeau et al. 1980), and glycolate synthesis (Oliver 1978), which might explain the reduced stomatal opening in the absence of the inhibitor. In a similar manner to glyoxylate, glycine addition also reduced light‐dependent stomatal opening in the presence and absence of the inhibitor (Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2). This would agree with the report that glycine alone impairs stomatal opening (Yoshida et al. 2016), perhaps reflecting its ability to reduce photorespiratory rate (Kang et al. 2018) and PGLP activity (Vérin‐Vergeau et al. 1980). On the other hand, the addition of serine allowed a recovery of stomatal opening in the presence of either inhibitor when compared to control values in the presence of serine (Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2). It is possible that serine is converted to 3‐PGA and then to malate. Indeed, guard cells also contain a serine dehydratase that converts serine to pyruvate (Fujitani et al. 2006), which fuels the TCA cycle and malate production. 3‐PGA addition was efficient in reducing HPMS‐related effects on stomatal opening; however, it appeared to have only a limited beneficial effect in the presence of AAN (Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2), this was not expected and remains to be explained. The addition of either PEP or malate to epidermal cells was to test whether the reduced light‐dependent stomatal opening in epidermal peels by a blocked photorespiratory cycle was linked to malate biosynthesis. Surprisingly, the addition of PEP led to a reduction in stomatal opening in the absence of inhibitors (Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2), perhaps reflecting PEP inhibition of glycolate biosynthesis (Oliver and Zelitch 1977). As expected, the addition of malate reduced the inhibitor‐induced lower stomatal opening (Figures 4 and 5, Tables S1 and S2). When taken together, a blocked guard cell photorespiration probably limits carbon supply to glycolysis and the TCA cycle, which negatively impacts malate production required for complete stomatal opening.

Conclusions

Guard cells have the potential to undergo a complete photorespiratory cycle. This pathway is important for light‐dependent stomatal opening, as a blocked cycle reduces light‐dependent stomatal opening in isolated epidermal peels. Supplying metabolites to bypass inhibited metabolic steps alleviates this reduction in stomatal opening. Therefore, a functional photorespiratory cycle in guard cells is essential for correct stomatal functioning. This emphasizes the metabolic dependence of guard cells on this pathway, which is probably required to maintain an adequate carbon supply for guard cell osmolyte production. Our findings provide a new perspective on the physiological significance of photorespiratory metabolism and its potential impact on plant water‐use efficiency and gas exchange regulation. Future research should explore the precise signaling mechanisms and pathways linking photorespiratory metabolism to stomatal opening in the light.
