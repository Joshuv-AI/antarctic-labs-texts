Early‐ vs. late‐onset gestational diabetes: Differential impact on maternal and neonatal outcomes
Authors: Baolai Li, Wenjun Ji, Rui Zhao, Jinchun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe timing of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) diagnosis may influence maternal and neonatal outcomes, yet the differential impact of early‐onset versus late‐onset GDM remains incompletely characterized, particularly in Chinese populations. To compare maternal and neonatal outcomes between early‐onset GDM (diagnosed before 20 weeks' gestation) and late‐onset GDM (diagnosed at 24–28 weeks' gestation).Material and MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 1847 singleton pregnancies complicated by GDM at Qingdao Municipal Hospital from January 2020 to December 2023. Participants were classified into early‐onset (n = 583) and late‐onset (n = 1264) groups. Primary outcomes included composite adverse neonatal outcome, preeclampsia, and cesarean delivery. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to calculate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) after controlling for maternal age, pre‐pregnancy BMI, nulliparity, family history of diabetes, and chronic hypertension.ResultsWomen with early‐onset GDM demonstrated significantly higher rates of insulin requirement (45.8% vs. 21.1%, p < 0.001). Early‐onset GDM was associated with increased risks of preeclampsia (aOR 1.85, 95% CI 1.38–2.47), cesarean delivery (aOR 1.84, 95% CI 1.53–2.28), and preterm birth (aOR 2.69, 95% CI 2.05–3.47). Neonatal complications were substantially elevated in the early‐onset group, including macrosomia (aOR 1.86, 95% CI 1.41–2.37), neonatal hypoglycemia (aOR 2.04, 95% CI 1.57–2.67), respiratory distress (aOR 2.60, 95% CI 1.78–3.71), and NICU admission (aOR 2.16, 95% CI 1.69–2.74). The composite adverse neonatal outcome occurred in 61.2% of early‐onset versus 37.7% of late‐onset cases (aOR 2.57, 95% CI 2.13–3.15).ConclusionsEarly‐onset GDM may represent a clinically distinct, higher‐risk phenotype with notably elevated maternal and neonatal morbidity compared to late‐onset disease. Given the observational design and the potential for residual confounding, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive. They require confirmation in prospective, multicenter studies before risk stratification by diagnostic timing or intensified surveillance can be advocated for routine clinical implementation.Keywords: early‐onset, gestational diabetes mellitus, late‐onset, neonatal outcomes, pregnancy outcomes, risk stratification

Key message

Early‐onset GDM (<20 weeks) suggests a distinct, higher‐risk phenotype with greater insulin needs, 2.6 times higher adverse neonatal outcomes, and more preeclampsia, preterm birth, and cesarean delivery than late‐onset GDM, warranting intensified monitoring and early screening in high‐risk pregnancies.

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) affects approximately 14% of pregnancies worldwide and represents one of the most common metabolic complications of pregnancy. 1 The prevalence of GDM has increased substantially over the past decades, paralleling the global rise in obesity and metabolic syndrome. 2 In China, the reported prevalence ranges from 14.8% to 21.1% depending on the diagnostic criteria applied, placing a significant burden on obstetric care systems. 3

Traditionally, GDM screening has been performed between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation using the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), as this period coincides with peak placental hormone‐mediated insulin resistance. 4 However, accumulating evidence suggests that a substantial proportion of women develop glucose intolerance earlier in pregnancy, and these cases may represent a distinct clinical phenotype with different underlying pathophysiology and risk profiles. 5 , 6 , 7

The landmark Treatment of Booking Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (TOBOGM) trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2023, provided compelling evidence that immediate treatment of GDM diagnosed before 20 weeks' gestation reduces adverse neonatal outcomes by 18% compared to deferred treatment 8 Secondary analyses from this trial further demonstrated that early‐onset GDM is associated with higher insulin requirements and more adverse perinatal outcomes compared to late‐onset disease; however, these post‐hoc observational findings should be interpreted with caution, as the TOBOGM trial was designed to evaluate treatment effects rather than causal phenotype comparisons, and reported differences in rare outcomes such as perinatal mortality were based on small event numbers 9 , 10 , 11

Early‐onset GDM may reflect pre‐existing metabolic dysfunction that becomes clinically apparent during pregnancy, whereas late‐onset GDM more likely represents pregnancy‐induced insulin resistance. 12 , 13 Women diagnosed early in pregnancy experience prolonged fetal exposure to hyperglycemia during critical developmental windows, potentially explaining the differential impact on outcomes. 14 The International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) has recently convened expert consensus panels to address the diagnostic and therapeutic implications of early pregnancy hyperglycemia. 15

Despite growing recognition of early‐onset GDM as a distinct clinical entity, comparative data from Chinese populations remain limited. The physiological and metabolic characteristics of GDM may vary across ethnic groups, and the applicability of findings from predominantly Western populations requires validation 16 Furthermore, the optimal risk stratification strategies and management approaches for early‐onset GDM in Asian populations remain to be established.

The present study aimed to compare maternal and neonatal outcomes between early‐onset and late‐onset GDM in a large Chinese cohort, with the hypothesis that earlier diagnosis portends a higher‐risk phenotype requiring more intensive management. Understanding these differential risk profiles is essential for developing targeted screening protocols and individualized care pathways.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Qingdao Hospital of Rehabilitation University (Qingdao Municipal Hospital), a tertiary referral center with an annual delivery volume of approximately 7000 cases, including approximately 600 GDM‐complicated pregnancies. We reviewed all singleton pregnancies with a diagnosis of GDM who delivered between January 1, 2020 and December 31, 2023.

Inclusion criteria were 1 : singleton pregnancy 2 ; confirmed GDM diagnosis according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria 3 ; prenatal care and delivery at our institution; and 4 complete medical records available for review. Exclusion criteria included 1 : multiple pregnancy 2 ; pre‐existing type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus 3 ; incomplete medical records; and 4 loss to follow‐up before delivery.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Qingdao Municipal Hospital (approval number: 2026‐KY‐013; approval date: January 26, 2026). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Diagnostic criteria and group classification

GDM was diagnosed using a 75‐g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) according to IADPSG criteria: fasting plasma glucose ≥5.1 mmol/L (92 mg/dL), 1‐h glucose ≥10.0 mmol/L (180 mg/dL), or 2‐h glucose ≥8.5 mmol/L (153 mg/dL) 17 Women with one or more of the following risk factors underwent early screening with a 75‐g OGTT at the first prenatal visit (typically 8–12 weeks gestation): pre‐pregnancy BMI > =25 kg/m2, prior GDM, family history of diabetes mellitus in a first‐degree relative, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), maternal age > =35 years, previous macrosomic infant (birthweight > 4000 g), or a documented history of impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose tolerance. Universal screening was performed at 24–28 weeks gestation for all women not meeting early screening criteria or with a negative early OGTT result. Women in the late‐onset GDM group who carried identifiable risk factors had either received a negative early OGTT result and subsequently met GDM criteria at universal 24–28 week screening (reflecting de novo or progressive glucose intolerance predominantly driven by third‐trimester placental insulin resistance) or had risk factors identified retrospectively from medical records but not formally documented at the first prenatal visit, a recognized limitation of retrospective risk‐stratified screening. Pre‐existing type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus was excluded based on pre‐pregnancy records and first‐trimester HbA1c values (HbA1c <6.5% and no prior diabetes diagnosis).

Participants were classified into two groups based on gestational age at GDM diagnosis: (1) Early‐onset GDM: diagnosis before 20 weeks' gestation; and (2) Late‐onset GDM: diagnosis at 24–28 weeks' gestation. This classification aligns with the TOBOGM trial definition and emerging international consensus. 8 , 15

Management protocol

All women with GDM received standardized management including medical nutrition therapy, self‐monitoring of blood glucose, and moderate physical activity. Glycemic targets were fasting glucose <5.3 mmol/L, 1‐h postprandial <7.8 mmol/L, and 2‐h postprandial <6.7 mmol/L. Pharmacological therapy with metformin or insulin was initiated when lifestyle modifications failed to achieve glycemic control within 1–2 weeks. 18

Data collection and outcome measures

Maternal baseline characteristics extracted from medical records included: maternal age, pre‐pregnancy body mass index (BMI), parity, family history of diabetes mellitus, prior GDM, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), chronic hypertension, gestational age at diagnosis, fasting glucose, 2 h OGTT glucose, and HbA1c at diagnosis.

Primary maternal outcomes included: preeclampsia, defined per ISSHP 2018 criteria as new‐onset hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg on at least two occasions ≥ 4 h apart) after 20 weeks' gestation in a previously normotensive woman, accompanied by proteinuria (≥ 300 mg/24 h) or evidence of end‐organ dysfunction (renal insufficiency, thrombocytopenia, impaired liver function, pulmonary edema, or new‐onset neurological symptoms), cesarean delivery, and preterm birth (< 37 weeks). Secondary maternal outcomes included gestational hypertension, postpartum hemorrhage (blood loss > 500 mL), premature rupture of membranes (PROM), and polyhydramnios.

Neonatal outcomes included: macrosomia (birthweight > 4000 g), large for gestational age (LGA, birthweight > 90th percentile), small for gestational age (SGA, birthweight < 10th percentile), neonatal hypoglycemia (blood glucose <2.6 mmol/L within 24 h of birth), respiratory distress syndrome, neonatal jaundice requiring phototherapy, birth trauma, NICU admission, and Apgar scores. The composite adverse neonatal outcome was defined as the occurrence of any of the following: respiratory distress, neonatal hypoglycemia, birth trauma, macrosomia, NICU admission, or low Apgar score (<7 at 5 min). 19

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test according to data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate.

Crude odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for binary outcomes. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) controlling for potential confounders including maternal age, pre‐pregnancy BMI, nulliparity, family history of diabetes mellitus, and chronic hypertension. These covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature. 20 , 21 , 22

A two‐sided p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data were handled using complete case analysis; the proportion of missing values for any single variable was less than 3% and considered negligible. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q‐Q plots, informing the selection of parametric or non‐parametric tests. The chi‐square test (Pearson) was applied consistently for categorical comparisons; Fisher's exact test was used when any expected cell count was less than 5. All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12 (SciPy 1.11.3; scikit‐learn 1.4.0; statsmodels 0.14.0).

RESULTS

Study population

During the study period, 27 856 deliveries occurred at our institution, of which 2412 (8.7%) were complicated by GDM. After applying exclusion criteria, 1847 women were included in the final analysis: 583 (31.6%) with early‐onset GDM and 1264 (68.4%) with late‐onset GDM (Figure 1). The primary reasons for exclusion were incomplete medical records (n = 198), pre‐existing diabetes (n = 142), loss to follow‐up (n = 136), and multiple pregnancy (n = 89).

Treatment modality

Significant differences were observed in treatment requirements between groups (p < 0.001). In the early‐onset group, 31.4% were managed with diet alone, 22.8% required metformin, and 45.8% required insulin therapy. In contrast, late‐onset GDM was more frequently controlled with diet alone (52.8%), with lower rates of metformin (26.0%) and insulin (21.1%) use. DISCUSSION

This large retrospective cohort study provides evidence suggesting that early‐onset GDM may represent a clinically distinct, higher‐risk phenotype with notably elevated maternal and neonatal morbidity compared to late‐onset disease. Women diagnosed before 20 weeks' gestation demonstrated significantly higher insulin requirements, increased rates of preeclampsia and cesarean delivery, and a 2.6‐fold higher risk of composite adverse neonatal outcomes after adjustment for potential confounders.

Our observational findings are broadly consistent with several observations reported in the TOBOGM trial and its secondary analyses, although the two studies differ fundamentally in design and our data should not be read as extending or confirming its randomized results 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 The insulin requirement of 45.8% in our early‐onset cohort is consistent with the 71% higher insulin use reported in TOBOGM secondary analyses, confirming that early diagnosis identifies women with more severe metabolic derangement 9 The composite adverse neonatal outcome rate of 61.2% in our early‐onset group exceeds the approximately 30% rate reported in TOBOGM, likely reflecting differences in outcome definitions and the inclusion of NICU admission in our composite endpoint 8 It is important to distinguish that TOBOGM was a randomized controlled trial designed primarily to evaluate the effect of treatment initiation timing on neonatal outcomes, whereas the present study is an observational retrospective cohort comparing phenotypic outcomes by diagnostic timing. Accordingly, outcome rate comparisons between our study and TOBOGM should account for these fundamental differences in study design, patient selection, and endpoint definitions.

The pathophysiological basis for the differential outcomes between early and late‐onset GDM likely involves multiple mechanisms. Early‐onset GDM may represent undiagnosed pre‐existing glucose intolerance that becomes apparent during pregnancy, characterized by both insulin resistance and beta‐cell dysfunction 12 , 23 In contrast, late‐onset GDM predominantly reflects pregnancy‐induced insulin resistance mediated by placental hormones, with relatively preserved beta‐cell compensatory capacity 24 This distinction is supported by our observation of higher pre‐pregnancy BMI, greater prevalence of PCOS and family history of diabetes, and more abnormal glycemic parameters at diagnosis in the early‐onset group.

An important interpretive caveat is the potential for confounding by indication and underlying metabolic phenotype severity. Women with early‐onset GDM in our cohort carried a systematically more adverse baseline metabolic profile—older age, higher pre‐pregnancy BMI, and greater prevalence of PCOS, prior GDM, and family history of diabetes—each of which independently increases the risk of adverse obstetric and neonatal outcomes irrespective of GDM timing. Although our multivariate analyses adjusted for these known confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured variables (including glycemic control trajectory, treatment intensity, and socioeconomic factors) likely persists. Therefore, the observed outcome differences should be interpreted as reflecting phenotypic differences associated with early‐onset GDM rather than causal effects of diagnostic timing per se. The timing of GDM diagnosis serves as a clinically useful marker of underlying metabolic severity rather than an independent causal driver of adverse outcomes. Future studies employing propensity score matching, inverse probability weighting, or instrumental variable approaches would provide stronger causal inference.

The prolonged duration of fetal hyperglycemic exposure in early‐onset GDM has important implications for fetal programming and development. Hyperglycemia during the first trimester—a critical period for organogenesis—may contribute to the increased rates of macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and respiratory distress observed in our study 14 , 25 The Pedersen hypothesis, which postulates that maternal hyperglycemia stimulates fetal hyperinsulinemia leading to excessive fetal growth, operates over a longer duration in early‐onset cases 26

The nearly three‐fold increase in preterm birth among women with early‐onset GDM (aOR 2.69) is a particularly concerning finding with significant clinical implications. This observation may reflect both the underlying metabolic severity and the higher rates of pregnancy complications (preeclampsia, polyhydramnios) that necessitate early delivery. 27 The cascade of prematurity‐related complications, including respiratory distress and NICU admission, contributes substantially to the excess neonatal morbidity in this population.

Our results may have implications for clinical practice, although they require confirmation before they can justify changes in care. First, they are consistent with calls for early pregnancy screening in high‐risk populations, as advocated by recent guidelines and the IADPSG summit report. 15 , 17 Women with risk factors such as obesity, prior GDM, family history of diabetes, or PCOS should undergo glucose tolerance testing at the first prenatal visit. Second, our data suggest that women identified with early‐onset GDM may benefit from more intensive glycemic management, earlier initiation of pharmacotherapy when indicated, and enhanced fetal surveillance. 28 It should, however, be acknowledged that the absolute magnitude of benefit from early GDM treatment in the TOBOGM trial was modest—approximately 6% absolute reduction in composite neonatal outcome, predominantly driven by a reduction in respiratory distress—which is an important consideration when designing population‐level screening programs and counseling individual patients.

The 2024 Diabetes Care secondary analyses from TOBOGM demonstrated that treatment benefit is greatest when early GDM is detected before 14 weeks' gestation (NNT = 12), compared to detection at 14–20 weeks (NNT = 26) 10 This finding underscores the importance of early identification and prompt intervention. Furthermore, the observation that approximately one‐third of women with early GDM “regress” to normal glucose tolerance by 24–28 weeks suggests phenotypic heterogeneity within this population that may warrant personalized management approaches. 11

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the retrospective design is inherently subject to selection bias and residual confounding. Critically, early OGTT screening was risk‐based rather than universal, introducing selection (verification) bias: women tested early were systematically higher‐risk, which may inflate apparent differences between groups. Second, pharmacological treatment (insulin or metformin) represents a potential intermediate variable in the causal pathway between GDM timing and outcomes, rather than a confounder. Our regression models did not adjust for treatment modality to avoid overadjustment bias; however, the substantially higher insulin use in early‐onset cases (45.8% vs. 21.1%) may have partially attenuated true biological differences. Third, gestational weight gain was deliberately excluded from multivariable models, as it is partly determined by GDM metabolic severity and management, rendering it an intermediate variable rather than a baseline confounder. Other potentially important variables unavailable in our dataset include glycemic control during pregnancy, smoking status, socioeconomic factors, and timing and intensity of pharmacotherapy, each representing potential sources of residual confounding. Fourth, the composite adverse neonatal outcome includes endpoints of heterogeneous clinical severity; macrosomia and NICU admission are influenced by local practice patterns, and the notably high composite rate (61.2% early vs. 37.7% late) reflects this heterogeneity. Pre‐specified sensitivity analyses excluding individual components were not performed; however, the consistently elevated rates of each individual neonatal outcome in the early‐onset group (Table 3) support the robustness of the overall finding. Fifth, our single‐center Chinese cohort limits generalizability to other ethnic groups and healthcare systems with different screening protocols; validation in multicenter and multinational cohorts is warranted. Sixth, we did not assess long‐term maternal or offspring metabolic outcomes, which represent important areas for future investigation. 29 Seventh, the early‐onset group received earlier intervention by virtue of earlier diagnosis, which may have attenuated outcome differences; the true disparity in untreated populations would likely be even greater. 30

Strengths of this study include the large sample size, comprehensive outcome assessment, and application of internationally recognized diagnostic criteria. The study period spans the post‐IADPSG era with uniform diagnostic standards, enhancing internal validity. Our detailed characterization of baseline metabolic parameters provides insight into the underlying pathophysiology distinguishing early from late‐onset disease.

CONCLUSION

Early‐onset GDM diagnosed before 20 weeks' gestation is associated with a higher‐risk metabolic phenotype characterized by increased insulin requirements and notably elevated rates of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes compared to late‐onset disease. The 2.6‐fold increased risk of composite adverse neonatal outcome, combined with higher rates of preeclampsia, cesarean delivery, and preterm birth, is consistent with early‐onset GDM representing a potentially distinct clinical phenotype. However, because this was a single‐center, retrospective study with a substantial risk of residual confounding, these results should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive. Prospective, multicenter studies are needed to confirm these associations before early pregnancy screening and intensified management are advocated for routine clinical implementation in high‐risk populations.
