Disseminated Mycobacterium avium and Talaromyces Marneffei coinfection in a patient of misdiagnosed tuberculosis and the challenges: a case report
Authors: Hui-Hui Wu, Yu-Kun Zhao, Yun Lai, Chun-Lei Wan, Qiang Liao, Yi-Hong Zheng, Sha Lu, Jing-Fa Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMycobacterium avium (M. avium) is emerging as an opportunistic pathogen and has been grouped with the nontuberculosis mycobacteria (NTM). Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, primarily affects elderly individuals with chronic lung disease and remains one of the leading causes of death in many regions worldwide. Distinguishing between NTM infection and TB is challenging due to similar clinical manifestations and histopathological characteristics. Background

Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are increasingly recognized as critical opportunistic pathogens in humans, causing a globally increasing number of opportunistic infections and mortality in immunocompromised populations, as well as affecting otherwise healthy individuals [1, 2]. While the lungs are most affected, the lymphatic system, skin, heart, soft tissues, bones, and joints are also frequently involved [2]. Diagnosing and treating NTM is challenging due to its similarity in symptoms and histopathological characteristics with tuberculosis (TB), as well as resistance to many antibiotics [3, 4]. NTM are diverse and ubiquitous in the environment; however, only a few species pose severe threats by causing opportunistic infections in humans, such as Mycobacterium avium (M. avium). M. avium is an emerging opportunistic pathogen worldwide that often causes nontuberculous infections in immunocompromised individuals [5]. Talaromyces marneffei (T. marneffei) is a pathogenic fungus that has devastating impacts on persons with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) or other immunocompromised conditions [6]. To our knowledge, only three HIV-negative cases of disseminated M. avium and T. marneffei co-infection have been reported [7–9]. Unfortunately, prompt diagnosis of this co-infection was often missed, resulting in ineffective treatment and poor patient prognosis. Herein, we present a novel case of an HIV-negative Chinese woman who was initially misdiagnosed with TB and received ineffective anti-tuberculosis therapies (ATT) for three years. Subsequently, she developed pulmonary lesions along with bone lesions accompanied by subcutaneous abscesses and cerebral abscesses before ultimately being diagnosed with disseminated co-infection of M. avium and T. marneffei.

Case presentation

The patient, a 44-year-old Chinese woman, presented with chronic cough, sputum production, recurrent low-grade fevers, and chest pain. Three years earlier, at the first-admission hospital, she had been diagnosed with secondary pulmonary tuberculosis, tuberculous pleuritis, sternal tuberculosis, and lymph node tuberculosis based on acid-fast bacilli (AFB)-positive sputum smears, chest CT findings, lung tissue histopathology (Fig. 1) showing chronic granulomatous inflammation, and positive acid-fast staining of chest wall tissue. She subsequently underwent a left upper lobe wedge resection and excision of chest wall masses, followed by two different courses of ATT. Despite these interventions, no clinical improvement was observed, and disease progression was documented.

Pathology of lung tissue resected from the left upper lobe. Chronic granulomatous inflammation is observed, with infiltration of histiocytes, lymphocytes, and Langhans giant cells. No typical caseous necrosis is present. a HE staining, ×40. b HE staining, ×400

One month before she presented to our institution, she sought medical attention at the second hospital due to multiple subcutaneous abscesses on the head, trunk, and limbs. Cultures from an ulcer on her trunk revealed NTM infection, and M. avium was identified through DNA microarray chip array testing. Consequently, she was diagnosed with disseminated M. avium infection and treated using amikacin, moxifloxacin, clofazimine, clarithromycin, and linezolid. However, her condition did not improve after one month of treatment; instead, she gradually developed mental symptoms such as disorganized speech and hallucinations, leading to her subsequent evaluation and treatment at our hospital. Her past medical history and family history were unremarkable.

On physical examination, the patient presented with pallor and cachexia. Subcutaneous abscesses and cutaneous ulcers accompanied by yellowish purulent discharge measuring 2 to 4 cm in diameter were observed on the head, chest, abdomen, and lower limbs (Fig. 2). Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis (white blood cell [WBC]: 18.62 × 109/L, neutrophils ratio: 92%, lymphocytes ratio: 2.6%), anemia (hemoglobin [Hb]: 4.4 g/dL, red blood cell [RBC] count: 3.35 × 1012/L), hepatic and renal dysfunction (gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase [GGT]: 80 U/L, serum creatinine: 135 µmol/L), elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR: 46 mm/h, normal range [NR]: 0–20 mm/h), increased C-reactive-protein levels (CRP: 103 mg/L, NR: 0–9 mg/L), elevated IgG levels (36.58 g/L, NR: 8.6–17.4 g/L). The CD4 + lymphocyte count was below average at 368/µL (NR: 414–1440/µL). The anti-HIV test, TPPA (Treponema pallidum particle agglutination test), and TRUST (Tolulized red unheated serum test) were negative.

Chest computed tomography (CT) showed bilateral lung infection with enlarged lymph nodes in the bilateral supraclavicular, neck, axillary, and mediastinal regions. Additionally, thickening of the left pleura, bilateral pleural effusion, and pericardial effusion were observed. Multiple areas of bone destruction involving the sternum, numerous ribs, vertebral bodies, and scapula were also seen (Fig. 3). Cephalic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed scattered lesions on the head and cheeks, along with abnormal enhancement in the left frontal lobe, suggestive of infectious lesions and multiple abscesses (Fig. 3).

Thickening of the left pleura, bilateral pleural effusion, and pericardial effusion were noted. b Multiple areas of bone destruction involving the sternum, ribs, vertebral bodies, and scapula were identified. c T2-weighted cephalic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a round, high-signal density mass with surrounding edema, suggesting an abscess in the left frontal lobe. d T1-weighted cephalic 3D spoiled gradient-recalled echo sequence (SPGR) image showed a round-shaped, low-signal density mass in the exact site of the left frontal lobe

Histopathological examination of a skin abscess in the right upper abdomen revealed diffuse histiocytoid cells and lymphocytic and neutrophil infiltration in the dermis. A few small-sized fungal spore-like structures, which were positively stained with Gomori’s methenamine silver (GMS), were identified in affected areas (Fig. 4). Subsequently, T. marneffei was isolated by culturing the pus from a scalp abscess (Fig. 5) and confirmed through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) sequencing.

In addition, next-generation sequencing (NGS) of paraffin-embedded tissue from the left upper lobe resected three years ago confirmed that the initial pulmonary infection was caused by M. avium, as opposed to Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Thus, the patient was ultimately diagnosed with disseminated co-infection by M. avium and T. marneffei. She received treatment consisting of oral itraconazole 0.2 g twice daily, clarithromycin 0.5 g twice daily, moxifloxacin 0.4 g once daily, ethambutol 0.625 g once daily, and doxycycline 0.1 g once daily. Subcutaneous abscesses were managed through incision, drainage, and local wound care. After two weeks of treatment, the patient became afebrile, and partial improvement was observed in cutaneous lesions. Her mental state was improved. Laboratory examination showed a slight recovery of leukocytosis (WBC: 15.44 × 109/L, neutrophil ratio: 90.4%). Liver and kidney function markers were improved (GGT: 39 U/L, serum creatinine: 103 µmol/L). The patient requested discharge for unknown reasons and was lost to follow-up.

Discussion and conclusions

The incidence of NTM infection has been increasing annually [10]. In some regions, the prevalence of NTM has already surpassed TB [10]. The Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), consisting of 12 species including M. avium and M. intracellulare, is the most common cause of NTM infection [11]. NTM and TB share similar clinical manifestations, including fever, night sweats, weight loss, cough, and expectoration. They also share overlapping histopathological characteristics, which include acid-fast positive bacilli, necrotizing granulomas, and multinucleated Langhans giant cells. As a result, NTM infections are frequently misdiagnosed as TB [3, 12]. Unlike TB, NTM, particularly M. avium, shows poor sensitivity to existing anti-TB drugs [4]. The prognosis for MAC infection remains poorly characterized. A recent review reported a 5-year all-cause mortality exceeding 25% in patients with MAC pulmonary disease, and higher risks of complications and death from disseminated MAC infections [13]. Therefore, misdiagnosis of NTM as TB can lead to inappropriate or delayed treatment, ultimately worsening clinical outcomes [14]. The study also indicates that accurate species-level identification of NTM significantly improves the cure rate of NTM infections [12, 15, 16].

Although ‌microbial culture is the gold standard for diagnosing both TB and NTM infections [17, 18]. The practical utility of ‌microbial culture is limited by low positivity rates, prolonged incubation times, and the need for specific growth conditions for certain NTM species [19]. Given these challenges, molecular diagnostic techniques such as quantitative PCR, line probe assays, high-resolution melting analysis, loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), and next-generation sequencing (NGS) have become increasingly important for distinguishing TB from NTM [20].

Unfortunately, our patient was initially misdiagnosed with TB solely based on sputum acid-fast positivity and pathological findings, without fulfilling the diagnostic gold standard [17, 18]. Moreover, no follow-up microbiological reassessment was performed during treatment, even when the patient failed to show an adequate clinical response.

Opportunistic co-infections involving NTM and fungi are increasingly recognized in immunocompromised individuals [21]. Among patients with NTM infections, the most common fungal co-infection is Aspergillus spp., with a reported prevalence of 7.8–12.2%, Less commonly, pathogens such as T. marneffei have also been identified [22, 23]. T. marneffei is an emerging opportunistic fungus that mainly affects patients with AIDS or other immunodeficiency disorders and is associated with high mortality [6], whereas in HIV-negative patients, the mortality rate is low and the infection usually responds well to antifungal therapy [24]. In rare cases, co-infection of NTM and T. marneffei in HIV-negative patients can occur [25, 26]. However, delayed diagnosis can lead to a poor prognosis [27]. Due to the clinical similarities between NTM and T. marneffei infections, such as cough, fever, and weight loss, the rate of missed diagnosis in co-infected cases can be as high as 77%, further exacerbating the prognosis [28].

A study indicates that elevated levels of anti-IFN-γ autoantibodies, WBCs, neutrophils, immunoglobulins (IgG, IgM, IgA), serum globulin, ESR, and CRP, and low CD4 + T cell count are considered risk factors for T. marneffei and NTM co-infection [25, 29]. Compared with single-pathogen infections, NTM and T. marneffei co-infections are typically characterized by higher levels of inflammatory markers, more severe inflammatory anemia, and multi-organ involvement [29]. Unfortunately, in our case, anti-IFN-γ autoantibody testing was not performed. Despite a one-month course of anti-NTM therapy, our patient exhibited increased levels of WBCs, IgG, ESR, CRP, together with anemia and reduced CD4 + T cell counts, and the patient’s condition deteriorated. This prompted us to pursue further histopathological and microbiological investigations, which confirmed concurrent T. marneffei infection.

Retrospective NGS analysis of paraffin-embedded lung tissue resected three years earlier identified only M. avium, and the corresponding histopathology examination revealed chronic granulomatous inflammation without fungal spore structures. Taken together, we suggest that the patient initially suffered from M. avium infection, but prolonged misdiagnosis as tuberculosis and ineffective anti-TB therapy allowed disease progression, eventually leading to secondary T. marneffei infection.

A standard anti-MAC regimen involves a combination of macrolides, rifamycin, and ethambutol (guideline-based three-drug therapy, GBT therapy) for a minimum of 12 months following sputum conversion. Other recommended treatments include aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and isoniazid [18, 30]. Amphotericin B and itraconazole are identified as the most effective drugs for treating T. marneffei, with a treatment course of 3–6 months [31]. In cases of co-infection, polypharmacy increases risks of hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, necessitating careful monitoring and management of potential drug–drug interactions (e.g., itraconazole–rifampicin).

Our patient showed clinical improvement, with defervescence and resolution of cutaneous lesions after two weeks of itraconazole, clarithromycin, moxifloxacin, ethambutol, and doxycycline therapy. Unfortunately, she was lost during the follow-up.

We presented a rare case of disseminated M. avium and T. marneffei co-infection in an HIV-negative patient who was initially misdiagnosed with tuberculosis. The previous three cases of this co-infection typically presented with symptoms including fever, cough, chest pain, lymphadenopathy, and painful skin erythema [7–9]. In addition to most of these symptoms, our patient uniquely developed the rare complication of cutaneous and cerebral abscesses. This case has prompted several clinical reflections. Firstly, in clinical practice, distinguishing NTM infections from tuberculosis remains challenging due to their overlapping clinical and histopathological features. Clinicians should strictly adhere to established diagnostic guidelines for tuberculosis. Microbiological cultures, molecular diagnostics, and histopathological examinations are indispensable both at the time of initial diagnosis and throughout treatment follow-up, as they can substantially reduce the risk of misdiagnosis. Secondly, co-infection with M. avium and T. marneffei is prone to missed diagnosis, which often results in delayed treatment and poor prognosis. Clinicians should maintain vigilance for such co-infections, particularly when disease progression persists despite apparently adequate anti-M. avium therapy. Clinical warning signs include persistently elevated inflammatory markers, worsening anemia, and involvement of multiple organ systems. The multi-site histopathological examination, repeated ‌microbial cultures, and molecular diagnostic techniques are essential to detect secondary infections.
