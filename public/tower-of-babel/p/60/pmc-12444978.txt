Synthesis of Stable Aluminum Nanoparticles at Varying Reactant Concentrations and Their Surface-Enhanced Raman Scattering Activity
Authors: K. A. Shiral Fernando, Samuel Franqui-Rios, Andrea N. Giordano, Rahul Rao, John H. Dunlap, Luke A. Baldwin, Christopher A. Crouse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aluminum nanoparticles (Al NPs) were synthesized via catalyzed thermal reduction of an aluminum precursor in the presence of a capping ligand. A systematic study was conducted to examine the effect of dilution on nanoparticle synthesis by varying the volume of anhydrous toluene across four dilution factors while maintaining constant molar quantities of the aluminum precursor, catalyst, and ligand. This methodology is relevant for scale-up processes, where more dilute conditions can mitigate nanoparticle reactivity and enhance safety. The resulting Al NPs were characterized with respect to the yield, size distribution, chemical composition (bulk and surface), and morphology. Consistent and favorable yields were observed across all of the dilution conditions. The synthesized Al NPs were further evaluated as low-cost substrates for surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS). While Au and Ag nanoparticles are known to exhibit SERS activity in the visible region for chemical warfare agent (CWA) surrogates, little is known about SERS performance in the ultraviolet (UV) region by using metallic substrates. Al NPs, with broadband optical absorption extending from the UV to the near-infrared (NIR), were investigated for this application. Rhodamine 6G (R6G), a standard dye, and dimethyl methylphosphonate (DMMP), a commonly used CWA surrogate, were analyzed by using 248.6 nm UV excitation. The Al NPs exhibited modest SERS activity for both analytes under these conditions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to examine both the synthesis of Al NPs under varying reagent concentrations by changing the solvent volumes and their SERS performance in the UV region for CWA surrogates. Further efforts aimed at reducing particle agglomeration and tailoring surface chemistry are expected to improve sensitivity and enable the development of cost-effective SERS-based sensors for trace CWA detection.

Introduction

Aluminum nanoparticles (Al NPs) have been extensively studied due to their high energy density and potential applications in hydrogen storage and generation, high-energy fuel additives, and as energetic materials. − Additionally, Al NPs are considered plasmonic photocatalysts because the decay of surface plasmons generates “hot” electrons and/or holes and these high-energy electrons and holes can facilitate redox reactions. − Al NPs are also known to exhibit strong absorption over a wide range of wavelengths (ultraviolet (UV) to near-infrared (NIR)), which extends their potential applications in surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) and the development of sensors for detecting molecules that produce weak signals with other modern detection techniques. Due to their highly reactive nature, passivation of the active, metallic Al core is required to prevent the premature oxidation of Al NPs. ,,− Additionally, their alane precursors also exhibit moderate sensitivity to air and high reactivity with water. As efforts are made to scale up the catalyzed thermal decomposition of alane precursors to enable large-scale manufacturing or continuous flow synthesis of Al NPs, it is important to address safety concerns related to the pyrophoricity and exothermic reactions of both the alane precursor and the resulting Al NPs. Simple dilution presents an affordable and attractive solution.

In prior studies, Bunker and co-workers introduced the synthesis of Al NPs through sonochemical decomposition of alane precursors in toluene with oleic acid as a capping agent or templating ligand. , They reported that the size of the Al NPs could be controlled by adjusting the ligand concentration, with higher concentrations yielding smaller Al core–shell NPs. Halas and co-workers reported the solvothermal synthesis of Al NPs from alane precursors also utilizing oleic acid as a capping agent, with tetrahydrofuran (THF) and 1,4-dioxane as an alternate solvent system. By varying the ratio of THF to 1,4-dioxane, they were able to synthesize Al NPs with sizes ranging from 70 to 220 nm, while maintaining all other reaction conditions constant. The synthesis of stable Al NPs across different ratios of THF to 1, 4-dioxane demonstrated that the particle size was directly related to the dielectric properties of the solvent, rather than its viscosity. Halas and co-workers expanded on this initial study and introduced a variety of reaction conditions leveraging different solvents and aluminum precursor combinations to produce a range of Al NPs with unique sizes and shapes. − However, these demonstrations are limited to small batches and are not suitable for large-scale manufacturing or continuous flow synthesis. As mentioned earlier, dilution is an effective strategy to control the exothermicity of the reaction and mitigate any undesirable oxidation concerns. Additionally, it can help reduce the level of agglomeration of the Al NPs product. Toward this end, we were interested in exploring the relationship between dilution factors toward particle yield and physical characteristics to identify reaction conditions that would be favorable for scaling up the process.

We also evaluated the potential of using Al NPs for SERS applications in detecting chemical warfare agents (CWAs) in the UV region, as Al NPs exhibit significant absorption over a wide range of wavelengths, from UV to NIR. It has been previously reported that Au and Ag nanoparticle-based SERS sensors can detect CWAs in the visible region, including common nerve agents such as soman, tabun, sarin, and cyclosarin. , These nerve agents are highly toxic and can be fatal to humans even with minimal exposure. , To the best of our knowledge, there are no reports in the literature regarding Al NPs-based sensors for detecting CWAs. Therefore, developing Al NP-based SERS sensors that operate in the UV to visible region is important for detecting a broad spectrum of CWAs. Previous studies have shown that Al NPs with different sizes and shapes can significantly enhance the Raman signals of molecules, including biological molecules, which are often challenging to characterize due to their weak signals using most modern characterization techniques. , Dimethyl methylphosphonate (DMMP) is a surrogate compound for CWAs, sharing a similar phosphonate group structure. Al NPs have the potential to serve as a cost-effective alternative SERS substrate for detecting trace amounts of CWAs across a wide range of wavelengths. This capability is essential for developing improved sensors capable of early detection of a broad range of CWAs.

In this article, we investigated the impact of varying concentrations of alane N,N-dimethylethylamine (alane DMEAA), titanium­(IV) isopropoxide (Ti-i-Pr) catalyst, and oleic acid (OA) on the synthesis of Al NPs. The initial synthesis of Al NPs was carried out by heating a mixture of alane DMEAA, Ti-i-Pr catalyst, and OA ligand at 80 °C, using 35 mL of toluene. Three additional syntheses were carried out under the same experimental conditions and with the same molar amounts of alane DMEAA, Ti-i-Pr, and OA, but with different volumes of toluene: 135, 235, and 335 mL, respectively. Four different Al NPs were synthesized with dilution factors and extensively characterized by using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The SERS activity was evaluated by separately depositing Rhodamine 6G (R6G) and DMMP onto silicon substrates coated with the as synthesized Al NPs. While the Al NPs demonstrated only modest SERS activity in the UV region for these molecules, these preliminary results confirm their potential as a low-cost substrate for SERS applications in this excitation region. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the synthesis of active Al NPs at different reagent concentrations by varying solvent volumes and to investigate their SERS activity in the UV region using a surrogate compound for CWAs.

Results and Discussion

Four batches of Al NPs were synthesized using different dilution factors by varying the total volume of anhydrous toluene, as described in the experimental section and shown in Table . All other reaction conditions, including the temperature and stirring speed, were held constant. The final volumes of anhydrous toluene in the four reaction flasks were 35, 135, 235, and 335 mL, respectively. Compared to our standard synthesis with 35 mL of anhydrous toluene, the reactions with 135, 235, and 335 mL of toluene represented dilution factors of 3.6, 6.4, and 9.3, respectively. Various research groups have investigated how different experimental factors, such as reaction temperatures, capping agent concentration, types of capping agents, solvent ratios, and Ti-i-Pr catalyst concentration, influence the growth, morphology, and size distribution of Al NPs. This paper addresses the role of reactant concentration (e.g., alane DMEAA, Ti-i-Pr, and OA), and its effect on the synthesis and properties of Al NPs when diluted with anhydrous toluene. Simple dilution offers an affordable, safe, and straightforward approach for large-scale production of Al NPs, including in flow synthesis. Utilizing low concentrations of Al NPs helps reduce the risk of unwanted pyrophoric or exothermic reactions. Al NPs synthesized using four different dilution factors with anhydrous toluene were characterized by microscopy, thermal analysis, and spectroscopy to evaluate their morphology, size distribution, chemical composition (both surface and bulk), and thermal behavior. To assess the reproducibility, the synthesis was repeated multiple times for each dilution condition, and the resulting samples were systematically analyzed.

All four Al NP samples were analyzed by using TEM microscopy to assess their morphology and size distribution. Figure shows the TEM images of the Al NPs, which were prepared by dispersion in 1-butanol using simple sonication followed by drop-casting onto carbon-coated TEM copper grids with a mesh size of 400. TEM images indicated that all four Al NP samples primarily consist of spherical nanoparticles. Additionally, a few cubic and triangular-shaped Al NPs were observed in each sample. The size distribution analysis was conducted for all four Al NP samples, each prepared using different dilution factors, based on multiple TEM images per sample. The resulting bar charts, along with fitted distribution curves, are presented in Figure S1. Size distribution analysis revealed that the undiluted Al NPs (regular) and those diluted by factors of 3.6, 6.4, and 9.3 had average sizes of 45, 40, 55, and 40 nm, respectively. TEM analysis revealed that variations in the concentrations of alane DMEAA, Ti-i-Pr, and OA did not significantly influence the overall growth of the Al NPs. However, the observed variations in the size distribution among the samples are attributed to differences in nucleation and growth kinetics arising from changes in the chemical environment during synthesis. These alterations likely affect key factors, such as precursor decomposition rates, surface passivation mechanisms, and nanoparticle ripening processes under varying reaction conditions.

XRD analysis was performed to determine the crystallinity and phase of the Al NPs. As shown in Figure , the narrow XRD peaks in all four samples indicate that the Al NPs are crystalline. ,, The peaks were identified as face-centered cubic (fcc) aluminum. ,, The broad peak observed at 2θ = 20° is attributed to amorphous carbon (from the organic molecule oleic acid). A peak related to amorphous carbon is visible in all of the Al NP samples, regardless of the dilution factors. The intensity of both the amorphous and fcc peaks of Al NPs in the XRD analysis (Figure ) slightly decreased with increasing dilution factors. Figure e shows the XRD data for Al2O3 peaks after the oxidation of undiluted Al NPs (regular) up to 1000 °C. The powder XRD peaks in Figure e correspond to the different phases of Al2O3 formed between 550 to 1000 °C.

XRD comparison of Al NPs synthesized with different dilution factors: Regular undiluted (a), dilution factor of 3.6 (b), dilution factor of 6.4 (c), and dilution factor of 9.3 (d). After burning regular undiluted Al NPs up to 1000 °C, they converted them to aluminum oxides (e).

The sizes of Al NPs at each dilution factor were calculated by using the Debye–Scherrer equation in Origin software, as shown in Figure S2. For this particle size estimation, the first three prominent peaks in each XRD spectrum, corresponding to 2θ values of 38.5, 44.8, and 65.2°, were selected for analysis. These peaks were used in the diameter calculations, and the average particle size was subsequently determined. Compared to the size distribution obtained from TEM analysis, the Debye–Scherrer-derived sizes were consistently smaller across all four Al NP samples. This discrepancy is primarily due to the fact that the Debye–Scherrer method estimates only the size of the crystalline domains based on the broadening of XRD peaks, thereby excluding contributions from amorphous regions. In contrast, TEM captures the total particle size, encompassing both the crystalline core and any surrounding amorphous shell. Furthermore, factors such as lattice strain and the presence of multiple crystalline domains within individual nanoparticles may also contribute to the differences observed between the two measurement techniques.

FTIR analysis was performed to identify the organic functional groups on the surfaces of Al NPs. Figure a shows the FTIR spectrum of neat OA before its use in Al NP synthesis, which reveals two major peaks at ∼1700 and ∼2900 cm–1. The peak at ∼1700 cm–1 is associated with the carbonyl group of oleic acid, while the peaks around 2900 cm–1 correspond to the symmetric and asymmetric C–H stretching vibrations. Figure b–e show the FTIR spectra of the four different Al NPs synthesized with varying dilution factors, with the spectral features of these nanoparticles being quite similar. The peak around 1700 cm–1 disappeared in all four Al NPs, indicating that oleic acid interacts with the surface of the Al NPs. A new broad peak appeared in the range of 3200–3600 cm–1 in all Al NPs samples, indicating the presence of −OH stretching mode. The disappearance of the carbonyl peak, combined with the emergence of the new −OH stretching mode, suggests that the carboxylic acid group of oleic acid has coordinated with the surface of the Al NPs through the carboxylate (COO–) group. This claim is supported through the presence of new peaks that appeared at ∼1383 and ∼1512 cm–1 in the FTIR spectra (Figure S3) for all of the Al NP samples. These peaks are attributed to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of carboxylate groups. It is widely accepted that the surface of Al NPs interacts with the carboxylic acid group, forming bridging, monodentate, and bidentate configurations. The FTIR analysis also confirmed that Al-OA core–shell nanoparticles were formed in all four syntheses, showing no noticeable differences. Therefore, dilution with toluene does not appear to affect the nucleation, growth, or formation of Al-OA core–shell nanoparticles.

TGA was performed in air to investigate the thermal behavior of Al NPs at various temperatures. Prior to analysis, all four Al NP samples were dried in a vacuum oven. During TGA analysis, the samples were heated from 25 to 900 °C in air (ramp rate 10 °C/min), as shown in Figure and Table . The weight loss observed between 25 and 200 °C is attributed to the evaporation of residual solvents in the Al NP samples. All four Al NP samples, synthesized with different dilution factors, contained residual organic solvents in the range of approximately 11–16%, as shown in Table . OA interacts with the active Al core, forming a carboxylate bond and creating a shell structure, as explained in the FTIR analysis. The weight loss observed in the temperature range of 200 to 500 °C is correlated to the combustion of the organic component of the shell. As shown in Table (column 3), all four Al NP samples exhibited similar weight percentages for the organic portion, approximately 20–22%. Concurrently, within the same temperature range of 200 to 500 °C, the amorphous Al2O3 layer surrounding the Al core formed. The amorphous Al2O3 layer reaches its maximum thickness of ∼4 nm, which is considered the critical thickness for the amorphous Al2O3 layer, around 550 °C. Upon reaching its critical thickness, the amorphous Al2O3 layer becomes metastable and converts to γ-Al2O3, which has a higher density and smaller crystalline size compared to the amorphous Al2O3. , The conversion of amorphous Al2O3 to γ-Al2O3 creates new passageways in the Al2O3 shell, as γ-Al2O3 cannot fully cover the entire active aluminum core. Air can pass through these newly formed passageways, leading to further oxidation of the active Al core. The weight gain observed between 550 and 650 °C (as shown in column 2 of Table ) is attributed to the oxidation of the Al core, facilitated by the newly formed passageways in the Al2O3 shell. The Al2O3 shell underwent further phase changes when the temperature increased beyond 650 °C. γ-Al2O3 converted to the θ and δ phases (intermediate phases) of Al2O3 at temperatures between 600 and 900 °C. ,, The θ and δ phases of Al2O3 have a higher density than γ-Al2O3 and also create new passageways in the Al2O3 shell. As air passes through these openings, it further oxidizes the Al core. Above 900 °C, thermodynamically stable α-Al2O3 forms, which has the highest density compared to other phases of Al2O3. The TGA curves of all four Al NPs showed additional weight gains after 650 °C due to further oxidation of the Al core, resulting from the formation of θ, δ, and α phases of Al2O3 (column 6, Table ). The total weight gains from 500 to 900 °C for all four Al NPs, synthesized with different dilution factors, are shown in the last column of Table . These weight gains are associated with the phase transitions (γ, θ, δ, and α) that occurred in the Al NPs after 500 °C. The undiluted Al NPs (regular) sample, synthesized without any toluene dilution, exhibited the highest weight gain percentage (19.2%) and the highest active Al content. In contrast, the other three Al NPs, synthesized with different dilution factors, showed weight gains ranging from 12.8 to 16.3%, which were slightly lower than that of the undiluted regular sample. The thermal analysis for all four Al NP samples confirmed that the nucleation, growth, and size distribution of the Al NPs were not significantly affected by the dilution factors (solvent volumes) used in their syntheses.

All four Al NPs were further analyzed with the EDS. In EDS, the samples are excited with an electron beam, causing core electrons to be ejected from the atoms. Outer-shell electrons move to fill these vacancies, releasing energy in the form of X-rays. The resulting spectrum is characteristic of the original atoms. The EDS analysis was used to evaluate the elemental composition and estimate the weight percentages of each element in the sample. This technique allows for the determination of the relative elemental composition and concentrations of different elements by analyzing a few micrometers (∼1–5 μm) beneath the sample’s surface. All four Al NP samples were dispersed in 1-butanol by sonicating for 30 s and then drop-cast onto carbon-coated TEM grids. The grids were allowed to dry in air overnight before being analyzed using a Gemini SEM500 instrument with EDS measurement capability. Table shows the weight percentages of carbon, oxygen, and aluminum. Representative EDS spectra for all four Al NP samples, prepared using different dilution factors, are shown in the Supporting Information as Figure S4. As shown in Table , all four Al NPs samples contained approximately 70% aluminum, 20% carbon, and 10% oxygen. The highest proportion of the active Al content was found in the regular Al NPs synthesized without any dilution. The TGA analysis showed a similar trend with the regular Al NP sample exhibiting the highest active Al content. After the four Al NPs were synthesized with different dilution factors, they were thoroughly washed several times with hexane to remove any unreacted OA and Ti-i-Pr catalyst. As a result, the Al NP samples were nearly free of unreacted OA and Ti-i-Pr after the washing process. The percentages of carbon and oxygen observed in the EDS analysis are associated with the shell structure surrounding the active Al core, which is primarily results from the OA. As noted earlier, the FTIR spectra of all four samples showed two new peaks at ∼1383 and ∼1512 cm–1, corresponding to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrational modes of the carboxylate groups. Both EDS and FTIR analyses confirmed that the shell structure of the Al NP samples is primarily composed of OA, regardless of the dilution factors used during their synthesis.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is considered one of the most versatile and widely used technique for analyzing the surface of nanoparticles. , In XPS, Al NP samples are exposed to soft X-rays with energies below 6 keV. This irradiation causes the ejection of core electrons, which are known as photoelectrons. The kinetic energy of these emitted photoelectrons is then measured by the XPS instrument. The XPS analysis and atomic compositions of each element in all four Al NP samples are shown in Table . Compared to EDS, which provides elemental composition information from a depth of about 1–5 μm, XPS is focused on the surface and provides detailed composition information from depth of approximately 1–10 nm. In the XPS analysis, the atomic percentage of aluminum varies between ∼15 and ∼21% across four Al NP samples synthesized at different dilution factors (Table and Figure S5). The atomic percentage of carbon remains relatively consistent across all samples, ranging from about 47 to 50%. Meanwhile, the atomic weight percentage of oxygen varies from ∼30 to ∼34% for the four Al NP samples. The elemental compositions of aluminum, carbon, and oxygen are nearly identical across all four samples. This consistency further confirms that the surface compositions of the four Al NP samples are similar. The minor variations in the atomic percentages of elements between samples may be correlated to the slight differences in the surface composition of the Al NPs synthesized at different dilution factors. As the dilution factors increased, the total volume of toluene in each reaction also increased. Consequently, the concentrations of the reactants (alane DMEAA, Ti-i-Pr, and OA) decreased with higher dilution factors. In XPS analysis, we observed a trace amount of titanium (Ti), less than 1%, in all of the Al NP samples. This presence is due to the incomplete removal of residual Ti-i-Pr from the synthesis, despite the washing procedure with hexane. However, this trace amount of the leftover Ti catalyst did not affect the overall performance or properties of the Al NPs synthesized at different dilution factors.

XPS mapping was performed by using CASA XPS software for all Al NP samples synthesized with varying dilution factors. This analysis provides insights into the surface oxidation states and valences of aluminum. The Al 2p peak was selected for mapping due to its high sensitivity to the chemical environment of aluminum atoms. The Al 2p spectra exhibit a clear distinction between metallic aluminum and its oxidized forms. Specifically, the metallic Al (Al0) peak appears at lower binding energies with a narrower width, while the aluminum oxide peak is observed at higher binding energies with broader width and greater intensity. Figure S6 shows the XPS mapping results for the four Al NP samples synthesized under varying dilution conditions. All samples exhibit characteristic peaks corresponding to metallic Al (Al0) at lower binding energies and Al2O3 at higher binding energies and intensities. These findings suggest that the surfaces of all Al NP samples are primarily passivated by an aluminum oxide layer or an oxide layer derived from the oleic acid used during synthesis. Furthermore, the XPS data reveal that the Al2O3-to-Al0 ratio varies with the degree of dilution. The undiluted (regular) Al NP sample displays the highest intensity for the Al0 peak, indicating a greater relative abundance of metallic Al at the surface compared with the other three samples. In contrast, samples synthesized under higher dilution conditions show an increased contribution from Al2O3, suggesting slight modifications in the surface compositions under these conditions.

Al NPs SERS Activity

In SERS, the Raman scattering of a molecule is significantly enhanced by metal nanoparticles, such as Au, Ag, Pt, and Al. ,− It is widely acknowledged that the enhancement of the Raman signal is due to the interaction of incident light with the localized surface plasmons of the nanoparticles, which amplify the electromagnetic fields around them. The Al NPs exhibit a size- and shape-dependent strong absorption across a broad wavelength range, from UV to NIR. ,, UV–vis absorption spectroscopy was performed on Al NPs, synthesized at varying dilution factors, using quartz substrates as the measurement platform (Figure S7). Despite being passivated with an oleic acid shell, the Al NPs demonstrated poor dispersibility in organic solvents (Figure S7), precluding reliable solution-phase optical characterization. As a result, all absorption measurements were conducted in the solid state on the quartz substrates. The acquired spectra indicate that all four Al NP samples exhibit broad plasmonic absorption features extending from the visible through the UV spectral range. This broadband plasmonic response underscores a significant advantage of Al NPs over traditional plasmonic materials such as Ag and Au, whose localized surface plasmon resonances (LSPRs) are primarily confined to the visible region. It is widely reported that Al NPs significantly enhance the intensity of Raman signals for organic molecules, biological molecules and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). , For example, Al-polydopamine (PDA) nanoparticles were used to detect trace amounts of benzopyrene, a substance toxic to living organisms, using SERS techniques. PDA molecules served as capping agents around the active Al core to prevent premature oxidation and also provided a molecular capture layer for capturing benzopyrene in contaminated water.

SERS is a promising technique for detecting CWAs, including common nerve agents such as soman, tabun, sarin, and cyclosarin. These nerve agents are highly toxic and can be fatal to humans even with minimal exposure. , Among them, sarin is particularly lethal and can cause symptoms such as headaches, muscle pains, cramps, and potentially death. , DMMP is an organic compound that shares a similar phosphonate group with CWAs. However, DMMP lacks the fluorine or cyanate groups typically found in common CWAs. As a result, DMMP cannot bind to human cells to induce toxicity, making it a safe surrogate for mimicking these CWAs in SERS measurements. To investigate the SERS activity of Al NPs, we used a common fluorescent dye R6G and DMMP as model compounds. During sample preparation, Al NPs were coated onto Si substrate, the desired molecule was then deposited on top of the Al NPs, and the Raman spectrum was measured.

The R6G shows a prominent peak centered around 1620 cm–1 in the Raman spectrum, which is attributed to the aromatic C–C stretching vibration modes, as shown in Figures and S8. The intensity of this peak was compared between neat R6G (1 mM) and R6G deposited on Al NPs-coated Si substrates. Compared to R6G deposited onto a Si substrate, each R6G deposited onto a Si substrate coated with Al NPs (synthesized with different dilution factors) showed a moderate enhancement of the Raman signal at 1620 cm–1. The changes in the intensity of the peak around 1620 cm–1 indicate that Al NPs moderately amplified the Raman signals, demonstrating SERS activity. R6G deposited onto a Si substrate coated with regular Al NPs (without any dilution) showed an approximately 4.5-fold enhancement (enhancement factor) in the Raman signal. R6G molecules deposited on Si substrates coated with Al NPs synthesized using dilution factors of 3.6, 6.4, and 9.3 showed Raman signal enhancement factors of approximately 2.6, 2.7, and 2.2, respectively. Among the four types of Al NPs synthesized with different dilution factors, the regular Al NPs exhibited the strongest SERS activity. As discussed in the XPS section, the surface compositions of Al NPs synthesized with different dilution factors were slightly different. According to the XPS data, regular Al NPs synthesized without any dilution showed a slightly higher Al content on the nanoparticle surface compared to the other three types of Al NPs. Therefore, the variations in the SERS signal around 1620 cm–1 for the R6G molecules were attributed to the differences in surface composition among the Al NPs synthesized with different dilution factors.

The regular Al NPs synthesized without any dilution exhibited the highest SERS activity for DMMP, which is consistent with the results observed in the R6G study. Similar to the SERS analysis with R6G, variations in the SERS activity were observed for DMMP when deposited onto Al NPs synthesized with different dilution factors. As noted earlier, these differences are attributed to slight variations in the surface composition of each of the Al NP samples, as discussed in the XPS analysis.

It is well-established that the SERS activity of CWAs in the visible region is significantly enhanced by noble metal nanoparticles, such as Au and Ag. , Additionally, it has been reported that the SERS activity for toxic organic molecules, such as PAHs, in the UV to visible region is typically enhanced when these molecules are drop-cast onto Al NPs. However, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate SERS activity of Al NPs in the UV region for surrogates of CWAs, such as DMMP. Our preliminary studies suggest moderate SERS activity in the UV region (using 248.6 nm excitation wavelength) for DMMP and R6G when these molecules are drop-cast onto Al NPs. However, by modification of the properties of the Al NPs, such as size, surface chemistry, removal of organic capping agents, the use of alternative capping agents, and employing different synthesis methods, the SERS activity could be significantly enhanced. These improvements could enable the detection of CWAs and their surrogates as well as the identification of trace amounts of CWAs in the future battlefield scenarios.

Conclusions

We successfully synthesized Al-OA core–shell NPs with varying dilution factors while maintaining their chemical and physical properties. The TEM analysis revealed that all four Al NPs, synthesized with different dilution factors, have similar morphologies. The size distribution analysis indicated that the average diameter of the Al NPs ranges from 40 to 55 nm. TGA, EDS, and XPS analyses revealed that the bulk and surface chemical compositions of the Al NPs synthesized with different dilution factors differ slightly from those of the regular Al NPs synthesized without dilution. FTIR analysis of all four Al NPs showed no significant differences among the samples. It indicated that the OA interacts with the active aluminum surface through the carboxylate group in all four samples. The stability of the Al NPs under ambient conditions was assessed over a period of three months using the aforementioned characterization techniques. The nanoparticles exhibited no significant changes in their structural or chemical properties, indicating effective surface passivation and long-term resistance to oxidation under atmospheric conditions. All four samples exhibited modest SERS enhancement for both R6G and DMMP. The differences in the SERS activity among the Al NPs, synthesized with different dilution factors, are attributed to variations in their surface chemical compositions, particularly the Al content, as discussed in the XPS analysis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the SERS activity of Al NPs in the UV region for surrogates of CWAs. Additionally, synthesizing Al NPs with varying dilution factors is crucial for optimizing future scale-up processes, including those in flow chemistry systems. Scaling up the synthesis of Al NPs using conventional methods, such as in a round-bottom flask with larger volumes of alane DMEAA, Ti-i-Pr, and OA capping agents, is highly hazardous due to the pyrophoric nature of both alane DMEAA and Al NPs. Therefore, using lower concentrations helps reduce the risk of unwanted pyrophoric reactions and affords the opportunity to explore other synthetic approaches, such as continuous flow chemistry.

Materials and Methods

Materials

All chemicals were used as received without further purification unless otherwise indicated. Alane DMEAA complex solution in a 0.5 M toluene solution, Ti-i-Pr, (99.999% trace metal basis, OA (>99% GC), Toluene (anhydrous, 99.8%), 1-butanol (anhydrous, 99.8%), and Hexane (>99%, reagent plus) were all obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received.

Al NPs Synthesis (Regular-No Dilution)

A twenty-five mL portion of alane DMEAA was added to a 250 mL three-neck round-bottom flask under a constant nitrogen flow. Next, 0.1 mL of Ti-i-Pr was added, and the mixture was stirred at 300 rpm for 5 min. In a separate vial, 0.2 mL of OA was dissolved in 10 mL of anhydrous toluene. This solution was then added to a mixture of alane DMEAA and Ti-i-Pr. The concentrations of alane DMEAA, Ti-i-Pr, and OA in the working solutions were 0.360, 0.01, and 0.02 M, respectively. The final volume of toluene in the reaction flask was 35 mL. The mixture was heated to 80 °C for 3 h while stirring at 300 rpm and maintaining a continuous purge of nitrogen gas. During the synthesis, the flask was equipped with a glass Liebig condenser, which circulated cool water to prevent the evaporation of anhydrous toluene. After 3 h, the mixture was left in a fume hood until it reached room temperature. The sample was transferred to centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant in each tube was discarded, and the precipitate was washed with anhydrous hexane. The washing process with hexane was performed in three consecutive cycles to remove all chemical impurities (e.g., alkyl amines, unreacted catalyst, and unused OA) introduced during the synthesis of Al NPs. After washing, the samples were transferred from the centrifuge tubes to clean vials and dried by flowing nitrogen gas over them. The final Al NP samples were recovered as a dark gray colored powder.

Synthesis of Al NPs with Dilution Factors of 3.6, 6.4, and 9.3

Three separate syntheses of Al NPs were carried out following a procedure similar to that described above for Al NPs Synthesis (Regular-No Dilution) . The total volumes were increased to 135, 235, and 335 mL by diluting with anhydrous toluene (35 mL of toluene used in the synthesis of regular Al NPs), resulting in dilution factors of 3.6, 6.4, and 9.3, respectively. With the exception of the final volumes of toluene, all other experimental conditions were kept the same for all three syntheses of Al NPs. The concentrations of each chemical in the different syntheses, with their varying dilution factors, are shown in Table .

Measurements

FTIR spectroscopy was performed using a Bruker α-P spectrophotometer equipped with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) sample attachment. TEM images were captured with an FEI Talos (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Cneta camera at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. Samples were prepared by diluting a small amount of powdered sample in 1-butanol and then sonicating the mixture for a few minutes using a bath sonicator. The homogeneous solution was drop-cast onto carbon-coated, 400-mesh copper TEM grids. Raman spectroscopy was performed using a DUV Raman PL 200 spectrometer with a 248.6 nm excitation wavelength and a three-stage TE cooled (−35 °C), back-thinned, and back-illuminated UV CCD detector. TGA analysis was performed using a discovery TGA 5500 instrument from TA Instruments. The analysis was conducted in air with a temperature ramp up to 900 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C per minute. XRD data was collected on a Rigaku Smartlab diffractometer. The Cu–Kα (1.5406 oA) X-ray radiation source was operated at 40 V and 44 mA. Samples were evenly dispersed on a standard Si wafer from Rigaku. The samples were then transferred to a diffractometer and analyzed at room temperature. EDS measurements were performed using a Zeiss Gemini 500 SEM instrument with an EDS capability. The samples for EDS analysis were prepared in a manner similar to that used for TEM analysis. The well-dispersed Al NP samples in 1-butanol were drop-cast onto lacey carbon-coated copper grids with a mesh size of 400. XPS data was collected using a Scienta Omicron XPS Lab system with an Argus analyzer. High-resolution (survey) scans were acquired with 0.1(1.0) eV steps with a pass energy of 20(100) eV. The energy scale was calibrated against the Au 4f and Cu 2p3/2 lines at 84.0 and 932.6 eV, respectively.

SERS Measurements

Si substrates were cleaned thoroughly by washing with acetone and drying with nitrogen gas. Synthesized Al NPs with different dilution factors (at least ∼0.24 mg of Al NPs) were drop-cast onto Si substrates and allowed to dry overnight. For the Raman measurements, 10 μL of a 1 × 10–3 M R6G solution or 10 μL of DMMP was drop-cast onto Si substrates coated with Al NPs and allowed to dry in air overnight. Raman measurements were performed at 10 different spots on each sample of Al NPs synthesized with varying dilution factors. The spectra from these measurements were then averaged to obtain the final spectrum for each sample. Background subtraction was applied to all spectra presented. The measurements were conducted using a laser power of 18 A, pulses 500, and frequency of 60 Hz.
