Improving Compliance With Safe Sleep Guidelines for Vulnerable Patients in a Community Safety Net Hospital Neonatal Intensive Care Unit
Authors: Andrew Beverstock, Samantha Carlton, Hillary Lee, Thomas Lu, Kelsi Morgan, Ashlee Smith-Patel, Kelli Phillips, Joseph Hagan, Pablo Lohmann, Caraciolo Fernandes, Lisa Owens, Daniela Dinu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Modeling safe sleep in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) increases the likelihood that parents will follow these practices after discharge. Many NICU infants are put to sleep unsafely, and unsafe sleep practices are the leading cause of nonnatural death among children locally. We created safe sleep standards for infants older than 32 weeks based on national guidelines: infants should be placed supine in a flat, empty crib and should never be swaddled loosely. We aimed to increase the weekly percentage compliance with safe sleep recommendations from a baseline of 58% (0.58 compliant) to 85% (0.85 compliant) by March 2025 in our level II and III NICUs based on random crib audits.Methods:In plan-do-study-act cycle 1, we educated residents and fellows about safe sleep practices in the NICU and changed our daily electronic medical record smart phrase to integrate safe sleep readiness. In PSDA cycle 2, we educated nurses about safe sleep and implemented nonrecline cribs. In plan-do-study-act cycle 3, we implemented bedside signs for each crib with reminders of our standards.Results:We successfully achieved our aim. Our mean compliance increased from 58% (0.58 compliant) at baseline to 91% (0.91 compliant) postintervention.Conclusions:Our project successfully improved compliance with safe sleep practices of the current nursing staff: our interventions moving forward will focus on embedding safe sleep practices into our NICU culture so that our changes are sustainable. Our ongoing interventions will be promoting “nursing safe sleep champions,” continuing to perform regular audits, and further implementing the use of sleep sacks instead of loose swaddles.

INTRODUCTION

Problem Description

Infant sleep-related deaths (SRDs) are an important cause of nonnatural child death, defined as death caused by external factors rather than by disease or body anomalies. In Harris County (which encompasses Houston, Texas, and several surrounding suburbs), infant SRDs accounted for 78% of nonnatural deaths among infants between 2008 and 2017.1 Prone sleeping position and surface sharing are major contributors to the risk of SRD.2 There are also significant racial and ethnic disparities in rates of SRD, with the highest rates among non-Hispanic Black and American Indian infants.3 Prematurity is also a significant risk factor.4

Available Knowledge

The 2022 American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guidelines5 for promoting safe sleep recommended using a firm, flat sleep surface and avoiding loose swaddling. These guidelines also emphasize early education and recommend that all staff “endorse and model safe infant sleep guidelines.”

The AAP produces specific guidelines for transitioning preterm infants to safe sleep for discharge.6 Extremely preterm infants in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) may not be ready to follow safe sleep practices from birth as they typically require respiratory support and are often placed prone or swaddled for developmental positioning. They should transition to safe sleep before discharge.

Previous research has shown that modeling safe sleep practices and providing education while in the NICU encourages parents to continue with safe sleep practices at home.7 Nurses are particularly influential8 as they control an infant’s sleeping position during hospitalization.

Despite this, many NICU infants do not sleep safely. Many nurses are skeptical of their influence over parental behavior.9 Previous studies have revealed nursing concerns about supine sleep, including infant comfort and concern about aspiration.10 NICU patients are continuously monitored, so a prone sleeping position may be safe in this setting; however, monitoring is not recommended for parents at home. Modeling this behavior may mislead parents into believing that this is the recommended sleeping position, despite the strong association with SRD.

Previous quality improvement (QI) projects have successfully improved safe sleep compliance.11–14 Successful interventions included implementation of wearable swaddles on the unit (though these are often expensive for families and may not be available in a resource-limited health system) and home sleep environment screenings before discharge.

Rationale

Given the strong influence of modeling safe sleep, it is vital to implement safe sleep as soon as it is developmentally and medically appropriate for the infant. For most patients, this is around 32 weeks postmenstrual age. We noticed that infants in our unit were frequently sleeping unsafely. Our institution had an existing policy of “implementing safe sleep after 34 weeks,” but there was no written documentation of specific standards. This was implemented only as a single Epic medical record order placed at admission, which frequently expired by the time it became applicable.

Specific Aim

We aimed to improve compliance with a bundle of safe sleep standard practices for infants 32 weeks’ postmenstrual age or older on minimal respiratory support from a baseline of 58% (0.58 compliant) to 85% (0.85 compliant) within 6 months based on weekly random crib audits.

METHODS

This single-center project adhered to the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) 2.0 guidelines.15 Our project was deemed institutional review board (IRB) exempt by the Baylor College of Medicine IRB. We applied the Institute for Healthcare Improvement Model for Improvement framework to the project. No funding was received.

Context

This project was completed at a community hospital in Houston, Texas. The hospital is part of Harris Health, a county hospital system that acts as a healthcare safety net, with a significant portion of uninsured patients from areas of high socioeconomic deprivation. The hospital is staffed by neonatologists, pediatric hospitalists, pediatric residents, and neonatal fellows. There are 5,494 deliveries per year. Among these deliveries, 17.9% are White, 22.5% are African American, 52.3% are Hispanic/Latino, and 7.3% are Asian or other. Uninsured patients comprise 44% of the population, and 22.6% are covered by Medicaid. The infants in our NICU population are at particularly high risk of SRD.

Infants from a gestational age of 23–36 weeks are admitted to either a 20-bed level II or 18-bed level III unit. Nurses in the level II unit cover 4–6 babies and 2–3 in the level III unit. Nurses work 12-hour shifts. Our NICU is small with relatively low nursing turnover, resulting in a close-knit culture where a small multidisciplinary team frequently works together.

Our hospital system uses Epic as our electronic medical record (EMR) (Epic Systems, Madison, WI). We have standardized note templates, which are used by all residents.

Safe Sleep Standards

We formed a QI collaborative consisting of neonatal fellows, attendings, and an education nurse specialist. Based on the AAP safe sleep recommendations, we designed a set of 5 “safe sleep standards.” These were the following:

After surveying nursing staff and occupational therapists, this guidance was applied to all infants 32 weeks postmenstrual age or older who were on “minimal respiratory support” (room air, low-flow nasal cannula, or high-flow nasal cannula ≤4 L/min). This definition covered all infants in our level II unit and a variable proportion of our level III infants.

Measures and Data Collection

We conducted a series of bedside audits in August and September 2024. We manually evaluated every baby in both the level II and III NICU units to determine whether safe sleep standards were being met. Audits were performed on both day and night shifts. Infants could be audited more than once per week if they remained in the NICU, but a maximum of once per day.

If an infant was not swaddled, we checked their sleeping position, the presence of additional items in the crib, and whether the bed was flat, and counted the total number of the 3 safe sleep standards that were met for each infant. If swaddled, we also evaluated whether the swaddle was tight or laid loosely over the top and whether the infant’s face was covered. Our primary project aim was the proportion of the 5 total safe sleep standards that were followed. To produce our control charts, we used the total number of safe sleep standards met for all infants that week as our numerator and the total number of possible sleep standards that could be met as our denominator. A second project goal (aim) was the proportion of infants who achieved compliance with all safe sleep practice elements (perfect safe sleep).

Due to pending IRB review, we only collected limited baseline data before beginning our interventions. We collected 4 weeks of baseline data. After our project was deemed IRB exempt, we conducted weekly bedside audits of all eligible infants. These audits were conducted by neonatology fellows and took place on different days of the week, depending on team member availability, and were discreetly completed at random times during shifts. This ensured that staff could not prepare ahead of time to falsely elevate compliance. Infants were not audited if they were being held by their family members.

These audits required being on-site at the hospital and were labor intensive due to the need to manually collect data. After completion of our second plan-do-study-act (PDSA) cycle, which showed sustained improvement, we switched to performing monthly audits. Data were entered manually into an online form that linked to a secure and anonymous spreadsheet to allow easy data collection and storage. We surveyed a total of 575 “sleep events.”

Interventions

We created a Pareto chart to determine the most important factors preventing safe sleep compliance (Fig. 1). This identified head-of-bed elevation and extra items in the crib as the 2 main reasons for noncompliance, with those 2 being responsible for 62% of all noncompliance.

Pareto chart displaying the reasons for infants not being safe sleep compliant in our NICU.

Our team created a fishbone diagram that helped identify factors that may have contributed to noncompliance (Fig. 2). The most important factor was nursing attitudes and provider buy-in, especially with beliefs surrounding the need for the head-of-bed elevation to address reflux precautions, a practice that is not recommended by the AAP.

Fishbone diagram examining barriers to following safe sleep guidance while in the NICU.

We created a key driver diagram to guide our team on potential interventions and an organized approach to the project implementation (Fig. 3).

Aim and key driver diagram showing factors affecting safe sleep compliance and key interventions to achieve the project aim.

Our safe sleep project coincided with the primary author redeveloping the admission and daily progress note templates. To capitalize on this, our first intervention was targeted toward physicians to integrate safe sleep into our unit workflow. We changed our monthly unit introduction to include safe sleep education. We also created a refreshable Smart List within our note templates, which acted as a daily reminder to review an infant’s safe sleep eligibility. Our residents present on rounds by reading the progress note, so this change made safe sleep a daily discussion for every baby.

Our second intervention focused on educating and empowering our nursing staff and promoting culture change. We provided a signature-recorded “in-service” teaching session on the new safe sleep standards to more than 80% of nurses in both units. We emphasized the key influence that nurses have as part of modeling safe sleep and highlighted the high risk of SRD for our patients.

Our third intervention involved a change in our cribs. Previously, we had used cribs that could be inclined to 30 degrees, making it easy to elevate the head of the bed. These were replaced with plastic bassinets, which could not be easily inclined. This “forced function” was highly effective because it prevented nurses from placing infants in a reclined sleeping position without having to use the crib in a nonrecommended way, which removed the possibility of error and was one of our most effective interventions.

Our fourth intervention was the creation of a sign (Fig. 4) that was placed at the end of the bed for every eligible infant. We solicited feedback from nurses on its content and design. We included the standards in both English and Spanish and reviewed the translation with native speakers to ensure that it was easily understandable. We piloted this sign with a small number of infants in the level III unit first, per local policies, and rolled it out to both units after approximately 10 weeks.

Bedside sign indicating safe sleep standards in both English and Spanish.

Analysis

Results were anonymized and stored locally according to institutional data storage policies. Statistical process control P-charts were created from the data using Excel and Minitab and analyzed using the Nelson rules.16

RESULTS

Our baseline data indicated a stable system with low rates of safe sleep within our units. The timings of our interventions are shown (Fig. 1). Compliance increased from a baseline mean of 58% (0.58 proportion compliant) to 91% (0.91 proportion compliant) by our follow-up period.

We evaluated how many infants were following “perfect safe sleep” standards. Figure 2 is a P-chart that displays the proportion of infants achieving “perfect safe sleep.” The proportion of “perfect safe sleep” increased from an average of 0.09–0.56 in the postintervention period.

DISCUSSION

Our project was successful in achieving our aim of more than 85% (0.85 proportion compliant) adherence to safe sleep guidance within a 6-month period. We achieved a significant shift, with all postintervention data points falling above the mean. All but one of our postintervention data points fell within our postintervention upper and lower control limits, indicating stability. After formal introduction of our bedside sign, we had 9 points above the new baseline mean, indicating a second significant shift, as displayed in Figure 5.

P-chart showing the proportion of all bundle elements compliant with safe sleep guidance.

We achieved a significant shift in both the proportion of our guidelines being followed and “perfect safe sleep”; however, many infants still had 1 metric that was not followed, so our improvement was less pronounced, as demonstrated on the P-chart (Fig. 6). During project planning, we discussed what metric to use: (1) looking at all bundle components or (2) only giving full credit for “perfect safe sleep.” We ultimately decided to look at the average of all components to allow “partial credit,” thus allowing us to detect smaller changes. We believe the advantage of taking this approach is that staff receive positive feedback for making partial changes, which we believed would increase compliance with the project. The disadvantage of this approach is that an infant is not sleeping fully safely unless they are following all the standards, and giving partial credit may falsely imply that this is safe sleep. We did not track “perfect safe sleep” as a measure until the end of our intervention period: Figure 5 was created retrospectively after our interventions had been implemented, and therefore, only the data from Figure 6 were used to guide interventions as part of our PDSA cycles. We would encourage others conducting similar projects to evaluate both measures throughout their project and to focus on “perfect safe sleep” as an end outcome while using process measures throughout to help guide interventions.

P-chart showing the proportion of infants compliant with achieving “perfect safe sleep.”

Compliance with a flat crib and an empty crib showed the most improvement in our project. Compliance with keeping the bed flat increased from 51% to 86%, whereas compliance with keeping the bed empty increased from 47% to 80% (data not displayed).

There were several key factors that led to this project’s success. We focused on changing our unit workflow to integrate safe sleep into daily rounds, thus embedding safe sleep practices into our unit culture. Using a forced function of changing the cribs so that the head of the bed could not be inclined significantly improved safe sleep bundle compliance. This simple QI principle of making the desired choice the default choice is a very powerful and effective strategy.17 We also designed for sustainability by changing note templates, which means that our improvements are more likely to persist as different staff members rotate through our NICUs. Prior research has shown EMR template changes are effective QI tools to achieve change, for example, improving subspecialty follow-up after discharge18 or improving documentation thoroughness.19 This was easy to implement and was effective because we used a standardized template across multiple units. Our project also showed that safe sleep guidance can be successfully implemented as infants grow. We chose a postmenstrual age of 32 weeks to begin safe sleep implementation, and many infants in our unit became eligible for safe sleep as they aged and were weaned from respiratory support. Using an EMR template that can be reselected as an infant meets criteria and is easy to implement. Napolitano et al20 successfully used a decision flowchart to help guide staff on when to trial safe sleep for infants.

Our biggest barrier was influencing nursing attitudes: the use of “reflux precautions” with an inclined head of bed was standard practice in our NICU despite limited evidence of efficacy. We initially faced significant resistance to changing this practice. Many QI projects do not lead to improvement because they do not access sufficient sources of influence to change behavior.21 Our project benefited from the small unit size. We have a limited number of nurses, with low staff turnover. We involved a high proportion of unit nurses on our project and had individual discussions with each nurse about their concerns. Our project suggests that it may be easier to drive culture change at smaller units with fewer staff than at large academic centers. The close working relationships between physicians and nurses at smaller community sites mean that interventions targeting culture change may be more successful. QI teams should consider unit size when deciding which interventions to implement. One previous project by Frey et al22 showed a 12.5% improvement in safe sleep practice adherence when they were able to educate 60% of the nurses in their unit. In larger units with high staff turnover, implementing education into staff induction and group education may be a more resource-effective strategy.

Other projects have been published that provide complementary methods for improving safe sleep compliance in different contexts. Molina et al23 evaluated safe sleep practices in a tertiary children’s hospital outside of critical care settings. We used similar audit standards based on the AAP guidance to create our safe sleep guidelines. They looked at percentage adherence to safe sleep guidelines and “perfect safe sleep” as separate measures to track their improvement. Similar to our findings, they found that additional items in the bed and the head of bed being elevated were the 2 most common reasons for noncompliance. This further emphasizes that these are key areas to focus on for safe sleep projects in all contexts.

Salada et al24 looked at safe sleep at a quaternary hospital without deliveries. This team successfully used Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA) as their QI tool. Unlike our PDSA cycle–based QI method, which identifies defects in a process and makes reactive changes to processes in response to deficiencies, FMEA aims to identify potential errors before they occur and can be a highly effective QI tool.25 Using a key driver diagram in our project aimed to follow similar FMEA principles, as we identified barriers to improvement as the basis for our interventions. The study by Salada et al24 reinforced the effectiveness of our interventions, including staff education and the use of a bedside safe sleep sign.

The context of our hospital project was different from many previously published safe sleep papers in that we implemented changes at a resource-limited community hospital serving a high-risk population. A project by Shaikh et al26 at a community hospital included term babies in the newborn nursery, which we did not include. Providing sleep sacks was their most effective intervention, which we did not have available but would consider as a future intervention. Our project shows that implementing these interventions is possible even in smaller community units.

Macklin et al11 completed a multihospital project with a much larger patient population. Their outcome measure was a reduction in infant SRD for patients after discharge. In Harris County, SRD data are not available to individual hospitals, and so we were unable to use this, but their multicenter project shows the feasibility of measuring this outcome for large hospital systems. They also used a safe sleep screening tool in the EMR, which would make data collection significantly easier.11 We were unable to alter the EMR beyond SmartPhrase templates, which limited our ability to do this; however, we would encourage others considering similar projects to use a safe sleep audit tool that is embedded into the EMR.

SRDs are rare, so proving an impact from our project would be challenging in a single community hospital, although this would be an ideal outcome measure with a multihospital system. We would have also ideally followed infants after discharge to determine whether parents were more likely to have followed safe sleep practices at home. Most infants in our system are followed by a separate pediatric group after discharge. Shadman et al27 did not show statistically significant improvements in caregiver home safe sleep practices after discharge; however, they used surveys and had a low response rate (46%) so may have missed small improvements. For a project targeting a reduction in a rare outcome such as SRD, a larger follow-up population may be needed to show a significant improvement.27

An important limitation of our project was our limited baseline data collection. Ideally, we would have collected more baseline data to strengthen our analysis; however, we were limited by local policies on data collection, and we could not collect data retrospectively due to the nature of our project. Ensuring an adequate period of data collection is important to assess the effectiveness of QI interventions.

Overall, this was an effective project. We successfully implemented safe sleep guidelines into our units and were able to sustain our change. Our interventions were easy to implement, did not require funding, and are easily generalizable to other small hospital NICUs.
