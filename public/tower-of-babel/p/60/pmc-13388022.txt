Gut Integrity Biomarkers and Parasitic Infections in Indonesian Children: A Cross-Sectional Analysis
Authors: Alpha Fardah Athiyyah, Andy Darma, Reza Gunadi Ranuh, Khadijah Rizky Sumitro, Ingrid S Surono, Subijanto Marto Sudarmo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeGastrointestinal integrity in young population warrants special attention. Infectious pathogens may induce changes in intestinal microbiota, facilitating gut permeability. This study aimed to investigate the gut integrity and inflammatory markers in children with parasitic and non-parasitic infection in Kupang and North Kodi, Indonesia.MethodsA cross-sectional study assessed the anthropometric measurement, socio-demographic factors and personal hygiene practices. Stool samples for helminthic and protozoan infections were analysed using standard microscopy, while blood samples for gut integrity (intestinal fatty acid-binding protein [I-FABP] and fatty acid-binding protein 6 [FABP6]) and inflammatory markers (soluble cluster of differentiation 14 [sCD14] and soluble cluster of differentiation 163 [sCD163]) were assessed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit.ResultsAs many as 80 stool samples taken from the children with age of 36–45 months to examine gut parasites in East Nusa Tenggara. Thirty-three from 80 children have intestinal parasites infection and 5 of them infected with 2 types of parasites. A total of 38 intestinal parasites were found with 47.37% protozoa and 52.63% helminths. The most predominant parasites found are Giardia lamblia (21.1%) for protozoans and Trichuris trichiura (26.3%) for helminths. Furthermore, significantly, the median levels of gut integrity biomarkers concentration were higher in non-parasitic group compared to parasitic group, as follows I-FABP 99.50 ng/mL (33.81–393.39); FABP6 56.13 ng/mL (2.43–234.69); sCD14 4.87 ng/mL (1.98–15.06) and sCD163 17.338 ng/mL (1.98–60.92) and significant in FABP6 and sCD14 (p=0.014; 0.001 respectively).ConclusionIn Kupang and North Kodi, intestinal parasitic infections remain a significant concern. The elevated markers of gut integrity and inflammation biomarkers in children with non-parasitic infection are quite concerning and need additional research to justify the causality.

INTRODUCTION

A healthy gastrointestinal tract carries fundamental role in health and immune function as the primary defense mechanism against pathogens such as parasites, viruses, and digestive bacteria. Hence, gastrointestinal tract integrity (GI) in young population requires particular awareness [1]. Pathogens that cause infections triggers alterations in the composition of the microbiota as consequently infectious pathogens will affect nutrient absorption and the body’s immune response [2]. Bacterial translocation is known as the initial stage for the entry of metabolites, endotoxins, and peptidoglycans, into the intestine and distant tissues and organs [3]. Destruction of the intestinal epithelial barrier found to positively correlate with increased intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP) in plasma due to parasitic infection, which may be related to damage to epithelial cells, indicates the production of cytokines. Increase in permeability of the GI tract may cause inflammation and dysbiosis [4]. Soluble cluster of differentiation 163 (sCD163) previously found as a inflammatory biomarker in parasitic infection like Leishmaniasis [5]. Even though sCD14 is commonly used to predict mortality of people with human-immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [6], the high level sCD14 may cause by the long-term effects of enteric parasitic infection [7]. FABP6 is one of the FABP family that highly expressed in tapeworm infection [8].

Gastrointestinal parasitic infections remain a significant epidemiological issue, especially in rural and developing regions, and an urgent public health concern, affecting millions globally [9]. As stated by 2014 report by the World Health Organization, there are more than 3.5 billion people experienced intestinal helminths infection, accentuating extensive social and economic devastation amongst rural communities in developing countries [10], and the highest prevalence are occurs both in tropical and sub-tropical regions [11]. Lower income population and poor sanitation facilitating the transmission of these infections, hence the prevalence rate in rural area particularly high [12], specifically young population in East Nusa Tenggara, Indonesia. These infections may cause to severe gastrointestinal complications and have long-term health consequences, impacting their growth, development, and overall well-being [13].

However, studies investigating the difference of gut integrity biomarkers between parasitic infection and non-infectious children is scant. Therefore, the aim is to understand the prevalence of gastrointestinal infections from parasite and as well as sociodemographic condition, and gut integrity biomarkers among children.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and ethical clearance

This study conducted on 80 children aged 36 to 45 months in Kupang and North Kodi, East Nusa Tenggara, Indonesia. This research was conducted in October to December 2021. This research is a part of previous cross-sectional research entitled, “Gut Microbiota Differences in Stunted and Normal Weight Children Aged 36–45 Months in East Nusa Tenggara, Indonesia.” The study ethic was established by the Ethics Committee of the Research Institute of YARSI University, Jakarta, Indonesia. The ethics registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with identifier number of 332/KEP-UY/BIA/X/2021. Informed consents were received orally and written to caregivers, then proceed signed a letter of consent before the children were included as participant. The design and ethical clearance of this study was similar with previous study by Athiyyah et al. [13].

Subject characteristics

The survey was undertaken utilizing structured questionnaire by interviews directly with the primary caregivers to gather sociodemographic data and evaluate hygiene practices. Sociodemographic data that collected for this research are sex (male and female); living environment (urban area which is Kupang and rural area which is North Kodi); nutritional status based on anthropometric measurement with weight-for-height Z-score (normal, wasted, and severely wasted); source of drinking water (mineral water, spring, tap water, and well water include as clean water; while unclean water are rainwater collection), type of sanitation facility (latrine with septic tank and without septic tank), also availability of handwashing facility.

Data of hygiene practice were self-reported. Child’s sex, rural or urban setting (specifically in North Kodi or Kupang), and drinking water’s availability and sources, and sanitation facilities were recorded. The group is divided by parasitic infected group (n=33) and non-parasitic group (n=47). The non-parasitic group are the children who were proven not infected by parasitic infection. Other source of infection (viral, bacteria, etc.) were not examined. In this study, children who appeared clinically healthy at the time of assessment were included. Consequently, participants with a history of prior acute or chronic illnesses, as well as those with previous antibiotic exposure, were not excluded and remained eligible for enrollment. This approach may have introduced residual confounding, particularly in a population with a high burden of infections and malnutrition, where subclinical disease and prior antimicrobial use can influence gut integrity and immune activation biomarkers. For the purposes of this study, acute illness was defined as a condition with a duration of less than six months, whereas chronic illness was defined as a condition persisting for six months or longer.

Stool examination and parasitic infection identification

The children stool samples (n=80) were collected from their parents or caregivers and subsequently stored in sterile containers, preserved in 10% formalin solution, for further parasitological examination at the Laboratory of Malaria, Institute of Tropical Disease, Universitas Airlangga, Indonesia, using Olympus standard microscopy (Brand) and lugol solution staining. The stools were collected in once but separated in half to identified the parasites and analysed with biomarker. The investigation focused on helminthic as well as protozoan parasites as explained in previous study [13]. Stool and blood samples were collected concurrently.

Blood preparation and gut inflammation biomarker

Three ml of blood were taken, followed to be kept in EDTA tubes, then centrifuged to separate serum from plasma at 2,000 to 3,000 RPM for 60 minutes. Polypropylene tubes used to transfer the serum sample. The sample then kept for later examination at −80°C. The levels of sCD14, sCD163, intestinal FABP6, and I-FABP assessed as the gut inflammatory biomarkers. ELISA kits were used following the instructions, respectively ELISA kit of sCD14 (Catalogue No. E0317Hu) and sCD163 ELISA Kit (Catalogue No. E0246Hu). The examination conducted in Integrated Laboratory from Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Airlangga in Surabaya, Indonesia.

Statistical analysis

To analysed the statistic, this study used the IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Co). The normal distribution of the dataset was analyzed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and p-value less than 0.05 considered as non-normal distribution. Categorical variables (sex, living environtment, basic immunization, nutritional status, source of drinking water, and type of sanitation facility) were presented in frequencies and percentages, then analysed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. While the continuous variables, such as level of sCD14, sCD153, I-FABP, FABP6, were presented as median with interquartile ranges and compared using Kruskal-Wallis test. Result of p-value<0.050 was considered significant. No post-hoct correction was done for multiple comparisons.

DISCUSSION

This study provided critical insights into the impact of gastrointestinal infections from parasite on children. Through a detailed examination of sociodemographic variables and the assessment of gut inflammatory biomarkers, our findings underscored the significant health burden these infections impose on affected children. The correlations was found to be significant between the infection and sociodemographic factors such as source of drinking water, source of drinking water, and sanitation facilities from previous studies which in accordance with another studies that report a high prevalence of parasitic infections in regions with restricted access to clean water and inadequate sanitation [1011].

The predominance of Giardia lamblia and Trichuris trichiura were in agreement with previous studies in Ethiopia and South West Sumba, where the hygienic conditions were similar with the result from this study [1214]. Gastrointestinal infections from parasite potentially damage the intestines, as indicated by increased plasma levels of gut integrity biomarkers [1215].

A higher level pf I-FABP plasma and other gut-specific FABP, namely, FABP6 indicated an early stage of enterocyte damage and inflammation [16]. A disrupted gut epithelial barrier facilitating the translocation of microbiota, resulting in systemic pro-inflammatory activation, by lipopolysaccharides bind soluble CD14 (sCD14), a pathogen identifier receptor, which may stimulate inflammatory responses [17].

The significant high level of I-FABP in patients with Entamoeba histolytica compared to other parasites, suggesting the damage of mucosal epithelial barrier. The ingested cysts through water contaminated by stool and or soil in foods, performed excystation in the small intestine and formed invasive trophozoites, migrated to the colon and damaged the mucosa epithelial barrier [18]. Before the cell death, Entamoeba histolytica performs trogocyitosis, hence damaging the integrity of the host cell membrane and caused cell death [19].

Since the FABP6 and sCD14 were lower in the helminth infected children compared to non-parasitic group, this finding suggests that I-FABP secreted due to the damaged of enterocytes, independent of on the pathogens, as observed in both bacterial or viral infection [20]. sCD14 is a glycoprotein, originated either from protease-mediated shedding of membrane-bound CD14 or liver synthesis which is a type II acute-phase reactant. Presepsin, a form of sCD14, has been recognized specifically increased in bacterial infections [21].

This study has several limitations. First, stool analysis in the parasitic group was restricted to the detection of intestinal parasites, while other enteric pathogens, including bacterial and viral infections, were not assessed. These unmeasured infections may contribute to environmental enteric dysfunction (EED)–related processes and could have influenced biomarkers of intestinal epithelial damage and systemic immune activation, such as I-FABP, FABP6, sCD14, and sCD163. Second, the cross-sectional design precluded the assessment of prior or recurrent parasitic infections. As chronic or repeated enteric infections are central to the pathophysiology of EED, the cumulative effects of secondary parasitic infections on intestinal barrier integrity and immune activation could not be evaluated.

Similar sample collection location from a study by Surono et al. [22] reported significant differences in the composition of gut microbiota between stunted and control group. Specifically, three genera—Lachnoclostridium, Faecalibacterium, and Veillonella—were consistently found to be significantly more abundant in the gut microbiota of stunted children across both sampling sites (p<0.0007). Additionally, Surono et al. [23] identified differences in immune status and short-chain fatty acid levels between stunted and non-stunted children. These findings may help explain the elevated levels of intestinal and immune biomarkers observed in children without detectable parasitic infections in this study. Such elevations may reflect underlying EED, characterized by chronic alterations in gut microbiota composition, increased intestinal permeability, and persistent mucosal and systemic immune activation—conditions that are highly prevalent in populations with a high burden of enteric infections and malnutrition. Moreover, the biomarkers assessed in this study are known to be chronically elevated in disease states associated with sustained intestinal epithelial injury and microbial translocation, including Crohn’s disease [2425], HIV infection [26], colorectal cancer [27], and malnutrition [28]. Recent antibiotic exposure reducing the microbiome diversity hence the changes in bacterial abundance may influenced the gut integrity itself [29], but history of recent antibiotic usage was not assessed. Since some Lactobacillus strains inhibit protozoan infection [30] by producing bacteriocins to protect from parasite adhesions [31], the presence parasite infection may reduced the number of bacteria also enhanced by the chronic disease [32].

In conclusion, this study provides novel information on gut integrity biomarker levels in parasitic and non-parasitic group, highlighting these biomarkers as indicators for intestinal damage in this population. Interestingly, the high level of these biomarkers in uninfected children make an unexpected pattern that warrants further investigation. While the present findings demonstrate an association between infection status and biomarker levels, they do not establish its causality. Based on these findings, targeted interventions should prioritize regular screening of at-risk children using these biomarkers to enable early diagnosis and timely treatment.
