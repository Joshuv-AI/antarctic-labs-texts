Fatal tricuspid valve inflow obstruction and inferior vena cava syndrome in a child with advanced Wilms’ tumor: a case report
Authors: Joyce Gimonge, David Muhunzi, Deogratias Nkya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWilms’ tumor is the most common primary renal malignancy in children. Although tumor extension into the inferior vena cava occurs in 4–10% of cases, intracardiac involvement is rare, reported in only 1%. In resource-limited settings, delayed diagnosis and inadequate access to specialized care may lead to fatal complications.Case presentationWe report the case of an 8-year-old Tanzanian girl presenting with a 6-month history of progressive abdominal distension, dyspnea, and lower limb edema. Imaging revealed a large right-sided renal mass with extension into the inferior vena cava and right atrium, causing tricuspid valve inflow obstruction. Despite receiving one dose each of intravenous actinomycin D and vincristine, her condition rapidly deteriorated, culminating in cardiogenic shock and death within 7 days of admission.ConclusionThis case underscores the importance of early recognition, prompt diagnosis, and multidisciplinary management of Wilms’ tumor with intracardiac and vascular extension. It highlights the need to strengthen pediatric oncology, surgical, cardiac, and intensive care infrastructure in low-resource settings to avoid preventable fatalities.

Introduction

Wilms’ tumor is the most common primary renal malignancy in children, accounting for approximately 6% of all pediatric cancers [1–3]. The tumor typically presents between the age of 2 and 5 years and is often discovered incidentally as an abdominal mass [4]. While early stage disease is frequently asymptomatic, advanced presentations may include hypertension, hematuria, abdominal pain, and constitutional symptoms such as fever and weight loss [4]. Tumor thrombus extension into the renal vein and inferior vena cava (IVC) occurs in 4–10% of cases [5]. The extension of the tumor into the IVC can cause partial or complete obstruction, leading to inferior vena cava syndrome [6–8]. Rarely, the thrombus may extend into the right atrium, where it can obstruct the tricuspid valve and precipitate right heart failure, cardiogenic shock, or pulmonary embolism [9–13]. Despite the potential severity, up to 50% of patients with intracardiac extension may remain asymptomatic, complicating early diagnosis [12, 13].

Treatment strategies for Wilms’ tumor are guided by two major protocols: the Children’s Oncology Group (COG), which favors upfront surgery, and the International Society of Pediatric Oncology (SIOP), which recommends neoadjuvant chemotherapy (8). While both approaches yield high survival rates in high-income countries (5-year survival > 90%), outcomes remain poor in low-resource settings owing to delayed presentation, inadequate diagnostics, and limited access to surgical and oncological care [14–16].

Here, we present a fatal case of Wilms’ tumor with extensive vascular and intracardiac involvement, emphasizing diagnostic and treatment gaps in resource-limited settings and offering insights for improving care pathways.

Presentation of the case

An 8-year-old girl from Tanzania’s Lake Zone was referred to a national-level facility with a 6-month history of progressive abdominal distension and a 2-week history of lower limb edema and respiratory difficulty. On admission, she was somnolent with a Glasgow Coma Score of 11, tachypnea (respiration rate (RR) 40 breaths per minute), and tachycardia (heart rate (HR) 150 beats per minute). Generalized pitting edema was noted, particularly in the lower limbs.

Abdominal examination revealed a firm, tender, right-sided mass occupying the right flank and extending across the midline. No signs of ascites were evident, and bowel sounds were normal. Cardiovascular examination revealed a pansystolic murmur best heard at the tricuspid area. The remainder of the systemic examination was unremarkable.

Laboratory tests revealed mild anemia (hemoglobin (Hb) 10 g/dL), moderate thrombocytopenia (platelets 65 × 109/L), and elevated serum creatinine (104.7 µmol/L) and blood urea nitrogen (12.4 mmol/L). Electrolytes were normal, and urine vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) levels were within reference range.

A transthoracic echocardiogram showed a large hyperechoic mass (5.7 cm × 3.1 cm) occupying the right atrium, obstructing tricuspid valve inflow with minimal residual flow (Fig. 1). There was total occlusion of the hepatic segment of the IVC (Fig. 2). The right atrial wall was notably distended, indicating elevated right atrial pressure. Although histopathological confirmation was not obtained, the clinical picture of a large abdominal mass, signs of inferior vena cava syndrome, and intracardiac extension with tricuspid valve obstruction strongly supported a diagnosis of advanced-stage Wilms’ tumor. The diagnostic formulation was consistent with established clinical and radiologic patterns described in literature for Wilms’ tumor with vascular involvement.

Treatment and progress

Upon admission, the child was stabilized and started on neoadjuvant chemotherapy per SIOP protocol. On day 2, she received intravenous actinomycin D (945 mcg) and vincristine (1 mg). Supportive care included fluid restriction, management of heart failure symptoms, and monitoring for metabolic derangements.

On day 3 of admission, the patient developed worsening respiratory distress and altered mental status. She was intubated and mechanically ventilated. Despite maximal supportive measures, her clinical condition declined rapidly, and she succumbed to cardiogenic shock on day 7 of hospitalization.

Discussion

Wilms’ tumor with intracardiac extension, although rare, represents a critical and often fatal complication that demands high clinical vigilance and swift, coordinated multidisciplinary intervention. In our setting, cases of intracardiac extensions are rare, aligning with existing literature. However, an unexpectedly higher prevalence has been observed in case series in Uganda, where 19% of cases of Wilms’ tumor presented with intracardiac extension, attributed to significant delays in seeking medical care [17]. In most low-income countries, children present to health facilities only after prolonged symptom progression, by which time the tumor has extended into major vessels and the heart. This pattern reflects late-stage disease at diagnosis in resource-limited settings and highlights the significant impact of diagnostic and referral delays on disease progression.

In this case, the child presented with a 6-month history of progressive abdominal distension and signs of inferior vena cava (IVC) syndrome, including lower limb edema and respiratory compromise, which are hallmarks of advanced disease with vascular and intracardiac involvement. Echocardiographic and radiologic findings confirmed a large right renal mass extending into the IVC and right atrium, leading to functional tricuspid valve obstruction. Despite treatment, the child succumbed to cardiogenic shock. While intracardiac extension itself is not inherently associated with poor prognosis [17], the accompanying cardiovascular complications can lead to rapid clinical deterioration and mortality, as seen in our patient.

In high-resource settings, transthoracic echocardiography is a standard first-line modality for detecting intracardiac extension in Wilms’ tumor [18]. However, in low-resource settings such as Tanzania, the availability of echocardiography machines and experienced personnel is often limited to tertiary-level facilities. In this case, echocardiography was performed at the national-level hospital, reflecting systemic delays in diagnostic evaluation. These limitations hinder the timely identification of intracardiac involvement, thereby delaying appropriate treatment and increasing the risk of complications.

Tumor histology is essential for risk stratification and treatment planning in Wilms’ tumor. In Tanzania, however, histopathological confirmation is typically obtained after surgery. Without a preoperative tissue diagnosis, clinicians must depend on clinical assessment and imaging findings alone. This constraint, in addition to delayed presentation and advanced disease at diagnosis, further complicates management and contributes to poor outcomes in resource-limited settings.

The cause of death in this case was cardiogenic shock secondary to right heart inflow obstruction by the intracardiac tumor. The obstruction impaired venous return and right ventricular filling, resulting in reduced cardiac output, multiorgan hypoperfusion, and eventual cardiovascular collapse. In ideal settings, Wilms’ tumor with intracardiac extension is managed with 6 weeks of preoperative chemotherapy (vincristine weekly, actinomycin D after every 2 weeks, often with doxorubicin) to reduce tumor burden [7, 8]. Surgery is typically performed 1 week after the final dose, once tumor regression is confirmed [7]. In our case, although chemotherapy was initiated, the disease was already advanced, and the patient succumbed before a second dose could be administered.

Conclusion

Wilms’ tumor with intracardiac extension presents a complex challenge, particularly in resource-limited settings such as Tanzania. Delays in diagnosis, limited access to diagnostic imaging, inconsistent chemotherapy availability, and the scarcity of specialized surgical and critical care services all contribute to poor outcomes. The progression to cardiogenic shock from tricuspid valve inflow obstruction in this case was preventable had the tumor been identified and treated earlier. Strengthening healthcare systems through better referral networks, diagnostic infrastructure, and multidisciplinary training is vital to preventing such outcomes in children with Wilms’ tumor, particularly in low-resource settings.
