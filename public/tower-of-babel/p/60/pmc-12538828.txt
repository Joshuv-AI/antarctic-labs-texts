Mechanism of DKK3 protecting brain tissues in ischemic stroke by regulating LGI1 expression and activity
Authors: DongLiang Zhou, Lei Miao, LinJuan Feng, HongWei Qin, Ying Xu, JianMin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the mechanism of Dickkopf-3 (DKK3) in protecting brain tissues in ischemic stroke (IS) by regulating LGI1 expression and activity.Methods220 patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) were enrolled to measure the serum DKK3 levels and analyze the predictive ability of serum DKK3 for early neurological deterioration (END) in AIS patients. A murine model of permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (pMCAO) was established. Neurological function recovery in mice was assessed using neurological function scores and behavioral experiments, and the volume of cerebral infarction in mice was measured. The histopathological morphology of the cortex in mice was observed by hematoxylin‒eosin staining; apoptotic neurons were evaluated by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling staining. DKK3 and leucine-rich glioma-inactivated protein 1 (LGI1) expression levels were measured by immunofluorescence, RT-qPCR, or Western blot, and their interaction was verified by bioinformatics analysis and Co-IP. A cellular model of oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) injury was established using HT22 murine neurons for in vitro investigation.ResultsDKK3 was downregulated in IS and shows potential as a biomarker for predicting END in AIS patients. DKK3 overexpression contributed to neurological recovery and attenuated brain injury in pMCAO mice. DKK3 co-localized and interacted with LGI1. LGI1 downregulation weakened the protective effect of DKK3 against pMCAO-induced brain damage and OGD-induced neuronal injury.ConclusionDKK3 protects against ischemic injury in permanent cerebral ischemia by regulating LGI1 expression and activity.

Introduction

Stroke encompasses a range of conditions marked by damage to brain tissue and an abrupt loss of localized neurological function, caused by a sudden rupture or obstruction of cerebral blood vessels leading to impaired blood flow to the brain [1–3]. Ischemic stroke (IS) accounts for approximately 69.6% to 70.8% of all stroke cases [4, 5]. Early neurological deterioration (END) following IS is characterized by a significant neurological deficit and worsening within a specific timeframe after IS onset, despite clinical intervention [6, 7]. END after IS is closely associated with poor prognosis such as post-stroke disability and death [8]. However, the ability of standard clinical biochemical indicators to predict END is inadequate [9], making the development of new molecular markers crucial for early diagnosis and better patient prognosis. Mechanical thrombectomy and intravenous thrombolysis using recombinant tissue-type plasminogen activator, the standard treatments for acute ischemic stroke (AIS), are challenged by a limited therapeutic window. Moreover, intravenous thrombolysis may also exacerbate blood–brain barrier damage, leading to conversion to hemorrhage [10–12]. Therefore, new therapeutic targets and methods are urgently needed.

Dickkopf-3 (DKK3), a member of the Dickkopf family, is a secreted glycoprotein implicated in various non-malignant diseases. It is particularly recognized for its modulatory role in the Wnt signaling pathway, which plays a fundamental role in various critical biological processes [13–15]. DKK3 was initially considered to be a biomarker for various tumors [16]. However, further studies have revealed that DKK3 antagonizes myocardial dysfunction and ventricular remodeling after infarction [17] and may antagonize atherosclerotic processes by modulating inflammatory responses as well as the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [18]. Serum DKK3 is negatively correlated with carotid intima–media thickness and 5-year carotid plaque progression [19]. In addition, the detection of DKK3 levels before cardiac surgery can predict the incidence of acute kidney injury after ischemia [20], suggesting that DKK3 can be a sensitive response to the deficits in function of blood supply organs after ischemia and hypoxia. END events after IS are significantly associated with indicators such as the extent of cerebrovascular atherosclerotic lesions and the intervention time frame for patients. Our previously published study demonstrated that predictive threshold levels of serum DKK3, determined via X-tile software, serve as a potential biomarker for in-hospital END in patients with AIS, and that low DKK3 levels are associated with increased in-hospital mortality [21]. Furthermore, DKK3 demonstrates protective effects against ischemic neuronal death in a model of focal cerebral ischemia induced by permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (pMCAO) [22].

These findings provided the foundation for the current study, which aimed to elucidate the expression pattern of DKK3 following IS and to mechanistically investigate its role in disease progression via the regulation of leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 (LGI1).

Materials and methods

Study population

Two hundred and twenty patients admitted to the Renhe Hospital of Baoshan District with a definite diagnosis of noncardiogenic AIS from January 2023 to September 2024 were included and signed an informed consent form. Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) onset time ≤ 24 h; (3) diagnosis in accordance with the criteria of the World Health Organization [23]; (4) signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients with a previous history of stroke; (2) patients with cardiac stroke; (3) patients undergoing interventional therapy; (4) patients with intracranial hemorrhagic disease; (5) patients with severe hepatic and renal insufficiency; (6) patients with severe stress conditions such as urinary tract infection or infection in other sites; (7) patients with organ failure, malignant neoplasm; (8) patients with other neurological or systemic inflammatory diseases; (9) patients who could not cooperate with all examinations.

An additional 36 age-matched healthy control subjects were recruited for the same time period. All protocols were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Renhe Hospital of Baoshan District, and the clinicopathologic characteristics of all included patients are shown in Table 1. Serum DKK3 measurement

Venous blood (4 mL) was drawn on an empty stomach early in the morning after admission, and the upper layer was collected after coagulation and frozen at – 80 ℃. The concentration of DKK3 in serum was detected by enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA) kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN,USA).

END72 diagnostic criteria

Neurological deficit was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). END72 refers to a rise of 4 points or more in the NIHSS score within 72 h from the disease onset, as compared to the score at admission.

Animals

SPF-grade male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from SiPeiFu (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mice were housed under the following conditions: 24 ± 2 °C, 45%−65% humidity, 12 h/12 h day/night (lights on at 07:00 and off at 19:00 daily), and all the mice were given standard chow and free access to water. The mice were acclimated to the environment for a week. The Experimental Ethics Committee at Renhe Hospital of Baoshan District sanctioned the use of all animals.

Mouse modeling and treatment

The pMCAO mouse model was developed following methods outlined earlier [24, 25]. Anesthesia for the mice was achieved using a gas mixture of 70% N2O, 30% O2, and 5% isoflurane for induction. Throughout the surgery, anesthesia was kept at 1.5%−2% isoflurane, while rectal temperature was regulated at 37 ± 0.2 °C using a heating pad. The mice were positioned on their left side, and a small horizontal cut was made between the right eye and ear to reveal the zygomatic arch. After removing an approximately 3-mm-long segment of the zygomatic arch and exposing the skull base and the trigeminal nerve branches, the skull was drilled to create a small opening, allowing access to the middle cerebral artery (MCA), which was then permanently ligated with a silk thread. The sham-operated group followed the same steps, but the MCA was left unligated. The success of modeling was assessed by using transcranial laser Doppler flowmetry to measure blood flow, which had to drop to less than 30% of the baseline, along with a Longa score of 1 or higher after waking [26].

Mice were randomly divided into 6 groups (n = 18): the Sham group, the pMCAO group, the pMCAO + oe-NC group, the pMCAO + oe-DKK3 group, the pMCAO + oe-DKK3 + sh-NC group, and the pMCAO + oe-DKK3 + sh-LGI1 group.

GenePharma (Shanghai, China) constructed and packaged the lentiviral vectors. Through genetic recombination, the pGC-FU vectors were designed to harbor expression cassettes for DKK3 overexpression (oe-DKK3) and its corresponding empty vector control (oe-NC), as well as for LGI1 knockdown (sh-LGI1) with its scramble RNA-based control (sh-NC). The vector and packaging plasmid were then co-transfected into HEK293T cells. The supernatant containing the released lentiviral vectors was collected, concentrated by ultracentrifugation, and the final titer of lentiviral vectors was adjusted to 2 × 108 TU/mL. The pMCAO procedure was carried out 2 weeks after the lentiviral intervention surgery, with bregma serving as the zero point, located 1.5 mm to the right, 1.2 mm backward, and 4.5 mm deep. A single injection of lentiviral vectors, totaling 5 μL, was administered into the right lateral ventricle of mice at a rate of 1 μL per minute and allowed to sit for 5 min. Equal volumes of saline were injected into the Sham group and the pMCAO group.

Neurological deficits and behavioral function

Neurological deficits were assessed in all mice prior to the experiment, and those with abnormal scores were excluded. Mice were evaluated for neurological function recovery at 24 and 72 h following surgery using the Longa score, modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS), and fatigue rotarod test, with the scoring done in a blinded manner.

Longa score: 0 points, no neurological deficits; 1 point, inability to fully extend the contralateral forelimb during tail lifting, mild neurological deficit; 2 points, turning to the contralateral side when lifting the tail but walking normally, moderate neurological deficit; 3 points, turning to the contralateral side or tilting when walking, severe neurological deficit; 4 points, inability to walk spontaneously and impaired consciousness.

The mNSS measures motor function, reflex, balance, and abnormal movement. The highest score achievable is 18, and a higher score correlates with a more severe neurological deficit. Scores ranging from 13 to 18 are considered severe impairment, 7 to 12 are moderate impairment, and 1 to 6 are mild impairment.

Fatigue rotarod test: The rotarod operated at speeds between 4 and 40 rpm. Each mouse was placed in turn on the rotating rod, and the start button was pressed once they were stable. Over the course of 300 s, the rotarod accelerated evenly from 4 to 40 rpm. The period during which the mice remained on the rotarod without falling was tracked, and times over 300 s were logged as 300 s. Conducted three times, the tests were done at 15-min intervals and then averaged.

Cerebral infarction volume measurement

Each group had six mice that were anesthetized and euthanized by an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg) 72 h after the surgical procedure. After freezing the brain tissues for 20 min, five consecutive slices were cut in the coronal plane, each slice being approximately 2 mm thick. Immediately, the slices were submerged in 4% 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 37℃ for 10 min, shielded from light, and subsequently immersed in formalin for 24 h. Uninfarcted brain tissue stained dark red, while infarcted areas appeared light yellow or white. Infarct volume = (Σinfarcted area × thickness)/(Σwhole brain area × thickness) × 100%.

Measurement of brain edema

After 72 h from the surgery, six mice per group were put under anesthesia and then euthanized. Once the olfactory bulb and brain stem were removed, the brain tissue was split into the right and left hemispheres and weighed (wet weight). Afterward, they were put in an oven at 80 °C for 72 h, then weighed again to determine the dry weight. Water content = (wet weight—dry weight)/wet weight × 100%.

Specimen collection

Six mice from each group were executed at 72 h postoperatively. A thoraco-abdominal incision was performed to access the heart, followed by cannulation of the left ventricle and fixation through intracardiac perfusion using chilled normal saline and 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 mol/L PBS (pH 7.4). When the liver turned completely white, the intact brain tissue was carefully removed on ice. On the injured side, brain tissue was excised, with part of it used for paraffin sections and the rest quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at −80℃ for other evaluations.

Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining

The paraffin sections were stained in hematoxylin solution for 3–5 min, differentiated in hydrochloric acid and ammonia for a few seconds, and dehydrated in 70% and 90% alcohol for 10 min. The sections were stained in eosin staining solution for 2–3 min, dehydrated in alcohol, cleared in xylene, and sealed with neutral resin. The sections were observed under a microscope to assess histopathological changes in the peri-infarct cortex area.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining

The paraffin sections were dewaxed, treated with proteinase K, and then incubated for 1 h with the TUNEL reaction mixture, shielded from light, using the TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Sections were stained with primary antibody NeuN (1:100, ab104224, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) and Alexa Fluor® 488-labeled secondary antibody (Abcam), then incubated with DAPI for 5 min. Five high-magnification fields of view were randomly selected and photographed in the peri-infarct cortex area in each slice by a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The number of TUNEL-positive cells (apoptotic cell number) was counted using Image J software.

Cell culture

In the field of neurobiology, the murine hippocampal neuronal cell line HT22 has become a vital tool for investigating neuronal function, neurotoxicity, and neuroprotective strategies, owing to its well-characterized physiological properties and ease of handling. HT22 cells share biological similarities with human neurons and exhibit typical neuronal morphological features, such as round or oval cell bodies and multiple processes. When cultured in vitro, these cells can form synaptic-like connections that support signal transmission, demonstrate rapid growth, and are convenient for observation and manipulation. Consistent with their established relevance, HT22 cells are widely used in ischemia research to model neuronal responses, as supported by multiple studies [27–29]. Therefore, HT22 was selected as the cell line for constructing the in vitro model in this study.

The HT22 cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were maintained in DMEM (GIBCO, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (GIBCO) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (GIBCO), and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The culture medium was replaced every two days. For subsequent experiments, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 107 cells per well.

Cell modeling and infection

HT22 cells were initially inoculated in glucose-free and serum-free DMEM (GIBCO), followed by placement in an anaerobic chamber with 95% nitrogen and 5% carbon dioxide at 37 °C for 8 h. HT22 not subjected to oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) exposure served as a control group.

A cell suspension at 4 × 104 cells/mL was prepared in complete medium, inoculated in a 6-well plate, and incubated at 37 ℃ for 16–24 h until the cell confluence was 30%. The appropriate amount of virus and viral infection enhancement solution were added according to the multiplicity of infection (MOI) and viral titer. Following 12 to 16 h of culture, the medium was exchanged for complete medium. After 72 h, the MOI that reported good cell growth and an 80% infection efficiency was chosen for further experiments. Stably infected cells were then screened using puromycin.

Cells were grouped into Control group, OGD group, OGD + oe-NC group, OGD + oe-DKK3 group, OGD + oe-DKK3 + sh-NC group, and OGD + oe-DKK3 + sh-LGI1 group. Lentiviral transfection was conducted 5 days in advance of OGD exposure.

3-(4,5-Di-methylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay

Cells were inoculated in 96-well plate according to 3 × 103/wells, 200 μL/well. After 24 h, 0.5 mg/mL MTT reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to each well, respectively. Following a 4-h incubation period, the supernatant was removed, 100 μL of DMSO solution was introduced, and the plates were shaken for 10 min before measuring the absorbance at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) leakage assay

Cytotoxicity was detected using the LDH release assay kit (Beyotime). Each well received 120 μL of cell supernatant and 60 μL of LDH detection solution. After 30 min, absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad).

Apoptosis detection

Cells were added to pre-cooled 500 μL of 1 × binding buffer, and 5 μL of Annexin-V-FITC and 2.5 μL of PI (Beyotime) were added to the cell suspension. After incubation for 15 min away from light, the cells were subjected to apoptosis rate detection on a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

ELISA

TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 in brain tissue homogenate and cell supernatant were detected using the corresponding ELISA kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Jiangsu, China).

Immunofluorescence

The paraffin sections were deparaffinized, treated with 2 mol/L HCl at 37 °C for 30 min, and then neutralized twice in borate buffer (pH 8.4) for 10 min each time. The sections were then blocked with 10% goat serum for 2 h and titrated with primary antibodies against NeuN (1:100, ab104224, Abcam), GFAP (1:100, ab4648, Abcam), Iba1 (1:100, ab283319, Abcam), DKK3 (1:50, ab186409, Abcam), and LGI1 (1:100, ab30868, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. The sections were exposed to the relevant secondary antibody for 2 h, followed by a 5-min incubation with DAPI staining solution, shielded from light, and sealed with an anti-fluorescence quencher. Using a laser confocal microscope (Leica), sections were examined, and five regions from each section were selected for image capture. Quantitative analysis was conducted with ImageJ software.

The cell slides were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, then permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and finally blocked with 1% BSA for 1 h. Then, primary antibodies DKK3 (1:50, ab186409, Abcam) and LGI1 (1:100, ab30868, Abcam) were added at 4℃ overnight. Fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibody was incubated for 2 h, and the slides were restained with DAPI for 15 min and photographed by laser confocal microscopy.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and high-quality RNA was confirmed by ND-1000 UV–visible spectrophotometer (Mettler-Toledo GmbH, Columbus, OH, USA). RNA was reverse transcribed with PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Qiagen, Duesseldorf, Germany). PCR primers were designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Table 2). The reaction system was performed with the TaqMan RT-PCR kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), involving pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 20 s, and extension at 70 °C for 10 s. Forty cycles were run. The data were analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCt method. Western blot

Tissue and cell proteins were extracted using a protein extraction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The protein concentration was measured by the BCA Protein Concentration Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were boiled at 100 ℃ for 5 min, followed by electrophoresis. The proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, blocked with 5% skim milk powder for 1 h, detected with primary antibodies rabbit anti-DKK3 (1:1000, ab186409, Abcam), rabbit anti-LGI1 (1:1000, ab30868, Abcam), rabbit anti-cleaved Caspase 3 (1:5000, ab214430, Abcam), rabbit anti-Bax (1:1000, ab32503, Abcam), and rabbit anti-β-actin (1:5000, AC026, ABclonal, Wuhan, China) overnight at 4 °C, and horseradish peroxidase-labeled goat-anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:5000, Abcam) at 37 °C for 1 h. Color was developed, and protein levels were analyzed.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Immune complexes were formed using endogenous Co-IP by incubating HT22 cell lysates with primary antibodies DKK3 (Abcam) or LGI1 (Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. Normal IgG was also used as an isotype control. Protein A/G magnetic beads (MCE, USA) were pre-washed twice with cell lysis buffer. The beads were separated, and then the immune complexes were incubated with the magnetic beads for 30 min at room temperature and 2 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed with lysis buffer, the magnetic beads were separated, and the supernatant was discarded. Then, 1 × SDS-PAGE Loading Buffer was added to the magnetic beads and heated at 95 ºC for 5 min; the magnetic beads were separated, and the supernatant was collected for Western blot detection.

Statistical analysis

Power analysis conducted with G*Power 3.1 indicated that a sample size of 11 animals per group would be required to achieve 80% statistical power (α = 0.05, two-tailed t-test), under the assumption of an effect size of 1.26, which corresponds to a 50% reduction in infarct volume. To account for biological variability, potential unexpected events during the experiment, and the requirements of multiple outcome measures, the sample size was increased to 18 animals per group.

Data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 (La Jolla, CA, USA). Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), representative operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to assess the predictive ability of serum DKK3 to identify END in AIS patients, and Pearson analysis was performed for the correlation analysis. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data distribution. Variables in normal distribution were compared by t test. The two sets of skew distribution variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, followed by Bonferroni correction. Comparisons between multiple groups were performed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. P < 0.05 was taken as a statistically significant difference.

Results

DKK3 is downregulated in IS and is linked to END in AIS patients

Serum DKK3 levels were lower in AIS patients than in healthy controls on admission (Fig. 1A). The clinicopathologic characteristics of all included AIS patients are shown in Table 1, in which the incidence of END was 15.0% (33/220). Patients with END had higher NIHSS scores than non-END patients on admission. Notably, END patients had significantly lower serum DKK3 levels than non-END patients (Fig. 1B). ROC curve analysis further demonstrated that serum DKK3 levels effectively predicted the development of END in AIS patients (Fig. 1C), with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.871 (p < 0.001), demonstrating high predictive accuracy for END. These results support the potential of serum DKK3 as a clinically useful biomarker to identify AIS patients at high risk of END. Further validation is warranted to establish optimal predictive cutoff values and to confirm its clinical utility in larger, multi-center cohorts. DKK3 ameliorates brain tissue injury in pMCAO mice

To confirm the pivotal role of DKK3 in pMCAO mice, stereotactic lentivirus-mediated gene delivery was employed to overexpress DKK3 in the brain. Western blot analysis confirmed effective DKK3 overexpression in the cerebral cortex (Fig. 2A). DKK3 upregulation effectively restored neurological function recovery in mice after pMCAO challenge (Fig. 2B-D). Also, DKK3 upregulation helped to reduce the volume of cerebral infarction and water content in pMCAO mice (Fig. 2E-G). Histopathological examination of the peri-infarct cortex by HE staining is presented in Fig. 2H. Compared with the sham group, which exhibited normal cellular architecture, well-arranged layers, and morphologically intact neurons, the pMCAO group showed a marked reduction in the number of cell layers and neuronal count. Neurons in this group also displayed karyopyknosis, irregular morphology, and uneven staining. DKK3 attenuates OGD-induced HT22 cell damage

In vitro experiments were also performed with lentiviral infection of OGD-induced HT22 cells overexpressing DKK3, and the infection efficiency was verified by Western blot (Fig. 4A). Discussion

In the present study, we employed the pMCAO model in C57BL/6 mice to simulate IS. Our results showed that DKK3 levels were significantly reduced in the ischemic penumbra cortex at 72 h after pMCAO. This pattern contrasts with a previous study [22] that reported an increase in DKK3 expression at 6 and 12 h after ischemia in medial and inferior cortical regions surrounding the infarct. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the timing of DKK3 measurement. The 6 and 12 h time points coincide with the phase of focal and near-maximal ischemic damage, during which endogenous protective mechanisms are activated. More intriguingly, Caffo et al. [32] recently investigated endogenous DKK3 pathway activation in a rat model of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (1 h ischemia followed by reperfusion for 1, 6, 12, or 24 h). They observed a significant increase in Dkk3 expression with prolonged reperfusion, peaking at 24 h, and proposed that Dkk3 exacerbates ischemic damage by inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Notably, the study conducted by Caffo et al. diverged from ours in two principal respects: the utilization of a different model (ischemia–reperfusion) and the examination of an earlier post-ischemic time frame. It is plausible that DKK3, as a protective molecule, is transiently upregulated to mitigate injury in the acute phase. However, as ischemic damage persists, compensatory resources may become depleted, resulting in a subsequent decline in DKK3 expression. Similar spatiotemporal expression dynamics have been documented for other protective genes in IS [33].

Furthermore, our immunofluorescence co-staining results diverged slightly from those reported by Carla et al. [22], showing stronger co-localization of DKK3 with the neuronal marker NeuN than with the astrocytic marker GFAP or microglial marker Iba1. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in experimental timing and anatomical sites examined. In alignment with our observations, Zhang et al. [34] similarly identified high DKK3 immunoreactivity co-localized with NeuN and Iba1, and to a lesser extent with GFAP, in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. Based on these collective findings, we propose that DKK3 may act as a protective factor during the initial phase of ischemia (within 24 h), characterized by a transient upregulation. This initial increase is likely driven by reactive astrocytes in the ischemic penumbra as part of an endogenous compensatory mechanism. As the pathology progresses, however, DKK3 expression becomes persistently downregulated in the penumbral cortex, where it is predominantly localized in neurons. This sustained decrease in DKK3 may contribute to worsened cerebral ischemic injury, as summarized in Fig. 8. To further investigate DKK3 dynamics in a controlled setting, we established an OGD model using HT22 cells. The pathophysiological processes involved in IS are complex and include multiple alterations, such as energy depletion, imbalance of intracellular ionic homeostasis, significant elevation of intracellular calcium levels, acidosis, excitotoxicity, free radical-mediated toxic injury, overactivation of the complement pathway, activation of glial cells, disruption of the blood–brain barrier, and infiltration of leukocytes, among others. These alterations are interrelated and collaborate with each other to exacerbate the ischemic and hypoxic damage to brain tissue in IS in different ways [35–37]. Therefore, the search for promising therapeutic targets that can protect damaged brain tissues and neurological functions is of great clinical significance. After establishing the trend in DKK3 expression, we studied its specific effects on post-IS injury in both in vitro and in vivo settings. The results reported that DKK3 ameliorated pMCAO-induced brain damage in vivo and attenuated OGD-induced neuronal injury in vitro. This is in line with past studies reporting the neuroprotective effects of DKK3 [26, 34]. Moreover, DKK3 has also demonstrated protective effects against ischemic injury in the context of cardiovascular diseases [15]. For instance, DKK3 mediates the protective effects of therapeutic hypothermia on myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury, including reductions in myocardial infarction size, myocardial injury, inflammatory response, and apoptosis [38]. These findings are consistent with our results, which reveal a protective role of DKK3 against neuronal apoptosis and neuroinflammation.

LGI1 is a secreted glycoprotein expressed predominantly in the central nervous system [39, 40]. According to reports, LGI1 regulates postsynaptic density 95 protein and glutamate receptor and promotes neurite growth [41, 42]. The expression of LGI1 is downregulated in the hippocampus of mice experiencing chronic unpredictable stress, and hippocampal LGI1 is an influential factor in neuromast growth and stress recovery [43]. Li et al. found that physical exercise improved cognitive function in rats with temporary MCAO by increasing LGI1 expression [44]. LGI1 and DKK3 co-expressed, co-localized, and interacted with each other, and DKK3 overexpression upregulated LGI1. This suggests that LGI1 might mediate the protective effects of DKK3 on brain tissues and neurons in IS. Moreover, silencing LGI1 partially counteracted the protective effect of upregulating DKK3 on pMCAO-induced brain tissue injury and OGD-induced HT22 cell damage.

Our study provides the first evidence identifying DKK3 and establishing a mechanistic link to LGI1 upregulation, demonstrating that LGI1 is essential for mediating the protective effects of DKK3 in IS. Although the signaling interplay between DKK3 and LGI1 in neuroprotection and inflammatory regulation remains incompletely elucidated, current literature suggests several plausible signal transduction pathways. These primarily include the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, modulation of neuroinflammation, and potential cross-talk between these mechanisms. The upregulation or downregulation of DKK3 influences the stability of β-catenin, thereby affecting the transcription of downstream target genes related to cell survival, apoptosis, and inflammation [45, 46]. Thus, DKK3 may influence neuronal survival, apoptosis, and neuroinflammatory responses via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [47–49]. On the other hand, LGI1 may affect synaptic transmission and neuronal excitability through its interactions with A Disintegrin And Metalloprotease (ADAM) 22 and 23 [50–52]. In anti-LGI1 encephalitis, patients exhibit a marked inflammatory response [53, 54]. Studies have also reported elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines in both serum and cerebrospinal fluid [55, 56]. Furthermore, in vitro stimulation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with LGI1 protein significantly increased the production of IL-10, an immunosuppressive anti-inflammatory cytokine [57]. This suggests that LGI1 may directly or indirectly regulate cytokine release from immune cells, influencing the inflammatory milieu. Therefore, the neuroprotective and anti-neuroinflammatory mechanisms of the DKK3–LGI1 axis may involve key downstream molecules such as the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway (potentially modulated by LGI1), inflammatory cytokines affected by LGI1-related immune responses, or exacerbation of neuroinflammation due to LGI1 dysfunction-induced network hyperexcitability. Further studies should explore the downstream molecular mechanisms of the DKK3/LGI1 axis in IS, including the spatiotemporal dynamics of DKK3 and LGI1 expression across different stages of IS and various cell types. Precise delineation of the downstream molecular events through which DKK3 and LGI1 affect neuroprotection and inflammation, along with evaluating the therapeutic potential of targeting these pathways in animal models, is strongly warranted.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the animal model used (pMCAO) may not fully recapitulate the heterogeneity and complexity of human IS, particularly due to the lack of reperfusion, which could limit the translational applicability of our results. Second, only male mice were included in this investigation. Since stroke pathogenesis and functional recovery are known to exhibit sexual dimorphism, this choice also constrains the generalizability of our findings. Third, the in vivo experiments were performed with a relatively small sample size. Therefore, subsequent animal research should incorporate alternative models (such as the middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion model) to better mimic human IS, include female animals to allow for sex-based comparisons, and employ larger sample sizes to improve statistical reliability. Additionally, although HT22 cells are convenient to use, their immortalized nature may not fully reflect the responses of primary neurons. Hence, follow-up work should integrate more physiologically relevant models, such as primary neuronal cultures, to enable a more comprehensive assessment of the role and mechanisms of the DKK3/LGI1 axis in IS.

Conclusion

DKK3 ameliorates brain tissue injury in pMCAO mice and OGD-induced HT22 cell damage by promoting LGI1 expression. This research supplies new reference evidence for the protective effects and molecular mechanisms of DKK3 in IS, and highlights the DKK3/LGI1 axis as a potential therapeutic target for IS.
