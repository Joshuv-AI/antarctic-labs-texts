INTRODUCTION

All societies benefit from a healthy population. Access to quality, evidence‐based medical care can improve health for all. Ethically, fair provision of such care should be a priority for everyone, everywhere. Regrettably, even in countries with sufficient resources and inclinations to do so, the world is far from providing necessary investment for the promotion and maintenance of health for all. The ongoing crisis in healthcare in the United States is a powerful example of this conundrum.

To this point recently, the CEO of a regional hospital in the 10th richest state in the US, underscoring a substantial increase in demand for psychiatric services in her community, noted “the frequent overcrowding in our Emergency Department was a direct consequence of the unmet demand for acute adolescent behavioral health services, with its crisis of boarding adolescents in the ED—many with suicidal ideation and/or behavior—for days or weeks without adequate support from state or local agencies and/or community‐based behavioral health resources. This lack of access to proper services in the community, and the poor reimbursement for care, especially among Medicaid and underinsured people, increased the suffering of impoverished families whose children were most frequently affected.”

Two decades ago, a group of experts and stakeholders met at the Institute of Medicine and called for a “new health system for the 21st century”. 1 The group's report offered bold reforms around national health priorities, transforming knowledge into practice, effective use of information technology (IT), innovative financial policies, and educational programs. 2 Today, many lament that this vision is still unrealized–health care, and health, in the United States are still extremely costly, fragmented, and inequitable. Reform efforts have only marginally succeeded in improving the situation despite innovation, technology, enormous economic resources, and one of the strongest medical research endeavors in the world.

More recently, the Learning Health System (LHS) model has offered renewed promise. 3 The definition of a LHS remains ambiguous to some stakeholders. A Learning Health System (LHS) is an approach that utilizes data and experiences captured as an integral by‐product of the delivery experience, seamlessly analyzed, and fed‐back upon and embedded in the delivery process to improve care. The Learning Health Project notes that an LHS could revolutionize healthcare practice, providing personalized, proactive services through the ongoing analysis of clinical data to inform and improve health decision‐making and practice. 4 This approach embodies many elements of an effective systems approach, particularly the feedback of information derived from its own operations to improve its performance in achieving desirable outcomes and reducing harmful errors. And yet, as Kohn et al 5 have pointed out, “the intentions of the LHS approach are laudable, but its impact has as yet been marginal”.

Applying the concept of “learning organizations” to healthcare has had mixed results. Currently, healthcare and research are still characterized by both a lack of true systems approaches and a confusion between understanding complicated and complex problems. With complicated problems, the components can be separated and dealt with systematically and logically, relying on a set of static rules or algorithms. On the other hand, complex problems, driven by multiple differing and often conflicting stakeholder interests, have no such degree of order, control, or predictability. 6 Six years after the first Learning Healthcare Project report and 13 years, since the Institute of Medicine popularized the concept, no nation, region, or individual healthcare provider has fully realized the promise of an LHS.

Key elements of these failures have been described and discussed by Kohn et al. 5 They underscored the need to envision an effective application of systems thinking—the ability to see an entire system holistically, recognizing that the individual components must be effectively interconnected and interoperable by design, not by convenience or happenstance, to achieve more than is possible by the individual components alone—that is, to achieve synergy of the whole.

Fortunately, rapid evolution has occurred in the application of systems thinking in a range of fields and enterprises, most notably in aviation, communications, energy, and technology, as well as healthcare. 6 Appreciation of the challenges in dealing with complexity has also increased. 7 , 8 Leveraging these advances by integrating not only technology but also operational processes, governing policies, and principles into a people‐centered care and research system—globally and domestically—may well be realizable. It necessarily begins with an understanding of what a true system is and the value propositions for each and all stakeholders.

In late 2022, Kohn et al 5 called for an inclusive national convening of thought leaders and innovators, encompassing the full spectrum of participants in our health and science enterprises, to envision what the future could be if we successfully think in systems terms. The desired outcome was to lay the foundation for an approach that considers how people, processes, policies, and technologies might work together to achieve a next‐generation Intelligent HealthScience System (IHSS) to realize this two‐decades‐old goal.

An IHSS, in contrast to an LHS, is a comprehensive approach to the integration of all elements of the healthcare enterprise, including cost and outcomes data, as well as scientific knowledge and biomedical research, systems thinking, and engineering principles by applying basic and medical research more effectively in the current health care environment through advanced technology, including artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning. Its systems approach focuses on improving health outcomes through an integrated holistic soft‐systems approach that encompasses people, processes, policies, and technologies.

The primary differences between an LHS and an IHSS lie in their focus, scope, and approach.

First, with respect to focus, an LHS emphasizes continuous improvement and innovation in health care by embedding knowledge generation processes into daily practice. An IHSS on the other hand focuses on creating a more efficient and interconnected health care system through application of systems thinking and engineering principles. It aims to enhance health outcomes through a soft‐systems holistic approach that includes people, processes, policies, and technologies.

There is also a major difference in the scope of these concepts. An LHS is primarily concerned with the health care delivery process and the integration of new knowledge into practice by “learning” from data acquired during patient care encounters to continuously improve care quality and efficiency. In contrast, an IHSS encompasses the entire health care and biomedical research ecosystem, including basic and medical research, the current health care environment, and advanced technology, as well as integration with payment, public health and social welfare processes and organizations. It aims to create a comprehensive system that promotes human health on a broader scale.

The approach to creating an LHS involves establishing a feedback loop where data from ongoing health care encounters are analyzed and used to improve future care. Most importantly, the approach to an IHSS applies systems thinking and engineering principles to address the complexity of health care and biomedical research. It aims to create a more integrated and efficient comprehensive system by considering the interactions between various components of the health care ecosystem—people, processes, policies, and technologies.

These differences in focus, scope, and approach clearly differentiate the two concepts. While both approaches aim to improve health care, the “LHS” approach focuses on continuous improvement within the care delivery process and its outcomes but falls short of a fully integrated system, whereas the IHSS takes a broader, more holistic approach to integrating health care and research and the processes for delivery, payment, and access that impact everyone.

In response to the call for action by Kohn et al (5), the global non‐profit Alliance for Clinical Research Excellence and Safety (ACRES) proposed and organized an envisioning exercise. The SYNERGY Forum, hosted by the National Academy of Medicine, met in Washington, DC, in December 2022. Bringing together diverse stakeholders in healthcare, science, technology, business, government, and patient advocacy, 9 the group undertook a fundamental challenge: to envision an integrated IHSS and to create a platform and an engine of people, processes, policy, and technology to design and realize such. This report, including group perspectives on persistent themes and minimal requirements for reform, summarizes and offers for consideration the rich experiences of the assembled leaders charged with this important challenge and sets up both a conceptual framework for designing an IHSS and an action plan of implementable effort.

THEMATIC PERSPECTIVES FROM FOCUSED BREAKOUT GROUPS

At SYNERGY, four multi‐disciplinary groups—in structured breakout sessions and interactive panel discussions among all parties—met over 2 days to discuss how to approach the design of an IHSS from different perspectives and emphases: Process, Policy, People, and Technology. Cross‐cutting discussions from each group were presented to the entire assembly for consideration. Goal: to find persistent themes across groups.

The most common shared themes across groups included:

Personal responsibility: Every individual actively takes part in the management of one's own health, including “owning” and “sharing” one's own health data in a standard structured digital format. Patients, in partnership with healthcare providers and advocates, assume responsibility for their health consistent with their abilities and desires.

“THE TABLE STAKES”—REQUIREMENTS FOR AN IHSS

In any gamble, whether at the casino or in a business or scientific endeavor, the minimal requirements to get into the game are known as “table stakes.” In a complex endeavor such as health‐system reform, these requirements can be far‐reaching, including costs, technologies, policies, and other factors, reflecting the baseline expectations that any player must meet to be considered a workable participant–simply put, “ante up or go home.” What are the table stakes for achieving an effective and responsive health system? This question was fundamental to the SYNERGY envisioning event reported here.

While some healthcare networks exhibiting characteristics of a learning health system do exist within the United States, they are still isolated, independent, and often competitive microcosms rather than a true LHS. Indeed, proprietary considerations and competition may perversely work against the desired goal of an integrated, accessible patient‐focused approach to safe and effective care. As noted by Kohn et al., 5 “an effective, integrated systems approach to health and science must encompass the entirety of healthcare and biomedical research—the people, processes, policies and technologies, and the many stakeholders, each with their own agendas and vested interests.”

SYNERGY's multi‐disciplinary stakeholder groups, in responding to the shared themes and exploring the dimensions of an effective system design from the four perspectives of people, processes, policy, and technology, identified the “table stakes”—that is, the “must have” requirements for developing an IHSS.

With urgency as a compelling underpinning for transformative change (vs. incremental change), the SYNERGY participants achieved consensus that, for real progress, all participants must subscribe to the following guiding principles as a foundation for creating a functional and equitable systems approach:

Target the neediest first .

Otherwise, overworked providers might simply shift responsibility for self‐care onto patients without helping patients to understand and agree with what “self‐management” means in each patient's case. The full spectrum of providers must engage in this effort to be effective.

This “convening authority” can also provide “sponsorship” of the effort. It can most profitably be a collaboration involving trusted third‐party partners that are innovative‐driven organizations dedicated to improving healthcare and research. More input can be solicited by an “authority” spanning, for example, systems dynamics, qualitative research, cultural anthropology, ethics, and Corporate Social Responsibility expertise, and social welfare groups. Inclusiveness can be enhanced by engaging community‐based organizations (CBOs) from not only health related entities but also from relevant supporting organizations. Eventually, extension to other groups, such as environmental, transportation and infrastructure organizations may be appropriate and necessary.

The scope of such a systems approach need not be limited to the United States. Indeed, global thinking with local action is a reasonable approach. Still, given the magnitude and complexity of the challenges faced, beginning at a national level and then expanding system capabilities internationally and ultimately globally, is possible, similar to the evolution of the global air‐transport and safety system that currently serves most of the world well, despite its imperfections.

Continued innovation is essential, including what has been referred to as “disruptive innovation.” To achieve an IHSS within a reasonable length of time will require a problem‐solving mentality to envision and design for the future, rather than only reforming for today. In “The Innovator's Prescription: A Disruptive Solution for Health Care,” the authors argue:

“… disruption is an innovation that makes things simpler and more affordable,” and “technology” is a way of combining inputs of materials, components, information, labor, and energy into outputs of greater value. (Christensen et al. 2 )

Progress toward improving health and healthcare through the application of technology, science, and social reform is clearly unsatisfactory. We have before us, yet again, another call for action to resolve the inequities, inefficiencies, and ineffectiveness of how we practice medicine and promote health.

This current call for action differs significantly in that it provides a new working framework, that is, a fully integrated systems approach. Effectively applied, this vision can overcome the barriers to progress that have stymied earlier efforts and prevented not just progress but also blocked the decent and ethical goals of achieving a better world in which all people share in and benefit from health and productivity. No one has yet designed architecture systems to meet these goals, but the rich collective experience of SYNERGY has helped to envision not only what is possible, but also a pathway to what is achievable.

CONCLUSION

The SYNERGY Forum identified many positive themes and goals, perhaps to the point of failing in its optimism to adequately acknowledge the many obstacles that have impeded progress for two decades, or to identify potential incentives for change and implementation. This must be an ongoing process. There is need to better understand the systemic issues that are responsible for the perpetuation of low‐value yet very costly care that has failed to yield the desired health outcome—a healthier, more productive populace. Yet there are still vexing social challenges that pose potential impediments. Is it possible to address such complex issues and reduce the provision of low value care without being too intrusive into the doctor/patient relationship? While equity, inclusion, empathy, and person‐centeredness are laudable goals and optimal outcomes are intended, ought there be ways to make allowances for some people who want to eat what they want, and continue to smoke, for example, even though it is not in their best health interests to do so? That incentives should address both positive reinforcement and negative/punitive sanctions is applicable to providers, but should there be patient incentives and sanctions? Many patients do not want to disrupt their lives and lifestyle to address “invisible problems” until they start feeling ill or their lifestyles are impacted negatively. How do we balance individual freedoms with broader social responsibilities, knowing that these patient behaviors increase societal costs of care?

Considering an IHSS as a change management project, one could readily list a multitude of obstacles (fear of the new, anxiety about the ability to adapt, fear of income loss, breaking with tradition, liability, too complex, too much at once) and discuss strategies, but doing so would suggest complete knowledge of those obstacles. We can predict obstacles, but the first thing to do is get acceptance of the idea that change is needed and to offer a pathway for how this effort can get started. Going forward, those involved in the design and implementation of an IHSS must critically examine these and other barriers and propose tangible strategies for overcoming them where evidence exists and suggest a research plan where more investigation is needed.

The SYNERGY Forum did not intend to offer a completed concept and blueprint for an IHSS as an idealized end‐state, but rather to offer a pragmatic approach and process through which to explore the incremental steps needed to transition from the current failed state to a new, more effective system. Addressing these complexities through an ongoing collaborative stakeholder‐driven process will be compelling and useful to policymakers, patients, and practitioners looking for actionable, effective approaches and solutions. The participants agreed that the event reported here should be only the first of a continuing series of such envisioning and planning exercises to build an IHSS.

The fundamental question remains—what can be done is largely known and desirable, but can this opportunity now be seized and realized?
