Modelling HIV-1 control and remission
Authors: Bharadwaj Vemparala, Shreya Chowdhury, Jérémie Guedj, Narendra M. Dixit
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Remarkable advances are being made in developing interventions for eliciting long-term remission of HIV-1 infection. The success of these interventions will obviate the need for lifelong antiretroviral therapy, the current standard-of-care, and benefit the millions living today with HIV-1. Mathematical modelling has made significant contributions to these efforts. It has helped elucidate the possible mechanistic origins of natural and post-treatment control, deduced potential pathways of the loss of such control, quantified the effects of interventions, and developed frameworks for their rational optimization. Yet, several important questions remain, posing challenges to the translation of these promising interventions. Here, we survey the recent advances in the mathematical modelling of HIV-1 control and remission, highlight their contributions, and discuss potential avenues for future developments.

Introduction

HIV/AIDS continues to be a major infectious disease challenge worldwide, with nearly 40 million people living with HIV-1 (PLWH) currently1. If left untreated, most PLWH experience progressive disease, with a gradual decline of their CD4 T cell counts culminating in AIDS in ~10 years. The current standard-of-care is combination antiretroviral therapy (ART), a cocktail of three drugs, which rapidly brings plasma virus down to undetectable levels and arrests disease progression1. ART, however, is not curative. Upon treatment cessation, the virus typically rebounds to pre-treatment levels with the resumption of progressive disease. ART must therefore be administered lifelong. Long-term ART not only creates issues of compliance, but is also associated with several adverse effects, which include weight gain, renal dysfunction, bone mineral density loss, and heightened risk of cardiovascular and neurodegenerative disorders1. There is an urgent need, therefore, for interventions that are either curative or elicit robust long-term remission. Devising such interventions is a major goal of HIV research today2.

Proof-of-concept of long-term remission is offered by two rare classes of PLWH: natural controllers and post-treatment controllers (Fig. 1a)3,4. Natural controllers spontaneously control the infection, without the need for any intervention (Fig. 1b). Post-treatment controllers experience long-term remission post-ART (Fig. 1c). In both these groups, the virus is thought not to be eradicated but remains at low enough levels that disease progression is arrested. Understanding the mechanisms underlying these control states is a key research goal today. Knowledge of the mechanisms may help trigger them in a broader population of, if not all, PLWH, obviating the need for lifelong ART5. Mathematical modelling is playing a crucial role in elucidating these mechanisms. The inability of ART to eradicate the virus stems from the early establishment of a latent reservoir that is long-lived, not targeted by ART or the immune system, and capable of reigniting progressive infection upon treatment cessation6. Significant efforts are underway to devise interventions that may help overcome this limitation of ART. These efforts are two-pronged: They aim to (1) suppress or eliminate the latent reservoir, and/or (2) strengthen the immune system to exert the necessary control following ART cessation1,2. These efforts, expectedly, gain from the current understanding of the mechanisms underlying natural and post-treatment control. Remarkable advances have been made in the recent years with interventions eliciting long-term remission in animal models with far greater success than ART7,8 and bringing promise of translation to humans9. Mathematical modelling is again playing an important role, aiding the understanding of the effects of these new interventions, both conceptually and quantitatively, and informing efforts for their translation and for the design of even more potent interventions. Highlighting these contributions of mathematical modelling is also a focus of this review.

The review is organized as follows. We start with a brief overview of the biology of HIV-1 infection, highlighting the different outcomes realized and the scope of ART. We then introduce the basic model of HIV-1 dynamics and its variants, which have helped understand the effect of ART and form the core of many modelling efforts. In Next, we present models aimed at understanding lasting and transient post-treatment control, followed by models of natural control. We end with our outlook on emerging interventions, the promises they hold, and the challenges they present.

Overview of HIV-1 infection and control

Excellent reviews present detailed descriptions of the biology, epidemiology, and treatment of HIV-1 infection (e.g., see ref. 1). Here, we summarize features of the within-host dynamics of HIV-1 infection that are central to the mathematical modelling of its control, highlighting the outcomes realized naturally and with ART.

Progressive disease

Following infection, the HIV-1 viral load in the plasma rises, attains a peak, and declines within a few weeks to a set point, where it lasts for years if untreated (Fig. 1b). The initial transients occur in the ‘primary’ phase of the infection. The attainment of the set-point marks the onset of the ‘chronic’ phase. In individuals who suffer progressive disease, which constitutes the vast majority of PLWH (Fig. 1a), the set-point is high, ~104–106 copies/mL. CD4 T cells are the primary target of HIV-1. After initial transients, the CD4 T cell count gradually declines, over many years, from the pre-infection level of ~800–1000 cells/μL to below ~200 cells/μL, the latter defined clinically as being associated with severe immune deficiency, or AIDS1.

Infected cells and the latent reservoir

Infection of a CD4 T cell leads to one of three fates: abortive infection, productive infection, and latent infection (Fig. 2a). Once inside a cell, HIV-1 RNA is reverse transcribed to DNA, which is then integrated into the host genome. In abortive infection, reverse transcription is incomplete and triggers pyroptotic cell death. 95% of the infection events in vivo are estimated to be abortive and are thought to drive the CD4 T cell count decline10,11. Successful reverse transcription and integration can lead to productive infection, with the integrated DNA, termed a provirus, serving as a template for viral transcription and progeny virion production. Less than 5% of the infection events are productive. Most proviruses are ‘defective’ due to the high error rate in reverse transcription and do not yield infectious progeny virions. The small fraction of replication-competent, or ‘intact’, proviruses yields progeny virions that can infect other uninfected cells. Typically, an infected cell produces 103–104 virus particles in its lifespan of ~1 day12. Productively infected cells die due to virus-induced cytopathic effects and/or effector responses of immune cells. Following successful integration, a tiny fraction of cells become ‘latently’ infected. These cells, predominantly of the resting memory phenotype6, constitute the latent reservoir. They remain transcriptionally silent, producing no virus. They can proliferate, however, and are long-lived, with half-lives extending over many months. Antiretroviral therapy

ART is typically initiated in the chronic phase of the infection when the viral load has attained its set point (Fig. 1c). Depending on the drugs used, it can block viral entry, reverse transcription, integration and/or the production steps of the viral life cycle1. It brings the virus down within weeks to undetectable levels (< 50 copies/mL) and maintains it there. Cessation of ART causes viral recrudescence, often within weeks, to levels similar to the pre-treatment set-point (Fig. 1c). The recrudescence is due to the stochastic reactivation of latently infected cells, which remain hidden from ART and the immune system13.

Natural controllers

In natural controllers, also called spontaneous controllers, which form < 1% of the individuals infected14, the set-point viral load is far lower than in progressors, typically < 2000 copies/mL. Of the natural controllers, ‘elite controllers’ have set points below 50 copies/mL and ‘viremic controllers’ between 50 and 2000 copies/mL1. CD4 T cell counts do not decline substantially in natural controllers. Natural controllers have a higher representation of ‘protective’ HLA class I haplotypes1, mount superior CD8 T cell responses15,16, and display a preference for provirus integration in the relatively inaccessible heterochromatin regions of the host genome17,18. How these factors help control the virus is yet to be fully understood. Complicating matters is the existence of ‘viremic non-progressors’, individuals with set-points comparable to progressors but little CD4 T cell count decline19.

Post-treatment controllers

Post-treatment controllers have high pre-treatment viral loads, like progressors, but low viral loads, resembling natural controllers, post-ART4,20. Early treatment initiation tends to improve the chances of post-treatment control in humans20 and animal models21,22. Depending on the definition used and the timing of treatment initiation, ~5–15% of treated individuals display post-treatment control20. Post-treatment controllers tend to have a significantly lower proviral burden21,23, lower T cell activation levels24 and improved CD4 T cell and NK cell responses23, compared to progressors. Again, how these features are responsible for post-treatment control remains to be fully understood.

Basic model of HIV-1 dynamics and its variants

The basic model of within-host HIV-1 dynamics, developed in the 1990s, considered processes essential to describing the set-point viral load and short-term changes in viremia from the set-point following initiation of treatment with antiretroviral drugs25,26. It thus modelled the time evolution of well-mixed populations of uninfected target CD4 T cells, productively infected cells, and free virions. At the set point, the production and loss rates were in balance for each population. Antiretroviral treatment disturbed this balance. Analysis of viral load changes triggered by this imbalance yielded estimates of viral and infected cell turnover rates. The high rates of turnover together with the high mutation rate of HIV-1 made evident the vulnerability of individual drugs to resistance and prompted the use of combination therapy25,26. Three drug combinations thus became the standard-of-care and remain so to this day.

The basic model was subsequently advanced to incorporate many additional features, including different target and infected cell subpopulations, drug pharmacokinetics, immune responses, and viral evolutionary processes, which offered useful insights into viral pathogenesis and the response to interventions. Modelling post-treatment control

Post-treatment control has been defined differently across studies, depending on the viral level associated with control and the duration, the latter varying from a few months to several years, over which the control was observed4,20. Here, we categorize these outcomes into two fundamentally distinct types: (1) control that is expected to last indefinitely, and (2) control over a (possibly long but) finite duration. In the VISCONTI study, which first reported well-characterized post-ART control, in the 14 post-treatment controllers identified, control was observed for a median of ~7.5 years, with many maintaining control till the end of the reported study period (> 10 years)4. In the CHAMP study, which analysed data from 14 different studies, ~20% of the 67 post-treatment controllers identified maintained control for > 5 years20. While subsequent loss of control cannot be ruled out, the long period of control observed in these individuals may be indicative of its durability. In other settings, control is transient and may last only weeks post-ART31. Whether the underlying mechanisms predominant in these two scenarios are different is yet to be established32. Mathematical modelling efforts have dealt with the scenarios differently: models of lasting control have identified control as a distinct steady state of the system, akin to the set-point in progressors but at a lower viral level. On the other hand, models of transient control have considered processes that lead to viral breakthroughs, predominantly via latency reversal and the ensuing breach of immune control. In this section, we focus on the former class of models. The latter are described in subsequent sections.

Control with ART

Inspired by the VISCONTI study, Conway and Perelson developed a model of viral dynamics that predicted the existence of a second steady state, an alternative set-point, representative of viral control33. Building on the growing evidence of the role of CD8 T cells (see below), they explicitly considered the effector CD8 T cell compartment. In particular, they considered the killing of infected cells by the effectors, the proliferation of CD8 T cells upon antigenic stimulation, and following previous studies34,35, their exhaustion at high levels of antigenic exposure (Fig. 3a). CD8 T cell exhaustion is triggered by sustained antigen exposure, is common to infections and cancers, and can be reversed at least in part upon suppressing antigen36. The resulting model predicted bistability over a range of values of the model parameters33. One steady state had high viremia and marked progressive infection, whereas the other had low viremia and represented control. The model predicted that low sizes of the latent reservoir and high CD8 T cell mediated killing rates of infected cells would drive the infection to the latter state of control post-treatment. The high viremia following de novo infection triggered CD8 T cell exhaustion and progressive infection. ART brought viremia down, which in turn reduced exhaustion. When the latent reservoir was small at the time of treatment cessation, the viral burst upon latency reactivation was likely to be small and could be controlled by the now better-primed CD8 T cells, especially when their killing efficiency was high. If the reservoir was large or the killing efficiency poor, the rebound viremia rose quickly, again triggering CD8 T cell exhaustion and progressive disease. The model, thus, offered a plausible explanation of the control observed post-ART. Subsequent studies have argued that bistability is common to many viral infections and helps understand the diversity of the outcomes they elicit37–39. Control with antibody therapy

An alternative intervention that has resulted in higher success rates of post-treatment control than ART has been the passive administration of HIV-1 broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs)7,9. These therapies are in human clinical trials9. Models have thus focussed on data from animal studies. In one such study, with three doses of a combination of two bNAbs administered soon after SHIV infection, 10 of 13 macaques exhibited viremic control for years7. The control was attributed to CD8 T cells because viremia resurged temporarily upon depleting CD8 T cells and recovered upon their restoration. Interestingly, in one macaque, viremia remained at high levels post-CD8 depletion even after the restoration of the CD8 T cells, marking a potential realization of the two distinct steady states in the same macaque.

Desikan et al. developed a model of HIV-1 dynamics with early bNAb therapy to describe the above data40. In addition to the CD8 T cell compartment, the model considered the pleiotropic effects of bNAbs41 (Fig. 3b), explicitly incorporating enhanced viral clearance and improved antigen presentation. Remarkably, the model provided excellent fits to longitudinal data of viral load from the macaques. It also offered an explanation of the greater success of bNAb therapy compared to ART in achieving post-treatment control. bNAbs not only lowered viremia and reversed CD8 T cell exhaustion, like ART, but they also increased CD8 T cell stimulation via improved antigen presentation, thus leaving a primed CD8 T cell pool to combat resurgent viremia post-treatment. The controller state was thus made more accessible than with ART. In other words, bNAb therapy effected a dynamical switch from progressive disease to lasting control more efficiently than ART. A challenge in translating this success to humans is the need to treat early after infection. Late treatment initiation appears to compromise the success of such treatments as well as ART21,42, the reasons for which remain to be elucidated. bNAbs appear to show greater promise when combined with latency-reversing agents or other drugs/vaccines8,9,43,44, which we discuss below.

Modelling transient post-treatment control

When interventions fail to establish lasting control, it is important to know why. Models have helped elucidate the causes and, in some cases, suggested quantitative targets for interventions to avert such failure. We classify these models into three broad categories: (1) Models of CD8 T cell mediated control; (2) Models of bNAb mediated control; and (3) Models of therapies targeting the latent reservoir. We discuss recent developments in each of these categories.

CD8 T cell-mediated control

This class of models aims to describe the mechanisms by which CD8 T cells exert viremic control and the mechanisms that lead to its loss. Many of the underlying mechanisms are common to the loss of natural and post-treatment control. Thus, some of the models discussed here apply to both scenarios. Modelling CD8 T cell responses can be quite involved, for they can be pleiotropic, cooperative, and multi-scale45. An ongoing debate, for instance, is on whether CD8 T cells predominantly exert cytolytic or non-cytolytic effects on virus-infected cells46–52, an issue discussed at length elsewhere45. Models of HIV-1 control have thus used approximate descriptions of CD8 T cell activity. When viral evolution is ignored, the models consider activation, effector function, and exhaustion of CD8 T cells and thus become similar in structure to models of post-treatment control above33,40. They may be extended to account for different CD8 T cell compartments, such as naïve, effector, and memory45,53. With this structure, loss of control occurs because of inadequate strength of the CD8 T cell response, either due to inadequate stimulation54 or due to exhaustion33. Models have then helped estimate the reduction in the latent reservoir, via, for instance, the use of latency-reversing agents along with ART, that would be required to prevent such viral breakthrough27.

More detailed models explicitly consider different CD8 T cell clones and the viral epitopes they recognize55–62. The models attempt to estimate the timing/rate of immune escape and to elucidate the evolutionary pathways involved (Fig. 4). They often mirror models developed previously to describe the development of resistance to antiretroviral drugs63–65. By examining the rate at which escape mutants at specific epitopes outgrow the wild-type, models have estimated the selection pressure exerted by CD8 T cells55–59. When escape mutations must be accompanied by compensatory mutations, recombination and epistatic effects can alter the evolutionary pathways in complex ways60. Further, analyses of larger genome segments–beyond those targeted by CD8 T cells, including, in some cases, full-length genomes–indicate that the evolution at any epitope may depend sensitively on the genomic background61,62. Recently, by employing large sequence databases and applying techniques from statistical physics to quantify the underlying fitness landscape, Barton et al. were able to simulate within-host viral evolution and predict the timing and the specific loci at which escape variants would emerge62. The predictions agreed well with observations from a cohort of 17 individuals in the acute infection phase. More recently, applying network theory to HIV-1 protein structures, residues of topological importance were identified, targeting which resulted in greater control66. These insights bring us a step closer to the design of potent CD8 T cell vaccines. The extent to which the findings apply to natural versus post-treatment control remains to be ascertained.Fig. 4Viral evolution and immune escape.Schematic showing mutation and recombination creating diverse viral genomes (middle), facilitating the selection of variants that escape antibody (left) and CD8 T cell (right) mediated immune pressures. Antibody-mediated control

bNAbs have typically been administered towards the end of ART, either just before or soon after the cessation of ART, to improve the chances of post-ART control. The effects of bNAbs in exerting viremic control in the absence of viral evolution have been modelled akin to the pleotropic effects considered in the studies above40,67,68 (Fig. 3b). The studies show, using fits of model variants to data, that bNAbs act by enhancing viral clearance as well as by increasing infected cell death67,68. Similar conclusions were arrived at with models describing the activity of synthetic antibodies, specifically eCD4-Ig69,70. When therapy involved a single bNAb, mutants resistant to the bNAb often emerged71–73. Models have been developed to describe the loss of bNAb mediated control due to the emergence and growth of such resistant strains68,74,75 (Fig. 4).

Because viral replication is nearly completely halted with ART, the emergence of resistant strains during ART is unlikely64. Interestingly, when the bNAb VRC01 was administered at the end of successful ART, viral breakthrough due to resistant strains was observed even while VRC01 concentrations in circulation were above in vitro suppressive levels72. It appeared thus that VRC01 resistant strains may have been formed before ART initiation, harboured in latently infected cells through ART, and caused breakthrough during VRC01 treatment post-ART. To test this hypothesis, a model was developed to predict the frequency of VRC01 resistant strains pre-ART and their re-emergence from the latent reservoir during VRC01 treatment74. The model considered mutation, recombination, and fitness selection pre-ART, like with models estimating the standing genetic variation of HIV-176–78. It predicted that single but not higher mutant strains resistant to VRC01 were likely to exist in the latent reservoir. Using stochastic simulations of latency reactivation and creating a virtual patient population to mimic inter-patient variations, the model successfully recapitulated the distribution of VRC01 breakthrough times observed in clinical trials72,74. The model offered a framework to predict the outcomes of other dosing protocols, via changing dosages or bNAb half-lives, enabling therapy optimization.

More recently, LaMont et al. developed a sophisticated population genetics-based framework to predict optimal bNAb combinations that could avert resistance75. They employed large amounts of viral sequence data from untreated individuals as well as data from deep mutational scans to identify potential escape mutations against specific bNAbs, the mutational pathways leading from the wild-type to the resistant strain, and the associated fitness costs. They then applied their population genetics framework to predict viral evolution under bNAb therapy. They predicted, in agreement with the model above74, that resistance was likely to arise from pre-existing strains. They also found that the spectrum of mutational pathways and the associated probabilities of their traversal dictated the likelihood and the timing of resistance at specific loci against given bNAbs. Remarkably, the model recapitulated the distribution of breakthrough times from three clinical trials. Applying the model, they then predicted that a cocktail of three bNAbs was necessary to avert the emergence of early resistance (< 56 d) with a high probability (> 0.99)75. Future studies may test these promising predictions.

An intriguing effect of bNAb therapy has been its ability to improve the endogenous humoral response79,80: For instance, administration of the bNAb 3BNC117 improved the neutralizing ability of the endogenous antibodies significantly over those present before therapy in chronically infected individuals79. Harnessing this vaccinal effect of bNAbs may aid in designing interventions for long-term control. Garg et al. developed a hypothesis to explain this effect81. They reasoned, following earlier studies82, that administered bNAbs would enter germinal centres, the sites of antibody affinity maturation83, and increase B cell selection stringency. In germinal centres, B cells compete for antigen that is presented complexed to antibodies on the surfaces of dendritic cells. B cells must extract the antigen for survival. Administered bNAbs that form high-affinity complexes with the antigen would make this extraction difficult. Only those B cells with receptors that have even higher affinities for the antigen would survive on average. Thus, administered bNAbs would increase the selection stringency for B cells in the germinal centres. Using a stochastic simulation model of the GC reaction, building on earlier descriptions84,85, Garg et al. tested this hypothesis81. The simulations corroborated the hypothesis and unravelled a quality-quantity trade-off constraining the germinal centre reaction. The higher the affinity of the administered bNAbs, the better the quality of the B cells selected and hence antibodies produced. However, because fewer B cells would thus get selected, the magnitude of the antibody output would be lowered. The trade-off was consistent with experiments82. Garg et al. applied the simulations to predict optimal bNAb dosing protocols for maximizing the humoral response factoring the trade-off81.

Models of the germinal centre reaction have also been used to predict optimal vaccination strategies84,86. The natural evolution of bNAbs is rare and, when it does occur, slow87. Designing vaccines to elicit bNAbs efficiently has been challenging88. Precursor B cells that could evolve to produce bNAbs occur infrequently and bind rarely to unmutated viral antigens. Thus, immunogens are now being designed that could target the precursor B cells, in a strategy called germline targeting, and then drive their evolution in germinal centres to bNAb producing cells88. Simulations such as the above may aid this immunogen design89,90. Predictions of such simulations remain to be tested experimentally.

Targeting the latent reservoir

Because reactivation of latently infected cells is the primary cause of the loss of viremic control, efforts have been ongoing to devise strategies to reduce the chances of this reactivation. One strategy is to reduce the size of the latent reservoir. Latency reversing agents (LRAs) help achieve this by reactivating latently infected cells, which may then be eliminated by the immune system—a strategy popularly termed ‘shock-and-kill’. To aid such efforts, modelling studies have predicted remission times as a function of the latent reservoir size and thereby estimated the extent to which LRAs must reduce the reservoir size for desired remission91,92. Models have also described the dynamics of latency reversal93–97. The first generation LRAs, however, while reactivating latently infected cells, did not succeed in reducing the reservoir size significantly98. Surprisingly, even following stem cell transplantation (see below), which lowered infected cell populations by up to ~5 log10, viral rebound was observed within six months of treatment cessation99–101, highlighting the challenge involved. More recent LRAs, which work by stimulating diverse innate and adaptive immune pathways, appear more successful in reducing the latent reservoir and inducing lasting remission102. The agents are now being used, even more promisingly, in conjunction with NK cells103 or bNAbs8. Interestingly, in a recent study with the latter LRA-bNAb combination, a fraction of the macaques treated displayed no rebound viremia post-treatment despite depletion of CD8 T cells, suggesting a state of control that may be far deeper than those requiring CD8 T cell support8. Whether this state resembles or approaches the state of the near absence of the virus, including in the latent reservoir, seen in a rare natural controller104, remains to be ascertained. Future modelling efforts may help better understand these possible states and optimize interventions.

Other, less evolved strategies to reduce the latent reservoir include stem cell transplantation and gene editing-based approaches. Remarkably, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) from donors carrying a homozygous deletion in the chemokine receptor CCR5, a receptor essential for virus entry, resulted in a cure in a few instances105,106. The risks associated with the procedure, however, restrict its use to cases that require it for treating other life-threatening conditions. Gene editing techniques based on zinc-finger nucleases or CRISPR/Cas-9 systems have been garnering interest107. For instance, autologous CD4 T cells with their CCR5 genes edited using zinc-finger nucleases were infused into PLWH to assess their ability to control viremia post-ART108. The treatment led to a delay in rebound and in some individuals improved CD8 T cell responses which were associated with the reestablishment of control, although transiently, post rebound108. CRISPR/Cas-9 based approaches are also being used to edit out proviral DNA from latently infected cells107. Models have been employed to inform the usage of such strategies, although they remain less well-studied than latency reversing agents. For instance, adapting a previous model of stochastic latency reversal91 to the transplantation setting, a model predicted that the chance of viral rebound was negligible when donor chimerism (a measure of the fraction of CD4 T cells from the donor) was > 90% post HSCT106.

A challenge to such efforts is the diversity of the CD4 T cell subsets constituting the reservoir and their widely distributed anatomical sites109. The chimerism may not be uniform across such sites. Furthermore, the different subsets have been shown to respond differently to latency reversing agents110,111. Besides, like with ART drugs112, drug penetrance may vary across sites. Models of temporal and spatial variation of ART drug levels have been developed to aid understanding of viral persistence and the evolution of drug resistance63,113,114. Similar models are yet to be developed for latency targeting agents. Importantly, human and non-human primate studies have helped quantify the reservoir burden in different anatomical sites as well as their response to latency reversing agents115,116. Advancing spatio-temporal ART models to latency targeting strategies, constrained by the latter reservoir data, would enable more accurate predictions of the outcomes of such strategies.

An alternative, and opposite, strategy to lower the chances of latency reactivation is to drive latently infected cells into potentially deeper states of latency. The major contributor to the maintenance of the reservoir in PLWH on ART has been argued to be the proliferation of latently infected cells117,118. Thus, anti-proliferative agents may help achieve such deeper latency states and prevent viral recrudescence. Modelling has been used to estimate the fold-change in reservoir size and treatment durations required for viral control under anti-proliferative agents119. A recent study observed, however, that latently infected cells had impaired proliferation capacity compared to uninfected cells120. Further, cells harbouring intact proviruses underwent fewer rounds of proliferation than those with defective proviruses. Thus, alternative routes to driving latently infected cells into deeper states of latency than possible with anti-proliferative therapies may have to be explored.

Modelling natural control

We finally turn to models of natural control. Fewer modelling studies have examined natural control than post-treatment control. An important conceptual advance was made by Košmrlj et al.121, who addressed the role of the protective HLA class I haplotypes, like HLA-B*57, in eliciting natural control. CD8 T cells in natural controllers tend to target a broader spectrum of viral variants than in progressors. They reasoned that this greater cross-reactivity was a consequence of thymic selection of CD8 T cells orchestrated by alleles such as HLA-B*57. The latter alleles are known to present fewer self-peptides in the thymus. During thymic selection, CD8 T cells that recognize self-peptides bound to HLA molecules with an affinity higher than a certain threshold are eliminated, a process termed negative selection. Also, cells that recognize no self-peptides with an affinity higher than another, lower threshold are eliminated, via positive selection. Cells that pass both these tests exit the thymus and serve as functioning CD8 T cells, targeting cells that present peptides that they recognize with affinities larger than the negative selection threshold. With HLA-B*57 presenting fewer self-peptides, thymic selection yields CD8 T cells that recognize peptides by binding to a few loci on them with high affinities. On the other hand, alleles such as HLA-B*8, which present many more self-peptides, yield CD8 T cells that recognize peptides by binding to a larger number of loci on the peptides but with lower affinities. Consequently, CD8 T cells restricted by HLA-B*57 can recognize viral variants with mutations at many more loci, particularly the loci at which they tend to bind weakly. They thus display greater cross-reactivity than cells restricted by other alleles like HLA-B*8. The model of HIV-1 dynamics above was extended to account for this differential reactivity of CD8 T cells. Viral evolution via mutations was also incorporated to describe potential immune escape. The model predicted that individuals with more cross-reactive CD8 T cells exhibited better viral control121.

More recently, the protective effect of HLA-B*57 has been argued to depend on the expression of inhibitory killer immunoglobulin-like receptors (iKIRs)122,123. iKIRs are typically expressed on natural killer (NK) cells but are also expressed on CD8 T cells. HLA class I molecules belong to the ligands of iKIRs. Remarkably, the greater the number of iKIRs expressed, the greater was the viral control observed in HLA-B*57+ individuals123. iKIRs have been shown to, among other effects, increase the lifespan of CD8 T cells. Using a model similar to those above, Boelen et al. showed how the increased CD8 T cell lifespan could lead to improved viral control with protective HLA alleles123. Interestingly, the diminished control with non-protective alleles was also amplified by iKIR expression, consistently with experimental observations123. Thus, by enhancing CD8 T cell lifespan, iKIRs tend to amplify both the increased and diminished control mediated by CD8 T cells in an HLA-dependent manner.

Recognizing the importance of the breadth of antigen presentation in exerting control, Viard et al.124 have recently constructed a metric of the ‘functional divergence’ of HLA class I allotype pairs to quantify the diversity of the peptides presented by them. They grouped HIV-1 epitope 9-mer peptides into clusters, called submotifs, that could be presented by individual allotypes. They then estimated the proportion of the submotifs that differed between the allotype pair at any HLA class I locus. This then was an estimate of the functional divergence of the allotype pair. The greater the divergence, the more diverse the set of peptides presented by the pair. Remarkably, they found a strong association between functional divergence and viremic control as well as slower progression to AIDS124.

While these insights into the role of HLA alleles are valuable, they may not be adequate for predicting the occurrence of natural control; protective alleles are neither necessary nor sufficient for natural control32. The response of the CD8 T cells, whether restricted by the protective alleles or not, maybe more predictive of control. Indeed, CD8 T cell targeting of highly networked residues, which we mentioned earlier, was associated with better viremic control independently of the HLA alleles66. To assess the dynamics of the CD8 T cell response and its effect on CD8 T cell control, a multiscale framework was recently developed to simultaneously analyse longitudinal in vivo measurements of viral load and ex vivo measurements of CD8 T cell activity125. The ex vivo measurements reported the ‘suppressive capacity’ of CD8 T cells, estimated by the reduction in the viral load they affect in the ex vivo infection cultures126. The framework developed analytical approximations of the ex vivo cultures that allowed analysis of this complex in vivo-ex vivo dataset. The model provided good fits to data from macaques and predicted, interestingly, that the suppressive capacity of the CD8 T cells within the first few weeks of infection was a correlate of long-term viral control. Future studies may evaluate the applicability of the correlation to humans.

Outlook

The progress in recent years in the development of interventions for long-term HIV-1 remission has been impressive. Mathematical models have made significant contributions to this progress. They have helped understand the potential origins of natural and post-treatment control, quantified the effects of interventions, offered mechanistic insights into their strengths and weaknesses, and devised frameworks for their optimization. Yet, our understanding of HIV-1 control remains incomplete and limits our ability to elicit it in most PLWH. This inability beckons further advances, including through new lines of investigation, via both experimentation and mathematical modelling. Below, we list a subset of the many outstanding questions that offer promising routes for future advances and to addressing which mathematical modelling can make important contributions.

Given the remarkable success of bNAbs in eliciting control, especially in conjunction with latency-reversing agents7,8, it would be fruitful to devise a framework that combines the effects of these agents and enables treatment optimization. Would such optimal combinations be successful in eliciting long-term remission in nearly all PLWH? If not, or even otherwise, is personalization of such combinations possible, especially given the emerging insights into the roles of iKIRs123 and HLA allotype pairs124 as well as the ability to use large volumes of genomic sequence and allied phenotypic data62,75?

Recent interventions with bNAbs appear to elicit two distinct states of control, one that is dependent on CD8 T cells and one that appears not to be7,8. It would be important to know the difference(s) between these states and the mechanisms that underlie them. Similarly, it is important to know whether the state of natural control is distinct from that of post-treatment control.

Efforts are ongoing to combine passive immunization and vaccination44. A model of the germinal centre reaction that synthesizes the effects of these interventions may help predict their combined vaccinal effect. The combinations may then be optimized to maximize the vaccinal effect, including via the generation of adequate bNAbs, and hence the chances of control. In these efforts, as well as in other combination studies, it would be important to assess whether the drugs would synergize or be constrained by the synergy-efficacy trade-off seen in a variety of settings127. The efforts may also have to contend with the low ceiling to affinity maturation in the germinal centres, the origins of which remain to be fully understood128.

Yet another intriguing observation is the diminishing chance of post-treatment control with the delay in treatment initiation21,22. What distinguishes early from late treatment initiation is yet to be understood. Further, it would be important to know how this dependence on the timing of treatment initiation differs between ART and bNAb therapies.

Finally, a number of new interventions are on the anvil (Table 1). Existing models may have to be advanced or new models developed to quantify their efficacies.
