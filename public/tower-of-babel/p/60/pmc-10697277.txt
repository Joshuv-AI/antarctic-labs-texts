Introduction

A volvulus is a serious surgical emergency caused by torsion or hyper-flexion of a bowel loop and its mesentery on a fixed point. Cecal volvulus was first identified as an important cause of intestinal strangulation by an Austrian pathologist, Rokitansky, in 1837 [1].

The presence of a long mesentery with a narrow base of fixation to the retro-peritoneum and an abnormally long and tortuous bowel increases the risk of developing volvulus.

Cecal volvulus is an uncommon cause of intestinal obstruction, accounting for just 1–1.5 % of all cases of bowel obstruction. The annual incidence of cecal volvulus is estimated to be between 2.8 and 7.1 cases per million [2].

Patients typically show clinical features of intestinal obstruction or small bowel ischemia, gangrene, and perforation. An abdominal CT scan confirms the diagnosis of volvulus. All cases of cecal volvulus need emergent surgery. The type of surgery depends on the viability of the intestine. Segmental resection is the treatment of choice for non-viable or gangrenous cecum. In cases where the bowel is viable, there are options such as manual detorsion alone or detorsion with fixation to the abdominal wall either by suture (cecopexy) or cecostomy [3].

Early diagnosis and treatment is crucial as cecal volvulus has high morbidity and mortality rates.

This case report has been reported in line with SCARE Criteria [4].

Cecopexy was performed with two Vicryl 3/0 sutures (suturing the cecum to the retro-peritoneum in the right iliac fossa), and the whole gut was readjusted. As the appendix was inflamed and had fecolith, an appendectomy was performed.

1.5 L of ascitic fluid was drained intra-operatively.

The patient had an uneventful postoperative course. There was a decrease in leukocyte count to 10,410/μL. The histopathology report of the appendix revealed acute appendicitis. He was discharged on oral medications on the 4th postoperative day. The patient is still on follow-up since June 2023, and no complications have occurred.

Discussion

Cecal volvulus with concurrent acute appendicitis is an infrequent presentation. To our knowledge, only three cases have been reported in the medical literature [5].

Cecal volvulus is the twisting or rotation of a hyper-mobile cecum around its mesentery, which ultimately causes intestinal obstruction. If it remains undiagnosed, it may cause impairment of blood supply, intestinal perforation, and fecal peritonitis [6].

There are three different types of cecal volvulus.Type 1: It forms by a clockwise axial torsion of the cecum. The location of the cecum in this type is the right iliac fossa or right lower quadrant.Type 2: It forms by twisting of the cecum and a part of the terminal ileum. The cecum is dislocated and mostly found in left hypochondrium.Type 3: The cecum folds upon itself. It is also known as a cecal bascule [7].

Cecal volvulus can occur due to other factors such as intestinal bands/adhesions, tumor, pregnancy, long mesentery, abdominal or pelvic mass, abdominal injury, large bowel obstruction, psychological disorders, sedentary lifestyle, mega-colon, and chronic constipation [7].

Common clinical features of cecal volvulus include colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, and tympanic and distended abdomen.

Laboratory findings are not diagnostic. Increased white blood cells and metabolic acidosis may indicate sepsis, ischemia, or peritonitis [9].

Abdominal radiographs may not be as effective in diagnosing cecal volvulus. There can be three typical signs present on an abdominal radiograph:•Cecum dilatation•Single air-fluid level in the right lower quadrant•Absence of gas in the colon [10]

Barium enema has a sensitivity of 88 % for diagnosing cecal volvulus. However, it is not recommended for patients suspected of gut perforation or necrotic bowel due to the risk of contrast extravasation [11].

CT scan is the most preferred investigation for diagnosing acute cecal volvulus.

The CT scan may show signs of ischemia or gut perforation, such as pneumoatosis intestinalis or pneumoperitoneum [7].

CT scan has a sensitivity of about 90 % for diagnosing cecal volvulus. However, in 10 % of the cases, it is diagnosed intra-operatively.

The CT scan of the case presented did not show any typical signs, and the diagnosis was made intra-operatively.

Our patient had raised serum lipase, initially raising the suspicion of acute pancreatitis. Both conditions may be related to the pancreas' inability to secrete against increased pressure caused by the cecum in the left hypochondrium. Fang et al. reported a case of cecal volvulus presenting as recurrent pancreatitis [12].

Cecal volvulus is a surgical emergency that requires immediate surgery and can be fatal if not managed promptly.

Operative treatment includes endoscopic reduction, manual detorsion, cecopexy, cecostomy, and right colectomy by open-midline laparotomy or laparoscopic approaches.

Endoscopic reduction is generally not recommended due to the low chance of success and high risk of intestinal perforation.

The surgical procedure is decided upon the intra-operative findings.

In case of viable bowel, readjustment of bowel and cecopexy is the preferred option.

In the case of necrotic bowel, the non-viable bowel must be resected.

The case presented had a viable bowel due to early surgical intervention, and manual detorsion with cecopexy was performed.

An experienced General surgeon and his team managed the patient in a tertiary care hospital.

Conclusion

Cecal volvulus is a rare cause of intestinal obstruction with multiple etiologies. The patient's history and physical examination may not precisely diagnose it. CT scan is the preferred imaging modality for diagnosing it. Early diagnosis and emergent surgery is necessary to avoid life-threatening complications such as bowel ischemia and perforation. The surgical procedure highly depends on the intra-operative viability of the bowel.

The successful outcome in this case was due to early intervention as the bowel was viable intraoperatively, for which manual detorsion and cecopexy was performed.
