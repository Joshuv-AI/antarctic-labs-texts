Intelligent microneedle patch with cobalt-iron Prussian blue nanozymes for accelerating diabetic wound healing via heme biosynthesis-driven immunomodulation
Authors: Yutong Zhu, Yichen Kuang, Runjie Miao, Mingliang Ning, Hangrong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A complex wound microenvironment with the presence of bacterial infection, overproduction of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), chronic inflammation, poor vascularization and hypoxia wound result in delayed healing of diabetic wounds. In this study, a novel antibacterial microneedle patch integrating cobalt-iron Prussian blue (CFP) nanoenzymes loaded with prodrug 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) for intelligent, multi-stage therapeutic intervention on diabetic infected wound healing. Specifically, ALA@CFP demonstrated peroxidase (POD)-like activity to initiate the in situ production of hydroxyl radicals in response to elevated H2O2 in the wound, which proficiently induced bacterial ferroptosis and dismantled the bacterial biofilms, while the catalase (CAT)-like activity alleviated hypoxia wound conditions via decomposing H2O2 to produce oxygen. Simultaneously, Fe2+ released from ALA@CFP facilitated the transformation of 5-ALA to heme, significantly amplifying downstream heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) activity and producing endogenous anti-inflammatory mediators, carbon monoxide and bilirubin. These molecules subsequently restructured the inflammatory wound microenvironment by instigating the polarization of macrophages from the M1-phenotype to the pro-repair M2-phenotype and upregulated the expression of anti-inflammatory factors, thereby fostering cell migration and angiogenesis. When embedded in a methacrylated gelatin/carboxymethyl chitosan hydrogel microneedle matrix, the system enables on-demand deep tissue delivery, achieving simultaneous antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic and regenerative effects in an infected diabetic mouse model. This study demonstrates a material-driven, metabolism-amplified strategy for intelligent wound repair, providing a promising platform for next-generation functional biomaterials.

Introduction

The 2021 report from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) speculates that the number of adults with diabetes will reach 643 million by 2030 and is predicted to rise to 784 million by 2045 [1]. In recent years, the incidence and associated healthcare costs of chronic diabetic wounds have exhibited an upward trajectory, imposing a considerable burden on patients' quality of life and global public health [2]. As is known to all, the process of skin wound healing is divided into a series of ordered phases, including inflammation, cell proliferation and tissue remodeling [3]. However, the wound healing process can be severely restrained in the case of diabetic wounds. Various adverse effects involved with bacterial infection, overexpression of pro-inflammatory factors, disruption of redox homeostasis, impaired angiogenesis, hypoxia and physiological dysfunction of the epidermal keratinocytes caused by prolonged hyperglycemia occurred at wound site, which severely hinder the wound healing process [[4], [5], [6]]. The current clinical modalities targeting diabetic wounds are predominantly focused on debridement, surgical intervention, antibiotic therapy [7,8]. Nevertheless, these strategies fail to comprehensively tackle the intricate wound environment in an integrated manner, leading to suboptimal therapeutic outcomes. Therefore, a comprehensive and multifunctional therapeutic strategy is in great demand to inhibit bacterial infection and hyperinflammation, while simultaneously facilitating cell migration and angiogenesis, thereby mitigating the unwanted burden at diabetic wounds.

Metal-based nanomaterials, including noble metals, metal oxides, composite metal-carbon and metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), have been developed for the purpose of mimicking catalytic functions of natural enzymes, such as catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD) or oxidase [[9], [10], [11]]. Compared to natural enzymes (such as peroxidases or catalases) which are often limited by the lack of versatility, those nanoenzymes possess multiple enzymatic activities which are more multifunctional and potential in wound conditions [12,13]. As typical MOF nanoparticles, cobalt-iron bimetallic-based coordination polymers (CPs) present great promises in drug delivery and antioxidation purposes owing to its large surface area, high porosity and enzymatic activities. It has been demonstrated that the metal ions within cobalt-iron nanomaterials can undergo abundant valence changes (Co2+↔Co3+, Fe2+↔Fe3+) to exert diverse enzyme catalytic activities [14,15]. For example, Pang et al. constructed a cobalt-iron Prussian blue analog (CoFe PBAs) surface modified with curcumin, which scavenged reactive oxygen species (ROS) via CAT-like activities and promoted the polarization of macrophages towards the M2-phenotype, providing a potential therapeutic approach for ulcerative colitis [16]. Xi et al. developed a core-shell structured cobalt-iron cascade nano-enzymes with multi-enzymatic activities to eradicate Helicobacter pylori [17], which could be attributed to the intrinsic POD-like activity that endow nanoparticles with the capacity to eliminate bacterial biofilms through the formation of hydroxyl radicals (·OH) [18,19]. Notably, although excessive accumulation of ROS can disrupt bacterial redox homeostasis and trigger lipid peroxidation, leading to bacterial ferroptosis and has emerged as a promising approach to eradicate bacteria [20,21] it may also pose a risk of exacerbating the inflammatory infiltration environment. Therefore, inflammation control and immunoregulation is of great importance to accelerate diabetic wound healing.

5-Aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA), a naturally occurring amino acid that ultimately transfers to heme through biosynthesis route [22], which is usually used as a validated drug for various inflammatory and immune-mediated disorders, such as psoriasis [23], liver injury [24] and Inflammatory Bowel Disease [25]. During the heme synthesis pathway, 5-ALA can be converted to protoporphyrin IX (PpIX) through the decarboxylation or dehydrogenation reaction on the pyrrole ring side chains, followed by the formation of heme within mitochondria and cytoplasm [26]. As the final metabolites of heme, carbon monoxide (CO) and bilirubin (BR) are endogenous anti-inflammatory products that effectively modulate intracellular oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [27,28], which are also reported to facilitate cell migration, angiogenesis and collagen deposition [29,30]. More importantly, as 5-ALA serves as a prodrug for heme formation, the ferrous ions released from the CFP nanomaterials can synergistically drive this route, thereby significantly promoting the heme biosynthesis pathway. Based on this rationale, we thus incorporate 5-ALA with CFP to form therapeutic nanoplatform for accelerating diabetic wound healing via efficient immunomodulation.

Diabetic wounds are often characterized by the presence of a thick layer of necrotic tissue and bacterial biofilms which could hinder the diffusion and penetration of therapeutic agents in hydrogels. By contrast, microneedles can physically bypass these heterogeneous barriers to deliver nanomedicines, thus promoting therapeutic efficiency. Herein, a novel multifunctional microneedle patch was constructed to repair diabetic infected wound by eliminating residual bacteria and remodeling the inflammatory microenvironment simultaneously. As illustrated in Scheme 1, CoFe PBAs (CFP) containing 5-ALA (ALA@CFP) was loaded into an ultraviolet (UV) cross-linked hydrogel microneedle composed of gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) and carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS). The inherent antibacterial property of CMCS can prevent external bacterial colonization within the wound. After being released from microneedles through swelling and enzyme-induced degradation effect, ALA@CFP exerts POD-like and CAT-like activities, catalyzes the generation of ·OH and O2 responding to the endogenous hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) within wound area, thereby inducing bacterial ferroptosis, disrupting the bacterial biofilm and providing O2 to vascularly damaged area. Meanwhile, ALA@CFP can efficiently promote the intracellular heme biosynthesis route by providing ferrous ions and 5-ALA simultaneously. Specifically, 5-ALA can be converted to PpIX, followed by the insertion of Fe ions via ferrochelatase-catalyzed reaction to form heme. Subsequently, heme can be degraded into CO and BR by heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1). These well-documented metabolites with immunomodulatory properties, can remodel the inflammatory microenvironment by facilitating macrophage M2-polarization, down-regulating pro-inflammatory factors and up-regulating anti-inflammatory factor expression, thereby creating a favorable environment for wound healing. As resulted, the established in vivo diabetic infected wound model demonstrated that the multifunctional nanocomposite microneedle patch exhibited effective antibacterial properties, reduced pro-inflammatory signs, promoted neovascularization, matrix deposition and epithelial lining, significantly accelerating the infected diabetic wound healing process. Collectively, our study offers a promising new strategy for repairing the diabetic infected wounds, possessing significant clinical research values and scientific significance.Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the construction of ALA@CFP microneedle patch for accelerating diabetic wound healing.Scheme 1

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterizations of ALA@CFP

A cobalt iron Prussian blue analog loaded with 5-ALA (ALA@CFP) was synthesized through a one-pot method. Specifically, Co(NO)3·6H2O and various concentrations of 5-ALA were dissolved in deionized water, followed by the addition of potassium ferrocyanide solution. The mixture was kept stirring for 6 h, and then ALA@CFP nanoparticles were collected after centrifuge. As shown in Fig. 1A–B, both CFP and ALA@CFP exhibited good dispersity in aqueous conditions, with an average diameter around 255 nm and 190 nm respectively. The ALA@CFP shows narrow size distribution and decreased PDI value (0.037) suggesting that the addition of 5-ALA promotes the uniformity of nanoparticles. Besides, ALA@CFP remained stable in aqueous solutions without violent fluctuations in sizes and PDI values (Fig. S1). The transmission electron microscope (TEM) images of CFP and ALA@CFP in Fig. S2 indicate that both CFP and ALA@CFP display nearly cubic shape, except that ALA@CFP disperses better with less congregate nanoparticles. The initial ZETA potential of CFP was −9.5 mV, which increased to −7.0 mV after 5-ALA encapsulation, implying the successful loading of 5-ALA (Fig. 1C). The encapsulation efficiency (EE%) and drug loading efficiency (DLE%) of the nanoparticles calculated by subtracting the content of 5-ALA in the supernatant from the feed amount are summarized in Table S1. After a comprehensive consideration of the drug encapsulation efficiency and drug loading rate, a dosage of 8 mg of 5-ALA was chosen for the preparation of ALA@CFP, with EE% of 5-ALA reached 90.5% and DLE% of 5-ALA reached 11.1%. These results also indicate the excellent 5-ALA carrying performance of cobalt iron Prussian blue analogs. The element mapping results (Fig. 1D) demonstrated the successful synthesis of cobalt iron Prussian blue analogs and uniform distribution of Co, Fe, N, C and O on nanoparticles. The crystal structure of ALA@CFP was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD), and according to the XRD pattern in Fig. 1E, the characteristic peaks of ALA@CFP are found to be consistent with the peaks of Co3[Fe(CN)6]2·10H2O in JCPDS No. 46-907 card, suggesting the cubic phase structure of nanoparticles. Subsequently, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out to assess the chemical composition and surface electronic states of the constructed nanoparticles. Characteristic peaks belonging to Co, Fe, N, C and O are observed in Fig. S3, which are consistent with the element mapping results. The Co 2p and Fe 2p orbitals were further analyzed through peak processing. As shown in Fig. 1F–G and S4, Co and Fe in CFP and ALA@CFP both exist as Co2+/Co3+ and Fe2+/Fe3+. Based on the calculation of peak areas, the ratio between II to III valence states for Co and Fe are 0.48 and 1.32 respectively. The coexistence of divalent and trivalent ions in nanomaterials is closely correlated with the enzymatic activities. Specifically, the divalent ions (Fe2+, Co2+) can work as the electron donor for Fenton and Fenton-like reaction to generate hydroxyl radicals and the increased peak area of Fe2+ in ALA@CFP further supports it's enhanced POD activity. Beyond catalytic kinetics, the existence of Fe2+ in Co-Fe frameworks also validates the hypothesis of providing Fe2+ for intracellular heme biosynthesis pathway.Fig. 1Hydrodynamic size distribution of (A) CFP and (B) ALA@CFP. It's worth mentioning that the incorporation of 5-ALA in CFP framework is not simply achieved by physical encapsulation during the synthesis process, the chelating effect between Fe2+ and 5-ALA also facilitates the formation of ALA@CFP. As the prodrug for biosynthesis of PpIX, the amino and carboxyl groups endow 5-ALA the ability to chelating with transitional ions, which mainly contributes to the different ratios of Fe ions and Co ions in ALA@CFP and CFP (Table S2). Specifically, it is found that significantly increased amount of Fe ions in ALA@CFP, indicates that 5-ALA not only acts as a “guest”, but also alters the elemental stoichiometry of nanoplatform. Thus, the releasing kinetics of CFP and ALA@CFP was further evaluated. According to the results in Fig. S5, both Co ions and Fe ions exhibited time-dependent releasing behaviors under different conditions at 37 °C. Thereinto, Co ions showed comparable release rates in both neutral and acidic environments, while Fe ions were released significantly faster in acidic condition, suggesting the acid-responsiveness of NPs. It is worth noting that the overall releasing rate of Fe ions from ALA@CFP is slower than that from CFP, also confirming the existence of the chelating effect between 5-ALA and CFP frameworks, thus impeding the releasing rate of Fe ions.

Enzyme-mimicking activity of ALA@CFP

It's worth mentioning that MOFs have been demonstrated to exhibit enzymatic selectivity in response to the dynamic pH variations in wound microenvironment [31]. Specifically, in the early acute phase of a diabetic wound, severe bacterial infection and the accumulation of acidic metabolites create a weakly acidic microenvironment (pH < 6.5). Under such acidic conditions, these nanomaterials tend to exhibit POD-like activity to convert H2O2 into highly toxic ·OH for bacterial elimination. Once the infection is cleared, the wound microenvironmental pH gradually neutralizes to physiological levels (pH∼7). And under neutral condition, nanomaterials tend to exhibit CAT-like activity and decomposes H2O2 into O2, thus alleviating hypoxia beneficial for angiogenesis and tissue regeneration [31,32]. For the in vitro evaluations of enzyme-mimicking activities of ALA@CFP, the concentrations of H2O2 were set from 0.5 mM to 5 mM based on the established literature [19,33].

To evaluate the CAT-mimicking activity of nanoparticles, the dissolved concentration of O2 in solutions contained with CFP or ALA@CFP and H2O2 were monitored. As depicted in Fig. 2A–B, the O2-generating rate and amount increase with the enhanced concentrations of either H2O2 or ALA@CFP. Similar trend was also observed in CFP solutions (Fig. S6), confirming the CAT-like activity of cobalt iron Prussian blue analogs. Subsequently, the POD-mimicking capacity of nanoparticles was further investigated. The ALA@CFP can trigger the generation of ·OH in the presence of H2O2, as confirmed by the occurrence of characteristic absorbance peak belonging to oxidized 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (ox-TMB) with blue color at 652 nm (Fig. 2C). Moreover, the absorbance intensity rose with the increased concentration of ALA@CFP, evidently demonstrating the robust POD-mimicking capacity of ALA@CFP producing ·OH. Considering the CAT and POD-like characteristics of ALA@CFP can deplete H2O2 content simultaneously by triggering the generation of O2 and ·OH. Therefore, the H2O2 consuming behavior of ALA@CFP was further investigated using H2O2 kit following the protocols. The standard curve of H2O2 was presented in Fig. S7, and the remaining content of H2O2 post incubation with various concentrations of ALA@CFP acquired based on the standard curve was presented in Fig. 2D. It's clear that the remaining content of H2O2 decreases along with the increased concentration of ALA@CFP, confirming the robust enzyme-like activities of ALA@CFP depleting the overproduced H2O2 at the wound area. In vitro antibacterial performance

It is known to all that bacterial infection is one of the key factors contributing to the delayed diabetic wound healing. The infective types of bacteria include Gram positive aerobic bacteria, Gram negative aerobic bacteria, anaerobic bacteria and fungi. Among which, S. aureus occupies around 17% within Staphylococcus which is the most common Gram positive bacteria in diabetic wound infection [34]. Therefore, S. aureus was chosen for evaluating the in vitro antibacterial performance of nanoparticles. The antibacterial results including images and statistical analysis of bacterial colonies were shown in Fig. 2E–F. The antibacterial efficiencies of CFP and ALA@CFP reached to 99.4% and 99.0% respectively, evidently demonstrating the excellent antibacterial activity of our designed nanomaterials. Considering the POD-like activity of nanoparticles can catalyze H2O2 into ROS under inflammatory condition, the detection of ROS in S. aureus was then conducted using DCFH-DA probe and observed by confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). According to the images displayed in Fig. 2G, strong green DCF fluorescence emitted from S. aureus cultured with CFP or ALA@CFP for 30 min, while no apparent DCF signals were captured in either control or ALA groups. The above results firmly indicate the constructed nanoparticles are capable of suppressing S. aureus growth through chemical dynamic therapy (CDT) of generating considerable amount of ROS. Notably, Glutathione (GSH) within bacteria could eliminate internal oxidative stress and protect it from ferroptosis, thus the intra-bacterial amount of GSH and lipid peroxidation level were further investigated, as shown in Fig. 2H and I. Either CFP or ALA@CFP treatment was found to significantly decrease the GSH content in S. aureus. Besides, the increased concentrations of MDA indicated the generation of lipid peroxides. Additional rescue experiments of using ferroptosis inhibitor (Ferrostatin-1, Fer-1) were conducted and the results were presented in Fig. 2J–K. The significantly decreased antibacterial efficiency of ALA@CFP with the increased concentration of Fer-1 evidently supports our conclusion that bacterial ferroptosis is predominant in the antibacterial mechanism.

Biofilms are complex assemblies of attached or aggregated bacteria. These multi-species communities are often attached to surfaces such as tissues and embedded in a self-secreted matrix, which forms physical barrier for bacterial survival. The matrix stabilizes bacteria against sudden changes in temperature, nutrient scarcity, mechanical forces or external treatments [35]. The existence of biofilms prevents the penetration of antibacterial drugs, thus resulting in drug resistance, posing great challenges for the management of bacterial infection. Therefore, the anti-biofilm capability of CFP and ALA@CFP were further investigated. The crystal staining results in Fig. 2L clearly showed that CFP and ALA@CFP both destroyed the pre-formed biofilms, while the biofilms cultured with ALA or without treatment remained the intact shape. The destructing efficiencies of CFP and ALA@CFP on pre-formed mature biofilms were calculated to be 73.0% and 67.4% respectively (Fig. 2M).

In vitro evaluations at cellular level

Dynamic fluctuation of macrophage abundance occurs during skin wound formation, with macrophages playing a crucial role in managing the inflammatory balance through their polarization dynamics [36]. Therefore, the cytotoxicity of ALA@CFP towards macrophages was firstly performed using CCK-8 kit. The 5-ALA to heme process is regulated by aminolevulinic acid synthase and tends to maintain equilibrium within cells, and the increase of endogenous heme can negatively restrain the production of 5-ALA, leading to the limited production of 5-ALA in macrophages. Therefore, external 5-ALA was introduced in this work to ensure the sufficient supply for heme formation and promotes the subsequent heme formation-metabolism process. As presented in Fig. 3C and D, obvious red fluorescence from PpIX was detected in ALA or ALA@CFP groups, confirming an increased generation of PpIX intermediate in RAW264.7 cells post different treatments. The production of downstream products including CO (Fig. 3E and F) and BV (Fig. 3G and H) was further evaluated by CLSM. As expected, RAW264.7 cells incubated 5-ALA or ALA@CFP all exhibited strong signals of CO and BV, confirming that the external 5-ALA could promote the biosynthesis of heme. More significantly, despite that the ALA@CFP group shows weaker PpIX signal than that in ALA group, the green fluorescence representing the generation of CO and BV is both promoted conversely in ALA@CFP groups, demonstrating that ALA@CFP can induce the ultimate transition from PpIX into CO and BV, thereby avoiding the phototoxicity caused by accumulation of PpIX. The generated BV can go through reduction reaction catalyzed by biliverdin reductase to form the final metabolite: bilirubin (BR) [38]. Therefore, the BR levels in RAW264.7 cells upon different treatments were determined by Total Bilirubin (TBIL) Colorimetric Assay Kit. As shown in Fig. 3I, the ALA@CFP group produced much higher level of BR than 5-ALA treatment, indicating that the Fe2+ released from nanoparticles could integrate with PpIX to form ferroheme which further accelerating the degradation of heme into anti-inflammatory CO and BR by upregulating the expression of HO-1 [39].

The subsequent degradation of heme catalyzed by HO-1 enzyme can generate CO and bilirubin, which are the well-documented metabolites with immunomodulatory properties in heme metabolite pathway [40,41]. Therefore, the immunomodulatory behaviors of ALA@CFP on RAW264.7 cells were further investigated. The polarization of RAW264.7 post various treatments was determined by flowcytometry with CD86 and CD206 antibodies to distinguish the M1-type and M2-type macrophages respectively. As shown in Fig. 4A, after being incubated with inflammation inducing drug Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), macrophages in PBS group tended to polarize to M1-type (30.6%). In comparison, macrophages in other treatment groups all tended to polarize to M2-phenotype where ALA@CFP exhibited the most significant effect on reducing the expression of CD86 (1.77%). Notably, the expression of CD206 in ALA and ALA@CFP groups both obviously increased as compared to PBS group, which is attributed to the anti-inflammatory effect of CO and BV derived from ALA, thereby promoting the M2-phenotype polarization. Among which, ALA@CFP displays the best inducing effect on CD206 expression (19.7%), proving its anti-inflammatory capacity which would be beneficial for alleviating wound inflammatory conditions. Furthermore, the influence of ALA@CFP on the migratory and blood vessel-forming ability of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) was investigated. Wound healing assay was firstly performed to assess the migratory behavior of HUVECs after various treatments and the corresponding migration rates were analyzed using Image J. As shown in Fig. 4B and C, as compared to control group (blank control), the migratory ability of HUVECs exposed to H2O2 (PBS group, negative control) was suppressed, with the migration rate of HUVECs dropped to merely 5% after 12 h incubation. By contrast, the migration rates all recovered to different levels after 5-ALA or nanoparticles treatments. Particularly, the migration rate of ALA@CFP group is found to elevate to 23.7 % after 6 h and further up to 43.9% after 24 h which is remarkably higher than that of PBS group. Then, the tube formation assay was carried out using Matrigel. HUVECs exposed to different materials were cultured on Matrigel for 6 h, and the results were presented in Fig. 4D and E. As expected, ALA@CFP presented much efficient in ameliorating the inhibitory effect on angiogenesis caused by H2O2, thus holding great promises in the restoration of damaged blood vessels to form new blood vessels at wound site.

The above results evidently confirmed that by providing both ferrous ions and metabolic precursor (5-ALA) in an integrated CFP nanoplatform, ALA@CFP can effectively remodel diabetic wounds from pro-inflammatory to pro-healing microenvironment (anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenesis) via facilitating 5-ALA/Fe-mediated heme biosynthesis pathway.

Preparation and characterizations of ALA@CFP MN

As an aqua-soluble chitosan derivative, CMCS exhibits various potentials in wound healing, hemostasis, antibacterial, etc [42]. Therefore, CMCS was further combined with gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) to establish UV-responsive biocompatible hydrogels. 10 wt% CMCS mixed with 15 wt% GelMA achieved rapid gelation within 2 min under UV light, as observed in Fig. 5A. Then the mechanical property of the CMCS-GelMA hydrogel was evaluated by oscillatory shear rheology. As shown in Fig. 5B, CMCS-GelMA hydrogel was in the sol state with a loss modulus (G″) larger than the storage modulus (G′), which soon went through a phase change from sol to the gel state with G′ much larger than G″ after a 20 s-UV light irradiation. Notably, the encapsulation of ALA@CFP further increased the mechanical properties of CMCS-GelMA hydrogels with slightly elevated storage modulus (G′), indicating their enhanced mechanical stiffness (Fig. S9A). In the oscillatory strain sweep (Fig. S9B), both hydrogels exhibit good structural stability and deformation resistance under an oscillatory strain sweep (0.1% to 1000%), thereinto, ALA@CFP-loaded hydrogel exhibits an earlier crossover point, suggesting the enhancement of structural rigidity slightly compromises the material's ductility. Then the composite microneedles (MN) containing 5-ALA, CFP or ALA@CFP were prepared under vacuum, named as ALA MN, CFP MN and ALA@CFP MN, respectively. The shape and morphology of prepared composite MNs were observed using SEM (Fig. 5C). The MNs are neatly arranged, and the needles are in quadrangular pyramidal shape with 650 μm in length and 15 μm in diameter. The fracture surface of MN needles was observed by SEM in magnified view (Fig. 5C). It's found that the entrapped nanoparticles uniformly dispersed in CMCS-GelMA matrix, as pointed out by yellow arrows. In order to visualize the loading and spreading of ALA@CFP in MNs more clearly, the CMCS-GelMA matrix was labeled with FITC (green fluorescence) and ALA@CFP was labeled with rhodamine B (red fluorescence). As shown in the 3D reconstructed images in Fig. 5D, the MNs exhibited homogeneous green fluorescence distribution, while the red fluorescence from nanoparticles mainly gathered in the needle tips which would be favorable for transdermal delivery. The axial failure force per needle of ALA@CFP MN was monitored to be 0.21 N, which is higher than 0.058 N, the minimum average microneedle penetration force for normal skin penetration [43,44] (Fig. 5E). Similarly, microneedle with comparable mechanical properties was also found in the treatment of diabetic ulcers [45], demonstrating our constructed microneedles could provide sufficient mechanical strength and efficiently penetrate diabetic skin. According to the digital photographs of diabetic mouse skin treated with ALA@CFP MN shown in Fig. 5F, the minimally invasive opening created by the microneedle patch disappeared after 5 min, underscoring the advantages of this painless and minimally invasive nature of microneedle patch. The releasing profile of ALA@CFP from CMCS-GelMA matrix was also investigated. As shown in Fig. 5G, the swelling behavior of CMCS-GelMA hydrogels facilitated the time-dependent release of ALA@CFP from the polymeric 3D network. As CMCS-GelMA hydrogel lacks the ROS-responsive chemical moieties, these hydrogels maintained structural integrity in H2O2 (1 mM) solution without accelerating the release of ALA@CFP. Interestingly, the addition of enzymes (e.g., proteinase) was found to enable the degradation of the CMCS-GelMA matrix, thus triggering the rapid release of ALA@CFP from the polymer matrix. The digital photograph of CMCS-GelMA hydrogels being kept under different conditions for 12 h at 37 °C further confirmed these welling and enzyme dual-mediated releasing mechanisms (Fig. S10). In vivo evaluations on diabetic wound healing

Before investigating the in vivo therapeutic effect of the constructed microneedle patch, the in vitro and in vivo biocompatibility were firstly evaluated. As shown in Fig. S11, ALA@CFP and CMCS-GelMA hydrogel matrix both displayed no obvious cytotoxicity to normal tissue cells (L929 cells). We further collected the major organs of healthy db/db mice being treated with ALA@CFP MN for 14 days, no obvious tissue damages or cell necrosis were observed in major organs (Fig. S12). All these results demonstrated good biocompatibility and biosafety of the constructed microneedle patch.

For the in vivo evaluations, a db/db mice (C57BL/KsJ) model was chosen to verify the therapeutic effect of ALA@CFP MN on accelerating the diabetic wound healing. The diabetic wounds on mice were firstly infected with S. aureus, and then the mice were randomly divided into 5 groups for various treatments, e.g., (1) control, (2) blank MN, (3) ALA MN, (4) CFP MN, (5) ALA@CFP MN. Conclusion

To sum up, in pursuit to reshape the unfavored microenvironment of diabetic wounds, including bacterial infection, oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, a novel multifunctional nanocomposite microneedle with 5-ALA loaded cobalt-iron Prussian blue nanoparticles (ALA@CFP) has been developed in this study. Specifically, the CAT-mimicking activity of ALA@CFP depleted excessive H2O2 to generate O2, while its POD-like activity also produced considerable ·OH to destruct biofilms and effectively inhibit S. aureus survival by triggering bacterial ferroptosis, thereby simultaneously mitigating hypoxia and infected conditions. More importantly, Fe2+ released from ALA@CFP could interact with the PpIX generated by 5-ALA to form heme through biosynthesis pathway, thus accelerating intracellular heme production which ultimately the degraded into anti-inflammatory CO and BR by upregulating the expression of HO-1. Macrophages treated with ALA@CFP is found to go through polarization from M1 to M2 type, along with the promoted secretion of anti-inflammatory factors (IL-10, TGF-β) and inhibited secretion of pro-inflammatory factors (IL-6, IL-1β). By providing both ferrous ions and metabolic precursor (5-ALA) in an integrated nanoplatform, ALA@CFP efficiently induces heme biosynthetic pathway and converts the wound to a pro-healing and anti-inflammatory microenvironment. According to the in vivo therapeutic results of ALA@CFP MN on db/db mice with infected diabetic wounds, the ALA@CFP MN exhibited the most significant effect on accelerating wound healing by promoting collogen deposition, angiogenesis, epithelization and anti-inflammation. This integrated platform successfully accelerated diabetic wound healing by concurrently remodeling the infective and inflammatory wound conditions, which could also be employed synergistically with glycemic control to comprehensively treat clinical diabetic complications. Despite these promising therapeutic outcomes, several aspects related to the clinical translation of this platform need to be concerned, including the comprehensive long-term biosafety evaluations, pharmacokinetics analysis on large animal models, the standardized manufacturing and storage stability. Overall, this study not only presents an effective strategy for chronically infected diabetic wounds, but also provides scientific references for other inflammatory and infectious diseases.

Experimental section

Materials and reagents

Co(NO3)2·6H2O, gelatin (Gel), methacrylic anhydride (MA), carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS), lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) were purchased from Shanghai Rhawn Technology Co., Ltd. 5-amino-4-oxovaleric acid (5-ALA) was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Potassium ferricyanide and hydrogen peroxide were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium Endothelial cell medium (ECM), Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM)-high glucose, penicillin/streptomycin and trypsin were purchased from Shanghai Basal Media Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Matrigel was purchased from Corning incorporated company. Lipopolysaccharide, fluorescent probe for carbon monoxide (FL-CO-1) and streptozotocin were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

The preparation and characterization of ALA@CFP

10 mg 5-ALA and 43.65 mg Co(NO3)2·6H2O were fully dissolved in 5 mL H2O, then added with 32.92 mg K3[Fe(CN)6] in 5 mL H2O. The mixture was stirred for 6 h at room temperature, followed by washed with ethanol and H2O three times respectively. The products (ALA@CFP) were centrifuged (13000 rpm, 20 min) and collected for experimental uses. The whole procedure was conducted under dark conditions, and ALA@CFP was freshly prepared for experimental uses. CFP was prepared through this method, except no 5-ALA was added.

The morphologies and element mapping of nanoparticles were observed and imaged using transmissive electron microscope (TEM, JEM-2100F) and scanning electron microscope (SEM, Quattro S, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Hydrodynamic diameters, dispersity and Zeta potentials of nanoparticles were characterized by Zetasizer Nano ZS unit (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). UV-vis spectra of nanoparticles were obtained using UV-2600 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were characterized by Rigaku D/Max 2200 PC and characterized by Thermo ESCAlab250, respectively. The concentration of dissolved O2 was monitored using portable dissolved oxygen analyzer (JPBJ-610L). Fluorescence intensities were obtained using fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-6000).

The preparation and characterization of CMCS-GelMA hydrogels and ALA@CFP MNs

1 g gelatin was fully dissolved in 10 mL PBS (pH = 7.4) at 50 °C, then this solution was added with 400 μL MA and continued stirring violently for another 3 h, which followed by transferring to 50 mL PBS solution (50 °C, pH = 7.4) to end the reaction. The products were dialyzed in H2O for 7 day and lyophilized for further uses. 12.5 mg LAP was fully dissolved in 10 mL PBS (pH = 7.4) at 50 °C under dark conditions. Then 750 mg GelMA and 500 mg CMCS were added and the mixture continued stirring until the components were completely dissolved. The final solution was stored under dark conditions. The MNs were prepared using silicon mold from Blueacre Technology company. The needle tips were in the shape of a square pyramid, with the height of 400 μm and tip diameter of 10 μm. The distance between every tip is 500 μm. 1 mL of CMCS-GelMA solution was mixed with 1 mg ALA@CFP and then transferred to the mold. After being kept under vacuum over 30 min, the mold was irradiated with 405 nm UV light for 5 min and kept in the glass dryer for 24 h to obtain ALA@CFP MNs.

The morphologies of MNs were observed and imaged using scanning electron microscope (SEM, Quattro S, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Rheological tests on GelMA-CMCS hydrogels were conducted using MCR301 Rheometer (Anton Paar GmbH, 512010-0915, Austria). The rheological testing of the UV-initiated cross-linking process was conducted by applying UV light irradiation on the exposed area of hydrogel during the rheological testing. For evaluating the mechanical properties of cross-linked hydrogels, rheology tests were conducted after the hydrogels were completely cross-linked by UV-lighting.

The evaluation of enzyme-like activity

The CAT-like enzymatic activity was evaluated by monitoring the generation of O2 in solutions containing nanoparticles and H2O2. Specifically, 100 ppm of ALA@CFP was added with various concentrations of H2O2 (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 mM) in PBS (pH = 7.4), then the concentrations of dissolved O2 were detected within 5 min. Additionally, various concentrations of ALA@CFP were added with 5 mM of H2O2 in PBS (pH = 7.4), then the concentrations of dissolved O2 were also detected within 5 min.

The POD-like enzymatic activity was evaluated by monitoring the UV absorbance changes of Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) in solutions containing nanoparticles and H2O2. Specifically, 100 ppm ALA@CFP was added in 2 mL solutions containing 1 mM H2O2 and 0.8 mM TMB, then the absorbance was detected every 1 min for 10 min in total.

The analysis of H2O2 concentrations

The decrease in H2O2 concentration was detected using H2O2 assay kit. 0-1.5 mM H2O2 standard curves was firstly acquired according to the protocols. Then 0, 50, 100, 150 and 200 ppm of ALA@CFP were incubated with H2O2, and the remaining contents of H2O2 were detected using H2O2 assay kit.

In vitro antibacterial experiments

The antibacterial capabilities of nanoparticles were evaluated using S. aureus. The S. aureus was cultured in nutrient broth. After being incubated with 50 ppm of ALA@CFP for 24 h, S. aureus mixtures were then transferred to EP tubed containing 4 mL saline. The S. aureus was removed and collected using an oscillator, which then diluted 100 times using saline. The diluted S. aureus solutions were spread evenly on the petri dishes, and incubated invertly at 37 °C for 18 h. For rescue experiments, S. aureus was treated with 50 ppm of ALA@CFP and 0, 0.1, 1 mM ferroptosis inhibitor (Ferrostatin-1, Fer-1) for 24 h before being spread evenly on the petri dishes, and incubated invertly at 37 °C for 18 h. The bacterial colonies were photographed and recorded.

For ROS-generating capability evaluation, S. aureus cultured in nutrient broth for 24 h was incubated with 50 ppm of ALA, CFP and ALA@CFP separately on titanium sheets for 30 min. Then ROS generation behavior of S. aureus upon various treatments was using DCFH-DA probe on confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Olympus). S. aureus incubated with 50 ppm of ALA, CFP and ALA@CFP for 6 h were harvested and the remaining amount of GSH and MDA were analyzed using GSH and MDA kit.

For biofilm analysis, S. aureus cultured in nutrient broth for 3 days until the biofilm was form, then the biofilms were incubated with 50 ppm of ALA, CFP and ALA@CFP separately for 24 h. The biofilms were then stained using crystal violet and photographed.

Cell culture

Murine leukemia monocyte/macrophage cell line (RAW264.7) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purchased from Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences and cultured in 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin contained DMEM-high glucose or ECM respectively. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in moist atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Cytotoxicity of ALA@CFP

The cytotoxicity of ALA@CFP was evaluated via CCK8 assay. RAW264.7 cells were firstly seeded on 96-well plate (6∗103 cells/well) and cultured for 24 h, then culturing medium was replaced with fresh medium contented with various ALA@CFP (0, 25, 50, 75 ppm). After 24 h, the cells were washed with PBS twice and replaced with fresh medium with 10% CCK8. Absorbances were detected using microplate reader at 450 nm post 1 h incubation at 37 °C. All samples were repeated 3 times.

The metabolic pathway of ALA@CFP at cellular stage

The metabolic pathway of 5-ALA bio-synthesizing heme was characterized by CLSM to observe the generation of protoporphyrin IX (PpIX), carbon monoxide (CO) and biliverdin (BV). Specifically, RAW264.7 cells were cultured with 50 ppm of ALA, CFP and ALA@CFP separately for 6 h, and then monitored by CLSM to record the fluorescence from intracellular PpIX (Ex: 405 nm, Em: 630 nm) and BV (Ex: 465 nm, Em: 525 nm). The generated CO was determined by FL-CO-1 fluorescence probe (Ex: 488 nm, Em: 520 nm).

Evaluations on cell migratory behavior

The cell migratory behavior was assessed by wound healing assay. HUVEC cells were seeded on 12-well plate (5∗104 cells/well) and cultured for 24 h until a confluent monolayer formed. The monolayer was scratched with a 200 μL pipette tip to build 3 wound gaps.

Angiogenesis experiment

The effects of MNs on angiogenesis was evaluated by tube formation assay. 20 μL of Matrigel diluted using culturing medium (1:1) was added to the bottom of a 6-well plate, which was then left in the refrigerator (4 °C) overnight. HUVEC cells (15∗104 cells/well) under different treatments were added into the 6-well plate for another 6 h-culturing, then the tube formation status was observed by CLSM.

Animal models

Male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Shanghai SLCAC company. Diabetes was induced by intraperitoneal injection of 50 mg/kg streptozotocin (10 mg/mL in citric acid-sodium citrate buffer) for consecutive 5 days. One week post STZ injection, the blood glucose levels of mice were continuously measured for 7 days, and mice considered diabetic were chosen for further uses.

To build up diabetic wound infection model, full thickness dorsal circular wounds were firstly established, which were subsequently coated with 10 μL of S. aureus solutions. All animal experiments were conducted under the protocol approved by Tongji University Animal Care and Use Committee (TJLAC-020-228).

In vivo assessment of diabetic wound healing

The mice were randomly divided into 5 groups (n = 5): control, blank MNs, ALA MNs, CFP MNs and ALA@CFP MNs. The infected wounds under different treatments were fixed with bandages for 2 days, then MNs were removed (0 day) and the wounds were photographed at 4, 8, 12 days later and calculated using ImageJ. At the end of treatment period, the mice were euthanized and the wound tissue were collected for immunohistopathological staining (CD31, αSMA, CD206, iNOS) and ELISA analysis (IL-1β, IL-10. TGF-β).

In vivo animal antibacterial evaluation

At day 0, the skin tissue (1 mm) was obtained and soaked in 2 mL saline. The S. aureus was removed and collected using an oscillator, which then diluted 100 times using saline. The diluted S. aureus solutions were spread evenly on the petri dishes, and incubated invertly at 37 °C for 18 h. The bacterial colonies were photographed and recorded. Additionally, skin tissue (1 mm) of different groups was also harvested for Giemsa staining to determine the infection status.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance of difference between groups was performed by independent t-test (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001 and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).
