A robust deep learning framework for automated fetal behavioral state classification: leveraging multi-center datasets to improve antepartum heart rate monitoring
Authors: Giulio Steyde, Isabelle Mueller, Margaret C. Shair, Edoardo Spairani, Luca Subitoni, Marta Campanile, Giovanni Magenes, William P. Fifer, Nicolò Pini, Maria G. Signorini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fetal behavioral states reflect the developing brain’s capacity to organize behavior into distinct sleep state patterns and are a window to evaluate the maturation of the nervous system throughout gestation. This study presents a deep learning-based methodology for the identification of behavioral states from fetal heart rate traces (FHR) recorded via cardiotocography (CTG). A Residual Attention U-Net was pre-trained on a large unlabeled dataset of over 7000 FHR recordings and fine-tuned on a multi-center dataset comprising 236 FHR signals with labeled states. These signals were collected across multiple countries using different cardiotocographs and annotated by clinicians with diverse backgrounds. The model showed high agreement with expert clinicians on a stratified hold-out test set, averaging a Macro F1-Score of 91% and Balanced Accuracy of 93% in distinguishing between Active and Quiet states. The model has the potential to enhance the evaluation of fetal behavioral states in clinical practice by assisting clinicians and ensuring objective, rapid, and reproducible results, as well as supporting future enhancements in electronic fetal monitoring.

Introduction

By approximately 32 weeks of gestation, the human fetus displays well-organized behavioral states, which are characterized by a combination of fetal eye and body movements and heart rate patterns. Fetal behavioral states (FBSs) serve as precursors to early neonatal behavioral states, highlighting the continuity from the fetal to the neonatal period [1,2,3,4]. FBSs are traditionally classified into four distinct classes based on the visual observation of multidimensional constructs, namely fetal heart rate (FHR) patterns, body movements, and eye movement traces, as first described in [5] and summarized in Table 1, [1,6,7]. The key FHR features utilized for identifying FBSs are accelerations and variability. Accelerations are abrupt increases in FHR above the baseline (>15 bpm amplitude, 15 s–10 min duration). Variability quantifies the fluctuations of the FHR around the mean and is defined as the average bandwidth amplitude in one-minute segments [8].

Clinically, it is common to collapse fetal behavioral states into two broader categories: Quiet State (by combining 1F and 3F) and Active State (by combining 2F and 4F) [1,6,7].

The emergence of a well-defined architecture of FBSs in late gestation plays a crucial role in brain development. Indeed, FBSs are early markers of neurodevelopmental trajectories closely linked to fetal brain maturation [3,9] characterized by marked individual differences [3]. In fact, FBSs reflect the developing fetal brain’s capacity to organize behavior into distinct sleep state patterns, offering insights into the maturation of the central and autonomic nervous system maturation [10]. For instance, altered sleep states distributions, such as increased time in quiet sleep and decreased active sleep in growth-restricted fetuses (FGR), may indicate clinical risk, allowing for timely interventions [11,12,13].

Conventional polysomnography is unfeasible for the human fetus. Moreover, the approach developed by Nijhuis et al. [5], which combines FHR monitoring (via cardiotocography [CTG]) with ultrasonic imaging of body and eye movements, presents significant practical limitations. To address these challenges, researchers demonstrated that a reduced subset of physiological signals (i.e., FHR and indices of fetal movements) can reliably classify states with performances comparable to traditional approaches [14,15,16]. Furthermore, FHR and fetal movements are routinely evaluated in CTG, making state evaluation a key part of antepartum monitoring.

Fig. 1 illustrates a FHR signal recorded via CTG with clearly defined Active and Quiet states. However, the distinction is not always pronounced; patterns are often less distinct, and noise may obscure signal clarity. Despite these limitations, the gold standard for FBSs classification in CTG requires expert visual scoring. This method is inherently time-consuming, requires clinical expertise, and is subject to inter- and intra-observer variability, limiting its utility. An automated, reliable, and reproducible method for fetal behavioral states classification would greatly enhance the objectivity and efficiency of electronic fetal monitoring.

Initial efforts to automate FBSs classification were made by Vairavan et al. [17] using a threshold-based algorithm based on FHR variability and accelerations or decelerations. This approach demonstrated good agreement with clinicians for Quiet state, while agreement for Active state was moderate. Similarly, Frank et al. [18] explored permutation entropy for FBSs classification. Both methods were developed and evaluated exclusively on magnetocardiographic recordings, which offer high FHR signal quality but require costly instrumentation unsuitable for routine monitoring. Furthermore, many prior studies relied on single-center datasets annotated by a single clinician [18,19]. This poses a significant limitation, as FHR signals may vary by device and protocol. Additionally, some FHR-derived parameters are highly susceptible to noise, making feature-based methods particularly vulnerable to artifacts and reducing their robustness. A comprehensive review of fetal sleep and automated sleep state detection was recently published by Tang et al. [20].

Here, we build on our previous work by introducing a deep learning-based framework to automatically obtain FHR-derived FBSs with performance comparable to expert clinicians. Previously, we developed a feature-based Hidden Markov Model (HMM) [19], which achieved approximately 84% accuracy against a single annotator. In this work, we move beyond the HMM by adopting a deep neural network (DNN) approach while leveraging the existing model for pretraining. Preliminary efforts on this topic were presented in our previous published work [21]. Here, we substantially advance this model by: (1) exploring novel and more flexible model architectures to improve performance; (2) incorporating multiple types of noise during training to increase robustness against signal artifacts; (3) utilizing multi-center datasets collected from four distinct geographical locations with different equipment for training and evaluation; and (4) integrating annotations from multiple expert clinicians.

Methods

Overview of the proposed methodological solution

Annotating FHR signals into behavioral states is a time-consuming task that requires expert knowledge, making it challenging to obtain sufficiently large, annotated datasets for successfully training deep architectures. To overcome such limitations, we employed a semi-supervised approach for training the deep neural network (DNN), which will be detailed in the next sections. This framework was employed as it allows leveraging both large non-annotated datasets, which are relatively easy to retrieve, alongside smaller labeled datasets [22].

The methodology section is structured as follows: section 2.2 outlines the pre-training phase, including a description of the large non-annotated dataset (2.2.1); data augmentation techniques (2.2.2); the DNN architectures implemented (2.2.3); and the pre-training strategy (2.2.4). Section 2.3 focuses on the training of the final model, detailing the annotated multicenter dataset (2.3.1); the annotation procedure (2.3.2); and the fine-tuning of the DNN (2.3.3).

Model selection and pre-training

The DNN was selected and pre-trained on a pretext task closely aligned with the final objective, and it serves as the basis for the actual training. In particular, the pretext task is to imitate the unsupervised Hidden Markov Model (HMM) described in [21], which identifies FBSs and previously showed good agreement with human-made annotations.

Despite its convenience for the purpose of pre-training, the described HMM presents some limitations: (1) it relies on the extraction of a wide set of features, (2) it has a tendency of overestimating the Quiet class at the expenses of the Active one, (3) shows good but sub-optimal results. For these reasons, replicating the HMM is not the final goal, but rather only a preliminary step.

Pre-training dataset

The characteristics of the dataset used for pre-training are described elsewhere [23,24]. In brief, clinicians have collected ultrasound-based CTG data as part of routine clinical practice at the University Hospital Federico II in Naples, Italy, since 2003. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to performing the CTG recording. The FHR signals were recorded using Philips Avalon cardiotocographs and stored via the 2CTG2 software [25], which reads the values of the FHR and Fetal Movements Profile (FMP) signals at 2 Hz. This sampling frequency was selected as the best compromise to represent all relevant FHR spectral components and limit the number of identical consecutive samples. FHR signals were then subject to a standard pre-processing procedure to identify insufficient-quality points, which are corrected through linear interpolation [25]. For the pre-training phase, 7600 FHR signals from fetuses whose gestational age (GA) was greater or equal to 32 weeks and whose traces had less than 10% of interpolated points were included. Signals were standardized by subtracting the global mean (139.96 bpm) and dividing by the global standard deviation (11.64 bpm), both computed as the average of the means and standard deviations across the sample population.

Data augmentation

FHR signals are susceptible to various sources of noise, including signal loss and outliers attributable to beat misidentification. Signal loss is commonly handled through linear interpolation, which, though simple, results in segments with artificially reduced FHR variability. Conversely, outliers can artificially introduce high variability into the signal. In our context, these artifacts can mimic episodes of Quiet or Active states, which the model needs to recognize and disregard, effectively distinguishing between genuine and spurious variability.

A commonly used approach to increase model robustness against noise, and in general to improve performance, is to employ data augmentation techniques. We performed random transformations on the input data in the magnitude domain, according to the taxonomy proposed by Iwana et al. [26].

Specifically, we randomly applied two noise types to replicate realistic artifacts found in FHR data.

Linear interpolations: to simulate signal loss, portions of the input FHR signals were randomly removed and linearly interpolated. The noise level is adjusted by changing the number and length of the interpolated segments.

Linear interpolations: to simulate signal loss, portions of the input FHR signals were randomly removed and linearly interpolated. The noise level is adjusted by changing the number and length of the interpolated segments.

Random outliers: to simulate beat misdetections, random isolated samples of the input signals were perturbed by adding gaussian noise. The noise level is adjusted by varying the number of perturbed samples and the magnitude of the perturbation.

Random outliers: to simulate beat misdetections, random isolated samples of the input signals were perturbed by adding gaussian noise. The noise level is adjusted by varying the number of perturbed samples and the magnitude of the perturbation.

Data augmentation was applied at specific stages throughout the pipeline, injecting different levels of noise, as detailed in the next sections.

Development of model architecture

The deep neural network (DNN) developed in this study significantly advances prior work by our research group [21] by exploring more recent architectures and a larger hyperparameters space.

All models developed assign to each FHR sample the probability of Active state (p(A)) and Quiet state (p(Q)), with the two probabilities summing to 1.0, i.e., p(A) + p(Q) = 1.0. The probability values can be easily converted into class labels by rounding, which means this task can be interpreted as a signal segmentation problem. In the pre-training phase, the labels are generated by the HMM: segments scored as Active and Quiet by the HMM were assigned {p(A) = 1.0, p(Q) = 0.0} and {p (A) = 0.0, p(Q) = 1.0}, respectively.

Three types of architecture were adapted to the FHR signal segmentation task and evaluated in this study. All the implemented architectures follow a 1D encoder-decoder design with skip connections and have been tailored to process signals of 2400 samples (equivalent to 20-minute segments) and generate a segmentation mask of the same length. More specifically, the final output layer applies a softmax activation function, which assigns to each signal sample the probabilities of being in an Active and Quiet state. The choice of a 20-minute input–output window aligns with the minimum required duration for a non-stress test [27].

U-Net

Ever since its introduction in 2015 [28], the U-Net architecture has been widely used for medical image segmentation [29]. Moreover, it has demonstrated good performances in signal segmentation tasks, as described in [30]. This architecture is built using sequential blocks of 1D convolutional layers, alternated with batch normalization layers. The encoder branch incorporates max pooling for feature down-sampling, while the decoder branch employs transposed convolutions to restore the original resolution of the signal. Hidden layers utilize a Leaky ReLU activation function.

Attention U-Net (AU-Net)

The Attention U-Net (AU-Net) [31] incorporates attention gates in the U-Net architecture previously described, enabling the model to identify key signal segments and selectively filter feature responses, retaining only those relevant to the task. More in detail, the attention gates allow the model to focus on the most informative features extracted by the encoder blocks, dynamically adjusting their importance based on the decoder’s contextual information at coarser scales (i.e., deeper levels). This capability is particularly crucial for our application, where the context in which signal features appear must be carefully considered. For instance, while accelerations are strongly associated with the Active state, they can occasionally occur in Quiet state. Whether an acceleration should be attributed to one state or the other depends entirely on the surrounding context.

Residual Attention U-Net (RAU-Net)

The Residual Attention U-Net [32,33] incorporates attention gates into the U-Net architecture, as in AU-Net, and replaces the convolutional units with residual convolutional units. The residual connections are added to improve gradient flow in deep models. This architecture combines the improvements of attention gates and residual networks, making it very flexible at the cost of increased model size. A graphical representation of this architecture, which we adapted to our signal segmentation task, is displayed in Fig. 2.

In all the architectures analyzed, the number of filters (N.F.) employed in the convolutions increases as a function of the DNN depth, following N.F. = F • 2D −1, where D is the model depth (i.e., the number of encoder/decoder blocks) and F is the number of filters at the base level. The kernel size used for the convolutions is fixed across the DNN.

Pre-training strategy

We explored the hyperparameter set presented in Table 2 by performing a grid search with 5-fold cross-validation. In all trials, we used Kullback-Leibler divergence as the loss function and optimized the model parameters using the Adam optimizer, with a maximum of 300 epochs. Training ended if the validation loss did not improve for more than 15 epochs.

Data augmentation was used at this step. In the training folds, the added noise was increased at each epoch to make the task progressively more difficult and avoid overfitting: the number of added linear segments ranged from 0 to 2, and the length of each from 10 to 360 samples (i.e., from 5 s to 3 min). The number of samples perturbed with Gaussian noise during training varied linearly from 0 to 10% of the signal length and the standard deviation of the Gaussian from 0 to 2 (corresponding to 0–23.3 bpm). In the validation fold, a constant level of noise was applied to ensure that performance evaluation was not biased by the lower noise levels of early epochs: a single segment of random length between 60 and 360 samples (i.e., from 1 to 3 min) was substituted by linear interpolation in half of the input signals and 5% of the signal samples were chosen randomly and perturbed by adding noise with standard deviation of 1.

The choice to include noise in the validation folds at this step was made to encourage the selection of models that are robust to these tailored, physiologically plausible noise perturbations, supporting their generalizability to real-world conditions.

The selected model architecture and hyperparameters were the ones which achieved the highest average Macro F1-Score in cross-validation when predicting the pseudo-labels generated by the HMM. The selected model was subsequently trained on the entire pre-training dataset, using the chosen hyper-parameters and applying the same data augmentation strategy.

Model fine-tuning

The model selected and pre-trained in the previous step serves as the basis for the target model, which is tasked with imitating human experts in FBSs identification. To achieve this, we fine-tune the pre-trained model using two distinct datasets, each annotated by two expert clinicians.

Dataset characteristics and pre-processing

NAPAMI.

A subset of 140 FHR recordings with gestational ages between 34 and 41 weeks was selected from the same dataset used for pre-training and described in paragraph 2.1.1. Only recordings with less than 10% interpolated samples and from singleton pregnancies were included. Additionally, we ensured that no signal appears in both phases by excluding these 140 traces from the pre-training subset. The selected FHR signals were submitted to the annotators for labeling.

PASS.

The Prenatal Alcohol in SIDS and Stillbirth (PASS) dataset was collected in three distinct locations: Cape Town (South Africa), North Dakota (USA) and South Dakota (USA). Detailed cohort characteristics are described in [34]. Institutional Review Board approvals for the Safe Passage Study were obtained from all participating sites, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. FHR and movement data were collected via a Doppler actocardiograph (Toitu MT-320/MT-516). The FHR signal was processed to detect and correct signal loss and artifacts, typically resulting from fetal or maternal movement or repositioning of the transducer. The denoised signals were then downsampled to 2 Hz for consistency with NAPAMI. For the present study, 96 recordings with less than 10% of interpolated samples were randomly selected by a researcher familiar with the cohort but not associated with this publication. All selected signals originated from singleton pregnancies between 34–38 weeks of gestation and were submitted to the annotators for labeling.

The characteristics of the labeled data from the NAPAMI and PASS datasets included in this study are summarized in Table 3. We highlight that the GA in the two datasets differs significantly, with PASS associated with lower GA (Wilcoxon rank sum test, p < 0.001).

All the FHR signals were standardized using the mean and standard deviation derived from the pre-training set. To ensure independent assessment of model performance and robustness, we randomly designated a stratified subset of traces for testing. Specifically, we allocated 15% of the recordings collected in Naples, Italy (n = 21), and 20% from PASS locations (Cape Town: n = 7, South Dakota: n = 7, and North Dakota: n = 7). These traces were set aside and used exclusively for the evaluation of the final trained model, to ensure they never influenced model development. The total duration of the test set is 34.2 h.

Labeling and annotator comparison

Behavioral states were coded by two expert clinicians, one affiliated with Federico II University Hospital (Italy) and one affiliated with Columbia University Medical Center (New York City, USA), using standard definitions of fetal states based on FHR characteristics [15,16]. Annotators were asked to score the FHR traces into two distinct states: Active or Quiet state. Segments not assigned to either state, due to poor signal quality or the absence of clear characteristics needed to identify a pattern, were labeled as Indeterminate. The annotations were formatted as vectors matching the length of the corresponding FHR signal, with each FHR sample labeled as either Active, Quiet, or Indeterminate.

Inter-rater reliability was established using a two-stage procedure. During an initial phase, clinicians reviewed representative recordings and iteratively refined decision rules and thresholds until a stable agreement was achieved (Macro F1-score > 0.70). This phase was intended to align interpretation of state boundaries rather than to resolve specific instances in the full dataset. Following the initial phase, both clinicians independently annotated all traces in NAPAMI and 32 traces from PASS without further discussion. Once inter-rater agreement was established, the second annotator independently coded the remainder of the dataset. Inter-rater agreement was quantified using Macro F1-score, Balanced Accuracy, and Cohen’s Kappa, a chance-corrected measure appropriate for categorical data [35,36].

The ground truth for training and evaluating the model is obtained from the combination of the annotations of Annotator 1 and Annotator 2. The signal segments in which the annotations were inconsistent are marked as Indeterminate.

Segments labeled as Active were assigned {p(A) = 1.0, p(Q) = 0.0}, those labeled as Quiet were assigned {p(A) = 0.0, p(Q) = 1.0}, and Indeterminate segments were assigned {p(A) = 0.5, p(Q) = 0.5}. This approach was systematically used whenever a reliable label could not be produced, either due to inter-annotator disagreement or due to annotator-level uncertainty. Because Indeterminate does not represent a clinically meaningful condition but rather codes segments for which no label could be assigned with confidence, we assigned these segments an intermediate target probability to reduce their influence in training and excluded them from model evaluation.

Fine-tuning procedure

The model was then fine-tuned using the annotated data (except for the test set), corresponding to 119 FHR traces from NAPAMI and 75 from PASS. The architecture and hyperparameters used are those selected during the pre-training step, and the batch normalization layers were frozen to preserve the information and prevent forgetting the feature distributions learned from the larger pre-training dataset. To select the optimal learning rate and number of epochs for fine-tuning, we performed a repeated 5-fold cross-validation with five repetitions, evaluating the following learning rates: [10−6, 10−5, 10−4, 10−3]. Moreover, to address class imbalance (see Table 1), the loss function was re-balanced using class-balanced weighting based on the effective number of samples [37]. More details on batch normalization freezing and class weighting can be found in the Appendix section (A.4).

Data augmentation was applied to the training folds of the fine-tuning dataset following the same approach used for pre-training, as described in section 2.2.4, while the validation fold was not modified to avoid creating a bias in the evaluation of the target dataset. After having obtained the optimal learning rate and number of epochs, the model was finetuned using the annotated data (except for the test set).

Results

Model selection and pre-training

All models evaluated in the pre-training phase exhibited good performances in the pretext task of imitating the Hidden Markov Model (HMM), with Macro F1-Score approaching 90%. Fig. 3 illustrates the results of the top 15 performing combinations in the pre-training step. The RAU-Net consistently outperformed the other architectures, consistently ranking as top performer against the other architectures (8 out of the 10 top positions, including first place).

The best-performing RAU-Net model has a depth of 5 encoder/decoder blocks, 3 as a multiplicative factor for the number of filters and a kernel size of 13. The selected learning rate is 10−4 and the number of epochs 27. This combination reached a Macro F1-Score of 89.95% ± 0.17% (mean ± standard error) in the task of predicting the pseudo-labels generated by the HMM.

The selected model has 335,800 parameters (1.28 MB), making it relatively lightweight compared to recent deep learning models. When evaluated on a workstation mounting an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 with 12 GB of VRAM, the inference speed of the model was around 30 ms per batch of 20-minute segments.

Annotator comparison

Overall, the agreement between the two annotators was substantial, with a Macro F1-Score of 88.5%, a Balanced Accuracy of 90.7% and a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.76. The agreement was comparable across the two datasets. For NAPAMI and PASS, respectively, the Macro F1-Scores were 88.6% and 84.8%, Balanced Accuracy was 90.4% and 92.5%, and Cohen’s Kappa was 0.77 and 0.68 ‒ values consistent with commonly accepted benchmarks for reliable behavioral coding [35,36]. Confusion matrices are reported in Table 4.

The most notable difference between the two annotators is that Annotator 1 labeled more segments as Quiet compared to Annotator 2 (27.3% vs. 22.9%). As a result, Annotator 1 labeled fewer segments as Active (72.3% vs. 75.9%). The prevalence of the Indeterminate label was low for both annotators, at 0.4% for Annotator 1 and 1.2% for Annotator 2.

As described in 2.3.2, the ground truth for training and evaluating the model is obtained from the combination of the annotations of Annotator 1 and Annotator 2, and the obtained class distribution is reported in Table 5. The signal segments in which the annotations were inconsistent are marked as Indeterminate, resulting in a total of 8.9% of Indeterminate in NAPAMI and 4.3% for PASS. In general, the proportion of Active and Quiet varied considerably across individual traces (Active: SD = 0.218; Quiet: SD = 0.207).

Model fine-tuning

The repeated 5-fold cross-validation identified 10−5 as the optimal learning rate, which was associated with a consistent decline in validation loss in all repetitions. Based on the average Macro F1-Score in the validation set, the model was fine-tuned for 35 epochs.

Prior to fine-tuning, the pre-trained model achieved a Macro F1-Score of 78.0 ± 4.9% (Mean ± SD) on the validation set containing the annotations made by the clinicians, hence showing the effectiveness of the pre-training step. After fine-tuning, this score increased to 91.9 ±2.5% (Mean ± SD) averaging across validation set repetitions. More details on the effect of class weighting and batch normalization freezing can be found in the Appendix in section A.4.

Model test

To obtain an unbiased estimate of the model performance, the DNN was evaluated on the hold-out test set, which was designed to include traces from all centers involved in this study. Results are depicted in Fig. 4, and numerical values are reported in the Appendix (Table A.1).

The agreement scores were obtained using the same approach previously applied to annotators. Specifically, the DNN outputs, which have the same length as the FHR signals and assign each FHR sample to either the Active or Quiet class, were concatenated and compared to the annotations provided by expert clinicians. The portions that were marked as Indeterminate by the clinicians were not included in the evaluation since they cannot be assigned to any class. On both datasets, the DNN reached a Macro F1-Score and Balanced Accuracy close or superior to 90%, comparable with the agreement with the two expert clinicians. As shown in Fig. 4, the 95% confidence intervals obtained by bootstrap resampling of the traces are relatively wide: Macro F1-Score [86.8–94.9], Balanced Accuracy [88.2–96.6]. This is expected, as the task is intrinsically uncertain on some traces, as indicated by inter-annotator disagreement.

Considering the overall distribution of the DNN predictions, it should be noted that the percentage of Active is very close to the gold-standard in both datasets, while the Quiet class is overestimated with respect to the gold standard, suggesting that the model tends to assign Quiet to signal segments which were marked as Indeterminate, either because clinicians disagreed or because the signal segments was of low quality. Nevertheless, the pre-trained model (prior to fine-tuning) underestimates the Active state, predicting it in only 59.38% of cases compared with a Gold Standard prevalence of 74.67%. Fine-tuning mitigates this class-prevalence mismatch, raising the Active prediction rate to 71.34% in the final model, which is close to the reference distribution.

Model performance was also evaluated on a trace-by-trace basis, with the results presented in Fig. 5. An explicative selection of prediction examples is shown in more detail, together with the ground truth, in Fig. 6. Except for isolated instances, the scores in both datasets consistently approach 90% for the traces in the hold-out test set. The application of the Wilcoxon rank-sum test did not reveal statistical differences in the scores between the two datasets (Macro F1-Score: p = 0.8307; Balanced Accuracy: p = 0.6781). Moreover, as detailed in Appendix A.5, performances did not significantly correlate with gestational age.

Discussion

The DNN described in this paper automatically labels FHR traces into Active and Quiet states with performance comparable to inter-rater agreement (Macro F1-Score ~ 90% on both datasets).

During pre-training, the RAU-Net architecture reached a Macro F1-Score of 89.95% ± 0.17% in imitating the HMM. Fine-tuning the pre-trained model led to a substantial improvement in the performance, increasing the validation Macro F1-Score from 78.0% ± 4.9% to 91.9% ± 2.5% (relative to annotators) and attenuating the HMM’s tendency to overestimate the presence of the Quiet state. Evaluation on the multi-center hold-out dataset yielded Macro F1-Scores and Balanced Accuracies close to or exceeding 90% on both datasets, values comparable to inter annotator agreement scores. Class-specific analysis indicated higher performances for the Active state (Macro F1-Score: 95.3% in both datasets) compared to the Quiet state (Macro F1-Score: 89.9% in NAPAMI; 83.9% in PASS). Finally, a trace-by-trace evaluation confirmed the model’s consistent performance across datasets (see Fig. 5), with no statistically significant difference between the two (Wilcoxon rank-sum test: Macro F1-Score: p = 0.8307; Balanced Accuracy: p = 0.6781). The RAU-Net outperformed simpler variants in pretrain and in the hold-out test set, as can be seen in the ablation study reported in the Appendix. We attribute these results to the synergy of the architectural components: residual connections enhance gradient flow, enabling deeper configurations and larger receptive fields; while the attention mechanism focuses the model on salient FHR patterns by suppressing irrelevant or noisy components.

To the best of our knowledge, and as recently reviewed in Tang et al. [20], higher performances have been reported only using magneto-cardiography, which relies on highly specialized, costly instrumentation available in a limited number of research centers.

A key methodological advantage of the implemented model is the ability to output state probabilities rather than a binary Active/Quiet classification. This captures the uncertainty inherent in real-world data and provides a measure of confidence for each predicted FHR sample. This is particularly valuable in instances of low signal quality or with intermediate features between the two states, where even human experts may disagree.

An additional strength of this work lies in the data diversity: recordings were collected across multiple countries using different cardiotocograph devices, span a broad gestational age range (34–42 weeks), and were annotated by clinicians with varied training backgrounds, contributing to the model's generalizability. While fetal movement patterns or actograms are often available in CTG and were referenced by the experts of this study during coding, we deliberately excluded this input from the DNN due to its inconsistent availability and format across devices. Notably, the DNN showed strong performance using FHR data alone, underscoring its potential utility in routine clinical practice.

A limitation of the current work is that the DNN focuses only on classifying the two primary fetal states commonly assessed in clinical practice: Active and Quiet. While this simplification omits the full range of the four states originally described by Nijhuis et al. [5], this choice is justified by the observation that the states 3F and 4F are infrequently examined as they have little significance in clinical practice [1,7]. An additional limitation is that our model was developed and validated exclusively on FHR signals derived from ultrasound-based CTG. While this aligns with standard clinical practice, it limits immediate applicability to data obtained with different modalities. In particular, the FHR can also be estimated from fetal ECG. In this case, the estimate is beat-to-beat and may retain higher-frequency components, potentially impacting model performance.

Building on these observations, we believe that the DNN presented lays the foundation for significantly improving antepartum heart rate monitoring by enhancing the evaluation of fetal behavior states (FBSs) in clinical practice, assisting healthcare providers and ensuring objective, reproducible results. Moreover, it paves the way for further research. A promising future direction is the application of the proposed model to the analysis of longer durations of fetal heart rate (FHR) recordings, now made feasible by the advent of wearable technologies capable of collecting FHR data outside clinical settings. Applying the model’s probabilistic outputs over extended monitoring periods enables the extraction of a consistent distribution of fetal behavioral states ‒ such as Active and Quiet ‒ which are believed to reflect underlying neurological maturation across gestation. This longitudinal quantification of state distributions could serve as a novel, non-invasive biomarker of fetal wellbeing, offering a more dynamic and physiologically grounded alternative to traditional static measures. Furthermore, integrating fetal behavioral states into fetal growth assessments allows for the stratification of growth curves by state, supporting the development of personalized and developmentally sensitive benchmarks. Such an approach recognizes that deviations in behavioral state organization and transitions may signal early indicators of developmental concerns, even when standard physiological parameters remain within normal ranges. Moreover, it facilitates stratified analysis of FHR features by behavioral states.
