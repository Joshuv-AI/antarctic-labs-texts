Authors: Emily M Eichenberger, Stephanie Pouch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvasive candidiasis is a serious and life-threatening disease in thoracic organ transplant recipients. Candida can transition from colonizer to opportunistic pathogen quickly in these hosts. This review explores the epidemiology, clinical presentation, diagnosis and management of invasive candidiasis in thoracic organ transplant recipients. We focus on the various manifestations of invasive candidiasis, including candidemia, endobronchial and anastomotic infections, pleural space infections, mediastinitis, and sternal osteomyelitis. We also discuss current therapeutic approaches, the challenges in managing these infections, and the emerging threat of resistant Candida species in this patient population.Keywords: Candida, Candidemia, Candidiasis, Lung transplant, Heart transplant, Invasive candidiasis

Introduction

Candida species are commensals of the gastrointestinal tract, skin, and genitourinary tract. In critically ill and immunocompromised patients, Candida may transition from colonizer to invasive pathogens and cause a variety of infections (Figure 1). With over 15 unique disease-causing species, invasive candidiasis is among the most common type of fungal infection among thoracic solid organ transplant recipients. The incidence of invasive candidiasis in solid organ transplant recipients is increasing1 and poses a significant therapeutic challenge in this population. In this review we will describe the epidemiology, risk factors, presentation, and management of invasive candidiasis in heart and lung transplant recipients including candidemia, endobronchial and anastomotic infection, pleural space infection, and mediastinitis and sternal osteomyelitis. Oropharyngeal candidiasis, vulvovaginal candidiasis and Candida infection of the urinary tract will not be covered in this review.

Spectrum of Candida causing invasive infections

Over the past 20 years there has been a shift in the predominant Candida species causing disease in thoracic transplant recipients from C albicans to non-albicans Candida species. This shift has been attributed to the increase in fluconazole usage, with studies noting a rising incidence of fluconazole-non susceptible invasive Candida infections.2 This epidemiologic shift has notable clinical implications. For instance, the Transplant-Associated Infection Surveillance Network (TRANSNET) identified a higher mortality rate associated with non-albicans invasive candida infections as compared to C albicans infections.1

The emergence of C auris is concerning. C auris is intrinsically resistant to most antifungal drugs and is associated with mortality rates reaching up to 67%.3, 4 It spreads rapidly in critically ill patients, has a high propensity to form biofilms and is extremely difficult to eradicate from surfaces.3, 5 It is challenging to identify and can be misidentified as other yeasts (i.e. C. haemulonii) leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment.2, 3, 5 Reports of C. auris infections in cardiothoracic transplant recipients are fortunately few, but increasing.5, 6 Early identification of C. auris in donor cultures and implementation of appropriate infection control measures are critical to prevent spread in this population. Candidemia in heart and lung transplant recipients

Incidence, risk factors, epidemiology

Candidemia is the most common type of invasive candidiasis in thoracic organ transplant recipients, though its overall incidence is relatively low, affecting 0.3%−5.5%7, 8, 9, 10 of heart recipients and 3.1–3.5%7, 11 of lung recipients. Candida bloodstream infection occurs early post-transplant7, 11, 12, 13 and typically arises from a central line related infection, mediastinitis, intraabdominal infection or gastrointestinal translocation.7 Universal predisposing factors for candidemia includes use of total parenteral nutrition, presence of central venous catheter and prior antibiotic therapy.14 Risk factors for candidemia in heart transplant recipients include delayed chest closure, use of temporary mechanical circulatory support post-transplant (i.e. extracorporeal membrane oxygenation), repeat surgical chest exploration, and renal replacement therapy.7, 15 Lung transplant recipients at increased risk for candidemia are those who were hospitalized pre-transplant, patients who required extracorporeal membrane oxygenation immediately post-transplant, and those requiring renal replacement therapy.7, 11

Presentation and diagnosis

Clinical presentation of candidemia is nonspecific and may occur on a spectrum ranging from asymptomatic leukocytosis to septic shock with fever, chills and hemodynamic instability.16

The gold standard for diagnosis of candidemia is identification of the yeast on routine blood cultures. Growth on standard blood cultures typically occurs within 96 h, though time to positivity varies by species.17 Isolation of Candida from blood culture is always clinically significant and should never be regarded as a contaminant.

Unfortunately, cultures are only positive in approximately half of all cases.18 There are non-culture based tests used in the diagnosis of invasive candidiasis including the serum beta-d-glucan (BDG) test, the T2Candida assay, and the Blood Culture Identification 2 (BCID2) Panel. The BDG test measures the presence of circulating Beta-(1,3)-D-glucan, a fungal cell wall component found in several fungi, including Candida species. BDG is highly sensitive for the diagnosis of invasive candidiasis19 but it is not specific for Candida. It is frequently positive in other invasive fungal infections such as Aspergillus or Pneumocystis infections. Additionally, Beta-(1,3)-D-glucan is abundant in the environment. It can result as falsely positive in patients on hemodialysis, recent receipt of albumin, intravenous immunoglobulin or beta-lactam/inhibitor antibiotics, and recent surgery. It may also be positive in the setting of airway colonization.16, 20, 21, 22, 23 When evaluated specifically in the lung transplant population, it had a low accuracy and a low positive predictive value.20 BDG has limited utility as the sole diagnostic agent for diagnosing invasive candidiasis cardiothoracic transplant patients.

The T2Candida assay is a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay with miniaturized magnetic resonance imaging that detects presence of Candida in whole blood. It reports grouped results on the basis of antifungal resistance patterns of various candida species (i.e. C albicans/ C tropicalis, C krusei/ C glabrata, and C parapsilosis). This assay is 89–91% sensitive and 98% specific. There is also a T2Cauris assay for diagnosis of C auris, C duobushaemulonii, and C haemulonii infection. Significant advantages of these assays include a rapid turnaround time (4 h) and retained sensitivity in the setting of antifungal pretreatment.17, 19, 24

The BCID2 Panel uses nucleic acid amplification testing on blood cultures to detect common bacteria and yeast, including C. albicans, C. auris, C. glabrata, C. krusei, C. tropicalis, and C. parapsilosis.25 The sensitivity of the Biofire BCID2 is 96.8–100% sensitive and 99.8–100% specific for diagnosis of these yeasts in blood culture.25, 26 In addition to accuracy, the BCID2 assay provide more rapid identification of pathogens than standard culture-based methods.27

Complications

Metastatic infection secondary to candidemia is more common in SOT recipients relative to non-SOT recipients.8 Endocarditis in particular is a feared complication of candidemia. Rates of endocarditis complicating Candida bloodstream infections range from 1.9%−5.9%,28, 29, 30, 31, 32 though data on the incidence of Candida endocarditis in specifically heart or lung transplant recipients is lacking.33 Cardiac involvement should be considered in the setting of persistently positive blood cultures, persistent fever, or embolic phenomena.34, 35

Approximately 3.8% of patients with candidemia develop endophthalmitis.31 Incidence of ophthalmic involvement of candidemia in thoracic organ transplant recipients is incompletely understood. For patients with persistent candidemia, visual changes, or concerns for endophthalmitis, a dilated eye exam is warranted.16

Management

The recommended initial treatment of candidemia is an echinocandin, with fluconazole as an acceptable alternative in non-critically ill patients who are unlikely to have a fluconazole resistant Candida species (Table 2).35 Voriconazole and newer generation azoles may be useful alternatives when additional mold coverage is needed. Ultimate therapy should be tailored to susceptibility results, though it should be noted that echinocandins have limited ocular and central nervous system (CNS) penetration and may not effectively treat chorioretinitis or CNS disease.35

Duration of treatment of candidemia is a minimum of two weeks from first negative blood culture, with longer durations required for patients with metastatic infection, persistent source of infection, or endocarditis. Central catheters should be promptly removed owing to the propensity of Candida to form biofilms on foreign devices.16 In the setting of native valve endocarditis or other endovascular Candida infection, guidelines recommend lipid formulation of amphotericin B with or without flucytosine or a high dose echinocandin. Step down therapy consists of fluconazole for susceptible isolates or for non-fluconazole susceptible isolates, other oral azole if susceptible.35

Endobronchial and anastomotic infection lung transplant recipients

Epidemiology

Isolation of yeast from bronchial washing cultures is common and usually indicative of airway colonization.5 It is exceedingly rare to have Candida pneumonia. However, Candida isolated from donor respiratory cultures or bronchial washings from early post-lung transplant recipients can lead to endobronchial invasive candidiasis particularly at the anastomosis site.36, 37, 38, 39, 40 Endobronchial candidiasis may develop due to disrupted bronchial circulation and devascularization of the bronchial anastomosis site during lung transplantation. Mucosal sloughing and necrosis can occur at the anastomosis, increasing risk for infection.36, 37

Clinical presentation

Anastomotic Candida infections can be asymptomatic or symptomatic. Asymptomatic patients may have anastomotic infections detected on routine bronchoscopy or changes in pulmonary function testing. Symptomatic patients may present with decreased ability to cough up secretions and noisy breathing. In severe cases patients may present with fever, wheezing, cough and hemoptysis.41

Diagnosis and management

Direct visualization with bronchoscopy coupled with tissue sampling for culture are needed for accurate diagnosis. Endoscopically, patients may have multiple white plaques with pseudomembrane formation and occasionally hemorrhagic bronchitis. Treatment is similar to that of candidemia with an echinocandin followed by tailoring of the regimen to the organism’s susceptibility profile.42, 43 There is some concern that systemic antimicrobial therapy alone may not adequately reach the infected bronchial anastomosis given that the area is devascularized.37 Adjuvant inhaled antifungal agents may be helpful in this setting, though this has not been adequately studied. Optimal duration of treatment is unknown and likely requires personalization based on evolution of bronchoscopic findings and overall clinical trajectory. In addition to medical management, some patients may require bronchoscopic interventions to relieve airway obstructive symptoms or debride necrotic tissue.

Prevention

American Society of Transplantation (AST) Guidelines recommend that in settings where lung transplant recipients are not receiving routine antifungal prophylaxis, patients should receive therapy directed against the Candida that is isolated in the donor respiratory sample. In these scenarios it is recommended that the empiric treatment be extended until bronchial anastomosis has healed.44 Of note, not all yeast isolated from donor BAL is identified to the species level. This has led to inadequate prophylaxis strategies which may place patients at risk for potentially avoidable invasive Candida infections from resistant strains.5

Pleural space infection in lung transplant recipients

Epidemiology

Pleural effusions are common in early post lung transplant recipients. They are likely a result of alveolar capillary permeability, perioperative pleural inflammation, graft ischemia and reperfusion, impaired lymphatic flow, and/or postoperative atelectasis.45 A striking 26%−42% of pleural effusions in lung transplant recipients are infected.45, 46 Candida is the leading cause of pleural space infections in the lung transplant population and may occur as a co-infection with other organisms.45 Candida may seed the pleural space at the time of surgery, enter via the multiple chest tubes present post-operatively, pass through from the airway to the pleural space in the setting of a complete bronchial anastomotic dehiscence, or seed the pleural space hematogenously in the setting of a disseminated infection.37, 45

Clinical presentation and diagnosis

Clinical presentation may vary from asymptomatic pleural effusion with leukocytosis to fever and sepsis.47 On ultrasound, pleural fluid may appear echogenic, complex, septated, or non-septated. Computed tomography (CT) imaging should be obtained and may demonstrate fluid loculation, pleural thickening or enhancement,48, 49 (Fig. 2) and may identify bronchial anastomotic dehiscence if present.50 Given the high prevalence of pleural effusions in recent lung transplant recipients and the relatively nonspecific clinical presentation of an empyema in this population, a low threshold for sampling pleural fluid is recommended. Most early post-transplant pleural effusions are exudative. Infection is more likely in patients with elevated pleural lactate dehydrogenase and nucleated cells. One study found that a pleural neutrophil percentage of >21% had a 70% sensitivity and 79% specificity for correctly identifying infection.45 Culture yielding growth of Candida from pleural fluid is diagnostic for Candida empyema. In cases where pleural fluid cultures are negative but suspicion for infection remains, next generation sequencing using 16 s and 18 s rRNA sequencing on pleural fluid may provide diagnosis. There is insufficient data regarding utility of serum BDG assay as an adjunctive diagnostic for Candida pleural space infection.

Management

Management of Candida pleural space infections is challenging. Echinocandins achieve low pleural fluid concentrations and may be associated with increased risk of treatment failure and development of echinocandin resistance.51, 52, 53 Guidelines note that in cases of treatment failure on echinocandin therapy, a dose increase or class switch to an azole may be warranted.51 In general, caution should be used when using echinocandins to treat Candida pleural space infections. Limited data indicate azoles may be associated with better outcomes in patients with Candida empyema,54 though guidelines do not provide a clear recommendation on optimal therapy for Candida pleural space infections.51 Duration of treatment is poorly defined in the literature, but is typically a minimum of 4 to 6 weeks from infected fluid removal, with ultimate duration ultimately tailored to clinical trajectory. Pleural space infections require management with early chest tube placement, and some patients require video assisted thoracoscopic surgery or decortication.37, 55, 56 In cases of pleural infection associated with anastomotic dehiscence, stenting, radiofrequency ablation or omental wrapping may be required to achieve source control.37

Candida mediastinitis and sternal osteomyelitis

Epidemiology

Candida mediastinitis and sternal osteomyelitis are uncommon complications following thoracic transplantation but are associated with high mortality.57 A study of 776 thoracic transplant recipients found that mediastinitis occurred in 21 (2.7%) of their patients, of which 14.3% were due to Candida.58 A more recent retrospective study of heart transplant recipients found that the incidence of Candida mediastinitis was 7.7% developed in heart transplant recipients.59

Candida mediastinitis may be monomicrobial or polymicrobial with other bacteria present.60 Risk factors specifically for the development of Candida mediastinitis have been described in general thoracic surgery patients. In this population, use of temporary mechanical circulatory support, mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy and reoperation after initial surgery increase one’s risk for Candida mediastinitis.59, 60, 61, 62 Candidemia accompanies mediastinitis in 35%−67% of the cases.60, 62

Clinical presentation and diagnosis

Patients may present with chest wall erythema, purulent sternal drainage, sternal instability, or wound dehiscence, though not all patients exhibit local signs or symptoms of infection.63, 64 Septic shock is an uncommon initial presentation.60, 62 While Candida mediastinitis and sternal osteomyelitis generally develop early post-surgery, diagnosis of is often delayed due to its more insidious onset relative to bacterial mediastinitis.62

Definitive diagnosis is made through a combination of imaging and tissue culture.64 Radiographic features of mediastinitis in thoracic organ transplant recipients may include substernal fluid collection, pneumomediastinum, mediastinal widening, or obliteration of fat planes.58 Nuclear imaging modalities including positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET/CT) with 18-F fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) or tagged white blood cell scans may play an adjunctive role in diagnosis of mediastinitis and/or sternal wound infections when standard imaging techniques are inconclusive.65, 66, 67

Management

Treatment consists of systemic antifungal therapy and surgical debridement. Often, repeat surgical debridement is required for adequate control of infection.62, 68 Irrigation of the chest cavity with amphotericin B is not recommended as it may cause irritation.35 Omental flap may be necessary in the setting of extensive candida sternal osteomyelitis.69 IDSA guidelines recommend antifungal treatment for 6–12 months for mediastinitis and sternal osteomyelitis, noting that fluconazole is the preferred agent for susceptible isolates.35 Repeated imaging can be used to evaluate for evidence of ongoing infection or resolution.

Antifungal prophylaxis for thoracic organ transplant recipients

Lung

While most lung and heart-lung transplant centers administer antifungal prophylaxis to lung recipients, there are some centers who opt for monitoring with airway surveillance and bronchoscopy. For those centers who do employ prophylaxis, protocols vary considerably, with differences in universal versus targeted prophylaxis, as well as the type, route, and duration of antifungal.70, 71, 72 The triazoles are the most commonly used drugs for antifungal prophylaxis in lung transplant recipients, with voriconazole being the most common, followed by itraconazole and then posaconazole.64, 71 These triazoles are an appealing option for prophylaxis given their broad spectrum of activity against Candida and molds, and their favorable volume of distribution. However, they require therapeutic drug monitoring, interact with calcineurin inhibitors, and have varying toxicity and side effects. Some centers use fluconazole prophylaxis, which provides good protection against many strains of Candida, but its use as the sole antifungal in lung transplant prophylaxis is discouraged by many experts given its lack of mold coverage.64 Echinocandins are less commonly used for prophylaxis in lung transplant recipients, but may be an alternative agent to a triazole when toxicity precludes further use.71 One study found elevated rates of breakthrough invasive candidiasis in lung transplant recipients on micafungin prophylaxis.38 Use of inhaled lipid formulation amphotericin B is sometimes used as adjunctive prophylaxis, though it does not prevent invasive candidiasis outside the respiratory tract when used as monotherapy.71 In instances where prophylaxis is not used, active surveillance with bronchoscopies may be useful. The current International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) guidelines recommend using inhaled liposomal amphotericin combined with fluconazole or an echinocandin for the first 2–4 weeks post-transplant to target Candida species.73

Heart

The current AST guidelines do not recommend routine universal prophylaxis against Candida for heart transplant recipients, citing low frequency of invasive candidiasis in this population.16 Recent studies identifying key risk factors for invasive candidiasis in this population propose considering a targeted antifungal prophylaxis approach for heart transplant recipients harboring one or more of the identified risk factors, including patients receiving renal replacement therapy,7, 74 patients with open chest7, 74 or repeat chest surgery after transplant, and those requiring post-transplant acute mechanical circulatory support.7 Antifungal prophylaxis appears to reduce the risk of invasive fungal infections in heart transplant patients,15, 74, 75 though further data is needed to determine the ideal targeted prophylaxis approach. Clinicians must balance potential benefits with harms including drug-drug interactions, tolerability and expense. The cardiothoracic transplant community would benefit from a randomized controlled trial assessing optimal fungal prophylaxis strategies in heart transplant recipients.

Conclusions

Invasive candidiasis remains a life-threatening infection in heart and lung transplant recipients. The emergence of resistant strains, coupled with the limited data on optimal management and preventative strategies underscores the urgent need for research. The transplant community would benefit from multicenter studies identifying optimal treatment strategies, including the optimal duration of therapy, and in identifying biomarkers that can assist in tracking resolution of infection for a more personalized treatment approach. Additionally, there is a need for a randomized controlled trial to determine targeted versus universal prophylaxis strategies to minimize risk of invasive candidiasis in this immunosuppressed population. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of the epidemiology, risk factors, management and prevention of invasive candidiasis in heart and lung transplant recipients will help improve outcomes and reduce the burden of this serious infection.
