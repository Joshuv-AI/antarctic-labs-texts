Predictive modeling for early diagnosis of dementia using sequential data analysis and data mining
Authors: Senthil Kumar G, Dhanagopal R
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDementia, a progressively debilitating neurodegenerative disorder, poses a growing challenge to global healthcare due to its subtle early onset and lack of accurate temporal diagnostic models. Traditional machine learning (ML) approaches for dementia prediction, including SVMs, Random Forests, and CNNs, often rely on static, cross-sectional data that fail to capture the progressive cognitive decline observed in real-world patient trajectories. To address these limitations, this study introduces a novel deep learning-based framework TCBiNet (Temporal Convolutional Bidirectional Attention Network) designed to model both short- and long-term symptom evolution using sequential clinical data. The architecture strategically integrates Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) to identify localized trends, BiLSTM for bidirectional temporal analysis, and a Temporal Attention mechanism to highlight critical symptom intervals. Implemented in Python using TensorFlow 2.11, the model processes longitudinal data from 2,149 patients aged 60–90, with cognitive, behavioral, and functional metrics segmented into 30-day intervals. Compared to conventional models such as CNN-LSTM and BiLSTM-DRL, TCBiNet demonstrated superior performance, achieving an accuracy of 99.51%, F1-score of 99.35, and AUC-ROC of 0.990 reflecting an improvement of over 4.4% in accuracy and 6.2% in F1-score relative to the best-performing existing models. This significant gain is attributed to the model’s capacity for temporal pattern mining and attention-guided symptom weighting. The proposed system not only improves prediction accuracy but also enhances interpretability and clinical relevance. By offering a robust, sequence-aware diagnostic tool, this research paves the way for proactive interventions in dementia care, encouraging further exploration into longitudinal neurocognitive modeling for early disease detection.Keywords: Early dementia diagnosis, Sequential data analysis, Temporal deep learning, Longitudinal health records, Temporal convolutional network, Bidirectional LSTM, Neurodegenerative disease predictionSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Neuroscience

Introduction

Dementia is a progressive neurodegenerative condition, and represents a large public health concern due to its growing prevalence and the absence of an effective early diagnostic tool. Early detection is important for meaningful intervention to slow disease progression and optimize patient outcomes1,2. Classical diagnostic paradigms rely almost exclusively on cross-sectional clinical observations and neuropsychological testing which are unable to account for the dynamics occurring with the evolution of symptoms over time3–6. In recent years, several ML approaches, namely Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests (RF), and Logistic Regression, have been considered in the classification of dementia7–9. However, while methods may diagnose dementia, they are often constrained by static representations of features without the modeling of real-time or time-dependent covariates related to behavioral change10,11. A few studies have examined Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) as well as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models to interpret sequential data. Still, these models can have difficulty capturing both short- and long-range temporal dependencies, can suffer from gradient instability and sometimes collapse which is not ideal for longitudinal studies12–15. To overcome the current shortcomings in early dementia detection, this work suggests an overall modeling system that makes use of both temporal patient data and deep-learning-based pattern inference. The framework proposes the TCBiNet as a hybrid architecture that combines TCNs with Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) networks, and an Attention mechanism to learn multi-scale temporal features based on longitudinal cognitive, behavioral, and sensor-derived data. Through the effective modeling of both short-term dynamics and long-term development, this method can more effectively model the diagnostic accuracy as compared to conventional techniques and provides a clinically-significant instrument to detect dementia in its early stages.

Research motivation

Dementia is detected too late by models that overlook its slow, ongoing process. Standard diagnostic methods ignore how behaviors and thoughts change over time for patients. The purpose of this study is to use sequential data to increase the speed and accuracy of medical diagnosis.

Significance of the study

This proposed framework with DL methods is designed to detect early signs of dementia over time. It can detect persistent cognitive shifts long before they become noticeable in the clinic. Early discovery allows timely actions that may delay the disease and improve the patient’s results.

Problem statement

Despite the potential of ML and DL methods in early dementia detection, there are multiple limitations to their clinical utility. As an example, Dhakal16 obtained high accuracy with SVM on the OASIS dataset, nevertheless, there is no external validation so the generalizability of the model is a concern. Likewise, Hashmi and Barukab17 trained the deep reinforcement learning using synthetically generated data, which constrained its applicability to clinical environments. The above issues reveal the necessity of models that can incorporate the time dynamics in longitudinal patient information. To fill in this gap, the current research suggests TCBiNet, a hybrid framework of TCN and BiLSTM networks that further analyzes cognitive and behavioral paths over time. The purpose of this method is to advance the early detection of dementia by predicting the short-term changes and long-term evolutionary trends to give a clinically relevant and generalizable solution.

Recent innovations and challenges

The latest research in dementia is using graph neural networks to understand similarities among patients, transformer models for predicting healthcare actions, and contrastive learning to improve how the course of a disease is represented over time. They improve on the learning of features provided by sparse and varied data sets. Even so, issues including uneven data distribution, understandable results, and linking data from various timelines keep them from being used widely in clinical settings.

Related works

Yang18 designed and trained a ML algorithm to predict dementia in diabetic UK Biobank patients, with Adaboost giving very strong results (AUC: 0.801). A 13-variable model (DRP-Diabetes) was made available to clinicians via a web tool. However, the study might not apply well to all populations because most of its reports were self-made and it did not use a large diversity of people. Hashmi and Barukab17 put forward a deep reinforcement learning method to solve the problem of class imbalance when diagnosing dementia with the OASIS MRI dataset, hoping to improve finding biomarkers and the accuracy of diagnosing dementia. The method made significant gains in precision (9%), recall (13%), and F-score (up to 10%) for categorizing dementia stages. However, due to generating data through simulation and not getting validated by other populations, the study may be subject to overfitting.

Dhakal16 introduced a dementia prediction model using OASIS data features and applied multiple ML algorithms. The model outperformed others, getting an accuracy of 96.77% when using all the features. Researchers improved the model’s performance by using data imputation, preprocessing, and selecting features using LASSO. At the same time, this method may not check results on outside datasets and might be prone to fitting data too well due to the single-source imaging dataset. Helaly, Badawy and Haikal19 suggested a complete CNN framework with transfer learning for identifying AD stages on the ADNI dataset. Among all the models, VGG19-based achieved the best accuracy of 97% and users can use a web application for remote diagnosis. On the other hand, the methods are dependent on having good-quality images and have not yet been validated clinically.

Rossini, Miraglia and Vecchio20 suggested a graph-based ML approach to detect mild cognitive impairment and early stages of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) from combined functional connectivity and biomarkers. The method supports finding and treating diseases early, based on the personal characteristics of each patient. Even so, its usefulness is not readily available because data integration is difficult and it is not broadly tested on patients. Maji21 used DL by developing MOD-1D-CNN and MOD-2D-CNN to help detect dementia. Their work had an accuracy rate of up to 95.72% when evaluating health metrics and images of people’s faces. Compatibility was boosted in a set of operations by using rule-based fusion. The scalability of the model is not very high since it relies on localized, small data and lack external validation.

Özaltın22 presented a hybrid method where ResNet-50, FTNCA, and KNN were applied to Alzheimer’s recognition, getting a precision of 100% after bringing down the features from 2048 to 344. The method helps achieve better classification results with less effort spent on computation. Even so, the number and distribution of data in the dataset may affect the model’s usefulness for practical purposes. Sreedhar23 created a Fuzzy RNN system by merging fuzzy logic and RNN to classify Alzheimer’s and dementia based on EEG, reaching a high degree of accuracy (98.82%) and sensitivity and specificity. The model is able to detect temporal patterns and performs better than other traditional classifiers. Even so, the lack of validation beyond EEG data stops it from being used widely in most clinical situations.

Andrade-Arenas24 used the SEMMA methodology in a decision tree to predict those more likely to suffer from AD. The study highlighted that people over 65 have a higher risk of getting this disease. This approach which uses few selected variables and is tool-dependent, might make it difficult for DL to be widely adopted. According to Spooner25 the C-TPM framework combines techniques for handling time series and techniques for finding similarities to recognize increased risks for AD in longitudinal patient data. It reached a concordance index of up to 0.87 and showed that it is both easy to understand and easy to utilize on a large scale. Still, the need for fixed values and laborious computing might make it challenging to deploy these methods quickly in real healthcare.

Recent works have examined DL methods of predicting AD with longitudinal and multimodal data. Poonam et al.26 suggested a Sequence-Length Adaptive Encoder-Decoder LSTM (SLA-ED LSTM) that elastically changes the decoder, according to changes in the length of sequences and inference. The model was tested on longitudinal data of the Alzheimer Disease Neuroimaging Initiative with an average test accuracy of 0.920, and an AUC of 0.982, showing that data at 9 patient visits effectively measures significant changes in cognition. On the same note, Saleh et al.27 highlighted the combination of multimodal cognitive scores, such as clinical dementia rating and Alzheimer disease assessment score, with LSTM-based architecture to improve predictive accuracy. Their Bayesian-optimized LSTM was superior to classical machine learning models such as random forest, providing consistent and sound predictions of AD progression. Collectively, these studies indicate the promise of adaptive and multimodal DL models of stable early-stage AD detection. In contrast, the TCBiNet framework, which incorporates TCN, BiLSTM, and attention, effectively addresses the variability of sequences and levels of resolution in temporal discrimination in early dementia prediction, while providing clinically interpretable and accurate predictions.

Predictive modeling for early diagnosis of dementia

The proposed system presents a clinically relevant and time-sensitive DL architecture to predict dementia at an early stage using multivariate patient records. The workflow of the proposed study is given in Figure.1. The dataset used consists of cognitive and functional variables and information diagnostic, including MMSE scores, functional assessment scores, and memory complaints. The next step is preprocessing these data - this begins with conducting missing value imputation and splitting patient records into sequenced windows on a 30-day basis, allowing for more micro-temporal analysis. The sequenced inputs are then processed by the novel TCBiNet architecture - which is a holistic framework incorporating TCN to identify short to mid-range trends, a BiLSTM module to facilitate long-range cognitive changes, and an attention mechanism that weights important time points associated with early dementia onset. This method does not rely on traditional models as it automatically mines for temporal disease and does not use human-crafted features and relies on a high level of interpretability of clinical information, clinical relevance, predictive robustness characteristics. This framework takes an end-to-end sequence-aware architecture and offers the novelty of transforming sparse clinical records into informative temporal pathways for the early diagnosis of dementia.

Data collection

The Alzheimer’s Disease Dataset [28] accessed from Kaggle, contains comprehensive health records for 2,149 patients aged 60–90, identified by unique patient IDs between 4751 and 6900. It contains numerous variables covering demographic variables (age, gender, ethnicity, education), lifestyle variables (BMI, alcohol use, smoking, diet, physical activity, sleep), and medical history (cardiovascular disease, diabetes, depression, head injury, hypertension). It also includes clinical measures (blood pressure, cholesterol), and different cognitive and functional measures (MMSE score, functional status, memory complaints, behavior indicators). The dataset al.so includes some symptoms associated with Alzheimer’s Disease (e.g., confusion, forgetfulness), as well as diagnosis status. This richly structured dataset is suitable for temporal patterns analysis, and the development of predictive models based on DL technology for early dementia diagnosis.

Dataset limitations

The dataset consists of 2,149 records of patients, which is enough to develop a model, but limits statistical power of small subgroups. The age distribution is uneven, with less cases of dementia as compared to non-dementia cases. To address the same, SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) was used during the training, where the classes were represented equally and to minimize bias in model learning. Also, the data is based on a particular clinical group, which can be a limitation to the ability to apply it to the wider population with other demographic or healthcare profiles. When interpreting predictive performance and clinical applicability, these limitations are considered.

Addressing class imbalance with SMOTE

To resolve the problem of class imbalance in the dataset, SMOTE was used. It creates artificial minority-class samples by using linear interpolations between the existing instances, thus maintaining the implicit distribution of clinical variables. This method maximizes exposure of the models to dementia cases without repeated data, minimizing biasness on the majority group and maximizing the sensitivity of the classifiers. With the establishment of a balanced training set, SMOTE achieves a robust learning experience, stabilizes performance metrics, and enables reliable early dementia prediction in unbalanced longitudinal health records.

Sequential pattern extraction (data mining)

The data mining task in this study is operationalized using DL-based sequential modeling, in which the aim is to identify discriminative temporal trends based on longitudinal patient health records. The conventional methods of data mining have been successful with the (static) tabular data but they are not usually sufficient to reflect the dynamic nature of clinical variables which holds vital information in identifying the onset of dementia. To deal with this, the current framework exploits powerful DL architectures that map segmented health sequences to temporally enriched feature representations. In particular, hybrid design is implemented combining TCN, BiLSTM networks, and a temporal attention mechanism. Hybrid TCN-BiLSTM-Attention architecture is optimized to capture full temporal dynamics in longitudinal clinical data. Long-term trends reflect slow, progressive alterations in cognitive and functional metrics, including consistent losses of MMSE or ADL scores, which indicate the general course of dementia. At the same time, short-term patterns indicate temporary fluctuations, abrupt excursions, or periodic anomalies in patient evaluations, indicating episodic cognitive decline or symptom fluctuation. Through shared modeling of these contrasting temporal features, the model generates rich, temporally aware feature representations that retain both local fluctuations and global trend direction. This facilitates the detection of clinically relevant trajectories, enhancing predictability and explainability for the early identification of dementia. Such a combination provides the opportunity to extract both the short-term fluctuations and the long-term progression patterns of cognitive and functional tests, such as Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores, Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scores, and self-reported memory complaints. The TCN component: captures local dependencies, local symptom fluctuations, BiLSTM: captures bidirectional temporal dependencies, and ensures that both previous and future clinical environments are maintained. The temporal attention mechanism also enhances these representations, as it gives more weight to important intervals that demonstrate early cognitive deterioration which makes the representations more interpretable and practically significant. By making deep learning the methodological center of the data mining pipeline, the framework that followed uncovered systematically clinically significant sequential patterns that would have remained hidden if the analysis was static and without a temporal dimension. This architecture not only enhances prediction precision but also guarantees the mined temporal features are representative of symptom trajectories that align with dementia progression trajectories.

Ablation study

This section describes the ablation study that was conducted to evaluate the utility of each component in the TCBiNet architecture. By performing an ablation study to the unit level (i.e. removing temporal convolutional blocks or attention) and measuring each contribution to overall performance, including input-level ablation studies also (i.e. using the isolated feature), the ablation studies quantify the ability of each input feature of TCBiNet.

The ablation study in Table 7. has highlighted each component’s additive contribution to the model’s total performance. Beginning with the TCN-only baseline, the addition of the BiLSTM module improved long-term sequence modeling for the task, providing a ~ 1.1% gain in accuracy. Subsequently, the addition of the temporal attention mechanism allowed the model to better focus on important time steps as attention weights were treated as additional features incorporated into the TCN features before classification. The comparison of F1-scores yielded a modest change from 96.10 for TCN-only to 97.73 for TCBiNet. The addition of the Flatten layer created a process by which TCBiNet obtained a more compact feature representation before classification. In addition, the application of SMOTE for class balancing further enhanced model performance, increasing sensitivity to minority-class instances and contributing to the final gains in accuracy and F1-score. In summation, the complete TCBiNet with all components converged with the best accuracy (99.51%) and F1-score (99.35%) together confirming the advantages in performance provided by the synergistic architectural months of TCBiNet.

Discussion

The results of this study validate that the integration of temporal modeling with DL greatly improves the early detection of dementia. Different from static models based on cross-sectional snapshots, the TCBiNet architecture can accurately model the non-linear development of dementia symptoms over time using a combination strategy of TCN, BiLSTMs, and Attention mechanisms. This architecture enables subtle and abrupt cognitive and functional status change to be detected with nuanced precision, providing predictive accuracy of over 99%. Temporal attention also increases model interpretability through weighting clinically important time points, a feature particularly vital in longitudinal clinical datasets where early signs are obscured by natural variability. The performance of the model was tested with thorough experiments involving ablation studies, interpretability analysis, and comparison with several baseline models, affirming the effectiveness of the framework under varying scenarios. A critical evaluation of the comparative outcomes points out TCBiNet outperforms baseline models because it can capture short-term dynamics with TCN, long-term dynamics with BiLSTM and clinically important temporal dynamics with attention mechanisms. Conversely, training models like CNN, CNN-LSTM, or DRL-XGBoost have low precision and recall due to lack of this integrated temporal sensitivity. These features in architecture are directly translated as clinical benefits in that they provide more reliable awareness of dementia earlier and with less false negative effects and more confidence in patient observation. Additionally, the study finds important details about data mining by pointing out that a combination of forgetfulness and confusion may suggest early stages of dementia. Based on feature importance, it was seen that scores from MMSE and changes in ADL over time were important, proving that the chosen features had clinical value. The approach used in the study which includes dealing with missing values and breaking down the time frame into 30-day windows, makes the information consistent and clear over time. Sequential modeling is shown to be better than standard models for making dementia predictions because all measures kept improving across different baseline combinations. As a whole, the research delivers impressive results and points out the role of considering time and symptom changes in creating accurate prediction systems for neurological diseases. The present study is dedicated to binary classification of the Alzheimer disease based on the Kaggle Alzheimer dataset, which contains labels indicating the presence or absence of the disease. Other types of dementia are not explicitly labeled in this dataset, including vascular, Lewy body, or frontotemporal dementia, so the analysis can only detect it in binary. But the TCBiNet architecture is designed to be multiclass-adaptable. With a few changes to the final softmax layer, which turns it into a probability generator over multiple categories, and a properly chosen multiclass loss, the model can distinguish between various subtypes of dementia. The TCN, BiLSTM, and Temporal attention modules still extract local, and global temporal dynamics at cognitive and functional indices, which makes sure that the subtypes trajectories are well represented. Therefore, binary detection is used in this case because of the limitations of the available data, although the proposed model offers a versatile structure that can be trained to do both binary and multiclass dementia classification without losing interpretability and clinical utility.

Conclusion and future work

In this study, TCBiNet was introduced and successfully used as a framework for the early prediction of dementia by analyzing current clinical data over time. By using TCN, BiLSTM, and Attention, the model was effective in recognizing the patterns of symptoms both over a short period and a longer span which led to better prediction results compared to CNN-LSTM, BiLSTM-DRL, and DRL-XGBoost. This model both increases the accuracy of diagnosis and enables doctors to easily interpret timing by watching the most relevant events in the patient’s progress. TCBiNet produced much better results than usual static models, reaching 99.51% accuracy along with an AUC-ROC of 0.99, proving its usefulness in real-time clinical environments.

Even though the research showed positive results, some aspects still need to be improved. At first, the model’s accuracy was checked using a particular dataset, but how it will work in different healthcare systems has not been confirmed. Also, while attention mechanisms make the model easier to understand, in some specific clinical uses, the model remains a black-box system. These limitations can be addressed in the future by using data from a broader population, bringing in modalities such as speech or images, and using explainable AI (XAI) methods to support medical professionals. Adaptive learning approaches could be added to make sure the model keeps improving as new information is received. As a result, subsequent research can focus on creating flexible, clear, and general models for predicting dementia which are useful for a range of clinical settings and continued innovation in tracking neurodegenerative diseases.
