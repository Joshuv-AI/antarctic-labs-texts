Idiopathic pneumoperitoneum: two cases with possible association to elevated intraluminal pressure
Authors: Shuling Wang, Xing Zhang, Wei Li, Jinlong Zhao, Li He, Ning Yang, Qiwei Zhao, Yang Wang, Jianwei Ma, Jian Zhang, Yihao Song, Fei Xu, Zhongxin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPneumoperitoneum is an uncommon condition characterized by free gas in the abdominal cavity, usually resulting from gastrointestinal (GI) tract perforation and requiring emergency surgery. Idiopathic pneumoperitoneum is a rare condition with no identifiable cause and might be managed conservatively in stable patients without peritonitis signs.Case presentationWe report two cases of idiopathic pneumoperitoneum diagnosed by Computed tomography (CT) scan without obvious evidence of GI perforation. The etiology remains unclear, the more plausible explanation would be that increased intraluminal or intra-abdominal pressure induced by intestinal obstruction or constipation may contribute to occult micro perforation or mucosal disruption, which may not be detectable on contrast-enhanced CT imaging. The patients exhibited varied clinical presentations and responded well to conservative management, with complete clinical recovery.ConclusionsThis report aims to emphasize the diagnostic challenges in differentiating idiopathic pneumoperitoneum from surgically urgent causes of pneumoperitoneum and to advocate for an appropriate approach to conservative management in stable patients, even when CT imaging demonstrates free intraperitoneal gas.Keywords: Idiopathic pneumoperitoneum, Non-surgical pneumoperitoneum, Pneumatosis cystoides intestinalis, Gastrointestinal tract perforation

Background

Pneumoperitoneum, characterized by the presence of intra-abdominal free gas, is most commonly (> 90% of reported cases) associated with gastrointestinal tract perforation [1] and is generally recognized as a surgical emergency, where delayed diagnosis and treatment have been associated with increased mortality [2]. However, approximately 10% of cases represent non-surgical pneumoperitoneum, or spontaneous pneumoperitoneum arising from abdominal, thoracic, or gynecological causes rather than bowel perforation [1, 3, 4]. In these rare instances, when radiographic detection of free intraperitoneal air is not accompanied by surgical indications- specifically in patients who remain hemodynamically stable without signs of peritonitis- conservative management may be considered [5]. While, idiopathic pneumoperitoneum specifically refers to cases within this non-surgical category in which a comprehensive etiologic assessment yields negative results after excluding all other known causes of pneumoperitoneum [6]. In patients without peritonitis, operative treatment is associated with increased morbidity and nonhome discharge, but not increased mortality [5]. Accurate differentiation between surgical and non-surgical etiologies is paramount, as it directly influences clinical decision-making and prevents unnecessary surgical intervention in patients with benign causes of free intraperitoneal air. We report two cases with free air in the abdominal cavity on CT scan associated with possible contributing factors, such as intestinal obstruction or constipation, which may lead to an increase in intraluminal pressure. However, no definitive causes was identified. They were treated conservatively after a comprehensive assessment and had a good recovery.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 77- year- old female patient for systematic clinical evaluation. She has dermatomyositis (DM) that is controlled with methotrexate and prednisone, and she is postoperative for hiatal hernia repair, but the surgical technique is unknown. She reported repeated abdominal bloating that had occurred a year before this admission that resolved spontaneously. One week prior to admission, the patient developed progressive abdominal pain and distension without an obvious trigger. The pain gradually intensified but remained manageable. The patient denied nausea, vomiting, fever, or chills. Bowel movements and passage of flatus were reduced compared to baseline. Physical examination revealed abdominal tenderness, but no guarding. An abdominal X-ray was performed, which demonstrated multiple areas of small bowel gas expansion with air-fluid levels arranged in a step-ladder pattern (Fig. 1A). Based on the clinical history and imaging findings, a diagnosis of partial small bowel obstruction was established. The patient improved clinically with conservative management and was discharged after symptom resolution. At follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with regular passage of flatus and stool without loss of weight, and this admission was initiated for systematic evaluation. Clinically the abdomen was soft and non-tender with reduced bowel sounds. Blood analysis showed: the WBC, C-reactive protein (CRP), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was within normal limit 5 × 109/L (3.5 ~ 9.5 × 109/L), 0.54 mg/L (0.00 ~ 10.00 mg/L), and 8 mm/h (0 ~ 20 mm/h), respectively; PR3, MPO, p- ANCA, and c- ANCA levels were normal. Abdominal (CT) scan showed massive intra-abdominal free gas, partial small-bowel obstruction, and thickened intestinal wall in the left upper and right lower abdomen (Fig. 1B). These findings are consistent with PET- CT, and malignancy has been excluded. The patient was asymptomatic without peritonitis, and the CT finding was free intraperitoneal gas without an obvious cause for pneumoperitoneum. Therefore, idiopathic pneumoperitoneum was diagnosed. It has been reported that pneumoperitoneum usually resolves within 2 days in two thirds of the cases and within 5 days in 97% cases when assessed by serial abdominal radiographs [7]. However, on PET-CT performed 5 days after admission, massive free intraperitoneal gas was still observed. Given the persistent massive pneumoperitoneum in the absence of perforation, a simple, safe procedure of percutaneous decompression [8] with a needle was chosen rather than conservative management alone. Her abdominal circumference decreased from 102 cm to 90 cm, the abdominal distention resolved, and repeat CT demonstrated reduced free gas in abdominal cavity (Fig. 1C).

Case 2

A 79-year-old female with a history of chronic constipation was admitted after free intraperitoneal air was identified on CT during routine follow-up, 10 years after resection of an ascending colon adenocarcinoma. She has a history of coronary artery disease managed with antiplatelet and anti-anginal therapy. Her relevant abdominal surgical history includes hysterectomy (indication unspecified) and left adnexal resection for an ovarian cyst. Clinically, the patient did not have abdominal distention. There was localized tenderness in the left abdomen with no features of peritonism. The laboratory findings were: WBC 7.6 × 109/L (3.5 ~ 9.5 × 109/L); CRP 10.87 mg/L (0.00 ~ 10.00 mg/L); PCT < 0.5 ng/ml (0.00 ~ 0.05 ng/L). Abdominal CT demonstrated free intraperitoneal gas in the vicinity of the left colon, and a large amount of solid fecal material within the colorectal lumen (Fig. 2A). The patient was asymptomatic with no clinical signs of peritonitis. CT imaging revealed free intraperitoneal gas, with no obvious etiology identified. Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with idiopathic pneumoperitoneum, and kept nil per mouth and a glycerol enema was administered to relieve constipation. A repeat abdominal CT performed six days later showed complete resolution of free gas (Fig. 2B), after which the patient was discharged.

Discussion and conclusions

This report describes two patients with pneumoperitoneum detected on CT scan in the absence of confirmed gastrointestinal perforation or obvious underlying etiology. Neither patient presented with clinical manifestations of peritonitis. Following comprehensive evaluation, both patients were ultimately diagnosed with non-surgical pneumoperitoneum, and the condition was subsequently classified as idiopathic spontaneous pneumoperitoneum since no cause could be determined. They underwent conservative management and achieved complete clinical recovery.

Pneumoperitoneum presents with diverse clinical manifestations, including abdominal pain, distension, fever, generalized peritonitis, massive ascites, and septic shock. In contrast, non-surgical pneumoperitoneum is distinguished by minimal abdominal pain and distension, along with the absence of peritoneal signs, fever, and leukocytosis [9]. Since operative intervention may raise mortality in pneumoperitoneum patients without peritonitis [5], distinguishing surgical from non-surgical causes by integrating clinical findings and underlying etiologies is crucial to prevent unnecessary surgery and enhance outcomes.

The possible causes of non-surgical pneumoperitoneum can be attributed to abdominal(30.6%), thoracic(43.4), gynecological(7.6%) and idiopathic (18.4%) based on the source of the air [10]. The abdominal causes consist of iatrogenic introduction with residual air from surgical procedures (60%), peritoneal dialysis (11%-34%), post-endoscopic procedures (25%) [10]. When iatrogenic etiologies are systematically excluded, pneumatosis cystoides intestinalis (PCI) emerges as a significant non-iatrogenic abdominal cause of pneumoperitoneum. In a recent study, among cases of extraluminal free air without iatrogenic introduction, PCI was observed in 24.7% of cases [6]. Intraperitoneal free air was detected in 9- 19.8% of PCI cases [11, 12]. PCI refers to a clinical or radiological finding of gas- filled cysts in the submucosa or subserosa of the intestinal wall [13], and the rupture of cysts can lead to non- surgical pneumoperitoneum without peritonitis [9]. PCI is mainly secondary to gastrointestinal, pulmonary, connective- tissue diseases and traumatic injury [14]. It has been reported that DM is involved in the development of PCI [15], and GI manifestations occur in up to 50% of DM patients [16], possibly due to intestinal vasculitis, thus damaging the mucosal membrane and immune barrier. Additonally, PCI development is associated with several drug classes: α-glucosidase inhibitors (AGIs) [17], immunosuppressants, and corticosteroids [18]. AGIs delay carbohydrate absorption, leading to bacterial fermentation, excess gas production, and gas translocation through compromised mucosa. Corticosteroids and immunosuppressants impair intestinal barrier function by depleting lymphoid tissue in Peyer’s patches, facilitating intraluminal gas diffusion into the bowel wall [19]. These mechanisms collectively contribute to PCI pathogenesis. Certain cases of pneumoperitoneum are associated with constipation alongside PCI. Elevated intestinal pressure serves as a key driver of this association, occurring either independently or in the presence of underlying PCI. According to the mechanical hypothesis of PCI pathogenesis, this process involves minute gas pockets migrating through microscopic perforations in intestinal wall structures- taking advantage of differential permeability that selectively allows gas passage while excluding solid or liquid bowel contents [20, 21]. Chronic constipation exacerbates this risk through two complementary mechanisms: (1) bacterial overproliferation that increases intraluminal hydrogen production and facilitates gas leakage into the intestinal wall; and (2) fecal loading and bowel distension that elevate intraluminal pressure, causing mechanical injury and microperforations in the mucosa [22]. Together, these mechanisms create favorable conditions for pneumoperitoneum formation.

Both patients showed pneumoperitoneum detected on CT scan in the absence of confirmed gastrointestinal perforation with clinical signs of peritonitis. Nither patients unwent rcent surgical nor endoscopic procedures, then non-iatrogenic abdominal cause of pneumoperitoneum should be considered. They suffered from intestinal obstruction or constipation before the onset of pneumoperitoneum, which may increase intraluminal pressure. Moreover, increased intestinal contents were observed, which may lead to bacterial overgrowth. The bacterial infection may produce gas and damage the intestinal walls allowing the gas to penetrate the intestinal walls into abdominal cavity when intraluminal pressure increased [23]. Additionally, the first patient presented with dermatomyositis and treated with prednisone and methotrexate, conditions known to predispose to PCI development. While elevated intraluminal pressure raised concern for potential PCI rupture, no conclusive evidence substantiated this occurrence. Although intraluminal pressure was not directly measured, we speculate that increased intraluminal pressure - potentially secondary to obstruction or constipation- may result in mucosal injury, microperforation, or the development and rupture of pneumatosis intestinalis, thereby contributing to pneumoperitoneum.

Non-surgical peumoperitoneum may also arise from thoracic conditions including blunt chest trauma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and severe coughing. These mechanisms involve alveolar or diaphragmatic defects that allow air to traverse mediastinal pathways to the retroperitoneum and bowel mesentery [24]. In contrast, gynecological causes illustrate another mechanism whereby air can pass upward through the fallopian tubes into the peritoneal cavity. Specific examples include sexual intercourse, vaginal douching, postpartum knee-chest exercises, bulb aspiration during pelvic examinations, and pelvic inflammatory disease [25]. Given that neither thoracic nor gynecological mechanisms were applicable to these patients, these causes were ruled out. These patients were therefore classified as idiopathic pneumoperitoneum.

For asymptomatic patients who are hemodynamically stable and without peritonitis, conservative management should be considered, although close monitoring remains mandatory [26]. Conversely, when patients present severe symptoms or aggravating factors, emergency surgery is indicated. Our findings corroborate previous observations: the patient was asymptomatic, with well-preserved general and local conditions and normal laboratory parameters representing typical clinical features of non-surgical pneumoperitoneum; consequently, we chose conservative treatment, and the patient achieved a favorable recovery.

The treatment of pneumoperitoneum without perforation is universally considered to be conservative, with initial management including supportive care and careful observation. It has been reported that pneumoperitoneum usually resolves within 2 to 5 days with conservative therapy in asymptomatic patients who have had perforation excluded by adequate examination [7]. However, when massive pneumoperitoneum persists beyond this expected absorption timeframe despite adequate observation, less invasive interventions such as percutaneous decompression may be considered. The decision to pursue decompression in our case was based on confirmation of idiopathic etiology through comprehensive imaging, failure of pneumoperitoneum to resolve within the expected timeframe, and the patient’s stable clinical status allowing intervention other than emergent surgery. This procedure is described as simple, safe, and effective, with the advantages of reducing surgery-associated morbidity and mortality, rapidly resolving pneumoperitoneum, and preventing abdominal compartment syndrome [8]. Many risks of surgical intervention are mitigated in this situation because the increased abdominal pressure displaces much of the intra-abdominal contents, making the procedure relatively safe when performed correctly. Percutaneous needle decompression is particularly appropriate for patients who are poor surgical candidates or in whom laparotomy may be avoided. Recent evidence demonstrates uniformly successful outcomes without procedure-related adverse events. In our patient, the procedure resulted in immediate clinical improvement, with abdominal circumference decreasing from 102 cm to 90 cm, complete resolution of abdominal distention, and significantly reduced free gas on repeat CT.

In conclusion, idiopathic pneumoperitoneum is a rare condition characterized by the presence of intra-abdominal free gas with unclear or unexplained origin. It usually presents with mild symptoms and does not require surgical treatment. Accurately assessing the underlying cause and the patient’s clinical status is essential to avoid unnecessary laparotomy. In hemodynamically stable patients without peritoneal signs, a conservative, non- operative approach is recommended. Conversely, the appearance of severe symptoms or signs of peritonitis necessitates prompt surgical intervention.
