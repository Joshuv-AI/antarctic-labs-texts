Enhancing antitumor immunity through oncolytic viruses: advances in genetic engineering
Authors: Akmal Zubair, Lazaros Lazarou, Abdullah M Alkahtani, Muhammad Yaqoob Shahani, Naila Afghan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnother novel type of immunotherapy that is being developed to specifically kill cancer cells is oncolytic viruses, which are genetically engineered to destroy cancer cells while leaving normal tissues virtually intact. Progress in genetic engineering has improved their therapeutic impact, allowing the integration of immune-activating factors such as GM-CSF and facilitating their use alongside checkpoint inhibitors. Beyond the direct killing of tumor cells, oncolytic viruses are also known to cause immunogenic cell death, resulting in the release of tumor-related antigens. Such antigens stimulate dendritic cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes, thereby intensifying the antitumor immune response in the body. However, several barriers still restrict their clinical effectiveness, including poor penetration within tumors, rapid clearance, and immune escape mechanisms. In order to deal with these obstacles, scientists are exploring higher-order platforms of delivery such as nanoparticle-based carriers, mesenchymal stem cells, and vesicle-mediated systems. These modalities will enhance the stability of the virus and the precision of the tumor targeting. Tailoring OV therapy to match the molecular and immunological characteristics of individual tumors could further optimize outcomes. Successfully overcoming these challenges would solidify oncolytic virotherapy as a transformative strategy in cancer immunotherapy, emphasizing the need for ongoing study and rigorously designed clinical trials to unlock its full potential.Keywords: Cancer, Oncolytic viruses, Therapy, Gene editing

Introduction

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) were chosen as a research topic due to such an important consideration since they have the ability to selectively infect and destroy carcinoma cells, but they do not damage normal cells. The treatment of cancer with attenuated oncolytic viruses is not an innovation or new idea. This has been demonstrated in case studies that indicate that there is a temporary decrease in tumor burden in the case of cancer patients, possibly due to endogenous microbial infections. This phenomenon was reported back in the middle of the 19th century. This was previous to the first clinical test involving OVs, which was reported in 1949. In the late 1890s, a patient with leukemia that showed flu-like symptoms, high inflammation, and decreased tumor cells was observed. The fact that viruses may play a therapeutic role and that the information has been accumulated due to the success of the discovery. Back in 1949, therapeutic research studies were initiated with the use of different non- attenuated viral strains [1, 2]. The OV community has targeted its attention in genetically modified viruses and live attenuated vaccines, which are safer to use in human beings. In the last two to three decades, there has been a new dawn of using modified viruses that are genetically modified in treating cancer. This has been influenced by the technological development of the viral engineering technology, and an increased sense of the living system science of the potential oncolytic viruses [3]. This has made the innovations such as the elimination of viral DNA segments and the adoption of therapeutic transgenes possible. Viral treatment is one type of treatment of tumours in which tumour is cured by virus by killing the tumor cells and preserving the healthy ones in different forms. This is termed as the virotherapy whereby the viruses are reprogrammed through biotechnology in order to treat cancer [4]. It is divided into three key sub disciplines; oncolytic viruses, which mainly infect neoplastic cells; viral plasmids employed to deliver genes in gene therapy and viruses that trigger immunotherapeutic responses. All of these treatments are founded on the practices that underpin the general approach, i.e. the therapeutic strategies [5]. Examples include viral-mediated suicide gene transfer, gene inhibition via RNA technologies, and the overexpression of certain genes to induce cancer cell death [6]. The anticancerous capability of these viruses is enhanced by their potential to fastly reproduce within the tumor cells and also the ability to destroy and kill the cancer cell membranes. Consequently, the defense mechanism can quickly identify and eradicate tumor cells due to the antigens produced during viral infection. An increasing number of breast cancer treatments now incorporate oncolytic viruses into their protocols [6]. Traditional cancer therapies have several drawbacks, notably their inability to selectively target tumor cells, destroying both diseased and normal cells. Viruses, however, have emerged as a unique and promising therapeutic option because they can selectively infect tumor cells by binding to specific receptors that are more produced on these cells [7, 8]. For example, the measles virus is known to target multiple myeloma cells, which overexpress receptors that make them particularly susceptible to infection.

Molecular mechanism of oncolytic viruses

Oncolytic viruses specifically attack cancer cells and replicate them and cause direct destruction of the tumor. This viral action leads to the destruction of infected cells to produce new viral particles and tumor-related antigens. The resultant cell death is very immunogenic, in that it releases danger signals of PAMPs, DAMPs and TAAs into the tumor environment. These cues lead the immune system to activate dendritic cells by increasing their ability to perceive molecules such as ATP, HMGB1, and calreticlin on dead cells and promoting the development and augmentation of the ability to uptake antigens [9–11]. Pattern-recognition receptors (such as TLRs and STING) perceive the viral nucleic acids, leading to the production of type I interferons and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1 2, IL-6, IL-12, IL-GM-CSF, and IL-TNF-1, etc.), which turn immunologically cold tumors into inflamed hot tumors with enhanced immune cell infiltration [12]. The natural response preconditions the strong adaptive immunity [13]. The released tumor antigens are processed by antigen-presenting cells and presented via MHC to the T cells, which provides them with the activation signals (TCR engagement, costimulation and cytokines) needed to prime T-cells. Inflammation (chemokines such as CXCL10, CXCL5) induced by OV draws CD8 + T cells into the tumor. These T lymphocytes are called cytotoxic T lymphocytes; they identify tumor epitopes and kill tumor cells, both infected and uninfected, thus controlling local and distant lesions. More importantly, OV therapy regularly results in immune memory: the death of tumor cells induced by the virus and repeated exposure to antigens stimulates differentiation of memory T cells, which would provide long-term safety against the repetition [14]. To conclude, oncolytic virotherapy is considered to be an in-situ vaccine, which is based on direct oncolysis of tumors combined with robust innate and adaptive antitumor immunity, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

T-Vec

Two mutations, one in the ICP34.5 DNA segment and another in the ICP47 DNA segment, are responsible for the production of T-VEC, a modified subtype of herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1). Additionally, the gene that codes for GM-CSF is located near the ICP34.5 loci [15]. The primary mechanism for directing viral replication toward cancer cells, thereby reducing pathogenicity in healthy cells, involves mutations in the ICP34.5 gene [16–18]. By deactivating ICP34.5, the virus is prevented from replicating in healthy cells because this gene normally inhibits the reaction of host cells against virus, particularly by preventing the shutdown of protein synthesis. ICP34.5-deficient HSV-1 can duplicate selectively in tumor cells and subsequently kill them because cancer cells often fail to respond to this shut-off signal [19]. The inhibition of the ICP47 gene impairs the function of the host cell’s antigen presentation transporter, preventing the repression of MHC class I production. This is expected to boost immunological responses against cancer cells [20–22]. Furthermore, timely activation of the adjacent US11 gene increases the likelihood of viral replication in cancer cells lacking ICP47. Although extensive preclinical evidence is limited, the expression of GM-CSF aims to enhance immune activation against tumors [23].

The anticancer action of an oncolytic virus and its selectivity to cancer cells compared to normal cells. In case an oncolytic virus is introduced to normal cells, either immune or non-immunological cells, innate antiviral defense mechanisms inhibit viral replication, therefore, leaving the healthy tissues undamaged in most cases. This immune effect can be reinforced by the recognition and elimination of the virus by immune cells. On the other hand, the oncolytic virus has the potential to enter the tumor cells, replicate, and accumulate in them successfully due to the presence of impaired antiviral signaling pathways and invalid cellular controls in cancerous cells [24]. The same process eventually results in cell lysis of cancer cells, and further emission of novel viral particles, which can infect other tumor cells in the immediate vicinity [25]. All these processes are accentuated by the fact that oncolytic viruses can take benefit of the weaknesses of cancer cells to cause patients to suffer specific tumor elimination without changing the normal functioning of the cells [26]. G47Δ

There are three mutations in the third version of oncolytic HSV-1, G47Delta. Todo et al. established it by introducing a second removal modification into the genomic content of the second route HSV-1 G207. The primary goal in creating G47Δ was to enhance its ability to induce specific antitumor immunity, thereby developing the efficacy of G207 against cancer while maintaining its safety profile. Both the γ34.5 and α47 genes are involved in generating two of the G47Δ mutations, similar to those found in T-Vec [31, 32]. Additionally, the incorporation of the LacZ gene from Escherichia coli into G47Δ results in the inactivation of the genetic unit of ICP6. ICP6 DNA segment encoding the major subunit of ribonucleotide reductase (RR), an enzyme required in the synthesis of the DNA of the virus [31]. The HSV-1 is not able to replicate outside of the fast-growing cells, which possess sufficient boron nucleotide reduction enzyme in the host to compensate for the malfunction of the ICP6. Because of these three engineered modifications, G47Δ is expected to exhibit significantly reduced virulence and enhanced safety in healthy tissues compared to G207 and T-Vec, both of which contain only two mutations [33, 34]. Unlike other oncolytic HSV-1 strains, G47Δ offers a broader therapeutic window because it can inhibit the proliferation of γ34.5-reduced variants like G207 while demonstrating greater duplication capacity in malignant cells. Moreover, the therapeutic window can be expanded due to the deletion of 47 genes of the US11 that leads to immediate-early expression instead of shut down of protein synthesis prematurely. Compared to G207, G47Δ exhibited significantly higher levels of both antitumor efficacy and replicative capacity [35]. In virtually all of the in vivo solid tumour models studied, namely glioma, breast, prostate, schwannoma [36], nasopharyngeal [37], hepatocellular [38], colorectal, malignant [39] peripheral nerve sheath [40] and thyroid malignancies [40], G47Δ was efficacious [41, 42]. Studies indicate that G47Δ can effectively eliminate cancer stem cells associated with glioblastoma. Currently, G47Δ is the lone advanced-creation HSV-1 that has been examined in human beings [43]. A total of $27.41 million is involved in its development. Patients with recurrent or residual glioblastoma (UMIN000015995) began participating in the second phase of clinical testing in 2015, following the successful completion of a stage I-II research conducted in Japan in 2014. During the first two weeks, two stereotactic injections of G47Δ (1 × 10^9 pfu) are administered directly into the cancer in brain. Subsequent injections are given every four weeks, with a maximum of six administrations in total. In February 2016, the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare (MHLW) officially denoted G47Δ as a “Sakigake” melistone therapeutic agent. The term means “ahead of the world new policy, the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency of Japan (PMDA) conducts initial evaluations and expedited reviews of selected drug candidates, including G47Δ. This designation is expected to facilitate a faster approval process by the MHLW. In addition to clinical trials for glioblastoma, a transrectal ultrasound-guided transperineal method (UMIN000010463) was used to administer 3.9 × 10^8 pfu of G47Δ into the prostates of affected individuals diagnosed with non-responsive prostate carcinoma to castration. Patients in the first cohort obtained 2 dosages of G47Δ, those in the second cohort obtained 3 doses, and those in the third cohort received four doses, following a fixed dose-escalation protocol. Patients have reacted positively to the therapy, and no substantial adverse events related to G47Δ have been reported to date. Since 2013, patients diagnosed with recurrent olfactory neuroblastoma have been enrolled in clinical testing, I stage (UMIN000011636).

The immunogenic cascade that is activated by cancer cell destruction (mediated by oncolytic viruses), as well as both the direct effect and the immune-mediated antitumor effect. After being infected, cancer cells are then destroyed, releasing newly formed viral particles and tumors and viral antigens, and danger signals like PAMPs and DAMPs. These cues lure and trigger the immune cells, such as natural killer cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells. The released antigens are then absorbed by dendritic cells, which are activated and then move to lymphoid organs where they present tumor-specific antigens to T cells. The resultant effect of this process is the production of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which are directed to tumor cells [44, 45]. These activated T cells subsequently return to the tumor microenvironment and identify and attach to tumor cells, unleashing their effector molecules (perforin, granzymes and pro-inflammatory cytokines). The end result of this synchronous action by immune cells is an increased cancer cell destruction beyond the already infected cells leading to a systemic, adaptive antitumor immune reaction [46]. JX-594

The JX-594 vaccinia virus, correspondingly recognized as Pexa-Vec, has been genetically modified. This modification includes a modification in the thymidine kinase DNA segment, which restricts the multiplication of the virus specifically to neoplastic cells [47]. Besides, the virus also incorporates the human GM-CSF gene thereby boosting its effectiveness in the treatment of malignant diseases which is a great advantage. The presence of the LacZ gene is another unusual aspect of the JX-594 as it is a marker. The vaccinia virus as a live attenuated vaccine has had a number of advantages, among them being established safety profile, high cytotoxicity and stability of the virus upon intravenous injection [48, 49]. A Phase I trial involved 42 patients and they confirmed that JX-594 as intralesional introduction was usually well tolerated, exhibited effective viral multiplication, GM-CSF generation and systemic distribution of intravenous vehicles in both primary and metastatic liver cancers [50]. The first dose-limiting toxicity was acute hyperbilirubinemia. The first step of dosing-escalation study involved the administration of escalating dosage of JX-594 to validate the appropriateness of this drug by intravascular method. A second dose-finding provides on the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma used 46 personages. The mean OS of the low dose cohort was 6.7 months and the mean OS of the high dose one was 14.1 months [50]. This indicates that a higher dose of JX-594 resulted in a statistically significant extension of OS (n = 14 vs. 16) [51, 52]. By the end of 2015, a Phase III trial (PHOCUS, NCT02562755) was initiated, recruiting patients with advanced hepatocellular carcinoma. Intralesional injections of JX-594 (10^9 pfu) are administered to the experimental group on days 1, 15, and 29 of every two-month cycle. These injections are given subcutaneously. Sorafenib is administered to the experimental group on day 43, while the control group begins treatment with four hundred milligrams of Nexavar two times in a day from the start of the treatment.

CG0070

The human E2F-1 promoter was responsible for directing the GM-CSF gene of human beings, and the E1A gene was introduced into Ad5 adenoviruses via genetic modification (48). Bladder cancer is commonly associated with mutations in the retinoblastoma tumor suppressor protein (Rb), which controls E2F-1. The dissociation of Rb from its binding site triggers the transcriptional activation of E2F-1. In the first phase of the clinical trial for CG0070, participants were selected from Individuals who had carcinoma of the bladder that is superficial and who had not responded to Bacillus Calmette-Guérin treatment. Intravascular injections of 3, 9, or 101 viral particles (vp) were administered every 28 days, six times a week, or as needed, in one or more doses. These injections were given intravenously in three different dosages. Commonly reported side effects included dysuria, bladder soreness, and increased urinary frequency. Grade 1–2 bladder noxiousness was among the extremely observed symptoms. None of the side effects related to the treatment were clinically significant or directly associated with the drug. Due to the increased number of patients receiving multiple doses, the response rate improved significantly, rising from 48.6% (17 of 35) to 63.6% (14 of 22). This increase was substantial. Twelve patients with a tumor in the bladder participated in the BOND randomized phase II/III study. Fifteen patients received CG0070, while seven were treated with standard intravesical therapy as a control (NCT01438112). A third stage of clinical testing is currently recruiting affected people with BCG-resistant superficial carcinoma of the bladder. In a high-risk patient category, the research showed that CG0070 treatment resulted in a longer-lasting result. However, no discernible difference existed between the two classes regarding the initial complete response. Four patients (57%) in the unaffected group reacted to CG0070 therapy, whereas eight patients (53%) did. Especially, the control group exhibited a higher response rate. Patients will receive an intravenous injection of CG0070 at a dosage of 10^12 viral structures per week for forty-two days. This administration schedule will be followed consistently. Six months after the initial therapy, it is recommended that patients who have shown signs of recovery undergo the same induction cycle again (BOND2, NCT02365818). Adenovirus-based oncolytic viruses

Oncorine, H101

In late 2005, the Oncorine (also known as H101) was approved by the China Food and Drug Administration (CFDA), as the first recombinant oncolytic adenovirus for the treatment of nasopharyngeal cancer [53–55]. To create this oncolytic virus, the removal of the E1B gene and an incomplete removal of the E3 gene were both necessary. In the stage III test, patients diagnosed with either head and neck tumors of squamous cells or gastric squamous cell cancer were enrolled. The study evaluated whether intratumoral injections of Adriamycin and 5-fluorouracil (AF) or cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil (PF) in combination with H101 were more effective than PF or AF alone. The trial results confirmed that intratumoral injection of H101 is a safe and effective therapeutic option. This research was crucial for obtaining CFDA authorization for oncorine’s use in combination cancer treatment [56]. Following its approval, Oncorine participated in Chinese clinical trials for four distinct types of malignancies. The whole reaction frequency for patients cured with Oncorine for malignant pleural effusion was 38%. Furthermore, the E1B 55 kDa gene is also targeted by the oncolytic virus Onyx-015, which was created by Onyx Pharmaceuticals in South San Francisco, USA. Treatment-based testing of Onyx-015 covered individuals suffering from squamous cell cancer of the neck and head (HNSCC), as well as pancreatic, ovarian, colorectal cancers, and premalignant oral dysplasia [57]. Study by Charles and collaborators established that topical use of Onyx-015 as a mouthwash may be an effective treatment for oral dysplasia [58]. In a stage I studied, Onyx-015 combined with gemcitabine was shown to be safe for treating pancreatic cancer. Among the 21 patients enrolled, six had controlled disease, 2 achieved an incomplete outcome, and two experienced a negligible response. Still, the existence of pre-existing deactivating antibodies poses a substantial confront to the efficacy of Onyx-015 therapy [59].

DNX-2401

Furthermore, DNAtrix located in Houston, the United States has come up with DNX-2401, a oncolytic adenovirus virus that is an oncolytic virus present in DNX-2401. The first one is the removal of 24 bp at E1A site. The RGD sequence is added in its turn using the HI-loop of the fiber knob. The advantage of this introduction is that it improves the ability of the virus to infect and generate in the cancer cells by amplifying the adenoviral infectivity, as well as that of the virus to be sequestered by the normal cells producing the coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor (CAR). This modification highlights the profile of effectiveness and protection of DNX-2401. DNX-2401 was used in treating two groups of patients in recurrent malignant glioma clinical trials [60]. A group received a single dosage whereas Group B received a two dosage of the first on day 1 in implanted catheters in several areas and the second one on day 14 after tissue shrinkage. In A Group, three of five patients who survived longer than three years had a tumor reduction of more than 95%, and this led to the overall survival rate of 25%. This data indicates that DNX-2401 is a promising therapeutic mediator in the treatment of glioma. Two preclinical phase I trials are underway: one is DNX-2401 with temozolomide in recurrent glioblastomas (NCT01956734), and the other one is DNX-2401 in combination with interferon-g (NCT02197169). Also, DNX-2401 is also being tested intratumorally in children with diffuse intrinsic pontine gliomas (DIPG) three to four weeks prior to radiation or chemotherapy as a follow up to a successful treatment with glioblastoma (NCT03178032). The findings of this study are yet to be provided. Moreover, an innovative one is a prevalent estimate of the intravenous delivery of pembrolizumab in conjunction with DNX-2401 right down tumors in patients with recurring glioblastoma or gliosarcoma (NCT02798406) [61]. ONCOS-102

There is a significant similarity between DNX-2401 and ONCOS-102 [62]; however, Targovax, a Norwegian company, incorporates GM-CSF, a potent immune stimulant, into their formulation. In a stage I analysis, ONCOS-102 was administered in conjunction with oral cyclophosphamide at modest doses for treating twelve patients diagnosed with solid tumors (NCT01598129) [63]. The study represented that the cure was well tolerated, produced a limited number of adverse effects, and elicited a tumor-specific immune response. Currently, thirty patients diagnosed with mesothelioma are participating in a stage I/II trial using ONCOS-102, used together with cisplatin (NCT02879669) and pemetrexed [64]. Additionally, a novel treatment regimen combining pembrolizumab and ONCOS-102 was administered to twelve patients with metastatic melanoma (NCT03003676). These findings indicate that ONCOS-102 induces a tumor-targeted immune response while causing minimal adverse effects [65].

VCN-01

VCN Biosciences SL, based in Barcelona, Spain, is responsible for developing the oncolytic adenovirus VCN-01. This virus incorporates hyaluronidase and specifically targets the Rb pathway. Numerous clinical studies are currently examining the use of this oncolytic virus. A study of stage I dose rise included affected individuals with advanced compact tumors cured with intravenous VCN-01, gemcitabine, or Abraxane, administered either alone or in combination. Subsequent trials include NCT02045602 and NCT02245603. Another study, NCT03284268, is conducting a stage I study to assess the safety as well as efficiency of VCN-01 in patients with refractory retinoblastoma (RTB) [64]. Research on glioma cells in both animal models and in vitro demonstrated that VCN-01 exhibits cytotoxic effects. Additionally, intravitreal injections of VCN-01 have been administered to pediatric patients with chemotherapy-resistant retinoblastoma by a team at SJD Barcelona Children’s Hospital. The data revealed that viral replication was sufficient to trigger effects against cancer in the vitreous pits without triggering an inflammatory state within the tumor [65].

LOAd-703

Lokon Pharma, based in Uppsala, Sweden, is responsible for producing the oncolytic virus LOAd-703. This virus is modified to produce the CD40L and 4-1BBL genes. LOAd-703, an oncolytic adenovirus, is now being assessed for effectiveness in two Stage I/II clinical studies. Two separate investigations have investigated the potential use of LOAd-703 in combo with gemcitabine or conventional chemical treatment. One trial (NCT02705196) focuses on patients with pancreatic cancer, while the other study (NCT03225989) involves patients with biliary, colorectal, ovarian, or other types of cancer [66].

ICOVIR-7 and ICOVIR-5

While the Catalonia Institute of Oncology was responsible for producing ICOVIR-5, the Targovax Institute of Oncology developed the oncolytic adenovirus known as ICOVIR-7. A total of twenty-one patients with solid tumors, who had either progressed to a more advanced stage or shown resistance to prior therapies, participated in the ICOVIR-7 clinical study. The trial demonstrated the treatment’s effectiveness against tumors, as evidenced by tumor stability or reduction in size. Additionally, the findings—which included 1 partial response (1PR), 2 minor responses (2MR), and 2 cases of stable disease (2SD)—indicate that ICOVIR-7 is suitable for utilizing in future therapeutic testing [67].

The virus was successfully administered systemically to individuals in a stage I clinical test with severe cancer that included dose escalation. Despite this, the tumors did not shrink as a result of the treatment. Oncolytic adenoviral viruses exploit the fact that most cancer cells have inactivated p53 to increase their rate of proliferation. Both Oncorine and Onyx-015 lack the E1B (55 kDa) gene, which usually inhibits apoptosis through its specific interaction with the p53 protein [68]. The E1B protein, with a molecular weight of 55 kilodaltons, helps normal cells reduce their capacity to support viral replication. In contrast, cancer cells lacking p53 allow the reconstituted virus to replicate [69]. Oncolytic viruses also contain a 19-kilodalton fragment of the E1B protein, which can block Bax activation and thereby prevent cell death. To differentiate itself from Onyx-015 and Oncorine, which target an oncolytic approach through the use of virus-mediated cytotoxicity and virus-mediated tumor cell-specific replication, DNX-2401 would be working on its ability to infect cancer cells. The RGD-4 C motif allows DNX-2401 to infect cancerous cells with defects in the tumor suppressor, Rb/p16, mechanism selectively [70–72]. Besides, the selectivity of the surface receptor of the modified grit protein is the reason behind the oncolysis after viral replication and the proliferation of the tumor cells that is independent of the receptor. VCN-01 carries the enzyme hyaluronidase which breaks the extracellular matrix (ECM), which is a physical barrier that tumor cells block the process of viral multiplication [73–75]. This degradation allows chemotherapeutic agents to penetrate deeper into the tumor, enhancing their efficacy. Specifically, LoAd703 was designed to inhibit tumor development [76].

Vaccinia virus

The vaccine virus (VV) was a member of the genus of Orthopoxvirus and a DNA virus between 190 and 195 kilobases in diameter. Viral particle is 270 nm in length and 350 nm in width with a brick like structure [77]. The inner structure of the nucleic acid protein complex in this virus is encircled in a single coating of phospholipid bi-layers as opposed to other viruses that use double stranded DNA which have the inner structure of a single coating of phospholipid bi-layers [78, 79]. It also shows an extremely complicated and asymmetrical system when compared to other viruses. Intracellular mature virions (IMVs) is the most widespread type of entity of viruses that have morphologic change [80]. The viruses can be introduced into the host cells by means of integration into the external cell outer membrane or via the receptor-mediated endocytosis. These entry modes occur in an acidic PH environment, yet the extraneous environment is neutral. It can do so through 8 virus proteins including H2, J5, L5, G3, G9, A16, A21 and A28 [81]. Although these peptides have not been shown to bind unique receptors on host cells, they remain functionally important [82]. The endoplasmic reticulum (ER), which surrounds the cytoplasmic mini-nuclei, is the only location where viral replication and progeny assembly take place [83]. Only the viral core contains the enzymes needed to start viral post-infection transcription [49]. Thymidine kinase (TK) gene performs an important role in the specificity of enzyme in viral replication. The upregulation of thymidine kinase is typical in the cancer cells types but almost nonexistent in the normal cell types. Through this difference, scientists have been able to construct a TK- gene deletion VV subtype that selectively replicates in types of tumor cells [49]. In the host cell, viral peptides cause the EGFR-RAS pathway, which results in the increased synthesis of TK. High velocity and rapid transmission of VV are also the main distinguishing peculiarities of this virus due to its active and quick life cycle [77]. Other strengths are that it is able to identify smallpox and has a properly defined genome of about 40 kilodaltons of genes. The oncolytic virus JX-594 is able to be injected intravenously due to its ability to resist complement and antibodies [50].

Reovirus

Double-stranded RNA is the defining characteristic of reoviruses, which are naturally occurring viruses that lack an envelope [84]. The viral structure comprises of two key apparatuses: the outer capsid and the inner core [85]. Reoviruses infect host cells through receptor-mediated endocytosis, whereby the junctional adhesion molecule A (JAM-A) receptor is bound. Since certain types of cancers express high levels of JAM-A, reoviruses present prospects in oncolytic virus therapy whereby the virus targets cancer cells selectively. Some examples of these cancers are breast cancer [86], diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [87], multiple myeloma [88], and non-small cell lung cancer. During infection, endosomes facilitate the secretion of the core of virus. This core can transcribe viral RNA following the acid-mediated breakdown of the outer capsid [89]. Transcription and translation processes that produce progeny viruses occur within viral inclusions in the cytoplasm. Reoviruses do not affect the host cell nucleus at any stage of their life cycle. Due to the frequent presence of RAS signaling abnormalities in malignancies, reoviruses have evolved mechanisms to preferentially target tumor cells [90]. Within cancer cells, there is an association between RAS regulation and the inactivation of protein kinase R (PKR) [91]. In healthy cells, binding of reovirus double-stranded RNA initiates PKR, which phosphorylates eukaryotic initiation factor 2 (eIF2), declaring it dormant and thereby inhibiting the process of translating viral mRNA. There are three distinct reovirus serotypes known to exist [92]. Dearing strain type 3 (T3D) is the third serotype and is used in the production of Reolysin® [92], an investigational oncolytic agent. This strain demonstrates excellent compatibility with intravenous administration and shows no evidence of dose-limiting toxicity or significant harmful effects. Furthermore, it exhibits potent anticancer activity [93].

Measles virus

In the 1970s and 1980s, medical professionals observed clinical cases in which individuals with blood cancer or Lymphatic cancer experienced remission following a measles virus attack [94]. Since then, MV has been systematically developed as an immunotherapeutic agent for cancer treatment. MV can exist in two morphological forms: spherical or filamentous, with diameters ranging from 120 to 250 nanometers. Its nucleocapsid consists essentially of a singular sequence of anti-sense RNA that is continuous throughout its length, encompassing approximately sixteen kilobase pairs in the entire genome. Subsequent fundamental research [95–97] demonstrated that MV functions in both the lysis and death of tumor cell types and can effectively replicate within malignant tumor cells. Oncolytic MV therapy targets tumor cells by the exploitation of cellular membrane receptors like CD150 and CD46. Although CD46 is stated on maximum healthy tissue cell types, MV does not cause disease in these cells because the attenuated oncolytic MV is eliminated via the interferon (IFN) antiviral pathway [98]. Most malignant cells have elevated CD46 expression relative to healthy cells, hence increasing the capacity of oncolytic MVs to infect malignant cells and disseminate inside the malignancy. Additionally, malignant tumor cells often lack a functional type I interferon system or have a diminished reaction against virus, making them more disposed to infection [99]. Both DNA methylation and cellular division are involved in this process. Oncolytic MVs show increased vulnerability to the IFN system, because their P, V, and C peptides, components of pathogenic MVs have been deleted [100]. Since different MV strains have distinct coding sequences for these proteins, engineering oncolytic MVs requires careful control [101]. To date, no comprehensive studies have investigated the effectiveness of oncolytic MVs concerning these modifications. The cytopathic effects of MV are closely linked to its oncolytic activity. invaded cancer cell types bind to form multinucleated syncytia, which disrupts cell membranes and ultimately leads to cell death. Several animal models of human-origin cancers have been used to validate protein expression, syncytium formation, the effects of oncolytic agents, and viral replication. These models include hematologic malignancies such as leukemia [102], as well as solid tumors [102, 103], including ovarian carcinoma [104], glioblastoma [105], liver [106], pancreatic [107], breast [108], and stomach cancers [109].

Newcastle disease virus

The virus with a negative-sense single-stranded RNA genome and belonging to type 1 avian paramyxovirus (APMV-1), is the Newcastle disease virus (NDV). NDV has evolved an immunological escape mechanism within the chicken host, which it exploits to infect other birds by interfering with the host’s interferon type I (IFN-1) mediated immune response [110]. Key to this viral escape strategy is the fact that, it is not reliant on the host species, but rather, it is advantageous in susceptible and non-susceptible hosts [111]. In non-permissive hosts, several NDV strains induce spontaneous eosinophilia and exhibit immunostimulatory properties [111, 112]. The pathogenicity and virulence of NDV enable it to be classified in three categories strains of mesogenic (moderate virulence), lentogenic (low virulence) and velogenic (high virulence) categories [113]. This increase in genome size is expected since prior studies have shown threefold to sixfold increase in the size of the NDV genome compared to other APMV-1 viruses. The genomic RNA has a 30-base-pair extragenic leader sequence at the 3 end and a 50-base-pair trailer sequence in the 5 end [114]. Such orbits are important in wrapping up newly formed RNA into viral particles and also in modulation of viral transcription and replication. The Newcastle disease virus (NDV) contains six genes that are arranged sequentially as 3’ -N -P -M -F -H N -L -5’. The nucleoprotein is coded by the N gene, phosphoprotein is coded by the P gene, the matrix protein is coded by the M gene, the fusion protein is coded by the F gene, the protein hemagglutinin-neuraminidase is coded by the HN gene and the large polymerase protein is coded by the L gene [115]. Gene editing technologies have facilitated the insertion of foreign genes with anticancer properties into NDV’s large genome, enhancing its potential as a therapeutic vector.

Cells infected with NDV initially use the HN molecule to bind to the cell-binding domain. When the α2,6-sialic acid is present then both HDV and HN attach to the host cell surface when α2,6-sialic acid is present. After attachment to the surface of the cell, the protein, which is called the fusion protein (F) is released. The virus may fuse with the host cell membrane and implant its genome into the cytoplasm of the host cell when HN and F cooperate [116]. Clathrin-mediated endocytosis and other forms of endocytosis are additional entry mechanisms that NDV may utilize [117]. Transcription of NDV RNA into messenger RNA (mRNA) occurs in the cytoplasm. Unlike human mRNA, viral mRNA retains a 5′-triphosphate (5′-PPP) group and is polyadenylated. The viral mRNA then uses the substrates and translation procedure of the host cell to create viral proteins. The freshly generated nucleocapsid is used as the positive sense in the next stage of viral replication [118]. The viral nucleocapsid is made up of three proteins (NP, P, and L) and an RNA genome that is around 15,000 nucleotides long. The P and L proteins are bound by the RNA-based RNA polymerase, which is necessary for the multiplication of the virus. Between 10^3 and 10^4 virus particles may be produced by each infected cell. When a virus buds from the cell membrane releases new viral particles, its genome is encased in the plasma membrane. In most cases, when healthy cell types from non-permissive vectors like humans or mice are infected, the infection fails to progress beyond the stage where NDV mRNA is translated into peptide, preventing viral replication and spread [119]. Tumor cell infection by NDV is linked to the breakage region of the F protein and the second sialic acid attachment site of the HN protein. While avoiding direct cell lysis and death, persistent infection may promote viral cell-to-cell transmission and tumor cell immunogenic cell death (ICD). This mechanism may help the virus avoid the host’s antiviral defenses [120]. Both naturally occurring and genetically modified NDV strains have potential as treatments for human tumors, either alone or in combination with standard cancer therapies [121].

Coxsackieviruses

In clinical research, the natural human coxsackievirus A21 (CVA21), marketed under the brand name Cavatak®, is the primary focus. This virus specifically targets a subset of cancer cells that express elevated levels of the surface markers CD54 and CD55. Cavatak® is currently being investigated for its effectiveness in treating a wide variety of cancers [122]. In 2015, a stage II therapeutic testing was conducted involving infected individuals diagnosed with period IIIc and IV malignant melanocytic neoplasms. The patient survival rate was 38.6%, with no further progress observed after six months. Eight patients had complete remission (CR), and the objective response rate (ORR) was 28%. The duration of response rate (DRR) was 21%, lasting at least six months. These outcomes were promising. Furthermore, evidence of activity was observed in distant lesions that were not initially injected [123]. Other Coxsackieviruses have the potential to act as anticancer agents, although only CVA21 has advanced to clinical trials. A distinct strain of Coxsackievirus, known as CVB3, has demonstrated targeted oncolytic effects in preclinical research versus non-small cell lung cancer [123]. As noted in this review, Coxsackievirus A13 (CVA13), Coxsackievirus A15 (CVA15), and Coxsackievirus A18 (CVA18) are three other Group A Coxsackieviruses which are also strong oncolytic virus against malignant melanoma. It is possible that the said viruses were capable of replicating efficiently in the cancer cells which expressed ICAM-1 to a high level of viral titers. Moreover, CVA13, CVA15, and CVA18 intratumoral injection of mice with subcutaneous SK-Mel-28 tumor xenografts caused a substantial decrease in the tumor volume. To determine the effect of preexisting immunity, serum samples of patients who have melanoma in stage IV and healthy people were examined to determine the presence of protective antibodies against these oncolytic viruses. Neutralization tests showed that, 3 of 21 subjects contained low concentrations of antibodies against CVA21 and no antibodies against CVA13, CVA15 or CVA18. Moreover, the serum of people who were positive on CVA21 did not cross-neutralize CVA13, CVA15, and CVA18. According to these results, CVA13, CVA15, and CVA18 seem to be good candidates in the application in a multivalent oncolytic therapy scheme in the treatment of malignant melanoma [124].

Seneca Valley virus

Due to its use of an RNA intermediate in its replication strategy, the oncolytic RNA virus SVV-001—also known as NTX-010 and belonging to the Picornaviridae family—is not able to combine into the recipient genome. When administered intravenously, this virus can infiltrate solid tumors exhibiting neuroendocrine features. Only a few antibodies have demonstrated resistance to SVV-001, and its relatively short replication cycle (approximately 12 h) suggests it may successfully infect tumors before the immune response is activated [123, 125]. Intravenous introdution of SVV-001 was well tolerated among individuals with advanced solid tumors participating in a preliminary clinical investigation exhibiting neuroendocrine characteristics. Moreover, those suffering from small-cell carcinoma of the lung showed evidence of anticancer activity, viral replication within tumors, and predicted viral clearance kinetics [125]. Given its safety profile in adults, evaluation of SVV-001 in pediatric patients with treatment-resistant or recurrent solid tumors has become feasible. SVV-001 appears to be a viable and well-tolerated option, whether administered alone or in combination with cyclophosphamide, based on the dosage levels tested in pediatric patients. However, the addition of cyclophosphamide did not improve outcomes; neutralizing antibodies persisted, reducing treatment efficacy [126].

Echovirus

The oncolytic, unmodified ECHO-7 virus, which is a member of the Picornaviridae family, is widely referred to by the name Rigvir®. Despite having been licensed to treat melanoma in Latvia in 2004, not much is known about this virus. Despite a few published case reports, no comprehensive clinical research has been conducted on the subjectA past study of 79 people diagnosed with initial melanoma (stages IB, IIA, IIB, and IIC) found that 66 patients who got Rigvir® after surgery had higher survival rates than those who just had surgical resection [127]. Based on these findings, Rigvir® may influence cancer recurrence [128]. However, more research is required to elucidate Rigvir®’s mechanism of action and therapeutic efficacy against malignancy, even though the results showed that it considerably increased survival rates in patients with initial stages malignancy without causing adverse reactions [127].

Retroviruses

Autonomous retroviruses selectively target developing cells and cells with defects in the interferon pathway, making them a promising option for anticancer therapy. Once these viruses are exposed to prodrugs like 5-fluorocytosine (5-FC), they may be modified to target cells, despite their inability to metabolize host cells. Another advantage of many prodrug systems is the potential for viral integration into the host genome, which may lead to viral persistence within residual cancer cells, potentially extending therapeutic effects. The replication-competent retroviral vector Toca-511, It allows cells infected with the murine leukemia virus to transform the medicament 5-fluorocytosine (5-FC) into the active anticancer agent 5-fluorouracil (5-FU). It produces the enzyme fungus cytosine deaminase (yCD) [129]. Toca-511 showed better overall survival rates and tolerability than a placebo in a first-phase clinical investigation that included 45 patients with an ongoing or evolving high-grade brain tumors [130]. Toca-511 is entering Phase II/III clinical studies after being designated an orphan medicine by the Food and medication Administration (FDA) in 2015 for the therapy of glioblastoma [127].

Rhabdoviruses

The Rhabdoviridae family of viruses, including the vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), may infect insects, pigs, horses, and cattle. One of the most significant advantages of using VSV as a cancer treatment agent is that the vast majority of people already possess antibodies capable of neutralizing the virus. New information from clinicaltrials.gov reveals that trials are currently underway for an oncolytic virus combining VSV-IFNβ with human sodium iodide symporter (NIS). This development is notable despite the limited amount of published clinical research on the subject. It appears that the administration of interferon-beta (IFNβ) reduces viral contamination in normal cells, while concurrently boosting the innate immunological reaction in tumor cells. This dual action results in the recruitment and provoking of immune cells that specifically target tumor cells. The virus’s selectivity depends on decreased interferon signaling, which underpins this mechanism [8, 127]. The use of iodine radioisotopes and the expression of human NIS by VSV-IFNβ-NIS have the potential to enhance oncolysis and enable noninvasive monitoring of viral infection. The Maraba virus, another species of the Rhabdoviridae family, also shows potential as a cancer therapy. To facilitate the development of MAGEA3, an attenuated strain called MG1 was created, featuring mutations in both the G and M proteins. MAGEA3 is encoded by a non-replicating adenovirus. The MG1 vaccine is now undergoing experiments for testing as part of a heterologous prime-boost approach using Antigen. This approach was adopted after it was determined that the MG1 vaccine alone did not provide sufficient protection [131, 132].

Engineering strategies for tumor selectivity and immune activation

Researchers have engineered OVs to enhance tumor specificity and to arm them with immunostimulatory functions. Key strategies include:

Genetic attenuation for safety and selectivity. Cancer cells Depending on deletion or inactivation of viral genes that are unnecessary in cancer cells (but necessary in normal cells) limits OV replication to tumors. Deletions in HSV ICP34.5 or E1B-55 K, and adenovirus E1A/E1B. These mutations enable the virus to be non-pathogenic in normal tissues and multifly in tumor cells that have defective anti-viral mechanisms. Similarly, replication can be further restricted to cancer cells by addition of tumor-specific promoters or microRNA target sequences into the genome of the virus [133].

Receptor retargeting and tropism modification

Tumor associated receptors can be expressed on the viral surface. As an example, adenovirus fiber proteins have been engineered (by addition of RGD motifs or affibody ligands) to bind receptors expressed in excess on cancer cells (e.g. EGFR, HER2). On the same basis, HSV glycoproteins (gD, gH) may be reconfigured to express tumor antigen receptors (such HER2 or EGFRvIII) exclusively, producing the fully retargeted oHSVs with high-selectivity therapeutic indexes. These strategies focus the viral uptake to tumors with minimum off-target infection [134].

Arming with immunomodulatory transgenes

The contemporary OVs usually encode genes that enhance immunity against tumors. As an illustration, numerous of the OVs can be engineered to secrete cytokines and chemokines (GM-CSF, IL-2, IL-12, IFN-b, CXCL11, etc.), costimulatory ligands, or bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTEs). A typical one is T-VEC (HSV-1 with ICP34.5/ICP47 deletions and two copies of GM-CSF) that boosts maturation of DC and priming of T-cells. Emerging OVs capture BiTEs which fuse tumor-antigens with the CD3 cell surface on T cells to maintain local T-cell stimulation in the face of BiTE limiting half-life. To conclude, armed OVs are localized TME-targeted Trojan horses that directly introduce immune stimulants in the TME, which transforms infected tumors into cytokine release and improved antigen presentation locations [135].

Clinical translation and combination therapies

Virotherapy has achieved several clinical milestones. Rigvir (2004) is an ECHO-7 picornavirus first OV approved in melanoma. In 2005 then China gave governmental approval to H101 (Oncorine, an E1B-deleted adenovirus) [136]. In 2015, the FDA/EMA gave approval to T-VEC (HSV-1 with GM-CSF) utility in melanoma, a breakthrough. Also, Japan granted approval to Delytact (HSV-1) as a glioma treatment in 2021, and nadofaragene firadenovec (adenovirus) was approved to treat bladder cancer in the US. Selectively, on January 21, 2025 FDA took a BLA on RP1 (a vaccinia HSV hybrid) combined with nivolumab in melanoma. These approvals underline the advancement of OVs into common practice [136].

OVs gain maximum efficacy when used together with other treatments. OVs sensitize cancers to immunotherapies and standard therapies by converting tumors into inflamed antigen-rich environments. OVs have synergy with immune checkpoint inhibitors, chemotherapies, radiation, and targeted agents as demonstrated preclinically and in humans. Indeed, recruitment and maintenance of tumor-specific T cells can be achieved by adding OVs to checkpoint blockade. Equally, using OVs with chemotherapy and radiation tends to increase cell kill of tumor and can result in less use of toxic drugs [137]. Examples that include a trial involving T-VEC combined with anti-PD-1 (pembrolizumab) as the remedy of malignant melanoma show a greater response rate than monotherapy. Concisely, in-going clinical trials are actively investigating OV combinations (comprising of PD-1/CTLA-4 blockers, CAR-T cells, kinase inhibitors etc.), which is the momentum of the translation process of oncolytic virotherapy [137].

Future perspectives and unresolved questions

Irrespective of improvement, there are still a few challenges. The heterogeneity of tumors and immunosuppressive microenvironment suppress the effectiveness of OV: physical barriers (thick stroma, extracellular matrix) have the potential to prevent the spread of the virus, and immune responses are suppressed by MDSCs, Tregs, and other factors [138]. Response is also hard to assess (e.g. whether it is genuine progression or pseudo-progression on imaging). Even safety and manufacture are problematic: the viral shedding, off-target effects will have to be addressed, and every new OV will have to undergo strict regulatory checks.

In the future, such challenges will be overcome by new technologies and methods. The research of single-cell genomics and biomarkers is likely to unmap the tumor and immune heterogeneity and inform the personalized OV treatment. Custom OVs based on tumor genetics and resistance might be made possible by gene-editing tools (e.g. CRISPR) [138]. Physical and immune barriers Novel combinations or payloads (e.g. expressing hyaluronidase to degrade stroma, or STING agonists to boost innate sensing) are under development to compromise physical and immune barriers. Artificial intelligence and predictive biomarkers added (including already existing antiviral antibodies, levels of interferon-stimulated genes, or the state of the STING pathway) may be used to find the appropriate OV and fit it to each patient. To be more precise, the future work will aim at the optimization of OV design, delivery, and patient selection to achieve the full capability of oncolytic virotherapy. These engineered viruses can be iso-specific and highly effective cancer immunotherapies with the development of synthetic biology and immunology [138].

Conclusion and perspectives

Oncolytic viruses have become an exciting and versatile scalpel in current cancer treatment, as it represents a rare mixture of immediate tumor cell destruction and strong immune stimulation. As has been emphasized in this paper, genetic modification has made it possible to design such oncolytic viruses that can be highly selective and tend to replicate best in malignant cells with the least amount of toxicity to normal tissues. Alterations like deletions of ICP genes and prodcution of immunostimulant transgenes e.g., GM-CSF, have greatly augmented the antitumor immunity by amplifying the occurrence of the immunogenic cell death, secretion of antigens and the recruitment of both the innate and adaptive immune effector cells. Oncolytic virotherapy in agents like T-VEC, G47Δ, JX-594 and DNX-2401 has shown an ability to produce sustained response in patients selective to the therapy, especially against immunogenic tumour, including melanoma and glioblastoma. Even with these developments, oncolytic viruses still have a number of obstacles that restrict their wider clinical potential. Other barriers, like the lack of adequate intratumoral viral proliferation, quick immunity elimination, antiviral neutralizing antibodies, and tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment, are still major EU. Novel delivery methods such as encapsulation of nanoparticles, carriers made of stem cells, and the use of vesicles are new approaches meant to enhance the stability of viruses, their invasion of tumors and systemically delivery of viruses. Further, combination modalities of oncolytic viruses and immune checkpoint inhibitors, chemotherapy, or cytokine-based immunotherapies have been encouraging in the area of clinical exercise since the so-called immunologically cold tumors have been converted into immune respondive tumors. Overall, and, more precisely, the transformative potential of oncolytic virotherapy as a whole and, most notably, its application in tumor therapy is justified by the accumulating preclinical and clinical data. The continued optimization of the viral designs, vehicle systems and patient selection using predictive biomarkers and clinical trials, which are designed to maximize treatment efficacy are bound to be very instrumental in ensuring therapeutic efficacies maximize. The continuous technological and immunological advances will make the individual and combinatoric cancer immunotherapy methods comprise oncolytic viruses in the next few years.
