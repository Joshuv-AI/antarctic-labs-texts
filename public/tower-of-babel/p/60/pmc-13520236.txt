Dyspnea and cough trajectories in older adults (aged 60–80 years) after lung cancer surgery: a 6-month longitudinal study
Authors: Rongjia LIN, Genmiao Yu, Xiaojie Pan, Wenshu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo characterize postoperative trajectories of dyspnea and cough and identify clinical factors associated with delayed recovery in older adults undergoing lung cancer surgery.MethodsThis single-center prospective longitudinal study enrolled older adults aged 60–80 years with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) undergoing video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS). Dyspnea and cough were assessed using the modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale (mMRC-DS) and the Leicester Cough Questionnaire Mandarin Chinese version (LCQ-MC) preoperatively and at multiple time points up to 6 months after discharge.ResultsAmong 292 patients (median age 68; 40.4% men), dyspnea recovered rapidly, with 95.2% reporting no clinically significant symptoms (mMRC-DS grade 0–1) by 6 months. In contrast, cough recovery was significantly more protracted. The LCQ-MC total score decreased from 20.29 preoperatively to 15.69 at discharge and, among patients who remained under follow-up, partially recovered to 17.39 at 6 months, remaining below preoperative levels (all P < 0.001). The median time to the composite recovery endpoint was 75 days. After multivariable adjustment, extent of resection remained associated with postoperative recovery. Patients undergoing VATS lobectomy demonstrated significantly slower recovery than those undergoing VATS sublobectomy (adjusted HR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.34–0.66; p < 0.001).ConclusionCough demonstrated a more prolonged postoperative recovery trajectory than dyspnea in older adults following lung cancer surgery. Recovery to preoperative symptom levels required a substantial postoperative period, and extent of resection remained associated with delayed recovery after multivariable adjustment. These findings highlight the importance of longitudinal postoperative symptom assessment in older adults undergoing lung cancer surgery.Trial registrationThis study was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200064378) on October 5, 2022. Introduction

Dyspnea and cough are prevalent and prognostically significant symptoms following lung cancer surgery, particularly in older adults [1, 2]. The number of older adults (aged 60–80 years) undergoing lung cancer surgery is rapidly increasing. However, the mechanism and characteristics of dyspnea and cough after lung surgery remain unclear [3, 4]. This may be associated with low muscle mass, physiological lung aging and low-grade chronic inflammation in older adults [5–7]. Recent studies have demonstrated that postoperative respiratory symptoms may persist for several months after lung cancer surgery and substantially affect functional recovery and health-related quality of life (HRQOL) [8–10].

However, recovery patterns and the co-evolving trajectories of dyspnea and cough in older adults after lung cancer surgery are not well delineated. Although previous longitudinal studies have evaluated postoperative symptom burden and quality-of-life recovery after lung cancer surgery, data specifically focusing on older adults remain limited [9, 11, 12]. To accurately assess this symptom burden, we measured dyspnea using the modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale (mMRC-DS), a guideline-recommended tool that evaluates functional limitations and is a core clinical trial endpoint due to its simplicity and feasibility [13]. Cough was assessed using the Leicester Cough Questionnaire Mandarin Chinese version (LCQ-MC), a 19-item instrument evaluating cough-specific HRQOL across physical, psychological, and social domains [14–16].

Therefore, we conducted a 6-month longitudinal study of older adults (aged 60–80) with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) following video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). The aims of this study were to characterize the postoperative trajectories of dyspnea and cough, evaluate time to recovery to preoperative symptom levels, and identify clinical factors associated with delayed recovery.

Methods

Study population

This study was performed at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Fujian Provincial Hospital (October 2022 to March 2024). Inclusion criteria: (1) aged 60 to 80 years, (2) underwent VATS, and (3) pathologically confirmed stage I-IIIa NSCLC. Exclusion criteria: (1) underwent bilateral pulmonary surgery or pneumonectomy, (2) planned postoperative intensive care unit admission, (3) receiving chemotherapy, immunotherapy or targeted therapy before surgery, or (4) declined participation/withdrawal.

Staging followed the eighth edition of the Union for International Cancer Control/American Joint Committee on Cancer lung cancer classification guidelines [17]. Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) were defined by European Perioperative Clinical Outcome definitions [18].

All patients underwent two-port VATS lobectomy or segmentectomy according to routine institutional surgical standards and perioperative protocols [19, 20].

Assessment measurements

The validated Chinese version of the mMRC-DS was used to assess dyspnea severity. This instrument grades functional limitation due to breathlessness from 0 (I only get breathless with strenuous exercise) to 4 (I am too breathless to leave the house or I am breathless when dressing/undressing), with patients selecting the single statement that best corresponds to their condition (Appendix 1) [21].

The impact of cough on HRQOL was evaluated with the LCQ-MC. The 19-item questionnaire is divided into physical (8 items), psychological (7 items), and social (4 items) domains. Each item is scored on a 7-point Likert scale, and a total score (ranging from 3 to 21) is calculated, where higher scores indicate better health status (Appendix 2) [14, 15].

Definition of recovery endpoint

Recovery was evaluated at the individual-patient level during postoperative follow-up. Dyspnea recovery was defined as return of the postoperative mMRC-DS score to the patient’s own preoperative level, and cough recovery was defined as return of the postoperative LCQ-MC total score to the patient’s own preoperative level. The composite recovery endpoint was defined as concurrent recovery of both dyspnea and cough.

Recovery time was defined as the first postoperative assessment time point at which the predefined recovery criteria were met. Because symptom assessments were performed at prespecified follow-up intervals, transient symptom fluctuations occurring between assessment time points could not be evaluated. Patients who achieved the composite recovery endpoint were no longer followed for subsequent symptom assessments.

Data collection and management

Data on the mMRC-DS and the LCQ-MC were prospectively collected and managed using a secure online database (jinshuju, a data collection and management platform developed by MININGLAMP TECHNOLOGY, Beijing, China, https://jinshuju.net/).

During hospitalization, trained research nurses conducted face-to-face interviews and recorded questionnaire responses directly into standardized electronic forms. Preoperative mMRC-DS and LCQ-MC assessments were generally performed during the preoperative admission period, typically within 1–3 days before surgery, whereas discharge assessments were performed on the day of discharge. Follow-up assessments were conducted remotely via telephone or WeChat at predefined time points (7 days and 1, 2, 3, and 6 months after discharge) using identical electronic questionnaires. On the jinshuju platform, data were processed in a de-identified manner, using hospital admission IDs as the only linkage, ensuring patient confidentiality.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean (SD), median (IQR), or number (%). Longitudinal changes in LCQ-MC scores were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, whereas longitudinal changes in dyspnea severity were descriptively summarized using proportions of mMRC-DS categories at each postoperative time point. The mixed-effects models included subject-specific random intercepts (estimated via REML) to account for individual heterogeneity. Because postoperative symptom follow-up was prospectively discontinued once patients achieved the predefined composite recovery endpoint, longitudinal analyses reflected conditional symptom trajectories among patients who remained under follow-up at each assessment time point, rather than unconditional population-average trajectories for the entire cohort. Adjusted least-squares means with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated, and Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons were conducted between all postoperative time points and the preoperative levels.

Time to recovery was visualized with Kaplan-Meier curves and compared using the log-rank test. Categorical variables were compared using χ², Yates’ correction, or Fisher’s exact tests, and continuous variables using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests.

Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to evaluate factors associated with postoperative recovery. Clinically relevant covariates, including demographic, pulmonary function, perioperative, and treatment-related variables, were simultaneously entered into the multivariable model regardless of univariate statistical significance. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox regression models was assessed using Schoenfeld residual-based tests.

A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using Stata/MP 18.0 (StataCorpLP).

Results

Characteristics of patients

Of the 304 patients who consented to participate, 292 met the inclusion criteria. Among these, 287 completed the 6-month evaluation or reached the recovery endpoint, while 5 withdrew during follow-up (1 at 7 days, 2 at 1 month, 1 at 2 months, and 1 at 3 months). Four patients were hospitalized for serious complications (1 for massive pleural effusion at 1 month, 1 for bronchopleural fistula and 1 for stroke at 2 months and 1 for haemoptysis at 6 months), one patient underwent repeated bronchoscopic treatment for bronchial stenosis within 2 months after discharge (Fig. 1). The clinical characteristics of the 292 patients with NSCLC are summarized in Table 1. The median age of patients was 68 years (IQR 64–70 years), and 174 were female (59.6%).

Longitudinal profiles and time to recovery of dyspnea and cough

At three and six months after discharge, the vast majority of patients (94.9% and 95.2%, respectively) reported no clinically significant dyspnea (mMRC-DS grade 0–1). Compared to the postoperative evaluation at discharge (78.1%), a progressive improvement in dyspnea severity was observed over time (Fig. 2A).

(A)Distribution of dyspnea severity assessed by mMRC-DS at each time point. Dyspnea was categorized as no or mild symptoms (mMRC-DS grade 0–1) and clinically significant dyspnea (mMRC-DS grade ≥ 2). B Longitudinal changes in cough-related quality of life assessed using the LCQ-MC, including physical, psychological, and social domains, as well as the total score. Longitudinal LCQ-MC trajectories represent observed scores among patients who remained under follow-up at each postoperative time point. Follow-up assessments were discontinued after achievement of the predefined composite recovery endpoint. mMRC-DS, modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale; LCQ-MC, Leicester Cough Questionnaire Mandarin Chinese version

The linear mixed-effects model, which accounted for variability among individual patients, revealed a significant effect of time on LCQ-MC scores (all p < 0.001). Marginal predictions of the adjusted least-squares means for the three domains and total score demonstrated gradual improvement over time. As shown in Fig. 2B and Table S1, the mean domain and total scores of LCQ-MC decreased rapidly after surgery, reached their nadir at discharge, and subsequently demonstrated gradual improvement among patients remaining under follow-up. These trajectories reflect LCQ-MC scores among patients who had not yet met the composite recovery endpoint; follow-up assessments were discontinued once recovery was achieved.

The mean scores of the three LCQ-MC domains and the total score exhibited a characteristic trajectory following surgery. The scores ranged from 6.72 to 6.80 across domains (total: 20.29) preoperatively, declined to 4.92–5.49 (total: 15.69) at discharge, and then recovered to 5.47–5.70 (total: 16.68) at 3 months and 5.69–5.94 (total: 17.39) at 6 months after discharge (all p < 0.001 for post hoc comparisons at both follow-up time points versus preoperative levels) (Table S1).

Figure 3A presents the Kaplan-Meier curves for recovery to preoperative symptom levels for the composite endpoint of dyspnea and cough during the 6-month follow-up period. The overall median time to the composite recovery endpoint (both dyspnea and cough) was 75 days (95% CI, 61 to 89) after discharge (Table S2).

Clinically relevant baseline covariates, including age, sex, body mass index, pulmonary function [%FEV1 (forced expiratory volume in one second)], smoking status, COPD/asthma, duration of anesthesia, extent of resection, PPCs, TNM stage, and adjuvant therapy, were simultaneously entered into the multivariable Cox regression model regardless of univariate statistical significance. After multivariable adjustment, extent of resection remained associated with postoperative recovery. Specifically, patients undergoing VATS lobectomy demonstrated significantly slower postoperative recovery than those undergoing VATS sublobectomy (adjusted HR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.34–0.66, p < 0.001). In contrast, PPCs, duration of anesthesia, TNM stage, and adjuvant therapy were not significantly associated with delayed recovery after multivariable adjustment (Table 2). Consistent with these findings, Kaplan-Meier analyses confirmed significantly divergent recovery trajectories. The median recovery time for the composite endpoint was markedly longer after lobectomy than after sublobectomy (137 vs. 46 days, Log-rank p < 0.001; Fig. 3B). Similar patterns were observed for dyspnea and cough when analyzed separately. No significant violation of the proportional hazards assumption was observed based on the global Schoenfeld residual test (p = 0.302).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this longitudinal study provides the first prospective characterization of the co-evolving trajectories of dyspnea and cough in older adults (aged 60–80 years) during the 6 months following lung cancer surgery. Furthermore, it identifies clinical factors associated with delayed recovery.

Among the symptom burden assessed, this study focused on the composite recovery of dyspnea and cough. The observed postoperative trajectories revealed a characteristic “V-shaped” pattern, marked by a sharp deterioration after surgery that reached its nadir at discharge, followed by gradual improvement among patients remaining under follow-up during the 6-month follow-up period. The overall median time to the composite recovery endpoint was 75 days. However, as shown in Fig. 2, the trajectories of dyspnea and cough diverged notably during this period. Dyspnea resolved more rapidly, with approximately 95% of patients reporting minimal symptoms (mMRC-DS grade 0–1) by three months. In contrast, cough exhibited a more protracted course, with LCQ-MC scores at 6 months remaining significantly below preoperative levels. This seeming discordance between the 75-day median composite recovery time and the persistently lower group-level LCQ-MC scores at 6 months reflects the distinction between individual-level time to return to baseline and group-level symptom trajectories. While many patients attained their personal preoperative symptom levels during follow-up, those who remained under observation at later time points represented a subset with slower recovery, thereby driving down the group means.

Additionally, both univariate and multivariable Cox regression analyses showed that extent of resection remained associated with postoperative recovery (Table 2). Patients undergoing lobectomy demonstrated significantly longer recovery times than those undergoing sublobectomy. Several mechanisms may potentially explain this association. Previous studies have shown that parenchymal-sparing sublobectomy is associated with superior postoperative functional recovery and quality of life, particularly in older adults [22–24]. Compared with sublobectomy, lobectomy involves greater parenchymal loss and surgical trauma, which may contribute to prolonged impairment in pulmonary reserve and postoperative respiratory symptoms [25–30]. In addition, previous studies have suggested that more extensive lung resection may be associated with worse postoperative cough-related quality of life and slower symptom resolution [31–33].

Recent randomized and longitudinal studies, including the JCOG0802/WJOG4607L trial, have increasingly supported sublobectomy as an important surgical option for selected patients with early-stage NSCLC [24]. Beyond its oncological feasibility, parenchymal-sparing resection may provide advantages in postoperative pulmonary function recovery and quality of life, particularly in older adults [34–36]. Our findings are generally consistent with these previous observations and further highlight the potential association between extent of resection and postoperative symptom recovery.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although conventional pulmonary function parameters were available for all participants, diffusion-capacity assessment (DLCO) was not routinely performed in all patients. Therefore, DLCO data were incomplete and could not be reliably incorporated into the present analyses without introducing potential selection bias. Because diffusion capacity may provide additional information regarding pulmonary reserve and postoperative functional recovery, future studies with standardized and comprehensive pulmonary function assessment are warranted [37].

Second, the LCQ-MC assesses cough-related impact over the preceding two weeks. Therefore, early postoperative assessments at discharge and 7 days after discharge may have included overlapping perioperative symptom experiences, and these early postoperative findings should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, preoperative LCQ-MC scores were close to the upper limit of the scale in many participants, which may have introduced a potential ceiling effect and limited the ability to detect further improvement in cough-related quality of life. Third, because postoperative symptom follow-up was discontinued after achievement of the predefined recovery endpoint, later postoperative assessments increasingly reflected patients with slower recovery. Therefore, longitudinal symptom trajectories should be interpreted as conditional trajectories among patients remaining under follow-up rather than full-cohort population-average trajectories. Fourth, although the LCQ-MC and mMRC-DS are validated instruments, questionnaire-based assessments in older adults may still be affected by symptom underreporting, caregiver-assisted completion, and regional dialect-related comprehension differences, potentially introducing measurement bias [38, 39]. Finally, this was a single-center observational study. Residual confounding and selection bias cannot be fully excluded despite multivariable adjustment.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrated distinct postoperative recovery trajectories of dyspnea and cough in older adults following lung cancer surgery, with cough showing a more prolonged recovery pattern. Recovery to preoperative symptom levels required a substantial postoperative period, and extent of resection remained associated with delayed recovery after multivariable adjustment. These findings highlight the importance of longitudinal symptom monitoring and postoperative recovery assessment in older adults undergoing lung cancer surgery.
