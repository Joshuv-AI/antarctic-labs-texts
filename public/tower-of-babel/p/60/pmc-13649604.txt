Single-cell RNA sequencing of hematopoietic and non-hematopoietic cells defines a distinct signature in atopic and NRG1 mouse lung
Authors: Yingjie Li, Ahmed Ghobashi, Syed-Rehan A. Hussain, Michelle Rohlfing, Jennifer Santoro, Matthew S. Bochter, Shin-ichi Abe, Qin Ma, Mitchell H. Grayson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Respiratory viral infections cause significant mortality. Pre-existing atopy has demonstrated protection from virus-induced death. Atopic mice with house dust mite (HDM) extract survive normally lethal parainfluenza (Sendai virus, SeV) and influenza A (IAV) infection. Neuregulin-1 (NRG1), markedly elevated in airways of atopic mice, has been shown to significantly attenuate mortality to SeV and IAV in non-atopic mice. Hypothesizing that atopy and NRG1 mediate protection from death through similar mechanisms, we anazlyed single-cell RNA sequencing data from CD45+ (hematopoietic) and CD45- (structural) lung cells prior to infection. Both treatments reduced alveolar macrophages, lymphatic endothelial cells, airway smooth muscle cells, and pericytes, while increasing alveolar type 2 cells. However, atopy led to increased fibroblasts with reduced club cells compared to NRG1 treatment. Further, in the hematopoietic cell compartment, atopy drove an increase in more inflammatory cells than NRG1 and associated with significant upregulation of immune cell signaling pathways, something not seen with NRG1 administration. Moreover, NRG1 knockdown did not reverse the protection from virus-induced death in mice with pre-existing atopy. Taken together, these data strongly suggest that atopy and NRG1 mediate increased survival from a respiratory viral infection through disparate mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-61413-w.

Introduction

Respiratory RNA viruses like influenza A virus (IAV), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and parainfluenza virus (PIV) cause significant morbidity and mortality1,2. In the 2009 pandemic caused by IAV H1N1, it was observed that patients with pre-existing asthma had significantly lower mortality due to H1N1 infection3. Further, we and others have reported that atopic mice are protected from lethal parainfluenza virus (Sendai virus, SeV) and IAV infections4–7. Moreover, we found neuregulin-1 (NRG1), an epidermal growth factor superfamily glycoprotein, was overexpressed in atopic mouse lung and provided a survival advantage against both lethal SeV and IAV infections when given exogenously as a prophylactic4. As we and others have shown, NRG1 plays a role in maintaining the homeostasis of airway epithelium4,8–10. Our data demonstrated that inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 were reduced in NRG1-treated airway epithelium, along with a reduction in phosphorylated MLKL (a downstream target in the necroptosis pathway). Although we could not rule out other mechanisms of survival, these data demonstrated that NRG1 reduces necroptosis which likely contributed to the increased survival4,11,12. To our knowledge, this was the first report showing a protective role of NRG1 in respiratory viral infection. Although we identified NRG1 in atopic mice, we do not know if the mechanism of protection in atopic mice is through NRG1. Indeed, NRG1 prophylaxis was associated with an approximately 75% survival rate, while atopic mice exhibited 100% survival from normally lethal IAV or SeV infections4. Hypothesizing that the effect of NRG1 and atopy would be similar on lung cell populations, we undertook the current study comparing NRG1 administration versus development of atopy on lung cell populations using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). While previous studies have employed a clinically relevant HDM mouse model to probe functional and cellular diversity, they have used either a much more chronic model of atopy or looked only at T-helper cells13,14. Since the administration of HDM (to make mice atopic) or NRG1 occurs before viral infection, we have performed scRNA-seq of both hematopoietic (CD45+) and structural (CD45-) cells from lungs of HDM and NRG1 treated groups prior to viral infection. Our data demonstrate that although both HDM and NRG1 groups had cell types similarly reduced or increased relative to the control (phosphate-buffered saline-treated) group, there were several differentially modulated lung cellular populations and signaling pathways in HDM versus NRG1, strongly suggesting that atopy and NRG1 use distinct mechanisms in their protection from severe respiratory viral infection.

Results

Single-cell survey of mouse lungs

To study the impact of the atopic state on lung cellular composition, we challenged mice intranasally with house dust mite antigen (HDM) to induce an atopic response, while phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) served as a non-atopic control. Additionally, to assess the effect of Neuregulin-1 (NRG1), a separate group of mice received intranasal NRG1 treatment. Following lung harvest at day 14 post-sensitization with HDM or on the 5th day of NRG1 treatment, cells were sorted based on the immune marker CD45, allowing the separation of structural (CD45-) and hematopoietic (CD45+) cells, which were then subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing (Fig. 1A). The HDM, NRG1, and control groups were visualized on UMAP for CD45- and CD45 + cells (Fig. 1B). We manually annotated cell clusters based on known marker genes (Tables S2-S3) and differentially expressed genes of clusters identified by the FindMarkers function in the Seurat package. Some clusters were further subclustered, curated or merged to refine the identification of distinct cell types. As a result, we delineated discrete cell populations for both CD45- and CD45+ compartments.

For CD45- cells, there were 14 cell types (Fig. 1C, Fig. S2A) belonging to three main populations, including endothelial, mesenchymal, and epithelial. The endothelial population consisted of general capillary cells (gCAP; Ptprb, Aplnr, and Gpihbp1), alveolar capillary cells (aCAP; Ednrb, Apln, Chst1, Car4, Hpgd, Acap2), lymphatic endothelial cells (LEC; Prox1, Mmrn1, Ccl21a, Nrp2), and others (e.g., arterial endothelial, venous endothelial cells). Two alveolar fibroblast populations were also identified: alveolar fibroblast 1 (AF1; Tcf21, Wnt2, Mfap4) and alveolar fibroblast 2 (AF2; Cdon, Mfap5, Scara5, Col14a1). Other mesenchymal populations also were identified, including pericytes, vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMC), airway smooth muscle cells (ASMC), and mesothelial cells. Various epithelial populations were identified, including alveolar type 1 cells (AT1; Ager, Rtkn2, Sema3b, Pdpn, Hopx), alveolar type 2 cells (AT2; Lamp3, Abca3, Kcnj15, Sftpc, Stfpa1), and Club cells (Scgb3a2, Scgb1a1, Cyp2f2, Lypd2, Bpifb1). We observed a small subset of megakaryocytes (Itga2b, Gp1bb, and Itgb3) in our CD45- sample, which may be attributable to technical issues during cell sorting or other procedural artifacts leading to sample contamination.

For CD45+ cells, we initially identified the main immune populations including dendritic cells (DC), macrophages, monocytes (MON), B cells, T cells, innate lymphoid cells, neutrophils, basophils, and mast cells. We identified subtypes of dendritic cells including mature DC (Ccr7, Lad1), plasmacytoid DC (Siglech, Tcf4, Ccr9), DC1 (Clec9a, Flt3, Zbtb46) and DC2 (Clec10a, Fcgr2b) (Fig. 1D, Fig. S2B). Identified macrophages included alveolar macrophages (Fcgr1, Adgre1, Siglec1, Abcg1, Fabp4, Pparg, Marco, Siglecf) and M2-like macrophages (Mrc1, Tgfb1) (Fig. 1D, Fig. S2B). Nonclassical MON (Cd52, Fcgr3, Ly6c-) and S100a8 and S100a9 positive MON (S1100a8/9 + MON; S100a8, S100a9) were identified (Fig. 1D, Fig. S2B). Additionally, multiple T cell subsets were identified, including subsets in CD4 T (Cd3e, Cd4, Cd40lg) and CD8 T (Cd3e, Cd8a, Cd8b1) cells. In CD4 T cells, naïve CD4 T (Ccr7, Sell, Lef1, Tcf7), CD4 tissue-resident memory T (CD4 TRM; Cxcr6, Itga1, Cd69, Itgae), and CD4 regulatory T (Treg; Il2ra, Foxp3, Izumo1r, Ctla4) cells were identified. CD8 T cells were mainly naïve CD8 T (Ccr7, Sell, Tcf7, Il7r) cells. Other identified immune cells included innate lymphoid cells (ILC; Il1rl1, Klrg1, Gata3), nature killer cells (NK; Nkg7, Klrd1, Klrc1), neutrophils (S100a8, S100a9, Mmp9, Il1b, Csf3r), and mast cells (Kit).

These findings confirm that our scRNA-seq dataset successfully captured diverse immune and non-immune cell populations, providing a detailed transcriptional profile of the lung microenvironment in response to HDM and NRG1 treatments. Starting with tissue harvest through making cell suspensions we have tried to limit sample to sample variation; however, we acknowledge that there are limitations related to isolation and dissociation of lung tissue, as well as the use of FACS sorting to identify CD45+ and CD45- populations, and that these limitations – primarily differential recovery of fragile or adherent populations and cell size-dependent capture efficiency during droplet encapsulation – all could have biased our scRNAseq data.

HDM and NRG1 alter lung microenvironment cellular composition

To investigate the effect of HDM exposure and NRG1 treatment on lung cellular composition, we performed density analysis, which revealed shifts in both CD45- and CD45+ cell populations across treatment conditions (Fig. 2A, Figs. S3A-S3B). While the effect of HDM and NRG1 on CD45- cells was relatively modest, we observed an increase in alveolar fibroblast populations in response to HDM compared to both NRG1 and control conditions (Fig. 2A, top). In contrast, CD45+ cells exhibited more pronounced changes (Fig. 2A, bottom). Specifically, HDM exposure led to an increased density of CD4 TRM, Treg, and dendritic cells relative to NRG1 and control, whereas NRG1 treatment resulted in a higher density of NK cells and neutrophils compared to both HDM and control (Fig. 2A).

To further validate our findings, we conducted pairwise statistical tests on the proportions of each cell type across the three treatment conditions. In the non-immune compartment, HDM-exposed mice exhibited an increase proportion in AF2, ciliated, and AT2 cells, along with a reduction in LEC, ASMC, and pericytes compared to control (Fig. 2B left). Similar reductions in LEC, ASMC, and pericytes were observed in the NRG1-treated mice relative to the control, while Club and AT2 cells appeared relatively increased in the NRG1 group compared to the control (Fig. 2B middle). In the comparison between the two treatment groups, the NRG1 group showed higher relative representation of Club cells and lower representation of AF2 cells compared with the HDM group (Fig. 2B right).

In the immune compartment, we observed a substantial shift in cellular composition following HDM exposure, with a significant increase in ILCs, M2-like macrophages, dendritic cells, and Treg cells, alongside reductions in naïve CD8 T cells and alveolar macrophages compared to control (Fig. 2C left), consistent with the density analysis (Fig. 2A bottom). In contrast, NRG1 treatment resulted in a more modest compositional shift relative to control, with only S100a8/9⁺ monocytes and neutrophils showing significant increases (Fig. 2C middle). When comparing NRG1 directly to HDM, ILC, M2-like macrophages, Treg, dendritic cells, and CD4 TRM were significantly reduced, while S100a8/9⁺ monocytes, neutrophils, and alveolar macrophages were elevated in NRG1-treated mice (Fig. 2C right).

Transcriptional and functional reprogramming of immune cells in response to HDM and NRG1 treatments

To gain deeper insight into the transcriptional regulation underlying shifts in CD45+ cells, we examined transcription factor (TF) activity across different conditions. As expected, TF activities were differentially altered across immune lineages. Notably, we observed significant increases in the activity of Rfxap, Rfx5, and Rfxank in dendritic cells, while a distinct set of TFs exhibited elevated activity in monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils compared to other immune cell types (Fig. S4A).

TF activity also varied markedly between different treatment conditions. Hoxa13 activity was specifically elevated in HDM-treated mice compared to both NRG1-treated and control mice (Fig. 3A), consistent with its increased activity in mature DC and DC1 (Fig. S4A) and the expanded DC population in HDM-treated mice (Fig. 2C left and right). In contrast, Cebpe and Znf335 exhibited increased activity in NRG1-treated samples relative to both HDM and control mice (Fig. 3A). Given their established roles in neutrophil maturation15, we observed that their activity was particularly enriched in neutrophils (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that alterations in cellular composition in response to HDM and NRG1 treatments are accompanied by corresponding shifts in lineage-specific TF activity.

To further explore the functional state of neutrophils in NRG1-treated mice, we performed DEG analysis comparing NRG1-treated mice to HDM-treated or control mice. Neutrophils from NRG1-treated mice exhibited significantly elevated expression of inflammatory genes, including S100a9, S100a8, and Ifitm2 (Fig. 3C). Significantly upregulated genes revealed strong enrichment in Reactome pathways including neutrophil degranulation and immune system activation and autophagy (Fig. 3D), indicating that the increased neutrophil abundance in NRG1-treated mice is associated with their activation.

Pathway enrichment analysis using the Hallmark gene sets was performed on CD45+ cells from HDM and NRG1 treated mice (Fig. S4). HDM-treated mice exhibited a significant upregulation of signaling pathways associated with the interferon-gamma response, interferon response, and complement activation in most immune cell types, including alveolar macrophages, B cells, and mature DCs compared to control mice (Fig. S4B). In contrast, NRG1-treated mice exhibited downregulation of immune-related signaling pathways, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) response, JAK-STAT3, and TNF-α in most immune cells, such as alveolar macrophages, naïve CD8 T cells, nonclassical monocytes, and Tregs (Fig. S4C).

To further elucidate intercellular communication patterns, we applied CellChat analysis. In HDM-treated mice, interleukin-6 (IL6) and insulin growth factor (IGF) signaling were significantly upregulated compared to both NRG1 and control mice (Fig. S4D left and middle). Conversely, immune cells from NRG1-treated mice exhibited enhanced activation of transforming growth factor (TGFβ), epidermal growth factor (EGF), and lymphotoxin (LT) signaling pathways compared to control mice (Fig. S4D right). Notably, γδ T cells emerged as key contributors to LT pathway activation in NRG1-treated mice (Fig. S4E middle). Meanwhile, alveolar and M2-like macrophages were identified as the major source of IL6 signaling in HDM-treated samples (Fig. S4E left). Given that alveolar macrophage abundance was reduced in HDM-treated mice compared to controls (Fig. 2C left), we conducted DEG analysis of alveolar macrophages between HDM and control or NRG1 mice. We observed a significant upregulation of immune response and interleukin signaling, with increased expression of chemokines such as Ccl17 and metalloproteinases including Mmp12 in the HDM-treated mice (Fig. 3E and F).

Differential regulation of TFs and their target gene networks in immune cells following HDM and NRG1 treatments

To investigate whether TF-gene networks differ across treatments, we focused on TFs (Hoxa13, Foxo3, Cebpe, Znf335, and Sox7) that exhibited differential active scores in CD45+ cells among the HDM, NRG1, and control groups. We constructed TF-gene regulatory networks based on the correlation between each TF and its target genes (see Methods; Fig. S5). While the majority of target genes showed similar regulatory patterns across all three groups, distinct differences were observed. For instance, Foxo3 exhibited a negative correlation with Serpine1 in the NRG1 group but a positive correlation in the HDM group. Similarly, Cebpe was negatively correlated with Ywhab in the NRG1 group and positively correlated in the HDM group, suggesting treatment-dependent regulatory shifts. Hoxa13 also displayed a stronger negative correlation with Epha4 in the NRG1 and control groups compared to HDM. Additionally, Bmp4, a known transcriptional target of Hoxa13, was expressed only in the NRG1 and control groups, with no expression detected in HDM-treated CD45+ cells. These findings highlight the dynamic nature of TF–gene interactions and suggest HDM and NRG1 can reshape transcriptional regulatory networks in immune cells.

Transcriptional and functional reprogramming of non-immune cells in response to HDM and NRG1 treatment

To investigate the effects of HDM and NRG1 treatment on non-immune cells, we performed pathway enrichment analysis using the Hallmark gene sets. Consistent with our findings in immune cells, HDM-treated mice exhibited significant upregulation of immune-related pathways, including IFN-γ, inflammatory response, TNF-α signaling in multiple non-immune cells, such as AF1, AT2, aCAP, and VECs, compared to control mice (Fig. 4A top). Similarly, enriched inflammatory response and TNF-α signaling were also observed in HDM-treated mice compared to NRG1-treated mice (Fig. 4A middle). AF1 and AT2 appeared to be the predominant functional non-immune cells in HDM-treated versus control mice.

To further explore the impact of HDM-treated mice on AF1 and AT2 cells, we conducted DEG analysis in these two cell types comparing HDM and control mice. HDM exposure upregulated key immune-related genes, including C3 and Retnla, in AF1 and AT2 cells, respectively (Fig. 4B). The upregulated genes in AF1 and AT2 cells were significantly associated with immune response pathways, including TNF-α signaling and the interferon response (Fig. 4C).

Interestingly, ciliated cells in NRG1-treated mice displayed significant upregulation of TNF-α, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and hypoxia signaling pathways compared to HDM and control mice (Fig. 4A middle and bottom).

We also examined broader pathway activity across non-immune cell types. Notably, TGFβ and hypoxia pathways were highly enriched in fibroblasts, with higher activity observed in HDM-treated and control mice compared to NRG1-treated mice (Fig. S6). Moreover, nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling pathways were highly active in both fibroblasts and endothelial cells across all conditions (Fig. S6). Of particular interest, we observed a notable increase in P53 pathway activity in NRG1-treated mice, with specific enrichment in endothelial cells (Fig. S6). This suggests a potential role for P53 signaling in endothelial responses to NRG1 treatment.

Survival in atopic mice is independent of NRG1

We previously reported that NRG1 is expressed at higher levels in atopic mouse lung and airways, suggesting a potential involvement in the survival mechanism with atopy4. However, our scRNAseq data demonstrate distinct pathways and cellular populations in the HDM (atopic) versus NRG1 groups, suggesting distinct and separate mechanisms preventing death due to a respiratory viral infection. To prove that NRG1 is not necessary for the protection seen in atopic mice, we used Nrg1 null mice (See Methods) that were made atopic with HDM, and examined the effect of a normally lethal viral infection (Fig. 5A, Table S4). However, as shown in Fig. S7, NRG1 could still be measured in the lungs (less so in the airways) of atopic Nrg1 null mice on tamoxifen. As a result we further injected an anti-NRG1mAb in these mice to remove any residual NRG1 (Fig. 5A). Depletion of NRG1 failed to prevent the protection from a lethal SeV infection, with all mice suriving and the weight loss being similar to what we have previously published in (NRG1 sufficient) atopic mice (Fig. 5B and C)4. Thus, even though atopic mice produce NRG1, it is not required for the survival advantage seen in atopic mice.

Discussion

Increasing evidence suggests that scRNA-seq can be used to perform unbiased analyses of cell diversity, cell-cell communication and for uncovering genes that regulate cell functions13,14. In this study we explored cellular and gene expression changes between the HDM atopic model and NRG1 administration before viral infection as both models can prevent mortality due to respiratory viral infection, with the objective of identifying cellular or pathway changes (compared to PBS controls) between the two treatments.

We utilized an acute atopic model where sensitization and challenge with HDM were done within 2 weeks, unlike the chronic model, where HDM was given 5 days/week for five weeks4,16. The acute model of HDM has reproducibly been shown to elicit strong cellular infiltration, serum IgE response, mucus cell metaplasia (MCM) and airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), sharing many similarities with human allergic asthma7,16,17. However, the early cellular infiltration and inflammatory phase are resolved shortly after antigen (HDM) removal16. On the other hand, NRG1 has been reported to be one of the regulators of goblet cell formation in human airway epithelial cells, was noted to be increased in the airways of mice sensitized and challenged to ovalbumin18, and had an anti-inflammatory effect in an HDM-induced atopic dermatitis model19. We have shown that during respiratory viral infection, both HDM (i.e., atopic mice) and NRG1 treatment reduced airway epithelium leak4. Further, NRG1 reduced necroptosis, increased regeneration of human airway epithelium and regulated genes involved in airway epithelium homeostasis following RSV infection4.

Utilizing scRNA-seq on isolated mouse lung CD45+ and CD45- cell populations, we delineated key cell clusters and pathways in both the HDM and NRG1 models (as well as PBS controls) to determine if the mechanisms of survival in both models were similar. In CD45- cells, both HDM and NRG1 treatments reduced lymphatic endothelial cells, airway smooth muscle cells, and pericytes, and increased AT2 cells when compared to PBS controls. HDM treatment specifically increased alveolar AF2 cells, and reduced club cells relative to NRG1 treatment, suggesting distinct activation of the innate immune response to HDM exposure. Indeed, the increase in club cells in the NRG1-treated mouse lung is consistent with NRG1’s function of airway epithelium regeneration and repair20–22. In terms of hematopoietic cells (CD45+), the only shared effect was that alveolar macrophages (AMs) were decreased in both treatment groups. HDM treatment increased a broad range of inflammatory cells, with reduced naïve CD8 T cells, AMs, neutrophils, and ILCs compared to NRG1 treatment.

Our findings suggest that HDM exposure elicits a strong inflammatory response, characterized by an expansion of CD4 TRM, Treg, and dendritic cells, along with concurrent reductions in naïve CD8 T cells and alveolar macrophages. In contrast, NRG1 treatment appears to reshape the immune landscape by increasing neutrophils and monocytes, while suppressing M2-like macrophages and dendritic cells. These observations indicate that NRG1 may promote a distinct immune response that potentially influences the balance between inflammation and tissue remodeling. Thus, the potential mechanism of survival from a respiratory viral infection may be associated with treatments that reduce lymphatic endothelial cells, airway smooth muscle cells, pericytes, and AM, while at the same time increasing AT2 cells. The observed reduction in ASMC in the acute HDM model has not been extensively explored as increases in ASMC are expected with chronic exposure to HDM due to epithelial-mesenchymal transition23. It is possible that the acute HDM model we used leads to only transient changes that resolve shortly after removal of antigen and, thus, do not affect ASMC the way a more chronic allergen exposure model would.

Furthermore, insight into the functional activity of various immune cell types shows that HDM-treated mice exhibited a significant upregulation of signaling pathways, while conversely, NRG1 treatment exhibited more attenuated immune-signaling in hematopoietic cells. Collectively, these findings highlight the dynamic reprogramming of immune cell composition and transcriptional regulation in response to HDM and NRG1 treatment. HDM exposure triggered a pronounced inflammatory response, characterized by expansion of dendritic cells, activation of IL6 signaling, and widespread immune activation. In contrast, NRG1 treatment primarily affects neutrophils, promoting both numerical expansion and inflammatory gene expression, while reducing immune enrichment.

There are two areas in which our findings are inconsistent with most of the literature. In our model we saw a reduction in ASMC rather than an increase. Due to HDM induced epithelial-mesenchymal cell transition, it would be expected that HDM treatment would increase these cells23. We do not know exactly why these cells were not increased in our model, but can speculate that it may have to do with the chronicity of HDM exposure. Our model utilizes an acute HDM exposure (sensitization and 5 challenges), whereas most other models are based upon a more chronic antigenic exposure. Therefore, it is possible that our model leads to only transient changes in some airway cells that resolves shortly after removal of HDM, and, thus, does not affect ASMC numbers.

NRG1 has been reported to increase mucous cell metaplasia in human airway epithelial cell culture18. However, in our model, administration of NRG1 failed to lead to increased mucous cells. Again, we do not know exactly why this occurs, but can speculate on several possible reasons. First, we administered NRG1 in a way that would lead to apical exposure of the airway epithelium rather than basolateral. It is quite possible that apical exposure does not drive mucous cell metaplasia. Further, our exposures were for 5 days (and likely were intermittent as the airway fluid level was cleared), while the culture model utilizes 7 days of continuous exposure (NRG1 is placed in the basolateral media and replaced with each media exchange). We utlitized recombinant combined NRG1α and β, while the in vitro studies have focused on NRG1β. NRG1α does not affect mucus levels in airway epithelium, although it does alter regeneration and repair of the epithelium18. Finally, there has been at least one other study (albeit in the gut) where goblet cell markers were not increased (actually were decreased) by NRG1 treatment24.

In conclusion, we compared the transcriptomic profile of both hematopoietic and non-hematopoietic lung cells isolated from the HDM atopic and NRG1 model using scRNA-seq. We hypothesized that similar cellular changes and pathways would be identified that could then correlate with the increased survival from normally lethal respiratory viral infections seen with these treatments. While we found some modest overlap in the effect on structural cells, the majority of our findings suggested unique effects of the two models. Compared to control mice, HDM exposure induced a robust immune response in non-immune cells, particularly in AF1 and AT2 populations, while NRG1 treatment uniquely enhanced immune-related activity in ciliated cells and promoted endothelial-specific activation of the P53 pathway. These differences led us to believe that increased survival from atopy and NRG1 were likely through different mechanisms. We acknowledge that since we have not compared cellular changes post viral infection we cannot specifically identify which changes in cell types and pathways are directly linked to protection during a viral infection. However, proof that NRG1 is dispensible for the survival seen in atopic mice came from our study where depletion of NRG1 in atopic mice had no impact on survival from a normally lethal SeV infection. Therefore, we conclude that while both HDM-induced atopy and NRG1 treatment before a respiratory viral infection can prevent mortality from the virus, the two treatments induce this protection through distinct and non-overlapping mechanisms.

Methods

Animals

C57BL6/J mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and bred in-house. Mice of both sexes were used for the experiments when they were 8–12 weeks old. No gender differences were noted in any animal experiments.

Atopic model

As we have published, mice were sensitized intranasally (i.n.) with 1 µg HDM extract (catalog no. XPB91D3A2.5; Stallergenes Greer USA, Boston, MA) and one week later challenged i.n. for five days with 10 µg HDM extract. Control mice were given phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) i.n. in place of HDM as above4,16.

NRG1 administration

500 ng of recombinant mouse NRG1 (carrier free, cat.# 9875-NR-050, Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO) was given i.n. daily for 5 days in C57BL6/J mice, as we have published4.

Sample preparation and single-cell RNA sequencing

For HDM or PBS-treated mice, lungs were harvested 72 h after last challenge, while for NRG1 treated mice, lungs were harvested immediately after the last NRG1 administration and a single cell suspension was made as we have published25–27. However, in order to prevent sample to sample variation we used the gentleMACS™ Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotech, Gathersburg, MD) that allows for semi-automated disruption of tissue with a specialized program, thus avoiding some of the random changes in the proportion of various cells types isolated due to physical disruption during harvesting of lung cells.

Cells were then stained with Alexa Fluor® 488 anti-mouse CD45.2 antibody clone 104 (cat# 109816, Biolegend) using standard techniques, and Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) was performed as we previously reported, sorting for both CD45+ and CD45- populations16. We collected 5 × 105 cells for both cell populations from each mouse and samples were prepared for single-cell RNA sequencing using Next GEM Single Cell Fixed RNA sample preparation kit (cat# 1000414) following the manufacturer’s protocol (CG000782 Rev C, 10X Genomics). The fixed cell suspensions were then prepared for sequencing library generation by the Genomics Shared Resource at The Ohio State University Comprehensive Cancer Center using Chromium fixed RNA profiling reagent kits No. 1,000,497 and Dual Index kit TS set A, 96 rxn No. 1,000,251 according to the manufacturer’s protocol (CG000527 Rev D, 10X Genomics) followed by sequencing using NovaSeq6000 platform. To minimize technical variability, all samples were processed concurrently by the same operator using the same platform and reagents, with sample fixation enabling uniform handling and reducing potential run-to-run variation.

Processing and cell clustering of the scRNA-seq datasets

Raw sequencing files were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10, version 2020 A) using Cell Ranger (10X Genomics; v7.1) for demultiplexing and gene-cell count matrix generation. The total number of cells, median gene counts, and total gene counts per sample fell within the expected ranges (Table S1). The resulting gene expression matrices were then imported into R (v4.2.1) using the “read10x” function and analyzed using Seurat (v4.3.0). Genes detected in fewer than three cells were excluded, and cells with fewer than 200 detected transcripts were removed. Additional quality control filters were applied to exclude cells with more than 6,000 detected genes or with mitochondrial transcript content exceeding 5% of total UMIs. To ensure strict separation of immune and non-immune compartments, CD45– samples were further filtered to remove cells expressing Ptprc, while CD45+ samples were restricted to cells expressing Ptprc. After quality control, 7,836 CD45- cells and 35,587 CD45+ cells were retained for downstream analyses. Because CD45- and CD45+ populations were isolated and sequenced separately, all subsequent analyses were performed independently for each population. Gene expression values were log-normalized, and the top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis (PCA). The number of principal components was determined based on inspection of the variance explained. Cells were clustered using the Louvain algorithm based on the top 30 principal components, with a resolution of 0.2 for both CD45+ and CD45- datasets. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was applied for low-dimensional visualization.

To evaluate potential technical batch effects across samples, we first performed qualitative visualization using UMAP and then conducted quantitative assessments using the Local Inverse Simpson’s Index (LISI) implemented in the lisi R package28. For the CD45– populations, the experimental design consisted of three biological conditions (HDM, n = 1; NRG1, n = 2; Control, n = 1). UMAP visualization colored by sample identity showed substantial mixing of cells from different samples within shared clusters, indicating intermixing among all samples (Fig. S1A left), suggesting minimal technical separation by batch. To quantitatively assess replicate mixing within the NRG1 condition, we computed the integration LISI (iLISI). The mean iLISI score was 1.77, approaching the theoretical maximum of 2.0, indicating robust mixing between replicates and minimal technical batch effects (Fig. S1B). For CD45+ population, three biological conditions were profiled (HDM, n = 3; NRG1, n = 3; Control, n = 3). Batch effects were evaluated separately within each condition. The mean iLISI scores for HDM, NRG1, and Control conditions were 2.11, 2.44, and 2.25, respectively, approaching the theoretical maximum of 3.0 expected under complete replicate mixing (Figs.S1C-S1E). Consistent with these quantitative metrics, UMAP visualization showed extensive intermixing of cells from different samples across nearly all clusters, with no evident segregation by sample or sequencing batch (Fig. S1A right). Importantly, all samples within each of the CD45– and CD45+ datasets were processed using the same single-cell sequencing platform and experimental protocol, further reducing the likelihood of systematic technical batch effects. Taken together, the concordant qualitative (UMAP) and quantitative (iLISI) assessments indicate that technical variation across samples is minimal compared to biological signal in both populations.

Based on these findings, downstream analyses were performed without additional batch correction or data integration. This decision was made to avoid potential over-correction, which could obscure biologically meaningful, condition-specific transcriptional heterogeneity relevant to atopic inflammation and NRG1-driven responses.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis

Differential expression analysis among clusters was performed using the FindMarkers function from the Seurat package, employing the Wilcoxon Rank Sum test. Comparisons between treatment groups within individual cell types were also conducted using FindMarkers. Genes with an absolute log₂ fold change greater than 0.25 and a Bonferroni-adjusted p-value of lower than 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Functional enrichment analysis

The enriched pathways of DEGs were analyzed using Enrichr29 and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). Significant DEGs were submitted to Enrichr for enriched Reactome pathways (Reactome Pathways 2024) identification. Reactome pathways with adjusted p-values lower than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. For GSEA, DEGs were ranked by their log2 fold change and then analyzed with the fgsea algorithm30. We tested pathway enrichment using the Hallmark gene sets, with the following parameters set in the fgasea function: minimum gene set size = 15, maximum size = 500, and number of permutations = 1,000. Hallmark pathways with p-values lower than 0.05 were reported as significantly enriched.

Gene-set and transcription factor activity analysis

Gene-set activity was inferred using the PROGENy framework in conjunction with the decoupleR package31,32. Mouse PROGENy networks were obtained using the get_progeny_data function (organism = “mouse”, top = 500) to get the top 500 most significant genes per pathway. Normalized RNA expression data were extracted from the Seurat object used as input for the multi-linear modeling (MLM) function run_mlm (with a minimum gene set size of 5) to infer activity scores. These MLM scores were then incorporated into the Seurat object as a new assay, scaled using ScaleData, and visualized via FeaturePlot to compare activity across conditions.

Transcription factor (TF) activity was predicted using a Univariate Linear Model (ULM) approach implemented in decoupleR32. A mouse TF regulatory network was retrieved with the get_collectri function (organism = “mouse”, split_complexes = FALSE). The same normalized RNA count matrix from the Seurat object was then used as input to the run_ulm function (with a minimum target size of 5) to compute the TF activity scores. The resulting scores were integrated into the Seurat object as a separate assay, scaled, and subsequently visualized to evaluate differential TF activity between experimental conditions.

TF-gene expression correlation analysis

To assess the relationship between transcription factor (TF) activity and the expression of their target genes, Pearson correlation analysis was performed separately for the HDM, NRG1, and control groups. TFs included in the analysis exhibited differential activity scores across the three experimental conditions. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control for multiple testing. TF-gene regulatory networks were constructed by defining edges based on correlation coefficients, with only significantly correlated target genes retained in the final network.

Compositional analysis

Cell type proportional differences among the three experimental conditions (HDM, NRG1, and Control) were evaluated within the CD45+ and CD45– compartments separately. For CD45+ compartment, with three independent samples per treatment group, cell type proportion differences were assessed using propeller, implemented in the R package speckle33. Briefly, cell type proportions were computed for each sample and subjected to logit transformation to stabilize variance. A linear model was fitted using limma with empirical Bayes moderation, which accounts for inter-sample variability across biological replicates. An overall F-test was first performed across all three conditions to identify cell types with significant compositional changes, followed by pairwise comparisons between conditions using defined contrasts. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across cell types for both the overall and pairwise tests. For CD45- compartment, given the limited number of biological replicates (HDM, n = 1; NRG1, n = 2; Control, n = 1), proportional differences among the three experimental conditions were assessed using pairwise comparisons implemented in the R package scProportion34. Briefly, Cell-type proportion differences between conditions were evaluated using a Monte Carlo permutation framework that accounts for stochastic sampling inherent in scRNA-seq. For each condition, the observed proportion of each cell type was calculated and summarized as the log2 fold difference. To estimate the null distribution expected if both conditions shared identical underlying cell-type abundances, all cells were pooled and randomly reassigned into two pseudo-conditions while preserving the original number of cells per condition. For each permutation, cell-type proportions and log2 differences were recalculated; this procedure was repeated 10,000 times. Permutation p-values were defined as the fraction of simulations in which the permuted difference was as or more extreme than the observed difference, with FDR correction applied across cell types. This approach provides a non-parametric, sampling-aware measure of significance that does not rely on assumptions of multinomial or binomial distributions and is well-suited for single-cell proportion analyses when biological replicates are limited. Cell density was computed for each sample using sc.tl.embedding_density, which is implemented through Scanpy35.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Intercellular communication among immune cells was inferred using the CellChat package36. For each condition, the mouse CellChat database (CellChatDB.mouse) was used as the source of curated ligand–receptor interactions, and analyses were restricted to secreted signaling by subsetting the database accordingly. Data preprocessing included the identification of overexpressed genes and interactions via the functions identifyOverExpressedGenes and identifyOverExpressedInteractions. Intercellular communication probabilities were then computed with computeCommunProb, and weak interactions (supported by fewer than 10 cells) were filtered out using filterCommunication. Pathway-level communication probabilities were inferred with computeCommunProbPathway, and overall network aggregation was performed with aggregateNet. CellChat object for each sample were merged and the signaling pathways were compared across samples.

Tamoxifen inducible NRG1 deficient mice

Nrg1flox/flox mice embryos (Accession No. CDBO743) were obtained from RIKEN, Japan37. To create a conditional knockdown of NRG1 we crossed Nrg1flox/flox mice with R26-CreERT2 mice. (B6.129Gt(ROSA)26Sortm1(cre/ERT2)Tyj/J ; Jackson Laboratories cat# 008463). The resulting Nrg1flox/flox X R26-CreERT2 mice (after verification that both Nrg1 alleles were floxed), we have termed “Nrg1 null” mice. To deplete NRG1, Nrg1 null mice were fed tamoxifen (TMX) containing chow (approximately 80 mg TMX per kg mouse weight) and sensitized and challenged to HDM as above. NRG1 levels were tested by ELISA at the end of day 21 as described4. Tamoxifen containing chow included a commercial tamoxifen diet (cat# TD.130857, Inotiv) and Nutra-Gel, AIN-93, bacon flavor (cat# NGB-2, BioServ).

HDM treatment and blocking of NRG1 in Nrg1 null mice

Nrg1 null mice were placed on TMX chow and sensitized to HDM on day 8 of TMX chow, followed by HDM challenge as described above4. To fully deplete NRG1, these mice were injected additionally with anti-NRG1 mAb (10 mg/kg s.c.; kind gift from Genentech,38,39 every other day of HDM challenge phase and the last dose was given with the normally lethal dose of SeV (2 × 106 pfu) and subsequent weight and mortality recorded.
