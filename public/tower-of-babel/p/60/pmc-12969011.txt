Engineering multifunctional microspheres for sequential regulation of osteoimmune microenvironment and bone remodeling balance to promote regeneration of osteoporotic bone defects
Authors: Zhiheng Luo, Jianxiong Ma, Yipei Yang, HongJie Zhang, Yuhan Du, Jing Dai, Yan Wang, Wei Zhang, Xinlong Ma, Yuxiao Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesOsteoporotic bone defect repair remains clinically challenging due to persistent low-grade inflammation, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), and dysregulated bone metabolism. Approaches relying solely on osteoclast inhibition are often insufficient, particularly for irregular osteoporotic bone voids. This study aimed to develop a multifunctional microsphere system capable of sequential osteoimmune regulation and bone metabolic remodeling.MethodsGelatin microspheres grafted with alendronate and loaded with epigallocatechin gallate (Gel@ALN@E) were fabricated via an emulsion–chemical crosslinking method to enable controlled dual-drug release. In vitro evaluations included ROS scavenging, inflammatory modulation, macrophage polarization, osteoclast differentiation, and osteogenic responses of rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells. Transcriptomic analysis was conducted to investigate immunoregulatory mechanisms. An osteoporotic rat bone defect model was used for in vivo assessment.ResultsGel@ALN@E effectively reduced oxidative stress and inflammatory responses by promoting macrophage M2 polarization, while concurrently suppressing osteoclastogenesis and restoring bone metabolic balance. This coordinated regulation significantly enhanced osteogenic differentiation. Transcriptomic analysis revealed the downregulation of related inflammatory pathways. In vivo, Gel@ALN@E markedly improved new bone formation, trabecular organisation, and cortical bone healing in osteoporotic defects.ConclusionThis sequential drug release system offers a promising platform for both immunomodulation and bone regeneration in osteoporotic defect repair.The Translational Potential of this ArticleThe composite engineered microsphere system Gel@ALN@E integrates local immunomodulatory and osteoclast-inhibitory functions to directly address key pathological microenvironmental features of osteoporotic bone defects. This integrative design highlights its comprehensive pro-regenerative capacity and provides support for its translational application in the clinical treatment of irregular osteoporotic bone defects.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and deteriorated bone microarchitecture, resulting in increased bone fragility and fracture risk [1]. Globally, osteoporosis affects hundreds of millions of people, particularly among the elderly. According to the World Health Organization, over 200 million individuals worldwide suffer from osteoporosis, affecting approximately 30% of postmenopausal women and 20% of men over 50 years of age [2,3]​. The progressive decline in bone density and microstructural integrity disrupts trabecular connectivity, compromising mechanical strength and leading to osteoporotic bone defects. Previous studies have shown a strong correlation between osteoporosis and the formation of bone voids, especially within cancellous bone, further exacerbating skeletal fragility [4]. Moreover, osteoporotic bone defects can predispose patients to subsequent fractures, compounding the clinical burden [[5], [6], [7]]. In clinical practice, osteoporotic patients frequently present with bone defects, and localized bone voids or micro-defects are often unexpectedly discovered during surgical interventions, posing considerable challenges for orthopedic surgeons [8]. These defects are typically irregular in shape, leaving insufficient time during surgery to fabricate customized tissue-engineered scaffolds that precisely conform to the defect morphology [9]. In recent years, microspheres have gained increasing attention in the field of tissue engineering. Owing to their controllable morphology, size, and surface properties, microspheres not only serve as precise delivery vehicles for drugs and bioactive factors but also act as three-dimensional scaffold materials that support cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [[10], [11], [12]]. Particularly in bone tissue engineering, microspheres have been extensively utilized for the repair of complex bone defects, attributed to their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and controlled drug release properties [13]. Gelatin, a commonly used natural macromolecule in tissue engineering, exhibits outstanding performance. Derived from collagen and composed of various amino acids, gelatin does not elicit significant immune responses or toxicity, thereby offering excellent biocompatibility [14,15]. Furthermore, gelatin possesses favorable biodegradability, undergoing enzymatic or hydrolytic degradation into non-toxic small molecules that can be metabolized and excreted by the body. The abundant amino and carboxyl functional groups and multiple active sites within the gelatin structure allow for further chemical modifications or conjugation with functional materials [14]. This enables the enhancement of its physicochemical properties, such as extending drug release profiles, improving stability, and enhancing targeting capabilities. Such characteristics make gelatin microspheres an ideal platform for controlled and targeted drug delivery systems [16].

The microenvironment of osteoporotic bone defects exhibits a series of pathological features that collectively impede bone regeneration and reconstruction. Primarily, these defects are characterized by continuous bone loss, significantly diminished bone remodeling capacity, and an imbalance wherein osteoclastic activity surpasses osteoblastic activity, resulting in disrupted bone metabolism [17]. Additionally, oxidative stress markedly increases within the defect sites, accompanied by persistent accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), exacerbating cellular damage and tissue degeneration [18]. Concurrently, a chronic inflammatory state persists, with excessive expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines further deteriorating the bone tissue microenvironment and inducing dysregulation of osteoimmune responses, severely impairing bone regenerative capacity [19]. Thus, therapeutic strategies for osteoporotic bone defects urgently require interventions that comprehensively modulate bone metabolism, mitigate oxidative stress, alleviate chronic inflammation, and restore osteoimmune balance.

Alendronate (ALN), a commonly used bisphosphonate, exerts potent anti-resorptive effects by inhibiting osteoclast activity and bone turnover, and remains a classical therapeutic agent for the treatment of osteoporosis [20]. ALN exhibits strong bone affinity, primarily binding to hydroxyapatite in bone tissue, where it inhibits osteoclast adhesion and activity, thereby reducing bone resorption and contributing to the maintenance or increase of bone mass [20]. However, its adverse effects cannot be overlooked. Systemic administration of ALN is frequently associated with irritation of the esophageal mucosa, leading to esophagitis or esophageal ulcers. Long-term use has also been linked to an increased risk of atypical femoral fractures and renal burden [1]. To mitigate these systemic side effects, recent studies have explored localized or targeted delivery strategies by incorporating ALN into biomaterials or scaffolds.

In osteoporotic patients, factors such as aging and hormonal imbalances contribute to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), placing the body under a state of chronic inflammation [1]. Persistent oxidative stress severely impairs the function of bone cells, particularly osteoblasts, ultimately compromising bone tissue integrity. Excessive ROS production results in oxidative damage to intracellular proteins, lipids, and DNA, thereby inhibiting osteoblast proliferation and differentiation while enhancing osteoclast activity [14].Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), a naturally occurring polyphenolic compound with potent antioxidant properties, possesses multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups capable of directly reacting with ROS, thus mitigating free radical chain reactions and preventing oxidative damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids [21]. It has been demonstrated that EGCG can prevent oxidative stress-induced senescence of human mesenchymal stem cells by activating the Nrf2 pathway [22]. Consequently, EGCG has shown considerable protective effects in maintaining bone health, particularly in combating bone diseases associated with oxidative stress, such as osteoporosis. Recently, the emerging concept of "immunoporosis" has provided novel insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying osteoporosis [23]. It highlights the critical role of the bone immune microenvironment, including immune cells such as macrophages, B cells, T cells, and various secreted cytokines, in regulating bone metabolism and remodeling, thereby influencing the progression of osteoporosis. EGCG exerts immunomodulatory effects by promoting macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Studies have shown that EGCG suppresses the NF-κB signaling pathway, reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibiting M1 macrophage activation [24]. Moreover, EGCG upregulates anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and Arg-1 and activates the RANKL/RANK signaling pathway, further promoting M2 macrophage polarization [25]. In addition, EGCG has been reported to modulate microRNA expression, enhancing M2 macrophage functionality [26]. For instance, EGCG-loaded nanoparticles have been developed to induce macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, effectively remodeling the inflammatory microenvironment and achieving therapeutic effects in periodontitis [27]. Thus, by suppressing M1 macrophage-mediated pro-inflammatory responses and facilitating M2 macrophage polarization, EGCG exhibits potent anti-inflammatory and tissue-reparative properties, offering promising applications in the treatment of inflammatory diseases and tissue regeneration.

In this study, gelatin microspheres were prepared using an emulsification method (Gel). Alendronate (ALN) was incorporated into the microspheres via acylation reactions (Gel@ALN), while EGCG was subsequently loaded through an adsorption process based on the swelling properties of gelatin (Gel@ALN@E). It is hypothesized that gelatin, as a natural macromolecular biomaterial, can provide excellent biocompatibility, sustained release capabilities, and biodegradability, thus offering a favorable osteoconductive and osteoinductive platform for tissue regeneration at defect sites. This platform is expected to support the adhesion and proliferation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and osteoblasts, as well as promote the secretion of osteogenic factors and the expression of osteogenic genes. The incorporated alendronate, serving as an anti-osteoporotic agent, is anticipated to inhibit osteoclast activity, restore bone metabolic balance at osteoporotic defect sites, and prevent excessive bone resorption, thereby facilitating normal bone remodeling. Simultaneously, EGCG is expected to reduce ROS levels at the defect site, modulate immune responses, and promote macrophage polarization towards the M2 anti-inflammatory and tissue-reparative phenotype, ultimately improving the local immune microenvironment. Materials and methods

Materials

EGCG was purchased from SAITONG (Beijing, China). Soybean oil was obtained from Aladdin Reagent (Shanghai, China). Gelatin (Type A), β-glycerophosphate, ascorbic acid, and N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) were acquired from Sigma–Aldrich Co. (Shanghai, China). Alendronate sodium was sourced from Bide Pharmatech (Shanghai, China). Acetone and Rhodamine B were procured from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Dexamethasone and hydrogen peroxide solution were obtained from Macklin Reagent (Shanghai, China). The Osteoblast Mineralization Nodule Staining Kit (Alizarin Red S method), Calcein/PI Cell Viability and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit, BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Chromogenic Kit, Actin-Tracker Red-5558 Probe, and Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). α-MEM and DMEM culture media were supplied by Gibco (USA). CCK-8 was acquired from BioSharp (Beijing, China). Primary antibodies against CD86, CD163, IL-1β, Arg-1, OPG, and RANKL were provided by Proteintech Group (Wuhan, China). PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 and APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD163 antibodies were obtained from BioLegend (USA).

Preparation of microspheres

An appropriate amount of gelatin was dissolved in ultrapure water at 60 °C to prepare a 15% (w/v) gelatin solution. Separately, 200 mL of soybean oil was preheated to 60 °C. After complete dissolution of the gelatin, the solution was added to the soybean oil and stirred at 60 °C with a stirring speed of 300 rpm for 30 min. The mixture was then rapidly cooled to 4 °C and stirred continuously for another 30 min. Acetone pre-cooled to 4 °C (300 mL) was then added to solidify the microspheres for 30 min. The product was collected by vacuum filtration, washed repeatedly with cold acetone to remove the oil phase, and vacuum-dried at room temperature to obtain microspheres [28]. Subsequently, sodium alendronate was added to the microspheres at a feed ratio of 10:1 (w/w), together with 80 mM 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), for crosslinking in 80% acetone solution at 4 °C for 24 h. After discarding the supernatant, the microspheres were collected and freeze-dried under vacuum [28]. For drug loading, microspheres were immersed in a 0.3 mg/mL EGCG solution at a 10:1 (w/w) feed ratio for swelling adsorption. Prior to subsequent cellular and in vivo experiments, all microspheres underwent γ-ray irradiation sterilization (JPY Ion-Tech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China).

Physicochemical characterization

The microstructure and surface morphology of the samples were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Elemental composition of the microsphere surfaces was characterized by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS; Bruker XFlash detector, Germany).

To assess adsorption capacity, microspheres were immersed in Rhodamine B solution for 5 min and imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8, Leica Microsystems, Germany). Functional groups were analyzed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher IS50, USA) across the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1. UV-Vis spectrophotometry (UV-2600i, Shimadzu, Japan) was employed to determine characteristic absorption peaks of EGCG.

An in vitro EGCG release profile was established by incubating 0.03 g microspheres in 10 mL PBS at 37 °C with 100 rpm agitation. Samples were collected at 0–24 h, with EGCG concentration quantified at 274 nm using UV-Vis spectrophotometry. Alendronate (ALN) release was monitored in physiological saline buffer, with phosphorus quantification performed via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; Agilent 7700, Agilent Technologies, USA).

Wide-angle X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed using an X-ray diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany), scanning from 2θ = 10° to 80° at a rate of 5°/min. The degradation behaviour of the samples was evaluated in accordance with ISO 10993 standards under pH 7.4 and pH 6.0 conditions. At each predetermined degradation time point, the samples were collected, freeze-dried, and weighed.

The degradation rate was calculated over a 6-week period using the formula:Weight loss (%) = (W0 - Wᵣ) / W0 × 100where W0 is the original weight and Wᵣ is the remaining dried weight.

Zeta potential measurements were performed using a Mastersizer (Malvern Instruments Limited, UK) to assess the surface charge characteristics of the samples.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was performed to evaluate the reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capability of microsphere extracts. Hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and superoxide-related radicals (O2•-) were detected using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) as a spin-trapping agent. For •OH detection, radicals were generated using a Fenton reaction system, while O2•- was generated using a xanthine/xanthine oxidase system. The radical-generating solutions were mixed with DMPO and microsphere extracts and incubated under dark conditions prior to measurement.

EPR spectra were recorded using a EMXplus-9.5/12 spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). The relative signal intensities were compared to assess the ROS scavenging efficiency, and the g-factor values were used to confirm the identity of radical species.

In vitro biocompatibility evaluation of microspheres

Cell culture

Rat Bone Marrow Mesenchymal Stem Cells (rBMSCs) were obtained from Shanghai Bihe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured in α-MEM complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and maintained in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator (Thermo Scientific, Series 8000 Direct Heat, USA). The culture medium was replaced every 48 h. RAW264.7 cells were sourced from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM complete medium, containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA). The medium was replaced with fresh culture medium every 1-2 days, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Cytotoxicity and cell adhesion properties of microspheres

To evaluate the effect of the material extract on cell proliferation, rBMSCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well. Each well was supplemented with 150 μL of complete medium extract prepared according to ISO 10993 standards. Cell proliferation was assessed on days 3 and 5 using a CCK-8 assay kit. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader (SpectraMax M4, Molecular Devices, USA) to quantitatively analyze cell proliferation activity. For the cytotoxicity evaluation, cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well, with 1000 μL of complete medium extract added to each well. After incubation for 2 and 4 days, cell viability and cytotoxicity were assessed using a Calcein-AM/PI double-staining assay kit. The staining working solution was prepared at a dilution ratio of 1:1000. The distribution of live cells (green fluorescence) and dead cells (red fluorescence) was observed and recorded using a live-cell fluorescence imaging system (Olympus IX73P1F, Japan).

Cell adhesion experiments were conducted using a 48-well plate format. rBMSCs were seeded directly onto the surface of γ-irradiated sterilized microspheres at a density of 6 × 105 cells per well. The cells were cultured in α-MEM complete medium for 2 and 4 days, respectively, before subsequent analysis. At the end of the culture period, the samples were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min, followed by three washes with PBS buffer. Cells were then permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 solution for 10 min and washed again three times with PBS. Phalloidin and DAPI were diluted at a 1:200 ratio in antibody working solution and added to the wells, followed by incubation at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. A live-cell fluorescence imaging system (Olympus, IX73P1F, Japan) was used to capture dual-channel images of the cytoskeleton (red fluorescence) and nuclei (blue fluorescence). The number of adherent cells was quantitatively analyzed based on the fluorescence signal. Additionally, microspheres bearing adhered cells were fixed with 4% PFA and then subjected to graded ethanol dehydration (50% to 100%). After complete drying and solvent evaporation, a platinum coating was applied to the sample surface. The microstructure of cell adhesion on the microsphere surface and the interactions at the cell-material interface were observed and documented using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

RAW264.7 cell polarization and flow cytometry analysis

The induction of RAW264.7 cell polarization was assessed using immunofluorescence staining. RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 100 × 105 cells/well. Initially, cells were treated with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Solarbio, China) in DMEM complete medium and incubated for 12 h. Subsequently, the experimental groups were cultured for an additional 24 h in DMEM complete medium containing microspheres.M1 phenotype markers included CD86 and IL-1β, while M2 phenotype markers included CD163 and Arg-1. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Secondary antibodies used for fluorescence detection included CoraLite488-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) and Cy3-conjugated Affinipure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), both purchased from Proteintech Group (Wuhan, China). Fluorescence imaging was performed using a live-cell imaging workstation (Olympus, IX73P1F, Japan), and fluorescence intensity was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software. Flow cytometry analysis was conducted using a BD FACS Aria™ III Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences, USA) to assess the phenotypic distribution and quantity of M1-and M2-polarized cells. PE anti-mouse CD86 and APC anti-mouse CD163 antibodies were used to label CD86+ and CD163+ cells, respectively.

Intracellular ROS visualization and quantification

For the detection of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), rBMSCs were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well. Except for the negative control group, all groups were treated with 40 μmol/L hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Experimental groups, excluding the negative and positive control groups, were cultured in α-MEM complete medium containing composite microspheres. After 6 h of incubation, cells were loaded with 10 μmol/L 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe. Green fluorescence intensity was observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica, DMi8, Germany), and ROS levels were quantitatively analyzed using a fluorescence microplate reader (excitation: 488 nm; emission: 525 nm).

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining

rBMSCs were seeded on the surface of microspheres in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After cell attachment, the culture medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium. For ALP activity detection, cells were induced for 7 and 14 days, respectively. The BCIP/NBT ALP staining working solution was prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions (33 μL BCIP + 66 μL NBT per 10 mL diluent), and 1 mL of the staining solution was added to each well and incubated for 30 min in the dark. The formation of blue-purple precipitates was observed under a light microscope.

Quantification of mineralization by Alizarin Red staining

The osteogenic mineralization capacity of rBMSCs on microspheres was evaluated using a mineralized nodule staining kit based on the Alizarin Red S method. rBMSCs were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After cell attachment, the culture medium was replaced with composite microspheres osteogenic induction medium. Cells were continuously cultured for 21 days. After 21 days, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with Alizarin Red S for 15–30 min, and washed with ddH2O. The formation of orange-red calcium deposits was observed under a light microscope. For quantitative analysis, calcium-bound dye was eluted with 10% cetylpyridinium chloride, and the absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader.

Osteoclast induction and inhibition

The differentiation of RAW264.7 cells into osteoclasts was conducted in 12-well plates, with a seeding density of 3 × 105 cells per well. Cells were induced for 7 days using DMEM medium (from Qiaomu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) supplemented with 100 ng/mL RANKL. The medium was refreshed every 2 days. For the experimental groups, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing composite microspheres. After 7 days of induction, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was performed to assess osteoclast differentiation.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and bioinformatic analyses of RAW264.7 cells

Total RNA was extracted from RAW264.7 cells using Invitrogen™ TRIzol™ Reagent (Thermo Fisher, USA) after 12 h of LPS induction followed by 24 h of treatment (the intervention method was consistent with previous sections). RNA/cDNA library sequencing was conducted using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Library quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis for RNA integrity and contamination, NanoPhotometer spectrophotometry for RNA purity, Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer for accurate RNA quantification, and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer for precise RNA integrity evaluation. Data quality control was performed using fastp to filter low-quality reads. Ribosomal RNA alignment was conducted using bowtie2, while genome alignment was carried out with HISAT2. Transcript abundance was reconstructed using StringTie, and gene expression levels were presented using both raw read counts and fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Bioinformatic analyses were performed using Omicsmart, a real-time interactive data analysis platform (http://www.omicsmart.com).

Proteins were extracted from RAW264.7 macrophages after different treatments using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of total protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% (w/v) non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NFATc1, TNF-α and β-actin. All primary antibodies were obtained from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). After incubation with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system.

Osteogenic and inflammatory gene expression detection

rBMSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates. After cell attachment, the culture medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium containing composite microspheres, and cells were induced for 7 days. Total RNA was extracted using Invitrogen™ TRIzol™ Reagent, followed by phase separation with chloroform and precipitation with isopropanol. RNA was then washed with 75% ethanol and resuspended in DEPC-treated water (Solarbio, Beijing, China). A 1 μL sample was used to assess RNA purity (260/280 ratio) using the Thermo Biomate 3S spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). For reverse transcription, the HiFiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cwbio, China) was used in a 20 μL reaction system. Following the synthesis of cDNA templates, qPCR was performed using the GreenTB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ RR420A (Takara, Japan) kit, with primers synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). qPCR amplification was conducted on the LightCycler® 480Ⅱ (Roche, Switzerland), and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The intervention of RAW264.7 cells and total RNA extraction followed the procedures described in section 2.4.8. Reverse transcription and qPCR quantification were performed as previously described.

RAW264.7–rBMSC co-culture system

A transwell-based co-culture system was established to investigate the paracrine interaction between macrophages and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells. RAW264.7 macrophages were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL, Solarbio, China) for 12 h to induce an inflammatory phenotype, followed by treatment with different material extracts. Subsequently, RAW264.7 cells were co-cultured with rBMSCs using a Culture-Insert in μ-Dish system (ibidi, Germany), allowing soluble factor exchange without direct cell–cell contact. The co-culture was maintained for 24 h [29].After co-culture, total RNA and protein were collected for qPCR and Western blot analyses, respectively. Culture supernatants were harvested for ELISA to quantify inflammatory cytokines and bone remodeling–related factors. ELISA assays were performed strictly according to the manufacturers’ instructions. All antibodies used in immunoblotting and immunofluorescence analyses were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

In vivo animal studies

Establishment of osteoporosis model in rats

Six-week-old female Sprague–Dawley rats were obtained from Zhejiang Viton Lever Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Jiaxing, China) and housed under sterile barrier conditions at the Shenzhen Institute of Advanced Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shenzhen, China). All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Shenzhen Institute of Advanced Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (SIAT-IACUC-220922-YGS-LYX-A2189). In brief, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane gas, and bilateral longitudinal incisions were made approximately 0.5–1 cm lateral to the midline over the ovaries. The ovarian arteries, veins, and oviducts were ligated. The incisions were closed using absorbable sutures, and each rat received a postoperative intramuscular injection of 50,000 units of antibiotics daily for three consecutive days. After 12 weeks of modeling, the establishment of osteoporosis was confirmed using micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT).

Establishment of distal femoral bone defects in rats

Rats that had completed osteoporosis modeling were anesthetized with isoflurane gas. A skin incision was made over the distal femur, followed by blunt dissection of the muscle to expose the femoral condyle. A unilateral defect measuring Φ2.8 mm × 3 mm was created using an electric drill. Except for the control (Ctrl) group, an appropriate amount of microspheres was implanted into the defect site in each group. The defect was sealed with a small amount of bone wax. After flushing with sterile saline, the muscle and skin layers were sutured sequentially. Postoperative care included administration of 50,000 units of antibiotics per rat daily for three consecutive days.

Micro-CT 3D imaging analysis

Six and twelve weeks after the bone defect surgery and microsphere implantation, three rats from each group were euthanized via CO2 overdose. The distal femur was surgically harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for one week. Ex vivo micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) scanning was performed using the SkyScan1276 system (BRUKER, Germany) at a resolution of 10 μm, followed by 3D reconstruction. Trabecular and cortical bone parameters were analyzed using CTAn and CTvox software.

Histological analysis

Prior to histological sectioning, distal femur specimens were decalcified in 10% EDTA solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 6 weeks. Following decalcification, tissues were dehydrated using an automatic tissue processor and embedded in paraffin using an embedding machine (Leica, Germany). Paraffin sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson's trichrome staining were performed according to standard protocols. For immunohistochemical staining, paraffin sections were first deparaffinized in xylene (2 × 5 min) and rehydrated in a graded ethanol series (100% × 2, 95%, 75%). Antigen retrieval was performed using a microwave oven in buffer solution. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with 3% H2O2 at room temperature for 10 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with goat serum at room temperature for 30 min. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following dilutions: OPG (1:500), RANKL (1:250), RUNX2 (1:250), NFATc1 (1:250) and VEGF (1:250). After three washes with PBS, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200) were added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. DAB was used as the chromogen, and sections were counterstained lightly with hematoxylin for 30 s, followed by rinsing with water. Sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted using neutral resin. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was conducted after standard rehydration of paraffin sections. Sections were immersed in TRAP staining solution (Guosheng Zhongyuan Technology Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China) and incubated at 37 °C for 20–30 min. The reaction was terminated by rinsing with distilled water (5 s). Nuclei were counterstained with methyl green for 1–2 min, followed by water rinse. After ethanol dehydration and xylene clearing, sections were sealed with neutral resin.

We determined the expression and localization of CD86-CD163 in tissues using Immunofluorescence double staining, and all antibodies were from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). All stained sections were imaged and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent samples per group. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (La Jolla, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for significance comparisons among groups, while two-way ANOVA was applied for comparisons involving multiple factors. Student's t-tests were employed for comparisons between two independent groups. Statistical significance was defined as ∗P < 0.05, while ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001 were considered highly significant.

Results and discussion

The surface of the microspheres exhibited a rough morphology, conducive to cell adhesion and proliferation in subsequent applications (Fig. 1B). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis revealed the elemental distribution of carbon (red), nitrogen (green), oxygen (blue), and phosphorus (cyan) on the microsphere surfaces (Fig. 1D). EDS quantification showed that phosphorus accounted for 9.64%, preliminarily confirming the presence of ALN on the microsphere surface (Fig. 1E). Further Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis demonstrated characteristic absorption peaks of ALN at 3300 cm−1 (O–H stretching vibration in P–OH groups, ranging between 3500 and 3200 cm−1) and at 920 cm−1 (P–O stretching vibration) [30]. The peak at 1637 cm−1 corresponded to C=O stretching vibrations in the amide linkage, confirming the formation of amide bonds [31]. Additionally, peaks at 1690 cm−1 (C=O stretching) and 1456 cm−1 (aromatic ring stretching vibration) corresponded to EGCG, and the peak at 1144 cm−1 (C–O–H stretching vibration) indicated successful loading of EGCG onto the gelatin microspheres [32,33] (Fig. 1F). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of ALN-loaded microspheres showed characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ = 13.65°, 19.80°, and 21.9°, further confirming the successful grafting of ALN onto the microsphere surfaces (Fig. 1G) [31].

To investigate EGCG release behavior, a simulated in vitro environment was employed. UV-vis spectroscopy revealed a specific absorption peak of EGCG around 270 nm (Fig. 1H). Quantitative analysis demonstrated a rapid cumulative release of approximately 0.56 mg (122μ M) during the initial 24 h (Fig. 1I). In contrast, ALN exhibited a relatively slower release profile, with approximately 45% released after 21 days (Fig. 1J).

The in vitro degradation study of the microspheres under different pH conditions demonstrated that, after 6 weeks of incubation, approximately 60% of the microsphere mass remained under the neutral environment (pH 7.4), whereas only about 35% remained under the mildly acidic condition (pH 6.0). This phenomenon may be attributed to the weakening of intermolecular hydrogen bonding and partial physical crosslinking interactions in an acidic environment, which reduces the stability of the network structure and renders the matrix more susceptible to structural disruption. In addition, enhanced penetration of water molecules into the gelatin network under acidic conditions may further promote polymer chain dissociation and mass loss. These results indicate that a mildly acidic microenvironment can accelerate the degradation of the gelatin matrix, thereby partially mimicking the local conditions present in inflammatory or osteoclast-active regions (Fig. 1K). Zeta potential analysis in ultrapure water revealed a positive charge (9 mV to 10 mV) for gelatin and a negative charge (−28 mV to −30 mV) for EGCG solutions, suggesting electrostatic attraction conducive to EGCG adsorption onto the gelatin microspheres. Moreover, the composite microspheres exhibited a positive surface charge, which is also conducive to cell adhesion (Fig. 1L). [34].

Gelatin, a commonly used biomacromolecule, possesses excellent stability, swelling capability, and adsorption properties [35]. The abundant amino and carboxyl groups on its surface provide an excellent platform for drug loading [36]. Chemical crosslinking facilitated stable bonding of ALN through amide formation, as evidenced by FTIR and XRD analyses showing characteristic P–O/P–OH peaks and crystallographic changes. The swelling-induced network expansion of gelatin microspheres, combined with intrinsic adsorption characteristics and electrostatic attraction (gelatin +9 mV vs. EGCG -28 mV), supported efficient EGCG loading. This dual-mode "chemical bonding and physical adsorption" drug-loading strategy not only prevented potential interference between co-loaded drugs but also achieved sequential therapeutic release profiles matched to microsphere degradation rates, meeting the temporal pathological demands of bone regeneration processes—initial anti-inflammatory effects followed by osteogenic promotion [37,38].

EPR spectroscopy was employed to evaluate the reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging ability of microsphere extracts. As shown in (Fig. 1M) and (Fig. 1N), characteristic EPR signals of •OH and O2•- were clearly detected in the control system. Compared with the Gel group, the Gel@ALN@E group exhibited markedly reduced signal intensities for both radicals, indicating an enhanced ROS scavenging capability [39]. The g-factor analysis (Fig. 1O) showed a characteristic value of approximately 2.0034, confirming the radical origin of the detected signals. These results demonstrate that Gel@ALN@E microspheres effectively eliminate ROS, which is beneficial for alleviating oxidative stress in pathological microenvironments.

Timely cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation post-injury are essential for normal cellular functions and effective tissue repair. In vitro anti-inflammatory effects

The pathological microenvironment of osteoporotic bone defects is characterized by persistent inflammation [40]. Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), a polyphenolic compound rich in aromatic ring structures, reacts with free radicals, stabilizing the unpaired electrons through resonance delocalization within its aromatic rings, thus forming intermediates with lower reactivity and efficiently terminating radical chain reactions [41]. However, similar to most bioactive substances, EGCG suffers from drawbacks such as low bioavailability, chemical instability, poor dispersion, and rapid metabolism [34,[42], [43], [44], [45]]. Hence, in this study, we loaded EGCG onto microsphere surfaces and employed a local drug delivery strategy to ensure EGCG's localized release at the bone defect site and its surroundings. This approach effectively scavenges ROS and modulates macrophage polarization phenotypes, thereby regulating cellular immune status and promoting bone tissue regeneration.

Flow cytometry analysis

As shown in Fig. 3I, macrophages in quadrant Q1 were labeled with CD86 antibodies (M1 marker), while those in Q4 were labeled with CD163 antibodies (M2 marker). The proportion of M1 single-positive cells was higher in the Gel and Gel@ALN groups, accounting for approximately 22.0% and 15.7%, respectively. The integrated analysis of CD86+ cells in Fig. 3J showed a significantly higher proportion in the Gel group compared to the Gel@E and Gel@ALN@E groups. Similarly, the analysis of CD163+ cells in Fig. 3K revealed a significantly higher proportion in the Gel@ALN@E group compared to the Gel and Gel@ALN groups (P < 0.05, P < 0.01).

The dynamic shift in macrophage phenotypes (M1 to M2 polarization) plays a central role in regulating inflammation and tissue regeneration during bone repair [37]. In the early (acute) phase of injury, M1-type macrophages predominate and promote the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) and ROS to clear pathogens and necrotic tissue, thereby initiating the repair process [46]. In the later stages of healing, M2-type macrophages become dominant, secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β) and pro-regenerative mediators (e.g., Arg-1, PDGF), which promote cell migration, extracellular matrix deposition, and osteogenic differentiation, ultimately leading to tissue homeostasis restoration [47].In this study, we found that the release of EGCG from the composite microspheres could modulate macrophage polarization from M1 to M2, thus regulating the inflammatory response and laying a foundation for the improvement of the immune microenvironment and subsequent osteogenesis.

Therefore, incorporating antioxidants at the injury site to effectively scavenge ROS, mitigate inflammation, and improve the pathological microenvironment is critical for effective tissue repair.

In osteoporosis, the balance between osteogenic reconstruction and osteoclastic activity is disrupted, with osteoclast activity exceeding osteogenesis, leading to increased bone loss, impaired regeneration capacity, and reduced bone mass. In osteoporotic bone defects, creating a local microenvironment that inhibits bone resorption and strengthens bone regeneration is crucial. Therefore, we investigated whether rBMSCs could differentiate into osteoblasts under the intervention of composite microspheres, and whether the microspheres could inhibit the differentiation of RAW264.7 osteoclast precursor cells.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP), an important monophosphate hydrolase involved in osteoblast differentiation and mineralization, serves as a reference for assessing osteogenic potential [49]. After 7 days of ALP staining, expression of ALP was observed in all groups, with stronger ALP expression at day 14 in the Gel@ALN, Gel@E, and Gel@ALN@E groups compared to day 7. Additionally, ALP expression was significantly higher in these groups compared to the Ctrl and Gel groups (Fig. 4C). After 21 days of osteogenic induction, Alizarin Red S staining of mineralized nodules revealed that the Gel@E and Gel@ALN@E groups had a higher number of mineralized nodules, with darker staining compared to the Ctrl and Gel groups. This suggests that the composite microspheres promote osteogenic mineralization of rBMSCs (Fig. 4D). Quantitative elution staining of mineralized nodules using hexadecylpyridinium chloride showed a significant increase in the Gel@ALN, Gel@E, and Gel@ALN@E groups compared to the Ctrl group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4F).

In this study, ALN was loaded onto the microspheres, with the aim of inhibiting osteoclasts and improving the local osteoclastic microenvironment. Osteoclasts induced from RAW264.7 cells showed that in the Ctrl and Gel groups, mature osteoclasts were abundant, with large areas, large cytoplasm, multiple pseudopodia, and multinucleation (n ≥ 3). In the Gel@ALN and Gel@ALN@E groups, the number of osteoclasts was significantly suppressed, and both the number and area of osteoclasts in the field of view were visibly reduced (Fig. 4E). Quantitative analysis of the osteoclast area percentage revealed that Gel@ALN, Gel@E, and Gel@ALN@E groups significantly reduced the osteoclast area compared to the Ctrl group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4G). This indicates that the ALN component of the composite microspheres effectively inhibits the number and differentiation of osteoclasts. Furthermore, qPCR analysis was conducted to confirm the regulation of bone metabolism-related gene expression across different treatment groups. The results indicated that the Gel@ALN@E group significantly increased the expression of the osteogenic factor OPG (P < 0.05) while significantly suppressing the osteoclastogenic factor RANKL (P < 0.05), suggesting that EGCG and ALN components synergistically promote osteogenesis and inhibit osteoclastogenesis (Fig. 4H and I).

At the transcriptional level (Fig. 6B), qPCR analysis demonstrated that, compared with the Gel group, the Gel@ALN@E group exhibited a significant downregulation of TNF-α mRNA expression (P < 0.01), accompanied by a marked reduction in NF-κB p65 transcriptional levels (P < 0.05). In addition, the mRNA expression of the osteoclast-associated factor RANKL was significantly decreased (P < 0.01), whereas the expression of the osteoprotective factor OPG was significantly upregulated (P < 0.01). These results indicate that, under inflammatory conditions, the composite microspheres are capable of modulating immune–bone remodeling–related gene expression at the transcriptional level.

At the protein secretion level (Fig. 6C), ELISA was further performed to quantify soluble factors in the supernatant of the RAW264.7–rBMSC co-culture system. Compared with the Gel group, the Gel@ALN@E group showed a pronounced reduction in TNF-α secretion (P < 0.001), suggesting effective suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Meanwhile, RANKL secretion was significantly decreased (P < 0.01), whereas OPG secretion was markedly increased (P < 0.05), indicating that the co-culture microenvironment was substantially shifted toward a phenotype favorable for osteogenesis and inhibitory to osteoclastogenesis.

At the signaling pathway level (Fig. 6D), Western blot analysis revealed that TNF-α protein expression was reduced in the Gel@ALN@E group, accompanied by inhibition of NF-κB pathway activation, as evidenced by decreased phosphorylation of p65 (p-p65), while total p65 levels remained largely unchanged. Concurrently, OPG protein expression was upregulated, whereas RANKL expression was downregulated. These protein-level findings were consistent with the trends observed in qPCR and ELISA analyses, further supporting that the material regulates immune–bone remodeling signaling through suppression of the NF-κB inflammatory pathway.

At the tissue and spatial distribution level (Fig. 6E), immunofluorescence staining combined with heatmap analysis demonstrated that, compared with the Gel group, the Gel@ALN@E group exhibited an overall reduction in the signal intensity of TNF-α and NF-κB-related markers, along with enhanced OPG signals and attenuated RANKL signals. The heatmaps visually illustrated the coordinated suppression of inflammation-associated signals and enhancement of osteoprotective signaling.

Taken together, from a mechanistic perspective, pro-inflammatory cytokines released by inflammatory macrophages, particularly TNF-α, represent key upstream drivers that inhibit osteogenic differentiation and promote osteoclast activation. Under LPS-induced inflammatory conditions, Gel@ALN@E treatment significantly reduced both the transcriptional expression and secretion of TNF-α, while concomitantly suppressing NF-κB pathway activation, thereby establishing an immunological basis for downstream remodeling of bone-related signaling pathways [29]. Previous studies have demonstrated that NF-κB signaling not only amplifies inflammatory responses but also directly participates in osteoclast differentiation through regulation of RANKL expression; thus, its inhibition is considered a critical node for restoring immune–bone homeostasis [51]. On this basis, the observed upregulation of OPG and downregulation of RANKL in the co-culture system further highlight a shift from immunomodulation toward osteogenic promotion. The OPG/RANKL axis serves as a central hub linking inflammatory immune status with bone remodeling balance: attenuation of inflammation is typically accompanied by restoration of OPG expression and suppression of RANKL signaling, thereby inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and creating a permissive microenvironment for osteoblast function.

Further quantitative analysis of key bone structural parameters, including BV/TV (bone volume fraction), Tb.N (trabecular number), Tb.Sp (trabecular separation), and BMD (bone mineral density), was conducted to assess the effect of different drug-loaded microspheres on bone defect repair (Fig. 7C–F). The results showed that the Ctrl group exhibited the weakest bone repair capacity at all time points, with the lowest BV/TV, Tb.N, and BMD, and the highest Tb.Sp, suggesting a lack of new bone trabeculae, poor connectivity, and large cavities. Although the Gel group showed slight improvements, the overall effect remained limited. The Gel@ALN and Gel@E treatment groups significantly increased BV/TV, Tb.N, and BMD, and decreased Tb.Sp, demonstrating better bone repair compared to the Ctrl and Gel groups. The Gel@ALN@E composite microsphere group showed significant improvements in all parameters, particularly at 12 weeks, where BV/TV, Tb.N, and BMD were superior to other groups, and Tb.Sp was significantly reduced compared to the Ctrl group (P < 0.0001), indicating that the surgical area had more newly formed trabeculae, with better density and connectivity, and a greater amount of newly formed bone.

Histological analysis

We performed HE, Masson, and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for different factors to assess new bone formation, healing status, and the expression of osteogenic-related factors in the tissue at the defect site. HE staining revealed that at 6 weeks, the Ctrl group exhibited a loose tissue structure with almost no new bone formation, primarily consisting of adipose tissue and bone marrow cavities, indicating poor healing. Compared to 6 weeks, there was slight new bone formation at 12 weeks, but the overall bone structure remained unclear, lacking bone bridging, and the bone defect repair was poor. The Gel group showed partial new bone formation at 6 weeks, but the trabecular bone was sparse. At 12 weeks, new bone increased, and the trabecular structure was slightly evident, though still insufficiently dense, showing limited repair. In the Gel@ALN group, at 6 weeks, there were more adipocyte vacuoles, and ALN inhibited bone resorption but did not significantly promote osteogenesis. At 12 weeks, significant new bone formation occurred, with a large amount of mature bone tissue, and the trabecular growth was more mature with denser connections. The Gel@E group showed similar findings to Gel@ALN at 6 weeks, with many adipose-like vacuoles and some signs of new bone formation. At 12 weeks, new bone was evident, expanding from the center outward, with clear bone bridge connections, and fibrous healing tissue appeared in the tissue. The Gel@ALN@E group showed more new bone formation at 6 weeks, with a denser structure compared to the other groups, and a greater number of trabeculae. Furthermore, we evaluated the new collagen formation using Masson staining to indirectly reflect the new bone formation. In the Ctrl group, at both 6 and 12 weeks, although some repair was observed, the collagen fiber content, indicated by blue staining, remained low, and the bone defect area was not fully filled. The percentage of new bone area was the lowest at both 6 and 12 weeks, with almost no new bone formation at 6 weeks, and only a slight increase at 12 weeks, with no significant difference. Both tissue morphology and quantitative data analysis indicate that, without intervention, the natural repair capacity of the bone defect is limited. In the Gel group, at 6 weeks, collagen fibers were slightly more abundant than in the Ctrl group, but still not rich. At 12 weeks, collagen fiber deposition increased further, but it was still not ideal compared to other intervention groups. The percentage of new bone area at 6 weeks was slightly higher than in the Ctrl group, and although there was an increase at 12 weeks, the improvement was minimal. This suggests that the Gel group has limited effect on defect repair. In the Gel@ALN group, at 6 weeks, collagen fibers were observed to have formed in the defect area, as shown by the blue staining. At 12 weeks, the distribution of collagen fibers increased and became more uniform, and a greater number of blue-stained newly formed trabecular bone regions appeared. The new bone area percentage at 6 weeks was higher than that in the Gel and Ctrl groups, with a significant increase at 12 weeks compared to 6 weeks. This indicates that alendronate slowed down bone loss, improved bone metabolic balance, and promoted collagen fiber deposition, as reflected both in tissue morphology and new bone area data. In the Gel@E group, at 6 weeks, collagen fibers were slightly more distributed than in the Gel and Gel@ALN groups; the percentage of new bone area was similar to the Gel@ALN group and significantly higher than in the Ctrl group. At 12 weeks, collagen fiber content was abundant, but the percentage of new bone was slightly lower than in the ALN group. In the Gel@ALN@E group, at 6 weeks, collagen fibers were notably increased in the central region of the defect; at 12 weeks, collagen fiber deposition was rich and organized, with more newly formed trabeculae emerging from the central bone bridge region. The new bone area percentage was the highest at both 6 and 12 weeks, significantly higher than in the Ctrl group (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 8B, Fig. S5). This suggests that the combined use of alendronate and EGCG has a favorable synergistic effect in vivo, and both the tissue morphology and morphometric analysis indicate that this combination yielded the best outcome in bone defect repair (P < 0.05, P < 0.0001). In addition, immunofluorescence double-staining of CD86 and CD163 was performed to evaluate macrophage phenotype distribution in rat bone tissues from different treatment groups. As shown in the figure, CD86 (red) and CD163 (green) were used as markers of M1 and M2 macrophages, respectively, with nuclei counterstained by DAPI (blue). The corresponding three-dimensional fluorescence intensity maps further illustrate the spatial distribution of these signals.In the Ctrl, Gel, and Gel@ALN groups, CD86-positive signals were widely distributed within the defect region, whereas CD163-associated fluorescence remained limited, indicating a predominance of pro-inflammatory macrophage-associated markers. In contrast, the Gel@E group showed reduced CD86-associated fluorescence together with a broader distribution of CD163 signals, suggesting that EGCG treatment promoted macrophage polarization toward an M2-associated phenotype and facilitated the distribution of M2-related cells. Notably, the Gel@ALN@E group exhibited a further decrease in CD86-associated signals accompanied by enhanced CD163 expression and distribution. Three-dimensional intensity analysis revealed an overall reduction in inflammation-associated signals and a relative enrichment of tissue repair–associated signals in this group. Collectively, these results indicate that the composite system more effectively promoted M2 macrophage polarization and contributed to the establishment of a local immune microenvironment favorable for tissue repair (Fig. 8C).

Immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 9A and B) was performed to assess the expression of the osteogenic factor OPG and the key transcription factor RUNX2, which plays a crucial role in skeletal development and bone tissue formation by directly controlling osteoblast differentiation, maturation, and bone matrix synthesis [53]. The results showed that the expression levels in the Gel@ALN@E group were higher than those in the other experimental groups, which was consistent with the semi-quantitative analysis and significantly higher than the Ctrl group (P < 0.0001). The osteoclast differentiation factor RANKL and TRAP staining were combined to assess the inhibitory effect on osteoclast activity. The results showed that the expression of RANKL in the Gel@ALN@E group was lower than in the other groups, and TRAP staining of osteoclasts showed a significant reduction in osteoclast number in this group, with a significant decrease at 12 weeks compared to the Ctrl group (P < 0.05). Given that NFATC1 is a key transcriptional regulator of osteoclast differentiation, its downregulation further supports the inhibitory effect of the composite microspheres on osteoclast differentiation. In addition, the expression of VEGF was also examined. Verification through in vivo implantation of microspheres in experimental animals revealed that the combined application of ALN and EGCG synergistically promotes bone defect repair. ALN maintains bone metabolic homeostasis by inhibiting bone resorption, reducing bone loss, and promoting osteogenesis, while EGCG promotes bone matrix synthesis by improving the inflammatory environment and enhancing the expression of osteogenic differentiation marker RUNX2. The composite microsphere group (Gel@ALN@E) showed collagen fiber deposition and new bone formation as early as 6 weeks, with structural reconstruction of the defect area achieved through bone bridging at 12 weeks. Its advantage lies in the bidirectional regulation of osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis: on one hand, upregulating OPG and activating RUNX2 to enhance osteogenic activity; on the other hand, inhibiting RANKL expression and the number of TRAP-positive osteoclasts, thereby restoring the “low osteogenesis - high resorption” imbalance in the OP bone defect microenvironment. Notably, although the ALN-only group (Gel@ALN) inhibited bone loss, its early osteogenic effect was weaker than that of the composite microsphere group, suggesting that EGCG plays an auxiliary role in activating osteogenesis-related pathways [54]. However, when EGCG was applied alone (Gel@E), it promoted fibrous tissue repair but showed insufficient trabecular maturity, further highlighting the inhibitory effect of ALN on bone loss in osteoporotic conditions. In conclusion, the synergistic effect of ALN and EGCG provides a promising in vivo repair effect for optimizing osteoporotic bone defects.

Conclusion

In summary, based on the pathological characteristics of osteoporotic bone defects, we have developed a dual-drug-loaded gelatin microsphere system with synergistic functions of "immune microenvironment regulation and bone metabolism remodeling." Through chemical crosslinking, the swelling properties of the gelatin network, and electrostatic interactions between components, the system achieved effective loading and sequential release of ALN and EGCG, while providing a suitable platform for cell loading and promoting osteogenic activity. In vitro studies demonstrated that the antioxidant and immunomodulatory functions of EGCG effectively scavenge reactive oxygen species, promote M2 macrophage polarization, and alleviate the inflammatory microenvironment. ALN, on the other hand, maintains bone metabolic balance by inhibiting osteoclast differentiation, thus creating favorable conditions for bone reconstruction and repair. The synergistic effect of both components enhanced the osteogenic differentiation capacity of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells and inhibited osteoclast activity. Transcriptomic analysis further revealed their inhibitory effects on inflammatory pathways and osteoclast differentiation-related signaling pathways, explaining their functional synergy at the molecular level. In vivo studies confirmed that the system significantly promotes new tissue fiber formation and bone microstructure remodeling, achieving a favorable in vivo repair effect. This drug-loaded microsphere system demonstrates promising potential for clinical translation in the repair and treatment of osteoporotic bone defects.
