Rank signaling drives basal cell-lineage infidelity leading to mammary tumorigenesis

ABSTRACT
Rank signaling regulates mammary gland development and epithelial differentiation. While Rank is expressed in both basal and luminal cells, its basal-specific role is unclear. Here, using inducible basal-specific Rank expression and lineage tracing, we show that Rank signaling regulates basal cell identity in postnatal mammary glands. Increased basal Rank activity disrupts basal and luminal identities, causing aberrant luminal-like differentiation, lactation defects, and premalignant lesions composed of hybrid basal-derived cells that progress to basal and luminal adenocarcinomas. Conversely, Rank loss reduces tumor formation and also impairs cell identity. Mechanistically, proteomic, transcriptomic, and chromatin analyses reveal that Rank activation drives epigenetic remodeling, leading to basal identity loss and tumor initiation. A basal Rank gene signature correlates with ductal carcinoma in situ recurrence, as well as poor outcomes in luminal breast cancers. Thus, basal Rank-driven lineage infidelity promotes pre-invasive lesions and transition to invasive breast cancer in females.

Introduction

The mammary gland (MG) is a specialized organ that develops postnatally and experiences dynamic morphological changes, from an initial expansion during puberty to subsequent cycles of growth/regression led by oestrous and pregnancy/involution cycles. The MG is organized as an epithelial bilayer comprising luminal cells (LCs) and basal cells (BCs) surrounding the LC layer. LCs are either hormone-sensing cells, which express hormone receptors (Hrs), or luminal progenitors (LPs), devoid of Hrs. Embryonic MG development relies on bipotent progenitors located in the basal compartment, able to give rise to LCs and BCs (reviewed in ref. 1). Upon birth, each epithelial compartment is mostly maintained by lineage-restricted progenitors (reviewed in ref. 1). However, postnatally BCs retain the competence for bipotency, triggered under specific conditions2,3.

Rank (receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B) and its ligand, Rankl, members of the TNF (receptor) superfamily, play pivotal roles in MG development4,5, having been the role of Rank signaling in LCs extensively studied3–8. Rankl is expressed by hormone-sensing LCs, triggering progesterone-dependent expansion of neighbouring Rank-positive cells, including BCs and LPs4,9,10. Ubiquitous, whole mammary epithelial and LC-specific deletion of Rank impairs alveologenesis and lactogenesis due to compromised proliferation and differentiation of mammary epithelial cells3–5. Gain-of-function mouse models, where Rank is mainly expressed by LCs (MMTV-Rank) show increased mammary epithelial cell proliferation and stemness, resulting in early alveologenesis and lactation failure6–8. Thus, proper regulation of Rank signaling is crucial for MG epithelial development.

Besides, Rank signaling plays a significant role in mammary tumorigenesis: genetic or pharmacological inhibition of Rank signaling prevents or attenuates mammary tumorigenesis7,11–17. Paradoxically, MMTV-driven Rank expression enhances LCs senescence delaying MG tumor onset, while promoting tumor aggressiveness once tumors are established7.

Although Rank is expressed by BCs4, BC-specific Rank signaling and its impact on MG development and cancer remain poorly understood. Our group has shown that luminal-specific deletion of Rank results in unfit LCs during pregnancy, favoring the reactivation of adult BC bipotency through Rank signaling to replace incompetent LCs and restore lactation3. Here, we demonstrate that, upon overactivation of Rank signaling, BCs reorganize their chromatin and undergo LC-like differentiation through epigenetic remodeling, leading to an aberrant basal-luminal hybrid cell population that eventually gives rise to mammary preinvasive lesions and ultimately breast adenocarcinomas. Moreover, we describe a basal Rank gene signature associated with recurrence of precancerous lesions and poor survival in breast adenocarcinomas.

Results

Enhanced basal Rank signaling disrupts basal and luminal cell identity

Our recent results suggest that Rank signaling plays a role in the postnatal reactivation of BC bipotency3. To directly assess the role of Rank signaling in BCs, we generated a novel mouse model enabling the inducible expression of Rank specifically in BCs and allowing fate mapping of control (K14-Ctrl) or Rank (K14-Rank) overexpressing cells (Gfp + ) at different developmental stages. Briefly, a doxycycline-inducible Tet-On/Cre-loxP system was used to allow BC-specific Cre activation (under the control of keratin 14 (K14) promoter), subsequent Rank expression, and tracing of recombined BCs and their progeny (see Methods for details) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Experimental and control mice were treated with doxycycline during puberty (weeks 4–8) (Fig. 1a), and BC-specific recombination was confirmed by co-localization of Ck14 and Gfp. Whole mount analysis of MGs failed to demonstrate major differences in fat pad invasion or epithelial morphology upon BC-enhanced Rank expression compared to control females (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Immunohistopathological analysis determined a similar ratio of BC/LC and Hr + /Hr- LCs within the luminal epithelium (Fig. 1b, e and Supplementary Fig. 1c–f). BCs, but not LCs, initially showed enhanced proliferation in K14-Rank mice that was not sustained over time (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 1g, h). Unlike in the MMTV-Rank model7, in which ectopic Rank expression is mainly targeted to LCs, no significant changes in the senescence marker SA-βGal were observed in BCs or LCs upon basal Rank expression (Supplementary Fig. 1i, j).

Next, we characterized the transcriptome of isolated Gfp+ BCs and Gfp- LCs from MGs of virgin K14-Ctrl and K14-Rank mice, and a complementary basal-specific Rank depletion mouse model (K14-RankΔ/Δ), in which no major alterations in MG development were detected3. High levels of basal-specific recombination were confirmed in all genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). LCs from all models clustered together upon PCA analysis, while BCs, particularly from K14-Rank mice, showed greater heterogeneity (Fig. 1g). Sample identity was confirmed by heatmap analysis of known luminal and basal markers (Supplementary Fig. 2c). K14-Rank BCs showed 211 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) compared to corresponding controls, including the upregulation of Mki67, in line with the increased levels of proliferation, but intriguingly, also LC-associated genes (Krt18, Ehf and Mfge8) and downregulation of myoepithelial/BC-associated genes (Oxtr, Fos and Egr1) (Fig. 1h, i and Supplementary Data 1).

No DEGs were found between BCs from K14-RankΔ/Δ and control littermates (Supplementary Fig. 2d), but gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed an opposite effect of Rank signaling in BCs according to Rank expression: K14-Rank BCs showed largely upregulation of gene sets (vs K14-Ctrl BCs), whereas in K14-RankΔ/Δ BCs most pathways were downregulated (Supplementary Fig. 2e and Supplementary Data 1). BCs from K14-Rank females upregulated gene sets associated with proliferation, Rank signaling (i.e., Nfκb signaling and inflammation/immunity), protein translation and cell differentiation (i.e. Slits/Robo regulation), while most of these gene sets were downregulated in K14-RankΔ/Δ BCs, confirming Rank functionality in K14 driven gain and loss of function models, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2f and Supplementary Data 1).

Considering the increased gene expression levels of luminal markers, we interrogated the K14-Rank BC gene data set against known BC and LC lineage differentiation gene signatures from the mouse and human breast18,19 (Supplementary Data 2). In line with the role of basal Rank signaling modulating BC bipotency and luminal differentiation3, BCs from K14-Rank mice showed upregulation of signatures corresponding to mouse LP, mouse alveolar (AV), as well as human luminal AV cells, in particular the basal/luminal (BL) subpopulation (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Fig. 2g). Moreover, we computed a basal RANK signature (Rank-BC) considering the top upregulated (Rank-BC-UP) and downregulated (Rank-BC-DN) DEGs derived from the transcriptomic profile of K14-Rank BCs (vs K14-Ctrl BCs) (Supplementary Data 1, see Methods for details). Rank-BC gene signature correlated with luminal /alveolar-secretory progenitor (LASP) cells from healthy donors of the human breast cancer atlas (HBCA), including the LASP2 cluster characterized by mixed lineage expression20 (Supplementary Fig. 2h). In these datasets, Rankl is expressed by hormone-sensing LCs (Supplementary Fig. 2i, j). Meanwhile, K14-RankΔ/Δ BCs showed downregulation of gene signatures associated with both BC and LC lineages when compared to corresponding controls (Fig. 1k).

Then, we aimed to understand how the modulation of BC Rank signaling affects LCs. Gfp- LCs isolated from K14-Rank and K14-RankΔ/Δ mice showed none or few DEGs when compared to K14-Ctrl LCs, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2k). Nonetheless, these Gfp- LCs shifted their identity when evaluated against known mammary epithelial lineage differentiation gene signatures18 (Supplementary Fig. 2l).

These results demonstrate that the modulation of Rank signaling in BCs alters not only BC, but also LC identity, not reflected by an obvious phenotype in the MGs from virgin K14-RankΔ/Δ3 and K14-Rank mice (Fig. 1b–f and Supplementary Fig. 1b–j).

BC-specific enhanced Rank signaling interferes with MG functionality

Considering the transcriptomic changes identified in BCs and LCs from virgin K14-Rank mice, we postulated that their MG functionality might be affected. To address the implications of ectopic basal Rank expression on lactation, we submitted K14-Rank females to a cycle of pregnancy and included untreated (UT) K14-Rank females to account for putative side effects related to K14-associated leakiness3 (Fig. 2a). Near one third of the K14-Rank females lost their litter (Fig. 2b) and K14-Rank nursed-pups presented reduced weight compared to pups from K14-Ctrl and UT females, indicative of defective lactation (Fig. 2c). Whole-mount and histological analysis at L1 revealed less alveolar coverage in K14-Rank than in K14-Ctrl MGs, coupled with an expansion of BCs (Fig. 2d, e and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). K14-Rank and K14-Ctrl MGs presented high levels of BC recombination at L1 (Fig. 2f, g). Intriguingly, L1 MGs from K14-Rank mice showed Gfp+ LCs, with higher Rank levels compared to K14-Ctrl mice (Fig. 2f, h, i and Supplementary Fig. 3c), in line with the gain of luminal gene expression markers by BCs and the shift to a luminal identity (Fig. 1i, j). Moreover, K14-Rank L1 MGs displayed an aberrant epithelial architecture characterized by small alveoli overexpressing Rank, similar to those found in MMTV-Rank females8, and underdeveloped alveoli where only BCs overexpressed Rank (Fig. 2i). Further morphological characterization revealed the presence of preinvasive lesions exclusively in K14-Rank L1 MGs (Fig. 2j). These preneoplastic lesions were present in all K14-Rank L1 MGs and contained Rank+ cells invading the lumen, suggesting a BC origin (Fig. 2j, k). K14-Ctrl and UT K14-Rank mice presented similarly low percentages of Gfp+ BCs and LCs at L1 (Supplementary Fig. 3d), discarding a contribution of K14-related leakiness to the presence of Gfp+ LCs and observed phenotypes. Enhanced BC Rank signaling leads to BC-derived preinvasive lesions and the appearance of B/L hybrid cells

Pregnancy supports MG Rank signaling due to progesterone-driven Rankl expression. To confirm that enforced basal Rank signaling promotes the preinvasive lesions found at L1, we mimicked a pregnancy-like state, inducing Rankl production by treating adult K14-Ctrl and K14-Rank females with medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA), a synthetic progestin that acts as a potent agonist of progesterone receptor (Pr) (Fig. 3a). Analyses of MPA-treated K14-Ctrl and K14-Rank females confirmed Rankl overexpression and proto-alveolar development (Supplementary Fig. 3e, f), validating MPA activity. BCs from K14-Ctrl and K14-Rank MGs sustained full recombination upon MPA treatment (Supplementary Fig. 3g, h). MGs from MPA-treated K14-Rank females showed an expansion of the BC compartment coupled with enhanced BC, but not LC, proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 3i–l). Indeed, MPA treatment boosted the incidence of preinvasive lesions in K14-Rank MGs, with full penetrance as observed at L1 (Fig. 3b and Fig. 2j). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) analyses revealed that these preneoplastic lesions originated from BCs, as they were positive for Rank and Gfp expression (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 4a–c). Cells within the lesions did no longer expressed BC/myoepithelial markers such as Ck5 and Sma1 (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e) but gained the expression of LC and LP markers (Ck8 and Ck6a) (Supplementary Fig. 4f), indicative of lineage identity dysregulation. Moreover, cells within the preinvasive lesions showed a B/L hybrid phenotype as they co-expressed Ck14, Ck8, and Gfp (Fig. 3c). Indeed, flow cytometry analysis of MPA-treated K14-Rank MGs unveiled a distinct Gfp+ B/L hybrid population (CD24hi, CD49fhi), absent in MPA-treated K14-Ctrl MGs (Fig. 3d).

Altogether, our data indicate that, upon enhanced basal Rank signaling, BCs lose their BC-restricted lineage specification and generate B/L hybrid cells that could give rise to BC-derived intraductal preinvasive lesions.

Enhanced Rank signaling leads to BC-derived mammary tumor development

The loss of restricted cell identity has been recently associated with breast cancer risk21,22. Since the preinvasive lesions formed by BC-derived Rank+ hybrid cells resembled DCIS, precursors of invasive breast cancer23, we hypothesized that they would eventually progress to invasive adenocarcinomas. Indeed, enhanced basal Rank signaling led to spontaneous mammary tumors in aged (approx. 70-week-old) virgin K14-Rank females. Whereas all K14-Ctrl females died without palpable mammary tumors, 60% of K14-Rank females developed MG tumors (Fig. 3e, f). Aged K14-Rank females showed multiple BC-derived mammary intraductal preinvasive lesions in their MGs, harboring Gfp+, as well as Rank overexpressing cells and cells co-expressing basal Ck14 and luminal Ck8 markers (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Immunohistopathological characterization of aged K14-Rank spontaneous tumors indicated they were Er- adenocarcinomas, positive for Gfp and Rank overexpression, denoting their basal origin (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 5c). Like the aberrant B/L hybrid population in MPA-treated mice (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 4d–f), K14-Rank BC-derived spontaneous tumors have lost the expression of some basal markers while gained luminal ones, being some tumor cells positive for both basal and luminal markers (Fig. 3h). Aiming to gain further insights into basal Rank induced tumorigenesis, we induced mammary carcinogenesis by using MPA and DMBA treatment14,24 (Fig. 3i). K14-Rank females showed similar tumor incidence but accelerated mammary tumor onset compared to control mice (Fig. 3j, and Supplementary Fig. 6a). Histological characterization of MPA/DMBA-induced mammary tumors revealed the expected tumor type heterogeneity in the control group, with a 64.7% incidence of adenocarcinomas but also myoepitheliomas and squamous tumors, while K14-Rank females almost exclusively developed adenocarcinomas (91.3%) (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 6b). Half of the tumors from K14-Rank mice expressed estrogen receptor (Er), while K14-Ctrl tumors were predominantly Er+ (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Lineage tracing analysis revealed that most K14-Rank tumors (86.9%) derived from Gfp+ Rank overexpressing BCs; they were classified as adenocarcinomas (60% Er- and 40% Er+) and sustained high Rank expression levels (Fig. 3l–n and Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). Only 3 out of 23 tumors in K14-Rank females, all Er- adenocarcinomas, were Gfp-, indicative of a luminal origin (Supplementary Fig. 6g, h), and 1 out of 7 females developed a lung metastasis, not detected in any K14-Ctrl mice (Supplementary Fig. 6i, j). These results indicate that enhanced BC Rank signaling exacerbates BC oncogenic potential, accelerates mammary tumor development and aggressiveness, and favors the appearance of adenocarcinomas over other tumor types.

Importantly, 46.6% of K14-Ctrl derived tumors (7 out of 15) were also Gfp+, therefore arising from BCs (Fig. 3l and Supplementary Fig. 6e). These Gfp+ tumors from control mice included Er+ adenocarcinomas and Er- adeno-squamous carcinomas (Fig. 3m, n), indicating that BCs with physiological levels of Rank generate luminal mammary tumors. To assess whether endogenous basal Rank signaling is required for mammary tumorigenesis, we treated K14-RankΔ/Δ females with MPA/DMBA (Fig. 3i). The loss of basal Rank showed a protective effect towards mammary tumorigenesis as all K14-Ctrl mice developed tumors (4 out of 4), whereas only 60% of K14-RankΔ/Δ females did (3 out of 5) (Supplementary Fig. 7a). In addition, tumor latency was delayed in K14-RankΔ/Δ compared to control MGs (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Although some K14-RankΔ/Δ females developed adenocarcinomas (Supplementary Fig. 7c), they lacked Gfp+ tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. 7d), denoting that they were originated by LCs. These results demonstrate that BCs with physiological levels of Rank can instigate mammary tumorigenesis similar to LCs, and that loss of basal Rank signaling prevents mammary tumor onset. Besides, the loss of BC lineage fidelity induced by enhanced basal Rank signaling favors BC transformation and breast cancer development.

In regard to the representation of transcription factor (TF) motifs in the differentially accessible chromatin regions (considering unique ATAC-seq peaks), the B/L hybrid population showed substantial differences, with increased chromatin accessibility for binding sites of Fos/Jun, Batf3/Jun, and Nfκb (Rel/a, Nfκb2) TFs when compared to K14-Rank BCs and LCs, followed by enrichment for LC identity TFs Elf1-5 and Ehf (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 8e and Supplementary Data 3).

Of note, BCs and, to a lesser extent, LCs also displayed differential chromatin accessibility profiles of known LC and BC-associated signatures26. Rank-overexpressing BCs compared to BCs from controls showed a clear upregulation of the luminal (LP/ML) chromatin profile (opening LP genes such as: Ehf, Trim46 and Wfdc18) (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 8f and Supplementary Data 3). Similar to hybrid B/L cells, motif enrichment for the TFs Batf3, Bnc2, Nfκb (Rel/a) and Batf/Jun was detected in K14-Rank BCs relative to controls (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Data 3), in line with transcriptomic data (Supplementary Fig. 2f and Supplementary Data 1) and Rank-Nfκb promoting BC bipotency3. These results indicate that K14-Rank BCs remodeled their chromatin and acquired luminal features before undergoing a detectable phenotypic change and transitioning to the B/L hybrid population. Indeed, one of the biggest changes in gene chromatin accessibility when comparing K14-Rank BCs to control BCs corresponded to Ehf (Fig. 4d), whose mRNA was also found upregulated in the transcriptome of K14-Rank BCs (Fig. 1i). The TFs Ehf, RelA, Jun/Fos, and Elf1-5 were recently described by Langille et al.22 as facilitators of basal to luminal/alveolar-like lineage conversion and tumorigenesis upon loss of epigenetic regulators and histone modifiers such as Asxl2. To investigate how enhanced basal Rank signaling remodels epithelial protein programs, we performed quantitative TMT-based proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses on freshly isolated epithelial organoids from virgin 8-week-old K14-Rank and K14-Ctrl females, which reflect the Rank gain-of-function epithelium. Rank-based proteomic profiling revealed that several chromatin regulators, including Kdm4a and Asxl2, together with CK5 occupied lower positions in the ranked distribution of K14-Rank samples relative to controls, whereas histone H1.3, Macro H2A1 and Ezh1, tended to occupy higher ranks (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Data 4). To interpret these shifts at the pathway level, we applied GSEA to the full ranked protein list and phosphosite rankings, which revealed enrichment of pathways associated with chromatin organization concomitant with a non-proliferative state (Supplementary Fig. 8g, Supplementary Data 4). These results suggest that activation of Rank signaling in BCs leads to chromatin remodeling and cell cycle arrest, which could be mediated through the regulation of epigenetic modifiers, resulting in basal to luminal conversion and generation of aberrant B/L hybrid cells.

Basal to luminal lineage conversion is altered by epigenomic targeting

Epigenetic reprogramming plays a critical role in the dynamics of the MG development and the differentiation of the mammary lineages (reviewed in ref. 25). Given that our data pointed to chromatin remodeling as the mechanism underlying basal and luminal lineage infidelity in the context of basal Rank signaling, we then analyzed the global levels of the repressive mark H3K27me3 as it plays a central role in maintaining the mammary stem cells/progenitor pool and in mammary lineage commitment27–30. K14-Rank BCs showed higher levels of H3K27me3 than K14-Ctrl BCs, in line with their luminal-like differentiation, whereas hybrid K14-Rank cells harbored increased H3K27me3 levels than BCs and lower than those detected in LCs (Supplementary Fig. 9a).

We then reasoned that Rank-driven regulation of H3K27me3 may contribute to B/L conversion and the appearance of the hybrid B/L K14-Rank cells. To assess this hypothesis, we made use of two epigenetic drugs, GSK-126 and GSK-J4, modifiers of H3K27 methylation status. GSK-126 suppresses H3K27me3 by inhibiting EZH2 methyltransferase, involved in maintaining luminal progenitors and restricting mature luminal differentiation27,28. GSK-J4 is a H3K27me3 demethylase inhibitor targeting members of the KDM histone demethylase family, including KDM630 but also KDM431, resulting in the upregulation of H3K27me3 levels (Supplementary Fig. 9b). K14-Rank and K14-Ctrl females were treated with GSK-126 and GSK-J4 prior to stimulating basal Rank signaling with doxycycline and MPA (Fig. 4g). In the presence of GSK-126 or GSK-J4, K14-Rank and K14-Ctrl MGs showed decreased or increased H3K27me3 levels, respectively, compared with MGs from untreated mice (Supplementary Fig. 9c), confirming that both treatments were effective in vivo. Morphological assessment and flow cytometry analyses revealed no major alterations in K14-Ctrl MGs, with Gfp expression being exclusively detected in BCs (Supplementary Fig. 9d, e). However, in K14-Rank MG both drugs led to an increase in Gfp+ LCs (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Fig. 9e), suggesting that the modulation of H3K27 methylation levels promotes basal to luminal conversion. Importantly, the percentage of Gfp+ B/L hybrid cells was reduced in GSK-J4-treated K14-Rank MGs while the opposite trend was observed upon GSK-126 treatment (Fig. 4i). We next assessed the impact of these epigenetic inhibitors on the premalignant lesions found in K14-Rank MGs. In untreated K14-Rank mice, all preinvasive lesions contained Gfp+ B/L hybrid cells co-expressing Ck14 and Ck8 (Fig. 4j, k and Supplementary Fig. 9f). In contrast, a drastic reduction of lesions containing Ck14+ Ck8+ hybrid cells was seen in GSK-J4-treated K14-Rank MGs where most lesions contained lineage-restricted Ck14+ Ck8- BCs. GSK-126 treatment also led to a moderate decrease in the frequency of lesions containing hybrid cells (Fig. 4j, k). Altogether, these results confirm that disrupting the epigenetic landscape by altering H3K27me3 directly influences the B/L hybrid population endorsed by basal Rank signaling.

Interestingly, the interrogation of the TBCRC 038 cohort revealed that Rank-BC-UP and Rank-BC-DN signatures correlated with DCIS recurrence (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Data 5). Moreover, pathways previously associated with DCIS recurrence (G2M/E2F pathways, MTORC1 signaling, and MYC targets V1)34, were upregulated in K14-Rank derived BCs (Supplementary Data 1). Then, we evaluated the prognostic potential of the basal RANK predictive signature in IDC samples by interrogating the METABRIC dataset35. Rank-BC signature was associated with poor survival in breast cancer patients from the METABRIC cohort, particularly in those harboring luminal breast tumors (Fig. 5h, i and Supplementary Fig. 10d). Together, our results denote that enhanced basal Rank signaling favors ipsilateral breast recurrence after DCIS, as well as poor survival in breast cancer patients, particularly in those with luminal breast cancer.

Discussion

Herein, we have unveiled that activation of Rank signaling in BCs results in expansion of the BC layer, impaired MG functionality during pregnancy and onset of preinvasive lesions, which eventually progress into breast adenocarcinomas (Fig. 6). First, these results denote a distinct role for Rank pathway in BCs compared to LCs, which, upon Rank overexpression, enter senescence and delay tumor formation7. Instead, BCs lose their lineage fidelity upon enhanced basal Rank signaling, acquiring luminal/alveolar features and losing basal-associated markers (Fig. 6a). Basal Rank signaling has paracrine effects on LCs, which also display unusual differentiation signatures. Aberrant BCs and LCs are then unable to give rise to a fully functional mammary gland, a phenotype not completely penetrant as the one seen upon modulation of luminal Rank signaling3,6. Despite BC-specific depletion not having an evident impact on MG morphology, transcriptomic changes reveal aberrant differentiation signatures.Fig. 6Proposed model showing basal Rank signaling as driver of mammary basal cell identity and breast cancer.a Enhanced basal Rank signaling in mammary gland disrupts BC lineage fidelity through epigenetic factors, driving aberrant luminal-like differentiation. b, c B/L hybrid cells, derived from Rank overexpressing BCs, show enhanced chromatin accessibility generating preinvasive lesions (b) and breast adenocarcinomas of all different subtypes (c).

Proposed model showing basal Rank signaling as driver of mammary basal cell identity and breast cancer.

a Enhanced basal Rank signaling in mammary gland disrupts BC lineage fidelity through epigenetic factors, driving aberrant luminal-like differentiation. b, c B/L hybrid cells, derived from Rank overexpressing BCs, show enhanced chromatin accessibility generating preinvasive lesions (b) and breast adenocarcinomas of all different subtypes (c).

Moreover, exacerbated basal Rank signaling leads to the onset of a basal/luminal (B/L) hybrid population, which co-expresses basal and luminal markers and is responsible for the preinvasive lesions detected in virgin mice and boosted upon pregnancy, aging and MPA treatment (Fig. 6b). In homeostasis, the intrinsic bipotency competence of BCs with physiological levels of basal Rank36–38 is exploited to generate proficient LCs3. Instead, upon ectopic basal Rank expression, a B/L hybrid population originates from BCs predisposed to malignant transformation.

Inherent BC plasticity plays a pivotal role in restoring MG functionality2,3, but also constitutes a driving force behind mammary tumor initiation and progression39–41. Although luminal and basal-like breast cancers were thought to arise from unipotent luminal progenitors42–46, recent evidence denote that activation of oncogenic signaling targeted to basal or luminal populations, drives the activation of multipotency and/or alveolar mimicry prior to tumor initiation, indicating that the dedifferentiation of epithelial cell lineages is key for the formation of multi-lineage mammary tumors21,22,40,41. Here we show that a population of B/L hybrid cells, that shows unrestricted lineage integrity, is instigated by enhanced basal Rank signaling and triggers heterogeneous mammary tumorigenesis, including basal but also Er+ adenocarcinomas (Fig. 6c).

Mechanistically, our results suggest that basal Rank signaling activates intrinsic BC plasticity and favors the onset of lineage-unrestricted B/L hybrid cells by epigenetic remodeling and regulation of chromatin accessibility. RANKL-responsive epigenetic mechanisms have been shown to reprogram differentiation of macrophages into osteoclasts, through chromatin reorganization by Batf1/347, which functions as a transcriptional repressor, whereas Asxl2 and Ezh2 are linked to Rank primary role in osteoclast formation and bone resorption48. Indeed, Batf3 is the top TF identified in Rank overexpressing BCs, concomitant with the loss of the tumor suppressive epigenetic regulators and prior to the detection of B/L hybrid cells. Besides, proteomic analyses point to the epigenetic factors, Kdm4a and Asxl2, which have been recently involved in basal plasticity, alveolar mimicry and transformation of mammary epithelial cells in tumor-prone models22, and the H3K27 methylation modifier Ezh1, as candidate mediators of the B/L switch. The finding that increased levels of H3K27me3 are associated with enhanced basal Rank in vivo further supports Rank regulation of chromatin status. Confirming that basal Rank signaling modulates the epigenetic landscape, critical for cell lineage determination in the mammary gland, treatment with epi-drugs disrupts the conversion of basal to luminal cells. Our data suggest that these epigenetic modifiers, by altering H3K27me3 levels, change the percentage of B/L hybrid cells prompted by Rank signaling, resulting in preinvasive lesions with fewer hybrid B/L cells, probably because they favor cell lineage differentiation and chromatin restructuring. We anticipate that the presence of fewer aberrant hybrid cells would lead to reduced tumorigenesis, supporting the use of inhibitors against key histone modifiers as anti-cancer drugs. In fact, GSK-J4 demethylase inhibitor has already been used in different cancer contexts and shown anti-tumorigenic potential 49,50. The enrichment of macroH2a1 and H1.3, involved in the regulation of facultative heterochromatin51,52, and analyses of the phospho-proteome, reinforce a non-proliferative state and heterochromatin remodeling that prime the basal to luminal conversion. Further insights related to RANK involvement in epigenetic modulation derive from human DCIS lesions in which BATF3 and RANK show a positive association whereas ASXL2 and EZH2 are negatively associated with RANK.

Multiple studies support the pro-tumorigenic role of Rank signaling6,7,14–17. Yet, this report establishes the activation of Rank signaling in BCs as a risk factor for breast cancer. Although it is widely accepted that most breast tumors arise from luminal progenitors, the cell of origin for different cancer subtypes is still a matter of discussion. In our study, we have unveiled that basal activation of Rank signaling is sufficient to drive spontaneous mammary tumors with basal but also luminal-like characteristics through the expansion of a hybrid B/L cell population. Moreover, even basal cells with physiological levels of Rank contribute to tumorigenesis equally to luminal cells and generate Er+ and Er- mammary tumors, indicating that basal cells are inherently plastic and therefore able to generate a spectrum of basal-to-luminal populations ultimately responsible for generating heterogeneous breast tumors. Importantly, physiological basal Rank signaling is required for the tumorigenic potential of BCs.

Despite extensive pursuit of identifying factors associated with DCIS progression, results are limited. Using comprehensively annotated case-control data sets of primary DCIS34, we have found that the basal Rank gene signature is predictive of DCIS recurrence and progression, corroborating that basal RANK signaling endorses breast tumorigenesis in the human breast. Indeed, in an independent cohort of DCIS, the higher levels of the basal Rank signature were found in the Her2+ subtype, which has been associated with ipsilateral recurrence in DCIS53. Upon further validation, this basal Rank signature can be exploited to assess which DCIS is likely to recur, favoring tailored therapies while avoiding unnecessary treatments. Intriguingly, we have unveiled that luminal human breast tumors, harboring enhanced basal Rank signatures, show poorer prognosis, highlighting not only the potential of BCs to originate luminal breast tumors but also the prognostic value of basal Rank signatures in luminal subtypes. This suggests that luminal tumors with “basal” features bear a poor outcome. In this line, the presence of hybrid populations, also called basoluminal tumor phenotype, has been recently linked to rapid disease recurrence in human breast adenocarcinomas54. Together, our results denote that BCs with aberrant Rank signaling could constitute the cell of origin of basal and also luminal breast cancer, underscoring the therapeutic relevance of Rank pathway in breast cancer.

Methods

Generation of the knock-in B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm5(CAG-Rank,-Gfp)Ics/N mouse line

The B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm5(CAG-Rank,-Gfp)Ics/N mouse line was generated by inserting a floxed neomycin selection-STOP cassette, followed by a construct containing murine Rank cDNA upstream of an IRES-eGfp-pA, in the Rosa26 locus. The targeting vector was electroporated into C57BL/6NCrl TB1 mouse embryonic stem cells, and clones were subjected to neomycin selection (G418). Homologous recombinant clones were identified by Southern blot analysis of the genomic DNA digested with the restriction enzymes EcoRV and HindIII (neomycin probe I), Pacl and Xbal (neomycin probe II), and Xcml and SexAI (5’ probe); and using an external probe amplified from the neomycin cassette or from the genomic DNA. The band sizes obtained for the KI band for the neomycin probe I were 10.4 kb (EcoRV) and 8.0 kb (HindIII), for the neomycin probe II, 10.8 kb (Pacl) and 4.6 kb (Xbal) and for the 5’ probe, Xcml (14.1 (KI) and 6.0 (WT)) and SexAI (15.0 (KI) and 6.8 (WT)). Chimeric mice were generated by microinjection into BALB/cN embryos. Germline male chimeras were crossed with C57BL/6NCrl females to investigate whether the recombined embryonic stem cells have contributed to the germ layer. B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm5(CAG-Rank,-Gfp)Ics/N were fertile and did not exhibit developmental phenotypes.

Experimental mice (K14-Rank) carried previous allele combined with the R26Sortm5(CAG-Rank,-Gfp)Ics/N allele were generated by C Mueller. K14-RankΔ/Δ mice were described in ref. 3. Control and experimental mice were treated with doxycycline (1 mg/ml doxycycline (ITW Reagents, #A2951.0025) in 20 mg/ml sucrose (Merk, # S9378)), ad libitum in the drinking water to achieve Cre recombination, as described in ref. 3. For enhancing Rankl expression, 6- or 8-week-old mice were subcutaneously treated with 50 mg/pellet of methyl-progesterone (MPA) (Innovative Research of America #NP-161) for 21 days. For modulating the epigenome (by targeting H3K27 methylation), 4-week-old mice were treated intraperitoneally 3 times per week during 5 weeks with 50 mg/kg of the EZH2 inhibitor GSK-126 (MedChemExpress, HY-13470-50 MG) or the KDM6 inhibitor GSK-J4 (MedChemExpress, HY-15648B-50 MG) dissolved in DMSO/Captisol® (Selleck Chemicals, S4592-5G). Mammary tumors were induced by MPA and 7,12-dimethylbenzanthracene (DMBA) (Fisher Scientific #408181000) as described14,24. Mice were monitored for tumor onset by manual palpation biweekly and measured by caliper once palpable tumors (3 mm diameter) were detected. Mice were sacrificed once tumors reached 10 mm diameter or at humane endpoint. Maximum tumor size was not exceeded in this study.

Whole mount staining, immunofluorescence (IF) and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

MGs were excised and stretched onto a glass slide. For whole mount staining MGs were Carnoy fixed (ethanol 95%: chloroform: glacial acetic acid (6:3:1)) for 2 h at room temperature (RT). MGs were washed with 70% EtOH and distilled water and incubated in carmine aluminum solution O/N at 4 °C. MGs were dehydrated by increased EtOH washes and stored in xylene. Visualization and capture were performed using a Leica MZ10-F stereo microscope. For IF and IHC, mouse tissue samples were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin. For IF, antigen heat retrieval (citrate buffer pH 6) was used on paraffin-embedded sections unless indicated. 5 µm tissue sections were blocked with 10% BSA-5% donkey/goat serum prior primary antibody incubation O/N at 4 °C (primary antibodies: Ck8 (Troma-I, DSHB, 1:400, clone Troma-I), Ck14 (AF-64, Covance, 1:400, polyclonal), Ki-67 (556003, Biolegend, 1:300, clone B56-RUO), Ck5 (905901, Biolegend, 1:500, polyclonal), Sma-1 (A2547-100UL, Merk, 1:400, clone 1A4), Pr (12683667, Fisher Scientific, manufacturer’s recommendations, clone SP2), Gfp (ab13970, Abcam, 1:1000, polyclonal), and Ck6a (PRB-169P, Covance, 1:50, polyclonal). Tissue sections were then incubated with corresponding fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 488 Donkey anti rabbit (A-21206, ThermoFisher Scientific, 1:400, polyclonal), Alexa Fluor 555 Goat anti-rat (A21434, ThermoFisher Scientific,1:400, polyclonal), Alexa Fluor 647 Goat anti-rabbit (A21245, ThermoFisher Scientific, 1:400, polyclonal), Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated AffiniPure Donkey anti-chicken IgY (H + L) (A78948, ThermoFisher Scientific, 1:400, polyclonal), and Alexa Fluor 488 Goat anti-mouse (A11001, ThermoFisher Scientific, 1:400, polyclonal) and Dapi (Merk Life, #D9542, 1:400) to stain cell nuclei for 2 h at RT and mounted with Prolong Gold Antifade reagent (Life Technologies #P36930). Confocal analysis was carried out using a Leica TCS SP5-MP confocal microscope. For IHC, tissue sections were incubated O/N at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Rankl (462, RnD, 1:200, polyclonal), Rank (AF692, RnD, 1:200, polyclonal), Gfp (ab13970, Abcam, 1:1000, polyclonal) and Er (AM (CRET94D/H9), CNIO Monoclonal Antibodies Core Unit, manufacturer’s recommendations, clone CRET94D/H9)) and detected with corresponding biotinylated-streptavidin horseradish peroxidase conjugated secondary antibodies from VECTASTAIN ELITE ABC kit Peroxidase (Vector laboratories, Manufacturer’s recommendations) and revealed with DAB substrate (Vector laboratories, #SK-4105). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. For Rankl IHC, tissue sections were treated with 100 μg/mL Pronase (Sigma-Aldrich, #10165921001) following antigen heat retrieval (citrate buffer pH 6). For Rank, antigen retrieval was performed by incubating tissue sections with Protease XXIV (Sigma-Aldrich #P8038-100MG). For IF analysis, B/L hybrid cells are considered as Ck8 + Ck14+ (co-expression of both markers), while luminal cells are identified based on Ck8+ and basal cells as Ck14 + / Ck5 +.

Epithelial cell isolation and flow cytometry sorting

Fresh MGs were mechanically dissected by scissor chopping after draining lymph nodes were removed and enzymatically digested with digestium medium (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) / F-12 (Biowest, #L0093-500ml), 0.6 U/ml collagenase A (Sigma-Aldrich, #1108879300), 2.85 U/mL dispase II (ThermoFisher Scientific, #17105041), 2% HEPES (ThermoFisher Scientific, #H8357-100 ml) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (ThermoFisher Scientific, #15140-122)) for 1 h rocking (50 rpm) at 37 °C. Samples were washed with CO2-independent medium (ThermoFisher Scientific, # 18045088) supplemented with 2 mM glutamax (Gibco, #35050-061). Single cells were isolated by sequential treatments with 0.25%trypsin / 0.1% EDTA solution (Sigma-Aldrich, #T3924-100ml) for 1 min at RT and dispase II / DNase I (ThermoFisher Scientific, #17105041 / Roche, #10104159001) at 37°C for 5min. Enzymatic digestions were blocked by adding CO2-independent medium (ThermoFisher Scientific, #18045088) supplemented with 2 mM glutamax (Gibco, #35050-061) and 5% FBS (ThermoFisher Scientific, #F7524-500ml). Erythrocytes were eliminated by treating samples with cold (4 °C) ammonium chloride solution (STEMCELL Technologies, #7008) for 1 min at RT. Cell aggregates were removed by filtering the cell suspension with a 40 μm strainer and single epithelial cells were counted manually using a Neubauer chamber. Single cells were blocked with blocking buffer (PBS + 0.5% Bovine Serum Albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, #13391 + 2 mM EDTA) for 20 min on ice. Single cells were incubated for 40 min on ice with the corresponding surface antibodies (CD24-AlexaFluor700 (564237, BD Pharmingen™, 1 μg/ml, clone M1/69), CD49f-AlexaFluor647 (562473, BD Pharmingen™, 1.25 μg/ml, clone GoH3), CD31-PECy7 (102417, Biolegend, 0.25 µg/ml, clone 390), and CD45-PECy7 (103113, Biolegend, 0.125 µg/ml, clone 30-F11)). Prior sorting, single cells solution was filtered by a 70 μm strainer. Cell sorting was performed using a BD FACSAriaTM cell sorter. The markers to specify BC, LC and B/L hybrid cells are based on the surface expression of CD24 and CD49f, commonly used for flow cytometry characterization and FACS isolation. BCs are identified as: CD24low, CD49fhigh; LCs as: CD24high, CD49flow; and B/L hybrid cells as: CD24high, CD49high.

Bulk-RNA sequencing

Sorted cells were directly collected into the lysis buffer from RNeasy micro kit (Qiagen, #74004). Samples were mechanically homogenized by passing through 30 G syringe 5-times, and RNA extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. From total RNA samples, 500 ng with an average rin= 7.4 (range: 6.1 – 8.4) were processed into cDNA sequencing libraries with the “QuantSeq 3’mRNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (FWD) for Illumina” (Lexogen, #015) with a UMI Second Strand Synthesis module. Briefly, library generation is initiated by reverse transcription with oligodT priming, followed by a random-primed second-strand synthesis. While primers from both steps contain Illumina-compatible sequences, random primers additionally feature 6 nt long Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) tags. Libraries are completed by PCR and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 550 (with v2.5 reagent kits) by following the manufacturer’s protocols. This library preparation kit generates directional libraries stranded in the sense orientation, i.e., the read1 (the only read in single read format) has the sense orientation. RNA-seq data has been submitted to GEO ID: GSE272902.

Data processing and normalization

For the analysis of the RNA-seq reads, Nextpresso v255 was used, as follows: UMI sequences within reads were extracted with UMItools56. The resulting FASTQ57 files were processed with bbduk (https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/) for the extraction of adapters, polyA and rRNA sequences. Read quality was verified with FastQC v0.11.7 (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). The derived FASTQ files were aligned to the mouse genome (GRCm39) with Hisat258. The obtained BAM files were sorted with SAMtools59, and the UMItools were employed once again for read deduplication. Htseq-count60 was used to count the number of reads that aligned within genes, using the Gencode vM29 gene annotation61. ComBat-seq62 was then used to adjust the batch effect.

Gene expression analysis

Differential expression was performed with DESeq263, keeping only those genes with more than 10 counts across the samples. Finally, those genes that had an adjusted p-value below 0.05 were selected. GSEAStandard64 was used to perform gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for the selected gene signatures, setting 1000 gene set permutations. Only those gene sets with significant enrichment levels (FDR < 0.25) were considered. A minimal and maximum number of genes per reference were defined as 10 and 500 respectively. GSEA was performed with gene signatures identified by previous literature at a single-cell level18. Summary GSEA bubble heatmaps were generated with ggseabubble65.

ATAC-seq sample preparation and sequencing

For ATAC-seq, 25.000 FACs-isolated cells were washed in cold PBS (500 g, 5 min, 4 °C) and lysed in 50 μL lysis buffer (10 mM TrisHCl pH 7.5, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 0,1% NP40, 0,1% Tween 20, 0.01% Digitonin, and 1x Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail) for 5 min on ice. Nuclei were isolated by centrifugation (500 g, 10 min, 4 °C) and treated with transposase Tn5 (Illumina, Nextera DNA Library Preparation Kit, #FC-131-1024). DNA was purified using MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, #28004). Optimal cycles for library preparation were determined by agarose gel based on nucleosome pattern integrity. All samples were then amplified by PCR using NEBNextHigh-Fidelity 2× PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, #M0541S) with primers containing a barcode to generate libraries. Subsequently, size selection of DNA fragments was performed using Sera-Mag Select Beads (Thermo Fisher, #09-981-123). A 0.7x volume of beads mixture was used to remove fragments larger than 600 bp, followed by the addition of an additional 1.1x volume to isolate fragments larger than 100 bp. Purified libraries were sequenced using NovaSeq system (Illumina) Pair End Run (64 and 150 bp) type at the Genomic Unit of CNIO (Madrid, Spain). ATAC-seq data has been submitted to GEO ID: GSE272968.

ATAC-seq data processing

Sequenced reads obtained from NGS were demultiplexed and quality filtered using FastQC. Subsequently, Bowtie 266 was used for paired-end read alignment against mouse (mm9) genome. Signal tracks were generated with RPKM normalization, using a bin size of 50 bp. For quantification of ATAC-seq data, differential signal analysis was performed at ENSEMBL annotated genes through the integration of R libraries, including ‘csaw’, ‘edgeR’, ‘rtracklayer’, and ‘statmod’. Bam files were used as input with the specific parameters: window width = 150, spacing = 50 and minq = 40. ‘normFactors’, ‘asDGEList’, and ‘estimateDisp’ functions were used for normalization and differential binding analysis using CPM values. Subsequently, the ‘glmQLFit’ and ‘glmLRT’ functions were used for comparative tests.

Visualization of signal tracks was performed using the UCSC Genome Browser, with mean values and a smoothing window between 6-12 pixels. MACS267 was used for calling broad peaks from ATAC-seq experiments. Peaks were result of common peaks between replicates. Differentially enriched motifs were identified using the Sequence Enrichment Analysis (SEA) tool based on the unique peaks in each comparison. The SEA tool (v5.5.5) was run using default parameters comparing sequences from ATAC-seq peaks against control sequences (shuffled input sequences). Known motifs from the JASPAR CORE 2022 vertebrate database were scanned. Enrich TF criteria (enrichment > 1, pval ≤ 0.05).

Epithelial organoid harvesting

Fresh MGs were mechanically dissected by scissor chopping after draining lymph nodes were removed and enzymatically digested with digestium solution (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) / F-12 (Biowest, #L0093-500ml), 2 mg/ml collagenase A (Sigma-Aldrich #1108879300), 2 mg/mL Trypsin (ThermoFisher, #27250018), 5% FBS (ThermoFisher Scientific, #F7524-500m), 5 mg/ml insulin (Sigma-Aldrich, #I6634-50MG) and 50 mg/ml gentamicin (Sigma-Aldrich, #G1272-10MG)) for 30 min rocking (55 rpm) at 37 °C. Samples were washed twice with DMEM / F-12 (Biowest, #L0093-500ml). Organoids were isolated by sequential treatments with Red Blood Cell Lysing Buffer Hybri-Max (Sigma-Aldrich, #R7757-100ML), for 1 min at RT, 20 U/ml DNAseI / 0.5 mg/ml dispase II (Roche, #10104159001 / ThermoFisher Scientific, #17105041) for 5 min at 37 °C, and 5 rounds of differential centrifugations (450 g for 10 sec). Pelleted organoids were collected in Protein LoBind 1.5 ml tubes (Eppendorf, #022431081) and snap-frozen at -80 °C before lysis for proteomic analysis.

Sample preparation for proteomic analysis

As FACS-sorted basal or luminal cells did not yield sufficient protein for deep mass spectrometry analysis, unbiased proteomic profiling was performed on isolated organoids, which constitute an enriched epithelial fraction. Epithelial organoids were isolated from virgin 8-week-old females as described in Epithelial organoid harvesting section. For this study, five independent K14-Ctrl and five independent K14-Rank biological replicates were analyzed. As no in vitro stimulation, culture, or treatment was applied at any point, the resulting proteomic and phosphoproteomic datasets reflect the basal in vivo epithelial state under Rank gain-of-function conditions. Following isolation, organoids were directly lysed for 15 min at 95 °C in 4% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). After cooling, lysates were treated with 10 units of DNase (Benzonase, Merck) at 25 °C and sonicated for 10 min using a Bioruptor to shear DNA. Protein concentration was quantified using a microBCA assay with BSA as the standard. Protein digestion was performed using the on-bead Protein Aggregation Capture (PAC) method with MagReSyn® Hydroxyl microparticles (protein-to-beads ratio 1:5) on a King Fisher automated instrument (ThermoFisher). Proteins were digested overnight (16 h) at 37 °C with 300 µL of trypsin/LysC in 50 mM TEAB (pH 8.0) at a protein-to-enzyme ratio of 1:100 (Trypzean, Sigma; LysC, Wako). The resulting peptides were dried in a speed vacuum and re-dissolved in 100 µL of 200 mM HEPES (pH 8.5).

TMTpro Labeling

Samples ( ~ 100 µg) were labeled for 1 h at 25 °C using Thermo Scientific TMTpro 18plex™ isobaric reagents following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each of the 18 TMT channels corresponded to an independent biological replicate, derived from distinct mice. TMTpro labeling efficiency was evaluated using a pooled sample. After labeling, reactions were quenched with 5% hydroxylamine. Labeled samples were combined in a 1:1 ratio based on total peptide amounts, determined by comparing overall signal intensities in an LC-MS/MS run. The pooled mixture was desalted using a Sep-Pak C18 cartridge (Waters) and dried before high-pH reverse-phase HPLC pre-fractionation. Although a full TMTpro 18-plex design was used to analyze 18 fully isogenic samples, only the data corresponding to TMT channels 126–131 C are reported in this study. For confidentiality reasons, the authors have decided to restrict the scope of this manuscript to this analysis. The remaining samples, although part of the same TMT labeling and MS acquisition run, are not presented here and will be described separately.

High-pH Reverse-phase chromatography

Peptides were pre-fractionated offline using an Ultimate 3000 HPLC system equipped with a sample collector. Peptides were dissolved in 100 µL of phase A (10 mM NH₄OH) and loaded onto an XBridge BEH130 C18 column (3.5 µm, 150 mm length, 1 mm ID, Waters). Phase B consisted of 10 mM NH₄OH in 90% acetonitrile (CH₃CN). Separation was performed using a gradient at a flow rate of 100 µL/min:0–50 min: 0–25% B, 50–56 min: 25–60% B and 56–57 min: 60–90% B. One-minute fractions were collected from 15 to 65 min, neutralized with 10 µL of 10% formic acid, and vacuum-dried. Based on UV absorbance at 280 nm, 40 fractions were combined into 8 pools for phosphopeptide enrichment.

Phosphopeptide enrichment

Phosphopeptides were enriched using MagReSyn® Zr-IMAC HP beads on a King Fisher automated instrument following the manufacturer’s protocol. Eluted fractions were acidified with 10% formic acid and vacuum dried. Flow-throughs from each pool underwent further fractionation into four subfractions using micro RP-high-pH chromatography and were reserved for total proteome analysis.

Mass spectrometry

LC-MS/MS analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer coupled to an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano system. Samples were loaded onto a trap column (Acclaim™ PepMap™ 100 C18, 5 µm, 20 mm length) at 10 µL/min in 0.1% formic acid (FA) for 3 min. Peptides were then separated on an EASY-Spray PepMap RSLC C18 column (2 µm, 75 µm × 50 cm, Thermo) at 45 °C using a 90-minute gradient at 250 nL/min:0–2 min: 4–6% buffer B (100% ACN, 0.1% FA), 2–60 min: 6–33% buffer B and 60–70 min: 33–98% buffer B. The mass spectrometer was operated in a data-dependent mode with a top-15 acquisition method. MS spectra were acquired at 60,000 resolution (200 m/z), covering 350 – 1500 m/z. Peptide ions were isolated with a 0.7 Th window and fragmented via higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) at a normalized collision energy (NCE) of 36. MS/MS spectra were acquired with a fixed first mass of 120 m/z at 45,000 resolution. Ion targets were 3e6 for MS scans (maximum IT: 25 ms) and 1e5 for MS/MS scans (maximum IT: auto). The mass spectrometry proteomics and phosphoproteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD054102 (proteomics) and PXD058134 (phosphoproteomics).

Proteomic and phosphoproteomic data analysis

Raw files were processed using MaxQuant (v2.1.4.0) with TMTpro 18-plex quantification enabled. Database searches were performed against the UniProtKB/TrEMBL mouse reference proteome (21,990 sequences; downloaded March 1, 2023) supplemented with a curated contaminant list routinely used in the CNIO Proteomics Unit. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine and TMTpro ( + 304.2071 Da) on peptide N-termini and lysine residues were set as fixed modifications. Oxidation of methionine, N-terminal acetylation and deamidation (N/Q) were set as variable modifications. A minimum peptide length of seven amino acids and up to two missed cleavages were allowed. Peptide-spectrum matches, proteins and phosphosites were filtered at a 1% FDR. TMTpro labelling efficiency and potential over-labelling were assessed via dedicated MaxQuant and Proteome Discoverer searches of pooled TMT samples. Labelling efficiency exceeded 98%, with <1% of total precursor intensity corresponding to unlabelled peptides. Over-labelling at serine, threonine and tyrosine residues accounted for 4.99% of total precursor intensity (6.46% of PSMs), well below reported values for TMTpro workflows, indicating minimal impact on quantitative accuracy. Reporter ion intensities were log₂-transformed and processed using Prostar (v1.30.0) and DAPAR (v1.22.3). LOESS normalization was applied independently to the proteome and phosphoproteome datasets. Entries with fewer than 18 valid reporter values were excluded. Together, these metrics confirm that TMTpro labelling was highly efficient with minimal unintended STY modifications, supporting the robustness of our quantitative proteomics workflow.

Because TMT-based quantification at the MS2 level (without FAIMS) is prone to reporter ion interference and ratio compression, and therefore not optimized for estimating small effect sizes at the individual-protein level68, we did not apply fixed fold-change cutoffs or multiple-testing–adjusted significance thresholds to define sets of differentially expressed proteins. Although per-protein statistical scores (e.g., p-values) are reported for visualization (e.g., volcano plots), downstream analyses were based exclusively on rank-based approaches that capture coordinated pathway-level trends. For the proteome, proteins were ranked solely by log₂ fold change (K14-Rank vs K14-Ctrl) and used as input for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA; v4.2.3) with the mouse msigdb_v2022.1.Mm collection (GMT format). For phosphoproteome-level analysis, GSEA was performed independently of protein abundance changes. Quantified phosphosites were first collapsed to a single value per gene to enable gene-centric enrichment analysis. For each gene, the phosphosite exhibiting the maximum absolute log₂ fold change across conditions was selected, and the signed log₂ fold change of that site was used to rank genes. This strategy captures the dominant phosphorylation change per protein while avoiding dilution or cancellation effects arising from phosphosites with opposing regulation on the same protein.

Protein-level fold changes were not used to normalize, weight, or adjust phosphoproteomic rankings, even when the corresponding protein was detected in the global proteome dataset. Proteome and phosphoproteome GSEA analyses were therefore conducted as independent analyses reflecting changes in protein abundance and phosphorylation state, respectively. All GSEA analyses were performed using the full ranked lists and the complete set of quantified proteins or phosphoproteins as background, consistent with a rank-based framework designed to detect coordinated pathway-level shifts rather than individual differentially expressed entities.

Generation of BC and LC predictive Rank associated gene signatures

Rank associated gene signatures from BCs was generated from transcriptomic data of K14-Rank BCs (vs K14-Ctrl BCs) (Supplementary Data 1). Genes were clustered in upregulated and downregulated (FC > 0; FC < 0). Clustered genes were sorted based on adjusted p-value. The top 100 upregulated and downregulated genes with smallest p-value were considered associated Rank-BC-UP and Rank-BC-DN gene signature. Basal RANK signature (Rank-BC) was generated by combining Rank-BC-UP and Rank-BC-DN gene signatures. The BC-DN genes were included as negative.

Public data analysis

For the analyses of the Human Breast Atlas20 the tl.score_genes function in Scanpy calculates a score for a specified set of genes by computing the average expression of these genes and subtracting the average expression of a reference set. This reference set is randomly sampled from the gene pool, ensuring that the score reflects the relative expression of the target genes compared to a background69.

The METABRIC dataset35 (n = 2509) was downloaded from cBioPortal (http://www.cbioportal.org/), and TBCRC data34 was obtained from Human Tumor Atlas Network (HTAN, https://humantumoratlas.org). K14-Rank BC signatures (up- or down-regulated DEGs vs. K14-Ctrl BCs) were used to assign a score to each patient using single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA)70. BC-DN score was subtracted from BC-UP score, and a global BC signature score was used. Patients were then ranked based on their signature score, and highest and lowest quartiles (Q1 and Q3) were compared for the clinical outcome of interest (survival or recurrence). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to visualize the differences in outcome, and the association of Rank signature with time to recurrence or death was quantified by the Cox Proportional Hazard model.

Gene expression analyses of DCIS and IDC

Previously published gene expression data from 57 DCIS and 313 IDC were obtained and PAM50 subtyping was performed as described in ref. 23. Signature Z-scores were calculated as the mean of the standardized gene expression of all genes within the Rank-BC-UP and Rank-BC-DN signatures present in the dataset. The Rank-BC-DN genes were included as negative numbers. Correlation between gene centred expression data of the PAM50 genes and the basal-like centroid was calculated for each tumor sample using Spearman correlation as described in refs. 23,33.

Statistics & reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software v.10. Data are represented as the mean +/- SEM. The measurements were taken from different mice, and we avoided technical replicates. Statistical analyses are described in figure legends. All investigators were blind to the time-point and treatment arms. Quantification of IF / IHC and data analysis was performed without knowledge of the identity of the samples.
