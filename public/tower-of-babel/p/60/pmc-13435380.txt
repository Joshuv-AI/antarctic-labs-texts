Bronchopleural fistula following sleeve resection for bronchial adenoid cystic carcinoma—a case report
Authors: Quan Yang, Wei Dai, Xing Wei, Qiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTracheobronchial adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) is a rare malignant airway tumor characterized by slow growth but marked submucosal extension, which often makes complete resection difficult. When repeated positive margins are encountered intraoperatively, the surgeon must balance oncologic radicality against preservation of lung function and the safety of airway reconstruction. Postoperative bronchopleural fistula (BPF) is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening complication after bronchial reconstruction.Case presentationA 33-year-old female was admitted with a chief complaint ofpersistent cough with hemoptysis and a tracheobronchial mass identified 1 year earlier, with symptom aggravation over the preceding three months. Fiberoptic bronchoscopy performed at an outside hospital revealed a mass arising from the bronchial wall. After excluding surgical contraindications at our institution, the patient underwent da Vinci robot-assisted right intermediate bronchial sleeve resection. Intraoperative frozen sections repeatedly demonstrated positive bronchial margins, and a function-preserving R1 sleeve resection was ultimately performed. On postoperative day 18, the patient developed a bronchopleural fistula at the anastomotic site, which was successfully managed with Chest tube drainage alone, resulting in significant symptom relief. At 5-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no radiological or bronchoscopic evidence of tumor recurrence or persistent fistula.ConclusionsThis case illustrates two key decision points in the management of tracheobronchial ACC. First, when R0 resection is anatomically difficult, function-preserving R1 sleeve resection followed by planned adjuvant radiotherapy may represent a reasonable alternative to pneumonectomy in selected patients. Second, postoperative anastomotic BPF does not invariably require endoscopic or surgical intervention; in carefully selected patients with a small fistula, adequate pleural drainage, preserved lung re-expansion, and no uncontrolled infection, conservative management can be effective.

Introduction

Primary tracheal tumors are rare, and adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) is among the most common histologic subtypes [1–2]. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment when technically feasible [3]. However, despite ACC often being characterized as a low-grade malignancy, its clinical behavior is distinctive, manifesting as longitudinal submucosal spread, perineural invasion, and extension beyond the gross tumor margin [4]. Pursuit of a microscopically negative margin may require extensive airway resection or even pneumonectomy, which may result in major functional loss and potentially compromise the safety of airway reconstruction [5]. Several series have suggested that incomplete resection followed by postoperative radiotherapy may still provide meaningful local control in selected patients with tracheobronchial ACC [6–8]. Accordingly, operative planning often requires individualized judgment rather than rigid adherence to a single surgical principle.

Bronchopleural fistula (BPF) is a serious complication after pulmonary resection or airway reconstruction [9]. Its management depends on fistula size, timing of onset, infection status, pleural space control, and the patient’s overall clinical condition. While endoscopic and surgical interventions play an important role in many cases, selected small fistulas may heal with adequate drainage and close surveillance [10–11]. In this report, we present a patient with tracheobronchial ACC who underwent function-preserving robot-assisted bronchial sleeve resection with microscopically positive margins and subsequently developed a small anastomotic BPF that was successfully treated conservatively. The purpose of this report is to highlight the clinical reasoning that linked oncologic decision-making and postoperative complication management in a single treatment pathway.

Case Presentation

Patient information

A 33-year-old woman was admitted on March 17, 2025, because of persistent cough and intermittent hemoptysis. A tracheobronchial lesion had been detected more than 1 year earlier, and the symptoms had worsened during the preceding 3 months. She had no history of smoking and no significant comorbidities. On physical examination, localized wheezing was heard over the right lung field.

Diagnostic assessment

Bronchoscopy performed at an outside hospital revealed a friable endobronchial mass, and biopsy suggested a salivary gland-type tumor. After admission, contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography demonstrated a mildly enhancing soft tissue lesion in the distal right main bronchus extending toward the opening of the intermediate bronchus, without evident mediastinal lymphadenopathy (Figure 1A). Repeat bronchoscopy at our institution confirmed an elevated, easily bleeding neoplasm extending from the distal right main bronchus to the intermediate bronchus (Figure 1B). Histopathologic review of the biopsy specimen, together with immunophenotypic features, supported the diagnosis of adenoid cystic carcinoma.

Routine laboratory tests and coagulation studies were unremarkable. Pulmonary function testing showed mild-to-moderate small-airway airflow limitation with mildly reduced ventilatory reserve, but no contraindication to surgery. After multidisciplinary discussion, surgical treatment was recommended.

Therapeutic intervention

On March 24, 2025, the patient underwent da Vinci robot-assisted right intermediate bronchial sleeve resection. Intraoperatively, the right main bronchus and intermediate bronchus were carefully mobilized, and the lesion was resected. Frozen-section analysis repeatedly demonstrated tumor involvement at both proximal and distal bronchial margins, even after additional margin resection. At that point, the operative strategy was reassessed. Achieving an R0 resection would likely have required a substantially more extensive resection, potentially including pneumonectomy. Given the patient’s young age, the anticipated long-term functional burden of pneumonectomy, and concern that further resection might increase anastomotic tension and impair vascularity, the multidisciplinary consensus with the patient’s family was to proceed with a function-preserving sleeve resection and plan postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy. End-to-end anastomosis between the right main bronchus and the intermediate bronchus was then completed, together with mediastinal lymph node dissection. No obvious air leak was detected at the completion of the procedure. Operative time was 195 min and estimated blood loss was 50 mL.

Postoperative pathology showed tumor measuring approximately 1.0 cm × 1.0 cm × 0.5 cm, with positive proximal and distal margins on paraffin sections. Lymph nodes from station 7 (9 nodes), station 10 (2 nodes), and station 11 (8 nodes) showed no metastasis or invasion. Combined with H&E morphology and immunophenotype, final pathological diagnosis was right main bronchial adenoid cystic carcinoma with R1 resection (Figure 2).

Postoperative course

The immediate postoperative course was initially uneventful. The chest tube was removed on postoperative day 3, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 4. On postoperative day 18, however, she developed sudden dyspnea and mild right-sided chest pain. Computed tomography performed at a local hospital showed a right pneumothorax with partial lung collapse, and she was urgently readmitted.

Chest tube drainage was performed under local anesthesia. Her symptoms improved promptly after pleural decompression. Repeat chest radiography confirmed right pneumothorax(80% lung collapse, Fig. 3A), and bronchoscopy demonstrated a 0.3 cm fistula at the bronchial anastomosis (Figure 3B). Importantly, there was no evidence of empyema, no severe systemic inflammatory response, and no major anastomotic dehiscence on bronchoscopy. Laboratory evaluation showed no marked leukocytosis, and C-reactive protein was only mildly elevated.

Given the small size of the fistula, satisfactory lung re-expansion after drainage, the absence of uncontrolled infection, and the patient’s overall clinical stability, conservative management was chosen. The chest tube was maintained, and the patient was closely followed with serial imaging and bronchoscopy. Ten days after drainage, pneumothorax had nearly resolved. One week after discharge, follow-up computed tomography showed only minimal residual pneumothorax and pleural effusion (Figure 4A), and bronchoscopy no longer demonstrated a definite fistula (Figure 4B). Two weeks later, the chest tube was removed because there was no significant air leak during coughing and no respiratory discomfort. At 5-month follow-up after the second discharge, the patient remained asymptomatic and was awaiting postoperative radiotherapy.

Discussion

This case highlights a typical clinical dilemma in the management of tracheobronchial ACC: the need to balance oncologic radicality with the safety and durability of airway reconstruction. Although a few similar cases have been reported, such a complex perioperative course remains uncommon. This case further illustrates several decision points in the management of airway malignancies and provides a representative example of their practical clinical relevance.

The first major decision point concerned the extent of resection. Because of the characteristic submucosal and perineural spread of adenoid cystic carcinoma, the microscopic extent of disease often exceeds the grossly visible tumor margin, and positive intraoperative margins are therefore not uncommon [3–4]. Achieving a true R0 resection may require a substantially wider resection, potentially even pneumonectomy, which would impose considerable long-term functional costs on a 33-year-old patient. In addition, further airway resection may increase anastomotic tension and compromise blood supply, thereby raising the risk of anastomosis-related complications. Previous studies have suggested that, in tracheobronchial ACC, microscopically positive margins combined with postoperative radiotherapy may still provide meaningful local tumor control and survival [6, 12–14]. Given the indolent growth pattern of this tumor and the patient’s young age, this individualized strategy was considered to provide a reasonable balance between oncologic radicality and postoperative recovery.

The second major decision point concerned treatment of the postoperative fistula. BPF after airway reconstruction is potentially catastrophic because it may lead to persistent pneumothorax, aspiration, empyema, and sepsis [8]. However, management should be tailored to the specific clinical scenario rather than determined solely by the existence of a fistula. The development of postoperative bronchopleural fistula in this case may have been related to the complex airway reconstruction required in the setting of the extensive submucosal spread characteristic of ACC, as well as increased anastomotic tension and compromised local blood supply. Conservative management was chosen because the fistula was small (0.3 cm), the patient had neither empyema nor uncontrolled infection, inflammatory markers were not markedly elevated, symptoms improved rapidly after pleural drainage, and imaging demonstrated satisfactory re-expansion of the remaining lung tissue. Bronchoscopy revealed a localized fistula rather than extensive anastomotic dehiscence. Although bronchoscopic intervention can be considered in selected patients with small fistulas and controlled infection [10, 15], the patient in this case became clinically stable after drainage, with a trend toward improvement in air leakage. Additional endoscopic intervention was therefore unlikely to provide clear additional benefit and might instead have interfered with anastomotic healing or delayed the initiation of postoperative radiotherapy. Surgical repair is generally more appropriate for patients with larger defects, persistent air leakage despite drainage, poor lung re-expansion, or purulent pleural contamination [16]. Taken together, these features supported conservative management as a reasonable first-line approach in this carefully selected patient.

The role of robotic surgery also warrants careful framing. Current evidence does not demonstrate a clear superiority of robotic-assisted over thoracoscopic or open approaches for tracheal tumor sleeve resection in terms of anastomotic complications or long-term outcomes [17–18]. Although the robotic platform may facilitate precise airway reconstruction through enhanced visualization and instrument dexterity [19], the risk of bronchopleural fistula appears to depend more on resection length, anastomotic tension, blood supply, and local tissue conditions than on the surgical platform alone [5].

This case offers several practical lessons. First, when margin-negative resection of central airway ACC is anatomically difficult, surgeons should explicitly weigh the oncologic benefit of further resection against the functional cost of pneumonectomy and the mechanical consequences for airway reconstruction. Second, early postoperative surveillance remains essential after sleeve resection, particularly when local tissue edema, extended resection, or margin-related concerns exist. Third, postoperative BPF should be managed using a stratified approach that incorporates fistula size, infection status, pleural drainage, lung re-expansion, and overall clinical stability. Finally, multidisciplinary planning is crucial, because the definitive value of an R1 resection can only be judged within a treatment strategy that includes consideration of adjuvant radiotherapy and long-term surveillance.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, it describes a single patient and therefore cannot establish the superiority of function-preserving R1 sleeve resection over more radical surgery, nor can it determine the comparative effectiveness of conservative versus endoscopic or surgical treatment for postoperative bronchopleural fistula. Second, the follow-up duration remains short for assessing long-term oncologic outcomes in adenoid cystic carcinoma. Third, although postoperative radiotherapy was planned as part of the overall treatment strategy, it had not yet been completed at the time of the last follow-up, which further limits any inference regarding long-term disease control. Therefore, the present case should be interpreted as a report of clinical reasoning and educational value in a carefully selected patient rather than as evidence of a broadly generalizable management strategy.

Conclusions

This case highlights that, in selected patients with tracheobronchial ACC and repeated positive margins, function-preserving R1 sleeve resection followed by planned adjuvant radiotherapy may represent a reasonable alternative to pneumonectomy. When a small postoperative anastomotic BPF occurs in a clinically stable patient with adequate drainage, preserved lung re-expansion, and no uncontrolled infection, conservative management may be a feasible first-line strategy.
