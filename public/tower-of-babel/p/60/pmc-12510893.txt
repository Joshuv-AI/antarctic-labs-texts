A Case of Ethmocephaly in a Clomiphene Citrate Induced Pregnancy: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Musie Negasi Gebreslase, Birhanu Kassie Reta, Awol Yeman, Haftom Guesh, Million Abraha, Dawit Negash, Awash Solomon, Yordanos Birhane
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEthmocephaly is the most severe form of holoprosencephaly, which results from incomplete cleavage of the forebrain during embryogenesis. It is incompatible with life. Here we reported a case of ethmocephaly in a clomiphene citrate induced pregnancy in a 32‐year‐old, gravida 6 woman, at a gestational age of 23 weeks. Upon ultrasound scanning, there were brain abnormalities, like a single dilated ventricle, absent falx cerebri, and facial profile abnormalities like proboscis. After delivery, on physical examination of the newborn, we found ocular hypotelorism, a 5 cm midline proboscis located above the eyes, and absent nasal structures. The patient had used clomiphene for two cycles prior to conception, with no other notable medical history. In conclusion, ethmocephaly is a severe and typically fatal condition requiring early prenatal detection for better planning. While there is no clear link between clomiphene use and holoprosencephaly, further research is needed to explore any potential risks and guide clinical practice.Keywords: clomiphene citrate, ethmocephaly, holoprosencephaly, hypotelorism, proboscis

Introduction

Holoprosencephaly (HPE) is a severe congenital brain disorder resulting from failure of complete cleavage of the forebrain early in fetal development, leading to a single ventricle, fused thalami, absent midline structures, and possible facial anomalies [1]. The facial abnormalities include single nostrils, narrowed eye spacing, and fused eyes with a deformed or absent nose, a single central eye, and facial clefts [2, 3]. HPE is estimated to occur in about 1 in 6000 to 1 in 16,000 live births and in approximately 1 in 250 early pregnancies [4].

A severe form of HPE called ethmocephaly is rare and is characterized by profound brain malformations and extreme facial deformities, such as a single central eye, closely set eyes, a flat nose, and a malformed nose bridge [3]. We reported a case of a severe variant of holoprosencephaly, which is ethmocephaly detected at a gestational age of 23 weeks.

Case History and Examination

A 32‐year‐old woman, gravida 6, para 5 (all alive, 2 via caesarean delivery), presented for her antenatal care (ANC) visit at 23 weeks of gestation. She had attended three ANC visits at a health centre with no obstetric ultrasound scan in the first two visits. During her third ANC visit, an obstetric ultrasound revealed brain abnormalities, prompting a referral to our hospital for further evaluation. The patient was treated with clomiphene for two consecutive cycles preconceptionally for infertility, starting on day two of her menstrual cycle (initial dose of 50 mg for 5 days, increased in the subsequent cycle). She became pregnant after the second cycle of clomiphene. The patient had no chronic medical conditions, did not consume alcohol, and did not smoke.

Clinical examination of the neonate revealed hypotelorism, an absent nasal structure, and a 5 cm proboscis positioned above the eye globes. The ears appeared normal and properly positioned bilaterally; the hard and soft palates, as well as all extremities, were normal. There were no visible vertebral spine defects or hair tuft features. The external genitalia were also normal (Figure 1).

Methods (Investigation and Treatment)

Our obstetric ultrasound scan identified a dilated single ventricle, fused thalami, and absent falx cerebri. The facial profile showed the presence of a proboscis (Figure 2). Other organs were normal, and the amniotic fluid levels were within the normal range.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow Up)

Immediately upon diagnosis of the ethmocephaly at gestational age of 23 weeks, the patient was counseled on the condition and prognosis, but she went home against medical advice. She revisited our hospital after 12 weeks, and a caesarean delivery was performed at 36 weeks of gestation to effect a 3 kg female neonate who, unfortunately, died within minutes of birth.

Discussion

Ethmocephaly is a rare and severe form of HPE, a congenital brain malformation that occurs with failure of the forebrain to properly divide into two hemispheres. In ethmocephaly, this failure to divide is pronounced, leading to a chain of profound structural abnormalities. This condition is marked by extreme facial deformities such as a flat face, hypotelorism, and a missing or severely malformed nose. This results from failure of complete development of the medial structures of the face and brain during early embryogenesis [5].

Etiology of ethmocephaly is mostly unknown. It is believed to be caused by different factors, including a combination of genetic and chromosomal abnormalities that disrupt normal brain development. Chromosomal disorders are estimated to account for 40%–50% of cases, notably including trisomy 13, trisomy 18, and triploidy. Mutations in specific genes are responsible for about 28% of cases [5, 6]. Other notable risk factors include maternal diabetes and maternal alcohol consumption, which are known to disrupt normal fetal brain development and significantly increase the risk of HPE [7, 8]. Certain prenatal exposure to drugs is also a risk factor, specifically retinoic acid and antiepileptic drugs, which can affect neural development and cholesterol biosynthesis inhibitors [9, 10, 11]. In the current case, genetic or chromosomal tests were not done, which we claim as a limitation of the study.

The pathophysiology of HPE may also involve infections such as cytomegalovirus, toxoplasma, and rubella, which have been suspected to play a role by interfering with normal fetal development [12, 13].

In our case, the pregnancy was the result of ovulation induction using 2 cycles of clomiphene citrate, which may have contributed to the occurrence of ethmocephaly, and similar cases have been documented in the literature [14]. Elefthriou et al. reported a case of severe HPE with cleft lip and palate resulting from a pregnancy induced by clomiphene citrate, suggesting a potential link between the medication and the brain malformations [14]. While there is limited direct evidence linking clomiphene citrate to HPE specifically, there are several studies that link ovulation induction with this medication to central nervous system abnormalities, including neural tube defects [15]. Reefhuis et al. investigated the association between clomiphene use and central nervous system defects, specifically focusing on anencephaly, and found an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 2.3 with a 95% confidence interval (CI) ranging from 1.01 to 4.07, suggesting a potential increased risk of anencephaly. The study also identified significant associations between clomiphene and cardiovascular and other malformations, indicating that its use might be linked to a broader spectrum of congenital anomalies [15].

When comparing our case to the literature, most reported ethmocephaly cases share severe midline facial hypoplasia, forebrain malformation, and high perinatal lethality, consistent with our findings [5, 15]. The few case reports describing ethmocephaly or HPE after clomiphene exposure, including our patient, suggest a possible temporal association. However, large studies confirm associations with some central nervous system and cardiovascular defects but provide limited evidence for HPE specifically [14, 15]. This highlights the importance of cautious interpretation: while ovulation induction may have contributed, chromosomal or genetic etiologies account for the majority of cases and cannot be excluded without molecular testing, which was not performed in our patient [5, 6]. If an active substance is not present during the vital early weeks of embryogenesis, the association between clomiphene and HPE may be considered unlikely. Geier et al. showed that clomiphene and its metabolites were absent in the blood of mothers during the organogenesis period, suggesting that clomiphene would not directly affect embryonic development at this critical time [16].

It's interesting to note that the serum concentration of zuclomiphene, a cis isomer of clomiphene, remains at least 10% of its peak level 28 days after a single 50 mg dose, suggesting that repeated administration of clomiphene at 28‐day intervals may lead to accumulation of the drug and potentially cause cumulative effects over time. Therefore, inducing ovulation with clomiphene may be more effective in subsequent treatment cycles, despite the same dosage being used [17].

This potential for drug build‐up and prolonged presence could contribute to the persistence of clomiphene's effects even after the “all or nothing” period, potentially leading to side effects in some mothers. This cumulative effect stresses how important it might be to monitor and adjust treatment to minimize adverse outcomes [14].

The majority of HPE cases have been shown to be female‐predominant, and our case is consistent with this trend. The exact mechanism for the higher prevalence of female cases is not fully understood. One theory is that female embryos can be more susceptible to the disruptions that lead to HPE, while another theory is that male embryos might be lost through spontaneous abortion [18]. Generally, ethmocephaly, a severe and typically fatal condition, requires early prenatal detection and detailed anatomical scanning for better pregnancy planning. While there is no clear evidence linking clomiphene treatment to an increased risk of holoprosencephaly, observed cases suggest the need for further research. Comprehensive studies are needed to investigate any potential teratogenic effects of clomiphene and to inform clinical practice.
