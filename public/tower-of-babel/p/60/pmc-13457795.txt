Hyperperfusion of stroke-like lesions and dilatation of the corresponding arteries are an epiphenomenon and not involved in their pathophysiology
Authors: Josef Finsterer, Sounira Mehri
Published: 2026

We read with interest the article by Song et al. on a retrospective study of the pathophysiology of stroke-like lesions (SLLs), the morphological equivalent of stroke-like episodes (SLEs) (1). The study used magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) to analyze vascular and perfusion changes during acute SLEs (1). In 17 patients with mitochondrial encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and SLE (MELAS) with cortical cytotoxic edema and subcortical vasogenic edema, corresponding to an SLL, MRA showed significant vasodilation of large vessels, predominantly of the middle cerebral artery on the symptomatic side, in 10 patients (59%) (1). Vessel diameter was also increased on the affected side compared to the unaffected side (1). The study is promising but requires further discussion.

First, the study was retrospective (1). Retrospective studies have several disadvantages, such as low data quality, missing data, the inability to demonstrate causal relationships (only associations can be shown), susceptibility to memory and selection biases, difficulties in controlling for confounding factors, and generally lower levels of evidence compared to prospective studies (2).

Second, hyperperfusion in the area of an SLL is a known phenomenon that has been repeatedly demonstrated using perfusion-weighted imaging (PWI), but it does not explain the development of an SLL (3). The cause of this hyperperfusion is unknown, but several hypotheses have been proposed regarding its occurrence. The most likely explanation is that hyperperfusion occurs as compensation for hypometabolism in the SLL area. A strong argument against the hypothesis that SLLs arise from a vascular problem is that ischemia in either the cortex or the entire territory supplied by a cerebral artery would lead to a chronic brain lesion, which is not the case for all SLLs. The course of an SLL can be favorable, without leaving any structural damage.

The third point concerns the description of a SLL as a mixture of cortical cytotoxic edema and vasogenic subcortical edema (1). This characterization contradicts previous studies and also the results of the present study. In two patients, no cytotoxic cortical edema with corresponding subcortical vasogenic edema was found (1). Furthermore, previous studies have shown that the entire area a SLL is characterized by hyperintensity on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), but the intensity varies in apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps (3). ADC maps are inconsistent and can be hypointense, isointense, or hyperintense (3). It should also be noted that cytotoxic edema can be caused not only by ischemia but also by hypoxia, cerebral infections, traumatic brain injury, intoxication, or metabolic disorders. One argument against a perfusion deficit is that SLLs often resolve completely without leaving structural damage (4).

Fourth, the criteria used to diagnose MELAS in the 17 included patients were not reported (1). MELAS is usually diagnosed according to either the Japanese or the Hirano criteria (5,6). Did all included patients actually meet one of these two diagnostic criteria? Knowing the diagnostic criteria used to diagnose MELAS is crucial, as some patients may only meet one of the two criteria, which can lead to false-positive or false-negative results.

The fifth point is that the dynamic nature of SLLs was not considered in the analysis (1). SLLs characteristically expand over weeks or months from their initial appearance until they reach maximum size and intensity (7). After reaching this maximum, SLLs typically regress in size and intensity, resolving either as normal brain parenchyma or as a persistent structural lesion manifesting as focal atrophy, cyst, white matter lesion, toenail sign, or laminar cortical necrosis (7). Since the degree of vasodilation can vary significantly depending on the stage of an SLL, it is crucial to know how long it takes for the vasodilation to resolve. Therefore, it is recommended to repeat MRA examinations to determine when cerebral perfusion returns to normal.

Sixth, it was not explained why there is a contradiction between the fact that the SLLs are not assigned to any vascular territory and the fact that hyperperfusion is detectable in PWI only in the area of the SLL, but not in the entire associated vascular territory. One would expect that the hyperperfusion would affect the entire vascular territory of the middle, posterior, or anterior cerebral artery. This contradiction requires an explanation.

The seventh point concerns mutations that cause MELAS, which can manifest not only in the brain parenchyma but also directly in the arterial walls (mitochondrial vasculopathy) (8). Therefore, it is conceivable that the hyperperfusion is a direct consequence of the underlying mutation and not a secondary effect due to compensatory hyperperfusion in the presence of SLL.

The eighth point concerns the number of patients with bilateral SLL included and whether the hyperperfusion and arterial dilation occurred bilaterally in these cases.

The ninth point concerns the lack of information on the SLL therapy used. Did the patients receive only a mitochondrial cocktail, nitric oxide (NO) precursors, or steroids? Since patients with SLL were presumably treated, it is conceivable that the vasodilation was also attributable to the administered medications.

Finally, we would like to refute the notion that MELAS is always hereditary. Mutations that cause MELAS can also occur spontaneously, which is why the family history is negative in these cases. Knowing whether MELAS is hereditary or sporadic is crucial for genetic counseling. Therefore, first-degree relatives should also be examined clinically and for the presence or absence of a causative mutation. In this context, heteroplasmy rates should be reported, as they are among the determinants of the phenotypic expression of the mutation. Furthermore, the discrepancy between the methodology section, which suggests that only MRI and MRA scans were used for the analysis, and the results section, which also mentions computed tomography (CT) scans, should be clarified. Another limitation of the study is that it does not specify how many of the 17 patients had previously developed SLEs before the patient being examined.

In summary, the dilation of a cerebral artery on the same side as an SLL does not support the hypothesis that SLLs are due to focal cerebral hypo- or hyperperfusion. Strong arguments against the vascular hypothesis include the fact that SLLs are not associated with any specific vascular territory, that cytotoxic edema within an SLL can resolve completely without leaving a structural lesion, and that not all patients with an SLL exhibit vasodilation, as demonstrated in the present study. Cortical cytotoxic edema most likely has a metabolic, rather than a vascular, origin.
