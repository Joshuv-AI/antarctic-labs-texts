Allosteric Modulation of Cannabinoid Type 1 Receptor by ZCZ011: Integration of In Vitro Pharmacology and Molecular Dynamics Simulation
Authors: Tara Chand Yadav, Lakshmipriya Indira Swaminathan, Venkat R. Chirasani, Zhixing Wu, Dai Lu, Bogna M. Ignatowska-Jankowska, Aron H. Lichtman, Sylvia Fitting, Barkha J. Yadav-Samudrala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Over the years, the cannabinoid type 1 receptor (CB1R) has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for addressing various neurodegenerative diseases including HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND). However, the therapeutic application of direct CB1R activation is often hindered by undesirable psychoactive side effects. To mitigate this issue, research has focused on utilizing positive allosteric modulators (PAMs) to enhance the CB1R activity indirectly. Preclinical studies have highlighted the efficacy of CB1R PAMs, such as ZCZ011 and GAT211, in mouse models of Huntington’s disease, neuropathic pain, and, more recently, HAND. Building on this evidence, we employed primary frontal cortex neuronal cultures and whole brain microglial cultures to investigate the direct and indirect effects of racemic ZCZ011 against the HIV-1 trans-activator of transcription (Tat)-induced excitotoxicity. In parallel, molecular modeling and molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using the ZCZ011 enantiomers (R)-ZCZ011 and (S)-ZCZ011, to elucidate their binding profiles at CB1R. Our in vitro studies revealed that ZCZ011 demonstrated neuroprotective effects against Tat-induced excitotoxicity in the presence of N-arachidonoylethanolamine (AEA) in a dose-dependent manner. Interestingly, racemic ZCZ011 exhibited neuroprotective properties even in the absence of AEA, deviating from the classical behavior of a true PAM. This prompted further investigation into the binding profiles of the enantiomers. Molecular modeling revealed that (R)-ZCZ011 and (S)-ZCZ011 bind to distinct sites on CB1R, aligning with the binding profiles of other CB1R allosteric modulators, GAT228 and GAT229. Notably, (R)-ZCZ011 exhibited a higher number of hydrogen bonds and both polar and nonpolar interactions with CB1R, enhancing the stabilization of AEA binding to CB1R. In summary, these findings suggest that (R)-ZCZ011 may function as both a PAM and an allosteric agonist, like GAT228, while (S)-ZCZ011 may act as a pure PAM, resembling GAT229. This dual functionality underscores the therapeutic potential of ZCZ011 in modulating CB1R activity for a number of neuroprotective applications.

Introduction

The cannabinoid type 1 receptor (CB1R) is the most abundant G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) found in the brain. CB1R is known to be involved in memory, motor function, feeding behavior, mood regulation, and stress − and was found to be highly expressed in the cortex, hippocampus, basal ganglia, and cerebellum. , Targeting CB1R through orthosteric ligands such as agonists including Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the primary psychoactive component of Cannabis, has long been known for its therapeutic use, with the earliest evidence discovered in China (2700 BCE), and the first written medical use was found in an Egyptian medical papyrus dated 1550 BCE. More recently CB1R agonists have gained attention for their therapeutic potential in numerous neurological disorders involving neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation. − However, the therapeutic potential of CB1R agonists is restricted due to their cannabimimetic effects such as subjective intoxication, acute memory impairment, effects on feeding behavior, anxiety that may include panic attacks, and decreased activity. ,

Due to the ongoing challenges with orthosteric ligands, treatment strategies have shifted toward alternative targets within the endocannabinoid system such as allosteric modulators. Positive and negative allosteric modulators (PAMs and NAMs, respectively) interact with sites on GPCRs that are topographically and structurally distinct from orthosteric or active binding sites. As a result studies are now focusing on developing and designing PAMs and NAMs of CB1R. − Small-molecule PAMs for CB1R may possibly act better than orthosteric agonists alone due to more selective biological activity and efficacy, making them less likely to cause cannabimimetic side effects. ,, Allosteric modulators efficaciously tailor GPCR activity to mitigate side effects by enhancing functional specificity and restoring receptor signaling. −

ZCZ011 and GAT211 (Figure ), 2-phenylindole compounds, are the first generation of PAMs that target CB1R in in vivo studies. ZCZ011 and GAT211 exhibit PAM activity at CB1R by stabilizing the active conformation of the receptor, leading to both G protein signaling and β-arrestin translocation to CB1R. ,, These compounds are racemic mixtures, meaning they consist of two enantiomers generated by a chiral carbon in their chemical structure. , Several in vivo investigations revealed that CB1R PAMs, including ZCZ011 and GAT211, display promising efficacy in alleviating symptoms in preclinical models of opioid addiction, glaucoma, epilepsy, neuropathic pain, , and Huntington’s disease. A previous study from our laboratory also demonstrated the therapeutic potential of ZCZ011 in a HIV-1 trans-activator of a transcription (Tat) transgenic mouse model. Based on sex and HIV-1 Tat expression, ZCZ011 demonstrated the potential to alleviate the symptoms of HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND), improving motor coordination and recognition memory in female mice only, devoid of cannabimimetic adverse effects.

Chemical structure of racemic (A) ZCZ011 and its structural analog (B) GAT211 and its enantiomers.

In in vitro studies, ZCZ011 enhanced agonist binding due to higher potency and efficacy, even at low concentrations. Importantly, at higher concentrations (>100 nM), ZCZ011 started to compete with CP55,940, indicating that the racemic ZCZ011 acted as PAM at low concentrations and as an orthosteric agonist at higher concentrations. Another study provided information on the binding sites for GAT211 enantiomers, GAT228 and GAT229, at CB1R, emphasizing that GAT228 bound at the intracellular transmembrane helices 1-2-4, facilitating it to act as both a PAM and an allosteric agonist at CB1R, while GAT229 bound at the extracellular surface of transmembrane helices 2/3, enabling it to act solely as a CB1R PAM. These studies prompted further investigations of the ZCZ011 enantiomer (R)- and (S)-specific allosteric modulation of CB1R.

The goal of the present study was to assess the direct and indirect neuroprotective effects of racemic ZCZ011 against HIV-1 Tat-induced hyperexcitability in frontal cortex (FC) neuronal cultures and to elucidate the structure–function relationship using molecular docking and molecular dynamics of both (R)- and (S)-ZCZ011 bound to CB1R, to determine whether the ZCZ011 enantiomers, like GAT211, exhibit distinct PAM and ago-PAM (mixed agonist/PAMs) binding pockets on CB1R.

Results

PRESTO-TANGO GPCR β-Arrestin2 Recruitment Assay

To evaluate the functional activity of racemic ZCZ011 at cannabinoid receptors, the compound was submitted to the NIMH Psychoactive Drug Screening Program (PDSP) at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. Functional screening was performed using the PRESTO-TANGO β-arrestin2 recruitment assay in HTLA cells, which allows for quantification of receptor activation through the recruitment of β-arrestin2 to the receptor. As expected, the reference agonist CP55,940, a potent nonselective CB1R/CB2R agonist, robustly activated both receptors. CP55,940 stimulated CB1R with a pEC50 of 6.63 ± 0.2 and an E max of 89.4% (Figure S1A) and CB2R with a pEC50 of 6.68 ± 0.3 and an E max of 86.8% (Figure S1B). In comparison, racemic ZCZ011 demonstrated weak partial agonist activity at CB1R, producing a bell-shaped concentration–response curve with a pEC50 of 7.09 ± 0.3 and an E max of only 26% on the ascending portion of the curve. This atypical response curve suggests a loss of activity at extremely high concentrations, potentially due to tachyphylaxis or solubility-related issues. Notably, ZCZ011 did not produce any measurable agonist activity at the CB2R across the tested concentration range.

Antagonist controls confirmed the specificity of the assay. Rimonabant, a selective CB1R antagonist, significantly inhibited CP55,940-induced CB1R activation with a pIC50 = 7.01 and an E max = 73.6% (Figure S1C), while SR144528, a CB2R-selective antagonist, suppressed CB2R activation with a pIC50 = 6.86 and an E max = 89.7% (Figure S1D). When tested in the presence of an antagonist, racemic ZCZ011 exhibited a biphasic response at CB1R, consistent with weak agonism at lower concentrations and potential inhibitory effects at higher concentrations. However, due to its low potency, accurate pIC50 values for ZCZ011 at either CB1R or CB2R could not be determined. The observed biphasic profile of ZCZ011 at CB1R may reflect pharmacological complexity arising from the use of a racemic mixture or possibly nonselective effects at high concentrations. It is plausible that one enantiomer acts as a weak agonist while the other exhibits antagonistic or neutral properties, resulting in the nonlinear response observed. Therefore, further characterization using enantiomerically pure (R)- and (S)-ZCZ011 is warranted for full elucidation of their individual pharmacological profiles and potentially to clarify the mechanism of action of ZCZ011 at cannabinoid receptors.

In Vitro Calcium Imaging

To study if ZCZ011 treatment, in the presence and absence of endogenous ligand AEA, produces neuroprotective effects, in vitro calcium imaging was conducted using primary frontal cortex (FC) neuronal cultures at day in vitro 21 (DIV21) (Figure A). A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed a significant treatment effect of Tat [F (3,177) = 73.84; p < 0.0001, Figure B]. Tukey’s post hoc test revealed that application of Tat (100 nM) onto the cultured neurons significantly increased the intracellular calcium ([Ca2+] i ) concentration compared to control (p < 0.001). Pretreatment with AEA (1000 nM) and ZCZ011 (1000 nM) alone did not produce any changes in the [Ca2+] i concentration as compared to the control but significantly differed from the Tat condition (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.0001, respectively).

Further, for concentration response experiments for ZCZ011, a one-way ANOVA demonstrated a significant treatment effect [F (6,304) = 27.62; p < 0.0001, Figure C]. Tukey’s post hoc test revealed that pretreatment of neurons with AEA (10 nM) did not decrease Tat-induced increase in the [Ca2+] i concentration (p = 0.99). However, pretreatment with racemic ZCZ011 and AEA (10 nM) significantly reduced the Tat-induced [Ca2+] i concentration in a dose-dependent manner (p’s < 0.0001). Interestingly, ZCZ011 alone (without AEA) also reduced the Tat-induced intracellular calcium response at 100 nM (p = 0.0001), which is not the nature of a true-PAM and needs further evaluation.

Similarly, to evaluate the role of CB1R PAM and Tat in mediating microglial neurotoxicity, calcium responses of neuronal cultures were assessed after bath application of microglia-conditioned media (MCM) derived from ZCZ011-, AEA-, and/or Tat-treated microglia in the presence and absence of CB1R (rimonabant) and CB2R (SR144528) antagonists. A one-way ANOVA revealed a significant treatment effect [F (5,264) = 32.63; p < 0.0001, Figure D]. Tukey’s post hoc tests demonstrated that application of MCM Tat onto neurons resulted in a significant increase in [Ca2+] i compared to control (p < 0.0001), which was significantly reduced by applying MCM cotreated with ZCZ011 (100 nM) + AEA (10 nM) + Tat (p < 0.0001). The protective effects of cotreatment of ZCZ011 and AEA-derived MCM were completely blocked in the presence of rimonabant (p = 0.93). On the contrary, SR144528 failed to block the neuroprotective effects of ZCZ011 in the presence of AEA, suggesting that the protective effects of ZCZ011 are CB1R-mediated.

Calcium Imaging at a Single-Cell-Resolution

We performed single-cell-resolution calcium imaging in FC neuronal cultures using Fura-2AM as a calcium sensor. Every condition was run in triplicate, and 15 neurons were randomly selected with a total of 45 neurons/condition for further analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on calcium traces. To ensure all traces contributed the same number of data points to the PCA and were comparable, all the calcium traces were interpolated to have the same number of time points. The principal components (PCs) were calculated from the covariance of the mean subtracted and standardized data. Figure A shows the heatmap for all neurons from 55 s to 2.5 min into in vitro calcium imaging for four conditions: control, Tat treatment, ZCZ011 + Tat treatment, and ZCZ011 + AEA + Tat treatment with the first three PCs shown as bar graphs. Tat (100 nM) was bath applied at a 1 min time point, and Figure A shows significant increase in ΔF/F for the Tat treatment, which was reduced considerably in ZCZ011 + Tat and ZCZ011 + AEA + Tat pretreatment. For each PC, a temporal PC was calculated from the weighted average of the full multineuronal time series (Figure B). The temporal PCs were calculated using a windowed version of PCA known as singular spectrum analysis. We found that the first three PCs accounted for over 75% of the variance in the data set (Figure C).

Distinct Binding Characteristics of ZCZ011 Enantiomers

The molecular docking results for the ZCZ011 enantiomers [(S)- and (R)-forms] with the active conformation of CB1R provided critical insights into the binding interactions (see trajectory Movies M1 and M2 for control conditions). The (R)-enantiomer displayed binding to both site 1 and site 2, with binding energies of −6.41 and −6.36 kcal/mol, respectively. At site 1 (Figure A,B and Movie M3), the (R)-enantiomer formed a hydrogen bond (H-bond) with Phe191 and engaged in numerous hydrophobic interactions with key residues, including Ile169, Ser173, Tyr172, Lys192, Leu165, Trp241, Gly194, Ile245, Ala198, Gly195, and Val249. At site-2 (Figure C,D and Movie M4), the (R)-enantiomer exhibited strong hydrophobic interactions, specifically with Leu209, Asp213, Phe155, Ala236, Val228, Ile212, Lys232, Val235, and Leu239. These residues form a hydrophobic pocket that further stabilizes the (R)-enantiomer, allowing it to maintain its binding energy and contribute to its overall binding efficacy.

In comparison, the (S)-enantiomer of ZCZ011 displayed binding energies of −6.59 kcal/mol at site 3 and −6.36 kcal/mol at site 2. While the binding affinity of the (S)-enantiomer at site 3 is slightly higher than that of the (R)-enantiomer, the nature of its interactions is notably different. At site 3 (Figure A,B and Movie M6), the (S)-enantiomer formed a H-bond with His154, contributing to anchoring within the binding pocket. This was complemented by hydrophobic interactions with Val161, Leu142, Leu238, Val234, Phe237, Trp241, Ser158, and Gly157. At site 2 (Figure C,D and Movie M5), the (S)-enantiomer primarily engaged in hydrophobic interactions with residues such as Phe155, Asp213, Ile212, Leu209, Lys232, Ala236, Leu239, and Val235 (Figure and Movie M6).

The distinct interaction profiles of the enantiomers support the hypothesis that the (R)-enantiomer may serve as a more effective modulator of CB1R, exhibiting both PAM and allosteric agonist activity. This may be due to its unique binding to the intracellular TMH1-2-4 exosite of CB1R. Like GAT228, this binding may stabilize a key conformational change, promoting its active rotameric state, which disrupts the “ionic lock” between the transmembrane helixes 3 and 6 and activates the receptormaking (R)-ZCZ011 a nonorthosteric agonist. Additionally, when CB1R is already activated by an orthosteric agonist, (R)-ZCZ011 binding further stabilizes the receptor’s active conformation, enhancing signaling and thereby acting as a PAM. This dual mechanism may be responsible for (R)-ZCZ011’s allosteric agonist profile.

Energetic Analysis of AEA Binding to CB1R in the Presence of ZCZ011 Enantiomers

To further investigate the energetic contributions of the ZCZ011 enantiomers to the binding of AEA, molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MMPBSA) analysis was performed on the equilibrated molecular dynamics (MD) simulation trajectories. The MMPBSA binding energies of AEA to CB1R in the presence of ZCZ011 enantiomers reveal significant differences in the energetics associated with receptor–ligand interactions. The binding energies for AEA in complex with the (R)-enantiomer (Figure E) at site 1 and site 2 are −38.43 and −36.74 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating a robust energetic favorability for AEA binding in the presence of the (R)-form. Similarly, the (S)-enantiomer (Figure E) at site 3 also demonstrated a favorable binding energy of −38.28 kcal/mol, although it was slightly less favorable than that of the (R)-form at site 1. In contrast, the binding energy of AEA in the presence of the (S)-enantiomer at site 2 decreased to −29.92 kcal/mol, indicating a significantly weaker interaction. Notably, in the absence of ZCZ011, AEA exhibited a binding energy of −35.16 kcal/mol, suggesting that the presence of ZCZ011 enantiomers, particularly the (R)-form, stabilizes the binding of AEA to CB1R more effectively than in the absence of ZCZ011. While both enantiomers enhanced AEA binding, the (R)-enantiomer consistently stabilized the agonist’s interaction with the receptor, reflected in its more favorable binding energies.

Enhanced Binding Interactions of (R)-ZCZ011 with CB1R Compared to (S)-ZCZ011

The interaction analysis between ZCZ011 enantiomers and CB1R revealed distinct binding dynamics. Figures F and F illustrate the frequency distribution of H-bonds between CB1R and ZCZ011 at different (R)- and (S)-enantiomer sites. The H-bond analysis revealed that the (R)-enantiomer of ZCZ011 formed two stable H-bonds with CB1R residues Lys232 and Asp213 at site 2. Conversely, at site 1, the (R)-form exhibited a single H-bond formation. Notably, it established strong hydrophobic interactions with Ile169, Ser173, Tyr172, Lys192, Leu165, Trp241, Gly194, Ile245, Ala198, Gly195, and Val249 as well as stable H-bonds with Phe191 at site 1. In contrast, the (S)-enantiomer formed fewer H-bonds at both binding sites than the (R)-enantiomer (Figure F).

We have also analyzed the total number of interactions (polar + nonpolar) between CB1R and ZCZ011 across all simulations. The (R)-enantiomer of ZCZ011 exhibited 800–850 interactions with site 1 and 750–800 interactions with site 2 (Figure S2A). The (S)-enantiomer induced approximately 100–200 less contacts with CB1R at both binding sites (Figure S2B). Altogether, the (R)-enantiomer of ZCZ011 exhibits a more robust and stable interaction profile with CB1R regarding hydrogen bonding and the overall contact frequency. While still engaging CB1R, the (S)-enantiomer forms fewer contacts and appears to exhibit weaker binding affinity, particularly at site 3, suggesting potential differences in their allosteric modulation effects on CB1R.

RMSD Analysis of CB1R and Ligands

To validate the receptor–ligand binding modes and assess the dynamic stability of the receptor, we conducted MD simulations on membrane-embedded CB1R bound to the (R)- and (S)-enantiomers of ZCZ011. We also simulated CB1R in complex with AEA without ZCZ011 and included an apo-CB1R as a control system. These simulations, each lasting 1 ms, provided insights into the stability and interactions within each binding scenario. The RMSD of CB1R in these ligand-bound states showed that the receptor’s structural integrity remained stable throughout, with RMSD values converging between 2 and 4 Å. This limited deviation from the initial structures confirms that the overall CB1R conformation was preserved across the simulation time frame (Figures G and G). Trajectory analysis illustrates the dynamic behavior of CB1R under varying conditions, with (R)- and (S)-enantiomers bound at different receptor sites and the control setups without ZCZ011.

We quantified the RMSD of ZCZ011 in both its (R)- and (S)-conformations across the simulation trajectories. The analysis revealed that ZCZ011, in both the (R)- and (S)-enantiomers, maintained remarkable stability throughout the simulation periods, with RMSD values consistently within the range of 1 to 2 Å. This stability indicates that the ZCZ011 enantiomers effectively interact with CB1R, leading to sustained receptor modulation without significant conformational shifts. In contrast, the RMSD of AEA exhibited increased fluctuations in the presence of bound ZCZ011 enantiomers, particularly with the (S)-enantiomer at both sites, and the (R)-form at site 2, with RMSD values ranging from 1 to 4 Å. Notably, the (R)-enantiomer’s binding at site 2 initially induced dynamics in the AEA molecule, subsequently reducing over time. This observation suggests that the PAM may exert an allosteric modulation effect on the agonist, influencing its binding stability and conformational dynamics. The increased RMSD of AEA in the presence of ZCZ011 indicates that the binding of ZCZ011 introduces dynamism in the agonist’s positioning, which is consistent with the characteristic behavior of allosteric modulators that can affect the spatial arrangement of orthosteric ligands.

Discussion and Conclusion

CB1R, first characterized in the 1980s, , is the most abundant GPCR found in the central nervous system (CNS) that is known to show neuroprotective effects by inhibiting glutamate release. − CB1R is coupled to Gαi, which decreases cAMP upon activation. Additionally, other noncanonical pathways have been identified for CB1R including ERK1/2. Both cAMP and ERK pathways are key regulators of synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory. , However, due to troubled history of orthosteric agonism/antagonism of CB1R, noncanonical pathways of CB1R, including allosterism, have gained significant interest for their therapeutic effects. Allosteric modulation of CB1R have multiple advantages such as targeting selective pathways in a controlled fashion to achieve therapeutic effects and circumventing adverse psychiatric side effects true for both PAMs and NAMs. To date, only a handful of PAMs have been tested in vivo, ,,− out of which only ZCZ011 has been tested for its therapeutic potential for HAND. In the present study, we aimed to investigate direct and indirect effects of racemic ZCZ011 against Tat-induced excitotoxicity on primary neuronal cultures. Additionally, we performed molecular docking and dynamics study using the (R)- and (S)-enantiomers of ZCZ011 to identify their binding site at the CB1R.

In the present study, racemic ZCZ011 was found to have weak agonist activity at CB1R in a β-arrestin2 recruitment assay similar to what was shown in previous studies, , whereas no effect was observed at the CB2R. In the presence of CB1R and CB2R antagonists, rimonabant and SR144528, respectively, racemic ZCZ011 displayed a weak agonist activity; however, at higher concentrations it showed inhibitory effects exhibiting a biphasic response, elucidating the complex pharmacology of racemic mixtures. With regard to neuroprotective effects against Tat-induced excitotoxicity in primary neuronal cultures, racemic ZCZ011 displayed protective effects in a dose-dependent manner in the presence of AEA. Interestingly, ZCZ011 without AEA also showed neuroprotective effects at a higher concentration, which contrasts with the nature of a true-PAM. ,, This may be due to the bitopic nature of ZCZ011, which resembles another CB1R PAM, GAT211. Both ZCZ011 and GAT211 have similar chemical backbones and consist of two enantiomers, (R)- and (S)-, due to a chiral center. Although these enantiomers’ physical and chemical properties are the same, they may have different therapeutic effects. Therefore, it is appropriate to consider the two enantiomers as two different compounds with different properties unless proven otherwise. The advancement of methods of producing enantiomerically pure compounds has led to a change in the regulatory perspective toward chiral drugs, which has resulted in a decline in the approval of racemic drugs. Studies have shown that GAT228 and GAT229, (R)- and (S)-enantiomers of GAT211, exhibit a distinctive pharmacological profile, with GAT228 acting as an allosteric agonist (ago-PAM; mixed agonist/PAMs) and GAT229 acting as a true-PAM, and since GAT211 and ZCZ011 have a similar chemical makeup it is not far-fetched to think that ZCZ011 enantiomers would also exhibit a distinct pharmacological profile.

The MCM data in the present study also showed that MCM pretreated with racemic ZCZ011 and AEA decreased the intracellular calcium concentration, which was upregulated in Tat-treated MCM. As expected, the neuroprotective effect of ZCZ011 was completely blunted in the presence of the CB1R antagonist, rimonabant, suggesting that the effects of ZCZ011 are mediated through CB1R. Interestingly, in the presence of the CB2R antagonist, SR144528, the neuroprotective effects of ZCZ011 were even more pronounced as compared to ZCZ011 alone. This may be due to the off-target effects of SR144528. Although SR144528 is known to be a selective CB2R antagonist, studies have shown that SR144528 has two off-site targets including the adenosine A3 receptor and phosphodiesterase 5 enzyme. The adenosine A3 receptor is expressed on microglia and is implicated in anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects, which may have exerted the additional downregulation of intracellular calcium caused by MCM SR144528 + ZCZ011 against MCM Tat-induced toxicity. For the in vitro calcium imaging data, we performed a PC analysis, which showed that except for the control group, the first PC was able to explain over 55% of variance for Tat, Tat + ZCZ011, and Tat + ZCZ011 + AEA groups, and the first three PCs cumulatively were able to explain over 70% variance for all the conditions. It is important to note that there were some fluctuations observed in the ΔF/F for ZCZ011 + Tat and ZCZ011 + Tat + AEA conditions, which may be due to the fact that different sets of cultures used for the study could vary in their overall health and network development.

There is a lot of mystery surrounding the binding sites of allosteric modulators at CB1R. Although CB1R was first identified in the late 1980s, the first crystal structure of human CB1R was not solved until 2016, , in which both studies utilized CB1R antagonist AM6538 and taranabant as a stabilizers. In 2022, another CB1R crystal structure was solved along with CB1R agonist CP55,940 and CB1R PAM, ZCZ011. The binding site of ZCZ011 was found to be only 7.9 Å away from the orthosteric site where aromatic residues Phe170, Phe174, Phe177, and Phe189 contain both orthosteric and allosteric sites. Interestingly, a recent mutagenesis study identified nine potential binding sites and it is believed that allosteric modulators may bind at numerous sites of CB1R. In the present study we performed molecular docking and molecular dynamics studies using the CB1R crystal structure (PDB ID: 6KPG for active conformation and PDB ID: 6KQI for inactive conformation) with AEA as the endogenous ligand and (R)- and (S)-ZCZ011 as allosteric modulators. We identified a total of three binding sites: sites 1 and 2 for (R)-ZCZ011 and sites 2 and 3 for (S)-ZCZ011 where site 2 was common for both enantiomers. When compared to the past studies, site 1 identified here is similar to the putative binding site of GAT229 and crystal structure of ZCZ011 and site 3 is similar to the binding site of GAT228. Interestingly, the common site 2 in the present study has been previously identified as the cholesterol binding site. Past studies have shown that allosteric modulators bind to the cholesterol site on human CB1R (hCB1R), , which is believed to be a key regulator of GPCR signaling, suggesting that CB1R allosteric ligands may compete for the cholesterol binding site with endogenous cholesterol.

In the present study we found that only (R)-ZCZ011 bound to the previously known crystal structure site of ZCZ011 with a binding energy of −38.43 kcal/mol, where the thiophene ring of ZCZ011 interacts with active site residues Phe191 and Gly195. (R)-ZCZ011 forms a hydrogen bond with the main chain of Phe191 and a π–π stacking interaction with the side chain Phe191 upon binding, stabilizing the active conformation of transmembrane helix 3. This observation is in line with the mutagenesis study where Phe191 was identified as a critical residue for binding of allosteric agonists. However, (S)-ZCZ011 bound to a spatially distant site 3 with a binding energy of −38.28 kcal/mol, where it formed a hydrogen bond with His154, similar to GAT228. , We also observed that the absence of ZCZ011 reduced the binding energy of AEA, suggesting that the presence of ZCZ011, especially the (R)-enantiomer, stabilizes the binding of AEA to CB1R. Additionally, we found that (S)-ZCZ011 forms fewer H-bonds and has fewer polar and nonpolar interactions with CB1R as compared to (R)-ZCZ011, suggesting potential differences in their modulatory effects on CB1R. Finally, even though the binding sites of (R)- and (S)-ZCZ011 were distant, both remained stable throughout the simulation periods, which shows that both the ZCZ011 enantiomers are effective modulators and influence the binding stability of CB1R, without inducing a significant conformational shift to the receptor.

In summary, these findings suggest that CB1R possesses multiple binding sites, with certain allosteric modulators (e.g., GAT211 and ZCZ011) potentially interacting with more than one site and exhibiting enantiospecific binding properties. Furthermore, the results highlight the significance of ZCZ011, where (R)-ZCZ011 appears to function as both a PAM and an allosteric agonist, similar to GAT228, while (S)-ZCZ011 demonstrates characteristics of a pure PAM, analogous to GAT229. Interestingly, another study reported that both enantiomers of GAT211 may act as an allosteric agonist that differ in their binding sites, which contrasts with our current findings. ,, This discrepancy may stem from the different CB1R models used. While we employed the CB1R crystal structures (PDB IDs: 6KQI and 6KPG), the previous study utilized an in-house hCB1R model based on a cryo-EM structure. However, additional in vitro and in vivo studies are warranted to validate these observations. One of the primary limitations of the present study is the absence of parallel in vitro dose–response data for racemic ZCZ011 and its individual enantiomers in the β-arrestin2 assay, owing to the limited availability of pure enantiomers. Future studies will focus on evaluating the (R)- and (S)-ZCZ011 enantiomers using both β-arrestin2 and complementary G protein-coupled assays to better characterize their binding and signaling profiles. Furthermore, we plan to investigate the distinct effects of each enantiomer on Tat-induced excitotoxicity and their potential neuroprotective properties in neuroHIV mouse models.

Materials and Methods

All experiments were approved by the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC Protocol No. 23.056) and conducted following the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85-23). The method section describes all the experimental designs used for this study.

PRESTO-TANGO GPCR β-Arrestin2 Recruitment Assay

Racemic ZCZ011 was submitted to the National Institute of Mental Health’s PDSP at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill for a functional GPCR assay, which was conducted as previously described. In brief, HTLA cells (a HEK293 cell line stably expressing a tTA-dependent luciferase reporter and a β-arrestin2-TEV fusion gene) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 2 μg/mL Puromycin and 100 μg/mL Hygromycin. HTLA cells were plated in DMEM supplemented with 10% dialyzed FBS in a previously poly-l-lysine coated 384-well white clear bottom cell culture plates at 15,000 to 20,000 cells in 50 μL/well density and were incubated overnight. For a 96-well DNA plate, the DNA plasmid was plated into one well at 0.5 μL/well in 10 μL of assay buffer. The DNA plate contained DNA samples, positive controls (DNA for receptors plasmid with cognate ligands), transfection controls (DNA plasmid for yellow fluorescent protein), and negative controls with buffer only. D2-Tango or V2-Tango constructs were used as the positive control. Two DNA plates were combined into one 384-well plate, with duplicate wells for every DNA and immediately followed by calcium phosphate transfection.

Plated DNA in 384-wells plates were diluted to a final volume of 100 μL with 0.25 M CaCl2 in 1 mM Tris-HCl buffer containing 0.1 nM EDTA adjusted to pH 7.6. The diluted DNA was then dispensed (50 μL/well) in duplicate onto the plated cells and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Odd-numbered columns on the 384-well plate contained Tango assay buffer, which served as a basal control, whereas the even-numbered columns contained drug stimulation solution prepared in sterile filtered Tango assay buffer at 6 μM. On the day of the assay, growth medium was replaced by 50 μL of serum-free medium supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were then stimulated by adding drugs (10 μL/well) and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Next day, the medium and drug solution were removed and 20 μL/well of BrightGlo reagent was diluted 20-fold with Tango assay buffer. The plate was incubated for 20 min at room temperature in the dark followed by luminescence measurement.

Primary Neuronal and Microglial Cultures

Primary neuronal cultures were derived from embryonic day 17 (E17) C57BL/6 mice (Charles River, Raleigh, NC) as previously described. Briefly, the FC was dissected from embryonic brains, minced, and incubated (30 min) with papain (1 mg/mL, Stemcell Technologies) in Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS without calcium, Invitrogen). Tissue was triturated, filtered through 70 μm pore nylon mesh, and plated on MatTek 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (1 × 105 cells/dish). The MatTek dishes were coated with poly-l-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich), washed, and dried a week prior. Cultures were maintained in neurobasal media supplemented with B27 plus (Invitrogen), l-glutamine (0.5 mM; Invitrogen), glutamate (0.025 mM; Sigma-Aldrich), and an antibiotic mixture (Invitrogen) at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. A complete media switch was done 24 h after plating the neurons, followed by 50% media change every 48 h until the day of the experiment. All experiments were performed on neuronal cultures at DIV21.

Primary microglial cultures were obtained from the whole brain of C57BL/6 (Charles River, Raleigh, NC) 0–1 days postnatal mouse pups. The meninges were carefully removed, and the brain punches were suspended in DMEM (Invitrogen) containing FBS and 7.5% sodium bicarbonate. The brain punches were suspended in six-well ultralow attachment plates to prevent the growth of neurons and astrocytes. Cells were grown for 14 days to confluency, and the cultures were treated with appropriate treatments on DIV14. The drug treatments were diluted in BrainPhys culture media (Stemcell Technologies) and an antibiotic mixture (Invitrogen) and collected after 24 h. Microglia-conditioned media (MCM) was filtered through 0.2 μm filters and frozen immediately at −20 °C and thawed right before the experiment. The MCM dilution used for the calcium imaging experiment was 1:4.

In Vitro Treatments

Both FC neuronal cultures and microglia cultures were treated with HIV-1 Tat1–86 (100 nM, ImmunoDiagnostics; clade 267 B), N-arachidonoylethanolamine (Anandamide, AEA, 3–100 nM, Tocris, USA), and ZCZ011. The concentration of Tat was set at 100 nM, based on previous studies − and its ability to induce cellular deficits in vitro that resemble those observed in post-mortem tissue from an individuals with HAND. , Racemic ZCZ011 was provided as a generous gift from Dr. Dai Lu (Texas A&M University) and was dissolved in DMSO and diluted to the final concentration in Hanks Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10 nM HEPES (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). An identical solution without ZCZ011 was prepared and used as control. Cultured microglia were additionally incubated with CB1R antagonist SR141716A (rimonabant, 100 nM, Tocris, USA), and CB2R antagonist SR144528 (SR144, 100 nM, Tocris, USA). At DIV14, microglial cultures were incubated with nine different conditions including: control, Tat (100 nM), AEA (10 nM), ZCZ011 (100 nM), AEA (10 nM) + Tat (100 nM), ZCZ011 (100 nM) + Tat (100 nM), AEA (10 nM) + ZCZ011 (100 nM) + Tat (100 nM), ZCZ011 (100 nM) + Tat (100 nM), ZCZ011 (100 nM) + Tat (100 nM) + SR141716A (100 nM), ZCZ011 (100 nM) + Tat (100 nM) + SR144528 (100 nM). Treatment with antagonists was conducted 30 min prior to AEA or ZCZ011 treatment. Tat was added to the cultures 30 min after AEA or ZCZ011 treatments and incubated for 24 h.

In Vitro Calcium Imaging

Live fluorescence cell imaging was conducted on the cultured FC neurons using a Zeiss Axio Observer Z.1 inverted microscope at 20× objective (Carl Zeiss) in a control environment (37 °C, 95% humidity, 5% CO2). To measure [Ca2+] i , the neurons were treated with a cell-permeant acetoxymethyl ester-linked [Ca2+] i indicator Fura-2AM (k d = 145 nM; 2.5 μM, Molecular Probes) diluted in HBSS (with Ca2+, Invitrogen) containing HEPES (10 mM, Invitrogen) and incubated for 30 min (37 °C, 95% humidity, 5% CO2). After 30 min, the neurons were washed and incubated with the appropriate treatments for another 30 min. Relative fluorescence ratio images were acquired at 340/380 nm excitation and 510 nm emission wavelengths. Tat (100 nM) was bath applied to the cultures at 1, 3, and 5 min marks, and the excitation patterns were recorded for a total of 15 min. For the MCM calcium imaging experiment, 1 mL of diluted MCM was applied to the cultures at 1 min of baseline imaging, and the excitation patterns were recorded for the remaining 14 min. Conversion to [Ca2+] i was calculated using an equation described previously. Fifteen neuronal stomata were randomly selected and assigned as regions of interest (ROIs), and quantitative analysis of [Ca2+] i was performed for each experiment. A minimum of three experiments were performed for each treatment group.

Principal Component Analysis

The neural traces were pooled per condition. To ensure that all the traces had the same number of time points, the data were interpolated to have samples every 6 s. After interpolation and pooling, the data were mean subtracted, and any ROIs that had any missing time samples were discarded. The pooled data was analyzed using PCA. The ROIs were sorted according to the descending order of contribution to the first PC. The same ordering was used to sort the neural traces. Time series capturing the neural dynamics associated with the first PC was obtained by using a windowed version of PCA. The window was chosen such that the vectors capture the most significant neural dynamics averaged over the entire population of neurons for the entire time segment.

CB1 Receptor Modeling

To model the active and inactive states of the CB1R, AlphaFold-V2.2 was used in a template-based approach. The active conformation was built based on the structure 6KPG, while the inactive state was derived from 6KQI. Additionally, any mutations in the original templates were reverted to their wild-type form to allow an accurate interaction analysis.

Molecular Docking of ZCZ011 Enantiomers

The (S)- and (R)-enantiomers of ZCZ011 were generated using OpenBabel and subjected to molecular docking against the CB1R’s active and inactive conformations. AutoDock Vina was employed for docking, with a grid box designed to fully encapsulate the orthosteric binding site, measuring 40 Å × 40 Å × 40 Å. A grid spacing of 0.375 Å was maintained to ensure the precision in ligand placement. The docking protocol generated 20 possible binding poses per ligand, followed by ranking based on a maximum energy difference of 3 kcal/mol. The minimum RMSD between poses was set to 1.0 Å, and the top nine poses for each enantiomer were selected to ensure diversity in the docking results.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations’ Setup

Molecular dynamics simulations were carried out using GROMACS v2021.5, employing the CHARMM36 force field for CB1R and the lipid bilayer. Force field parameters of AEA and ZCZ011 enantiomers were generated using CGenFF. The CHARMM-GUI server was used to construct the simulation system, embedding CB1R in a lipid bilayer composed of POPE, POPC, POPS, and CHOL in a 5:3:1:1 ratio. The orientation of the receptor in the lipid bilayer was estimated based on data from the orientations of proteins in membranes database. , The systems were solvated in a box filled with TIP3P water molecules, and NaCl was added to neutralize the charge at a concentration of 150 mM. The disulfide bridge between Cys257 and Cys264 was maintained throughout the simulation, as it plays a critical role in maintaining the structural stability of the receptor’s extracellular loops. PROPKA was used to assign the appropriate protonation states to ionizable residues under physiological pH conditions.

Before the production run, the system underwent energy minimization using the steepest descent method to remove unfavorable steric interactions. A two-step equilibration process followed this: first in the NVT ensemble for 5 ns, with a 2 fs time step, and temperature maintained at 310 K using the v-rescale thermostat; and then for 10 ns in the NPT ensemble, with a time step of 2 fs, the pressure was controlled using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat set at 1 bar. During equilibration, positional restraints were applied to the protein and ligand, gradually reducing from 1000 kJ/mol·nm2 to zero. The production simulations were run for 1 ms with constant temperature (310 K) and pressure (1 bar) using the v-rescale thermostat and Parrinello–Rahman barostat, respectively. The LINCS algorithm was applied to constrain all bonds involving hydrogen atoms, while long-range electrostatics were treated using the particle–mesh Ewald method. For both van der Waals and Coulomb interactions, a cutoff of 1.2 nm was employed, and the Verlet cutoff scheme was used to handle neighbor lists efficiently. Each ligand–receptor combination was simulated in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and capture diverse receptor dynamics.

Trajectory Analysis

Postsimulation analyses were conducted using built-in GROMACS tools and in-house scripts. The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) analyses were performed to evaluate the system stability and flexibility over the simulation period. Hydrogen bond interactions and nonbonded contacts of activator (AEA) and PAM (ZCZ011) with CB1R were examined in detail. Protein–ligand interactions were analyzed using LigPlot+, while Chimera-X generated high-quality structural visualizations. Trajectory movies were generated using VMD-1.9.4 to illustrate the dynamic behavior of the system further.

To quantify the interaction energies between (R)- and (S)-ZCZ011 and CB1R, MMPBSA analysis was conducted using the gmx_MMPBSA tool. The analysis utilized simulation trajectories generated in GROMACS. Binding free energy calculations were performed using the end-state method, which combines molecular mechanics energies with solvation energies derived from the Poisson–Boltzmann model and nonpolar solvation terms. The results provided quantitative insights into the binding affinities of the two enantiomers.

Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± the standard error of the mean (SEM). For the PRESTO-TANGO assay, the luminescence was recorded as relative luminescence units. In vitro data were analyzed using ANOVAs followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. An alpha level of p < 0.05 was considered significant for all statistical tests. SPSS Statistics 28 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) and Prism GraphPad 10.1.1 (San Diego, CA, USA) were used for data analysis and data graphing, respectively.
