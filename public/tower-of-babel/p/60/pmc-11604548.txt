Immune Shielding of Human Heart Valves
Authors: Marijn C Peters, Arnaud Zaldumbide, Esmee J Groeneveld, Martijn JWE Rabelink, Janneke H Peerlings, Antoon van den Bogaerdt, Carlijn VC Bouten, Rob C Hoeben, Marie-Jose Goumans, Abraham van Wijk
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Visual AbstractKey Words: congenital heart disease, heart valves, immune evasion, tissue bioengineering

The shortage of donor organs is a major issue in transplantation medicine, with waiting lists and donor procedures for heart transplantations and implantation of suboptimal cryopreserved organs (heart valves, skin, cornea). For patients with outflow tract–related congenital heart disease, multiple avital valve replacements are required throughout life to accommodate cardiac growth and/or early valve degeneration. The current best option for young patients with a defective aortic valve is the patient’s own pulmonary valve placed in aortic position (pulmonary autograft), because this valve can grow with the heart and does not elicit immune rejection.1, 2, 3 However, limiting durability of this procedure is the need for a pulmonary valve substitute, a pulmonary donor valve (pulmonary homograft).2,4 In contrast to the pulmonary autograft, homograft valves are avital, cannot grow, and will at some point degenerate caused by immune degeneration by the acceptor. As availability of homograft valves and size of the valve annulus are the main consideration, valves are rarely human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-matched for transplantation. Consequently, the presence of donor cells or cell debris in the donor tissue can elicit immune responses in the patient causing early degeneration, calcification, and immune rejection.5 In an attempt to limit immunogenicity, decellularization protocols have been generated that show reduced immunologic responses in preclinical studies and trial.6,7,8,9 Although they are promising for adult valve replacements, the absence of valve cells is detrimental for valve structural integrity10 and prevents valve somatic growth11 essential for durability in young patients. If we could make cellular human valves hypoimmunogenic, they are likely to be a more durable valve alternative in young patients.

Moving toward hypoimmunogenic donor valves, we can learn from mechanisms in nature that evade immune responses. Cancer cells escape immune reactivity by down-regulating HLA-mediated antigen presentation on the cell surface.12,13 Similarly, during pregnancy, the maternal immune system is tolerant of allogeneic paternal antigens where syncytiotrophoblast cells at the interface between maternal and fetal tissue have low HLA class I and II expression as well as strong expression of CD47, a membrane protein that can interact with several surface receptors to inhibit phagocytosis.14,15 HLA-down-regulation is also induced by viruses to prevent recognition of infected cells by the human immune system.16, 17, 18 Herpes virus human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) remains persistent in the human hosts by strong mechanisms of immune evasion throughout the host’s lifetime.19, 20, 21 This is achieved by the US1-US11 region of the HCMV genome encoding the proteins US2, US3, US6 and US11 that interfere with HLA-mediated antigen presentation.21 US2 was initially shown to cause degradation of HLA class I.22,23 In later studies, US2 was found to intervene with HLA class II antigen presentation by causing the degradation of HLA-DRα and DMα chains in the cytosol.21,24 Alongside down-regulation of HLA-mediated antigen presentation, immune escape of tumor cells was found to be achieved by resisting natural killer (NK) cell and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-mediated killing through blocking the perforin/Granzyme B pathway.25 Based on these immune escape mechanisms occurring in nature, previous studies have targeted HLA-mediated antigen presentation to achieve genetic masking of cells from the immune system.26,27 CRISPR-Cas9-mediated down-regulation of HLA class I and II and up-regulation of CD47 yielded hypoimmunogenic induced pluripotent stem cells.15 To aid successful transplantation of β-islets, combined lentiviral delivery of the HCMV-encoded US2 and serine protease inhibitor 9 (serpin 9) gene blocking the perforin/Granzyme B pathway protected human β-cells from immune destruction after transplantation in pancreatic islets in mice.26 To date, these approaches only succeed in cell engineering and not tissue engineering.26

As living nonimmunogenic autologous root transplantations in children, as we know from the Ross operation, show good durability, we hypothesized that the ability to effectively transduce human donor heart valves with immune evasion genes would expand valve durability and performance. Here, we demonstrate that the lentiviral transduction profile extends from isolated heart valve endothelial cells (VECs) and valve interstitial cells (VICs) to intact, extracellular matrix (ECM)–rich valve tissue. Furthermore, we demonstrate that combined lentiviral delivery of US2 and Serpin 9 genes mediates HLA down-regulation in human heart valves ex vivo at relatively low doses. Finally, we show that US2 and Serpin 9 gene delivery can be utilized to mediate immune evasion from HLA reactive T cells. Taken together, these attributes make lentiviral delivery of US2 and Serpin 9 well suited as a pioneering therapy to address and improve heart valve transplantations in children and potentially find application in other areas of modern transplantation medicine.

Methods

Valve isolation

Aortic valves were obtained from patients undergoing surgical valve replacement at Leiden University Medical Centre (Leiden, the Netherlands) and the Wilhelmina Children’s Hospital at Utrecht University Medical Centre (Utrecht, the Netherlands). Leaflets were obtained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki protocols and approved by the medical ethical assessment committee of Leiden University Medical Centre and the Wilhelmina Children’s Hospital at Utrecht University Medical Centre (Utrecht, the Netherlands, TCBio number: 22-828). For pediatric valves, informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians. Male and female pulmonary valves were obtained from donor hearts processed by the Heart Valve Department of multi tissue bank ETB-BISLIFE (Beverwijk, the Netherlands), following rejection of these valves for clinical use in homograft valve transplantation. Donor mediation took place via The Dutch Transplant Foundation (Leiden, the Netherlands), and permission for research was given for each donor. After removal, leaflets were kept on ice in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher 10313021) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma Aldrich F7524) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (p/s).

Valvular interstitial cells

VICs were isolated as previously described.28 Briefly, leaflets were incubated in VIC culture media (DMEM/10% FBS/1%p/s) containing collagenase type II (Genaxxon Bioscience C4341.0100) for 16 to 24 hours at 37°C. Tissue was homogenized using a serological pipette and filtered through a 40-μm cell strainer followed by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 minutes. Supernatant was aspirated, and cells were resuspended in VIC media followed by 5 minutes of centrifugation, after which cells were seeded in a T25 flask. Culture medium was refreshed every 48 hours, and cells from passage 2 to 8 were used for experiments.

Valvular endothelial cells

Human aortic VECs were isolated as previously described.28 As tissue was obtained from surgical waste material and endothelial cell yield from diseased valves was often limited, additional VECs were commercially obtained (Lonza 00225975) and thawed and maintained according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, VEC media (EGM2-MV Lonza CC-3202) was equilibrated to 37°C while the cryovial was thawed in 37°C water bath. After careful mixing of the cell suspension, VECs were seeded in a T25 flask. At a confluence of 70% to 85%, VECs were passaged by washing with HEPES buffered saline solution (Lonza CC-5034) and trypsinization in Trypsin/EDTA (Lonza CC-5034). VECs were characterized as positive for CD31 and vWF expression and as negative for Vimentin and ⍺SMA. Cells from passage 4 to 9 were used for experiments.

DNA constructs

pLV-CMV-US2-bc-Spi9 was generated from 2 intermediate cloning vectors (pLV-CMV-US2-bc-GFP29 and pLV-CMV-Spi9-bc-GFP26) as previously described.26,30 In short, pJET-Spi9 vector has been generated by cloning a Serpin 9 (Spi9) cDNA PCR fragment into pJET2.1/blunt vector (Fermentas, St Leon-Rot, Germany) using the following primers: Spi9 Fw 5′-TACATAAGGTTACACTAT-3′ and Spi9 Rv 5′-AACCCTTGTGTTAAGTAA-3′ on human placental cDNA (Agilent Technologies). Using a NotI/XbaI Spi9 containing fragment, pJET-Spi9 was cloned into a pLV-CMV-bc-GFP vector. To generate the combined vector, the green fluorescent protein (GFP) fragment from pLV-CMV-US2-bc-GFP was removed after NsiI/MscI digestion and blunting reaction and replaced by the SmaI/SmaI Spi9 encoding fragment of pLV-CMV-Spi9-bc-GFP.

Lentivirus production and transduction

Lentiviruses were produced in 293T cells with a third-generation production system as previously described.31 Virus titer was quantified using antigen capture enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) on HIV p24 levels (Zeptometrix). Cells were transduced with pLV-CMV-IRES GFP with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 PFU/cell (2ng p24 corresponding to 2,500 infectious particles) and with pLV-CMV-US2-bc-Spi9 with a MOI of 1 PFU/cell in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich). Directly following transduction, plates were centrifuged at 600 g for 30 minutes to increase transduction efficiency. Valve tissue was transferred to a 12-well plate and transduced with 3.2 ng/mm3 virus in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene for 24 hours before media was changed. Medium was changed every 2 days after transduction. Transduction efficiency was determined after 4 days. To determine transduction efficiency, immunocytochemistry, and flow cytometric analyses were used.

Immunostaining

One day after transduction, media was changed and cells were fixed 4 days later using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Sigma Aldrich 158127). For intracellular antigens, cells were permeabilized in 1% Triton-X100 (Sigma Aldrich 9036-19-5) in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) for 10 minutes followed by blocking in 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Thermo Fisher 10735078001) in PBS for 1 hour. For surface HLA staining, no permeabilization was used. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies in 1%BSA/PBS overnight at 4°C followed by 3 × 10” washing in PBS and incubation with secondary antibodies. After 3 × 10” washing, cells were mounted using ProLong gold antifade reagent (Thermo Fisher P36930). Valve tissue was fixed in 4% PFA for whole leaflet screening with a multiphoton microscope (Zeiss 780 LSM) and embedded in Tissue Tek O.C.T. compound (Sakura, 4583) for cryosectioning with a cryostat (Thermo Fisher, Epredia CryoStar NX50 Cryostat). Following cryosectioning at 10-μm thickness, tissue was fixed in acetone (Thermo Fisher, 177170025), permeabilized in 1% Triton-X100, blocked in 1% BSA, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, incubated with secondary antibodies after washing and mounted using ProLong gold antifade reagent.

Primary antibodies used were: αSMA (rabbit, Abcam ab5694, 1:200), Vimentin (rabbit, Cell signaling 57415, 1:200), vWF (rabbit, Abcam ab6994), CD31 (mouse, R and D Systems AF3628), anti-GFP (chicken, Abcam ab13970, 1:100), HLA-A2-PE (mouse, BD Pharmingen 558570, 1:100), HLA-ABC (rabbit, Thermo Fisher # PA5-104359 1:100). Alexa-555 and Alexa-488 antibodies were used as secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher, 1:200). 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used as a nuclear marker for permeabilized cells and tissue, whereas Hoechst was used as a nuclear marker for unpermeabilized cells/tissue in case of HLA staining. Confocal microscope (Leica SP8, LASx software) imaging was used for cell and sectioned leaflets and intravital multiphoton microscope (Zeiss, Zen black) was used for tissue imaging of whole leaflets. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ32 and Imaris software (Oxford Instruments, Imaris 9.9). For image quantification, an ImageJ macro was designed and used for all images per experiment to quantify GFP+DAPI+ cells between untransduced control and LV-CMV-GFP treated tissue (a mean of 5 images per tissue slice was analyzed for 5 tissue donors [2 adult diseased, and 3 child diseased heart valves]).

Flow cytometry

Cells were detached using Trypsin/EDTA and collected in 15-mL tubes after VIC media induced trypsin inactivation. After centrifugation at 300 g for 5 minutes, cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in 4% PFA for fixation. After 30 minutes fixation, cells were centrifuged, washed with PBS, and resuspended in 0,1% BSA/PBS. After centrifugation, cells were resuspended in 0.1% BSA/PBS containing HLA-A2-PE antibody (mouse, BD Pharmingen 558570, 1:100). GFP and HLA-A2-PE expression was confirmed by flow cytometric analysis using FACS LSRII and FACS diva software (BD bioscience). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo analysis software. Flow cytometry antibodies used were as follows: anti-GFP (chicken, Abcam ab13970, 1:100), HLA-A2-PE (mouse, BD Pharmingen 558570, 1:100), PE anti-human HLA-DR, DP, DQ Antibody (BioLegend, 361715), and Pacific Blue antihuman HLA-DR Antibody (BioLegend, 307623).

T-cell coculture

CD8+ T cells were transduced with a T-cell receptor as previously described and specific for HLA-A2.33,34 HLA-A2 reactive CD8+ T cells were thawed from liquid nitrogen and cultured in T-cell media: Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM) (Gibco 12440053), GlutaMAX (Gibco 35050061), 10% FBS, 1% P/S, and IL-2 (Novartis Pharma, 200 U/mL). LV-CMV-US2-Spi9 transduced VICs and control VICs were plated in an F-bottom 96 wells-plate (2.0 × 104 cells/well). VICs were carefully washed with EDTA, and IL-2 (200 U/mL, Novartis Pharma)- containing VIC medium (DMEM/10% FBS) was added. At a 1:1 ratio, HLA-A2 reactive T cells were added in a total volume of 100 μL. Supernatant was extracted after 4 hours coculture after centrifugation of the plate at 400 g and transferred to a U-bottom 96-well plate in which samples were stored before ELISA.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

MIP1β concentrations in the supernatant were measured using Human CCL4 (MIP-1β) uncoated ELISA according to manufacturer’s instructions (Gibco 88-7034). Consecutively, light absorption was measured using an iMark microplate reader (Bio-Rad).

Statistical analysis

Heart valves from 6 donors from Leiden University Medical Centre, 2 donors from ETB-BISLIFE, and 4 pediatric donors from Wilhelmina Children’s hospital were used. Commercially available VEC lines from 3 donors were used. The number depicted in the individual figures represents the number of independent experiments performed with separate cell batches. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software.35 Normality was assessed using a Shapiro-Wilk test. To compare 2 normally distributed groups, a Student’s t-test was performed. For 3 or more groups and the assessment of 1 parameter, 1-way analysis of variance statistical test was used. Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was used as post hoc analysis to determine significance at P < 0.05. Results are shown as mean ± SEM.

Results

Lentiviruses robustly transduce human VICs and VECs

To develop a gene delivery strategy that meets the need for a specific, high-efficiency, safe approach for clinical translation, we utilize lentiviral gene therapy, a strategy that is successfully employed by several first-in-human studies for ex vivo gene therapy.36, 37, 38, 39 To our knowledge, no study thus far has assessed whether human heart valves can be genetically modified by lentiviral particles. Therefore, to investigate whether we could genetically modify human heart valves, we first transduced the 2 valvular cell types (VECs and VICs) with a lentivirus carrying a GFP reporter gene under control of the human cytomegalovirus immediate early promoter (LV-CMV-GFP) (Figure 1A). Cells were isolated from patients ranging from age <1 to >70 years undergoing surgical valve replacement and from healthy donors after circulatory death at age <50 years. VICs isolated from human valves were positive for vimentin and αSMA indicative of VIC activation under 2-dimensional culture conditions (Figure 1B). Because diseased valves did not contain large VEC numbers after surgical excision and handling, VECs were commercially obtained (Factor VIII positive and αSMA negative), and endothelial identity was verified by positive expression of CD31 expression and vWF (Figure 1B). Four days after lentiviral transduction, VICs and VECs were assessed for GFP expression compared with untransduced control cells. In VICs, stable GFP expression in the nucleus and cytoplasm was found at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 (1 viral particle per cell based on p24 levels) (Figure 1C). Flow cytometry showed 66% ± 6% (mean ± SEM of 3 independent experiments) of the cells expressed GFP (Figures 1D to 1F). Similarly, GFP expression was found in human VECs (MOI = 1) (Figures 1G to 1J). One week after transduction with the lentivirus carrying the GFP reporter gene no signs of cytotoxicity were observed (Supplemental Figures 1A and 1B).

Targeting HLA-A2 expression in an HLA heterogeneous cell population

Previously, we have shown human β-islets transduced with lentivirus containing human cytomegalovirus-encoded US2 protein and serine protease inhibitor 9 (serpin 9) efficiently down-regulated HLA expression and could evade immune reactivity by cytotoxic T-cells after transplantation.26,30 To test whether targeting HLA expression could achieve heart valve hypoimmunogenicity, we first examined VICs and VECs for surface HLA-A2 expression, the most prevalent HLA haplotype in humans.40,41 We found a large heterogeneity in HLA-A2 expression between donors (Supplemental Figure 2A). For clinical application of this new technology, it is pivotal that we can target HLA surface expression of all cells in all donor and of all haplotypes. To test whether, despite this heterogeneity, we can achieve HLA down-regulation, we used our previously developed viral particles LV-CMV-US2-Spi926 and incubated VICs and VECs for 24 hours at MOI = 1. Four days post-transduction, single transduction at an MOI of 1 yielded down-regulation of HLA-A2 expression in some but not all donors (Supplemental Figure 2B). As the use of low viral titer aids clinical application and efficiency in higher fluid volumes, we decided not to increase viral concentration but instead first test repeated transduction in combination with centrifugation-based virus inoculation (spin-transduction) (Figure 2A). Using spintransduction, we observed increased down-regulation of surface HLA-A2 expression with efficient transduction extending throughout the different donors (Supplemental Figure 2C). Surface HLA down-regulation was strongly down-regulated in both older donors (Donors 1-3, age >70 years) and younger donors (Donors 4 and 5, age <20 years) (Supplemental Figure 2).

We next sought to determine the capacity of LV-CMV-US2-Spi9 to genetically modify both valve cell types using 2-time spin-transduction. One week after transduction, we observed protein expression of Serpin 9 and decreased HLA-ABC levels in LV-CMV-US2-Spi9 transduced VICs and not in LV-CMV-GFP transduced VICs (Figures 2B and 2D). Tropism of LV-CMV-US2-Spi9 extended to both VICs and VECs where transduction down-regulated HLA-A2 expression in VICs to 29% ± 3% compared with 77% ± 5% in untransduced control subjects (Figures 2E to 2H) and in VECs decreased to 54% ± 6% compared with 86% ± 6% (Figures 2I to 2L). There were no signs of LV-CMV-US2-Spi9 transduction-induced cytotoxicity, and stable transgene expression was evaluated up to 8 weeks post-transduction (Supplemental Figures 3 and 4).

Lentiviral US2/Serpin 9 down regulates both HLA class I and II in heart valve cells

Next, we investigated whether lentiviral delivery of US2 and Serpin 9 also decreased HLA class II expression on the heart valve. First, we used a polyclonal antibody reactive to HLA-DP, DQ, and DR to investigate HLA class II expression on the VICs, where we observed significant HLA down-regulation after transduction with LV-CMV-US2-Spi9 compared with control subjects from to 51% ± 11% to 16% ± 9% (Figures 3A to 3C). Because the potential of HLA-DR matching of heart valves has been suggested to decrease immunogenicity,42 we specifically analyzed HLA-DR expression after transduction where we observed down-regulated HLA-DR expression to 24% ± 8% compared with 72% ± 12% in untransduced control subjects (Figures 3D to 3F).

Lentiviruses efficiently transduce valve cells in the intact valve leaflet

To investigate whether we could apply this method on the intact valve leaflet essential to enable future clinical application, we assessed transduction efficiency of lentiviruses in the 3-dimensional environment of the valve leaflet (Figure 4A). Human heart valves were used to gauge the overall efficiency of lentiviral transduction across a variety of tissue donors (age, diseased/healthy) (Figure 4B). We applied the same technique of 2-time spin-transduction using 1.6 ng p24/mm3, assuming roughly 40 × 103 cells/mm3 to resemble an MOI = 1, and assessed transduction of intact tissue after 7 days. Using multiphoton microscopy excitation at wavelengths of 760 nm (elastic fibers) and 840 nm (collagen bundles) via second harmonic generation in combination with confocal microscopy, we visualized valve extracellular matrix and cellular GFP expression (Figure 4C). This analysis showed LV-CMV-GFP transduced valve cells within the top layers of the valve (z-stack imaging of 40-100 μm) (Figure 4C). To determine the efficiency of transduction, we made a single cell suspension of the transduced tissue and measured total GFP expression using flow cytometry showing LV-CMV-GFP viral particles transduced 49% ± 11% cells within the valve (Figures 4D to 4F). To obtain insights into the localization of these GFP+ cells within the valve, we investigated transverse cryosections of the transduced intact valve leaflet tissue. We observed robust GFP expression throughout the valve with 93% ± 9% at low viral dose at whole transverse sections to a depth of 700 μm (Figures 4G and 4H). At time of analysis, 1 week after LV-CMV-GFP transduction, we did not observe signs of toxicity (Supplemental Figure 5A).

Lentiviral US2 and Serpin 9 delivery can down-regulate HLA expression in the human valve leaflet

To genetically ablate surface HLA expression in the intact valve leaflet, we used the same method to transduce intact human valve leaflet tissue (1.6ng p24 viral particles) and assessed transduction after 7 days from both adult and pediatric tissue donors, with media changes every 2 days (Figure 5A). To quantify the efficiency of transduction, we measured HLA expression using flow cytometry showing LV-CMV-US2-Spi9 decreased surface HLA-A2 expression to 12% ± 2% within the valve compared with 28% ± 4% in control tissue (Figures 5B to 5D) without causing cytotoxicity (Supplemental Figure 5B). Furthermore, assessment of HLA-ABC levels similarly showed decreased expression of LV-CMV-US2-Spi9 transduced valve (20% ± 4%) compared with control subjects (49% ± 12%) (Figures 5E to 5G). To determine HLA levels in deeper valve layers and include a wider range of HLA haplotypes alongside HLA-A2, we stained transduced tissue with a polyclonal antibody against HLA-A, -B, and -C and assessed HLA+ cells at 140 and 700 μm from the tissue surface using confocal microscopy. The baseline HLA expression measured in the cryosections of transduced valve tissue and the degree of HLA down-regulation corresponded with the measured flow cytometric level (Figures 5D to 5F). Confocal microscopy of the cryosections showed decreased HLA-ABC levels at both distances from tissue surface to 6% ± 1% within the valve compared to 38% ± 5% in LV-CMV-GFP (Figures 5E and 5F).

Discussion

Here, we demonstrate the potential of LV-CMV-US2-Spi9 as a genetic tool to mediate immune evasion of human donor heart valves. By enabling ex vivo modulation of valve immunogenicity at low doses, LV-CMV-US2-Spi9-treated valves could obviate the need for immunosuppressive medication in valve replacements.

Lentiviruses have been used as an approach to mediate stable transgene delivery and are currently being implemented as the preferred method for ex vivo gene correction in clinical trials.43, 44, 45 By delivering, replacing, or silencing genes via nonviral or viral vectors, medical research is making important steps toward curing diseases.46 The development of self-inactivating lentiviruses and third-generation production systems have made it possible to safely deliver genes into the host genome, which has made it to clinical trials in both adult and pediatric patients.36,37,45,47 In this study, we overcome the challenge of efficient gene delivery by robustly delivering genes and altering cellular surface HLA-A2 and -ABC expression without the need for tissue dissociation and subsequent recellularization.26 To our knowledge, only one previous study used lentiviral gene delivery in human valve cells in which a high viral load (MOI = 50) was used to overexpress Akr1B1 to study its function in aortic valve disease.48 Here, we were able to robustly transduce VICs at low viral titer (MOI = 1) using a short period of centrifugation-based virus inoculation, which could be translated to valve tissue, further supporting potential for clinical translation. No previous study reported gene delivery in intact 3-dimensional heart valve tissue. Previous studies have tested lentiviral-based gene delivery via direct injection of a high virus titer into rat muscle and liver tissue where long-term transgene expression was observed.49

Although the Ross procedure is widely used as the golden standard of valve replacement in the pediatric population,1,2,50 the availability of (small sized) homograft donor valves is a limitation in valve replacements in children.50, 51, 52 In addition, the pulmonary cryopreserved homograft does not grow because it is avital. So, the child will outgrow the pulmonary homograft requiring a replacement with a new donor valve caused by the size mismatch. But as a living autograft, the patients’ pulmonary valve placed in the aortic position does grow. With this study, we suggest that a vital donor valve that is shielded from immune rejection might function as an autograft in the Ross operation, requiring less reoperations and therefore less donor valves needed per patient. Important to note, while our approach targets immunogenicity, it does not solve the issue of homograft viability and the possibility for banking of living valves. However, targeting the immunogenicity rather than cellularity of donor heart valves would pair well with partial heart transplantations, a new upcoming and promising procedure to deliver growing valve implants following HLA-histocompatibility principles and immunosuppressive medications similar as in heart transplantations.53, 54, 55 Furthermore, methods for banking living valves are becoming of increased interest and might be within reach with new developments in the transplantation field (eg, organ perfusion systems).56

The issue of donor valve immunogenicity after implantation has been previously studied. Alternatively, decellularization of homografts and in vitro and in situ tissue engineered heart valves currently in development are promising to tackle the issue of immunogenic and nongrowing valve implants.6,7,57, 58, 59, 8, 9, 60, 61, 62 The use of homograft decellularization and thereby removing immunogenic cells has been deemed safe and durable in young adult patients (median age of 16.5 and 30.4 years) 5 years after implantation as tested in clinical trials (ESPOIR [European Clinical Study for the Application of Regenerative Heart Valves]/ARISE trials).8,9 Although decellularized homografts are less immunogenic, it remains an avital tissue valve that is not suitable for application in a heart of a young child. Because the homograft has a dense extracellular matrix, remodeling and in situ recruitment of cells can be challenging and therefore it is not likely and not shown that this valve will significantly grow. Our approach, instead of decellularization or culturing/recruiting cells, would make use of the innate cellular network of the intact human valve as it functioned during the donor’s lifetime while targeting detrimental immune-degenerative processes that could arise from recognition of nonself.53, 54, 55 As long as tissue engineered heart valves are not (yet) available, genetically engineered human valves might fill the gap, while simultaneously, immune evasive strategies, as described here, could have applications in tissue engineered heart valves and xenografts.

As an alternative approach to lentiviral delivery of viral immune evasion genes, other recent studies have achieved generating hypoimmune stem cells through CRISPR-Cas9 knockout of HLA by targeting the coding sequence of B2M, and showed the potency of this method to evade immune recognition.15,63,64 However, B2M knockout did show the activation of NK cells and showed inefficiency in vivo by still maintaining HLA class II expression and NK cell reactivity, and activating γδ-T-cells.34,65, 66, 67 Additionally, the use of US2 (combined with Spi9) provides a clear advantage because one transgene can target different arms of the immune system (targeting both HLA class I and II21, 22, 23, 24), bringing efficiency to the technology.

During our characterization of HLA levels in valve cells, we encountered heterogeneity of HLA levels, illustrated by double-peak flow cytometric plots most likely stemming from high variation in VIC phenotypes and cell size in human valves. The success of this viral-based strategy highly depends on extensive tropism of LV-CMV-US2-Spi9 to decrease immunogenicity of all cells present in the human valve. By incorporating centrifugation-based transduction, we were able to effectively down-regulate surface HLA expression without compromising transduction efficiency at low dosage.

Study limitations

Although our study is using clinically relevant whole human tissue extracts from both pediatric and adult patients and donors, an important limitation of this study is it’s in vitro nature and we will need animal studies to validate our concept under in vivo conditions to proceed to move toward clinical application. In a clinical setting, the proportion of T cells responsive to mismatched HLA has been estimated to be 1% to 10%.34,68,69 With low viral dosage, we achieved 38% reduction of a specific T-cell clone targeting HLA-A2. Although it is difficult to speculate whether this magnitude of reduced T-cell activation is sufficient to aid in postimplantation immune shielding and the graft rejection process in vivo is far more complex than our in vitro immunogenicity assay, our use of HLA-A2 specific T-cell clone reflects a higher effector/target ratio than under in vivo circumstances, making this 38% reduction (using only a multiplicity of infection of 1) an important proof of concept highlighting the potential of this strategy. Furthermore, future engineering efforts could modify additional features including the choice of promotor and concentration could be increased. It remains vital as follow-up of the current study to investigate longevity of the US2 expression and consider replacing the CMV promotor by a version more suited for driving long-term expression (eg, EF1a, PGK, CAG). Furthermore, the specificity of US2 is an important factor to take into consideration22,23,29,70 for the capacity of our technology to completely inhibit valve immunogenicity. In this study we show down-regulation of HLA class 1 and II by LV-CMV-US2-Spi9. Previously, US2 was shown to down-regulate surface expression of all HLA-A and -G and most HLA-B alleles, whereas all HLA-C and -E alleles were resistant to US2-mediated degradation.29 The resistance of HLA-C to US2 down-regulation could explain the remaining levels of HLA observed in LV-CMV-US2-Spi9-treated valves. Particularly the regulation of HLA class 1 on the cellular surface has been suggested to be important for T and NK cell interactions and immune evasion while other HLA loci might aid in suppression of NK activation.29 Whether the specificity of US2 in combination with Spi9 will be sufficient to mediate donor valve immune escape or whether additional factors need to be used should be assessed in follow-up studies including animal studies. Nevertheless, the results of this study combined with the previously reported successful immune evasion of LV-CMV-US2-Spi9-transduced human primary β cells implanted in the renal capsule of mice26 does show the potential of this technology.

Conclusions

We have shown that lentiviral gene delivery can be used as a powerful tool to access valve cells in donor valves and modulate gene expression causing valve immune rejection. Next to the direct implication of our findings on human donor valve transplantations, our findings also provide scope to immune evasion and gene delivery in the transplantation of larger organs and potentially to xenotransplantation.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Despite advances in clinical care for patients with congenital heart disease, there remains an urgent need for a suitable valve replacement for young patients. The field of tissue engineering has not yet succeeded in creating a biograft with growth capacity and still faces issues in clinical translation. The promising upcoming field of partial heart transplantation emphasizes the potential of living donor valves as a valve replacement but still requires immunosuppressive medications. Our study identifies a new promising approach to solve the issue of immune rejection of valve implants.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: These findings show a potential strategy to eliminate immune-degeneration of implanted donor heart valves, increase durability, and optimize the use of donor heart valves. When successful, this new technology will have large implications for modern transplant technology and may prevent the use of immunosuppressants/anticoagulants and extensive animal testing.
