Survival Odds to Minimize Risk Heterogeneity Bias in Heart Failure Trials: Application to Dapagliflozin
Authors: Robin Myte, Andrea Mattsson, Matt Poole, Dustin J. Little, Per Nyström, Alasdair Henderson, Brian L. Claggett, Samvel B. Gasparyan, Scott D. Solomon, John J.V. McMurray
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Patients with cardiovascular conditions like heart failure (HF) often exhibit significant heterogeneity of the risk of clinical events. In clinical trials, large risk heterogeneity can result in an underestimation of treatment effects derived from Cox proportional hazards models. This occurs due to selection bias when estimating the hazard ratio, stemming from a disproportionate reduction of event-free patients in the control group compared with an effective active group over time, ultimately reducing the statistical power. Therefore, it is important to explore alternative analysis methods for outcome trials that are robust with respect to risk heterogeneity.METHODS:We used clinical data from 2 dapagliflozin HF trials—DAPA-HF (Dapagliflozin in Patients with Heart Failure and Reduced Ejection Fraction) and DELIVER (Dapagliflozin in Heart Failure with Mildly Reduced or Preserved Ejection Fraction) to characterize the extent of risk heterogeneity and nonproportionality of hazards in HF. We then evaluated a candidate method for estimating treatment effects in HF outcome trials, namely the survival proportional odds model, and compared this to traditional Cox regression in a simulation study.RESULTS:In the dapagliflozin trials, nonproportional hazards were a larger issue in the HFpEF population of the DELIVER trial compared with the more homogeneous heart failure with reduced ejection fraction population of the DAPA-HF trial. In simulations of populations with varying degrees of heterogeneity, the survival proportional odds model was more robust to heterogeneity and demonstrated higher power compared with traditional Cox regression in high heterogeneity populations, while performing similarly or slightly worse in more or less heterogeneous populations. Reanalyses of the dapagliflozin trials confirmed these findings, with the survival proportional odds model providing consistently higher power in the DELIVER trial and similar power in the DAPA-HF trial.CONCLUSIONS:In HF trials, the survival proportional odds model is a viable and more robust alternative for analyzing time to event outcomes, also providing an intuitive interpretation of the treatment effect directly linked to survival probability: improved odds of being event-free in the active group compared with the control group.REGISTRATION:URL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifiers: NCT03036124 and NCT03619213.

In patients with cardiovascular conditions, such as heart failure, there is often a considerable difference in the risk of clinical events between patients, so-called risk heterogeneity.1 In an adequately sized randomized clinical trial with a parallel group design in a relatively risk-homogeneous population, measured and unmeasured patient characteristics at baseline will generally be well balanced between treatment groups, with balance maintained with time, leading to unbiased estimates of the treatment effects. In populations with high risk heterogeneity, however, effective treatments tend to retain more high-risk patients at risk in the active therapy group, while the placebo group becomes depleted of these patients over time, leading to postrandomization selection bias in hazard ratios (HRs) derived from Cox regression.2–4 If heterogeneity in risk is unexplained by measured baseline characteristics, statistical analyses cannot fully adjust for differences among patients remaining at risk in the treatment versus the placebo group. In fact, because the HR compares the instantaneous event risk among patients who have not yet had an event, postrandomization selection bias is inherent to its definition—meaning a treatment that prolongs survival does not necessarily reduce the hazard at every time point.5,6

In this study, we evaluate a potential solution to risk heterogeneity: analysis of time-to-first end points using the semiparametric survival proportional odds model (SPOM).7,8 This method assumes converging hazards between treatment groups but still produces a single treatment effect measure independent of time—the survival odds ratio (SOR). The main appeal of the SPOM is its ability to provide consistent and clinically interpretable treatment effect estimates even when hazards are not proportional, although maintaining much of the framework of the widely used Cox regression by simply replacing the proportional hazards assumption with a similar assumption on proportional odds. Our study aims to compare SPOM and Cox regression under conditions of varying risk heterogeneity using both simulations and data from 2 recent heart failure trials—DAPA-HF (Dapagliflozin in Patients With Heart Failure and Reduced Ejection Fraction) and DELIVER (Dapagliflozin in Heart Failure With Mildly Reduced or Preserved Ejection Fraction).9–11 Our findings indicate that SPOM is more robust to heterogeneity compared with Cox, resulting in less attenuation of treatment effect estimates and greater statistical power in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) trials.

Cox Proportional Hazards Model

The enduring popularity of the Cox proportional hazards model—or Cox regression—in clinical trials is likely due to the numerous advantages it offers:

Distribution-free method: Cox regression does not require assumptions about the form of the unknown distributions for estimation purposes.

Distribution-free method: Cox regression does not require assumptions about the form of the unknown distributions for estimation purposes.

Minimal assumptions: besides the common assumption of noninformative censoring found in many survival analysis methods, Cox regression requires only the proportionality of hazards to be satisfied.

A single summary measure: it provides a single summary measure, the HR, which quantifies the treatment effect across the entire duration of the follow-up without the need to specify a time point for evaluation.

A close connection between the Cox regression with a single covariate with the nonparametric log-rank test. This allows for a straightforward calculation of the statistical power, based only on the required number of events. The test is optimal under the proportionality of hazards assumption.12

When the proportionality of hazards assumption is violated in a meaningful way—notably in the presence of unexplained risk heterogeneity—several alternatives to Cox regression can be considered. Generally, these alternatives resolve the nonproportionality issue but introduce other complexities. For example, some models may require additional distributional assumptions (such as parametric distributions, for example, the negative binomial regression applied to a single event) or produce treatment effect estimates that are time-dependent, thus, cannot be summarized with a single summary measure. One such time-dependent alternative is the Restricted Mean Survival Time model.13 This model provides a single summary measure from a distribution-free estimation; however, the estimate depends on the timepoint of evaluation, which limits its generalizability, similar to a milestone analysis comparing percentages of patients with an event at a given timepoint. Specifically, such milestone analyses disregard data after the selected milestone. The milestone should be specified in advance with no clear method for its selection, and the impact of the milestone on the analysis can be substantial.14 Longer timeframes of evaluation will provide larger treatment effects, even if the event rates in both groups are constant over time.

Survival Proportional Odds Model

The SPOM addresses the issue of nonproportional hazards without introducing additional complexities.7,8,15 By substituting the proportional hazards assumption with the assumption of proportional survival odds, SPOM naturally allows hazard functions to converge over time—a feature implicitly built into the model. Like Cox regression, the method remains distribution-free and provides a single, interpretable summary measure—the SOR—that captures the treatment effect over the entire study follow-up. Therefore, the SPOM preserves advantages 1 to 3 listed above of the Cox regression listed above (for bullet point 4 and the considerations of the statistical power, see below). The SOR also has additional advantages:

It is a causal treatment effect measure since it is based on the comparison of survival functions. On the contrary, the HR does not have a causal interpretation (with the exception of no treatment effect or an unverifiable assumption of independent counterfactual event times is met4).

It is a causal treatment effect measure since it is based on the comparison of survival functions. On the contrary, the HR does not have a causal interpretation (with the exception of no treatment effect or an unverifiable assumption of independent counterfactual event times is met4).

The SOR can be interpreted as the ratio of odds of being event-free in the active group compared with the control group. Conversely, 1/SOR represents the ratio of odds of experiencing an event.

Similar to an HR, a value of 1/SOR below 1 indicates a positive treatment effect, a value of 1 indicates no effect, and values above 1 indicate harm.

The HR is often interpreted in terms of risk reduction, for example, HR=0.8 is interpreted as a 20% risk reduction in experiencing an event. This interpretation is not technically correct, since the hazard rate is the probability that a patient who has survived up to a certain time will experience the event in the next instant. On the contrary, 1/SOR is directly linked to the odds of experiencing an event (Figure 1). Hence, 1/SOR=0.8 is clearly a 20% reduction in the odds of experiencing an event. For example, if the survival probability in the control arm at the end of follow-up is 80%, the odds of an event in the control arm are 4 to 1. A 20% reduction in the odds of an event results in a 5 to 1 odds of an event in the active arm. Therefore, when communicating the results of the analyses, it is more intuitive to describe an increase in the odds of remaining event-free rather than to explain a reduction in hazard.

Simulations

To compare methods, we conducted a simulation study mimicking typical heart failure trials, with known treatment effects. We generated individual patient data from typical HF 1:1 randomized trials of up to 4000 patients followed for 3 years. The event times in each treatment group were simulated assuming separate Weibull distributions with distinct scale and shape parameters—allowing for nonproportional hazards between groups. Parameter values were chosen to represent multiple scenarios spanning different levels of risk heterogeneity, informed by Weibull parameters estimated in DAPA-HF and DELIVER trial data (see Table S1). For each scenario, we simulated 1000 trials. In each simulated trial, we estimated treatment effects using Cox regression (HR) and SPOM (1/SOR). We then assessed statistical power for each method as the proportion of the 1000 simulated trials in which a statistically significant effect was detected (1-sided alpha=0.025) and also evaluated type 1 error.

Analyses of Real Clinical Trials

To compare SPOM and Cox methods, we reanalyzed 2 real heart failure trials of dapagliflozin—the DAPA-HF and DELIVER trials. For the primary end point in each trial of time to first worsening of heart failure or cardiovascular death, we calculate the Kaplan-Meier curves with different fitted parametric proportional and nonproportional hazards models corresponding to underlying assumptions of common primary analysis models (Table S2). We estimated treatment effects as HRs using Cox regression and as SOR or 1/SOR for comparability using SPOM, both models adjusted for the trials’ stratification factor of type 2 diabetes status. We also evaluated the statistical power of each model by repeatedly bootstrapping resampling the data from each trial 1000 ×, rerunning the analysis for each resample, and then calculating how often each method detected a statistically significant treatment effect (using a 1-sided alpha level of 0.025). All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.1.0), using the survival package for Cox regression and the timereg package for SPOM analyses. Both the DAPA-HF and DELIVER trials were approved by institutional review committees at each trial center, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Results

Figure 2 shows Kaplan-Meier curves for the primary end point in each trial with different fitted parametric proportional and nonproportional hazards models. For the DAPA-HF trial conducted in the heart failure with reduced ejection fraction population, even the simplest exponential family offers a good conformity with the Kaplan-Meier estimates, suggesting that the heart failure with reduced ejection fraction population is relatively homogeneous (Figure 2A). In contrast, the HFpEF population (DELIVER) provides a different illustration (Figure 2B). The active treatment group maintained a constant incidence rate, whereas the control group experienced an increased incidence rate for the first one and a half years, followed by a curve bend and a convergence of the control Kaplan-Meier curve with the active curve. This figure suggests the occurrence of postrandomization selection bias, where the patients in the control group become less frail over time, causing the Kaplan-Meier curves for both groups to draw closer together over time than their initial separation.

In the HFpEF study, proportional hazards models fit the data poorly regardless of parametric distribution, indicating a more complex difference in event-time distributions in treatment groups than a proportional difference in hazard. For nonproportional hazards models, the nonparametric Kaplan-Meier curves are well approximated by a parametric Weibull distribution with different shape parameters in both groups (the exponential distribution, that is, Weibull with shape parameter 1, fitting the active group reasonably well). This indicates violations of both the proportionality of hazards and the proportionality of odds assumptions.

Figure 3 illustrates the true cumulative incidence of our 4 simulation scenarios. Scenarios 1 and 2 provide cases of low heterogeneity where the incidence rate in active and control groups is either constant (scenario 2) or almost constant (scenario 1). Scenarios 3 and 4 provide cases of large heterogeneity and, as a consequence, converging survival curves (and hence, converging hazards). Figure S1 provides comparisons between 1/SOR versus the HR over time for these 4 scenarios. For low heterogeneity cases, the 1/SOR is numerically either very close to or slightly above the HR over time. This means that no or numerically lower overall treatment effect estimate of 1/SOR (average survival ratio over time) is expected compared with the average HR. Therefore, since the SPOM usually has a higher SE than the HR from the Cox regression, the SPOM will have lower statistical power than the Cox regression in low heterogeneity populations.

Figure 4 illustrates the power analysis for these 4 scenarios, confirming either no or a small decrease in power in the SPOM compared with the Cox regression. On the contrary, for scenarios 3 and 4, we see that over time, the HR is increasing more rapidly than 1/SOR. Although larger heterogeneity increases both estimates, the HR is increasing faster than 1/SOR. Therefore, although the 1/SOR has a higher SE, it is expected that low attenuation of the treatment effect will result in higher power for the SPOM in highly heterogeneous populations. Discussion

Historically, conducting successful HFpEF trials has been challenging. Treatments effective in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction populations often failed to demonstrate efficacy in HFpEF populations, sometimes narrowly missing statistical significance for the primary end point. Although there may be mechanistic reasons why a treatment may work at the lower range of ejection fraction but not at the higher ends, the statistical methods used, specifically the time-to-first event analysis with the Cox proportional hazards model, may also be a contributing factor. Power improvements have been shown in the reanalysis of HFpEF trials using models not based on HR.1

In response to these challenges, some contemporary HFpEF trials have started using total event end points as primary end points.16 It is often assumed that total event analyses provide higher statistical power, as they incorporate more events, including not only the first events of patients but also recurrent events. However, this is not always the case.17 Total event end points may yield a wider CI than time-to-first event end points owing to a more complex SE structure than Cox regression.

Another fundamental difference lies in the treatment effect estimate. Although sometimes used interchangeably, the HR used in time-to-first-event and the rate ratio used for total events analysis are not the same.18 The rate in total events analysis is defined as the expected number of events in a small time interval and is estimated from the Poisson process with a robust SE in the Lin-Wei-Yang-Ying model.19 This means that the treatment effect estimate is more robust to postrandomization selection bias due to population heterogeneity. However, total event analyses rely on different assumptions that may also be violated in HFpEF trials (eg, due to event clustering20), and a direct replacement of the Cox regression with the total event analysis using the Lin-Wei-Yang-Ying method is not easily justifiable. Total event end points address a different therapeutic goal—the overall disease burden—and therefore, serve a different scientific objective. Hence, the choice between time-to-first event and total events analysis is not solely a statistical decision, but also a clinical one.

On the other hand, replacing the Cox regression with SPOM does not necessitate a change of the end point or additional assumptions. The SPOM effectively replaces the assumption of proportionality of hazards with the assumption of proportionality of odds, which implies converging hazards over time. This is a more realistic assumption in large heterogeneity populations, similar to HFpEF trials where active treatment maintains consistent benefit while control patients experience early events and the control group becomes less frail over time, resulting in convergence of hazard rates of the 2 groups.

The SPOM provides a different treatment effect measure, the SOR, which is not affected by postrandomization selection bias, is more robust to population heterogeneity, is directly on the survival curves as estimated by the Kaplan-Meier estimator, and is interpretable causally as the ratio of odds of being event-free in the active group compared with the control group. In contrast, HR is based on the more abstract mathematical concept of hazards and is frequently misinterpreted as a simple risk ratio, although it actually reflects the ratio of the instantaneous risk of an event among a selected subset of patients who have not yet experienced the event. In large heterogeneous populations, the SPOM outperforms the Cox regression in terms of statistical power and can be considered as an alternative primary analysis method in heart failure trials with time-to-first event end points. On the contrary, the SPOM will potentially lead to a reduction in statistical power in relatively homogeneous populations.

As discussed earlier, in the case of the DELIVER trial, the proportionality of hazards assumption was violated, as reported in the primary publication.10 On the contrary, in a similar HFpEF trial, FINEARTS-HF (Finerenone in Heart Failure With Mildly Reduced or Preserved Ejection Fraction) trial,21 visual inspection of the Kaplan-Meier curves does not indicate a violation of the proportional hazards assumption (the curves in both curves are proportionally similar). This is further supported by the numerical equality between the rate ratio from the Lin-Wei-Yang-Ying model in the primary total event analysis (RR = 0.84, 95% CI, 0.74–0.95) and the HR from the Cox regression in the time-to-first event analysis (HR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.76–0.94]). This suggests no reduction in the treatment effect estimate when using a method robust to population heterogeneity. We suspect that this population is less heterogeneous than DELIVER; therefore, the gain in efficiency with SPOM would not be substantial (but still likely advantageous to Cox regression).

Another approach to addressing heterogeneity is to adjust for baseline prognostic factors in the Cox model and estimate the treatment effect conditional on these covariates. In this way, patients with the same risk according to baseline factors are compared, in theory preserving the balance created by randomization over time. Several methods exist for covariate adjustment, including novel approaches.22 An interesting concept is the 5-step stratified testing and amalgamation routine for Cox regression, which uses baseline covariates to segment the heterogeneous trial population into subpopulations of prognostically homogeneous patients (risk strata).23 The main problem with any covariate adjustment approach as a solution to risk heterogeneity is that substantial residual heterogeneity will likely remain—even with covariate adjustment—because key risk factors are not measured in the trial or may not even exist. This is evident in heart failure, where multivariable prognostic models demonstrate only moderate predictive ability—typically with a C-index around 0.70 (0.5 indicates no discrimination, 1.0 indicates perfect prediction)24—suggesting that most of the heterogeneity remains unexplained despite the use of current prognostic factors. With that said, our results show that even when adjusting for NT-proBNP in the dapagliflozin trials, SPOM consistently outperforms Cox regression also in estimating the conditional treatment effect.

The survival odds model has some limitations. The model relies on the proportionality of odds assumption, which may also be violated in heterogeneous populations. Visual diagnostic tools can be applied to assess this assumption. We showed that in HFpEF-like trials, this assumption is likely to be violated to some extent. However, in heterogeneous populations, the proportional odds assumption is more likely to be met, offering a more robust model with higher statistical power than the Cox regression model, which similarly has violated assumptions. It should also be noted that the estimation of the SOR usually results in wider CIs than those of the HR from the Cox regression model. On the contrary, because of the selection bias leading to convergence towards 1 of HR in populations with large heterogeneity of risk, the Cox regression loses power compared with the SPOM, since the SOR is more robust to heterogeneity. Finally, this study has evaluated the applicability of SOR in heart failure trials only, in a setting where most patients do not experience events during follow-up (only around 10%–20%). Therefore, the conclusions may not be widely applicable in other settings, such as when the outcome of interest is very common or events accrue more rapidly.

In populations with low heterogeneity, the SPOM, having a higher SE compared with the Cox model, is likely to lead to a decrease in statistical power, as no attenuation of the HR is expected in these populations. Therefore, the SPOM cannot replace the Cox regression model in all settings. Instead, population heterogeneity should be considered when choosing this model as the primary analysis method for time-to-first event end points. In the heart failure with preserved ejection fraction populations, the SPOM seems a particularly good fit.

In all cases, the unexplained heterogeneity of the underlying population should be considered before using the SPOM as the primary analysis method. Future research should focus on estimating the degree of heterogeneity and determining the cutoff point at which increased heterogeneity reduces the power of the Cox regression compared with the SPOM.
