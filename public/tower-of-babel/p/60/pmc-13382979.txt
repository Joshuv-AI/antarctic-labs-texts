ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesThe apolipoprotein E (APOE) haplotypes are known to be associated with dementia, with the ε4 haplotype associated with higher risk. It has been suggested that the APOE ε2 allele serves as a protective factor for dementia. However, data on the effects of the homozygous APOE ε2/ε2 genotype are limited, likely due to the rarity of the APOE ε2/ε2 genotype. Furthermore, the association between APOE genotypes and dementia may differ across self-reported race. We aim to investigate the association between APOE genotypes and dementia overall and across self-reported race, with a focus on the potential protective effects of the ε2 haplotype and differences across race.MethodsData from 7 large, community-based, prospective cohort studies from the Dementia Risk Prediction Pooling Project: Age, Gene/Environment Susceptibility-Reykjavik Study, Whitehall II study, Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study, Cardiovascular Health Study, Honolulu-Asia Aging Study, Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, and Framingham Heart Study and its associated cohorts were used. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the cause-specific hazard ratios of dementia by APOE genotypes overall and by self-reported race.ResultsThe study consisted of 45,022 participants (10% Asian, 15% Black, 75% White, 52% male) with a mean age of 56 years (SD: 14.5) at baseline. Compared with participants with an APOE ε3/ε3 genotype, those with an ε2/ε3 genotype had a lower risk of dementia (HR: 0.87, 95% CI 0.80–0.96). There was an indication of protective effects of the ε2/ε2 genotype compared with the ε3/ε3 genotype (HR: 0.98, 95% CI 0.71–1.37). These associations were similar among Black and White participants. The detrimental effect of an ε4/ε4 genotype compared with an ε3/ε3 genotype was also seen overall and among Black and White participants. Those with an APOE ε4/ε4 genotype experienced dementia onset approximately 8 years earlier than those with an APOE ε3/ε3 genotype.DiscussionIn this large, pooled cohort, the presence of at least one APOE ε2 allele was associated with lower risk of dementia overall and by self-reported race, suggesting a protective effect. APOE ε4/ε4 was associated with an earlier age of dementia onset with differences across race.

Introduction

The Apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene is an established genetic risk factor for Alzheimer disease and other related dementias. It has 3 common haplotypes, ε2, ε3, and ε4, with robust evidence showing that carriers of the APOE ε4 haplotype (ε3/ε4 or ε4/ε4 genotype) have a higher risk of mortality and dementia, and an earlier age of dementia onset, with the greatest risk in ε4/ε4 homozygotes.1-5 Conversely, it has been suggested that APOE ε2 carriers (ε2/ε2 or ε2/ε3 genotype) live longer and are older at dementia onset, suggesting a protective effect on cognitive decline and dementia.1,5-11 Findings on ε2/ε2 homozygotes are not consistent across studies due to small numbers of individuals with this genotype.1,5,7,8,12 Existing studies may have been limited by the rarity of the ε2 allele frequency, with only 1% of the population having the homozygous ε2/ε2 genotype, making it difficult to obtain a large enough sample size to ascertain the association between the APOE ε2/ε2 genotype and dementia risk.

Some studies have found the association between APOE genotype and dementia to differ by race and ethnicity,5,10,13-15 but few studies have adequate diversity to examine this association, especially among ε2/ε2 homozygotes. Among the few studies that have investigated this association, the focus was primarily on the role of APOE ε4 and ε2 allele carriers rather than all APOE genotypes10,13,14 within Black and White populations.10,14 Two individual-level meta-analyses examined all combinations of the common APOE alleles in association with dementia risk in Black, Hispanic, Asian, and White individuals. Both found that the association of APOE ε4 genotypes was stronger among Whites than other racial/ethnic groups, whereas whether there is a similar pattern for the ε2/ε2 and ε2/ε3 genotypes was not clear.5,16 Data were drawn from a mixture of study designs (cross-sectional, case-control, cohort, and family-based) and participant sources (community/population, clinical/hospital, and autopsy/brain bank) and consisted of an older population; therefore, the results may not be generalizable to population-based samples. Longitudinal population-based studies with large sample sizes are required to investigate the association between APOE genotypes and dementia by race and ethnicity to provide accurate effect estimates.

Accordingly, this study investigated the association of APOE genotype with risk of dementia, with a particular focus on the potential protective effects of the ε2/ε2 genotype. In addition, the analysis investigated the role of self-reported race in this association and investigated the impact of APOE genotypes on age at onset of dementia. By pooling data from 7 population-based prospective studies from the Dementia Risk Prediction Pooling Project (DRPP),15 the analysis had a sufficient sample size to undertake analyses stratified by race.

Methods

Study Population

The DRPP pooled data from 16 longitudinal cohort studies to facilitate dementia research and generate a dementia risk prediction model.15 The DRPP cohort consists of 119,061 individuals from the following European-based and United States-based studies: the Three-City Study (3C), the Age, Gene/Environment Susceptibility-Reykjavik Study (AGES-Reykjavik), the Rotterdam Study (RS), the Whitehall II study (Whitehall), the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study (ARIC), the Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS), the Honolulu-Asia Aging Study (HAAS), the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), the Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke (REGARDS), the Sacramento Area Latino Study on Aging (SALSA), and the 6 participant cohorts from the Framingham Heart Study (FHS) (Generation 1, Generation 2, New Offspring Spouse, Generation 3, Omni 1, and Omni 2).1 3C and RS are validation cohorts and did not contribute individual-level data. Details of the DRPP and the characteristics of each cohort study in DRPP have previously been described elsewhere.15

The sample for this study consisted of 45,022 participants with data on APOE genotype and dementia ascertainment from the following DRPP cohorts: AGES-Reykjavik, ARIC, CHS, FHS, HAAS, MESA, and Whitehall. Participants were excluded if they belonged to the validation cohorts, did not have data on dementia (n = 38,527), APOE genotype (n = 9,392), or did not report their race as White, Black, or Asian (n = 1,716) (Figure 1). Hispanic individuals who did not also report their race as White, Black, or Asian were excluded due to the small sample size for investigating associations of APOE genotype among the Hispanic population.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

DRPP data use at the University of Minnesota was approved by the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board. The DRPP and individual cohorts have been approved by their local Institutional Review Boards. Written informed consent was received from all participants.15

APOE Genotyping

APOE haplotypes were estimated from rs7412 and rs429358 single-nucleotide polymorphisms. Cohort-specific APOE genotyping methods are presented in eTable 1. The APOE genotypes are ε2/ε2, ε2/ε3, ε2/ε4, ε3/ε3, ε3/ε4, and ε4/ε4. In some analyses, APOE genotypes are combined to represent heterozygous and homozygous carriers: APOE ε2 (ε2/ε2 & ε2/ε3) and APOE ε4 (ε3/ε4 & ε4/ε4).

Dementia Ascertainment

The definition and ascertainment of dementia varied across cohorts. AGES-Reykjavik, ARIC, CHS, FHS, and HAAS ascertained dementia using ongoing surveillance protocols involving cognitive testing, neurologic examinations, phone interviews, proxy interviews, and consensus diagnosis.17-21 Whitehall identified incident dementia cases from International Classification of Disease (ICD)-10 codes in hospitalization discharge records, inpatient and community care records, and death certificates.22 MESA identified incident dementia from ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes in hospitalization discharge records and death certificates.23 Detailed cohort-specific ascertainment methods are presented in eTable 2.

Covariates

Race was self-reported at the baseline examination for each contributing cohort. Black, White, and Asian participants were included in the analysis. Sex was categorized as female and male. Education was categorized based on the highest level of school partially completed or completed: “no school/grade school,” “high school,” and “technical/vocational/college/graduate/professional.” Smoking was classified as “nonsmoker,” “current smoker,” and “former smoker.” Physical activity was recorded as MET minutes per week of self-reported moderate and vigorous activity. Hypertension was categorized from diastolic (DBP) and systolic blood pressure (SBP) readings and the use of antihypertensive medication as: “stage 2 hypertension” (SBP ≥140 or DBP ≥90 mm Hg), “stage 1 hypertension” (SBP ≥130 and/or DBP ≥80 mm Hg and/or antihypertensive medication), “elevated blood pressure” (SBP ≥120 and DBP <80 mm Hg and no anti-hypertensive medication), and “normal blood pressure” (SBP <120 and DBP <80 mm Hg and no antihypertensive medication). High cholesterol was categorized from total cholesterol (TC) and the use of lipid-lowering medication as: “hypercholesterolemia” (TC ≥ 240 mg/dL and/or use of lipid-lowering medication), “elevated cholesterol” (≥200 mg/dL and no lipid-lowering medication), and “normal cholesterol” (<200 mg/dL and no lipid-lowering medication). Diabetes was categorized as “diabetes” (fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL or HbA1C ≥ 6.5 or nonfasting plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dL or use of antidiabetic medication), “prediabetes” (fasting plasma glucose ≥100 mg/dL or HbA1C ≥ 5.7 or nonfasting plasma glucose ≥140 mg/dL and no use of antidiabetic medication), and “normal” (fasting plasma glucose <100 mg/dL or HbA1C < 5.7 or nonfasting plasma glucose <140 mg/dL and no use of antidiabetic medication). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated in kg/m2 from weight and height measurements.

Data Harmonization Across Cohorts

Forty-five variables were harmonized from the DRPP studies using the methods described by Stephen et al.24 The psHarmonize R package was developed by the DRPP investigators to create an analytic data set for pooled analyses of the DRPP cohorts. As described above, demographic, clinical, behavioral, and genetic data were standardized across the cohorts using a harmonization sheet that provides instructions and coding decisions for variable alterations for use with the “harmonization” function of the package. Cohort-specific summary reports were generated to assess the success of harmonization.24 After harmonization, missing data were imputed across cohorts using multilevel joint modeling multiple imputation with 25 imputed data sets.

Statistical Analysis

APOE and Dementia Incidence

Demographic characteristics of the analytic population were described overall and by APOE genotype using proportions, means, and standard deviations. All analyses were performed using the imputed data. Crude incidence rates (IR) of dementia by APOE genotype were calculated as the number of dementia cases per 1,000 person-years. Rates among Black and White participants were calculated by APOE genotype (ε2/ε2, ε2/ε3, ε2/ε4, ε3/ε3, ε3/ε4, ε4/ε4), while rates among Asian participants were calculated in groups based on ε2 and ε4 carriership (ε2/ε2 & ε2/ε3, ε3/ε4 & ε4/ε4, ε2/ε4, ε3/ε3) due to small numbers of homozygous ε2 and ε4 carriers in this population (ε2/ε2: n = 15; ε4/ε4: n = 20). Follow-up time began at visit 12 for the original FHS cohort and at the baseline visit for all other cohorts. It ended at dementia diagnosis or was censored at end of study follow-up, loss to follow-up, or death, whichever occurred first.

To estimate the overall association between APOE genotype and incident dementia, Cox proportional hazard models were used with APOE ε3/ε3 as the reference group. Three models were fit: an unadjusted model, a model adjusted for age, sex, and cohort, and a fully adjusted model adjusted for age, sex, cohort, education, and cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking, physical activity, and BMI). To evaluate the self-reported race-specific associations of APOE genotype with incident dementia, Cox proportional hazard models were stratified by race, and hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated with APOE ε3/ε3 as the reference group in each analysis. Models estimating the risk of dementia among Black and White participants were modeled with APOE genotype, while models estimating the risk of dementia among Asian participants were modeled with combined genotypes (APOE ε2 and ε4 carriership) due to small numbers in these groups. As described above, 3 models were fit and results from the fully adjusted models are reported. Cause-specific hazard ratios are reported for all survival analyses to model the hazard of dementia, treating mortality as a competing event.

Sensitivity Analyses

Because some APOE genotypes are suggested to be associated with higher or lower mortality, mortality was considered a competing risk.1,25 First, a sensitivity analysis was conducted such that Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the cause-specific hazard ratios (HRs) for mortality, with follow-up time censored at death, at the end of study follow-up, loss to follow-up, or incident dementia, whichever occurred first. As with the cause-specific dementia survival analysis, 3 models were fit, and the results from the fully adjusted model are presented. This allowed a comparison of the association of APOE genotype and incident dementia with the association of APOE genotype and dementia-free survival. A second sensitivity analysis was conducted using a subdistribution proportional hazards model for a competing risk assessment, as described by Fine and Gray.25 To provide further context on the APOE genotype/dementia relationship, a third sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine the association between carriership of each haplotype and incident dementia. For the first version of this analysis, 3 binary variables were constructed for each haplotype to represent the presence of at least 1 allele for the given haplotype (for example, for ε2 having any ε2 coded as 1 and having no ε2 coded as 0). These variables were then modeled separately as the exposure in association with dementia risk. For the second version of this analysis, ε2 and ε3 carriers were separately compared with the ε4/ε4 genotype to provide HRs that are directly comparable. Finally, hazard ratios were calculated by removing one cohort at a time to ensure a single cohort was not driving the association between APOE genotype and dementia.

Age at Dementia Onset

To estimate the median age of dementia onset by APOE genotype, Weibull accelerated failure time (AFT) models using age as the time scale and adjusted for sex, cohort, education, and cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking, physical activity, and BMI) were used. To assess the median age of dementia onset with APOE genotype by self-reported race, similar Weibull AFT models were used such that the data were subset to the race of interest. Models estimating the median age of dementia onset among Black and White participants were modeled with APOE genotype, while models estimating the median age of dementia onset among Asian participants were modeled with combined genotypes (APOE ε2 and ε4 carriership). Age at onset analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.5. All other analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1.

Data Availability

Reasonable data requests can be submitted to be reviewed by DRPP and individual cohorts to ensure that data can be shared without compromising patient confidentiality or breaching intellectual property restrictions.26 Participant-level demographic and clinical data may be partially restricted based on previously obtained participant consent.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

Baseline demographic characteristics of the study population are presented overall and by APOE genotype (Table 1). The mean age of participants at study entry was 56.5 years (SD: 14.5 years) Fifty-two percent of the study population were male, and 47.8% were female. Ten percent of the population identified as Asian, 14.6% as Black, and 75.2% as White. The frequency of APOE ε2/ε2, ε2/ε3, ε2/ε4, ε3/ε3, ε3/ε4, and ε4/ε4 genotypes in the total study sample was 0.7%, 12.1%, 2.5%, 60.3%, 22.3%, and 2.1%, respectively. The prevalence of APOE genotype differed across self-reported race (eTable 3). The APOE ε2/ε2 genotype was most prevalent among Black participants (1.2%), followed by White (0.6%) then Asian (0.3%) participants. The APOE ε4/ε4 genotype was also most prevalent among Black participants (4.3%), followed by White (1.9%) then Asian (0.4%) participants.

APOE Genotypes and Dementia Risk

During 919,149 person-years of follow-up, with a median follow-up of 18.1 years, 6,254 participants were diagnosed with dementia. The crude dementia incidence rate among those with an APOE ε3/ε3 genotype was 5.89 (95% CI 5.69–6.09) per 1,000 person-years (Figure 2A). The incidence rate among those with an APOE ε2/ε3 genotype was lower at 5.11 (95% CI 4.70–5.53) per 1,000 person-years. The incidence rates for those with an APOE ε3/ε4 or ε4/ε4 genotype were higher than the ε3/ε3 genotype group, as expected,. These patterns were consistent across self-reported race (Figure 2B), with the addition that among Black participants, dementia incidence was lower among those with an APOE ε2/ε2 genotype compared with those with an APOE ε3/ε3 genotype.

In the overall population, hazard ratios adjusted for age, sex, cohort, education, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking, and physical activity showed similar results to the crude incidence rate estimates (Figure 3A). Participants with an APOE ε2/ε2 genotype did not have a decreased risk of dementia compared with participants with an APOE ε3/ε3 genotype (HR: 0.98, 95% CI 0.71–1.37), while an APOE ε2/ε3 genotype was protective (HR: 0.87, 95% CI 0.80–0.96). The risk of dementia was higher in those with an ε3/ε4 (HR: 1.86, 95% CI 1.76–1.97) or ε4/ε4 (HR: 3.64, 95% CI 3.22–4.12) genotype. Some variations were seen in the associations when examined by self-reported race (Figure 3B). Neither the ε2/ε2 genotype in Black and White participants nor ε2 carriership in Asian participants were associated with dementia risk. An ε2/ε3 genotype was associated with lower dementia risk in White participants only (HR: 0.86, 95% CI 0.78–0.96). Both the ε3/ε4 and ε4/ε4 genotypes were associated with increased dementia risk compared with the ε3/ε3 genotype in both self-reported Black and White participants; however, the magnitudes of association were stronger among White participants. The hazard ratio for the ε4/ε4 genotype was 2.56 (95% CI 2.01–3.27) among Black participants and 4.00 (95% CI 3.45–4.62) among White participants. ε4 carriership had no association with dementia risk among Asian participants (HR: 1.27, 95% CI 0.97–1.66).

In sensitivity analyses estimating the cause-specific hazard ratio of mortality by APOE genotypes, the risk of mortality did not differ in any APOE genotype group compared with the ε3/ε3 genotype (eTable 4). In analyses based on the Fine & Gray competing risk model, the hazard ratio for dementia was slightly attenuated among all genotypes (eTable 5). In the analysis of APOE carriership where carrier statuses were modeled separately, the HR for ε3 carriership vs noncarriership was 0.57 (95% CI 0.52–0.62) and the HR for ε2 carriership was 0.76 (95% CI 0.71–0.82) in the fully adjusted models (eTable 6). Conversely, the HR for ε3 carriership vs e4/e4 genotype was 0.33 (95% CI 0.29–0.37) and the HR for ε2 carriership vs ε4/ε4 genotype was 0.26 (95% CI 0.23–0.30). The HR for ε4 carriership vs noncarriership was 1.96 (95% CI 1.87–2.07). In further analyses removing one cohort at a time, the hazard ratio for dementia by APOE genotype did not differ considerably for any one set of cohorts (eTable 7).

Age of Dementia Onset

The median age of dementia onset varied by APOE genotype. After adjusting for demographic, clinical, and behavioral factors, participants with an APOE ε2/ε3 genotype had the oldest median age of dementia onset (94.02, 95% CI 93.48–94.56) followed by participants with an APOE ε2/ε2 genotype (93.51, 95% CI 91.45–95.57) (Table 2). Those with an APOE ε4/ε4 genotype experienced dementia onset approximately 8 years earlier than those with an APOE ε3/ε3 genotype (ε4/ε4 median age: 85.53, 95% CI 84.84–86.22; ε3/ε3 median age: 93.18, 95% CI 92.92–93.45). Black participants compared with White participants had a lower median age of dementia across all APOE genotypes except for ε2/ε2. For example, those with an APOE ε3/ε3 genotype had a median age of dementia onset 3 years younger than their White counterparts (age 90.00, 95% CI 89.33–90.67 and age 93.20, 95% CI 92.90–93.49, respectively) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this large, pooled population-based cohort study, participants with an APOE ε2/ε3 genotype had significantly lower risk of dementia compared with those with an ε3/ε3 genotype. Imprecise estimates preclude a conclusion on the potentially protective effects of the APOE ε2/ε2 genotype. Dementia risk was higher among self-reported Asian, Black, and White participants with an APOE ε4/ε4 genotype compared with those with an APOE ε3/ε3 genotype, with the strongest association among White participants, suggesting that the relative increase in dementia risk varies across race among those with an APOE ε4/ε4 genotype. The risk of dementia was lower among both Black and White participants with an APOE ε2/ε3 genotype, but no reductions in dementia risk for those with an APOE ε2/ε2 genotype were seen in either group. On average, those with an APOE ε4/ε4 genotype experienced dementia onset 8 years earlier than those with an APOE ε3/ε3 genotype, and Black participants had a lower median age of dementia onset than their White counterparts across all APOE genotypes except ε2/ε2.

Our data contribute to further understanding the effect of the APOE ε2/ε2 genotype on dementia risk. The APOE protein supports lipid transport in cells in both the brain and other parts of the body. In the brain, the ε2 allele is thought to be protective against Alzheimer disease through pathways of lower amounts of beta-amyloid and amyloid plaque loads and reduced tau phosphorylation.27 In the body, the ε2 allele is associated with lower levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and higher levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and triglycerides compared with ε3.1,28 There is evidence that peripheral lipid profiles also mediate the effects of the ε2 allele on dementia risk, with some research showing that up to 36% of the protective effects of the ε2 allele is mediated by peripheral lipid species.29,30 While previous studies examining the role of the APOE ε2 allele on dementia risk suggest protective effects against dementia,8,11,13 studies on ε2/ε2 homozygotes had conflicting results.6,31-33 We did not find statistically significant evidence of an overall protective effect for those with an APOE ε2/ε2 genotype, although the effect estimates were in the protective direction. We did observe a significantly lower risk of dementia among participants with an APOE ε2/ε3 genotype, consistent with previous studies.6,8,16 The protective effects of the APOE ε2/ε3 genotype were more pronounced than those observed for the APOE ε2/ε2 genotype, especially in White participants. However, our sensitivity analysis looking at allele carriership rather than genotype also showed that ε2 carriership is more protective against dementia than ε3 carriership when referenced to the ε4/ε4 genotype, indicating that the ε2 allele may be more protective against dementia than the ε3 allele. It is important to note that despite our large sample size, precision was limited for the analyses of the APOE ε2/ε2 genotype, given the rarity of this variant (only 0.7% in our population). Future studies including a larger sample of individuals with APOE ε2/ε2 genotype are needed to investigate this observation further.

The investigation of the association between APOE genotypes and dementia across self-reported race in this large, longitudinal pooled cohort showed that although Black participants with an APOE ε4/ε4 genotype have the highest absolute risk of dementia, the relative increase in risk associated with the ε4/ε4 genotype compared with the ε3/ε3 genotype is smaller for Black participants than for White participants, consistent with previous findings.5,10,13,16,34-36 This suggests that the higher risk of dementia in Black individuals may be due to factors other than the presence of an APOE ε4 allele.37 Consistent with previous findings, we observed a lower risk of dementia among Black participants with an APOE ε2/ε3 genotype compared with those with an APOE ε3/ε3 genotype.5,16 It has been shown previously that the APOE ε2/ε2 genotype is associated with significantly lower dementia risk among White participants11; however, our study did not demonstrate this protective effect. We observed a significantly lower risk of dementia among White participants with an APOE ε2/ε3 genotype, consistent with previous studies.5,16 While our study expands on previous research by including Asian participants, our findings remain inconclusive due to the sample size constraints and a relatively small number of dementia cases among Asian participants. This highlights the importance of, and reflects the call for, the inclusion of participants of all racial and ethnic backgrounds in longitudinal studies and dementia genetics research.38,39

Consistent with the literature, the median age of dementia onset was later among participants with an APOE ε2/ε2 or ε2/ε3 genotype. The median age of dementia onset among Black participants was earlier across almost all APOE genotypes compared with White participants. The median age of dementia onset was earlier for participants with an APOE ε4/ε4 genotype and nearly 1 year earlier in Black participants compared with White participants with an ε4/ε4 genotype, consistent with previous findings.10 These findings are in agreement with the current findings of heterogeneity in dementia risk across race among those with an APOE ε4/ε4 genotype, as well as previous findings.10

In this study, race was self-reported by participants, and only included those who identified as Asian, Black, or White. Therefore, it is important to make the distinction that in these analyses, we are not using race as a substitute for genetic variability, in accordance with the 2023 National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine guidelines on using descriptors in genetics.40 Furthermore, we acknowledge that race is a social construct that encompasses factors such as socioeconomic status, geography, cultural beliefs and practices, physical and social environments, systemic racism, and discrimination-related stress, and that the construct of race may vary cross-nationally.40-42 We were able to partially control for this by including cohort as a covariate in our models. In a study examining the association between APOE genotypes and dementia across race, ethnicity, and genetic ancestry,16 it was noted that genetic ancestry may not explain all of the differences seen in the risk of dementia, suggesting that analyses by racial groups might reflect health disparities in the dementia risk.

This study has many strengths. We are the first to contribute, to the best of our knowledge, a comprehensive examination of the association between APOE genotypes and dementia, with a focus on APOE ε2/ε2, across race in a large sample of participants from longitudinal, population-based cohort studies. Previous studies that have examined this association in longitudinal cohort studies describe the role of APOE having at least one ε2 and/or ε4 allele, rather than APOE genotypes.10,11,14 Furthermore, previous studies with results on all APOE genotypes were case-control studies6,8,31-33 or used a mixture of study designs which included participants recruited from clinics/hospitals and autopsies/brain banks.5,16 Therefore, a strength of this study is its pooling of individual participant data exclusively from population-based longitudinal cohort studies, providing effect estimates that are more generalizable while also characterizing these associations by self-reported race. Furthermore, data from the DRPP cohorts were rigorously harmonized, allowing analysis of individual participant data for a more robust analysis. The success of this harmonization is supported by stratified analyses showing that no single cohort was overly influential. Finally, our inclusion of competing-risks models strengthens our ability to draw conclusions about these relationships, taking into account the competing risk of mortality.

Limitations of this study include the small number of APOE ε2/ε2 homozygotes. Although we found trends of lower dementia risk in the overall study population and across race, the prevalence of APOE ε2/ε2 genotype is rare. In this study, just 309 of 45,022 (0.7%) participants had an APOE ε2/ε2 genotype, exemplifying the difficulty of obtaining precise effect estimates to assess protective effects for dementia, even in a large sample size such as this. Furthermore, our study did not have data on global or local genetic ancestry to refine our estimates.37 Self-reported race does not accurately represent genetic ancestry or variability, so we cannot fully explain the differences in the association between the APOE genotypes and dementia risk across racial groups. The generalizability of these results may be limited as demonstrated by the older median age at dementia onset compared with what is commonly reported in the literature, likely due to a volunteer-bias across studies and older baseline ages for some cohorts.

This comprehensive study demonstrates the relationship between APOE genotypes and dementia risk, emphasizing the significance of considering both genetic factors and racial diversity in understanding disease susceptibility. By pooling data from 7 longitudinal, population-based studies within the Dementia Risk Prediction Pooling Project, we provided a detailed examination of the association between APOE genotypes and dementia across racial groups. Our findings reaffirm the substantial impact of the APOE ε3/ε4 and ε4/ε4 genotypes on risk of dementia, while also demonstrating the protective effect of the APOE ε2/ε3 genotype. Furthermore, our study underscores the importance of addressing racial disparities in dementia research, as the association between APOE genotypes and dementia risk varied among different racial populations. Our findings highlight the importance of considering racial diversity in the study of genetic factors for dementia and contribute to better understanding the effects of the APOE ε2/ε2 and ε2/ε3 genotypes on dementia risk.
