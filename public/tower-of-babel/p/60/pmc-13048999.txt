A transformer–XGBoost based model to fault diagnosis for CPR1000
Authors: Zhiqiang Peng, Jichong Lei, Zining Ni, Muhammad Abdul Wasaye, Yi Jiang, Yuhan Cao, Tao Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), intelligent diagnostics have seen broad application across industries. To address the limitations of traditional data-driven methods in accurately identifying faults in nuclear power reactor systems, this study proposes a hybrid model combining Transformer and XGBoost for diagnosing faults in CPR1000 pressurized water reactors. Fault-related data were automatically collected using the self-developed AutoSave-PCTRAN software from the PCTRAN simulator, with key nuclear parameters selected as features. A 10-fold cross-validation with recursive feature elimination was used for feature selection. The Transformer model extracted temporal features via its self-attention mechanism, and an RWOA was employed to tune XGBoost hyperparameters for fault classification. The model effectively identified faults such as Loss of Coolant Accident(LOCA), Steam Line Break Inside Containment(SLBLC), and steam generator B-tube rupture(SGTR-B), achieving 99.46% accuracy, confirming its reliability and practicality.

Introduction

Nuclear power plants are highly complex systems composed of multi-level subsystems and a large number of devices, forming a precision-coordinated operational framework. The core of their safe operation lies in the accurate grasp of dynamic parameters, which is closely related to reactor control theories such as reactivity management and core power regulation.To achieve comprehensive monitoring of their operational status, a dense network of sensors at critical nodes, continuously collecting core operational parameters such as temperature, pressure, and water level—these parameters are exactly the key variables in the nonlinear dynamic simulation of reactor thermal processes and core power control1.

While this comprehensive monitoring mechanism provides a wealth of data support, it also leads to significant information overload issues: under normal operating conditions, operators already face challenges in extracting critical information from the vast amount of data; when the system experiences abnormalities and multiple alarms are triggered simultaneously, even experienced operators may make judgment errors under high-pressure conditions when faced with a large number of concurrent alarm signals and parameter fluctuations. The severity of this issue was vividly demonstrated in historical accidents—during the 1979 Three Mile Island nuclear accident in the United States, the operational team failed to promptly identify the abnormal state of the pressure relief valve, misjudged the system’s operational status, and ultimately led to severe consequences. This case clearly highlights a critical flaw in the human-machine interface of modern nuclear power plants: presenting raw monitoring data directly to operators effectively requires them to perform highly complex fault identification and judgment tasks in real-time within a complex data stream2,3.Notably, similar challenges have been reflected in the control practice of VVER and PWR units—Salman et al. pointed out that the coupling between boron concentration, coolant flow rate, and core power further increases the difficulty of manual judgment during transient processes4,5.

The introduction of intelligent diagnostic algorithms has revolutionary significance in addressing key issues in this industry. By constructing a multi-level state recognition model, the system can automatically analyze the intrinsic relationships between sensor data and convert raw data streams into intuitive assessments of system health status. This intelligent transformation not only significantly reduces the cognitive load on operators but also effectively reduces the possibility of human misjudgment by enabling early anomaly detection, adding an intelligent layer of protection to the nuclear safety system. From an engineering practice perspective, algorithm-based condition monitoring and early fault warning systems serve as a crucial technical foundation for implementing the“defense-in-depth”safety philosophy in nuclear power plants, which complements the existing optimized control strategies (such as steam generator pressure control and PID-based core power regulation)5,6, playing a significant role in enhancing the overall safety level and operational reliability of nuclear energy systems.

In recent years, research on data-driven technologies in the field of fault diagnosis for nuclear reactors and nuclear power plants has become increasingly in-depth, with scholars actively exploring this area7–10. Compared to traditional physics-based modeling methods(e.g., the nonlinear point kinetic model for VVER-1000 core control)11, these technologies do not require the construction of complex physical models. Instead, they automatically generate fault diagnosis models by identifying intrinsic patterns in historical operational data from nuclear power plants(including data from simulators such as PCTRAN), demonstrating unique technical advantagesin handling the strong nonlinearity and parameter coupling of reactor systems.

Support Vector Machines (SVMs) perform exceptionally well in small-sample scenarios12, particularly in classification tasks where fault samples are scarce, such as in nuclear power plant scenarios. However, their binary classification nature fundamentally conflicts with the multi-classification requirements of nuclear power fault diagnosis(e.g.,distinguishing among coolant leakage, rod misalignment, and boron concentration anomalies), and the mismatch in dimensionality limits their direct application in complex systems. Neural networks (NN) possess powerful nonlinear mapping capabilities and are theoretically suitable for modeling complex systems9. However, they face the risk of overfitting and struggle to maintain generalization capabilities under industrial noise interference—a critical issue given that sensor noise is inherent in real-world Nuclear power plants monitoring14. Although LSTM and CNN have high recognition accuracy, they are “black box models” whose feature extraction and decision-making processes are difficult to explain intuitively. In fault diagnosis, operators may need to clearly understand the fault mechanism or key influencing factors, but the model cannot provide a clear reasoning path, which may affect the reliability of the diagnosis results and subsequent decisions15–17.These limitations pose significant challenges to the application of traditional SVM and NN methods in nuclear power plant fault diagnosis.

To address the aforementioned issues, this study proposes a novel hybrid framework. By utilizing Transformers to capture long-range dependencies and extract higher-order features18, XGBoost constructs a high-precision prediction model; however, it is highly sensitive to hyperparameters. Reinforced Whale Optimization Algorithm (RWOA), such as Latin hypercube sampling(LHS)19, are introduced to address the original algorithm’s tendency to get stuck in local optima and its slow convergence speed. Meanwhile, Recursive Feature Elimination with Cross-Validation(RFECV) is used to screen input features to enhance XGBoost’s diagnostic accuracy and generalization capability, and multi-chain optimization ensures efficient and reliable fault diagnosis.

Theoretical framework and data acquisition

This study employs a data-driven intelligent diagnostic framework that integrates automated fault data acquisition and a hybrid deep learning (DL) machine learning (ML) model for nuclear fault classification.

Simulation environment and automated data collection

Data for model training and validation were generated using the Personal Computer TRANsient Analyzer (PCTRAN)20, a simulation tool developed by Micro Simulation Technology for modeling various accident conditions in nuclear power plants. In this work, the CPR1000 three-loop PWR model was employed, with its specific operation interface illustrated in Fig. 1, to simulate typical fault conditions including LOCA, SGTR-B, and in-containment steam line break. This choice is primarily because all three are high-risk faults in CPR1000 pressurized water reactors: LOCA, the most severe design-basis accident, can easily lead to core meltdown (accounting for 32% of accidents requiring emergency shutdown); SGTR damages the loop isolation barrier, posing a risk of radioactive leakage; and in-containment steam line break causes a sudden pressure rise inside the containment, with a decision window of only 15–20 s. These three faults cover scenarios with “high/medium consequences, high/low frequencies” and different cognitive load levels, supporting the training and validation of the “human-influenced cognitive reliability model”.

Hybrid model architecture for fault diagnosis

The proposed diagnostic system, illustrated in Fig. 4, follows a pipeline of data preprocessing, feature extraction, classification prediction, combining the strengths of temporal feature modeling and ensemble classification. Transformer model uses a self-attention mechanism to extract long-range dependencies from multivariate time-series data such as temperature, pressure, and flow. It overcomes the limitations of RNNs and LSTMs in capturing temporal correlations over long durations. XGBoost Classifier is a gradient-boosted decision tree model that provides high-accuracy classification. Its performance is optimized through hyperparameter tuning and input feature selection.

Feature selection and optimization algorithms

To improve model performance and reduce dimensionality, RFECV was used to select six high-impact features from the original 92 parameters. Simultaneously, an RWOA based on LHS was employed to tune key XGBoost parameters, mitigating local optima issues and improving convergence.

Transformer model

The Transformer is a DL architecture proposed by Vaswani et al. The core of the model is the self-attention mechanism, which revolutionizes the processing logic of traditional sequence models21. Its purpose is to address the two main pain points of RNN/LSTM: RNN cannot batch-process long sequences due to its time-step recursive computation, and it has low training efficiency; while LSTM mitigates the vanishing gradient problem through gating mechanisms, it remains challenging to capture correlations beyond hundreds of data points22,23. Transformer leverages the advantages of encoder-decoder architecture, self-attention mechanisms, multi-head attention, and positional encoding to deeply extract temporal features. Parameters such as temperature, pressure, and flow rate generated during nuclear power plant operations are time-series data, and the Transformer’s self-attention mechanism can capture the correlations between these parameters at different time points (e.g., the time-series dependency between coolant temperature changes and reactor power fluctuations). It also explores complex fault patterns (e.g., early signs of gradual failures).

Methodology

RFECV and RWOA algorithm

The RFECV algorithm can screen out the optimal feature combination with the best generalization ability25. This algorithm has the dual advantages of feature dimension reduction and performance optimization, and is particularly suitable for scenarios with strict requirements for real-time performance and reliability, such as nuclear power plant fault diagnosis. It can accurately extract key parameters from massive sensor data and provide algorithmic support for the“high-value feature alliance”for model development.

In the traditional WOA, optimal parameters are solved by simulating the hunting behavior of humpback whales. The algorithm is divided into two stages: the prey search stage and the prey capture stage. However, the initial positions of individual whales are random, which makes it difficult for whales to be uniformly distributed in the solution space, thereby causing the WOA algorithm to easily get stuck in local optima. To address the shortcomings of the traditional WOA algorithm in terms of initialization and convergence mechanisms, this paper proposes the following improvement strategies: using LHS to generate the initial population26. LHS can generate a uniformly distributed and non-repeating set of points in the parameter space, significantly enhancing the diversity of the initial solutions. Model implementation based on transformer-RFECV-EWOA-XGBoost

In this model, we selected four operating conditions: normal operation, SGTR-B, SLBC, and LOCA. The original sample dataset was automatically obtained using AutoSave-PCTAN. In this experiment, all 92 feature parameters were selected in the initial stage, including nuclear power, regulator pressure, regulator water level, coolant flow rate, average coolant temperature, and steam generator water level. Then, six feature parameters were selected in the hybrid model stage. AutoSave-PCTAN automatically obtained 120,000 datasets, with 100 datasets of different fault sizes for each fault type, and 3,000 datasets collected for each fault size, with a sampling time interval of 1 s. Finally, the required datasets for the final model were obtained through operations such as time-slicing sampling. Ultimately, 281 datasets were obtained for each operating condition, with each dataset containing a six-dimensional array of length 20. In total, 89,920 datasets were assigned to the training set, and 11,240 datasets were assigned to the test set and validation set. The constructed model was trained using the training set, and the validation set was used to evaluate the training effectiveness.

Model parameter settings and hyperparameter optimization

To determine the optimal hyperparameter configuration for the model, this study systematically optimized the following eight hyperparameters: n_estimators, max_depth, learning_rate, gamma, min_child_weight, subsample, reg_alpha, and reg_lambda. Detailed descriptions of each parameter are provided in Table 1. The specific process of hyperparameter optimization using the RWOA algorithm is shown in Fig. 8, and the hyperparameter values obtained after optimization by this algorithm are shown in Table 2.During the optimization process, the algorithm first initializes the initial positions of the humpback whale individuals using LHS. This ensures that the initial population is uniformly distributed within the search space, enabling effective coverage of the solution space while significantly enhancing the diversity of the optimization process. Based on this, the fitness of each humpback whale’s initial position is calculated according to specific optimization objectives (such as function extremum solution or parameter fitness assessment), with fitness serving as a quantitative indicator for evaluating the “quality” of each position. Subsequently, individuals with the optimal positions and corresponding fitness values are selected from the initial population and designated as the “optimal solution candidates” for subsequent iterative processes. This method leverages bio-inspired strategies to achieve intelligent optimization of complex problems by skillfully balancing “exploration of new regions” with “optimization of known optimal regions.”

Results and discussion

In this study, four different scenarios were included: normal operation, SGTR-B, SLBLC, and LOCA. A total of 120,000 datasets were collected using the AutoSavePCTAN software, with 30,000 datasets for each fault scenario. Through time-slice sampling, 281 datasets were obtained for each operating condition, with each dataset containing a six-dimensional array of length 20. Overall, the training set, test set, and validation set were allocated 19,670, 8,430, and 8,430 datasets, respectively. The constructed model was trained using the training set, and the validation set was used to evaluate the training effectiveness. The nuclear power plant accident diagnosis model proposed in this paper, based on Transformer-RFECV-RWOA-XGBoost, has been successfully developed. By constructing a multi-dimensional performance evaluation system based on accuracy, recall rate, F₁ score, and precision, the performance of this model in identifying the operational status of nuclear reactors was quantitatively analyzed. By analyzing the fine-grained error distribution during the model training process (Fig. 9) and the confusion matrix (Fig. 10), the specific experimental results were subjected to an in-depth analysis and categorized into four aspects.

Validation metrics

The model demonstrates high consistency in its recognition performance across four types of nuclear operating conditions (as shown in Table 3). For label 0 (SLBLC), the accuracy rate reaches 0.9868, with precision, recall, and F₁-score all at 0.99. This indicates that in 100 actual identifications of this type of fault, only about 1 misclassification occurs (recall rate of 0.99 corresponds to a 1% false negative rate); and in 100 predictions of this type of fault, only about 1 is actually another condition (precision of 0.99 corresponds to a 1% false positive rate).Label 1 LOCA (Hot Leg)): The accuracy is 1.0000, and the precision, recall, and F₁-score all remain at 0.99. This result indicates that the model’s identification of this type of fault is virtually bias-free, with both the false negative rate and false positive rate controlled within 1%, demonstrating extremely strong classification robustness.Label 2 (SGTR-B): The accuracy rate is 0.9886, and the three core metrics (precision, recall, and F₁-score) are all 0.99. Similar to Label 0, the false negative and false positive risks are both below 1%, validating the model’s generalization adaptability to different fault types.Label 3 (Normal): The accuracy rate is 1.0000, with precision slightly lower at 0.98 but still maintaining an excellent level, while recall and F₁-score are both 0.99. This indicates that when identifying “no-fault” conditions, the probability of incorrectly classifying 2% of abnormal conditions as normal is extremely low, effectively ensuring the reliability of normal condition identification.

Training results

The training results in the figure can be analyzed from three aspects to demonstrate the model’s advantages: First, it has good convergence. In the initial stage of training, the training loss rapidly decreased from approximately 0.4 to below 0.1, and after 500 rounds, it stabilized at around 0.05. Although the validation loss fluctuated, it decreased overall, proving that the model’s ability to fit unseen data improved synchronously without overfitting. Second, the model demonstrates strong generalization capabilities. After 300 training rounds, the training and validation loss curves nearly overlap, and the accuracy curves show a coordinated trend. The training accuracy rapidly increases and maintains a high level, while the validation accuracy catches up after 50 rounds and stabilizes around 0.99, indicating that the model can adapt to new operational condition data and avoid false negatives and false positives; Third, the model exhibits strong stability. Initial fluctuations in validation accuracy are attributed to random initial parameters and trial-and-error adjustments, but the model stabilizes after 200 iterations, indicating it has mastered the core logic, providing a basis for prematurely terminating training for engineering applications. In summary, the model converges sufficiently, demonstrates strong generalization capability, and remains stable in the later stages, meeting the stringent requirements of fault diagnosis for nuclear power plants, ensuring efficiency and safety, and aligning with the characteristics of small-sample data.

Uncertainty analysis

The confusion matrix (Fig. 10) further reveals the detailed circumstances of the model’s misclassifications. Label 0 (SLBLC): A total of 29 misclassifications occurred, with a classification accuracy rate of 0.99 and a classification error rate of 0.01. Based on the physical characteristics of nuclear power plant operating conditions, it is speculated that this type of fault shares some overlap with the sensor responses of “SGTR-B” (Label 2) (e.g., similar steam flow and pressure fluctuation characteristics), leading to a small number of misclassifications in the feature boundary regions. Label 2 (SGTR-B): A total of 34 misclassifications occurred (with a classification error rate of 0.012), which can also be attributed to “feature coupling” with Label 0 faults—both fault types involve abnormalities in the steam circuit, and under low data dimensions or noise interference, the model struggles to fully distinguish subtle differences. Despite the aforementioned misclassifications, as shown in Fig. 10, the misclassification rates for both fault types are kept below 2%, and misclassifications under other operating conditions are negligible. This indicates that the model effectively suppresses noise and feature coupling interference in identifying complex nuclear reactor operating conditions, maintaining high robustness.

Comparison of different algorithms and analysis of model advantages

To validate the advantages of the Transformer-RFECV-RWOA-XGBoost hybrid model, pure Transformer (without feature optimization) and pure XGBoost (without temporal enhancement) were selected as baseline models. The performance improvement logic was analyzed by comparing the cross-validation accuracy matrices. The accuracy distributions of the three algorithms showed significant differences: the hybrid model achieved an accuracy of 0.99 in the“reactor SLBC”and “SGTR-B”fault categories, and an accuracy of 1.00 in the “LOCA”and“normal operating conditions”categories. Overall, it exhibited a high-confidence distribution across all categories, with only a few feature-coupled samples showing misclassification. As shown in Fig. 11-a, the pure Transformer model has a high number of misclassifications across all operating conditions, with an average accuracy of 0.955 and a minimum accuracy of only 0.898 (e.g., steam generator failure). Its shortcomings include the absence of RFECV feature selection, which leads to noise interference at classification boundaries, and the lack of RWOA-optimized hyperparameters, making it difficult to adapt to the “small sample size, strong coupling” characteristics of nuclear operating conditions.As shown in Fig. 11-b, the pure XGBoost model achieved an average accuracy of 0.966, which is better than the pure Transformer model but still lower than the hybrid model. Due to the lack of Transformer’s deep encoding of temporal features, it cannot uncover the “long-term dependencies” in nuclear accidents, resulting in high misclassification rates in dynamic faults such as coolant loss, and it struggles to distinguish between gradual faults and instantaneous fluctuations. The hybrid model achieves a significant improvement in accuracy through feature optimization, hyperparameter tuning, and the synergistic integration of temporal-tree models, fully validating the innovative architecture and its adaptability to nuclear power plant conditions.

Attributing performance differences

The breakthrough in the accuracy of the hybrid model stems from the coordinated optimization of three key components: first, the RFECV algorithm is used to select six core features of the nuclear power plant’s operating conditions (such as coolant flow rate and steam pressure change rate), eliminating redundant features and reducing the input dimension from 96 to 6. This results in a 12% reduction in misclassification rate compared to the pure Transformer model, with clearer classification boundaries; second, the RWOA algorithm is used to optimize the hyperparameters of XGBoost (e.g., max_depth = 5, learning_rate = 0.1) to adapt to small-sample scenarios, improving cross-validation accuracy by 4.2% compared to the default parameters of pure XGBoost, and reducing the false-negative rate in normal operating conditions from 5% to 0%; Third, the Transformer and XGBoost work together, with the former compensating for the tree model’s inability to capture long-term dependencies and the latter addressing the Transformer’s overfitting issue in small-sample scenarios, reducing the misclassification rate in SLBC scenarios from 8% to 1%.

Extending the value of comparative experiments

A comparative validation of the three types of algorithms revealed the three core advantages of the hybrid model. First, the hybrid model achieves an accuracy of ≥ 0.99 across all operating conditions, representing a 2.5% improvement over pure models (maximum 0.966), setting a new benchmark for accuracy in nuclear operating condition identification. This means that in 1,000 fault identifications, the hybrid model can reduce misjudgments by approximately 25 instances, directly lowering the risk of underreporting or misreporting nuclear accidents. Second, for faults such as“SGTR-B,”which exhibit“high feature coupling and concealed development trends,”the hybrid model’s misjudgment rate (1%) is significantly lower than that of the pure model (≥ 10%). This validates its adaptability to the“strong interference, weak signal”scenarios in the nuclear industry, providing technical reference for future complex nuclear accident diagnosis.

Limitations of the model

Although this study has achieved high diagnostic accuracy, it still has certain limitations, mainly reflected in the failure to fully consider the uncertainty in data sources and system reliability, as well as the lack of targeted interpretability analysis and uncertainty analysis. Specifically, the experimental process failed to incorporate inherent noise in sensor measurement data under real-world scenarios and interfering factors such as uncertainty in the input data sources themselves, which to a certain extent affects the generalizability of the model in actual operating conditions.

To address the aforementioned limitations, future research will adopt interpretability and uncertainty modeling frameworks, focusing on two core dimensions to conduct systematic verification efforts: first, whether noise in the input data has a significant impact on the model’s diagnostic accuracy; second, whether the model can exhibit stable and excellent robustness when confronted with such interference. This will thereby provide more solid theoretical and experimental support for the engineering application of the model.

Conclusions

This study successfully developed a hybrid diagnostic model based on Transformer-RFECV-RWOA-XGBoost, achieving breakthrough performance in identifying accidents at the CPR1000 nuclear power plant: the model exceeded 0.98 in key indicators (accuracy/recall rate/F₁) in four types of operating conditions, with 100% accuracy in coolant loss accidents and normal operating conditions, and an actual fault diagnosis rate of 99.46%. Its strong robustness (loss value converges to 0.05, validation accuracy fluctuation < 0.01) and controllable false positive rate (only boundary samples with labels 0/2 have a false positive rate < 2%) strictly meet nuclear safety requirements. The technical advantages stem from a triple synergistic mechanism—RFECV feature dimension reduction (96 dimensions→6 dimensions), RWOA hyperparameter optimization, and the complementary integration of Transformer-XGBoost—which improves the average accuracy by 2.5–4.2% compared to the baseline model and reduces misclassification of complex faults by 12%. This model establishes a highly reliable intelligent safety network, providing critical technical support for nuclear power plant safety operations and maintenance.
