Development and validation of a novel risk stratification signature derived from migrasome and tumor microenvironment-related genes for molecular subtyping and improving clinical outcomes in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Huifang Chai, Chuchu Zhou, Zixin Xue, Min Zhang, Ling Qiao, Jianjun Wang, Feng Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe tumor microenvironment (TME) and migrasomes released by tumor cells significantly influence carcinogenesis and immune evasion. However, our understanding of the prognostic and therapeutic implications of migrasome and tumor microenvironment-related genes (mtmRGs) in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) remains limited.MethodsWe explored the relationship between mtmRGs and HNSCC prognosis by utilizing The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases. Subsequently, we developed an innovative prognostic signature, and assessed its prognostic significance using the Kaplan-Meier method, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC), and Cox regression analyses. To explore the underlying mechanisms, we conducted gene set variation analysis (GSVA), gene set enrichment analysis (GESA), and immune infiltration analysis. A nomogram was developed to estimate the overall survival (OS) rates for HNSCC patients. Lastly, we chose P4HA1, which was part of the signature, for additional experimental validation in vitro and in vivo.ResultsThe mtmRGs signature effectively classifies HNSCC patients into two distinct risk subgroups, with the high-risk cohort demonstrating significantly poorer OS. The risk score serves as an independent prognostic factor for HNSCC patients; those with lower risk scores are more likely to exhibit favorable responses to immunotherapy, particularly with CTLA4 inhibitors. Furthermore, a lower risk score is significantly correlated with the sensitivity of HNSCC patients to cyclophosphamide, gemcitabine, and axitinib.ConclusionThis study presents an innovative gene signature associated with mtmRGs, which may be utilized both for predicting survival and directing personalized chemotherapy and immunotherapy regiments for patients with HNSCC.Keywords: Migrasome, tumor microenvironment, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, prognostic signature

Introduction

Worldwide, head and neck cancers (HNC) rank as the sixth most prevalent cancer type, with an estimated annual occurrence of approximately 650,000 new cases [1]. The most prevalent form of HNC is head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), known for its high mortality linked to postoperative recurrence, metastasis, and unfavorable prognosis [2]. In spite of numerous advancements in surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy over recent decades, the 5-year survival rate for patients with HNSCC continues to hover around 40%–50% [3,4]. Currently, a range of clinical pathological parameters, such as clinical stage, local recurrence, depth of invasion, and metastasis to cervical lymph nodes, have been identified as established factors influencing patient prognosis. Nevertheless, these prognostic indicators, along with the conventional TNM staging system, fall short in effectively stratifying patients and predicting outcomes. Significant discrepancies in survival rates are frequently noted among individuals classified within the same category [5]. Consequently, it is imperative to discover promising new biomarkers and unravel molecular mechanisms that contribute to carcinogenesis to inform the development of innovative diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for HNSCC.

The migrasome is a recently identified cellular structure. This organelle-like entity arises from the detachment of cells at the ends of contraction fibers during the process of migration [6]. Typically, its diameter ranges from 0.5 to 2 μm and it comprises various cellular elements, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [7–10]. Regarding the mechanism of its formation, migrating cells generate slender contraction fibers, where the tips of these fibers gradually accumulate materials, ultimately developing into vesicular structures that separate, resulting in the formation of migrasome [11,12]. Tumor cells exhibit a heightened ability to migrate, which plays a crucial role in the process of tumor metastasis. It is possible that migrasome offers a novel mechanism to facilitate tumor cell migration [13,14]. As tumor cells migrate, they release migrasomes, which might harbor molecules that influence extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), to break down extracellular matrix elements like collagen and fibronectin. This degradation assists cancer cells in more easily penetrating tissue and migrating to distant sites in the body [15–18]. Additionally, migrasome may act as a platform for signaling, releasing factors that attract nearby cells or provoke changes in other cells, thereby enhancing the migratory capacity of tumor cells [19–21].

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a multifaceted ecosystem comprising various cell types, including tumor cells, immune cells, fibroblasts, and vascular endothelial cells, along with the extracellular matrix, which significantly influences carcinogenesis, immune evasion, and resistance to therapy in malignancies [22–26]. Migrasomes can transport signaling molecules such as growth factors, cytokines, or chemokines to modulate intercellular communication within the TME [27,28]. For instance, migrasomes may deliver vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which is known to facilitate tumor angiogenesis [29]. Tumor angiogenesis is essential for the proliferation and metastasis of tumors, as it supplies necessary nutrients and oxygen to tumor cells and acts as a conduit for these cells to disseminate into the bloodstream and establish distant metastases. Furthermore, migrasomes can also influence the functionality of immune cells in the TME [30]. Within this environment, the activity of immune cells is frequently suppressed. By releasing certain immune regulatory molecules like PD-L1 [31], migrasomes may inhibit immune cell function, assisting tumor cells in evading immune detection. Given the critical roles of both migrasomes and TME in tumor progression, recent studies have identified novel TME-associated gene signatures for the diagnosis and prognosis of various cancers, including breast [32], ovarian [33], lung [34,35], colon [36], and bladder cancer [37]. Additionally, Qin et al. conducted a comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of migrasomes and found that migrasome-related genes (MRGs), particularly TSPAN4, exhibited high expression levels in kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), mesothelioma (MESO), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), and uveal melanoma (UVM). Further survival analyses indicated that TSPAN4 served as a prognostic risk factor in skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), UVM, brain lower grade glioma (LGG), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), acute myeloid leukemia (LAML), and liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), while acting as a protective factor in KIRP, MESO, and KIRC [14]. Despite this, there are few studies that focused on the roles of migrasomes and TME-related genes (mtmRGs) in the initiation, progression, and prognosis of HNSCC.

Currently, bioinformatics analysis serves as a crucial link between fundamental research and clinical applications by extracting and interpreting biological data [38]. Traditional approaches often focus on one or a few genes or proteins; however, bioinformatics analysis enables the consideration of complex biological systems as integrated units. Its potential in areas such as disease prediction, precision medicine, and drug development is increasingly recognized in clinical settings [39,40]. For instance, by employing algorithms such as the Cox proportional hazards model and random forest, clinicians can integrate patient clinical characteristics (e.g. age and stage) with molecular data (including gene mutations and gene or protein expression) to estimate the risk of postoperative recurrence or survival rates [41]. Furthermore, tumors are classified into various molecular subtypes (e.g. Luminal and HER2+ types of breast cancer) based on gene expression profiles (such as those from the TCGA database) to inform targeted therapy decisions [42,43]. The degree of immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment can also be assessed using bioinformatics tools (e.g. the ESTIMATE algorithm), which aids in predicting response rates to immunotherapy (such as PD-1 inhibitors) and avoiding ineffective treatments [44]. Additionally, the genetic variability of drug metabolism genes (including those from the CYP450 family) in patients can be analyzed to forecast drug metabolism rates and potential toxic reactions, thereby facilitating personalized dosing strategies [45]. In this research, we recognized the prognostic mtmRGs in HNSCC and constructed a new prognostic risk signature to predict the prognosis and therapeutic response of HNSCC patients. The validation of this risk signature was performed using a combined GEO database that included two independent external cohorts. Subsequently, we verified the expression of the identified signature genes and assessed their prognostic significance by utilizing the GEPIA and GSE65858 databases, respectively. Furthermore, we investigated the correlation between risk scores, immune infiltration levels, responses to immunotherapy, and sensitivities to chemotherapy agents. Lastly, we conducted preliminary explorations into the molecular mechanisms through which the prognostic risk gene P4HA1 affects HNSCC prognosis via both in vitro and in vivo experimental methods. Consequently, our study may provide new directions for personalized treatment strategies and prognostic assessments for patients with HNSCC, highlighting the interplay between migrasome and TME.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The RNA sequencing data along with clinical follow-up details for HNSCC were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and served as the training dataset. All Fragments Per Kilobase Million (FPKM) transcription values underwent log transformation and were subsequently converted into transcripts per million (TPM) prior to analysis. The chip datasets that included follow-up information and contained over 50 cases, GSE41613 (n = 97) and GSE65858 (n = 270), were sourced from the publicly available Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). In the TCGA_HNSCC RNA-sequencing data and the GEO chip datasets, samples lacking comprehensive clinical follow-up information, such as survival time and survival status, were excluded. To address duplicate gene symbols or instances where multiple probes represented a single gene, the gene with the highest mean expression was selected. For the establishment of an external validation cohort, the GSE41613 and GSE65858 datasets were combined as the test datasets, with the ‘sva’ package being employed to adjust for potential batch effects and other unwanted variations inherent in the different databases [46]. Following preprocessing, we identified 527 TCGA_HNSCC samples and 347 GEO dataset samples. The clinical characteristics of the samples are detailed in Table S1. Gene-level copy number variation (CNV) and mutation data for HNSCC were sourced from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Information regarding tumor microenvironment-related genes and migrasome genes was derived from earlier research [47,48]. Subsequently, by identifying the overlap between migrasome-related and metabolism-related genes associated with HNSCC, a set of migrasome and metabolism-related genes (mtmRGs) was defined for further analysis.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The DEGs were identified using the ‘limma’ package in R (version 4.4.1) by comparing normal samples with tumor samples, as well as high-risk groups against low-risk groups utilizing data from the TCGA and GEO databases. A |Log (2) fold change| greater than 1 and an adjusted p-value of less than 0.05 were established as the criteria for identifying DEGs. To visualize these DEGs, the ‘GdcVolcanoPlot’ package in R was utilized to create a volcano plot.

Construction and validation of prognostic signatures based on differentially expressed mtmRGs

A novel prognostic gene signature related to migrasome and metabolism was developed through both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses by utilizing the ‘Glmnet’ and ‘Survival’ R packages [49] based on differentially expressed mtmRGs source from the TCGA_HNSCC database. The risk score for every patient was computed based on the expression levels of these key genes and their corresponding regression coefficients, as follows: Risk score=∑i=1nCoefi×expri. In this formula, ‘Coef’ and ‘expr’ stands for the regression coefficient and the expression level of each pivotal mtmRGs, respectively. The variable ‘n’ denotes the count of prognostic mtmRGs in the risk model. The integrated GEO database, which consists of data from GSE41613 and GSE65858, was utilized to validate the prognostic signature. The prognostic value of the signature was assessed using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. To further ascertain whether the risk signature serves as an independent risk factor, univariate and multivariate Cox analyses were employed. Following this, we conducted correlation analyses between risk scores and clinical parameters, as well as stratification analysis and nomogram construction based on clinicopathological characteristics. Finally, calibration plots and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) were employed to evaluate the prediction accuracy and dependability of the nomogram model.

Analyses of immune landscape, immunotherapy response and chemotherapy susceptibility

The comparison of the immune landscape among low- and high-risk groups was conducted utilizing various immune-related algorithms. The immune activity or function of immune cells, as well as the immune pathways for each sample, was evaluated through single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). Furthermore, the immune score, stromal score, and estimate score were assessed using the ESTIMATE algorithm. The composition of infiltrating immune cells within each tumor sample was obtained from both the TIMER database and the CIBERSORT algorithm. The expression levels of immune checkpoint genes and members of the class I human leukocyte antigen (HLA) family were analyzed between high-risk and low-risk groups via the ‘limma’ package. The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA) was employed (https://tcia.at/), which offers extensive immunogenomic analyses founded on the TCGA dataset. We harnessed the TCIA database to assess the predictive capability of risk scores concerning the immunotherapy response in HNSCC patients by using the immunophenoscore (IPS). Additionally, the sensitivity of 198 drugs for each HNSCC patient was evaluated based on pharmacogenomics data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC, https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) by utilizing the R package ‘oncoPredict’. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of chemotherapeutic agents were contrasted between HNSCC patients categorized with high- and low-risk scores, where a reduced IC50 value signified increased sensitivity.

Cell culture, stable transfection of shRNA and rescue experiment

Cellular cultivation, plasmid assembly, transfection procedures, and rescue experiments were conducted as described in prior studies [50]. Relevant details can be found in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section of the article.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer, which contained 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.4, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, and a 1× cocktail of protease inhibitors (Roche, Lewes, UK). Protein concentrations were determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Pierce, Rockford, IL). Equal protein amounts were subjected to SDS-PAGE and afterwards transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes with a pore size of 0.45 μm. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the specified primary antibodies. After the primary incubation, membranes were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Inc., NJ) and the ChemiScope 3000mini system (CLiNX, China). The strength of the bands of interest was recorded and quantified through Grayscale scanning with Image J software (NIH), while GAPDH served as the internal control [50,51]. Comprehensive details regarding the antibodies employed in this research can be found in Supplementary Table S2.

Transwell and wound healing assays

Transwell filters (Coster), featuring pore dimensions of 8.0 μm, underwent treatment with Matrigel (BD Biosciences) that was diluted at a 1:6 ratio in serum-free medium. A total of 2 × 104 cells in 200 μl of the serum-free medium were placed into the upper chamber that had been coated with Matrigel and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After gently scraping off the cells from the top of the filter using cotton swabs, those that remained attached to the bottom were fixed using 3.7% formaldehyde and subsequently stained with 1% crystal violet (Sigma). The number of invading cells was then counted under a microscope at a magnification of 200×. For the wound healing assay, roughly 5 × 105 cells were seeded in 12-well plates. These cells were allowed to grow in antibiotic-free medium until they reached 90% confluence. A linear wound was then carefully created in the confluent monolayer using a pipette tip and a ruler. After three washes with PBS, fresh serum-free medium was added. Photographic documentation of the wounds was taken at time 0 h and again at the 24 h interval. Throughout the experiment, the cells were maintained in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Wound size was evaluated at three random locations perpendicular to the wound. The wound healing rate (%) was calculated as follows: ((0 h scratch area − 24 h scratch area)/0 h scratch area)) × 100% [52]. Each experiment was thoroughly repeated at least three times.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments conducted in this research received approval from the Animal Care and Research Committee at the Medical School of Henan University, under the approval number HUSOM-2018-282. Five-week-old female BALB/C nude mice (Weitong Lihua Animal Co. located in Beijing, China) were randomly divided into the experiment (shP4HA1) and control (shNC) groups (n = 5/group), and were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility with unrestricted access to food and water at our institution. HN4 cells, persistently infected with lentiviruses carrying either shP4HA1 or a control shRNA, were collected for the analysis of pulmonary metastasis in mice. A single-cell suspension containing 2.6 × 105 cells in 200 μL of PBS was administered via tail veins of the BALB/C nude mice to create a lung metastasis model. After a duration of six weeks, all mice were euthanized through the administration of an excessive dose of pentobarbital sodium [53,54], and their lungs were excised and fixed in 10% neutral formalin. The metastatic capability was evaluated using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

Statistical analysis

All statistical evaluations were performed using R software (version 4.4.1) or the SangerBox platform (http://sangerbox.com/), while GraphPad was employed for processing experimental data. Continuous data is presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless indicated otherwise. When comparing continuous variables across two groups, the Student’s t-test was applied if the data followed a normal distribution; otherwise, the Wilcoxon test or Kruskal–Wallis test was utilized. The selection between the chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test for evaluating categorical variables was based on the sizes of the samples. Relationships among datasets were assessed using Spearman’s correlation. All analyses conducted in this research were two-sided, and a significance level of 0.05 was established for statistical relevance.

Results

Screening migrasome and tumor microenvironment-related genes (mtmRGs) affecting the prognosis of patients with HNSCC

In this research, we initially identified 4061 the tumor microenvironment-relate genes and 7 migrasomes genes through a comprehensive literature review (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4) [47,48]. Subsequently, we conducted a correlation analysis of individual genes using the cBioportal and Ualcan databases, resulting in the detection of 7,179 and 4,506 migrasome-related genes (mRGs) in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), respectively (|Spearman’s Correlation| ≥ 0.4, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). To explore the close connection between migrasomes and the tumor microenvironment, we identified a total of 722 genes as both migrasome and tumor microenvironment-related genes (mtmRGs) through Venn diagram intersection analysis (Figure 1A). We further examined the differential expression of these 722 mtmRGs in HNSCC tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues using the TCGA database. This analysis revealed 147 differentially expressed mtmRGs (DE-mtmRGs) based on the criteria (|logFC| > 1.0, p < 0.05), comprising 81 genes that were upregulated and 66 that were downregulated (Figure 1B and Supplementary Table S7). Then, a univariate Cox regression analysis indicated that 20 out of the 147 DE-mtmRGs were significantly correlated with patient prognosis, which included eight protective mtmRGs and 12 risk mtmRGs within the TCGA_HNSCC database (Figure 1C and Supplementary Table S8). Additionally, the network plot distinctly illustrates the relationships among the expression levels of these 20 mtmRGs (Figure 1D). Given that chromosomal regions often exhibit alterations such as losses or gains in HNSCC patients [55], we retrieved CNV data from the TCGA database to further investigate the chromosomal alterations of these mtmRGs and their respective chromosomal locations (Supplementary Figure S1A,B). Notably, FN1 showed significant alteration with ‘loss’ on chromosome 2, whereas PTPRB primarily exhibited a ‘gain’ alteration on chromosome 12. Finally, we assessed the mutation prevalence of these 20 mtmRGs across 504 HNSCC patients, finding mutations in these 20 mtmRGs present in 78 individuals. Figure S1C–F highlights the top 10 mutated mtmRGs, underscoring their critical involvement in the pathogenesis of HNSCC. In particular, PTPRB and FN1 stood out as the most frequently mutated mtmRGs.

Establishment and validation of a mtmRGs-based prognostic risk signature

Acknowledging the influence of mtmRGs on the outcomes for HNSCC patients, a prognostic signature was developed to explore the intricate complexities associated with HNSCC. Using the TCGA_HNSCC database as the training cohort and a combined database comprising GSE65858 and GSE41613 as the testing cohort, we performed further multivariate Cox regression analysis on the previously mentioned 20 prognosis-related mtmRGs. A prognostic risk model was created utilizing six genes: FN1, PLAU, P4HA1, EBF1, PRR11, and RGS4 (Supplementary Table S9 and Figure 2A). The coefficients for each gene in the signature are illustrated in Figure 2B. The risk score formula is defined as follows: risk score = (0.143 × FN1 expression) + (0.206 × PLAU expression) + (0.328 × P4HA1 expression) + (−0.569 × EBF1 expression) + (0.311 × PRR11 expression) + (0.149 × RGS4 expression). Figure 2C demonstrates the relationship between risk score and the expressions of FN1, PLAU, P4HA1, EBF1, PRR11, and RGS4. Notably, the expression levels of FN1, PLAU, P4HA1, PRR11, and RGS4 showed significant differences when comparing tumor tissues with normal tissues based on the GEPIA database (Supplementary Figure S2A). Additionally, survival analysis indicated that HNSCC patients exhibiting high expression levels of FN1, PLAU, P4HA1, and RGS4 experienced shorter survival times in the GSE65858 database, as determined by LOGpc online tools (https://bioinfo.henu.edu.cn/DatabaseList.jsp) (Supplementary Figure S2B). Furthermore, based on the median risk score, HNSCC patients in both the training and testing cohorts were classified into high-risk and low-risk groups accordingly. Principal component analysis (PCA) and t-SNE indicated markedly distinct distribution patterns between the high-risk and low-risk groups, reflecting the efficacy of this prognostic risk model (Supplementary Figure S2C and S2D). Moreover, Kaplan–Meier curves illustrated that individuals in the high-risk category had a poorer prognosis (Figure 2D,F). The time-dependent ROC curves for OS in the TCGA training cohort at 1, 3, and 5 years demonstrated area under the curve values of 0.646, 0.706, and 0.659, respectively, while the testing cohort presented values of 0.653, 0.640, and 0.638 (Figure 2E,G). Figure 2H,I illustrate the risk scores along with survival status distributions. The DCA conducted within the TCGA cohort suggested that this model serves as a useful tool in clinical practice, potentially benefiting patients with HNSCC in terms of OS (Figure 2J–L).

Comparision of the mtmRGs signature with other reported gene signatures

To further assess the predictive capability of the mtmRGs signature, five existing gene signatures derived from the works of Wang [56], Jiang [57], Cao [58], Zhang [59], and Liu [60] were chosen for comparative analysis. Utilizing the corresponding genes from these five risk models, we calculated the risk score for each patient by employing the same methodology applied in the TCGA training cohort. Subsequent analyses included time-dependent ROC and Kaplan-Meier survival assessments. We found a significant prognostic difference between high-risk and low-risk groups across all five risk models. The ROC analysis indicated that the AUC for the mtmRGs signature concerning 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were 0.646, 0.706, and 0.659, respectively. Notably, these AUC values surpassed those of the gene signatures presented by Wang, Jiang, Cao, Zhang, and Liu (Figures 2E and 3A–E). Furthermore, as illustrated in Figure 3F, our risk model based on mtmRGs achieved the highest C-index at 0.64. Additionally, the RMS time curve for all six prognostic models further showed that this 6-gene signature exhibited the steepest slope (Figure 3G), suggesting an enhanced capability for estimating HNSCC survival through the mtmRGs prognostic signature.

Establishment of a prognostic nomogram for HNSCC patients

Taking into account the impact of clinicopathological factors on the predictive model, in order to quantitatively forecast the prognosis of patients and provide a basis for clinical decision-making, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed utilizing the TCGA_HNSCC database. We determined that risk score, age, and clinical stage serve as independent prognostic risk factors significantly affecting the prognosis of HNSCC patients (Figure 4A,B). Then, a nomogram was subsequently developed based on the essential variables of multiple factors (Figure 4C). To validate the accuracy of this predictive model, calibration curves were employed, revealing that the predictions for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival outcomes were closely aligned with the standard reference curves (Figure 4D). This alignment indicates a robust predictive performance for the model in question. Additionally, we utilized DCA to further assess the model’s reliability [61], and findings suggested that this nomogram serves as an effective tool for predicting both short and long-term survival in HNSCC patients (Figure 4E). The accompanying forest plot highlights that within the nomogram, risk score and age emerged as the predominant factors influencing prognosis (Figure 4F). Collectively, these findings suggest that the nomogram, which incorporates risk scores derived from mtmRGs, presents a valuable and practical method for predicting patient outcomes in a clinical setting.

Association of the risk model with immune infiltration and immunotherapy responses in HNSCC

The immune microenvironment of tumors is intricately linked to the effectiveness of treatments and the outcomes for patients with malignant tumors. We conducted a study examining the correlation between risk scores and immune cell infiltration utilizing data from TCGA_HNSC. Figure 5A illustrates that there is a positive correlation between risk scores and the presence of natural killer T cells, as well as CD56dim and CD56bright natural killer cells. In contrast, a negative correlation was observed with the presence of activated B cells, activated CD4/CD8 T cells, eosinophils, immature B cells, immature dendritic cells, MDSC, macrophages, mast cells, monocytes, T follicular helper cells, type 1 T helper cells, and type 17 T helper cells. Our ssGSEA analysis corroborated these findings, revealing that the majority of immune cell types were less prevalent in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group, which suggests that HNSCC patients with elevated risk scores experience an immunosuppressed condition (Figure 5B). Moreover, applying the CIBERSORT algorithm, we recorded a notable reduction in the presence of several immune cell types including naïve B cells, plasma cells, CD8+ T cells, memory activated CD4+ T cells, follicular helper T cells, regulatory T cells, and resting mast cells, accompanied by a significant rise in memory resting CD4+ T cells, resting NK cells, M0 macrophages, and activated mast cells within the high-risk cohort (Figure 5C). Additionally, analysis using the ESTIMATE algorithm indicated that the low-risk group exhibited higher immune and ESTIMATE scores (Figure 5D). These findings strongly imply that the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment may play a role in the poorer prognosis observed in high-risk HNSCC patients, which warrants further investigation for validation.

In recent times, extensive research has explored the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors in cancer immunotherapy, with many patients suffering from HNSCC experiencing substantial clinical advantages from this treatment. Nevertheless, identifying patients who are likely to benefit from immunotherapy poses a significant challenge. Our findings revealed that 15 out of the 20 immune checkpoints exhibited considerable modulation within the high-risk cohort (Supplementary Figure S3A). The expression levels of immune checkpoint genes such as GEM, CD276, and CD47 showed a strong positive correlation with risk scores (Supplementary Figure S3B–D), whereas CTLA4 and PDCD1 expressions were found to negatively correlate with risk scores (Supplementary Figures S3E, F). To further our investigation, we examined the expression levels of class I human leukocyte antigen (HLA) family members, given their crucial role in neoantigen presentation and the activity of cytolytic T cells by displaying intracellular peptides on the cell surface. A deficiency in HLA can hinder the capacity of cells to present novel antigens, potentially resulting in immune tolerance [62]. Our results demonstrated significant differences in the expression of multiple HLA family members between the two risk categories (Supplementary Figure S3G). Specifically, the levels of HLA-DRB6, HLA-DRB5, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DRA, HLA-DQB1, HLA-DQB2, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DQA2, HLA-DPB1, HLA-DPB2, HLA-DPA1, HLA-DOA, HLA-DOB, HLA-DMA, and HLA-DMB were markedly reduced in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group. The cancer immunome atlas (TCIA) (https://tcia.at/) serves as a resource providing detailed immunogenomic analyses derived from TCGA data. Given the above results, we further leveraged the TCIA database to assess the immunotherapy responses of HNSCC patients categorized by different risk scores using the immunophenoscore (IPS). The findings demonstrated that the IPS for both the CTLA4 inhibitor and the combination of CTLA4 and PD1 inhibitors in the low-risk cohort was notably greater than that observed in the high-risk cohort (Figure 5E–H). This strongly implies that patients with HNSCC who possess a lower risk score are likely to experience a more favorable response to immunotherapy, particularly with CTLA4 inhibitors.

Risk score predicts chemotherapeutic benefits in HNSCC

Although chemotherapy plays a crucial role in the clinical management of head and neck tumors, the diversity among tumors presents significant challenges when selecting anti-tumor medications. In this study, we chose five chemotherapeutic agents—paclitaxel, cyclophosphamide, gemcitabine, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil—alongside three targeted therapies: axitinib, erlotinib, and gefitinib, which are frequently employed in the treatment of HNSCC. We then determined the inhibitory concentration (IC50) of each drug and examined the relationship between risk score and the clinical response to these chemotherapy agents using the R package pRRophetic. Our analysis revealed that HNSCC patients classified as low-risk exhibited lower IC50 values for cyclophosphamide, gemcitabine, and axitinib. On the other hand, patients in the high-risk category demonstrated a more favorable response to the chemotherapy agent paclitaxel (Figure 6A–H). Furthermore, to further assess the effectiveness of risk score as a predictive index for drug response in HNSCC, we estimated the IC50 values for 198 medications in TCGA_HNSCC patients by performing the oncoPredict package (Supplementary Table S10). Our results suggested that both high- and low-risk groups could show increased sensitivity to bortezomib, dactinomycin, docetaxel, sepantronium bromide, daporinad, vinblastine, among others, which may serve as reliable guidance for clinical treatment. Supplementary Table S11 provides detailed information regarding the top 10 sensitive drugs for each risk subgroup. However, the relationship between the sensitivity of chemical drugs and the aforementioned risk scores is currently derived solely from bioinformatics analyses. A significant number of experimental studies will be required in the future to validate these relevant conclusions. In summary, the mtmRGs associated with risk scores that we developed show significant promise in predicting patient prognosis and the advantages of immunotherapy and chemotherapy.

Association of risk scores with biological functions and pathways

Initially, a GO enrichment analysis encompassing biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) was conducted using 814 differentially expressed genes associated with risk. These genes were determined based on the median risk score via the limma package from the TCGA_HNSCC dataset. The BP primarily relate to the production of immune response mediators (GO:0002440), leukocyte mediated immunity (GO:0002443), and immunoglobulin production (GO:0002377). In terms of CC, key associations include immunoglobulin complex (GO:0019814), plasma membrane signaling receptor complex (GO:0098802), and T cell receptor complex (GO:0042101). The FM predominantly cover antigen binding (GO:0003823) (Supplementary Figure S4A, B). Subsequently, we applied the GSVA and GSEA packages to examine the differential enrichment of KEGG pathways between groups classified as high-risk and low-risk. The high-risk group, noted for having the poorest prognosis, is particularly associated with ECM_RECEPTOR and FOCAL_ADHESION pathways, which are critical routes for tumor cells to evade their original growth sites and establish themselves in new locations (Supplementary Figure S5A,B). Conversely, the pathways involved in PRIMARY_IMMUNODEFICIENCY and CELL_ADHESION_MOLECULES_CAMS were notably enriched within the low-risk group (Supplementary Figure S5C). These findings suggest that risk-related genes in HNSCC are largely concentrated in biological pathways associated with ECM receptors and focal adhesion, which are intricately linked to immune responses, indicating their potential role as significant factors influencing the prognosis of high-risk HNSCC patients.

Validation of key indicator P4HA1 of mtmRGs risk model by experiments

Prolyl 14-hydroxylase subunit α1 (P4HA1) serves as the critical limiting component of prolyl 14-hydroxylase (P4H) [63]. Prior studies have suggested that P4HA1 can enhance the aggressive progression of prostate, breast, and pancreatic cancers [64–66]. Our findings indicated that P4HA1 emerged as the key risk factor within the mtmRGs-related risk model (Figure 2A,B). The level of P4HA1 protein expression in HNSCC tissues was notably elevated compared to the normal control group, as evidenced by data from the Protein Human Atlas (PHA) database (Supplementary Figure S6A). Survival analyses performed via Kaplan-Meier across various independent GEO databases (GSE65858, GSE31056, and GSE3292) and TCGA_HNSCC supported the idea that P4HA1 functions as a negative regulatory element affecting the survival of HNSCC patients, utilizing the LOGpc online platform (https://bioinfo.henu.edu.cn/DatabaseList.jsp) (Supplementary Figure S6B). However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which P4HA1 influences malignant behaviors and patient prognosis in HNSCC remain to be elucidated. Consequently, P4HA1 was selected for further study.

Initially, we explored the possible functions of P4HA1 in distinct HNSCC cell lines by employing the CancerSEA database (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/CancerSEA/). Research conducted by Puram SV (EXP0063) revealed a positive association between heightened P4HA1 levels and various processes including metastasis, angiogenesis, quiescence, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and hypoxia, with Spearman coefficients recorded at 0.40, 0.40, 0.34, 0.33, and 0.32, respectively (Figure 7A). Utilizing GSEA analysis, several essential regulatory genes associated with the TGF-β signaling pathway were identified as consistently enriched in HNSCC patients exhibiting high levels of P4HA1 in the TCGA_HNSCC, GSE30784, and GSE27020 datasets, indicating a favorable relationship between P4HA1 expression and the TGF-β signaling pathway (Figure 7B). Subsequently, we evaluated the protein expression of P4HA1 in the HOK-16A, CAL27, HN4, SCC25, FaDu, and HN6 cell lines through Western blotting methodologies. Importantly, P4HA1 was markedly overexpressed in HN4 and SCC25 cells relative to the normal oral epithelial reference line, HOK-16A (Figure 7C). To achieve silencing of endogenous P4HA1, we carried out lentiviral transfection using shRNA targeted against P4HA1 in both HN4 and SCC25 cells (Figure 7D). Our findings illustrated that P4HA1 knockdown significantly reduced proliferation and migration in both cell lines, as evaluated by the colony formation assay (Figure 7E), transwell migration assay (Figure 7F), and wound healing assay (Supplementary Figure S6C). Furthermore, an in vivo assay for lung metastasis demonstrated that mice with HN4/sh3P4HA1 presented fewer pulmonary metastatic nodules and reduced lung weights compared to the mock control group (Figure 7G–I and Supplementary Figure S6D). Additionally, we evaluated the primary proteins involved in the TGF-β signaling pathway, with results depicted in Figure 7J. Following the knockdown of P4HA1, total expression levels of Smad2 and Smad3 showed no significant alterations, whereas the phosphorylation levels of both Smad2 and Smad3 were considerably diminished. Moreover, the ectopic expression of P4HA1 through lentiviral particles in the knockdown cells notably restored the phosphorylation levels of p-Smad2 and p-Smad3.

Previous studies have shown a significant relationship between the activation of the TGF-β signaling pathway and the malignant transformation, as well as the invasive behaviors, of various tumors [67,68]. We hypothesized that these phenomena could be linked to the EMT of cancerous cells. As a result, we examined the expression levels of key proteins associated with EMT. Our results demonstrated that the knockdown of P4HA1 in HN4 and SCC25 cell lines markedly decreased the levels of N-cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail, while concurrently increasing the expression of E-cadherin and γ-catenin (Figure 7J). These findings indicate that the overexpression of P4HA1 may influence EMT properties in HNSCC cells, potentially affecting the prognosis of patients with HNSCC through the activation of the TGF-β signaling pathway.

Discussion

Over the last decade, there has been no significant improvement in the survival duration of HNSCC, and the persistent high mortality rate represents a significant challenge [69]. Therefore, developing a reliable classifier to classify patients based on varying risks and prognoses is crucial for enhancing the advantages provided by personalized treatments and timely follow-up care. The discovery of migrasomes as a novel cellular organelle in migrating cells occurred in 2015 [11,12]. These structures are believed to facilitate intercellular communication and may have both physiological and pathological roles. The identification of migrasomes has opened up new avenues in tumor treatment research [28,70]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is undeniably a critical area of focus in tumor studies [25,71], and a certain correlation exists between migrasomes and the TME [13,28]. Nonetheless, earlier investigations failed to examine these two aspects in tandem. To remedy this gap, the current study uniquely links migrasomes with the tumor microenvironment. Following this, a prognostic risk signature based on mtmRGs was created, demonstrating promising results in risk classification for both training and testing cohorts via thorough analysis of transcriptional profiles and clinical data. This signature provides moderate accurate predictions for the clinical outcomes of HNSCC patients (with AUC values in the range of 0.64–0.70) and is associated with the presence of tumor-infiltrating immune cells, as well as the effectiveness of immunotherapy and common chemotherapy agents. These results offer fresh insights for enhancing discussions regarding patient prognostication and stratification, while also promising beneficial clinical applications in immunotherapy and chemotherapy.

In this research, we established a strong risk scoring system made up of six specific genes: FN1, PLAU, P4HA1, EBF1, PRR11, and RGS4. Prior research has indicated certain connections between these genes and the tumorigenesis as well as the pathogenesis of cancer. For instance, FN1 (fibronectin 1), a structural glycoprotein of considerable size, has been observed to be overexpressed in a variety of solid tumors [72,73]. This gene mediates numerous interactions between cells and the extracellular matrix, playing a critical role in processes such as cell adhesion, migration, growth, and differentiation [74,75]. Recent findings have indicated that the expression of FN1 is linked to lymph node metastasis in cancers affecting humans, including thyroid cancer [76], clear renal cell carcinoma [77], and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [78]. Enhanced activation of FN1 signaling has been related to the advancement of EMT [79,80]. PLAU (urokinase plasminogen activator), commonly referred to as uPA, exhibits various biological effects across numerous physiological and pathological processes, including keratinocyte proliferation [81], rheumatoid arthritis [82], and airway inflammation [83]. PLAU is notably overexpressed in different tumors such as breast cancer [84], gastric adenocarcinoma [85], and HNSCC [86]. Furthermore, PLAU facilitates tumor progression by enhancing tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT [87–89]. P4HA1 (prolyl 4-hydroxylase, alpha polypeptide), which is a crucial enzyme for collagen synthesis, consists of two identical alpha subunits paired with two beta subunits. Studies have reported P4HA1 as a negative prognostic indicator in glioma while also promoting the migration and invasion of glioma cells by expediting the EMT process [90]. The overexpression of P4HA1 induced by hypoxic conditions has been linked to the advancement of colorectal cancer [91]. Moreover, Li et al. reported a significant increase in both mRNA and protein levels of P4HA1 in tissues affected by HNSCC when compared to normal tissues, with elevated expression correlating with decreased overall OS in HNSCC patients [92]. EBF1 (early B cell factor 1) acts as a transcription factor that plays diverse roles in differentiation and metabolic processes [93]. It is abundantly expressed in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and significantly contributes to the tumorigenicity and progression of TNBC. This is achieved through the formation of a transcriptional complex with HIF1α targets, primarily by inhibiting p300 activity [94]. Recent research has indicated that EBF1 aids in cancer progression by negatively influencing the p53 signaling pathway or regulating the expression of telomerase reverse transcriptase [95,96], but the precise impact of EBF1 on the progression of HNSCC remains unclear. PRR11 (proline-rich 11), an oncogene that has gained attention in recent years, is located on chromosome 17q22 and encodes a protein consisting of 360 amino acids [97,98]. Earlier studies have demonstrated that PRR11 is frequently overexpressed in various cancers, and this increased expression has been associated with enhanced cancer progression [99,100]. For example, in gliomas, PRR11 is found to be highly expressed and shows a positive correlation with matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9), which together contribute to the clinical advancement of gliomas. Glioma patients exhibiting co-expression of both PRR11 and MMP9 experience a reduced 3-year OS rate [101]. In the context of bladder cancer, PRR11 has been identified as a prognostic biomarker associated with immune invasion [102]. Additionally, in colorectal cancer, PRR11 promotes both cell proliferation and migration by stimulating the EGFR/ERK/AKT signaling pathway [103]. Furthermore, another risk gene, RGS4 (regulator of G protein signaling 4) is a key component of the R4 RGS protein family that has been extensively researched in both the central nervous system and the circulatory system [104]. It serves as a multifunctional scaffold protein that facilitates the assembly of proteins engaged in regulatory signal transduction [105]. The role of RGS4 in cancer has received increasing attention in scholarly work. Previous investigations revealed that RGS4 significantly impeded the migration and invasion of breast cancer cells through its GAP-mediated activity [106]. Similar inhibitory effects were noted in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells [107]. Thus far, the findings mentioned above align partially with the results of the current study, where FN1, PLAU, P4HA1, PRR11, and RGS4 have been identified as upregulated in HNSCC tissues. Notably, the overexpression of FN1, PLAU, P4HA1, and RGS4 shows a strong association with poor clinical outcomes in patients with HNSCC. Furthermore, this innovative prognostic signature comprising six mtmRGs may offer valuable insights into novel molecular subtypes and enhance the prognostic evaluation of HNSCC, thus paving the way for tailored treatment and management strategies for future patients.

The signaling pathway of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) represents a highly conserved and intricate system of cellular communication. This pathway modulates numerous target genes in a manner that can either support or inhibit their function, largely dependent on the cellular context [108]. It is vital in regulating tissue development, differentiation, apoptosis, proliferation, and maintaining homeostasis [109]. When this signaling mechanism is disrupted, it can lead to various diseases, including different types of cancers [110]. In both normal and precancerous cells, TGF-β typically operates as a tumor suppressor by hindering cell growth and ensuring genomic stability [111]. During the initial stages of cancer, TGF-β signaling work to inhibit the proliferation of tumor cells through the induction of apoptosis. However, at more advanced stages of cancer, the expression of TGF-β is increased, transforming it into a key driver of tumor growth and the spread of cancer [112]. Increasing evidence indicates that TGF-β is secreted by diverse cell types within the tumor microenvironment, contributing to processes such as extracellular matrix deposition, angiogenesis in tumors, the formation of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and the suppression of immune responses that counteract tumors [112–114]. Consequently, TGF-β is also viewed as a critical player in enhancing the invasion and metastasis of cancer cells, including the process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition [115,116]. Given its dual role as both a tumor suppressor and an active promoter of tumor progression and metastasis, understanding the function of TGF-β signaling in tumorigenesis and the malignant transformation of tumors could yield important new insights for diagnosing and predicting the outcomes of HNSCC, along with developing innovative molecular targeted therapies. Currently, the effectiveness of existing targeted treatment options is limited, and the complexities arising from the heterogeneity of various cancer types underscore the necessity for a more profound exploration of the regulatory mechanisms of the TGF-β signaling pathway [111]. Understanding this is essential for formulating effective personalized therapeutic strategies. In this study, we found that elevated levels of P4HA1 were notably linked to the activation of the TGF-β signaling pathway through GSEA analysis. Silencing P4HA1 led to a reduction in the phosphorylation of smad2 and smad3, while simultaneously enhancing the expression of E-cadherin and γ-catenin, and decreasing the levels of N-cadherin, Vimentin, and snail in the HN4 and SCC25 cell lines. Our findings suggest that the activation of the TGF-β signaling pathway may play a crucial role in the progression of HNSCC marked by P4HA1 overexpression.

The originality of this research lies in our thorough investigation of how mtmRGs influence the prognosis of HNSCC, uncovering important avenues to enhance current knowledge by incorporating mtmRGs into cancer risk assessments. These mechanisms are essential to understanding tumor metastasis and immune evasion, which in turn facilitates a deeper comprehension of tumor development. Additionally, this integrated method identifies new therapeutic targets and combination therapies that have the potential to enhance treatment effectiveness. Furthermore, our model evaluates patient responsiveness to anticancer medications, delivering dual benefits. This aids in decision-making not only for immunotherapy but also for conventional chemotherapy, offering a notable advantage compared to existing models that generally concentrate solely on prognosis or the response to immunotherapy. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that this study comes with certain limitations. Primarily, our analysis depends heavily on publicly accessible datasets (TCGA and GEO), which may not adequately capture the full spectrum of HNSCC patient diversity worldwide. Expanding our study to include a larger and more varied cohort, integrating multi-center data and representing different ethnic backgrounds, would significantly improve the applicability of our results. Additionally, while we implemented the ComBat algorithm to adjust for batch effects among the datasets, these corrections might not cover all sources of technical variation. This possibility could introduce subtle biases in the gene expression data. Future investigations should focus on enhancing harmonization techniques and including more datasets to substantiate our model’s reliability. Furthermore, it is essential to note that the proposed mtmRGs-related risk model is founded on bioinformatics analysis. Although we have performed preliminary validations of P4HA1 through in vivo and in vitro approaches, the crucial roles of the other five prognostic risk genes- FN1, PLAU, EBF1, PRR11, and RGS4 -cannot be dismissed in HNSCC. Their specific mechanisms and clinical relevance warrant further exploration, indicating that there is still considerable work required to transition from our findings to practical applications in clinical environments. Finally, it is worth noting that in recent years, the rise of artificial intelligence (AI) within the medical sector, particularly in oncology, has created remarkable opportunities for precision medicine. Specifically, in the realm of HNSCC, the integration of AI has demonstrated exceptional potential due to the extensive imaging data available from various modalities (CT, MRI, PET, histopathology) alongside the intricate decision-making processes involved in planning treatments. AI’s capability to analyze large volumes of multidimensional information and detect subtle patterns that may elude human perception renders it an excellent solution for tackling the challenges associated with HNSCC management. Nevertheless, a significant obstacle for AI applications in HNSCC lies in the absence of standardized protocols for data acquisition and processing. Differences in imaging parameters, reconstruction techniques, and methods of annotation can markedly affect the performance and generalizability of models. Additionally, the opaque nature of many AI algorithms presents hurdles for both clinical acceptance and approval by regulatory bodies. For clinicians to trust and effectively implement these tools in patient treatment, an understanding of how AI models arrive at their decisions is essential. Although there are ongoing efforts to create explainable AI solutions for medical uses, these initiatives are still limited in scope. Therefore, we propose integrating our mtmRGs risk model with existing AI-driven models developed for HNSCC, which may provide substantial benefits for both clinicians and patients.

Conclusions

This research represents the initial thorough and systematic examination of migrosome and tumor microenvironment-associated genes in HNSCC, alongside the development of a model that exhibits good predictive capabilities for clinical outcomes, immune responses, and targeted therapies for patients with HNSCC. Moreover, additional in vitro and in vivo studies indicated that P4HA1 played a significant role in modulating the proliferation and metastasis of HNSCC cells, linked to the activation of the TGF-β signaling pathway. In summary, our investigation offers new perspectives on the mechanisms underlying HNSCC and potential strategies for treatment or management of this group of patients. It is important to emphasize that the proposed mtmRGs-related risk model is based on bioinformatics analysis, highlighting that substantial effort is still needed to translate our findings into practical applications in clinical settings.
