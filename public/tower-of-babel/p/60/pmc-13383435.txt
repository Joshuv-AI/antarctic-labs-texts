Current practices and innovative diagnostics for Malaria in Ethiopia: a scoping review
Authors: Getasew Mulat Bantie, Destaw Fetene Teshome, Gizew Dessie Asres, Nurlign Abebe Moges, Melaku Tadege, Abeba Kassahun Awoke, Abraham Amsalu Berneh, Addisu Gasheneit Ferede, Damtie Lankir Abebe, Etsegenet Kindie Tiruneh, Amare Alemu Melese, Negese Sewagegn, Gizachew Yismaw Wubetu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor several decades, malaria has been a significant health concern in the African region. It continues to be a major contributor to illness, hospitalizations, and death, particularly among young children and pregnant women. Despite the national and regional efforts towards achieving the 2030 malaria elimination strategy, Ethiopia still faced an increment in malaria cases. While studies exist, a thorough synthesis or mapping of practices and innovative diagnostics for Malaria was previously lacking.ObjectiveTo explore current practices and innovative diagnostics for Malaria in Ethiopia, 2024.MethodThis scoping review was conducted following Arksey and O’Malley’s methodological steps to develop research questions, identify relevant studies, include/exclude studies, extract data, and report the findings. To ensure methodological quality, we used the PRISMA guideline and PRISMA-ScR checklist.ResultsThirteen studies were eligible, and 38.5% of them were published between 2008 and 2024. The majority (84.6%) of the studies were conducted using cross-sectional study designs, while one (7.7%) of them was conducted by RCT design. Most were conducted in the Amhara region (43.75%), followed by the Oromia region (31.25%). Light microscopy, rapid diagnostic test (RDT), polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) were the identified malaria diagnostics so far in Ethiopia, with each having unique advantages and limitations.ConclusionThe current study underscored the need for a tailored, highly accurate, user-friendly, and applicable diagnostics that addresses the unique limitations of each malaria diagnostic modality to support the malaria elimination and control strategy in Ethiopia.Keywords: Malaria, Diagnostic methods, Scoping review, Ethiopia

Introduction

Malaria diagnostics are included in the global and national strategies to decline malaria burden at least by 90% at the end of 2030 [1, 2]. This strategy recommends innovative, timely and accurate diagnosis of the malaria species to initiate appropriate treatment and reduce the costly impact on patients’ lives and healthcare services. Innovative malaria diagnostics are improved or new methods to detect malaria faster and more accurately than traditional tests/microscopes and rapid diagnostic tests) [3]. However, malaria is an overwhelming public health problem in Sub-Saharan Africa, with the highest burden in under-five children and in pregnant women [4–11]. Ethiopia has an increased prevalence of confirmed malaria cases: 1.1 million in 2021, 1.5 million in 2022, and 1.8 million in 2023, respectively [12]. This could be linked with the diagnostic failure of the available malaria diagnostic tests and the effects of mutation of the malaria parasite [13–22]. Thus, a simple and affordable innovative diagnostic algorithm is mandatory for resource-unprivileged, malarious, low-income countries. However, the existing diagnostics were not assessed in Ethiopia. Therefore, this scoping review aimed to mapped the breadth of available evidence regarding malaria diagnostics in Ethiopia considering heterogenous evidence in the review. In doing so, we can assess the unique advantages and limitations of the current practices and existing malaria diagnostics, which will underpin and facilitate both the development of target product profiles for new diagnostics and the future development of novel diagnostics themselves.

Methods and materials

A scoping review is a type of evidence synthesis used to map the breadth and nature of existing literature on a topic, clarify key concepts, and identify research gaps [23]. This scoping review of literature was conducted to synthesize and analyze existing literature on the advantages and limitations of current practices for malaria diagnosis in the Ethiopian context and also help to map out the breadth and depth of the available evidence. The context of Ethiopian health care settings. All studies carried out in Ethiopia involving malaria diagnosis were considered for inclusion, while all studies other than those in the English language and done out of Ethiopia were excluded. A population, concept, and context (PCC) framework was used to determine the eligibility of our primary research question (Table 1).

The review was carried out following a protocol based on the scoping review methodological framework outlined by Arksey and O’Malley [24], which was later modified by Levac et al. (2010) [25]. The process involves six steps: identifying the research question, identifying relevant studies, selecting studies, charting data, collating, summarizing, and reporting results, and consultation (optional) [25, 26]. The PRISMA-ScR checklist was used to support comprehensive reporting of methods and findings. The checklist was initially published online in 2018, and it contains 20 essential reporting items and 2 optional items to include when completing a scoping review [27], and it was guided by Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) scoping review guidance [28]. The final protocol was registered prospectively within the open science framework on the first of November 2024 at https://osf.io/pqudh/.

Identifying the research question

The purpose of this scoping review was to gather evidence on the diagnostic tests in use for malaria diagnosis in Ethiopia. Moreover, this review was to identify the enablers and barriers for quality malaria diagnosis at primary health care settings to achieve universal health coverage. The review question was proposed as “What malaria diagnostic tests are in use, their barriers and enablers for malaria diagnosis in Ethiopia?” The concept: malaria diagnosis to deliver quality malaria-related primary health care, were considered.

Identifying relevant studies

The four electronic databases, PubMed (n = 76), Google Scholar (n = 200), Scopus (n = 145), and ScienceDirect (n = 1100), were navigated for studies that described malaria diagnosis. These were followed by complementary searches. Initial searches of these databases identified articles relevant to the review questions and facilitated refining the search strings for each database to ensure the capture of relevant literature.

The search terms used for PubMed were ‘’Malaria’’ OR “Malaria“[Mesh] AND ‘’Diagnosis’’ OR “Diagnosis“[Mesh] OR Rapid Diagnostic Tests OR Microscopy OR RDTs OR molecular diagnostics OR PCR OR polymerase chain reaction AND “Inventions“[Mesh] OR innovative technologies OR new tools OR point-of-care OR artificial intelligence OR digital health OR nanotechnology OR biosensors AND Ethiopia OR Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia OR “Ethiopia“[Mesh].

While for Scopus, the following terms were used: “Malaria AND diagnosis OR rapid AND diagnostic AND tests OR microscopy OR rdts OR molecular AND diagnostics OR Polymerase AND chain AND reaction AND innovative AND technologies OR new AND tools OR point-of-care OR artificial AND intelligence OR digital AND health OR nanotechnology OR biosensors AND Ethiopia.”. Trials were also done to retrieve from available university repositories.

Moreover, to locate the eligible articles from Google Scholar, citation searches for the first ten pages were used. The keywords used in the search strategy were built on key concept (malaria diagnosis), and search terms were customized to each database. Boolean operators were used depending on each database. The search included articles published in English from the inception of each database up to September 25, 2024. The search strategy was not limited by study design, or year. All the studies conducted in the Ethiopian context were included.

Selection of studies

Based on the title and abstract, screening was done initially by the first author (Ph.D. fellow) and further assessed by the second author (Ph.D. holder researcher). There were no disagreements between these authors. Authors included all relevant studies that dealt with the issue of malaria diagnosis. The literature search resulted in 1,521 citations. After screening 1,521 potentially relevant articles, 267 were excluded due to context issues (not conducted in Ethiopia), and five were excluded due to duplication. Moreover, 1,231 articles were excluded during title and abstract screening. Subsequently, trials were done to search for the full access of the papers, and 17 were accessed and included in the study. However, finally, four studies were excluded because the reports consisted of two or more countries’ reports (Fig. 1).

Data charting /extraction

A data-charting form was developed to extract data from each study, comprising the author list, year of publication, title, objective, study design, study setting, diagnostic modality, test principle, advantages/enablers, and limitations/barriers. A descriptive-analytical method was used to extract context-oriented information from each study. The first author extracted the data, and the second author verified it. The third reviewer acted as a mediator to resolve disagreements. The full text of 17 articles was reviewed using a data extraction sheet. Four out of the 17 full-text articles were excluded from the study, as two had both Ethiopia’s and another country’s reports simultaneously. Moreover, the other two articles were excluded because they used the other country’s dataset for analysis, though the authors were Ethiopians. Finally, 13 research reports were included in the review, and the data elements were extracted using an Excel spreadsheet.

Collating, summarizing, reporting of results and synthesis

For included articles, we abstracted detailed information on study characteristics (e.g., authors, year of study conduct, study setting, study design, objectives, and key findings). Subsequently, data was abstracted by two reviewers (myself and one additional reviewer) to ensure accurateness and uniformity.

Results

Characteristics of the study/publication

In this study 1,521 articles were reviewed for relevance. Considering different screening and eligibility criteria, only 13 articles were included and used for evidence synthesis and mapping. Of these 13 articles, 11 (84.6%) were conducted using cross-sectional study designs, while one was an RCT and one was a systematic review and meta-analysis, respectively. These studies were conducted between 2008 and 2024. Most were conducted in the Amhara region (43.75%), followed by the Oromia region (31.25%). The larger share of studies conducted in the Amhara region may be attributed to a higher disease burden or from the study eligibility criteria (Table 2).

Malaria diagnostic methods/modalities in Ethiopia

In this review four malaria diagnostics were identified. These were light microscopy, rapid diagnostic test (RDT), polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP). The review showed that there were two types of microscopes: light microscopes and portable digital microscopes. Moreover, RDT had different types, namely, RDT, uRDT, the Partec rapid malaria test (PT), Para Screen RDT, the SD BIOLINE Malaria Ag Pf/Pan test, and the CareStart™ malaria Pf/PV combo test/RDT. Similarly, PCR was also presented in various forms: PCR, mobile qPCR, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). Likewise, there were two types of LAMPs reported, LAMP and LAMPREG (Table 2).

The Noul miLab digital microscopes are fully automated portable digital microscopes that prepare a blood film from a droplet of blood, followed by staining and detection of parasites by an algorithm [33]. This microscope is more specific and sensitive at very low parasitemia (< 200 parasites/µL). Moreover, this microscope determines parasite density, and it is a very crucial malaria diagnosis modality in areas where high frequencies of hrp2/3 deletions [34] (Tables 3 and 4).

Limitations

Light microscopy: The major limitations of diagnosing malaria via light microscopy are the turnaround time of Giemsa stain, which took more than an hour, and the lack of infrastructure, lack of skilled laboratory experts for slide preparation, technologist training, and quality assurance [29–31]. Moreover, the diagnosis efficiency of light microscopy is very low for species-specific and mixed infection detection or subclinical infections reliably. It has significant limitations in detecting low parasitemia levels and asymptomatic cases due to the process of Plasmodium falciparum sequestration. These shortcomings often lead to underestimation of the true malaria burden [32, 35, 36] (Tables 3 and 4).

Rapid diagnostic test

Advantages

RDT: It is a rapid diagnostic test that detects malarial parasite antigens, such as histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2), which detects only P. falciparum, and Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH) or aldolase, which detects all Plasmodium species and it has a good specificity and is crucial in resource-limited settings; it is simple to use and needs little expertise, it doesn’t require electricity, and results can be obtained in few minutes [31]. Moreover, RDTs are highly portable, cost effective, and require minimal infrastructure, making them essential for malaria diagnosis in remote, resource-limited areas [37, 38]. (Tables 3 and 4).

uRDT (Ultrasensitive rapid diagnostic test): Is an important type of rapid diagnostic test in detecting asymptomatic individuals [32]. (Tables 3 and 4).

Partec rapid malaria test (PT): it detects parasite antigens, which showed good sensitivity and specificity, has very short turnaround times, requires little training, and is applicable under field conditions [29]. The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of PT were 93.8%, 87.9%, 86.4%, and 94.6%, respectively, compared to light microscopy [29] (Tables 3 and 4).

Para Screen RDT: It is a test that detects malaria antigen in blood flowing along a membrane containing specific anti-malaria antibody. It has good sensitivity and specificity and can be used in population-based studies in the countryside [30]. Para Screen RDT exhibited.

more sensitivity to PF or mixed infections [30] (Tables 3 and 4).

The SD BIOLINE Malaria Ag Pf/Pan test: This test showed an excellent performance in diagnosing Plasmodium infections in an endemic setting. This point-of-care test could be used as an alternative to microscopy in places where P. falciparum is endemic and microscopy is unsuitable [39] (Tables 3 and 4).

Care-Start™ malaria Pf/Pv combo test/RDT: Care-Start™ RDT could be used for epidemiological studies [40] (Tables 3 and 4).

Limitations

RDT and uRDT: Showed a low sensitivity rate. The failure in detection was due to undetectable level of HRP-2 or HRP-3 [31, 32]. High prevalence of parasites with pfhrp2 deletion including dual deletions (pfhrp2 and pfhrp3) and antigens persistence after treatment is a serious cause of concern as these patients could not receive appropriate diagnosis and treatment [41–43] (Tables 3 and 4).

Partec rapid malaria test (PT): Determination of parasitemia with the Partec rapid malaria test is difficult, because the red blood cells may overlap each other and make the parasites invisible under the UV light [29] (Tables 3 and 4).

Para Screen RDT: Lack of sensitivity of RDTs at low parasitemia. Moreover, it exhibited.

less sensitivity to Plasmodium vivax infection [30] (Tables 3 and 4).

The SD BIOLINE Malaria Ag Pf/Pan test: It is less sensitive to diagnose non-Plasmodium species than Plasmodium species [39] (Tables 3 and 4).

CareStart™ malaria Pf/Pv combo test: The findings of CareStart™ RDT should be interpreted cautiously by considering its seasonal variation [40] (Tables 3 and 4).

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

Advantages

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR): It is the molecular detection of DNA/RNA, and it is more sensitive for malaria detection [31] PCR-based detection of malaria in Ethiopia primarily targets the 18 S ribosomal RNA (18 S rRNA) gene for both genus-wide and species-specific identification of Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax. Other gene targets, such as the cytochrome b (cytb) gene and the cytochrome oxidase I (COX1) mitochondrial gene, are also used to enhance sensitivity, particularly for low-density infections [44, 45] (Tables 3 and 4).

Mobile qPCR: It is a molecular diagnosis that amplifies the plasmodia genes and can be used for surveillance purposes with low cost and reduced weight for transport and also can identify hidden parasite reservoirs. It can also be used for screening asymptomatic malaria infections [46] (Tables 3 and 4).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR): It is a molecular diagnosis and is highly sensitive and specific regardless of parasitemia doses and/or mixed infection [32, 47, 48] (Tables 3 and 4).

SYBR Green I-based real-time PCR: Its efficiency is very high for species-specific and mixed infection detection [32, 47, 48] (Tables 3 and 4).

Limitations

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR): It requires well-trained staff, sophisticated laboratory equipment, and a good quality assurance system [31]. It is expensive for clinical use in remote areas [47]. It is also heavy to transport to peripheral areas and needs infrastructure [48] (Tables 3 and 4).

Mobile qPCR: the identified limitations are lack of running water and frequent interruption of light in the surveillance field. Moreover, the risk of cross-contamination during the manual DNA extraction process and absence of laboratory capacity in endemic countries [46] (Tables 3 and 4).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR): It has lower quality than LAMP [32] (Tables 3 and 4).

Loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP)

Advantages

Loop-mediated isothermal DNA amplification (LAMP): It is a molecular diagnosis, and detection of Plasmodium in saliva and urine samples provides a new approach for malaria diagnosis without piercing/hurting the blood vessels/fingerpicks. Moreover, LAMP is sensitive at parasite densities below 100 parasites/µl. It reduces the diagnostic and treatment costs as compared to microscope and RDT. Moreover, detecting infected blood donors before transfusion is another potential use of LAMP. LAMP may provide reliable results in basic rural laboratories without the need for specialized infrastructure and can represent a powerful tool in malaria control programs [49]. This is an innovative molecular technique that is simpler and faster with excellent diagnostic accuracy [31, 50] (Tables 3 and 4).

LAMPREG: It is a molecular diagnosis and it measures the impact of active case detection of malaria in pregnant women. In addition, it measures birth outcomes in women offered active detection of Plasmodium infection [33] (Tables 3 and 4).

Limitations

Loop-mediated isothermal DNA amplification (LAMP): It takes about an hour for investigation, which is more than thin and thick blood films (< 40’), and the cost of diagnosing a sample is high compared to microscope and RDT [49]. It has lower specificity than microscopy and RDT [50]. Moreover, contaminating DNA can cause false positives, making a negative sample appear positive. It may also reduce sensitivity by competing with target DNA, leading to missed low-level targets. Contamination can create misleading bands or fluorescence, complicating data interpretation [51–54]. (Tables 3 and 4).

LAMPREG: Loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) technology does not differentiate the sexual and asexual stages of the parasite [33] (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

In Ethiopia, malaria ranks among the top five communicable diseases, significantly affecting the health and well-being of the community. It is crucial to understand the current practices in malaria diagnosis, along with their advantages and limitations, at the national level. This review synthesizes evidence on these diagnostic practices, providing valuable insights for policymakers and relevant stakeholders. Such information can aid in the development of appropriate diagnostics aimed at reducing the morbidity and mortality associated with malaria infections.

The current study explored the different malaria diagnostics in Ethiopia. These were light microscopy, rapid diagnostic test (RDT), polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP). Each diagnostic modality had different names and capacities for precisely diagnosing malaria.

The light microscope is considered the gold standard for malaria diagnosis, offering higher specificity (99.1%) than portable digital microscopy (86%) [55, 56] and superior diagnostic accuracy compared to several rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs): Partec Rapid Malaria Test (sensitivity 93.8%, specificity 87.9%), ParaScreen RDT (sensitivity 47.5%, specificity 98.5%), SD BIOLINE Malaria Ag Pf/Pan Test (sensitivity 99.5%, specificity 98.0%), and CareStart™ Malaria Pf/PV Combo Test (sensitivity 95.7%, specificity 82.7%) [29, 57–59]. Light microscopy can also be applied in population-based surveys [29–32].

However, it has notable limitations, including reduced efficiency in detecting mixed infections, low parasitemia, and asymptomatic cases [32]; time-consuming Giemsa staining; reliance on skilled personnel for slide preparation; and challenges with quality assurance [29–31]. Despite being a gold standard, these shortcomings can lead to underestimation of the true malaria burden [32, 35, 36], highlighting the need to complement microscopy with more sensitive tools, such as molecular assays, to strengthen malaria control and elimination programs.

Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) are user-friendly, do not require electricity, and provide results within minutes, making them ideal for resource-limited settings. However, they have limitations, including low sensitivity at low parasitemia, inability to accurately quantify parasitemia, reduced sensitivity for Plasmodium vivax infections, and variability in performance with seasonal fluctuations [29–32, 39, 40]. While RDTs are convenient and suitable for resource-limited settings, their lower sensitivity at low parasitemia, reduced detection of Plasmodium vivax, and seasonal variability imply that relying solely on RDTs may underestimate malaria prevalence. This underscores the need to supplement RDTs with more sensitive diagnostic tools, such as microscopy or molecular assays, for accurate surveillance and effective malaria control programs.

PCR and LAMP are highly sensitive molecular methods for malaria detection, capable of identifying low-density and mixed infections, with PCR amplifying Plasmodium genes and LAMP applicable to non-invasive samples such as saliva and urine. However, conventional PCR requires skilled personnel and sophisticated infrastructure, limiting its use in remote settings, while LAMP, although more field-adapted, may involve longer processing time, slightly lower specificity in some contexts, and higher costs [31, 33, 49, 50]. Mobile qPCR platforms also face operational challenges, including lack of running water, unstable power supply, contamination risks, and limited laboratory capacity in endemic areas [31, 32, 46–48].

Recent innovations in portable, battery-or solar-powered PCR systems, such as the mini-dbPCR-NALFIA with mini16 thermal cycler, demonstrate diagnostic performance comparable to conventional laboratory PCR [60, 61]. These advancements support the decentralised use of molecular diagnostics, which could substantially improve detection of low-density, asymptomatic, and mixed infections. Policymakers should therefore consider integrating portable PCR into malaria diagnosis and elimination strategies, particularly in resource-limited and hard-to-reach settings, in a stepwise manner.

Limitations of the study

This study had a restriction to English-language-based publications, with a heavy reliance on Google Scholar and ScienceDirect, which introduces a potential selection and language bias.

Conclusion and recommendation

The current study explored the different malaria diagnostics in Ethiopia. These were light microscopy, rapid diagnostic test (RDT), polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP). Each diagnostic modality had different names and capacities for precisely diagnosing malaria. Each identified diagnostic modality has its limitations. However, PCR has better diagnostic accuracy, and the Ethiopian government may consider investing in portable, chargeable, battery- or solar-powered PCR systems by expanding them in a stepwise manner and using them as a gold standard routine diagnostic tool in all primary healthcare units.
