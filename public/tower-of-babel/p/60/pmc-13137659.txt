Estimating intervention impacts when timing is unclear: an AR-LagDT model with distributed lags
Authors: Xiangliang Zhang, Ninghui Li, Kunpeng Wu, Peng Wang, Tao Li, Pengchao An, Zuhuan Ren, Wen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interrupted Time Series (ITS) analysis is widely used to evaluate intervention impacts, but conventional methods like the classical segmented regression (CSR) and "prediction" models (ARIMA, ETS, etc.) have limitations. CSR assumes an immediate effect, while "prediction" models rely on counterfactual estimates that become unreliable over time. To address this, we propose the Autoregressive with Lag Distributed Term (AR-LagDT) model, which explicitly accounts for intervention effect ambiguity using distributed lag functions. By incorporating varying fuzzy statuses (the onset and duration ambiguity of the intervention effect) and multiple distribution patterns, the AR-LagDT model provides more precise estimates of intervention dynamics. We validate the model through an empirical case study on Japan’s COVID-19 State of Emergency and its impact on the Human Mobility Index in Tokyo. To enhance usability, we develop a user-friendly software application for AR-LagDT model. Our findings demonstrate the model’s ability to capture the temporal progression of intervention effects, providing estimates of maximum impact, cumulative effect, and time-dependent changes. In the empirical analysis, AR-LagDT (e.g., MSE: 7.0139) demonstrate superior in-sample fit to CSR (e.g., MSE: 14.2741) while achieving a broadly similar performance level to the average of "prediction" models (e.g., MSE: 8.1845, ranging from 5.6944 to 14.1337). Moreover, the AR-LagDT model offers clearer interpretability of time-varying impacts. Despite challenges in selecting appropriate fuzzy statuses and distribution patterns, this model offers a robust and flexible framework for impact evaluations in various disciplines and enhances accessibility for researchers conducting intervention analyses in epidemiology, public health, and policy evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02834-z.

Introduction

Assessing the impact of interventions on measurable outcomes is of paramount importance to human concern in epidemiology, health policy, and social sciences [1–3]. The Interrupted Time Series (ITS) design is commonly used to evaluate health interventions, particularly when randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are impractical [4, 5]. It is often the only viable method for assessing large-scale public health policies [6]. The classical segmented regression (CSR) model, a widely used ITS approach, assumes a predetermined intervention time point [7], distinctly separating pre- and post-intervention phases. However, this model presumes immediate and direct intervention effects, an assumption that may not align with real-world scenarios [2].

In real-world scenarios, the exact timing of an intervention’s impact is often uncertain, with impacts rarely manifesting immediately at the nominal intervention point. Anticipatory responses may lead to early effects [4], as seen in the American Association of Colleges of Nursing’s Clinical Nurse Leader Intervention Program, where trainees implemented learned practices before completing training [7]. Additionally, many interventions include pilot phases before full implementation [2]. For example, under England’s 2012 Health and Social Care Act, Clinical Commissioning Groups assumed partial budgetary responsibility in April 2012, with full transfer completed by March 2013 [8, 9]. In brief, some intervention effects typically emerge over a distribution period rather than at a single time point [10–12].

The CSR model does not account for the inherent uncertainty in intervention effects. It either overlooks this ambiguity or excludes data points corresponding to the period of effect uncertainty before modeling [13, 14], both of which can bias parameter estimation. Furthermore, CSR model is limited to assessing immediate and long-term effects, preventing a comprehensive analysis of the dynamic process of intervention impact. "Prediction" type model, such as the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model, generate a "counterfactual" prediction for the post-intervention period, with the intervention impact estimated by subtracting the predicted time series from observed outcome time series [15]. While this approach enables characterization of the evolving intervention impact, it is highly dependent on the accuracy of "counterfactual" predictions, which becomes much more difficult to guarantee as the number of post-intervention time points increases. Stabilizing the counterfactual trajectory is essential for credible impact estimation. Common strategies include rolling-origin evaluation, which assesses baseline robustness by shifting the prediction origin across pre-intervention periods. Comparator approaches also serve as counterfactual stabilizers. Methods such as Synthetic Control Method (SCM), augmented SCM, and Bayesian Structural Time Series (BSTS) generate stabilized counterfactuals through weighted control groups or structural state-space models. While widely used to mitigate counterfactual drift, these approaches depend on the availability of valid control groups or unaffected covariates, and their performance degrades when suitable controls are absent or intervention exposure is widespread. Moreover, "prediction" type models do not inherently account for intervention effect ambiguity.

Daisuke addressed uncertainty of intervention effects by incorporating distributed lag functional terms into the "prediction" type model, proposing the ARIMAITS-DL model to account for intervention effect ambiguity [16]. However, the ARIMAITS-DL model remains fundamentally a predictive approach. In Daisuke’s study, the number of predicted post-intervention time points (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=$$\end{document} 42) was nearly equivalent to the pre-intervention period (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=$$\end{document} 48), raising concerns regarding the reliability of "counterfactual" predictions. Moreover, the default effect distribution patterns in the ARIMAITS-DL model (uniform and normal distributions) are insufficient to capture the heterogeneity of intervention effects observed in real-world scenarios, such as skewed distributions.

To overcome uncertainty of intervention effects while step outside the "counterfactual" prediction mindset of "prediction" type model, we propose the Autoregressive with Lag Distributed Term (AR-LagDT) model, which directly identifies and separates intervention impacts from the observed outcome time series while accounting for autocorrelation. Inheriting Daisuke’s assumption of a distributed intervention effect, we further refine the model by explicitly considering the role of different fuzzy statuses (the onset and duration ambiguity of the intervention effect, manifested as position variations of the distribution period relative to the nominal intervention time point) and alternative distribution patterns (uniform, normal, log-normal [right-skewed], and log-normal flip [left-skewed] distributions). AR-LagDT differs from prediction-based Interrupted Time Series [17] design because it does not construct counterfactuals through post-intervention forecasting. Instead, it follows the identification logic of classical distributed-lag and intervention transfer-function models [18–20]. The observed post-intervention time series is decomposed into AR dynamics and a convolved, time-bounded kernel capturing the intervention effect. Consistent with the idea of distribution-lag [21] and transfer function [22], AR-LagDT models the intervention response as a weighted sum across multiple time periods. The time-bounded kernel during uncertain period of intervention effects provides the structural restriction needed for identification and ensures realistic gradual onset and decay patterns. AR and kernel parameters are estimated jointly, with ridge regularization mitigating collinearity among lag kernel terms [23]. The AR-LagDT model enables estimation of intervention effects at each post-intervention time point, facilitating analyses of the maximum impact, cumulative effect, and temporal progression of intervention impacts, and provides a unified framework for estimating intervention impacts when the intervention timing is unclear. Through the following sections, we demonstrate the AR-LagDT model's effectiveness in accurately capturing intervention dynamics while addressing limitations of existing models, as well as some inherent potential challenges in its application.

Methods

In this Section, we elaborate on the conceptual framework of the AR-LagDT model and the methodology for parameter estimation. To facilitate adoption, we have developed a stand-alone, user-friendly application that is publicly accessible to anyone. Furthermore, we evaluate the model’s fitting performance against CSR model and the "prediction" type model (ARIMA, ETS, Holt-Winters, NNAR, and Prophet).

In one outcome time series, the observed values are influenced not only by the intervention effects but also by their preceding values, reflecting the inherent autocorrelation commonly observed in time series analysis. To account for the uncertainty in intervention effects, this study employs a distributed lag function to portray. By integrating these two concepts, we are intended to enhance the identification and estimation of intervention impacts on the outcome time series. Overall, we introduce a distributed lag function term to capture the uncertainty of intervention effects and incorporate it as an exogenous variable in the ARIMAX model. The coefficients of the distributed lag function term are computed through parameter estimation of the ARIMAX model, which in turn identifies the impact of the corresponding intervention on the outcome time series.

The parameter estimation of Eq. 6 is essentially a multiple linear regression parameter estimation problem. Given the strong multicollinearity among \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\boldsymbol{X}}$$\end{document} (more precisely, strong multicollinearity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\boldsymbol{P}}$$\end{document}), we consider using ridge regression to solve the problem of multicollinearity, thus the7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}={\left({{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{{\boldsymbol{T}}}{\boldsymbol{X}}+\alpha {\boldsymbol{E}}\right)}^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{{\boldsymbol{T}}}{\boldsymbol{Y}}$$\end{document}

In this study, we use the determinant method of ridge parameter estimation to estimate the ridge parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}. Previous studies have shown that if the determinant of a matrix is greater than 0.1, then the matrix is considered not pathological (multicollinearity) [24, 25]. Therefore setting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}=0.001 ~ 1 (step = 0.001), we select the smallest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} satisfying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D\left(\alpha \right)$$\end{document}>0.1 as the chosen ridge parameter. Although, other threshold values can certainly be set, for simplicity, this study adopts a threshold of 0.1, which is also a widely accepted standard in many other studies [25]. In addition, we draw the ridge trace diagram of the parameter estimation process in the result section, namely L-Curve method, which can intuitively help us judge whether the selected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} is reasonable or not. We have also included a sensitivity analysis with the threshold (det > 0.1) relative to the interval [0,1] in the Supplementary Materials (Fig S1-12).

In addition, we estimated the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the ridge parameters using a full-series Bootstrap. The entire time series was resampled with replacement as a single contiguous block, and this procedure was repeated 10,000 times to obtain the empirical distribution of the ridge-regression estimates. For a specific scenario, the empirical 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of each ridge regression estimate were taken as the lower and upper bounds of the corresponding 95% CI.

The statement of emergency (SoE) is a large-scale public health intervention to curb the rise in the number of infections by reducing human mobility. The aforementioned HMI is an important indicator to measure human mobility, so we can quantify the impact of interventions SoE by decomposing the change in HMI. Although the date of the SoE declaration was fixed and well-defined (the nominal time of the intervention, April 07, 2020, in this study), the impact of the SoE began on HMI earlier than the date of the official declaration. Before the first official SoE declaration, the public was also aware from various other sources that an unknown novel coronavirus was spreading. For personal self-protection reasons, the public naturally took some initiative to reduce their outings, which may have impacted human mobility. From this perspective, the actual intervention timing was ambiguous, and it was reasonable to assume intervention effects were distributed over a period time rather than at a specific, fixed time point (April 07, 2020). Thus, the intervention SoE was regarded as an appropriate test case that met the assumptions of our AR-LagDT model. In the following, we demonstrate how the AR-LagDT model handled such cases (real-world outcome time series). In our research design, we consider the pre-intervention time series to be stationary without applying week/season adjustments, which is consistent with the actual characteristics of the test case (HMI outcome time series). Our primary focus was to assess ambiguity when intervention timing is unclear rather than periodic decomposition.

Order of AR-LagDT

Before we utilized the AR-LagDT (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p,s,{l}_{1},{l}_{2}$$\end{document}) model to estimate the impact of intervention SoE, we must first set the orders of the model. For the autocorrelation of the HMI time series, we can select the order with commonly used criteria: Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Compare to AR (1) (417.8908 and 422.8681) and AR (3) (415.0629 and 424.9265), AR (2) was the better choice with smaller AIC (413.5802) and BIC (421.0122). Therefore, we can set the order \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p$$\end{document}=2. In this study, for simplicity, we posit that the human mobility index on a given day is influenced solely by policy interventions implemented within a 10-day window preceding that day, with earlier interventions deemed negligible, namely \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s=10$$\end{document}.

Discussion

In this study, we employed the distributed lag function (using different PDFs) to account for the ambiguity of intervention effects while incorporating the autocorrelation of the outcome time series. We proposed the AR-LagDT model, which allows for the direct identification and separation of intervention impacts from the observed outcome time series, providing accurate estimates. Additionally, we decomposed intervention effect ambiguity into different ambiguous scenarios, which represented by varying relative positions within the distribution period and multiple distribution patterns, facilitating a more detailed examination. Using the SoE in Tokyo as an empirical case study, the AR-LagDT model estimated intervention effects at each time point following the SoE, enabling an analysis of the maximum impact, cumulative effect, and temporal progression of its influence on the outcome time series of HMI.

The existence of a distributed period of intervention effects between pre- and post-intervention phases is a common phenomenon. For unplanned interventions, such as political changes [26] or disruptions in the supply of illicit drugs [27], defining a precise intervention time point is often challenging. In such cases, assuming a gradual effect distribution over time may be the most appropriate approach. The use of the distributed lag function enables a systematic analysis of intervention effect ambiguity. While prior studies have explored time series methodologies for scenarios with uncertain intervention timing, such as the ARIMAITS-DL model proposed by Daisuke and applications of fuzzy set theory [16, 28, 29]. These models primarily focus on maintaining the intervention effect within the outcome time series for "counterfactual" prediction. However, prediction-based models face inherent limitations, as their accuracy diminishes with increasing prediction horizons due to growing uncertainty and data complexity [30, 31]. This constraint has been well-documented in the example application of predictive models in this study, such as ARIMA, Holt-Winters and NNAR. In our empirical study, the training set consisted of 48 time points, while the prediction set included 42 time points. This near-equivalent training-to-prediction ratio presents significant challenges for maintaining prediction accuracy in conventional "predictive" type models. Although ARIMA and NNAR achieve slightly lower errors on some in-sample metrics, these models show highly unstable and potentially implausible counterfactual trajectories during the post-intervention period, making their intervention-impact estimates unreliable. This is because of prediction-oriented models minimize short-term forecasting error but do not account for ambiguity in intervention onset, duration, or lag structure.

The purpose of AR-LagDT is not to outperform prediction type models in prediction accuracy, but to provide a stable and interpretable framework for estimating time-varying intervention impacts under ambiguous conditions. The AR-LagDT model overcomes this limitation by bypassing reliance on extended counterfactual predictions and directly extracting intervention effects from the observed time series. This capability allows for the estimation of the dynamic progression of intervention impacts, including the instant maximum effect and its corresponding time point. Such insights enhance researchers’ understanding of intervention effectiveness and inform decision-making processes in policy evaluation and program optimization [32, 33].

A potential challenge in applying the AR-LagDT model lies in determining the appropriate fuzzy statuses within the distribution period and selecting suitable distribution patterns for intervention effects. In this study, we evaluated 12 different scenarios and observed variability in estimated intervention effects across different settings, with more pronounced differences among the four distribution patterns. A priori selection of the most appropriate pattern, which guided by literature review or expert opinion, is preferable, as it ensures alignment with real-world phenomena [34, 35]. However, in cases where prior information is unavailable, a data-driven selection approach can serve as an alternative. This method should balance statistical fit (e.g., mean squared error) with interpretability from epidemiological, medical, and policy perspectives [36]. Additionally, sensitivity analyses across multiple scenarios are crucial for validating the robustness of the model [3]. If similar intervention impacts are detected under different hypothetical scenarios, confidence in the research findings is strengthened [4].

Strengths

A major strength and novel contribution of this study is the use of the distributed lag function to characterize the ambiguity of intervention effects while enabling the direct separation of intervention impacts from the outcome time series and their subsequent estimation. We incorporated four different probability density functions (uniform, normal, left-skewed, and right-skewed) to represent distinct distributed lag patterns, effectively covering nearly all plausible real-world intervention effect distributions. Additionally, by varying the relative positions of the distribution period, we accounted for different fuzzy states of intervention effects, further aligning our model assumptions with real-world conditions.

To demonstrate the practical applicability of our approach, we applied the AR-LagDT model to assess the impact of the SoE intervention on the HMI. This empirical study serves as a concrete example of how the model can be implemented, making it more accessible to researchers and practitioners across various fields. By providing a clear methodological framework, the AR-LagDT model offers a flexible and intuitive tool for future intervention analyses.

We have created a standalone, user-friendly application that is openly available to everyone in order to make it easier and more affordable to use our suggested AR-LagDT model. Additionally, the software’s original code is freely available to the public on GitHub. To enhance user engagement and facilitate a comprehensive understanding of the software’s practical implementation, a supplementary demonstration video has been provided. This resource systematically illustrates the software’s functional application and operational workflows, offering users an empirically grounded visual aid to reinforce conceptual clarity and procedural familiarity.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the selection of different fuzzy states and distribution patterns was not guided by a fully data-driven approach. Instead, we emphasized their real-world interpretability, as their selection should correspond to their practical meaning rather than solely minimizing a model-fitting metric. Literature reviews and expert opinions may provide more reasonable choices for determining these parameters, as different model-fitting metrics emphasize different aspects of model superiority and may yield varying selection results [37, 38].

Second, to estimate the parameters of the AR-LagDT model, we employed ridge regression to address multicollinearity and improve estimation efficiency. However, this approach introduces a trade-off between improved efficiency and a tolerable level of bias [23, 39]. The ridge parameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}) in this study was determined using a commonly used method, but alternative estimation techniques (such as the Hoerl-Kennard formula, the variance inflation factor and so on), are available [40, 41]. Further research is needed to assess the robustness of different ridge parameter estimation techniques in the AR-LagDT model. Besides, although the AR-LagDT model builds upon ARIMAITS-DL by accounting for the skewed distribution pattern of intervention effects, the actual distribution patterns may be more diverse. Future research may benefit from exploring hybrid distributions or non-parametric distributions.

Besides, the current version of AR-LagDT is designed for stationary pre-intervention series and a single intervention without control groups. In scenarios involving strong non-stationarity, multiple or overlapping interventions, or available control time series, the model may require further extensions, such as incorporating time-varying AR structures, multiple distributed-lag terms, or comparator-assisted formulations.

Conclusion

When intervention effects are distributed over time and follow different patterns (ambiguity of interventions effect), these conventional models, such as CSR model and prediction type models, may be insufficient. To address this limitation, we developed the AR-LagDT model, which employs distributed lag functions to explicitly characterize intervention effect ambiguity. We utilized four probability density functions to represent four distinct lag distribution patterns and incorporated varying distribution period positions to account for different fuzzy states. AR-LagDT providing estimates of maximum impact, cumulative effect, and time-dependent changes of intervention impacts for each scenario.

AR-LagDT delivered comparable performance and clearer time-varying impact interpretation, while ARIMA and NNAR performed better on some in-sample metrics but showed notably less stable long-term predictive behavior, limiting the credibility of their counterfactual predictions. In the application of AR-LagDT, the choice of kernel is crucial for obtaining credible estimates of time-varying intervention impacts and is also a potential challenge. The selection should be compatible with the temporal characteristics of the observed data, and demonstrate stability under reasonable variations through sensitivity analyses. When prior empirical or theoretical knowledge exists, an a priori choice of kernel is generally preferable. Otherwise, a data-driven approach may be adopted. Future methodological extensions may further enhance AR-LagDT, including block-bootstrap procedures for improved uncertainty quantification, constrained or regularized \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega$$\end{document} to facilitate more interpretable lag-weight discovery and the generalization of the AR-LagDT. These extensions will support broader applications of AR-LagDT in intervention evaluation across public health, epidemiology, and social policy research.
