Assessment of Meat Handlers’ Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices Regarding Meat Safety in Retail Meat Shops
Authors: Tsraynesh Teamr, Destaalem Gebretsadkan, Weldemelak Girmay, Mebrahtu Weldegebriel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFoodborne diseases (FBDs) affect nearly 600 million people annually worldwide, with contaminated meat representing a major source of illness. In developing countries such as Ethiopia, inadequate meat hygiene, poor handling practices and limited regulatory enforcement increase the risk of contamination. Meat handlers play a critical role in preventing foodborne pathogens, yet gaps in knowledge, training and personal hygiene practices remain significant public health concerns.ObjectivesTo assess knowledge, attitude and practices (KAPs) towards meat safety among meat handlers in meat retail shops in Mekelle, Tigray, Ethiopia, and to determine the association of risk factors to meat safety.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted among 182 randomly selected meat handlers. Data were collected through semi‐structured interviews using a questionnaire, and participants were selected via simple random sampling. Descriptive statistics summarized socio‐demographic characteristics and KAP levels. Chi‐square tests examined associations between independent variables and KAP scores. On the basis of Bloom's taxonomy, scores ≥70% were classified as good.ResultsOf the participants, 75.82% were male, 40.11% had at least secondary education, and 51.65% had received meat safety training. Good knowledge (88.46%), positive attitudes (92.31%) and good practices (71.98%) were observed. Knowledge, attitudes and practices were significantly associated with education and work experience (p < 0.05).ConclusionsAlthough meat handlers demonstrated generally good KAP towards meat safety, targeted training, consistent personal protective equipment (PPE) use, and regular medical check‐ups are essential to reduce contamination risks and improve public health outcomes.Keywords: attitude, knowledge, meat handler, meat retailers, meat safety, Mekelle, practices

Introduction

Globally, nearly 600 million people become sick each year from consuming contaminated food (World Health Organization 2015). Foodborne illness remains one of the leading causes of human disease, with the consumption of contaminated meat being a major contributor (Jacob et al. 2010).

Foodborne diseases (FBDs) result from biological, chemical and physical hazards that contaminate food at various stages of production and preparation (Adzitey 2016). In developing countries, the contamination and cross‐contamination of raw meat are significant factors contributing to foodborne illnesses (Adesokan and Raji 2014). Contamination sources have been identified as the animal's skin, as well as during processing stages such as evisceration, pluck removal and trimming (Govender et al. 2013).

In many developing countries, strict control over meat hygiene and safety remains challenging, and meat for human consumption is often approved primarily through visual inspection (Bakhtiary et al. 2016). Several major factors contribute to foodborne illnesses, including inadequate food handling procedures, unhygienic work environments, lack of medical examinations for meat handlers, poor personal hygiene and insufficient access to safe water for food preparation (Bhandari et al. 2022). Despite these challenges, FBDs are preventable if food protection principles are adhered to, from primary production to consumer consumption (Tessema et al. 2014).

Food safety is a significant public health issue worldwide. In Ethiopia, for instance, the demand for meat products is growing rapidly, with eating raw meat becoming increasingly popular as a status symbol (Zerabruk et al. 2019). Food safety encompasses the practices and conditions that prevent contamination from toxic chemicals or microorganisms, which remains a global concern (World Health Organization 2015). Specifically, meat safety involves a series of actions aimed at implementing standards, codes of practice and regulations to ensure the meat consumed is safe. Hygiene practices must be followed at every stage of production, processing and transportation and in the handling of meat, slaughter equipment and the surrounding environment (Bhandari et al. 2022).

The Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point (HACCP) system is globally recognized as a vital tool for ensuring food safety, effectively eliminating chemical, biological and physical hazards (Oloo et al. 2017). As the most effective system currently available for enhancing the safety of meat and meat products, HACCP has been endorsed for decades by governments and scientific communities (Tomašević and Đekić 2017). The system employs a preventive approach, controlling hazards at every stage of the food chain, from primary production and processing to storage, marketing and consumption (Chen et al. 2018). The HACCP system must be regularly reviewed, especially when there are significant changes in processes, ingredients, products or equipment that could affect potential hazards or control measures. The implementation of good hygiene practices (GHPs) should be assessed through hazard analysis and appropriate control measures to ensure food safety (World Health Organization 2023).

The knowledge, attitude and practices (KAPs) of food handlers play a crucial role in preventing foodborne outbreaks (Sharif and Al‐Malki 2010). Meat handlers have a critical role in preventing the spread of pathogens, either from contaminated utensils or from the animal itself, such as Escherichia coli and other pathogens. They may also carry pathogens specific to humans, like Hepatitis A, Noroviruses, Salmonella Typhi, Staphylococcus aureus and Shigella, on their hands, skin, hair or any cuts or sores, which they can transmit to consumers (Havelaar et al. 2013).

Poor meat handling and sanitation practices, coupled with inadequate safety laws, weak regulatory systems and a lack of education and training for meat handlers, contribute to the occurrence of FBDs. Meat vendors often use unclean tables and unhygienic knives, further increasing health risks. So, study on KAPs of food handlers for food safety is very crucial for urgent need of public health education, policy makers, regular training and retraining of meat handlers to safeguard the practices of meat handling safely and minimize the risk of foodborne disease (Ahad et al. 2025).

Therefore, this study aims to assess knowledge, attitude and practices towards meat safety among meat handlers in meat retail shops in Mekelle, Tigray, Ethiopia, and to determine the association of risk factors to meat safety.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

The study was conducted in meat retailers of Mekelle, Northern Ethiopia. Mekelle is the capital city of Tigray regional state and is located at a distance of 783 km s north of Addis Ababa with geographic coordinates of 13°32′ N and 39°33′ E. The altitude of the area is 2200 m above sea level. Climatologically, the area is classified as ‘mid‐land’ with an effective temperature between 14°C and 20°C (Beyene 2016) (Figure 1).

Map of the study area, Mekelle city.

Study Population

The town has a total of 282 meat retailers, and the study was focused on a sample of 182 meat handlers in Mekelle city. These individuals were selected because they were directly involved in the handling and sale of meat, ensuring that the sample was relevant and representative of the population of interest. Given that the total population of Mekelle city was 550,000, the study aimed to capture a meaningful subset of this larger population to gather accurate data on the meat retail houses. The lists of meat retailers were obtained from the Bureau of Revenue which provided comprehensive and official records from the city's seven sub‐cities, ensuring the sample selection was based on reliable and well‐documented sources. This method of data collection and sample selection strengthens the validity of the study by ensuring it is both representative and grounded in authoritative information.

Study Design

A cross‐sectional study was conducted from March 2024 to September 2024 to assess the KAPs towards meat safety on meat handlers in meat retail shops in Mekelle, Tigray, Ethiopia. This design provided a snapshot of the current KAPs at a specific point in time, ensuring relevant data for improving meat safety practices.

Data Collection

Data were collected through face‐to‐face interviews using a semi‐structured, pretested questionnaire, which included socio‐demographic details and assessments of KAPs related to meat safety.

Instrument for Data Collection

The semi‐structured format allowed for consistency while accommodating flexibility in responses. The questionnaire was pretested to identify potential issues, enhancing its reliability. The questionnaire was adapted from previously published research (Akabanda et al. 2017, Tegegne and Phyo 2017), translated into the local languages of Tigrigna and retranslated to English to ensure clarity and consistency and minimize translation bias. The questionnaire was divided into four sections: Section A focused on socio‐demographic characteristics; Section B assessed knowledge of meat safety with 14 ‘Yes’ or ‘No’ questions, categorizing scores above 7 as good knowledge and scores of 7 or below as poor knowledge based on Bloom's Taxonomy (Tegegne and Phyo 2017). Section C assessed attitudes with 13 ‘Agree’ or ‘Disagree’ questions, where a score above 6 indicated a positive attitude, and Section D assessed practices with 18 questions, where scores above 9 indicated good practices. This structured approach provided a comprehensive, standardized method for assessing KAPs, ensuring both validity and reliability in the data collection process.

Data Analysis

Data were checked, coded and entered into Microsoft Excel 2010 for organization and subsequently analysed using STATA statistical software version 13. Descriptive statistics such as frequency and percentage for qualitative data were employed to summarize the socio‐demographic characteristics and KAPs of the respondents, providing an overview of the data distribution. To examine the relationship between socio‐demographic characteristics and KAPs scores, a chi‐square (χ 2) test was applied. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, indicating a meaningful association between the dependent and independent variables. This approach ensures rigorous analysis by using appropriate statistical techniques to draw reliable conclusions about the data.

Discussion

Food safety, including meat handling, has a direct relationship with public health issues; poor hygienic meat handling practices can lead to serious health problems (Ehuwa et al. 2021). The socio‐demographic findings indicated that the majority of meat handlers were male. These findings strongly matched previous studies with Biswas et al. (2024) and 76.75% (Sarma et al. 2022) but differed from others, which reported 81.8% (Shati et al. 2021) and 55.6% (Stratev et al. 2017), which have reported a higher proportion of females involved in food handling. Conversely, the work of slaughtering is quite demanding and complicated, making it difficult for women to be employed in slaughterhouses; however, they can find jobs in certain poultry slaughterhouses, particularly in rural regions (Banuree 2019). This difference may be due to differences in cultural and social norms, employment opportunities and gender roles across the study area.

In this study, 30.77% of the respondents were aged between 26 and 35 years, which aligns with 33.3% (Kimindu et al. 2024) in Kajiado, Kenya, but not with Akabanda et al. (2017); a greater number (39.1%) of the participants were in the age bracket of 41–50 years in Gahan. Older food handlers have good food handling practices than younger food handling hygienic practices (Tegegne and Phyo 2017). The literacy rate of meat handlers, who primarily had a secondary education (40.11%), was higher compared to (Tegegne and Phyo 2017) majority of the respondents (52.7%), who were at the level of primary. This finding is higher than a study conducted in and around Babich and Gedo Towns, West Shewa Zone (Urgesa and Sorsa 2024). Participants with secondary education or higher exhibited a greater understanding of meat safety. This finding aligns with multiple studies indicating that higher levels of education among meat handlers correlate with increased knowledge of food safety (Chekol et al. 2019). However, this is not consistent with the findings of Al‐Mohaithef (2014), which might be due to variations in study design, sample size or socio‐cultural differences among the study population.

The 33.52% of the respondents had 6–10 years of experience. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Lagos State, Nigeria (36.71%) (Kehinde et al. 2020) but contradicts those of Gebru et al. (2023) the majority of (64.1%) respondents had service years of less than 4 years. About 51.65% of respondents in this study reported having regular medical check‐up; this result was lower than the study conducted by Gutema et al. (2021) (98%) and Agmas et al. (2020) (62.38%) of respondents had a regular medical check‐up (Gopinath et al. 2012). This difference might be due to variations in research methods and geographic factors.

In this study, about 51.65% of meat handlers were found to have received training in meat safety, which is higher than a study conducted in and around Babich and Gedo Towns, West Shewa Zone (Urgesa and Sorsa 2024); all of the slaughter personnel (100%) have not attended food safety training. This finding is supported by a study conducted in Chitwan (Khanal and Poudel 2017). However, it contrasts with studies conducted in Nairobi and Isolo, which revealed that none of the meat handlers or butchers had formal training in meat handling hygiene (Chepkemoi et al. 2015).

The practice scores of meat handlers were significantly linked to their educational status and training, consistent with findings by da Cunha et al. (2019). Furthermore, practice was significantly associated with educational status, years of experience, meat safety training and regular medical check‐ups. Participants with over 6 years of experience were more knowledgeable than those with less than 6 years (Teferi et al. 2021).

Overall knowledge, attitude and practices levels were found to be good in this research. This finding is consistent with a study conducted among food vendors in Owerri, Imo State, Nigeria (Iwu et al. 2017), which reported a knowledge score of 81%, also considered good. This finding was higher than those from previous studies (34.1%) in Debre Markos (Alemayehu et al. 2021) and (65.5%) in Lemi Kura, Addis Ababa (Fekadu et al. 2024). The observed differences might be attributed to variations in study methodology, education and training.

In this finding, most of meat handlers recognized that improper handling of meat can pose health hazards to consumers, with an awareness level of 91.76% (Alimentarius 1997).

The majority of study participants (97.25%) indicated that regular hand washing before and after meat processing can reduce the risk of contamination. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Ghana, where 98.7% of respondents acknowledged the importance of handwashing, and 91% of meat handlers in Jigjiga abattoir and retail meat shops in Ethiopia (Akabanda et al. 2017, Tegegne and Phyo 2017). Proper hand washing by food handlers has been shown to decrease the risk of FBDs such as diarrhoea (Kehinde et al. 2020, Al‐Kandari et al. 2019).

Most 75.27% of the respondents in this study demonstrated poor knowledge about common foodborne pathogens associated with meat. In contrast, a study conducted in 2021 in Ambo and Holleta found that 66.7% of food handlers had acceptable knowledge of foodborne pathogens (Gebremedhin et al. 2021). A total of 71.43% of respondents recognized that meat handlers with open wounds and gastroenteritis should not handle meat which is higher than the finding reported by Urgesa and Sorsa (2024). Most 85.6% of the respondents did not know the fact that people with open skin injury, gastroenteritis and ear or throat disease should not be allowed to handle meat.

The overall attitude of participants to meat safety in this study was good with a score of 92.31% which aligns closely with the 92.2% reported in Yeka Sub City, Addis Ababa (Mulat et al. 2024). However, this result is significantly higher than the 31% observed in Bole Subcity, Addis Ababa (Abdi et al. 2020). Conversely, the present finding is slightly lower than the 94.6% reported in Bishoftu City (Abunna et al. 2022).

This study showed that 68.8% of the respondents agreed with wearing clean PPEs. This study aligns with the findings from a study in Jigjiga, which reported 68.1% (Tegegne and Phyo 2017). However, this finding was lower compared to the 81.3% reported in a study conducted in Southern Tigray, Mehoni (Gebru et al. 2023).

In this study, 63% of the respondents at meat retailers strongly agreed that meat handlers with wounds, injuries on their hands or gastroenteritis should not handle meat. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Owerri, Imo State, Nigeria, which reported 70.5% (Iwu et al. 2017). However, this finding was lower than which reported that 85% of respondents acknowledged the risks associated with handling meat by individuals with open skin injuries (Al‐Shabib et al. 2017). Meat handlers with open skin injuries, gastroenteritis or ear and throat diseases may refrain from engaging in meat production.

The overall practices of participants in meat safety in this study were good, with a score of 71.98%. This finding is higher than 40.1% reported in Debark (Chekol et al. 2019), and 57.7% found in southern Tigray, Mehoni (Gebru et al. 2023). However, it is lower than the 89.43% observed in Jordan (Sharif et al. 2013). These variations may be due to differences in study design and the types of training provided.

The respondent's practices of wearing PPE were not satisfactory (28.57%) which wear gowns and hairnets, whereas the 21.98% of the respondents never used any protective equipment. In addition, only a small percentage (4.95%) utilize all recommended PPE. This finding is inconsistent with the results of Al‐Shabib et al. (2017), which indicated that 80% to 97% of food handlers frequently wore gloves, masks and caps while handling food. In contrast, only 40.11% of meat handlers in our study wore gowns, suggesting that these individuals may be potential sources of microbial contamination. It is crucial to implement all possible measures to reduce or eliminate such contamination (Ahad et al. 2025). The differences observed may be due to variations in study design, the effectiveness of food safety training programmes for handlers and a general lack of awareness among customers.

In this finding, most 72.53% of respondents wash their clothes daily after work. This finding contrasts with another study, which reported that 68.2% of participants did not wash their aprons after each day's work (Ashuro et al. 2023). Additionally, our result is lower than that of a study conducted in Wadi Salih Garsila, Central Darfur, Sudan, where 99% of respondents reported washing their clothes daily after work (Abduelrahmana et al. 2024). The disparities in these results may be attributed to cultural differences and environmental factors. This finding is consistent with the 93.84% reported in Khulna, Bangladesh (Biswas et al. 2024). A significant 70.88% of respondents in this study reported that they do not remove personal items such as rings, necklaces and watches while handling meat. This finding contrasts with a study conducted in 2017, Trakia University, Bulgaria, where only 47.8% of respondents wore accessories like rings and bracelets while cooking food (Stratev et al. 2017). The higher percentage in the present study might be influenced by cultural practices or personal habits that place less emphasis on removing personal accessories during meat handling.

Conclusion and Recommendation

Meat handlers in Mekelle city demonstrated a commendable level of knowledge regarding safe meat handling and processing, which was reflected in their positive attitudes towards meat safety. However, a significant number were unaware of common foodborne pathogens and the potential for seemingly healthy handlers to contaminate meat. There was a notable gap in the use of PPE, increasing the risk of contamination. Knowledge levels were significantly associated with factors such as educational background, years of experience and participation in meat safety training. Similarly, meat handling practices were strongly linked to educational status, work experience, meat safety training and regular medical check‐ups. Attitudes towards meat safety also showed a significant correlation with education level, experience and routine medical examinations.

Meat handlers should wear PPE while handling meat to minimize the risk of contamination. Greater emphasis must be placed on the risk of asymptomatic carriage of S. aureus and Salmonella. In addition, implementing regular training programmes is essential to educate meat handlers on proper meat safety and handling practices. Awareness campaigns should be conducted to inform individuals about the risks of foodborne pathogens and the importance of food safety. Furthermore, regulatory enforcement should be strengthened to ensure compliance with meat safety and hygiene standards through improved monitoring and adherence measures.
