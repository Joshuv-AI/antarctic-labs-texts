Strain-localized luminescent e-skin for high-resolution pressure mapping and visual force feedback
Authors: Zixiong Wu, Shuwen Chen, Shicheng Fan, Zheng Qiao, Jiaming Qi, Yusheng Zhang, Chwee Teck Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectronic skins (e-skins) that emulate human intuitive tactile perception with visual feedback capabilities are vital for next-generation wearables, robotics, and biomedical applications. However, existing visualized pressure mapping systems either require dense pixelated arrays that add bulk, complexity and signal crosstalk, or rely on rigid or semi‑rigid architecture, and are not able to conform to dynamically changing curved, soft surfaces. Here, we report a soft mechano-electroluminescent e-skin for high-resolution visualized pressure mapping. Quantitative pressure sensing is achieved on curved and compliant substrates. Built with an ultrathin, entirely soft microstructured architecture, it harnesses strain-localized deformation and intrinsic, in‑situ force–electric–optical coupling within a continuous device stack to achieve high‑fidelity pressure mapping without discrete sensing pixels. It achieves a spatial resolution of 30 μm (847 dpi) and high luminescent sensitivity (1.12 cd·m⁻²·kPa⁻¹). This e-skin allows real-time mapping of pressure distributions and recognition of fine tactile features like fingerprints. Integrated with plantar sensors and also laparoscopic tools, it also enables visual force feedback, enhancing gait analysis and surgical training, respectively. This approach offers a soft, compact, multimodal platform for intelligent tactile interfaces.Subject terms: Polymers, Sensors and biosensors, Conjugated polymers

Introduction

E-skins that emulate the sophisticated sensory capabilities1 of human skin can play significant roles in medical robotics2, prosthetic and rehabilitation devices3, wearables, and human-machine interfaces4. Human skin primarily contains four types of mechanoreceptors and a nociceptor that collaborate to enable intuitive perception of spatially innocuous tactile contacts and pressures. This includes the ability to sense irregular surfaces, 3D features, high-resolution spatial pressure distributions and respond to mechano-injury5. Accurate and intuitive pressure perception is critical in scenarios where natural tactile feedback is compromised or absent. In clinical settings, patients with peripheral nerve damage or sensory neuropathy lose the ability to sense mechanical stimuli, increasing risks of pressure-induced injuries, chronic ulcers, and even amputations. Similarly, in robotic prosthetics and minimally invasive procedures such as laparoscopy, the lack of tactile sensation can lead to unintended tissue damage or impaired precision. These challenges underscore the urgent need for e-skins that can deliver high-resolution, real-time, and intuitive pressure feedback in ways that are both accessible and scalable.

Recently, numerous flexible electrical pressure mapping systems have been reported to mimic mechano-sensory functions using thousands of piezo-resistive6–12, piezo-capacitive13–19, piezoelectric18,20–23 and triboelectric24–27, as well as emerging ionic/hybrid sensing systems28–30, typically combined with complicated external electrical signal-processing or display systems31–38. However, these systems often suffer from the limitation of low resolution for pressure mapping with a pixel density of no more than 100 dpi31,32,38. Additionally, the presence of multiple connecting wires, large size, crosstalk between adjacent pixels, and reliance on complex external signal processing or display systems make them costly, inconvenient, and user-unfriendly38. Various integrated user-interactive mechano-sensitive optical pressure mapping systems have been proposed to convert pressure directly into intuitive emissive signals34,39–43. However, some systems only provide mechanoluminescent pressure imaging without digital pressure information43; some can only display a color change for the average pressure without indicating pressure distribution information40. While some electroluminescent sensors enable integrated dual-mode sensing41,44, their low resolution31,38,40, complicated lithography methods31,38,40, use of toxic Cd-based quantum dots, and complex procedures involving seven coating layers42 limit their application. Moreover, most of these luminescent mapping systems are built on plastic substrates, which lack the softness and conformability required for application on curved or deformable surfaces29,31,38,42.

Here, we present a soft, ultrathin, mechanically defined luminescent e-skin (eLuminator) that achieves high-resolution pressure mapping, quantitative sensing, and real-time in-situ visual force feedback on compliant curved surfaces. It employs an ultrathin, all-soft micro-structured architecture that leverages strain-localized deformation and intrinsic in-situ force–electric–optical coupling within a continuous device stack, achieving high-fidelity pressure visualization without discrete sensing pixels. The device integrates an ultrathin microstructured mechanical contact layer with a continuous electroluminescent stack, establishing an intrinsic in-situ force–electric–optical coupling pathway. This pathway not only enables deformation-localized mechanical transduction with high-fidelity, high sensitivity, and a wide range, but also concurrently modulates interfacial capacitance, thereby integrating intuitive optical feedback with digital quantitative sensing in a dual‑mode platform. Consequently, spatial resolution is governed by mechanically confined strain propagation rather than the lithographic pitch of conventional pixelated sensors, yielding a high spatial resolution of 30 µm (847 dpi) and high luminescent sensitivity (1.12 cd·m⁻²·kPa⁻¹). The capacitive channel provides calibrated quantitative pressure readout from the same interface, exhibiting a sensitivity of 0.011 kPa⁻¹ and a broad pressure range up to 180 kPa. Owing to its fully soft, ultrathin build, the device conforms seamlessly to dynamically curved instruments, as demonstrated by its direct integration onto a laparoscopic grasper, enabling intuitive force visualization during surgical training. We further demonstrate its versatility through high-resolution fingerprint imaging and real-time plantar pressure mapping, highlighting its potential for pressure-distribution sensing in diabetic foot care. Our work establishes a framework for visual tactile feedback with high-resolution tactile imaging, with applications in biomedical diagnostics, surgical training, and human-machine interfaces.

Results

Design of the eLuminator

Integrating pressure sensors and display devices for high-resolution pressure mapping presents challenges, including the need for multiple arrays, complex configurations, and significant crosstalk31,32,38. Instead of integrating devices by physical connections, we designed a mechano-sensory e-skin (eLuminator) that emulates the multilayer structure of human skin (Fig. 1a). The eLuminator comprises three layers: the top pressure-sensitive microstructured (PSM) electrode layer with microdomes, the middle electroluminescent (EL) layer, and the bottom plain transparent electrode layer.

The top PSM electrode layer is fabricated by sequentially spraying silver nanowires (AgNWs) and a mixture of poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT: PSS)/polyurethane (PU) onto a soft microstructured composite substrate with a microdome array (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1). The microdome structure is chosen for its high-pressure sensitivity across a wide pressure range and excellent multi-directional sensing performance45–47. The arrayed microdomes (15 μm in height, 20 μm in diameter, 10 μm in spacing) are made of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and replicated from a mold created using photolithography. Instead of using a pure PDMS film as the substrate, a ferrocrete-like composite is used, fabricated by laminating a fibrous expanded PTFE (e-PTFE) (Supplementary Fig. 2) onto the replica with uncured PDMS. In this composite, the e-PTFE acts as a steel skeleton and the infiltrated PDMS acts as concrete, ensuring both the mechanical robustness and softness. After spray-coating with AgNWs and a mixture of PEDOT:PSS/PU, the microstructure transitions from smooth to relatively rough and conductive (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3). In this hybrid structure, AgNWs form an interconnected metallic mesh that bridges local gaps and enables efficient long-range charge transport. Meanwhile, PEDOT: PSS, uniformly dispersed within the PU matrix, creates soft yet conductive percolation networks. The PU component further enhances the mechanical stability and adhesion of the overall coating. At the microscale, these materials cooperatively assemble into hybrid conductive pathways, where PEDOT:PSS interfaces intimately with AgNWs to support continuous electron flow, even under mechanical deformation such as strain or bending (Fig. 1d). This layer is opaque due to the microstructure-induced light-scattering. The detailed fabrication process can be found in the Experimental Section and Supplementary Fig. 4.

The intermediate EL layer is composed of a mixture of poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF)-based fluoroelastomer, solvents, and ZnS: Cu particles. The mixture is poured into a mold and allowed to dry at room temperature to create a porous elastomer matrix film embedded with ZnS: Cu particles (Supplementary Figs. 5–6). The bottom transparent electrode layer is fabricated by sequentially spraying AgNWs and a mixture of PEDOT: PSS/PU on the e-PTFE/PDMS composite film (Supplementary Fig. 7). The porous network of AgNWs, the thin PEDOT: PSS/PU composite layer, and the transparent and unstructured PDMS substrate contribute to the high transparency of this layer (Fig. 1e).

By integrating the three layers, a visually enhanced conformal, pressure-sensitive eLuminator is produced (Fig. 1f). Owing to the thickness of ~10 µm for the e-PTFE/PDMS composite substrate and the thickness of ~18 µm for the electroluminescent layer, the integrated eLuminator is soft and ultra-flexible with a thickness of ~70 μm (Supplementary Fig. 8). This allows it to conform well to curved surfaces, such as tubes and human skin (Supplementary Fig. 9). The thickness of the PSM layer plays a critical role in determining the device’s spatial resolution. As shown in Fig. 1g, h, a thin sensing layer enables localized deformation of the elastomer directly above the EL layer, confining luminescence to the contact region and preserving a one-to-one mapping with applied pressure for high-resolution imaging. In contrast, a thicker layer promotes lateral stress diffusion, leading to signal crosstalk and spatial blurring, thus degrading resolution. The eLuminator can be used for the direct visualization of foot pressure distribution (Fig. 1i), fingerprint profile (Fig. 1j), and tactile information of the grasper (Fig. 1k). These features hold promise for applications such as diabetic foot diagnosis, user identification, and surgical training.

Working mechanism and electromechanical characterization

The mechano-sensory eLuminator integrates a piezoresistive conductive layer and an electroluminescent dielectric layer into a single device. When two electrodes of the eLuminator are connected to an AC power supply, it operates in the electroluminescent visualized pressure mapping mode (Fig. 2a). In the forward electric field, electrons are captured in nearby Cl donor sites, while holes are trapped by Cu acceptor sites. Upon reversal of the field, the injected electrons recombine with the trapped holes, resulting in electroluminescence (Fig. 2b). Without pressure, the electric field is insufficient to excite the luminescence of ZnS: Cu phosphors. However, when pressure is applied, the microdomes are compressed, reducing the height of the microstructures and increasing the contact area between them and the electroluminescent layer. This leads to an enhanced electric field experienced by the phosphor particles. Once the electric field reaches a critical level, the phosphors are excited and begin to emit light. The greater the pressure applied to the eLuminator, the shorter the distance between the electrodes, which results in a higher electric field and, consequently, brighter luminescence. The intensity of the electroluminescence is thus indicative of the magnitude of the applied pressure. In addition to providing a visual indication of pressure, the luminescence distribution can be captured by a camera and converted into a quantified pressure distribution heatmap for precise mapping.

When the two electrodes of the eLuminator are connected to a multimeter, it enables digital capacitive pressure sensing. The middle electroluminescent layer also functions as a dielectric, enabling it to be considered as a capacitor. The capacitance (C) of a capacitor is described by the formula

where ε is the permittivity of the dielectric, S is the surface area of the parallel electrodes, and d is the distance between the electrodes. Capacitance is therefore directly related to the permittivity of the dielectric, the area of the parallel electrodes, and the distance between the two electrodes. Pressure reduces the distance between electrodes and increases the parallel electrode area, thereby increasing the capacitance. Furthermore, the pressure-induced compression of the elastomer component in the middle layer can increase the volume fraction of phosphor particles, which, in turn, raises the effective permittivity48, further enhancing the capacitance. Consequently, changes in capacitance can also indicate the level of applied pressure.

Since both the visualized and digital pressure sensing performance primarily rely on the properties of the PSM and EL layers, it is crucial to characterize their electromechanical performances. We first tested the stress-strain behavior of each functional layer and the entire eLuminator. As demonstrated in Fig. 2c, the EL layer, the PSM layer, and the e-skin each exhibit a physical breaking strain of more than 100%. The eLuminator has a Young’s modulus of 4.7 MPa, which is within the range of human skin’s Young’s modulus (5 kPa-140 MPa)49. To assess flexibility, we also evaluated the electrical breaking strain of the PSM layer. Figure 2d indicates that a pure AgNWs network can withstand only a strain of 5%. However, with the addition of PEDOT: PSS/PU and its increased volume, the electrical breaking strain increases to 50%, although resistance also increases (Supplementary Fig. 10). Given the trade-off between conductivity and stretchability, we decided to use 50 μl of PEDOT: PSS/PU solution for the PSM layer unless otherwise specified.

To assess the pressure-sensing capabilities of the PSM layer, we developed a piezoresistive pressure sensor by laminating the PSM layer onto interdigitated silver (Ag) electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 11). The total resistance change is primarily caused by contact resistance change under pressure, which results from the deformation of the heights of the microdomes. Thus, the sensing performance of the piezoresistive sensor can reflect the pressure-responsive performance of the PSM layer. Supplementary Fig. 12 shows that the piezoresistive pressure sensor responds sensitively to pressure, with a sensitivity of 0.017 kPa−1 in the low-pressure region (<30 kPa) and 0.003 kPa−1 in the high-pressure region (>30 kPa), indicating the excellent pressure response of the PSM layer. The PSM layer also demonstrates electrical robustness and stability. As depicted in Fig. 2e, the pressure-sensing layer remains relatively stable even after 2000 continuous loading-unloading pressure cycles, with a peak pressure of 60 kPa at a loading rate of 60 kPa s−1. This durability is due to the combination of flexible conductive silver nanowires, PEDOT: PSS and elastomeric PU, which provide both conductivity and strain resilience. Initially, AgNWs form a conductive network, which is subsequently bridged by conductive polymer PEDOT: PSS (Supplementary Fig. 13). The inclusion of PU endows the PSM layer with enhanced flexibility and softness, helping to dissipate pressure-induced strain and protecting AgNWs from air contact. This addresses the challenges of poor adhesion, optical degradation and increased resistance and oxidation when exposed to air, thus enhancing its electrical stability50. Owing to the softness, elasticity, and strain stability of each layer, the eLuminator can conform to curved surfaces and still function effectively even with small bending strain (Fig. 2f, g). It is important to note that due to Poisson’s effect, an ultra-high stretching strain on the eLuminator may exert pressure on the microstructured layer, potentially leading to a false electroluminescent response.

Vth is the threshold voltage across the device, defined as the voltage where the luminance reaches 1 cd·m−2. f is the frequency of the applied voltage, and VELth is the threshold voltage across the phosphor layer. From Eq. (2), it is evident that Leffective is influenced by parameters of the power source, the EL layer, the PSM layer, and the plain electrode layer. Leffective increases with a rise in V0, f, P, and T, and decreases with a rise in R0, RE, and Vth. Therefore, a lower original resistance R0 of the PSM layer, a lower resistance RE and higher transparency T of the plain electrode layer, and a higher voltage V0 and frequency f of the power source are preferred to optimize performance.

To optimize the electroluminescent-pressure response and investigate influential factors, we first examined the transmittance of the PSM layer and the transparent electrode layer. As indicated in Supplementary Fig. 15, the patterns seen through the PSM electrode layer are much more blurred than those through the plain electrode layer, suggesting that the plain electrode layer has higher transmittance. This can be attributed to the significant light scattering caused by the microstructures on the PSM layer, even prior to the addition of AgNWs and PEDOT:PSS/PU coating. Consequently, we selected the plain electrode layer for observation. We then investigated methods to optimize the transmittance of the plain electrode layer. Supplementary Fig. 16 demonstrates that by adjusting the amount of AgNWs coating on the electrode layers, the transmittance levels change based on their initial resistance, ranging from 68% at 88.8 Ω/sq to 92.9% at 40,600 Ω/sq at a wavelength of 550 nm. To achieve an optimal balance between transparency and resistance, we selected an electrode with a resistance of 398.2 Ω/sq and a transparency of 76% at a wavelength of 550 nm. Despite its excellent conductivity and transparency, the electrode also exhibits good flexibility, maintaining its conductivity under deformation (Supplementary Fig. 17).

We also investigated the effects of both frequency f and voltage levels V0 of the AC power source on the electroluminescent intensity. We first measured the luminescence of the device under different frequencies from an AC power source at a constant peak voltage of 100 V and applied pressure of 30 kPa. For this experiment, the thickness of the EL layer was consistent, and the electrode resistance was approximately 400 Ω/sq. As depicted in Fig. 3a, the luminescent intensity significantly increased with the rising frequency within the frequency range of 0 to 1 kHz but the rate of increase slowed down beyond 1 kHz. Although the luminescent intensity continued to rise, higher frequencies place greater demands on instruments and driving circuits, leading to increased dielectric losses and energy consumption, and can result in audible noise. Consequently, we determined that the optimal frequency for operation is 1 kHz. Subsequently, we assessed the impact of voltage amplitude on luminescence. Figure 3b illustrates that the luminescent intensity initially rose slowly and then more sharply as the voltage of the power source increased at a set frequency of 1 kHz and pressure of 19 kPa, 97 kPa, and 155 kPa. However, operating at excessively high voltages for extended periods can lead to EL layer breakdown, resulting in dark zones and potential safety hazards. Therefore, we identified 150 V as the suitable voltage level.

Figure 3b also indicates that the luminescent intensity increases with pressure at a constant driving frequency. This observation is consistent with the behavior of the threshold voltage Vth and Eq. (1). Vth is defined as the minimum voltage required to achieve a predefined noticeable luminescence Lth. Substituting Lth into Eq. (1) can express Vth as a function of the applied AC frequency. Furthermore, according to the electroluminescence threshold model, Vth is proportional to Eeffective ∙ d, where Eeffective is the effective electric field, and d is the thickness of the dielectric layer. The application of pressure reduces d, leading to a higher Eeffective, which in turn lowers Vth required to excite the ZnS:Cu phosphors. Thus, Vth is inversely proportional to pressure. For the device, the fitted threshold voltage at a frequency of 1 kHz decreased with increasing pressure, ranging from 57 V at 19 kPa to 43 V at 155 kPa. This inverse dependence of Vth on pressure directly explains the corresponding enhancement of luminescent intensity observed in Fig. 3b.

We further investigated the electroluminescent pressure response of the eLuminator and its influencing factors. As depicted in Fig. 3c and its inset, the luminescence intensity consistently increases with rising applied pressure for all devices at AC voltages ranging from 100 V to 250 V and a frequency of 1 kHz. The devices demonstrate higher sensitivity in the low-pressure range (0–30 kPa), with lower sensitivity in the high-pressure range (>30 kPa), affording a broad pressure sensing range (0–180 kPa). This suggests a less significant change in luminescent intensity at higher pressure compared to the lower pressure. At an AC voltage of 150 V with a frequency of 1 kHz, the sensitivity is 1.12 cd∙m−2 ∙ kPa−1 within the low-pressure regime and 0.15 cd∙m−2 ∙ kPa−1 within the high-pressure range. The threshold pressure, defined as the pressure at which the luminance reaches 1 cd·m⁻², can be inferred from Fig. 3c. The threshold pressure varies with the AC voltage level and is generally below 19 kPa for voltages ranging from 100 V to 250 V. To measure the response time of the eLuminator, we applied pressure with a pen point and recorded the change in luminescence using a camera. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 18, the luminescent intensity peaked at roughly 30 ms after the pen-point contact and then gradually diminished, completely fading around 30 ms after the pen was lifted. Although the peak brightness was achieved in 30 ms, luminescence was detectable as early as 15 ms, indicating a visualized pressure response time of about 15 ms.

In the digital pressure sensing mode, the EL layer functions as a dielectric layer, and the equivalent circuit of the capacitive pressure sensor is depicted in Supplementary Fig. 19. Figure 3d presents the capacitive response and sensitivity of the device across two distinct linear regions. Within the low-pressure range (0–30 kPa), the sensitivity is 0.011 kPa−1, and this range exhibits the most pronounced change in capacitance with applied pressure. In contrast, the high-pressure range (30–180 kPa) has a relatively lower sensitivity of 0.002 kPa−1. These results indicate that our eLuminator is adept at detecting not only small pressures with high sensitivity but is also capable of accurately measuring larger pressures over a substantial range, extending up to 180 kPa. The high sensitivity of the eLuminator allows it to register the pressure from gentle finger touches and even the weight of a small ring (Supplementary Fig. 20). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 21, the normalized capacitive and electroluminescent sensitivities follow nearly identical pressure–response trends, with high sensitivity at low pressures and a gradual decrease at higher loads. This consistent correlation originates from the fact that both signals are governed by the same pressure-induced deformation of the PSM layer. In the capacitive mode, compression of the microstructured layer increases the effective electrode contact area and reduces the effective dielectric thickness, resulting in an increase in capacitance. In the electroluminescent mode, according to Eq. (2), the applied pressure decreases the resistance of the PSM layer, which increases the effective voltage across the EL layer and thereby enhances the luminance output. Therefore, although the two channels rely on different transduction mechanisms, both are regulated by the same mechanical deformation process under pressure, giving rise to nearly identical pressure–response trends. At low pressures, the microstructures deform more easily, leading to high sensitivities in both modes, whereas at higher pressures, as the deformation gradually approaches saturation, the sensitivities of both channels decrease accordingly.

The eLuminator also demonstrates a rapid digital response to pressure. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 22, when a bottle cap weighing 0.85 g is rapidly placed onto the eLuminator and then removed from it, the response durations for the loading and recovery phases are 110 ms and 150 ms, respectively. This indicates a pressure response time of approximately 110 ms, which is noticeably longer than the optical response time of ~15 ms. The calculation method of response time is discussed in Supplementary Note 3. This difference in response speed originates from their distinct operating mechanisms. The optical response is driven by the rapid electric field-induced carrier recombination in ZnS: Cu particles, which occurs almost instantaneously upon field application. In contrast, the capacitive response involves mechanical deformation of the microdome structures and dielectric relaxation in the elastomeric matrix, both of which inherently require more time to reach equilibrium.

Because of the fast response, the capacitance changes nearly synchronously with the applied force (Supplementary Fig. 23). Additionally, the device exhibits excellent pressure-sensing stability. The eLuminator maintains consistent performance even after 1000 bending cycles, as demonstrated in Fig. 3e. The eLuminator was also tested with periodic pressure stimulation ranging from 0 to 10 kPa at a loading rate of 10 kPa/s. Following 4000 cycles, the pressure response of the eLuminator remained stable, confirming its durability and reliability over extended use, as shown in Fig. 3f. Such high stability is primarily due to the flexibility, elasticity, and mechanical robustness of each layer, especially the functional layers. This resilience to bending and pressure cycling ensures that the eLuminator can function reliably under various long-term, strain-related conditions. Furthermore, the broad pressure-sensing of the eLuminator, capable of detecting from gentle touches to strong impacts, makes it suitable for a wide array of applications.

High resolution of static pressure imaging and dynamic tactile tracking

The eLuminator is capable of displaying high-resolution images with excellent quality and high contrast, making it effective for both static pressure distribution mapping and dynamic tactile tracking (Fig. 4a). Fine details, such as sophisticated fingerprint patterns with distinct valleys and ridges, can be clearly visualized through variations in light intensity when pressure is applied to the optimized device. In general, when a patterned pressure is applied to the eLuminator, the mechano-electrical-optical behavior of the device goes through three stages: first, the applied object pattern generates an initial pressure distribution (Pinput) on the surface of the top PSM electrode layer; then, this pressure distribution is transmitted to the interface between microdomes and the EL layer (Ptransmit); and finally the transmitted pressure leads to spatial variations in the electrical field within the EL layer, initiating luminescence in specific areas and thus creating a patterned luminance output (Ltransmit).

The spatial resolution of pressure imaging is primarily determined by the cumulative fidelity of mechano-electro-optical transmission. It begins with mechanical transmission, where the thickness of the PSM substrate and the geometry of the microdome array determine how applied pressure is redistributed on the EL layer. Under localized pressure, the PSM substrate deforms, leading to pressure redistribution and distortion in Ptransmit, thereby affecting the electric field distribution, electroluminance, and effective spatial resolution. Specifically, the transmitted compression increases the contact area at the microdome tips, leading to localized field intensification, which excites microscale luminescence and further influences the resolution (detailed in Supplementary Note 4). Finally, the finite size, distribution, and excitation region of the phosphor particles impose a fundamental physical constraint. Consequently, the overall imaging resolution is co-determined by the synergistic interplay of the dome array configuration, the PSM substrate thickness, and the EL particle properties.

To evaluate the maximum pressure imaging resolution, we investigated the dome array pitch, the EL particle characteristics, and the PSM layer thickness. In our device, the bottom electrode and PSM layer can be considered to form a continuous surface, while the dome array features a pitch of 30 μm, with each dome measuring 20 μm in diameter and spaced 10 μm apart. The EL layer, composed of densely and uniformly distributed ZnS: Cu particles (~25 μm in diameter), projects an effectively continuous luminescent surface without distinct pixelation. Critically, since the particle diameter is smaller than the dome pitch, the spatial and pixel resolution are primarily governed by the dome array geometry. Consequently, as per Supplementary Note 5, the 30 μm pitch sets the theoretical resolution limit at 847 dpi. A detailed comparison with representative previously reported pressure sensors is provided in Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Table 1.

To validate the high resolution and investigate the influence of the PSM layer on resolution, we conducted tests by applying pillar arrays to devices with PSM layers of varying thicknesses (Fig. 4b, c), the zoom-in figures are shown in Supplementary Fig. 25. As illustrated in Fig. 4d, when the top transmission layer is 10 μm thick, the eLuminator can clearly depict the distinct patterns of pillar arrays (with a radius of 400 μm and a gap of 400 μm, corresponding to a pixel resolution of 63 dpi), as well as those with smaller-sized pillars (with a radius of 200 μm and a gap of 200 μm, corresponding to a pixel resolution of 127 dpi). Conversely, an eLuminator with a thicker PSM layer of 200 μm shows a blurring of the pattern images of the detailed pillar arrays, making them difficult to distinguish (Fig. 4e). This blurring is due to higher thickness and rigidity contributing to increased localized strain and larger bending curvature, resulting in more distortion of the transferred pressure distribution, as shown in the simulation in Supplementary Fig. 26 and explained in Supplementary Note 6. In contrast, a thin and soft PSM layer can deform more conformably and transmit the pressure distribution accurately, thereby maintaining high resolution.

This conclusion can also be verified by the mapping of sophisticated fingerprints. For an eLuminator with a 10 μm thick PSM layer, a clear spatial 3D light intensity profile of high contrast can be obtained (Fig. 4f), while for an eLuminator with a 200 μm thick PSM layer, only blurred microtextured fingerprint images can be seen (Fig. 4g). Generally, the fingerprints feature ridge distances of 300–700 μm, and a height difference between ridges and valleys of 40-60 μm (Supplementary Fig. 27)52, which falls within the eLuminator’s distinguishing capacity (spatial resolution of 30 μm, pixel resolution of 847 dpi). Thus, the reason for the clear electroluminescent visualization of the fingerprints mainly lies in two factors: first, the microtextured sizes of the patterns are within the distinction capacity of the eLuminator; second, the thin PSM layer ensures ultralow pressure distortion during pressure transmission.

The eLuminator also provides the capability of continuous pressure mapping, which facilitates the acquisition of shape and size information of objects. To illustrate this, we fabricated stamps in the shapes of letters: “N”, “U”, and “S” using 3D printing techniques. When the stamps are pressed onto the eLuminator, a uniform luminescent distribution is observed, with the letters’ boundaries clearly defined with minimal distortions, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 28 and Supplementary Movie 1. The eLuminator is also capable of visualizing dynamic pressure, demonstrated by its ability to capture the traces of writing motion on its surface. This is achieved by writing letters on the eLuminator and recording the motion traces with a camera, as schematically presented in Fig. 4j. The camera captured the traces of the writing motion at 0.5 s intervals, as shown in Fig. 4k. By compiling images taken at various time intervals, a coherent representation of the writing trace for the letter L is revealed (Fig. 4l). The real-time writing event is depicted in Supplementary Movie 2. We further evaluated the dynamic sensing ability of the device under conditions involving simultaneous dynamic loading and mechanical deformation. For this test, the eLuminator was integrated onto a soft air-inflatable bladder that undergoes cyclic stretching-relaxation deformation, thereby introducing both time-varying pressure inputs and stretch–relaxation deformation of the substrate. As shown in Supplementary Movie 3, the eLuminator exhibits stable, repeatable, and temporally synchronized luminescence modulation throughout the entire cycle, confirming its ability to reliably track dynamic pressure fluctuations while maintaining robust performance under continuous mechanical deformation.

Biomedical application of the visualized user-interactive eLuminator

We demonstrate the intuitive visual pressure mapping capability in various applications, such as displaying foot pressure distribution to alert diabetics of ulcer-prone areas and providing visual cues to enhance force control during laparoscopy training. The spatial distribution of luminescent intensity can indicate areas experiencing the greatest pressure. All luminescence images were acquired under standardized conditions: a fixed camera distance, constant ambient lighting, and uniform AC driving voltage (150 V, 1 kHz). A calibration curve was established using a 1 cm² stamp under known pressures to convert pixel intensity to pressure values, ensuring quantitative accuracy across experiments.

To illustrate real-time foot pressure mapping, a conceptual setup is schematically shown in Fig. 5a. The eLuminator is first affixed to the surface of a transparent box. When patients stand on the device, the luminescent signals emitted from the other side can reflect the pressure distribution and be shown in a mirror positioned at the bottom. This setup allows for the easy visualization of the foot pressure distribution and the identification of potentially abnormal areas directly by human eyes. Additionally, the luminescence can be captured by a camera and converted into corresponding pressure values. This is achieved by quantitatively estimating the pressure distribution based on the pressure response-luminescence curve, as illustrated in Fig. 5b. The curve is constructed by applying varying pressures to the eLuminator using a 1 cm×1 cm 3D-printing stamp and subsequently capturing images of the luminescence distribution and calculating the mean pixel intensity associated with each pressure level.

As a proof-of-concept, we constructed a 4 cm × 4 cm eLuminator. We then simulated the process of foot pressure mapping by applying a 3D-printed foot model to the eLuminator. The experiment utilized an AC power supply with a voltage of 150 V and a frequency of 1 kHz. After applying the foot model to the eLuminator, the resulting luminescent distribution was recorded and presented in Fig. 5c. The region exhibiting a higher luminescent intensity corresponds to the area experiencing greater pressure, a visual observation that can be easily discerned by the naked eye. Subsequently, image processing techniques were employed to generate a pixel intensity mapping of the captured image, as depicted in Fig. 5d. By utilizing the response curve established beforehand, we successfully derived the corresponding pressure distribution of the model foot, which is illustrated in Fig. 5e. This demonstration effectively showcases the feasibility of obtaining pressure distribution by analyzing the luminescent images.

We further evaluated the application of the eLuminator in simulated laparoscopy training, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 5f. Laparoscopic procedures demand a high level of technical skill because of limited visibility and lack of tactile feedback, making effective training essential. In particular, conventional training methods often lack real-time feedback regarding tissue handling. Tissue injury during laparoscopic grasping is known to originate from sharp, edge-localized stress concentrations, rather than from uniform compression across the grasper–tissue interface53–55. As a result, damage typically occurs within thin, spatially confined regions, highlighting the need for sensing systems capable of resolving these narrow over-pressure bands. By integrating the eLuminator into laparoscopic graspers, our system delivers immediate, quantitative, and high-resolution feedback on both applied force and technique, thereby supporting the development of safer and more precise laparoscopic training (Fig. 5f). When the eLuminator-equipped grasper is used to manipulate tissue, both the contact area and the magnitude of force can be visualized through changes in the illuminated region and luminescence intensity.

We demonstrated this capability using a tissue model in a simulated laparoscopic training setting, as shown in Fig. 5h and Supplementary Movie 4. Initially, in the absence of grasper-tissue contact, no luminescence was detected. As the applied force was incrementally increased, the grasper established closer contact with the tissue, resulting in a proportional increase in luminescence intensity and illuminated area. Upon force release, both luminescence intensity and illuminated area gradually reduced. Quantitative analysis of the maximum and mean luminescence intensities is presented in Fig. 5g, and the luminescence heatmap in Fig. 5i illustrates the spatial distribution of contact pressure across the tissue surface during grasping.

Further, we applied the grasper to a blood vessel model in a simulated manipulation task, where the luminescence accurately delineated the vessel’s shape during manipulation, enabling real-time visualization of contact location and pressure distribution (Supplementary Movie 5). We further quantified spatial performance by measuring the pressure transition width, which is the spatial distance required for the signal to rise from 10% to 90% of the peak value along the contact boundary. Two devices were fabricated: a high-resolution version (~10 μm pressure-sensing layer) and a lower-resolution version (~200 μm pressure-sensing layer). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 29, the high-resolution device produced a substantially sharper boundary (~0.64 mm) than the low-resolution device (~1.56 mm). Because clinically relevant over-pressure bands are themselves narrow, a broad transition blurs these high-stress regions, whereas the high-resolution eLuminator preserves them clearly. Thus, the high-resolution configuration more accurately captures injury-related pressure peaks and provides immediate visual feedback that can help trainees recognize and avoid localized over-compression during grasping. We also performed a simulated appendectomy task on an appendix model as a proof-of-concept training demonstration, during which the grasper manipulated and secured the model while the eLuminator provided real-time feedback on both the contact area and applied force (Supplementary Movie 6).

Overall, the integration of the eLuminator into laparoscopic graspers enables immediate and objective feedback on force application and technique, facilitating the efficient acquisition of safer and more precise surgical skills. This innovation holds promise for improving the effectiveness of laparoscopic training and ultimately enhancing clinical outcomes.

Discussion

Developing electronic skins that seamlessly merge sensing and visualization has been a long-standing challenge, often hampered by fundamental trade-offs between resolution, sensitivity, structural complexity, and mechanical compliance. Here, we introduce a bio-inspired electro-optical architecture that integrates display capabilities directly into the sensing mechanism, unifying these functions within a monolithic design. This intrinsic fusion enables an unprecedented combination of performance metrics, including high spatial resolution of 30 μm (847 dpi), high electroluminescent sensitivity of 1.12 cd·m⁻²·kPa⁻¹, fast response time below 15 ms, low luminescent pressure threshold of 19 kPa, and quantitative capacitive pressure sensing with 0.011 kPa⁻¹ sensitivity over a broad range of 0-180 kPa. Fabricated from non-toxic and flexible materials, the trilayer structure ensures both ease of production and biocompatibility, making the eLuminator a promising tool for diverse biomedical applications such as foot pressure mapping and laparoscopy training. Despite these advances, the capacitive sensitivity of the present device remains moderate relative to that of some ultrahigh-sensitivity capacitive pressure sensors, owing to the design trade-offs required for high-resolution optical pressure visualization and stable dual-mode operation. Future work will focus on improving capacitive sensitivity through dielectric/interface optimization while preserving the spatial resolution, optical performance, and structural simplicity of the current system.

This work also outlines pathways for future development to broaden the technology’s applicability. The device’s performance in controlled settings provides a strong foundation for further exploration of its long-term durability under repeated mechanical cycling and varying environmental conditions. Moreover, the current configuration presents a promising route for achieving compatibility with portable electronics, where employing high-k dielectric materials or advanced structural designs can significantly reduce the operating voltage and enhance the wearability. Addressing these aspects represents a critical and achievable step toward integration with next-generation, battery-powered wireless wearable systems.

The eLuminator platform also establishes a versatile foundation for numerous research and application opportunities. For instance, multi-spectral systems can be developed by incorporating phosphors with distinct emission wavelengths, such as ZnS: Mn for orange and ZnS: Cu, Al for blue-green, into patterned or stacked architectures. This approach enables direct optical encoding of multidimensional information like pressure, shear, or temperature into color output, thereby enhancing data density and interpretability. Furthermore, integration with emerging flexible electronics, particularly organic photodetector arrays, could create fully soft sensing-display feedback loops capable of real-time on-skin visualization and closed-loop control for next-generation soft robotics or intelligent prosthetics. In summary, the eLuminator platform establishes a foundation for advanced visuo-tactile e-skins, with demonstrated capabilities in high-resolution pressure mapping and multimodal sensing for biomedical diagnostics, human-machine interfaces, and soft robotics.

Methods

Materials

Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrene sulfonate(PEDOT: PSS) OE-000 (1.5 wt%) was purchased from Shanghai Ouyi Organic Photoelectric Materials Co., LTD; Silver nanowires solution (AgNWs) (5 mg/ml 90 nm/50 μm) was purchased from Guangzhou Nano Chemical Technology Co., LTD; Aqueous polyurethane dispersion (PUD, leasys 3458; 520 mg/ml) was purchased from Wanhua chemical group Co., LTD; ZnS: Cu Powder was purchased from Shanghai Keyan Photoelectric Technology Co., LTD; Fluoroelastomer poly(vinyidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene-co-tetrafluoroethylene) (P(VDF-HFP-TFE), DyneonTM FE 5730, 3M); Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) (SylgardTM 184); Resin clear V4 (Formlabs); DMSO. e-PTFE film (Xinyu Dachuan Fluorine Technology Co., Ltd)

Preparation of PEDOT: PSS/PU mixture

Polyurethane dispersion was used for higher flexibility and uniform coating. Before mixing, the concentration of PUD was diluted to 4 wt% in weight ratio with deionized water before use, and the PEDOT: PSS was premixed with DMSO with a weight ratio of 5 wt% in PEDOT: PSS. After that, 4.6 g diluted PUD was stirred at 600 rpm, and 2 g PEDOT: PSS (at 14 wt% of the mixture) was added into the stirring PUD by using a syringe. After that, the mixture was filtered with a 0.45 μm syringe filter before use.

Preparation of PSM layer

A silicon wafer mold with a microdome pattern (each dome is 20 μm in diameter and 15 μm in height with 10 μm spacing) was fabricated by photolithography. After silanization, a mixture of PDMS (ratio of elastomer base/curing agent = 10:1) was poured into the mold, followed by curing at 70 °C for 2 h to form a mold with a complementary microstructure. After the PDMS mold was silanized, a mixture of PDMS (ratio of elastomer base/curing agent = 10:1) was poured into the mold. Then, an e-PTFE layer was mounted onto the PDMS and pressed to squeeze extra PDMS out of the film. The as-fabricated mold, along with PDMS and e-PTFE, was placed in the oven and cured at 70°C for 2 h. Then, the cured composite substrate was peeled off, and the microstructured side was treated with oxygen plasma for 2 mins at 15 W. The plasma-treated substrate was then placed on a hot plate at 70 °C, and 300 μl AgNWs solution (5 mg/ml; 90 nm in diameter; 50 μm in length) was spray-coated on the microstructured side using air. Then, 50 μl PEDOT: PSS/PU mixture was spray-coated to form a hybrid conductive film on the substrate.

Fabrication of the electroluminescent layer

To prepare the electroluminescent layer, 5 g of fluoroelastomer was first dissolved in 25.5 ml acetone. Subsequently, the ZnS: Cu at 50 wt% was mixed with the above solution. The resulting mixture was then blade-coated onto a glass substrate using PI tape as a spacer. The mixture was spread with a doctor blade and dried at room temperature to prevent rapid bubble formation during heating.

Fabrication of a transparent electrode

The spraying of the conductor was conducted on a hot plate at 70 °C. First, 300 μl AgNWs solution (5 mg/ml, 90 nm in diameter; 50 μm in length) was spray-coated on the plasma-treated PDMS/e-PTFE composite using an airbrush. Then, 50 μl PEDOT: PSS/PU mixture was spray coated to form a hybrid transparent electrode film on the substrate, followed by annealing at 150 °C for 10 mins.

Fabrication of flexible and ultrathin e-skin

The ultra-thin PSM layer was affixed to a supporting glass substrate, with copper wire attached to the layer using silver paste. Next, the electroluminescent layer was placed on top of the pressure-sensing layer using double-sided adhesive tape. Subsequently, a transparent electrode was applied and affixed to the electroluminescent layer using double-sided adhesive tape, with copper wire also connected to this electrode.

Characterization

Electrical characterization of the sensors was tested by a Keithley DMM6500 digital multimeter. The static loads, continuous loads, and loading cycles were applied with Shimadzu EZ-SX with a computer controller. The resistance of the electrodes was measured by a multimeter (Fluke 287 true RMS multimeter). The transmittance of the electrodes was measured by a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-3600). The luminescent intensity was measured by a luminescent and color meter (Konica Minolta CS-200). Written informed consent was obtained from all volunteers prior to their participation in the study.

Foot pressure sensing application

The foot pressure sensing experiment demonstrates the eLuminator’s ability to visualize and quantify pressure distribution in real time. A 4 cm × 4 cm eLuminator was attached to a transparent acrylic box and powered by an AC voltage of 150 V at 1 kHz. A 3D-printed foot model was used to apply pressure on the device surface, and the luminescent emission from the opposite side was captured by a digital camera. The mean pixel intensity of each pressure level was extracted using MATLAB to establish a calibration curve between luminescence intensity and pressure. The calibrated curve was then applied to the recorded images to generate spatial pressure maps, illustrating the feasibility of real-time visualized foot pressure monitoring.

Laparoscopy simulation application

The laparoscopy simulation experiment evaluates the use of the eLuminator in providing real-time force feedback during simulated surgical training. A standard laparoscopic grasper was integrated with a 1 cm × 2 cm eLuminator at its tip and operated under 150 V, 1 kHz AC excitation. Artificial tissue and organ models were used to mimic grasping scenarios, including soft tissue, blood vessels, and an appendix model. The luminescence intensity and illuminated area were recorded by an HD camera and analyzed frame-by-frame using MATLAB. Variations in brightness and illumination corresponded to the magnitude and area of applied force, confirming the capability of the eLuminator to provide immediate, visualized, and quantitative feedback on grasping pressure in a fully simulated surgical environment.
