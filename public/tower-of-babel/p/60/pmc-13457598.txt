53BP1-independent Shieldin-BRCA1 antagonism at replication-coupled double-strand breaks
Authors: Yi-Li Feng, Meng Wang, Ge Li, Ruo-Dan Chen, Rui Yao, Shun-Li Dong, Xiu-Jun Cai, An-Yong Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BRCA1-deficient tumors are hypersensitive to PARP inhibitors (PARPi) due to impaired homologous recombination (HR). The 53BP1-Shieldin complex inhibits end resection at replication-independent DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), promoting PARPi sensitivity in BRCA1-deficient cells. However, its role in replication-coupled DSBs is less clear. Here, we show that loss of Shld2 or Shld3, but not 53bp1, confers PARPi resistance in mouse embryonic stem cells lacking a functional BRCA1 BRCT domain. Unlike 53bp1 loss, deletion of Shld2 or Shld3 partially restores HR at replication-coupled DSBs, reduces BRCA1-linked insertion/deletion signatures, and promotes RAD51 loading. This 53BP1-independent function requires the CST complex and counteracts residual BRCA1 coiled-coil domain activity in RAD51 loading. Shld2 loss also confers PARPi resistance in Bard1-null cells retaining residual BRCA1, but not in cells expressing RING-less BRCA1 with degraded BARD1. These findings identify a 53BP1-independent function for Shieldin in sustaining HR deficiency in BRCA1-deficient cells, providing new mechanistic insights into PARPi resistance.

Introduction

The tumor suppressor BRCA1 plays a critical role in homologous recombination (HR), one of two primary DNA double strand break (DSB) repair pathways, by regulating at least two key steps: 5’−3’ end resection, which requires the exon 11-encoded region, and presynaptic RAD51 nucleofilament assembly, facilitated by BRCA2/PALB2 that is recruited by the coiled-coil (CC) domain of BRCA11,2. Germline and somatic BRCA1 mutations compromise HR, significantly increasing the risk of breast, ovarian, prostate, and other cancers. This HR deficiency renders BRCA1-deficient tumors highly sensitive to inhibitors of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARP), such as Olaparib, through a synthetic lethality mechanism3,4. However, acquired resistance to PARP inhibitors (PARPi) frequently develops, with HR restoration as a key underlying cause5.

53BP1 and its downstream effectors limit end resection and suppress HR of classical replication-independent double-ended DSBs (deDSBs) in BRCA1-deficient cells such as those with BRCA1 alleles lacking exon 11 or the RING domain6,7. Loss of 53BP1 or its downstream effectors restores HR and confers PARPi resistance in these cells. In particular, 53BP1 interacts with RIF1, which in turn recruits the Shieldin complex—composed of SHLD1, SHLD2, SHLD3, and REV7—to protect DSB ends from excessive nucleolytic degradation, thereby promoting the other primary DSB repair pathway non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)8–17. Shieldin further engages the CTC1–STN1–TEN1 (CST) complex, which either directly imposes end resection blockade or recruits DNA polymerase-α-primase for fill-in synthesis to facilitate NHEJ and suppress HR10,18,19. Shieldin has also been implicated in preventing RAD51 loading onto 3′-single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) overhangs following ionizing radiation (IR)-induced DSBs in a 53BP1-dependent manner20. Notably, while being widely regarded as a 53BP1 effector in suppression of end resection, Shieldin is dispensable for 53BP1-dependent processes such as V(D)J recombination, chromatin stabilization, and PTIP-mediated resection suppression11,20–23, indicating Shieldin-independent functions of 53BP1 in these contexts. However, whether Shieldin can act outside the 53BP1-Shieldin pathway remains unclear.

Due to impaired HR, BRCA1-deficient tumors accumulate characteristic genomic mutational signatures, including base substitutions, short insertions/deletions (indels), unbalanced translocations, and microhomology (MH)-mediated tandem duplications (TDs)24–28. These BRCA1-linked mutational patterns serve as biomarkers for HR deficiency and potential PARPi sensitivity in cancer treatment25,29. While HR dysfunction in classical replication-independent deDSBs is often considered a primary driver of BRCA1-linked mutational signatures, emerging evidence suggests that replication-coupled DSBs, rather than classical deDSBs, drive formation of mutational signatures such as short indels, translocations and MH-mediated TDs in BRCA1-deficient cells30–32. Studies of replication-coupled DSBs, converted from DNA nicks, which are the most frequent DNA lesions in mammalian cells, reveal structural and mechanistic differences in damage response and repair from classical deDSBs30,32–36. In most of these studies30,33–35, DNA nicks induced by Cas9 nickases (Cas9n) are frequently converted into replication-coupled DSBs, which are preferentially repaired by HR between sister chromatids. However, Cas9n-induced nicks can also stimulate HR in the presence of exogenous ssDNA donors without conversion into replication-coupled DSBs37–39. End resection at replication-coupled DSBs is found to be active in cells where BRCA1 is depleted by RNA interference or disrupted by CRISPR/Cas9-induced indels at exon 10 of BRCA1, implying that BRCA1 is likely dispensable for end resection at replication-coupled DSBs33. It is possible that replication-coupled DSBs limits the 53BP1-BRCA1 antagonism to a post-resection step such as the RAD51 filament formation33. Paradoxically, 53BP1 loss confers PARPi resistance in cells lacking BRCA1 exon 11, but not in cells lacking the CC domain, exons 5–13, or the BRCT domain of BRCA140–42. Further, the BRCA1-mutant cancer cell line HCC1937, which lacks a functional BRCA1 BRCT domain and has little SHLD2 expression, exhibits robust IR-induced RAD51 focus formation and displays PARPi resistance41,43. These observations raise a question as to whether 53BP1 and Shieldin function differently in response to replication-coupled DSBs compared to classical deDSBs.

Here, using Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT mouse embryonic stem (ES) cells, which lack the functional BRCA1 BRCT domain, we demonstrate that Shieldin plays a 53BP1-independent role in repairing replication-coupled DSBs arising from DNA nicks. Loss of Shld2 or Shld3, but not 53bp1, confers PARPi resistance in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells, likely by restoring HR repair of replication-coupled DSBs. Shieldin disruption also reduces short indel formation in these cells, while 53bp1 loss has no such effect. Further, this 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin requires the CST complex. Mechanistically, by directly binding to ssDNA, Shieldin antagonizes residual RAD51 loading mediated by the CC domain of destabilized, BRCT-less BRCA1 proteins, independently of 53BP1. In contrast, in Brca1Δ11/Δ11 mouse ES cells lacking Brca1 exon 11, Shieldin acts with 53BP1 to inhibit HR repair of classical, replication-independent deDSBs, and both restrict HR at replication-coupled DSBs. Consistently, Shieldin promotes PARPi sensitivity at least in part independently of 53BP1 in Bard1–/– cells, where most BRCA1 proteins are degraded. However, this Shieldin activity appears to counter residual BRCA1 retained in Bard1–/– cells, rather than antagonize BARD1 directly. Together, these findings suggest that while Shieldin and 53BP1 act epistatically to suppress end resection at deDSBs in BRCA1-deficient cells, Shieldin can independently inhibit RAD51 loading that is mediated by the CC domain of residual BRCA1 during HR repair of replication-coupled DSBs, thereby promoting PARPi sensitivity in BRCA1-deficient cells.

Results

Shieldin promotes PARPi sensitivity independently of 53BP1

Mouse ES cells with truncated BRCA1 BRCT domains are hypersensitive to PARPi4. To determine the role of 53bp1 and Shld2 in the PARPi sensitivity of Brca1-deficient cells, we generated isogenic Brca1∆BT/∆BT mouse ES cells, which lack a functional BRCA1 BRCT domain, and several Brca1∆BT/∆BT clones lacking 53bp1, Shld2, or both (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a-d). As expected, Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells were hypersensitive to PARPi and the topoisomerase I poisons Camptothecin (CPT), compared to Brca1+/+ cells in cell survival assays (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1e,f). However, while loss of 53bp1 did not confer resistance to PARPi or CPT in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells, loss of Shld2 did, regardless of the 53bp1 status (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1e,f). Competition assays and clonogenic survival assays further confirmed that Shld2 loss, but not 53bp1 loss, conferred resistance to PARPi and CPT in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells (Fig. 1c,d, Supplementary Fig. 1g, and Supplementary Fig. 2). Consistently, the elevated frequency of PARPi-induced radial chromosomes observed in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells was not suppressed by 53bp1 loss, but was markedly reduced by Shld2 loss, irrespective of 53bp1 status (Fig. 1e). Similarly, loss of Shld3 also rendered Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells resistant to PARPi and significantly reduced the frequency of radial chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 3a-e). In the human cancer cell line MDA-MB436, which lacks functional BRCA1 BRCT domains, depletion or deletion of Shld2, but not of 53bp1, confers PARPi resistance (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 4). To evaluate whether this 53BP1-independent role of Shieldin also applies to Brca1∆11/∆11 cells, we generated isogenic Brca1∆11/∆11 mouse ES cells using a paired Cas9-sgRNA approach44 (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Subsequent deletion of 53bp1 and Shld2 in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells was confirmed by Sanger sequencing, followed by Western blot (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 5b). Consistent with previous studies6,11,13, deletion of either 53bp1 or Shld2 conferred PARPi resistance in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells and suppressed the increase of PARPi-induced radial chromosomes observed in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells (Fig. 1b,d,e and Supplementary Fig. 5c). This suggests that 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin is not evoked in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells.

Shieldin suppresses HR of replication-coupled DSBs independently of 53BP1

As HR restoration is a known mechanism of PARPi resistance in BRCA1-deficient cells5, we wondered whether 53BP1 and Shieldin play distinct roles in HR suppression in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells. Using mouse ES cells carrying a single-copy SCR-RFP reporter integrated at the Rosa26 locus30,45,46, we assessed the effects of 53bp1 and Shld2 loss on HR of classical deDSBs and replication-coupled DSBs in a Brca1-deficient context. Compared to Brca1+/+ cells, Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells showed significant reduction in both STGC, LTGC, and total GC (i.e., STGC + LTGC) during HR repair of classical deDSBs, but exhibited an elevated LTGC bias (Fig. 2b-e). STGC was also reduced during HR of replication-coupled DSBs induced by Cas9n in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells (Fig. 2b). However, LTGC was reduced in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells with Cas9D but was greatly stimulated with Cas9H (Fig. 2c). Consequently, Brca1 deficiency enhanced LTGC bias on one strand but not the other (Fig. 2e). Given that nicks induced by Cas9D and Cas9H respectively encounter the leading and lagging strands of DNA replication at the Roas26 locus30,31, these results indicate strand-asymmetric regulation of LTGC by BRCA1 during repair of replication-coupled DSBs, consistent with our previous observations30.

As expected, deletion of 53bp1 or Shld2 had minimal effect on HR in Brca1+/+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 6a,b). Notably, while loss of 53bp1 partially restored both STGC, LTGC, and total GC in repair of Cas9-induced deDSBs in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells, Shld2 loss had little or no impact (Fig. 2b-d). Moreover, restoration of STGC was greater in Brca1∆BT/∆BT53bp1–/–Shld2–/– cells than in Brca1∆BT/∆BTShld2–/– cells (Fig. 2b), suggesting that 53BP1 at least in part suppresses HR of classical deDSBs independently of SHLD2 in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells. Nevertheless, loss of either 53bp1 or Shld2 reduced LTGC bias in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells to levels comparable to Brca1+/+ cells (Fig. 2e).

In contrast, Shld2 loss, but not 53bp1 loss, significantly rescued STGC, LTGC, and total GC in repair of replication-coupled DSBs induced by Cas9D in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells (Fig. 2b-d). This rescue was evident even in the absence of 53bp1 (Fig. 2b-d). Similarly, Shld2 loss, not 53bp1 loss, restored STGC and overall GC in repair of Cas9H-induced replication-coupled DSBs to levels observed in Brca1+/+ cells (Fig. 2b-d). However, while LTGC bias induced by Cas9D remained largely unchanged across all genotypes, LTGC bias induced by Cas9H in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells was more strongly reduced by Shld2 loss than by 53bp1 loss. The strong reduction by Shld2 loss persisted in the absence of 53bp1 (Fig. 2e). These findings demonstrate that SHLD2 suppresses HR at replication-coupled DSBs via a 53BP1-independent mechanism in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells.

In Brca1∆11/∆11 mouse ES cells carrying a slightly modified SCR-RFP reporter30, STGC, LTGC, and total GC were all reduced during HR repair of Cas9-induced classical deDSBs compared to Brca1+/+ cells (Fig. 2f-h). Loss of either 53bp1 or Shld2 partially restored STGC, LTGC and total GC, although the rescue was more pronounced upon 53bp1 loss than upon Shld2 loss (Fig. 2f-h). This difference may reflect Shieldin-independent functions of 53BP111,20–23. However, neither 53bp1 nor Shld2 loss affected LTGC bias during HR of Cas9-induced deDSBs in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells (Fig. 2i). In contrast, during HR repair of Cas9n-induced replication-coupled DSBs in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells, STGC and total GC were reduced, whereas Cas9D-induced LTGC was only minimally impaired and Cas9H-induced LTGC was even elevated, resulting in much stronger LTGC bias for Cas9H (Fig. 2f-i). Loss of 53bp1 or Shld2 restored STGC and either altered or even enhanced LTGC, total GC, and LTGC bias during repair of Cas9n-induced replication-coupled DSBs (Fig. 2f-i). Together, these findings indicate that, unlike in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells, both 53BP1 and Shieldin contribute to suppression of HR at replication-coupled DSBs in the Brca1∆11/∆11 background.

Shieldin promotes replication-associated indel formation independently of 53BP1

Previously, we demonstrated that the small indel signature in BRCA1-deficient cells arises from replication-coupled DSBs, not replication-independent deDSBs30. To test whether 53BP1 and Shieldin function in this NHEJ-mediated formation of BRCA1-associated indel signature, we first analyzed Cas9-induced indels at four endogenous loci––Rosa26, Ldha, Col1a1, and H11. As previously reported30, the overall indel frequencies were similar between Brca1+/+ and Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells (Fig. 3aand Supplementary Fig. 7a). In line with a defect in BRCA1-mediated end resection, deletion lengths were significantly shorter in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells (Fig. 3b,c and Supplementary Figs. 7b-e, 8a,b, and 9a,b). In Brca1+/+ cells, loss of either 53bp1, Shld2, or Shld3 had no impact on the frequency or deletion length distribution of Cas9-induced indels (Supplementary Fig. 9a-c and 10a,b). In contrast, in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells, 53bp1 loss restored deletion lengths of Cas9-induced indels to levels observed in Brca1+/+ cells, whereas Shld2 or Shld3 loss had no such effect (Fig. 3b,c and Supplementary Figs. 7b-e, 8a,b, and 9a-c). Consistent with previous study30, Cas9D and Cas9H-induced indels occurred at higher frequency and showed a bias toward longer deletion in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells compared to Brca1+/+ cells (Fig. 3a-c and Supplementary Figs. 7a-e, 8a,b, and 9a-c). While loss of either 53bp1, Shld2, or Shld3 did not affect the frequency or deletion length distribution of Cas9n-induced indels in Brca1+/+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 9a-c and 10a,b), loss of Shld2 or Shld3, but not 53bp1, restored both the frequency and the deletion length distribution in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells to levels comparable in Brca1+/+ cells (Fig. 3a-c and Supplementary Figs. 7a-e, 8a,b, and 9a-c). This rescuing effect of Shld2 loss still occurred even in the absence of 53bp1, indicating that Shieldin remains fully functional and drives indel signature formation independently of 53BP1 (Fig. 3a-c). These findings suggest that Shieldin promotes formation of BRCA1-linked indel signature induced by replication-coupled DSBs in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells through a 53BP1-independent mechanism.

We also examined indel formation induced by Cas9 or Cas9n in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells. The frequency of Cas9-induced indels was unchanged in Brca1+/+, Brca1∆11/∆11, and Brca1∆11/∆11 cells lacking either 53bp1 or Shld2 (Fig. 3a). Deletion lengths were shortened in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells compared to Brca1+/+ cells, and this shortening was not reversed by loss of 53bp1 or Shld2 loss (Fig. 3b,c and Supplementary Fig. 8a,b). In contrast, Cas9n-induced indels in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells, compared to Brca1+/+ cells, exhibited increased frequencies and longer deletions––changes that were fully or partially abrogated by loss of 53bp1 or Shld2 (Fig. 3a-c and Supplementary Fig. 8a,b). These findings suggest that the indel signature also arises specifically from replication-coupled DSBs, rather than classical deDSBs, in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells. The formation of this signature is regulated epistatically by 53BP1 and Shieldin in this Brca1-deficient background, contrasting with the 53BP1-independent role of Shieldin in promoting replication-coupled indel formation in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells.

Shieldin exerts 53BP1-independent function through CST

As both CST and ASTE1 act as a downstream effector of 53BP1-Shieldin10,47, we wondered whether they would mediate 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells. We knocked out Ctc1 in Brca1∆BT/∆BTShld2+/+ and Brca1∆BT/∆BTShld2−/− mouse ES cells and established several isogenic clones (Supplementary Fig. 11a,b). Deletion of Ctc1 rendered Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells resistant to PARPi as did Shld2 loss, but had no additional impact on PARPi resistance of Brca1∆BT/∆BTShld2−/− cells (Fig. 4a,b and Supplementary Fig. 11c). These results suggest that CST mediates 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin. In addition, consistent with previous studies10,48, inhibition of DNA polymerase α suppressed the formation of PARPi-induced radial chromosomes in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells, mimicking the effect of Shld2 or 53bp1 loss (Supplementary Fig. 11d,e). However, this treatment had no detectable effect in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells, similar to 53bp1 loss, but in contrast to loss of Shld2, Shld3, or Ctc1 (Supplementary Fig. 11d,e). Shieldin inhibits RAD51 loading independently of 53BP1

To determine whether the 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin involves suppression of end resection at replication-coupled DSBs in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells, we first analyzed RPA32 phosphorylation in response to CPT. CPT-induced RPA32 phosphorylation accumulated in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells, but such accumulation was reduced upon loss of either 53bp1 or Shld2 (Fig. 5a). As expected, both PARPi and CPT induced comparable γH2AX focus formation, marking replication-coupled DSBs, in both Brca1+/+ and Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells, as well as in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells lacking 53bp1, Shld2, Shld3, or Ctc1 (Fig. 5b,c and Supplementary Fig. 13a-d). While PARPi or CPT-induced RPA32 focus formation was defective in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells, this defect was rescued by loss of 53bp1, Shld2, or Shld3 (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 13a,c). Similarly, PARPi- and CPT-induced RAD51 focus formation was defective in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells and this defect was partially or fully restored by loss of 53bp1, Shld2, or Shld3 (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 13b). However, the restoration was much weaker upon 53bp1 loss, suggesting that Shieldin exerts a prominent and partially 53BP1-independent role in counteracting RAD51 loading in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells. Consistently, Shld2 loss further enhanced PARPi- and CPT-induced RAD51 focus formation despite the absence of 53bp1 (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 13b). In addition, deletion of Ctc1 fully rescued both RPA32 and RAD51 focus formation upon PARPi treatment in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells (Supplementary Fig. 13c,d), further confirming the contribution of CST to 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin. Besides mouse ES cells, CPT also induced comparable γH2AX focus formation in MDA-MB436 cells regardless of SHLD2 status; however, SHLD2 deletion caused more CPT-induced RAD51 focus formation than 53BP1 deletion (Supplementary Fig. 13e). This again indicates a 53BP1-independent role of Shieldin in counteracting RAD51 loading in human cancer cells expressing BRCT-less BRCA1.

In contrast, this differential role between 53BP1 and Shieldin was not observed in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells where 53BP1 and Shieldin likely act epistatically to inhibit end resection. Loss of either 53bp1 or Shld2 similarly restored PARPi-induced RPA32 and RAD51 foci in these Brca1-deficient cells (Fig. 5d,e). It is possible that the 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin in suppressing RAD51 loading is dispensable in Brca1∆11/∆11 cells, in which RAD51 loading may be fully efficient at resected DNA ends.

To further validate the role of Shieldin in break-induced RAD51 focus formation, we used Cas9-gB2 or Cas9D-gB2 to induce classical deDSBs and replication-coupled DSBs, respectively, at hundreds of genomic repeat sites as previously described (Supplementary Fig. 14a)30. Both Cas9-gB2 and Cas9D-gB2 triggered robust γH2AX foci in Brca1+/+ and Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells, as well as Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells lacking 53bp1, Shld2, or both (Supplementary Fig. 14b,c). RAD51 focus analysis revealed that Shld2 loss restored RAD51 foci in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells regardless of 53bp1 status, whereas 53bp1 loss alone had minimal effect (Supplementary Fig. 14b,c). Transient depletion of Rad51 re-sensitized both Brca1∆BT/∆BTShld2–/– and Brca1∆BT/∆BT53bp1–/–Shld2–/– cells to PARPi (Supplementary Fig. 14d,e), further supporting a 53BP1-independent role for Shieldin in counteracting RAD51 loading activity in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells to confer PARPi sensitivity.

To further exclude an effect of end resection on RAD51 loading for the 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin, we performed ssDNA detection assays at Cas9- and Cas9D-induced DSBs at the Ldha locus of Brca1∆BT/∆BT and Brca1∆11/∆11 mouse ES cells (Supplementary Fig. 15a). We found that end resection was inefficient at Cas9-induced DSBs in Brca1∆BT/∆BT and Brca1∆11/∆11 cells compared with Brca1+/+ WT cells; however, loss of either 53bp1 or Shld2 significantly rescued this resection defect (Supplementary Fig. 15b,c). In contrast, at Cas9D-induced replication-dependent DSBs, end resection in both Brca1∆BT/∆BT and Brca1∆11/∆11 cells was as efficient as in Brca1+/+ WT cells, consistent with a previous study showing that end resection at replication-coupled DSBs is BRCA1-independent (Supplementary Fig. 15d,e)33. In this context, loss of either 53bp1 or Shld2 did not significantly affect end resection (Supplementary Fig. 15d,e). Taken together, these results imply that 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin acts to inhibit RAD51 loading at replication-coupled DSBs in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells.

53BP1-independent function of Shieldin requires ssDNA binding of Shieldin

As previously reported49,50, RAD51 can be loaded onto 3’ ssDNA not only through the canonical BRCA1-PALB2-BRCA2 complex, but also via an alternative, BRCA1-independent mechanism involving RNF168 and its PALB2-interacting domain (PID). Given that Shieldin inhibits RAD51 loading in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells independently of 53BP1, we tested whether this occurs through inhibition of the alternative RNF168-PALB2 pathway. We deleted the RNF168 PID in both Brca1∆BT/∆BTShld2–/– and Brca1∆BT/∆BT53bp1–/–Shld2–/– cells (Supplementary Fig. 16a,b). Biallelic deletion of the RNF168 PID slightly reduced PARPi-induced RAD51 focus formation in Brca1∆BT/∆BTShld2–/– cells but had no effect in Brca1∆BT/∆BT53bp1–/–Shld2–/– cells (Supplementary Fig. 16c). Moreover, RNF168 PID deletion did not re-sensitize either cell line to PARPi, indicating that the PARPi resistance conferred by Shld2 loss is not mediated via the RNF168-PALB2 pathway (Supplementary Fig. 16d,e). Thus, the 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin does not appear to involve inhibition of RNF168-PALB2-mediated RAD51 loading in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells.

As SHLD2 harbors OB-fold domains that bind ssDNA13,14,51, we hypothesized that Shieldin bind to 3’ ssDNA at replication-coupled DSBs independently of 53BP1, thus inhibiting residual RAD51 loading and conferring PARPi sensitivity in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells. To test this, we used a paired Cas9-sgRNA approach to disrupt the ssDNA-binding motif of SHLD2, which contains three critical residues (i.e., W463, F468, and G469) in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells13 (Fig. 6a). An in-frame deletion of the 39-bp motif generated the SHLD2∆WFG allele, which has impaired ssDNA-binding capacity, whereas an out-of-frame deletion removed the OB1-3 domains, generating the SHLD2∆OB1-3 allele13 (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 17a). Similar to Shld2 loss, both mutant Shld2 alleles conferred PARPi resistance and restored PARPi-induced RAD51 focus formation in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells (Fig. 6b,c and Supplementary Fig. 17b). Conversely, overexpression of SHLD2, but not its mutants lacking DNA binding activity, restored the PARPi sensitivity and suppressed RAD51 focus formation in Brca1∆BT/∆BTShld2–/– cells (Supplementary Fig. 17c-e). Due to a deletion at the SHLD2 locus, the BRCA1 BRCT-defective human cancer cell line HCC1937 expresses little SHLD2 and exhibits moderate resistance to Rucaparib43 (Supplementary Fig. 18a-c). We thus stably overexpressed SHLD2 and SHLD2-∆OB1-3 in HCC1937 cells and found that overexpression of SHLD2, not SHLD2-∆OB1-3 with impaired ssDNA-binding capacity, increased PARPi sensitivity and suppressed RAD51 focus formation (Supplementary Fig. 18c,d). These results suggest that more extensive depletion of Brca1 reveals a 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin in mediating PARPi sensitivity of Brca1-deficient cells.

Discussion

We identified a previously unrecognized, 53BP1-independent role of the Shieldin complex in modulating PARPi resistance and DSB repair in Brca1-deficient cells. Specifically, in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells that lack a functional BRCT domain but retain the CC domain, Shieldin inhibits RAD51 loading mediated by residual BRCA1 proteins in a 53BP1-independent manner. This leads to partial HR restoration at replication-coupled DSBs, decreased accumulation of NHEJ-mediated indels, and acquired PARPi resistance upon deletion of Shld2, Shld3, or Ctc1, but not upon 53bp1 loss. This scenario is further supported by the evidence that Shieldin executes 53BP1-independent function in Bard1–/– cells where BRCA1 is prone to degradation. In contrast, in Brca1Δ11/Δ11 and Brca1ΔRING/ΔRING cells, Shieldin functions together with 53BP1 to suppress either end resection or RAD51 loading and promote NHEJ at replication-coupled DSBs, mediating the PARPi sensitivity of these Brca1-deficient cells. These findings highlight the context-specific role of Shieldin, which can regulate DSB repair in either a 53BP1-dependent or -independent manner depending on the nature of a BRCA1 mutation. In addition, PARPi resistance develops in Bard1–/– cells upon Shld2 loss and in Brca1ΔRING/ΔRING cells when either 53bp1 or Shld2 is deleted. Given no chromatin binding by BRCA1 in Bard1–/– and Brca1ΔRING/ΔRING cells, this raises the possibility that BRCA1’s function in end resection and RAD51 loading at replication-coupled DSB ends may occur at least in part independently of chromatin-bound BRCA1.

The Shieldin complex cooperates with the 53BP1-RIF1 axis to protect DSB ends from resection following IR or genotoxic treatment8,9 (Fig. 8g). HR repair initiates with short-range resection by CtIP and the MRE11/RAD50/NBS1 (MRN) complex, followed by long-range resection by DNA2 or EXO162. BRCA1-BARD1 promotes this long-range resection and subsequently recruits PALB2-BRCA2 via the CC domain of BRCA1 to load RAD51 onto ssDNA, forming presynaptic filaments for strand invasion63,64. Concurrently, BRCA1 antagonizes 53BP1’s anti-resection activity. 53BP1 accumulates at DSB-flanking chromatin and blocks resection via downstream effectors PTIP and Shieldin8,9,20. Loss of 53BP1, PTIP, or Shieldin partially rescues resection and HR in BRCA1-deficient cells. In addition, previous work has shown that Shieldin may disrupt RNF168 recruitment at replication-independent DSBs to block RAD51 loading in Brca1Δ11/Δ11 cells; this function requires 53BP120. Notably, at replication-independent DSBs, enriched H4K20me2 enhances 53BP1 binding and further inhibits resection, exacerbating HR deficiency65,66. Using Cas9 to induce replication-independent DSBs in mouse ES cells, we found that 53bp1 loss partially restored HR in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT and Brca1Δ11/Δ11 cells, but not in Brca1ΔCC/ΔCC cells, consistent with 53BP1’s role in suppressing end resection only when BRCA1-mediated end resection is defective. Notably, while the 53BP1-Shieldin pathway limits hyper-resection at unprotected telomeres in TRF2-deficient somatic cells and thus promotes telomere fusion, Trf2 deletion in mouse ES cells fails to recruit 53BP1 to unprotected telomeres and does not induce telomere fusion67.

However, when replication-coupled DSBs were induced by Cas9n, 53bp1 loss failed to restore HR in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT and Brca1ΔCC/ΔCC cells. These DSBs arise during replication fork collapse at DNA nicks and are processed in a chromatin context favoring BRCA1-BARD1 binding, due to the dilution of H4K20me2 by H4K20me0 in replicative chromatin68,69. While this chromatin environment reduces 53BP1 recruitment, our findings show that HR in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells is rescued by the loss of Shld2, Shld3, or Ctc1, implicating a 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin-CST in this setting. SHLD2 and CST have the ability to bind 3’ ssDNA9,70. Because inactivating this ssDNA-binding ability of SHLD2 disrupts its 53BP1-independent function, our study suggests that Shieldin-CST may directly engage resected DNA ends at replication-coupled DSBs via ssDNA binding independently of 53BP1-mediated recruitment, thereby inhibiting residual RAD51 loading and, by extension, HR in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells.

Unlike in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells, Shld2 loss rescues HR within the 53BP1-Shieldin axis in Brca1Δ11/Δ11 cells and does not even rescue HR in Brca1ΔCC/ΔCC cells following replication-coupled DSBs. In Brca1Δ11/Δ11 cells, end resection appears to be efficient at replication-coupled DSBs induced by Cas9n, and the CC domain of BRCA1 remains intact. If neither end resection nor RAD51 loading is impaired, why is Cas9n-induced HR defective in these cells? This question remains unresolved. However, it is still possible that end resection at replication-coupled DSBs is less efficient, but that this deficiency requires more sensitive detection. Nevertheless, since Brca1Δ11/Δ11 cells retain robust RAD51 loading, the anti-RAD51 loading effect of Shieldin may be prevented by the presence of strong RAD51 loading. In addition, in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells, Shieldin suppresses RAD51 loading independently of 53BP1, bypassing the end-resection step to inhibit HR. By ruling out RNF168/PALB2-mediated RAD51 loading in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells, we established that the BRCA1 CC domain is required for this 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin.

Based on these observations, we propose the following model for 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin (Fig. 8g): (1) In BRCA1+/+, BRCA1Δ11/Δ11, and BRCA1ΔRING/ΔRING cells, 53BP1-independent activity of Shieldin is too weak to counter strong RAD51 loading by the intact CC domain of stable BRCA1 WT or variants. (2) In BRCA1ΔBT/ΔBT and BARD1–/– cells, where RAD51 loading mediated by the CC domain of residual BRCA1 is inefficient, 53BP1-independent activity of Shieldin is able to effectively block RAD51 loading, sustaining HR deficiency and PARPi sensitivity. (3) In BRCA1ΔCC/ΔCC and BRCA1–/– cells, absence of the BRCA1 CC domain renders the 53BP1-independent activity of Shieldin futile in countering BRCA1 CC-mediated RAD51 loading. Given that Shieldin on chromatin antagonizes RNF168-mediated RAD51 loading at replication-independent DSBs in a 53BP1-dependent manner20, it is possible that Shieldin also directly engages ssDNA independently of 53BP1 while simultaneously antagonizing RNF168 recruitment on chromatin within the context of the 53BP1-Shieldin axis. These two activities appear to be differentially triggered dependent on the type of DSB induction and the specific BRCA1 mutation. Whether coordination between these two activities is required remains to be determined.

In BRCA1-deficient tumors, defective HR leads to characteristic short indel accumulation, not from classical deDSBs but from replication-coupled DSBs arising during DNA replication at DNA nicks27,30. Because NHEJ dominates in replication-independent DSB repair, BRCA1 deficiency does not significantly affect NHEJ frequency, though it may shorten NHEJ-induced deletions due to impaired resection. Shieldin, which binds ssDNA longer than 50 nt13,14,51, may be hindered by short resected ends in this context. Moreover, in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells, where BRCT domain loss destabilizes BRCA1-BARD171, 53BP1 recruitment is enhanced, enabling sufficient PTIP activity to protect ends even in the absence of Shieldin20. The 53BP1-PTIP activity may render the 53BP1-Shieldin axis non-essential for end protection in replication-independent DSBs. Replication-coupled DSBs, in contrast, are typically single-ended and contain long ssDNA tails from collapsed forks, promoting a break-induced replication (BIR)-like repair. In HR-deficient contexts, these DSBs are often repaired by NHEJ when converging forks provide a second end. This switch to error-prone repair explains the longer NHEJ-associated deletions in BRCA1-deficient cells. The finding that Shieldin promotes indel formation from replication-coupled DSBs in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells, regardless of 53bp1 status, reinforces its 53BP1-independent role in HR inhibition.

To determine which HR step is affected in repair of replication-coupled DSBs, we assessed end resection and RAD51 loading upon treatment with CPT and PARPi. In Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells, RPA32 focus formation induced by CPT and PARPi was restored by deletion of 53bp1 or Shld2, suggesting both factors suppress resection. However, only Shieldin disruption restored RAD51 foci, indicating that Shieldin, but not 53BP1, suppresses RAD51 loading mediated by the CC domain of BRCT-less BRCA1. This 53BP1-independent activity of Shieldin also operates in Bard1–/– cells, where only residual full-length BRCA1 is retained. This suppression of RAD51 loading is absent in Brca1Δ11/Δ11 and presumably in Brca1ΔRING/ΔRING cells, where BRCA1-mediated RAD51 loading is intact. In other words, Shieldin independently limits residual BRCA1-mediated RAD51 loading in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT and Bard1–/– cells, promoting NHEJ-based indel formation. In contrast, Brca1Δ11/Δ11, Brca1ΔCC/ΔCC and Brca1ΔRING/ΔRING cells rely on the 53BP1-Shieldin axis to suppress end resection and generate indels. In Brca1–/– cells, 53bp1 or Shld2 loss restored end resection but had no effect on RAD51 loading or indel formation. These findings support two distinct Shieldin functions: one with 53BP1 to inhibit resection, and one independent of 53BP1 to block residual RAD51 loading by insufficient BRCA1 activity. In this 53BP1-independent function, Shieldin is epistatic with CST; however, DNA polymerase α-primase, which can be recruited by CST to synthesize DNA on resected ends and promote NHEJ10, appears to be dispensable in this context. This suggests that no DNA synthesis is required for Shieldin-CST-imposed blockade of RAD51 loading independently of 53BP1. In tumors with BRCA1 promoter hypermethylation72, similar to tumors with BRCA1 BRCT truncation or BARD1 loss, this 53BP1-independent function of Shieldin-CST may also be relevant because of residual BRCA1-mediated RAD51 loading in these tumors (Fig. 8g).

HR-deficient tumors are highly sensitive to PARPi, which induce replication-coupled DSBs by blocking SSB repair. However, directly assessing HR deficiency in tumors remains a major clinical challenge. As an alternative, genome-wide sequencing can detect HRD-associated mutational signatures, including short indels arising from repair of replication-coupled DSBs29. We show that PARPi sensitivity in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT, Brca1Δ11/Δ11, and Brca1ΔCC/ΔCC cells closely correlates with the presence of such indel signatures. For example, 53bp1 loss restores HR at replication-independent deDSBs in Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells but does not reduce indels or confer resistance. These findings indicate that indel formation at replication-coupled DSBs is a more accurate predictor of PARPi response than HR status alone. Mechanistically, this reflects the role of Shieldin in enforcing error-prone NHEJ repair at replication-coupled DSBs in BRCA1-deficient cells. In Brca1ΔBT/ΔBT cells, truncated BRCA1 allows weak RAD51 loading via its intact CC domain, which is antagonized by Shieldin in a 53BP1-independent manner to promote PARPi sensitivity. This mechanism may also operate in BRCA1 promoter-hypermethylated tumors. Clinically, evaluating Shieldin pathway integrity and indel generation at replication-coupled DSBs may provide a more precise biomarker for predicting PARPi responsiveness.

In summary, Shieldin, together with CST, inhibits HR and promotes NHEJ of replication-coupled DSBs in BRCA1-deficient cells, particularly in those expressing BRCA1 that is unstable or at a low level. This 53BP1-independent function enables indel formation and sustains PARPi sensitivity. In contrast, the classical 53BP1-Shieldin axis is required to counteract BRCA1 during end resection at both replication-independent and replication-coupled DSBs. The divergent roles of Shieldin help explain differential PARPi resistance across BRCA1 mutations. Our findings not only reveal a novel mechanism by which Shieldin regulates HR independently of 53BP1, but also indicate the importance of considering BRCA1 mutation context and Shieldin status when predicting PARPi response.

Methods

Plasmids and cell culture

Plasmid px330 was originally obtained from Addgene (Cat #42230) and the SpCas9 cassette was cloned into a pcDNA3β-Hyg-based expression vector. Plasmids expressing Cas9D and Cas9H were generated by site-directed mutation from pcDNA3β-Cas9 using KOD-Plus-Neo Kit (TOYOBO). Mouse Shld3 was cloned into a pcDNA3β-Hyg-based expression vector. Three SHLD3 point mutants R171A, W137A, and D221A, two human SHLD2 mutants ∆WFG and 3 A, and two human CTC1 mutants R975G and R987W were generated by site-directed mutation. Other human CTC1 mutants ∆OB-G, 1196-∆7, A227V, and V259M were previously constructed54. Plasmids of sgRNAs were constructed from the U6-sgRNA vector. The sgRNA target sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The newly constructed plasmids were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Mouse ES cells were grown in DMEM medium supplied with 20% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco), 2mM L-glutamine (Gibco), 0.1mM-mercaptoethanol (Sigma), 0.1 mM non-essential amino acid (Gibco), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco) and 1000 U/mL leukemia inhibitory factor (Millipore) on either mouse embryonic fibroblast feeders or gelatinized plates73. The human breast cancer cell line MDA-MB436 (BRCA1 c.5277 + 1 G > A) and HCC1937 (BRCA1 5382insC) were grown in DMEM medium supplied with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco).

Paired CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene disruption and in-frame deletion

Mouse ES cells with out-of-frame and in-frame deletion of a specific gene were generated by paired Cas9 strategy44. Briefly, 2×105 mouse ES cells were transfected with the expression plasmids for paired sgRNAs along with Cas9 in a 24-well plate, and were seeded onto a gelatin-coated 10 cm plate for single clones without any antibiotic selection. Single clones were picked, expanded, and verified by Sanger sequencing of targeted PCR amplicons and Western blot. The Brca1∆BT allele with out-of-frame deletion was generated by paired Cas9-sgRNAs targeting the BRCT-encoding exon 16 of Brca130. The Brca1∆11 allele with in-frame deletion of exon 11 was generated by two rounds of paired Cas9-sgRNAs targeting exon 11. The Brca1∆CC allele with in-frame deletion was generated by paired Cas9-sgRNAs targeting the IKL motif of the CC domain40. The Brca1∆RING allele was generated by deleting the exon 2, producing a transcript in which the exon 1 is directly spliced to exon 3. The Brca1∆CC-BRCT allele was generated by in-frame deletion of IKL motif from the CC domain of BRCT-less BRCA1 using paired Cas9-sgRNAs in Brca1∆BT/∆BT mouse ES cells. 53bp1, Shld2, Shld3, Ctc1, Bard1, and Rnf168 were similarly deleted by paired Cas9. Brca1–/–53bp1–/– cells were previously established by Cre-mediated biallelic deletion of Brca1 exons 5-13 in the absence of 53bp142, and Shld2 was further deleted similarly by paired Cas9. The sgRNA target sequences and the PCR primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1 and 2, respectively.

Transfection and reporter assays

Transfection of mouse ES cells was performed with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) in a 24-well plate as previously described30,74. Total 2 × 105 mouse ES cells harboring the SCR-RFP reporter were transfected with 0.5 μg total DNA. For siRNA experiments, cells were transfected with 20 pmol siRNA. Transfected cells were analyzed for GFP+RFP− and GFP+RFP+ frequencies using the Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX flow cytometer 3 days (d) post-transfection. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) data were analyzed using the CytExpert 2.0 software. The STGC and LTGC frequencies were calculated after being corrected with background readings and normalized with transfection efficiencies30. STGC and LTGC were induced by Cas9 and Cas9n with sgRNA1b in Brca1∆BT/∆BT cells containing the classical SCR-RFP reporter and with sgEmx-7 and sgEmx-12, respectively in Brca1∆11/∆11 and Brca1∆CC/∆CC cells containing the modified SCR-RFP reporter “EMX1-SCR”30.

Lentivirus production and cell infection

HEK293T cells were transfected with either lentiCRISPR v2 vector or pLVX-EnCMV vector with psPAX2 packaging and pMD2.G envelope plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Culture media was collected 60 h later and filtered through a 0.45 mm filter. Cell lines were infected with lentivirus using polybrene and infected cells were selected based on antibiotic resistance.

Immunofluorescence

For RAD51 and RPA32 staining, 2 × 105 cells with distinct genetic background were grown on glass coverslips in a 24-well plate. Cells were centrifuged for 800 g×5 min and incubated for 24 hours (h) and then treated with or without PARPi (Olaparib: 4 μM, 4 h) or CPT (4 μM, 4 h). For MDA-MB436 and HCC1937 cells, 2 × 105 cells were treated with 10 μM CPT for 10 h. To induce DNA breaks by Cas9 and Cas9n, 2 × 105 cells were transfected with expression plasmids for Cas9/Cas9D and sgRNA targeting B2 repeats, and incubated for 24 h. Cells were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, followed by blocking in 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS. Cells were probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and Alexa Fluor 488/594-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, stained with DAPI, and imaged by a fluorescence microscope (Leica DM 4000). The captured images were analyzed by the cell counter. The following primary antibodies used for immunofluorescence were anti-RAD51 (A6268, 1:500) and anti-RPA32 (A26498, 1:500) from Abclonal and anti-γH2AX (#05-636, 1:1000) from Millipore. The second antibodies were Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies (#115-585-003, 1:1000) or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (#111-545-003, 1:1000) from Jackson ImmunoResearch.

Analysis of metaphase spread

To analyze chromosomal aberrations, cells were incubated with 0.05 μM Olaparib for 24 h. 0.2 μg/mL colchicine (NSC 757, Selleck) was added to arrest the cells in metaphase for 2 h. The cells in metaphase were harvested and washed with cold PBS twice, and then incubated in 0.075 M KCl at 37 °C for 20 min. Hypotonic cells were then centrifuged for 10 min at 800 × g and fixed in Carnoy’s fixative (methanol:acetic acid = 3:1) for 20 min at room temperature twice. Cells were resuspended with 0.5 mL Carnoy’s fixative and the suspension was dropped on a lean slide to release chromosomes. These slides were stained with 1% Giemsa solution for 5 min and mounted with neutral balsam (Sangon Biotech). All metaphase spread plates were imaged at a magnification of 1000x using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DM 4000). The chromosomal aberrations such as radial chromosomes were scored and calculated.

The CCK-8 assay for cell viability

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates in triplicate at 1500 cells per well. For the siRNA assay, 2 × 105 cells were transfected with 20 pmol siRNA in 24-well plate. At 24 h post-transfection, cells were harvested and seeded in 96-well plates in triplicate at 1500 cells per well. After 12 h, cells were treated with Olaparib (S1060, Selleck), Niraparib (S2741, Selleck), or CPT (S1288, Selleck) at the indicated concentrations for 3 d with DMSO as a mock treatment control.

For MDA-MB436, 1 × 105 cells were transfected with 20 pmol siRNA in a 24-well plate. At 12 h post-transfection, cells were harvested and seeded in 96-well plates in triplicate at 1000 cells per well. After 8 h, cells were treated with Rucaparib (HY-10617A, MCE) for 6 d, followed by culture in drug-free medium for an additional 6 d. For experiments using sgRNA, 1 × 105 cells were transfected with 0.5 μg Cas9-sgRNA in a 24-well plate. At 12 h post-transfection, cells were harvested and seeded in 96-well plates in triplicate at 1500 cells per well. After 24 h, cells were treated with Rucaparib for 12 d.

Cells were then replaced with fresh medium containing CCK-8 reagents (KTC011001, Abbkine) to incubate for 2 h before measuring the absorbance at 450 nm by Thermo Scientific Multiskan GO. Cell viability at each treatment was determined by comparing to the DMSO mock treatment and calculated with the equation: (ODSample – ODBlank)/(ODDMSO – ODBlank) × 100%.

The survival competition assay

To assess the PARPi or CPT sensitivity of mutant cells, survival competition assays were performed45. Briefly, to establish an isogenic WT mouse ES cell line stably expressing GFP, mouse ES cells containing the SCR-RFP reporter were transfected with expression plasmids for Cas9-sgRNA to induce HR that generates GFP+ cells. GFP+ cells were sorted by flow cytometry and cultured and a single GFP+ clone was grown and established. 1.66 × 105 test mouse ES cells that are GFP– were respectively mixed with 0.33 × 105 isogenic GFP+ WT cells at the ratio of 5:1 and plated in 24-well plate, each in triplicate. At 12 h after plating, cells were treated with Olaparib, Niraparib, or CPT at the indicated concentrations for 3 d with DMSO as a mock treatment control. The percentages of viable GFP+ cells for each treatment were determined by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX). Percentages of viable GFP− cells were calculated for each treatment and corrected as the ratio to the corresponding percentage of viable GFP− cells with the DMSO mock treatment. Relative cell survival for each test cell line was derived by normalizing the corrected percentage of viable GFP− cells for each test cell line with the corrected percentage of viable GFP− cells set at 100% for WT cells.

Clonogenic survival assays

Approximately 2000 mouse ES cells were plated onto 6-well culture plates coated with 0.1% gelatin. At 18–20 h after plating, Olaparib was added to the medium at the indicated concentrations. After 7–10 d, cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Clonogenic survival for a given treatment was calculated relative to the plating efficiency of mock-treated controls4.

Indel signature analysis by Illumina deep sequencing

To analyze the frequency and pattern of indels induced by Cas9 or Cas9n, cells were transfected with expression plasmids for Cas9/Cas9n-sgRNA targeting a specific genomic site and NHEJ was induced. At 72 h post-transfection, cells were harvested and genomic DNA was isolated30. The targeted regions of less than 300 bp in genomic DNA were amplified by PCR with primers listed in Supplementary Table 2. Next-generation sequencing was performed at Novogene Co. Ltd (Beijing). PCR products purified with PCR Clean-up kit (Axygen) were end-repaired, adenylated at 3′ ends, ligated with adapters, purified, and amplified by the second round of PCR to incorporate the P7 and P5 Illumina adapters according to the manufacturer’s protocols (Yeasen, Hieff NGS Ultima DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina). Sequences were analyzed to determine the indel frequency and identify the indel pattern at repair junctions using DBS-Aligner30,73. In particular, the frequency of NHEJ-mediated indels is calculated as the ratio of indel reads to total reads from targeted Illumina sequencing and normalized by respective transfection efficiency.

DNA end resection measurement by qPCR

Analysis of DNA end resection mediated by Cas9/Cas9n was performed as previously described, with modifications75. Total 2 × 105 mouse ES cells were transfected with 0.35 pmol sgRNA and 0.35 pmol Cas9 or Cas9D mRNA using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) in a 24-well plate. The mRNAs encoding Cas9/Cas9D and the sgRNA targeting the Ldha locus of mouse ES cells were purchased from Start Bioservices and Tsingke Biotechnology, respectively. At 12 h and 18 h post-transfection, cells were harvested, and genomic DNA was extracted. 200 ng of genomic DNA was digested in a 20 μL reaction volume with PstI (NEB) or subjected to mock treatment at 37 °C overnight. 1 μL of digested or mock-digested DNA was used as the template in a 20 μL qPCR reaction containing 10 μL of 2×ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme #Q711) and 0.5 μM of each primer, using a CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). qPCR was performed for 40 cycles, with denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The sequences of sgRNA and qPCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1 and 2. The percentage of ssDNA (ssDNA%) at selected sites was determined as previously described75. For each sample, ΔCt was calculated by subtracting the Ct value of the mock-digested sample from the Ct value of the digested sample. ssDNA%, used as a measure for end resection, was calculated using the equation: ssDNA% = 1/(2^(ΔCt-1) + 0.5)*100.

RNA interference and quantitative reverse transcription-PCR

Small interference RNAs (siRNAs) targeting mouse genes and a ‘Scramble’ siRNA as control were purchased from RiboBio Co (Supplementary Table 2). 2.0 × 105 mouse ES cells were transfected with 20 pmol siRNA by Lipofectamine 2000. RNAs were reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme). Quantitative reverse transcriptase (qRT)-PCR was performed on qPCR CFX 96 Thermocycler (Bio-Rad) using gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 2).

Western blot

For western blot, cells were harvested 2 h and 4 h post treatment with chemicals and lysed with RIPA buffer for 30 min. Cell extractions were separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and analyzed by western blot with corresponding antibodies. The following primary antibodies used for immunoblot were anti-α-Tubulin (A6830, 1:1000), anti-RPA32 (A26498, 1:2000), anti-H3 (A22348, 1:2000), anti-Flag (AE005, 1:1000), and anti-Myc-tag (AE070, 1:1000) from ABclonal, anti-HA (#3724, 1:2000) from CST; anti-53BP1 (ab21083, 1:500) and anti-p-RPA32 (ab87277, 1:1000) from Abcam,; anti-BRCA1 and anti-BARD1 antibody was a gift from the L.Y. Lu Lab.
