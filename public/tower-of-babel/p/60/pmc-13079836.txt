Prevalence of excessive screen time and its associated factors among schoolchildren in Damascus, Syria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lana Alshayeb, Mayssoon Dashash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study assessed the prevalence of excessive screen time (≥ 2 h/day) among schoolchildren in Damascus, Syria, and identified associated sociodemographic and device-related factors. A cross-sectional, school-based survey was conducted from 2023 to 2024 using multi-stage cluster sampling to recruit 892 children aged 11–14 from 22 schools across Damascus. Data were collected via anthropometric measurements and structured questionnaires. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression were used to identify predictors of excessive screen time. Overall, 85.1% of children exceeded recommended screen time limits, with a mean daily duration of 3.44 ± 1.60 h. Male participants were nearly four times more likely than females to exceed recommendations (OR = 3.902, 95% CI 2.366–6.436), and obesity was associated with excessive screen use (OR = 2.659, 95% CI 1.457–4.852). Television viewing presented a significant risk (OR = 4.898), while owning a personal device was the strongest predictor (OR = 11.359). Bedtime screen use and prolonged device exposure (≥ 5 years) further increased the risk. These findings highlight a high prevalence of excessive screen time, driven by modifiable behavioral and device-related factors. The results underscore the importance of targeted interventions to foster balanced digital habits and mitigate health risks associated with prolonged screen exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42464-5.

Introduction

Children and adolescents grow up in a society where modern technology is deeply integrated into daily life. From an early age, they are surrounded by various electronic devices, including mobile phones, televisions, and other digital gadgets1,2. Due to rapid technological developments, a wide range of screen-based activities, including social media usage, video streaming, online gaming, and educational applications, have become commonplace in daily routines3,4.

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines screen time (ST) as the amount of time spent sitting in front of a screen without engaging in physical activity5. This includes the use of smartphones or tablets, video gaming on consoles or computers, television viewing, and computer use2,6.

The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) recommends limiting entertainment-related screen time to less than 1–2 h per day for children older than two years and adolescents7. Similarly, several countries, such as Canada, Spain, and Australia, have issued comparable guidelines for screen use within this age group8.

Excessive screen time has emerged as a major public health concern in recent years, particularly among adolescents, largely as a consequence of the COVID-19 pandemic. According to Madigan et al.9, screen time among children and adolescents increased by 52% during the pandemic, primarily due to social isolation and restrictions on outdoor and recreational activities.

The prevalence of excessive screen time varies widely, ranging from 10 to 93.7% in high-income countries and from 21 to 98% in middle-income countries6. Analysis of data from the 2013 International Children’s Accelerometry Database indicated that more than two-thirds of participants spent over two hours per day engaged in screen-based activities10. In addition, a report by Common Sense Media found that children aged 8–12 spend an average of four hours per day on digital media, while adolescents aged 13–18 years spend more than 6.5 h daily3.

Although screen use offers several benefits, including facilitation of learning, access to information, social interaction, and rapid communication4, accumulating evidence suggests that prolonged screen exposure is associated with multiple adverse health and psychological outcomes among infants, children, and adolescents4,11.

Excessive screen time has been linked to poor sleep quality12,13, decreased physical activity1,4, impaired academic performance1,6,14, and mental health issues, including internal conditions such as depression and anxiety, as well as external conditions such as attention -deficit/ hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and behavioral disorders4,9,15.

Moreover, prolonged screen exposure is associated with increased cardiometabolic risks, including obesity, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) dysfunction, and hypertension, which can lead to cardiovascular diseases1,4,16,17.

Additional associations have been reported between excessive screen time and unhealthy dietary behaviors, such as irregular breakfast consumption, increased intake of energy-dense foods, and eating disorders1,2,18. Adverse effects on children’s oral health have also been documented in relation to early and excessive screen use19.

Understanding the prevalence and determinants of excessive screen time is crucial for informing public health interventions and policies that promote healthier digital behaviors among children and adolescents. Screen time is influenced by multiple factors, including socioeconomic status, family environment, peer influence, and individual behavioral characteristics20–23.

Given the significant and widespread effects of screen time on children and adolescents, identifying its correlates is essential for designing effective clinical, educational, and public health interventions aimed at preventing overuse and mitigating adverse health and developmental outcomes, particularly among high-risk groups.

Despite the growing body of international literature on screen time, research examining its prevalence and associated factors among children in the Arab world, especially in Syria, remains scarce. The unique sociopolitical and environmental context in Syria, including prolonged conflict, limited access to safe recreational spaces, and infrastructural challenges, may influence children’s screen use patterns in ways that differ from those reported in other settings. Addressing this gap is critical for informing context-specific and evidence-based public health strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of excessive screen time (≥ 2 h/day) among school-aged children and adolescents in Damascus, Syria, quantify daily screen exposure, and examine its associations with sociodemographic, anthropometric, behavioral, and device-related factors, thereby providing empirical evidence to support targeted preventive and educational interventions tailored to the Syrian context.

Materials and methods

Study design and settings

This cross-sectional, school-based study investigated screen exposure time, excessive screen time (≥ 2 h per day), and its associations with anthropometric nutritional status, lifestyle behaviors, and demographic characteristics among children aged 11 to 14 attending schools in Damascus, Syria. Between 2023 and 2024, a cluster sampling approach was employed to recruit a sample of students from elementary and intermediate schools.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Scientific Research Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, University of Damascus (IRB No. UDDS-2601-09052022/SRC-1550), and written permission was obtained from the Ministry of Education. Informed consent was obtained from the parents prior to data collection. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Population sampling

A multi-stage cluster sampling technique was employed to minimize selection bias. According to the Central Census Office of the Syrian Arab Republic, Damascus is divided into eleven administrative districts. In the first stage, the city was stratified into these districts. In the second stage, two elementary or middle schools were randomly selected from each district, yielding a total of 22 schools. In the final stage, all students enrolled in the selected classes were invited to voluntarily participate in the study. An information letter outlining the study objectives and procedures was distributed to parents through classroom teachers.

The sample size was calculated using the formula: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = \frac{{z^{2} p\left( {1 - p} \right)}}{{d^{2} }}deff$$\end{document}, which is recommended for descriptive cross-sectional studies.

As the prevalence of excessive screen time was unknown, the population proportion (p) was set at 50% to yield the maximum sample size. A standard normal variate (z) of 1.96 was used for a 5% type I error, with the desired margin of error (d) set at 5%. and the design effect (deff) set at 2. To account for potential non-response or dropouts, the calculated sample size was increased by 10%, resulting in a required sample of 846 participants. Ultimately, 892 children were recruited.

Eligibility criteria were based on parental informed consent. Only Syrian boys and girls aged 11–14 years who used digital devices were eligible for inclusion. Children. Children were excluded from the study if parental consent for participation was not provided.

Study protocol and data collection

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered to the children and their parents. The children’s section gathered information on age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and type of school. Parents completed a separate section addressing household socioeconomic characteristics, including parental education, occupation, and number of family members.

The questionnaire also included details regarding screen-based activities, including the type of screen devices used, ownership of personal devices, purposes for screen time, the number of devices available at home, years of usage, screen use at bedtime, sleep duration, and other time-related activities.

A structured questionnaire was developed specifically for this study to assess screen-related behaviors and device usage patterns among Syrian schoolchildren aged 11–14. Face validity was assessed in a pilot sample comprising 10% of participants who were not included in the final analysis. Based on feedback from the pilot testing, minor modifications were made to the questionnaire layout to improve clarity, while the content remained unchanged. Inter-rater reliability was satisfactory, with all items demonstrating Cohen’s kappa coefficients of ≥ 0.70.

Outcomes assessment

Parents’ educational attainment was categorized into three levels: low (primary education or less), moderate (middle or secondary education), and high (university degree or higher). Family size was recorded as the number of individuals living in the same household and was categorized as small, medium, and large based on the distribution of household size within the study population. Obesity was treated as an independent explanatory variable in the analysis to examine its association with excessive screen time.

Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed using principal component analysis based on multiple household-level indicators, including parental occupation, parental education, type of school attended, and the availability of digital devices (computers or tablets) at home. These variables were combined into a single composite index, which was subsequently categorized into tertiles representing low, moderate, and high SES. Father’s occupation was used solely as an input variable for constructing the SES index and was not analyzed as an independent predictor.

Children’s screen time was assessed by asking parents to report the average number of hours their children spent using computers, smartphones, iPads, or other tablets, playing video games, and watching television or movies on weekdays and weekends. Average screen time for a typical weekday was multiplied by five, and for a typical weekend day was multiplied by two to determine total screen time. Each child’s average daily screen time was then determined by adding these figures and dividing by seven. Participants were classified as low screen users (< 2 h/day) or excessive screen users (≥ 2 h/day) based on American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry guidelines7.

Anthropometric measurements, including height and weight, were obtained following the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) protocols. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a wall-mounted stadiometer with participants barefoot. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a digital scale, with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2), and BMI-for-age percentiles were determined using CDC growth charts. Nutritional status was categorized into non-obese and obese groups24.

Indicators of sleep duration were gathered from parents, who were asked how long their child typically slept each night on weekdays and weekends. The weighted mean of weekday and weekend data was used to obtain sleep duration per night. According to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine’s Consensus Statement, children and adolescents aged 6 to 12 who slept at least 9 h per day and adolescents aged 13 to 17 who slept at least 8 h per day were classified as meeting the recommendations of sufficient sleep duration, while those who slept fewer hours per day were classified as having insufficient sleep25.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and indicated non-normal distributions. Accordingly, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two groups, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons among more than two groups. Associations between categorical variables were examined using the Pearson chi-square test. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with excessive screen time. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 892 schoolchildren aged 11–14 years participated in this study, with females comprising approximately 53% of the sample. More than half of the participants were in the younger age group (11–12 years). Regarding nutritional status, the majority of children were classified as non-obese 71.7%.

In terms of family characteristics, most participants belonged to medium-sized households (38.3%). Nearly half of the families (45.1%) were classified as having a moderate socioeconomic status. Prevalence of excessive screen time

The study found that a significant number of participants, 759 individuals (85.1%), were identified as engaging in excessive screen time, with an average daily total screen time of 3.44 ± 1.60 h (Table 1).

Sociodemographic correlates of excessive screen time

Male participants, older children ages 13–14 years, Children who were overweight or obese, children who belong to smaller families, children from higher socioeconomic status households, and children of working mothers exhibited the highest prevalence of excessive screen time, with statistically significant associations (p < 0.001, p = 0.012, p < 0.001, p = 0.020, p < 0.001, p = 0.036 respectively).

These factors were also associated with significantly longer mean daily screen time, except for maternal occupation, which showed no significant difference in mean screen time (p < 0.001), except for maternal occupation, which showed no significant difference in mean screen time (p = 0.740) (Table 1).

In contrast, no significant associations were identified between family structure (p = 0.807, p = 0.188), father’s education (p = 0.083, p = 0.088), or mother’s education (p = 0.496, p = 0.551) and the prevalence of excessive screen time or average total screen time (Table 1).

Device-related factors and screen use patterns

The mean frequency of digital device usage was highest for smartphones (93.4%), followed by television (83.1%). The main purpose for using screen devices among children was gaming (95%), followed by watching videos (81.6%). Nearly 31% of children reported owning a personal device. A large number of children engaged in screen-based activities during bedtime hours (72.6%) (Table 2).

Among all screen devices used by children, 95.5% of those using Tablets, and 92.2% of those using gaming devices reported screen time of ≥ 2 h, with the highest average screen time observed in children using video game devices and consoles.

Device-related factors associated with excessive screen time

Use of televisions, computers, and tablets demonstrated a strong association with excessive screen time (p < 0.001, p = 0.014, p = 0.011, respectively). Furthermore, these variables, along with the use of video game devices, were linked to significant differences in mean total screen time (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Screen-related activities such as gaming, social networking via screen devices, and watching videos were also strongly associated with prolonged screen time (p < 0.001). Additionally, using screens for chatting significantly correlated with increased screen time (p = 0.005) (Table 2).

Moreover, factors such as owning a personal device, an increasing number of available devices in the household, and longer years of use were associated with a higher prevalence of excessive screen time and greater average total screen time (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Children who reported screen use at bedtime demonstrated significantly higher prevalence of excessive screen time and longer mean daily screen duration compared with non-users (p < 0.001). No significant association was observed between sleep duration and excessive screen time and mean daily screen duration (p = 0.217, p = 0.115) (Table 2).

Multivariate analysis of factors associated with excessive screen time

A multiple logistic regression analysis was performed to identify key variables associated with the risk of excessive screen time. The results indicated that seven key factors were significantly associated with excessive screen time, including gender, obesity, television use, ownership of a personal device, engagement in gaming, duration of device usage, and screen use at bedtime (Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides critical insights into the prevalence and factors associated with excessive screen time among children in Damascus, Syria, highlighting both sociodemographic and device-related contributors.

To our knowledge, it is the first study to examine excessive screen time in children within the Arabic world, particularly in Syria. Given that cognitive development in healthy children is nearly complete by the age of six, the age group of 11–14 years was selected for this study, as this period is pivotal for establishing independent care-seeking behaviors, daily habits, and food preferences that influence adult practices26. A cross-sectional design was employed, with a sample of 892 children selected through multi-stage cluster sampling to ensure representation across various socio-economic, demographic, and cultural backgrounds in Damascus. As the capital and largest urban center of Syria, Damascus serves as a socio-demographic hub, hosting populations from across the country, including a significant number of internally displaced persons from conflict-affected areas.

Direct comparisons with studies in other countries pose challenges due to variations in methodological designs, non-representative sampling, measurement approaches, cutoff points, and the range of screen-based devices considered.

The present study revealed a high prevalence of excessive screen time, with 85.1% of children exceeding the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommended limit of less than two hours per day7. This prevalence exceeds that reported in several other countries, including China (42.4%)27, and Brazil (79.5%)28, but is comparable to findings from India, where Garg et al. reported an excessive screen time prevalence of 88.7% among children aged 8–14 years26.

Several factors may explain this high prevalence. Increased accessibility to electronic devices, changes in family routines, and the growing tendency toward simultaneous use of multiple digital platforms have collectively contributed to prolonged screen exposure among children29.

These trends were further amplified during the COVID-19 pandemic, as lockdown measures and school closures led to increased reliance on digital devices for both leisure and educational purposes30.

Beyond the pandemic, context-specific factors unique to the Syrian setting likely play a substantial role in shaping children’s screen use behaviors. Prolonged insecurity, parental concerns regarding children’s personal safety, and the scarcity of safe outdoor recreational spaces significantly limit opportunities for physical and social activities outside the home.

Moreover, recurrent electricity outages may paradoxically intensify screen engagement during periods of power availability, resulting in prolonged and concentrated screen use. Together, these structural and environmental constraints reinforce sedentary behaviors and contribute to the widespread reliance on screen-based activities observed among Syrian children.

The study’s findings reveal significant sociodemographic differences in screen time patterns. Excessive screen use was almost four times more common among boys compared to girls, with boys exhibiting a significantly higher mean daily screen time. These findings are consistent with a study conducted in New Delhi, India31, where boys had 1.36 times the odds of excessive screen use compared to girls, as well as with reports from Malaysia and China demonstrating higher screen time prevalence among males27,32.

In the Syrian context, this disparity may be partially explained by sociocultural and environmental factors. Prolonged insecurity, parental concerns regarding outdoor safety, and limited access to safe recreational spaces have shifted children’s leisure activities toward indoor alternatives. Boys, in particular, may be more likely to engage in screen-based entertainment, especially gaming and other screen-intensive activities33, which serves as a readily accessible and socially accepted substitute for outdoor play. Additionally, boys are more frequently granted access to personal devices and gaming consoles, reflecting gender-based differences in parental supervision and device allocation. Gaming, which is time-intensive and immersive, was the most prevalent screen-related activity in this study and was strongly associated with excessive screen time, further contributing to the observed gender gap.

Conversely, some studies have indicated higher screen time among females34, which is often attributed to cultural norms that encourage girls to spend more time indoors, engaging in studying, household tasks, or social media activities such as chatting or messaging. For example, girls may be more inclined to use screens for social networking, potentially increasing their screen exposure. However, other studies, such as Guedes et al., have found no significant gender differences in screen time29. These inconsistencies may arise from varying definitions of screen time, including the types and number of devices considered.

Older children demonstrated a higher prevalence of excessive screen time and significantly greater mean screen time compared to younger children. This trend may be attributed to increased autonomy, peer influence, and academic demands, which often necessitate greater screen use. Additionally, as they enter adolescence, a developmental stage characterized by changing goals and behaviors, older children are more prone to use screens for entertainment and socialization. In the Syrian context, these factors are further compounded by prolonged school disruptions, limited extracurricular opportunities, and restricted outdoor mobility due to ongoing security concerns. As a result, older children may increasingly rely on screen-based activities for entertainment, social interaction, and coping with environmental stressors. These results align with research undertaken by Guedes et al. in Brazil29, and Jain et al. in India30. However, the multivariate analysis did not find these differences to be statistically significant.

Furthermore, neither family type nor parental education level was statistically associated with excessive screen time in this study. In contrast, previous studies, including one conducted in China, have shown that parental education level is negatively correlated with the prevalence of screen time27, this relationship may be attenuated in the Syrian context. Widespread economic hardship, displacement, and infrastructural challenges may limit parents’ ability to regulate children’s screen use regardless of educational attainment, reducing the protective effect typically attributed to higher parental education.

Children from families with higher socioeconomic status were more likely to engage in excessive screen time and longer average daily screen use. This difference can be attributed to increased access to multiple devices in affluent households, enabling longer screen use, as evidenced by our finding that a greater number of devices and personal device ownership are associated with prolonged screen use. Similar associations have been reported in previous studies28,29, although inconsistencies across regions—such as findings from Southern India reporting no SES-related differences35, underscore the context-dependent nature of digital behaviors, particularly in conflict-affected and resource-constrained settings.

Obesity was significantly associated with excessive screen time in this study (OR = 2.659), with obese children reporting an average daily screen time of 3.94 ± 1.64 h. Consistent with previous meta-analytic research, children who are overweight or obese are more likely than their normal-weight peers to spend more than two hours per day in front of a screen10. These findings align with previous studies showing that screen-related sedentary behaviors frequently replace physical activity and encourage unhealthy snacking, contributing to weight gain and metabolic dysfunction36,37.

While prolonged screen use may contribute to the development of obesity through reduced physical activity and increased caloric intake, it is also possible that children with obesity engage in more screen-based activities due to lower physical activity or social preferences. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, causality cannot be inferred, and the relationship is likely bidirectional. Future longitudinal research is warranted to clarify the temporal direction and mechanisms underlying the association between obesity and excessive screen time.

Smartphones emerged as the most popular digital device among children (93.4%), followed by televisions (83.1%). Similar trends have been reported in other research, emphasizing the increasing prevalence of smartphone usage26. This shift can be attributed to several factors, including affordability, portability, versatile applications, larger screen sizes, and interactive features, particularly amplified during the COVID-19 pandemic. Mobile screen media devices have facilitated solitary viewing, reducing daily screen time associated with traditional media like television26.

The results are consistent with Musa et al. study, which indicates a strong association between personal device ownership and excessive screen time38. Although only 31% of schoolchildren own gadgets, owning a personal device increases the likelihood of engaging in excessive screen time (OR = 11.359). In the Syrian context, personal devices may grant children a high degree of autonomy over their screen activities, as family rules and supervision are harder to enforce when access is unrestricted. This autonomy is especially pronounced in households where parents face economic pressures or psychosocial stressors, limiting their capacity to consistently monitor digital behavior.

The availability of multiple devices was found to contribute to increased screen time significantly. These results align with earlier studies indicating that increased access to devices correlates with more screen-related activities39. In conflict-affected settings such as Syria, digital devices often serve as primary sources of entertainment, education, and social connection, particularly in the absence of safe outdoor environments and structured recreational alternatives. Consequently, increased device availability may unintentionally promote sedentary behaviors and prolonged screen engagement.

The cumulative impact of early and prolonged screen exposure on behavioral patterns was further highlighted by the fact that children with a longer history of device usage (≥ 5 years) were significantly more likely to engage in excessive screen time (OR = 9.022).

Device-specific usage patterns have emerged as significant factors contributing to excessive screen time. The highest rates of extended screen usage were observed among children using tablets (95.5%) and video game devices (92.2%), with the latter group recording the longest average screen time of 4.71 ± 1.59 h. Multiple logistic regression analysis indicated that prolonged television viewing significantly increases the likelihood of exceeding recommended screen time limits (OR = 4.898).

In numerous Syrian households, television is more readily accessible than interactive devices like tablets or smartphones, which amplifies its impact. This trend fosters sedentary behavior, which can extend to other screen-based activities. Additionally, engaging television content, particularly from streaming services, often leads to prolonged viewing times, reducing opportunities for alternative activities and further reinforcing screen dependency.

Moreover, recreational screen activities were more strongly associated with excessive screen time than educational use. Children who used screens for gaming, social networking, or chatting reported higher average screen exposure, with gaming emerging as the most prevalent activity and a strong predictor of excessive screen use (OR = 4.082). To explain the relationship between gaming and screen time, in the Syrian context, gaming provides an accessible form of entertainment and social interaction, especially when outdoor play is limited due to insecurity and lack of safe spaces. The immersive and reward-driven nature of gaming, combined with challenges in parental supervision, contributes to prolonged and excessive screen use.

Bedtime screen usage emerged as a significant indicator of both excessive screen time (OR = 2.348) and prolonged average daily screen exposure. Analysis of overall sleep patterns, however, indicated that total sleep duration was not significantly associated with excessive screen use. This lack of association may reflect variability in children’s sleep schedules, compensatory sleep on weekends, and potential reporting bias in parental recall. In the Syrian context, frequent disruptions due to environmental stressors, household routines, and school schedules may further obscure the relationship between total sleep duration and screen exposure.

These findings are consistent with previous research29,30, which has demonstrated that bedtime screen use can negatively affect sleep quality by delaying sleep onset, causing psychological and physiological stimulation, and reducing melatonin production due to screen brightness, particularly blue light. This disruption can also prolong wakefulness, as children often remain engaged with devices beyond their intended bedtime40.

Thus, bedtime screen use remains an important target for interventions. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine, endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), recommends removing screens from bedrooms and avoiding device use at least 30 min before bedtime25.

The findings of this study underscore the high prevalence of excessive screen use among Syrian adolescents and highlight an urgent need for context-sensitive public health strategies. The strong associations observed with modifiable factors such as personal device ownership, screen use at bedtime, and gaming behaviors indicate that multi-level interventions are both feasible and necessary. At the family level, parental education initiatives should emphasize structured screen time rules, delayed ownership of personal devices, and the establishment of screen-free routines, particularly before bedtime. Schools may play a pivotal role by integrating digital health literacy and screen time awareness into existing health education curricula, thereby fostering balanced and responsible technology use among students.

At the community and policy levels, interventions must account for the unique environmental constraints faced by Syrian children. Ongoing security concerns, limited access to safe outdoor recreational spaces, and recurrent electricity outages necessitate innovative and adaptable approaches. Developing supervised, low-cost indoor or community-based physical activity programs, alongside promoting offline recreational alternatives, may help reduce reliance on screen-based entertainment.

Ongoing security concerns, limited access to safe outdoor recreational spaces, and recurrent electricity outages necessitate innovative and adaptable approaches. Developing supervised, low-cost indoor or community-based physical activity programs, alongside promoting offline recreational alternatives, may help reduce reliance on screen-based entertainment. At the same time, a balanced approach is essential. While several studies have demonstrated the positive role of electronic devices and educational applications in enhancing learning experiences, behavior, academic performance, and overall quality of life41–44, unregulated and prolonged recreational screen use may pose significant health risks. Therefore, public health and education policies should aim to maximize the educational benefits of digital technologies while minimizing excessive and unsupervised screen exposure among children, particularly in resource-limited and post-conflict settings.

The strengths of this study include its theoretical framework, a large sample size that allows for age-stratified analyses, and the evaluation of multiple sociodemographic and behavioral predictors. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design limits causal interpretations, and self-reported measures of screen time, predominantly provided by parents, may introduce reporting bias, potentially leading to underestimation of associations. Additionally, variations in definitions of excessive screen time across studies hinder strict comparisons.

Future research should build upon these findings by employing longitudinal designs and objective measurement tools, such as screen-tracking applications, to strengthen causal interpretation. Targeted intervention studies focusing on high-risk groups identified in this study—particularly boys, children with obesity, and personal device owners—are warranted. Moreover, research in the Syrian context should extend beyond prevalence estimates to explore the psychological, behavioral, and broader health consequences of excessive screen exposure, including sleep quality, cardiometabolic health, oral health outcomes, and mental well-being. Incorporating validated mental health assessments, including measures of stress-related and post-traumatic symptoms, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of screen use in conflict-affected populations and inform the development of culturally appropriate and sustainable interventions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a high prevalence of excessive screen time among children in Damascus and identifies significant associations between excessive screen use and multiple sociodemographic, behavioral, and device-related factors. Key determinants included male sex, obesity, personal device ownership, longer duration of device use, television viewing, and engagement in screen-based activities such as gaming. These findings highlight the importance of targeted public health interventions addressing these modifiable risk factors to mitigate the potential adverse health and developmental consequences of excessive screen exposure among children.
