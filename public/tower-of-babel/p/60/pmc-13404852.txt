What are the current consent processes for adolescent vaccination in Canada? An environmental scan of practices, challenges and alternative approaches
Authors: Umar Yunusa, Addisu J Zeleke, Laura Reifferscheid, Thilina Bandara, Shannon E MacDonald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesConsent processes for adolescent vaccination in Canada have been identified as potential barriers to vaccine uptake. This study examined current practices for obtaining consent for adolescent vaccines across Canadian jurisdictions to better understand the variation, complexity and opportunities for improvement.DesignAn environmental scan.SettingThe study was conducted between 25 March 2025 and 27 February 2026, across public health systems in Canada. Data were drawn from relevant written documents and from key informants (KIs) representing public health, vaccine administration and immunisation policy in each province and territory.ParticipantsWe included 76 written records and input from 17 KIs.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was adolescent vaccination consent process, including the age of majority, legal age of consent for vaccination and consent modalities. The secondary outcomes included challenges associated with the existing consent processes and proposed recommendations for improvements.ResultsThe age of majority was 18 or 19 years in all jurisdictions. The legal age at which adolescents could consent to vaccination ranged from 13 to 16+ years in some jurisdictions, while others did not specify a formal age threshold. Paper-based consent remained the predominant modality, although implied, verbal (in-person) and telephone consent were also used. Electronic consent (e-consent) was being piloted in some jurisdictions. General challenges include logistical burden of paper-based processes and language barriers, whereas adolescent-specific challenges include inconsistent assessment of adolescent capacity and parental concerns about reduced authority. Suggested improvements included simplifying and standardising consent processes, expanding opportunities for adolescent self-consent and integrating e-consents alongside existing modes.ConclusionsDespite considerable variation, there is broad recognition of the need to improve the consent process for adolescent vaccination. Simplifying consent processes may promote more timely and equitable access to immunisation and support efforts to improve adolescent vaccination coverage in Canada.

Introduction

The WHO has defined adolescence as the phase of life between childhood and adulthood, specifically from ages 10 to 19. It is a unique stage of human development when the adolescents experience rapid physical, cognitive and psychosocial growth.1 The number of vaccines for the adolescent population is gradually increasing globally2 3 with the aim of improving the health of adolescents and protecting them against infectious diseases.2 In Canada, adolescents are recommended to receive vaccines against diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis (in combination as DTaP), hepatitis B, human papillomavirus (HPV) and quadrivalent conjugate meningococcal vaccines (MenC-ACYW-135), as well as seasonal vaccines against influenza and COVID-19.2 4 However, among these vaccines, only the DTaP booster has met the 2025 national coverage goal of 90% for adolescent vaccines.5 6

Vaccines provide the greatest protection against vaccine-preventable diseases.7 8 Their administration may, however, be associated with potential risks to a very small proportion of recipients.9 While most of the risks/side effects are mild, for example fever, headache, muscle ache, loss of appetite and feeling tired, more serious reactions such as seizures, swelling of the arm/leg and other severe reactions may occur.9 10 A valid consent is therefore required before administering any vaccine, to prepare the recipient to make an informed decision about vaccine acceptance. For a consent to be valid, the person consenting must have capacity to consent, the consent must be given voluntarily and it must be specific and informed.11 12 Having the capacity to consent entails that the person consenting understands the possible benefits as well as potential risks of the decision.11 Apart from being a professional responsibility of the healthcare provider, obtaining an informed consent legally protects both the vaccine provider and vaccine recipient.13 14

Parents/guardians are usually required to provide this consent on behalf of their children/wards, with the exception of adolescent children deemed to be mature minors.15 16 A mature minor has been defined as an individual 18 years of age or less who is capable of providing informed consent or refusal for their own healthcare.12 16 17 Under the mature minor doctrine, when a minor has the maturity and capacity to make their own decision (‘Gillick Competent’), the minor’s consent is acceptable and the parent’s is not required.18 When it comes to practical application of consent in adolescent vaccination, the concept of mature minor varies in different contexts across the world.19 For example, in Ireland, the Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Spain and the UK, 16 years is the minimum age at which children can consent to medical treatments without parental approval. However, in other countries, such as Bulgaria, Finland, France, Greece, Hungary, Italy, Malta, Romania and Slovakia, minors do not have the legal authority to make autonomous decisions about their medical treatments until they reach the age of majority.11 15 20–22

In Canada, consent practices for adolescent vaccination vary across provinces and territories, unlike in some countries with nationally standardised age-based consent laws.2 3 15 20 22–24 Given the realities and different interpretations of the consent guidelines for adolescents and how it may act as a potential barrier to vaccine uptake,3 25 it is important to understand how parents, adolescents and immunisation providers are engaged in the vaccination consent process across the country. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that current consent approaches may present challenges to achieving optimal adolescent vaccination coverage.2 23 26–28 We therefore examined the consent processes for adolescent vaccination used across the 13 provinces/territories of Canada and explored the alternatives to improve consent approaches. This is with a view to support policy makers and other stakeholders by providing empirical data for evidence-informed decisions regarding future adolescent vaccination consent processes.

Operational definition of terms

Adolescent

For the purpose of this study, we will consider adolescents as people who are between 10 and 17 years. Although the WHO defines adolescence as 10–19 years, individuals aged 18 years and older are legally recognised as adults in Canada (with the age of majority being 18 in most provinces and 19 in others) and are able to provide independent consent for healthcare without parental involvement.

Consent process

Consent processes are the series of activities carried out by vaccine providers through using a medium such as a paper-based form, online form, verbal (in-person), phone call or electronic platform to obtain valid approval from a parent/guardian and/or an adolescent before a vaccine is administered to an adolescent.

Step 5: KI consultations

We identified experts in public health, vaccine administration, and immunisation policy from each province/territory. Recruitment was purposive and drew on the professional networks of the research team, supplemented by snowball sampling through referrals from initial contacts. Each KI received a tailored email summarising jurisdiction-specific evidence that was found through our search of literature and was asked to validate the retrieved information and respond to structured, open-ended questions regarding current consent processes, challenges, future plans or opportunities to improve consent in their province or territory. Validation of findings by the KIs included confirming whether documented policies reflected actual/current processes, identifying implementation gaps, clarifying ambiguous consent policies, updating outdated information, and explaining contextual differences across jurisdictions. KIs were given the option to respond via email or to request a virtual meeting to elaborate on their answers. They were also invited to refer the research team to a more suitable contact if they felt unable to address the questions adequately. These activities were conducted between 29 July and 5 December 2025. From the additional information provided by the KIs, relevant data that aligned with the study objectives were extracted according to the predefined items in the structured data extraction sheet developed for the written source of evidence. During the validation process, some KIs also provided additional records, including updated practice guidelines and immunisation manuals. As these documents were obtained directly from experts, they were included without undergoing the initial screening process.

Step 6: evidence synthesis

Key findings from both the written sources of evidence and KIs were integrated and presented thematically, guided by the study objectives and emerging themes. Integration of findings involved comparing data from written sources and KIs inputs to identify areas of convergence, complementary insights and divergence. In cases of conflicting information across sources, the most recent legislation, clinical practice guidelines and immunisation manuals were prioritised. These documents represent the current authoritative standards governing practice within their respective jurisdictions and are typically informed by evidence syntheses, expert consensus and regulatory requirements.34 35 Consequently, information from current guidelines and practice documents that was validated by KIs superseded conflicting information obtained from peer-reviewed literature or other sources. On completion of the evidence synthesis, the results were shared with respective KIs to confirm that they accurately reflected the consent processes and on-the-ground realities within their province or territory. This validation process took place between 9 February and 27 February 2026.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study. The study involved consultations with KIs who were experts in immunisation and public health practice.

Consent process for adolescent vaccination

Age of majority

The age of majority is the legally recognised age at which an individual is no longer considered a minor and is granted full legal rights and responsibilities, including the ability to make decisions without parental or guardian consent. In Canada, the age of majority varies by province and territory, ranging from 18 (AB, SK, MB, ON, QC and PEI) to 19 (BC, NB, NS, NL, YT, NWT and NU) years.17 36–39 The summary of the age of majority and other key results is presented in online supplemental table 2.

Legal age to provide consent for vaccination

The legal age at which adolescents can provide consent for vaccination varies across Canada. Some provinces and territories specify a minimum age threshold (SK, MB, QC, NB, NL, NU); others do not have a formal age (figure 2).40–43 However, these jurisdictions may still recognise the capacity of adolescents of certain ages to consent based on maturity. For example, in YT, ‘13–14 years is considered the age where youths may be mature enough to make their own medical decisions’. In BC, parental consent is generally sought for those 12 years of age and younger to be immunised, though a child of this age may provide their own consent in exceptional circumstances. In Toronto (ON), students 14 years and older can provide consent to receive vaccines at Public Health Clinics held in secondary schools.44

Mature minor doctrine

Despite the variations in the legal age at which adolescents can provide consent to vaccination, the mature minor doctrine is recognised in most of the provinces and territories (n=11, 84.6%), with notable exception in QC. Although the term is not used in ON, minors do have the ability to consent under the Health Care Consent Act as there is no age cut-off provided.40 The mature minor doctrine allows an adolescent under the age of majority (18–19 years) to independently provide consent if they are assessed to have the capacity. Healthcare providers are responsible for determining the capacity of adolescents who are below the legal/recognised age of consent, or in jurisdictions without a formal age threshold.45 Where an adolescent is judged capable, their decisions must be respected and should not be overridden, including by parents.46–48

Modes of consent by parents

Different modes of consent are in use across the provinces and territories in Canada. These processes include paper-based (written), verbal (in-person), telephone, electronic consent (e-consent) and implied (in-person) (figure 3).49–52

Paper-based (written) consent

Paper-based consent remains the predominant method of obtaining consent for adolescent vaccination in all the 13 provinces and territories in Canada. It is typically the first and primary approach used to obtain consent before vaccinating an adolescent, especially when a parent or guardian is not present at the immunisation appointment.46 53 54 In school-based immunisation programmes, which is the most common setting for adolescent vaccination, consent forms are distributed to students at school and sent home to parents/guardians.46 48 55 56 These forms are typically part of an information package that includes: a letter to parents/guardians, a consent form to be signed and returned, and factsheets on the vaccines being offered.46 57–59

Verbal consent (in person)

Several provinces and territories, including BC, AB, SK, MB, ON, QC, NB and NWT accept verbal (in-person) consent for vaccination.53 56 60–62 Verbal consent is typically employed when a parent or guardian is physically present at the immunisation appointment or in situations where a written consent form has not been returned.53 Although verbal consent is accepted in these jurisdictions, it must be documented by the healthcare provider to ensure legal compliance and maintain a record of authorisation.58

Telephone consent

Telephone consent is commonly used as an alternative when signed consent forms are not returned, a parent or guardian is not physically present to give verbal consent, or when an adolescent is deemed unable to provide self-consent. In these circumstances, the healthcare provider contacts the parents or legal guardian to obtain verbal consent via the telephone.62 This approach is accepted in BC, AB, SK, MB, QC, NS, NWT, NU and YT.48 60 62

Electronic consent

E-consent is the process of giving consent for vaccination using electronic or digital tools such as website, app or email instead of paper-based forms or verbal processes. The processes have recently been piloted in select regions across Canada. For example, NS and NB began implementing e-consent options in 2022/2023 for school-based immunisation programmes.50 Additionally, digital online platforms are reported to be used for collecting vaccination consent in certain regions of BC and ON.

Implied (in-person) consent

Implied consent occurs when the physical presence of an adolescent or their parent/guardian is interpreted as consent, without the need for signing a form. In some clinical settings, SK and NB recognise implied consent when a capable adolescent or parent/guardian presents for a vaccination appointment.60 63

Modes of consent by adolescent

Although parental consent remains the predominant approach for adolescent vaccination across Canada, several jurisdictions permit adolescents to provide their own consent under specific circumstances. Adolescents may self-consent when they present for immunisation without a signed consent form, when they wish to make a decision that differs from that of their parent/guardian, or when a parent or guardian cannot be reached to provide verbal consent. In all cases, the healthcare provider must determine whether the adolescent has the capacity to provide informed consent.

The extent of adolescent involvement in the consent process varies across jurisdictions. Practices range from adolescents co-signing consent forms with parents/guardians to independently signing consent forms or providing verbal consent. For example, some school-based consent forms in ON require student signatures in addition to parental consent. In QC, adolescents 14 years and older may provide verbal consent, and in SK, adolescents are required to sign their own consent forms.56 In YT, capable minors may consent independently, and once consent is provided, they are recognised as the sole decision-makers.64 KIs noted increasing recognition of adolescent self-consent, with verbal (in-person) consent preferred in situations of parental disagreement or unavailability.

Variations in consent processes within provinces/territories

Beyond provincial and territorial differences, additional variation in adolescent vaccination consent process exists at the subprovincial level of local health units, districts or authorities, as reported in BC, AB, ON, NB and NWT. Differences may include the age at which adolescents may consent, the modes of administration of consent and whether adolescent self-consent is permitted. For instance, in AB, the mature minor doctrine is recognised but primarily applied in public health centres rather than in school settings. Although there is no formal age threshold in ON, some local policies allow students aged 14 years and older to self-consent at secondary school clinics.44 Despite this variability, harmonising efforts are evident within some provinces/territories. For example, in the NWT, regional authorities operate under a memorandum of understanding to align consent policies with territorial standards.

Duration of consent

The duration for which consent remains valid for adolescent vaccination varies across provinces and territories. In some jurisdictions, consent remains valid for the entire vaccine series (AB, SK, NS, PEI, YT, NWT and NU),48 or by time (2 years within school-based clinics in BC and one school year in NB). In MB, consent is normally valid for an entire series of vaccine for no more than 1 year after it is given, except for one-time immunisations. In QC, when consent is obtained at the initiation of a multidose vaccination series, administered at specified intervals, it remains valid for the entire series if full information is provided; however, the period of consent cannot exceed 24 months and can be withdrawn at any time. In NL, consent is valid for 1 year except for primary series consent which is valid for 2 years.65 In ON, consents are valid for up to 2 years or for an entire vaccine series depending on the specific consent being provided.44 Regardless of the duration, consent may be revoked at any time if withdrawn by the parent or adolescent, or if there is a change in the vaccine product or in the client’s medical status.

Upcoming changes in policy or practice

Study findings indicate that some provinces/territories are reviewing and updating their current consent practices. In NWT, the regional consent Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) is being consolidated into a single territorial immunisation programme SOP, which is expected to standardise consent process across the territory. PEI plans to offer school-based catch-up vaccination for graduating students, enabling adolescent self-consent without prior parental approval. YT has recently updated the informed consent section of its Immunisation Programme Manual,64 while some regional health authorities in BC have started implementing hybrid digital-paper consent.

Challenges associated with current consent process

Logistical burden of paper-based consents

The distribution, return and processing of paper-based consent forms in school-based immunisation programmes create a substantial logistical burden.49 Delays in sending forms home, short response timelines and non-return of forms (sometimes interpreted as refusal) can impede timely consent.49 55 66 Limited coordination between schools and public health nurses, student absenteeism and high nursing workloads further challenge form collection prior to vaccination appointments.49 55 67 KIs also identified frequent issues such as missing information, documentation and transcription errors during data entry, difficulties recording consent withdrawals and duplicate forms from separated parents. Another logistical burden reported was the additional requirement for public health nurses to contact parents by phone to obtain consent when forms are missing or incomplete.

Technological challenges associated with e-consent

Although e-consent is being implemented in some provinces as an alternative to the paper-based consent, it presents unique challenges. Its effectiveness varies across regions, with limited internet access in remote areas and lack of familiarity with digital platforms creating barriers. Gallant et al68 noted that e-consent may exacerbate inequities, as families without reliable internet may be unable to provide consent. Other practical challenges include e-consent notifications being overlooked, forgotten or filtered as junk, or parents forgetting to complete or submit forms.49 69 Duplicate submissions can also occur, either because a parent forgets they have already completed a form or cannot update information on an existing consent, leading them to fill out another.

Conflicting consent policies and communication gaps

Variations in consent policies across public health units and for school-based immunisation programmes have been reported in some provinces/territories. This variation may influence how consent is implemented within the same jurisdiction. For instance, in ON, some school policies require parental consent for vaccination in school settings, regardless of adolescent’s ability to consent, creating conflicts between institutional requirements and provincial/local consent frameworks. Healthcare providers expressed concerns about potential legal consequences if parental consent was not obtained or if an adolescent’s decision conflicted with parental wishes.

Language and health literacy barriers

Evidence suggests that some parents experience difficulty in reading or understanding the consent forms due to their language, length and the use of scientific and technical jargon in presenting immunisation information.49 55 69 This challenge was particularly noted among families for whom English or French is not a first language, and may hinder parents’ or guardians’ comprehension and timely completion of consent forms. In addition, language barriers may also extend to adolescents, with reports indicating communication challenges between students and public health nurses in some settings.56

Parental concerns about reduced authority

Some parents perceived the mature minor process as undermining their authority, despite its intent to protect youths’ autonomy. In several provinces and territories, the law prioritises the decision of a mature minor over that of a parent or guardian, allowing adolescents to override parental wishes, including refusals of vaccination.45 52 58 Parents and guardians expressed concern about this right to override their medical decisions. In AB, some parents specifically reported fears that signing consent for a non-COVID-19 vaccine might result in their child being vaccinated against COVID-19 without their approval.

Inconsistent assessment of adolescent consent capacity

Inconsistencies have been reported in how healthcare providers assess adolescents’ capacity to act as mature minors when consenting to vaccination.53 70 This may result in variability in determining whether an adolescent was capable of providing informed consent.48 Even where case-by-case assessments are used, providers reported difficulty making definitive judgements, noting that some 13 and 14 years old may demonstrate sufficient maturity to consent independently.36 In certain instances, court involvement has been required to establish an adolescent’s capacity, further complicating the process.36 71 These challenges have contributed to provider reluctance to accept adolescent consent in the absence of parental involvement, as reported in ON.72

Jurisdictional efforts to improve consent process

Several provinces and territories have implemented or are exploring strategies to address challenges associated with adolescent vaccination consent processes, as reported by KIs. Collectively, these initiatives focus on reducing administrative burden, enhancing consistency, improving accessibility and facilitating informed decision-making across jurisdictions. A key area of innovation has been the transition towards e-consent to address the logistical limitations of paper-based consent processes. In NB, a postcard containing a QR code linked directly to an e-consent platform and student preparation information has been introduced, with plans for supplementary email reminders and social media outreach. Notably, paper-based consent options continue to be offered alongside e-consent to promote accessibility and accommodate varying levels of digital access. Similarly, the NWT is harmonising regional consent procedures into a single territorial immunisation consent SOP, with the goal of improving consistency and standardising consent practices.

Efforts to improve accessibility to information have also focused on addressing language barriers. For example, Manitoba has translated Grade six immunisation factsheets into multiple Indigenous and non-Indigenous languages, with additional translations underway, thereby helping ensure that vaccine information is accessible to parents and guardians from diverse linguistic backgrounds.73 Other jurisdictions are focusing on clarifying legal and operational aspects of the consent process. These include efforts to strengthen guidance on mature minor consent and substitute decision-makers to reduce uncertainty among healthcare providers, schools and families, particularly in school-based immunisation programmes. In PEI, planned implementation of catch-up vaccination opportunities for high school students prior to graduation, particularly for HPV vaccination, may further enhance access by enabling eligible adolescents to provide self-consent where appropriate.

Discussion

This study highlights the variable landscape of the consent process for adolescent vaccination across the provinces and territories in Canada. Findings indicate that adolescents can independently consent to vaccination once they reach the legal or recognised age for consent in their province or territory. For adolescents who are younger than the recognised age for consent, healthcare providers are responsible for assessing their capacity to consent under the mature minor doctrine. If deemed capable, their consent must always be respected. In line with the mature minor doctrine applied across many jurisdictions in Canada, when a minor has the maturity and capacity to make their own decision (‘Gillick Competent’), the minor’s consent is required, not the parents/guardians.18 This finding is consistent with consent laws and practices in countries such as the UK and Australia, where mature minors who demonstrate sufficient decision-making capacity may independently consent to healthcare interventions, including vaccination.74 75

While the mature minor doctrine allows adolescents judged to have the capacity to provide self-consent for their own vaccination, healthcare professionals bear primary responsibility for assessing adolescent capacity and obtaining valid consent.3 However, studies consistently report variability, uncertainty and limited confidence among providers in applying mature minor principles in vaccination settings.76 77 In ON, some providers reportedly prioritise chronological age over maturity when deciding whether to accept consent from adolescent girls.72 A key challenge identified in this study is the lack of a standardised tool to assess adolescents’ capacity to consent across most Canadian jurisdictions.53 70 In the absence of such a tool, assessments of maturity and decision-making capacity are subject to variability, potentially resulting in inconsistent determinations; an adolescent deemed a mature minor by one healthcare provider may be judged incapable by another under similar circumstances.48 This highlights the need for a standardised, evidence-informed assessment tool to support equitable and defensible consent practices across Canada. While standardisation could reduce variability and complexity, it may also introduce challenges. For example, in ON, consent processes for school-based immunisation programmes are tailored to local contexts, limiting the feasibility of province-wide or national approaches.

Expanding self-consent opportunities for adolescents has also been met with criticism. Some parents and advocacy groups argue that the mature minor doctrine undermines parental authority and threatens the integrity of the family unit.78 Others contend that vaccination is too important a health decision to allow minors to make without parental guidance.78 Evidence further suggests that promoting adolescent self-consent may create discordance between adolescents’ and parents’ vaccination preferences,3 76 placing healthcare providers in situations that require balancing adolescent autonomy, parental involvement and public health interests. To address these challenges, the Government of Canada has recommended additional training for healthcare providers on seeking informed consent and assessing adolescent capacity.79 It has also suggested legal support to healthcare providers who accept consent from adolescents deemed mature minors and who wish to be immunised without parental involvement. Overall, any proposed changes to adolescent self-consent must carefully consider issues of comprehension, accessibility, feasibility and adaptability to local contexts.

Our study identified paper-based, verbal (in-person), telephone, electronic and implied (in-person) consents as the primary modes used for adolescent vaccination. These modes apply to both parental consents and adolescent self-consent, but their use varies across provinces, territories and clinical contexts. For example, in some jurisdictions, verbal consent is standard when parents are present or adolescents have reached the legal age to consent.53 Our findings demonstrate substantial variation in how consent requirements are operationalised across Canadian jurisdictions. Differences in the acceptance of verbal, telephone, electronic and written consent reflect varying legal interpretations, administrative procedures and approaches to risk management.45 58 60 While these jurisdiction-specific adaptations may address local needs, they can also introduce complexity and inconsistency into the consent process. For families, differing requirements may create confusion regarding consent expectations, particularly when moving between jurisdictions. For healthcare providers, additional documentation, witnessing or verification requirements may increase administrative burden and reduce efficiency.69 80 81 The continued reliance on paper-based processes in some settings, despite the availability of electronic platforms, further illustrates the challenges of modernising consent systems while maintaining legal and ethical safeguards. Greater consistency in consent procedures, while preserving flexibility for local contexts, may reduce uncertainty, streamline administration and improve equitable access to adolescent immunisation services.45

Similar to other countries, we found that use of e-consent is increasing in Canada.82 83 However, as one KI noted, ‘an electronic option could be inadvertently adding more steps and/or barriers to the informed consent process, but may provide more time for adolescents to review and process information about immunisations in advance of the school immunisation clinic’. This highlights the need to balance efficiency with accessibility when implementing e-consent. Offering multiple consent options may help address these challenges by accommodating diverse preferences and circumstances. Regardless of the mode of consent used and who provides it, access to clear and accessible information on vaccine safety and efficacy is essential for informed decision making and has been associated with greater vaccination intentions and uptake. It enhances understanding among adolescents and parents and is consistently associated with increased vaccination intentions and improved uptake.84 Simplifying consent processes may therefore involve expanding verbal, telephone and e-consent options and reducing reliance on paper-based forms through secure digital platforms and direct communication with parents and guardians. Overall, the main challenge is not the absence of consent mechanisms, but variability in how they are applied across jurisdictions.

Recommended strategies to improve consent processes

Simplifying and standardising the consent process

Our findings indicate substantial variability in consent materials and communication approaches across jurisdictions, suggesting there might be value in simplifying and standardising consent processes. Standardising the content and format of consent forms across Canada, using plain language and multiple languages, has been proposed to support informed decision-making and equity.72–74 Consent materials could be co-developed with schools and parents, translated where appropriate and revised to ensure clarity and accessibility.45 56 66 67 Additional strategies could include short multilingual explanatory videos and making nurses available in schools to address questions directly.

Integrating electronic consent into existing modes

Expansion of e-consent systems might help address the logistical challenges associated with the paper-based consent processes. KIs reported paper-based forms to be prone to incomplete information and require follow-up, whereas e-consent platforms can reduce errors through structured mandatory fields. A dual-system approach, maintaining paper-based options alongside e-consent, could ensure accessibility. As e-consent expands, standardised guidance will be important to ensure consistency in documentation and implementation. Linking e-consent systems with provincial immunisation registries and enabling real-time data transfer has been proposed to further streamline workflows and improve efficiency.82 83

Reminders

Incomplete return of consent forms was a commonly identified operational challenge, indicating the need for systematic reminder strategies. These reminders may be delivered through online platforms, email, social media or mailed postcards to address forgotten or unreturned forms.50 74 It has also been suggested that consent forms be reviewed in advance of clinic days to identify contraindications, clarify incomplete information and verify consent when necessary.

Improving communication between school/parents/healthcare providers

Our findings highlight communication gaps between schools, families and healthcare providers as a key challenge in the consent process. Strengthening coordination through early distribution of consent forms and advance scheduling of vaccination clinics may provide families with adequate time to make informed decisions.49 67 Clear and accessible communication is also essential, including materials that explain mature minor consent and its role in supporting capable adolescents rather than overriding parental authority.74 Increased engagement between public health teams and schools can further streamline communication, including direct contact with parents through email reminders and follow-up. Teachers may also play a supportive role by educating students, assisting with consent distribution and collection and helping manage student anxiety, thereby improving workflow efficiency for public health nurses and increasing consent return rates.55

Expanding opportunities for adolescent self-consent

Variation in the enactment of mature minor consent across jurisdictions suggests a need for more consistent implementation of adolescent self-consent processes where capacity is demonstrated. Allowing adolescents assessed as mature minors to provide consent at the point of vaccination supported by appropriate documentation may improve access and timeliness.74 75

However, concerns regarding parental authority and provider confidence remain barriers.78 These may be addressed through targeted training on capacity assessment, public education on the mature minor framework and digital consent systems that support documentation, confidentiality and informed decision-making.79

Standardising the process of assessing adolescents’ consent capacity

Inconsistencies in how adolescent capacity is assessed by healthcare providers across jurisdictions underscore the need for standardised tools and guidance. Developing structured assessment frameworks may reduce variability in practice, improve fairness and strengthen provider confidence in determining capacity to consent.48 75 Such tools could reduce inter-provider variability, enhance fairness and improve confidence and consistency in decision-making across settings and jurisdictions.

Clear policies and legal frameworks

Our findings indicate ongoing jurisdictional variation in consent legislation and interpretation of the mature minor doctrine, highlighting the need for clearer and more consistently operationalised legal frameworks. Explicit guidance on age thresholds and decision-making authority may reduce uncertainty in practice. In situations involving shared decision-making authority or disagreement regarding immunisation, vaccination should be deferred and appropriate legal or administrative guidance sought.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this environmental scan is its use of both passive (written sources of evidence) and active (KI consultation) data collection, enabling a comprehensive and triangulated understanding of consent processes. The inclusion of evidence from all 13 Canadian provinces and territories further strengthens the study. Although validation of written sources through KI consultations was achieved for 12 jurisdictions within the study timeframe, information from the remaining province was captured only via written evidence. Additionally, this study uniquely examined multiple interrelated dimensions of the consent landscape, including age of majority, age of consent, mature minor doctrine, validity of consent, recent policy changes and modes of consent. This broad scope enhances the overall understanding of how adolescent vaccination processes are structured and implemented across Canada.

In some instances, discrepancies were identified across sources regarding the legal age at which adolescents may provide consent. In such cases, the most recent clinical practice guideline, legislation and immunisation manuals obtained from government/health authority websites and validated by KIs were considered authoritative and superseded information from peer-reviewed literature or other sources. For example, the peer-reviewed article by Coughlin85 reported the age of consent in SK as 16 years and older, whereas information obtained from the Saskatchewan Immunisation Manual and the 2025–2026 Provincial School Immunisation Policy indicates that children 13 years and older can legally provide consent63 86; therefore, the latter was treated as authoritative. Although the initial recruitment of KIs was done through the research team’s professional networks, this potential limitation was mitigated by asking the KIs to refer the team to more suitable experts if they felt unable to adequately address the questions. Due to the focus of the study and the environmental scan methodology, a substantial proportion of the written sources of evidence consisted of grey literature, including government reports, practice manuals and clinical guidelines. Thus, quality appraisal of these sources was not a relevant step; instead, it was most relevant to prioritise the most recent and updated documents and validate the information by KIs.

Conclusions

This environmental scan highlights a complex and highly variable landscape for obtaining consent for adolescent vaccination across Canada’s provinces and territories. Differences were noted in the recognised age of majority, legal age of consent, application of mature minor doctrine and modes of consent used. Despite jurisdictional variations, major challenges such as the logistical burden of paper-based consent processes and language barriers are common across regions. Importantly, there is a broad recognition of the need to improve and modernise consent practices. Key priorities include simplifying and, where feasible, standardising consent processes, integrating e-consent systems and enabling capable adolescents to self-consent. Regardless of who provides the consent, supporting informed decision-making remains key. Strengthening consent processes may reduce administrative burden, improve consistency in implementation and enhance healthcare providers’ legal and professional confidence while promoting timely and equitable access to immunisation for adolescents. Future research and policy efforts should evaluate the impact of modernised and more standardised consent practices on uptake-related outcomes, while continuing to protect adolescent autonomy.
