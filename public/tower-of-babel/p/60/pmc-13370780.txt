Degradable Cationic Polyesters with Tunable Anion-Induced Upper Critical Solution Temperature Coacervation
Authors: Maria Kaliva, Maria Karouzou, Maria Vamvakaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Herein, we report degradable cationic polyesters combining pH responsiveness with tunable, anion-induced UCST phase behavior in aqueous media under mildly acidic and physiologically relevant pH conditions, accompanied by reversible self-coacervation. These main-chain polyesters feature ionizable amine side groups paired with Cl– (PE-Cys-Cl) or BF4 – (PE-Cys-BF4) counterions, enabling UCST transitions triggered by NaCl, NaBF4, and notably by the pharmaceutically relevant anion salicylate (SAL). Cloud point temperature was tuned over a broad range of 8–91 °C by controlling anion type, pH, polymer concentration, and the anion-to-cation repeat unit ratio (A/CRU). Mechanistic studies revealed liquid–liquid phase separation into dynamic, water-rich coacervate droplets. Importantly, the hydrolytic degradation was strongly counterion-dependent, with PE-Cys-Cl exhibiting substantial degradation under physiological conditions compared to low-molecular-weight PLLA, whereas the more hydrophobic PE-Cys-BF4 polymer remained largely unchanged. These materials provide a versatile proof-of-concept platform for anion-triggered UCST phase separation, opening opportunities in responsive coacervation, anion sensing, and drug delivery related applications.

Introduction

Stimuli-responsive polymers, often referred to as “smart” materials, undergo abrupt and reversible property changes in response to external stimuli and are widely explored for applications in biomaterials, catalysis, drug delivery, sensors, and actuators. − Among these materials, thermoresponsive polymers are particularly attractive, as temperature represents a noninvasive and precisely tunable trigger. , Notably, multiresponsive polymer systems that integrate temperature, pH, and ionic-strength cues have emerged as powerful platforms for achieving enhanced control in complex environments.

Thermoresponsive polymers typically exhibit either a lower critical solution temperature (LCST) or an upper critical solution temperature (UCST), corresponding to phase separation upon heating or cooling, respectively. ,, While LCST-type polymers, most notably poly­(N-isopropylacrylamide), have been extensively studied, − UCST-type transitions in aqueous media remain comparatively rare, despite their considerable potential for biomedical applications. To date, UCST behavior has been reported in various solvent systems, including water/organic solvent mixtures, particularly water/alcohol mixtures, as well as in protic organic solvents such as alcohols, , with few examples in purely aqueous media. Aqueous UCST behavior has been demonstrated in nonionic systems, including poly­(N-acryloyl glycinamide), − ureido-derivatized polymers, and acrylamide-based copolymers, as well as in zwitterionic polymers, where UCST transitions are driven by Coulombic interactions. −

Cationic polymers, including polyelectrolytes and polymeric ionic liquids, may also exhibit UCST behavior, driven by ionic interactions, particularly in the presence of hydrophobic counterions − or multivalent ions. Certain polycations display dual responsiveness to both salt concentration and temperature, becoming turbid upon salt addition and redissolving upon heating. ,,, Given their relevance in gene and drug delivery, tissue engineering, − antimicrobial materials ,, and biosensing , polycations exhibiting UCST transitions represent a particularly promising yet underexplored class of thermoresponsive polymers.

Beyond classical coil–globule transitions, some thermoresponsive polymers undergo self-coacervation, forming liquid, polymer-rich droplets rather than collapsed chains. Above the LCST or below the UCST, partial dehydration drives liquid–liquid phase separation into coacervate droplets dispersed in a dilute phase. , Unlike coil–globule transitions, coacervation involves minimal conformational rearrangement, preserving molecular integrity, enabling efficient encapsulation of sensitive bioactive compounds. − UCST-type coacervation has been reported for nonionic , and for ionic polymers bearing guanidinium groups, whereas analogous behavior in ammonium-functionalized polymers has not yet been reported. Despite their promise, thermoresponsive self-coacervates remain far less explored than LCST systems or complex coacervates, although growing interest is being driven by their biomimetic phase behavior and biomedical potential. ,

Most thermoresponsive polymers, including coacervate-forming systems, are derived from nondegradable vinyl-based materials, which limits their biomedical translation and long-term sustainability. In contrast, biodegradable polyesters such as polylactide and polycaprolactone offer more sustainable alternatives, − but their crystallinity and limited chemical functionality restrict structural and responsive tunability. Incorporation of functional pendant groups into polyester backbones can overcome these limitations, as demonstrated by recent pH-responsive degradable polyesters bearing alkene and carboxylate moieties. Nevertheless, degradable UCST polymers remain rare. Wolf et al. reported carboxylic acid-functionalized poly­(phosphonate)­s synthesized via a multistep process, exhibiting UCST behavior only under acidic conditions. Similarly, uracil-functionalized polycaprolactone, obtained through a four-step synthesis, exhibited UCST transitions only at elevated temperatures (60–72 °C) and low pH. More recently, Nicolas and co-workers reported nonionic polyesters with tunable UCST behavior prepared by radical ring-opening copolymerization of cyclic ketene acetals with acrylamide, which also showed rapid degradation. Degradable UCST-type coacervates are even more scarcely explored. While LCST-type coacervation has been reported for several polyesters, ,− UCST-type transitions have been observed mainly in polyphosphonates and polypeptides. Collectively, these gaps highlight a compelling opportunity to develop degradable polycations exhibiting UCST-type coacervation in purely aqueous media.

Herein, we introduce a new class of degradable, low-T g cationic polyesters that combine pH responsiveness, anion-induced UCST-type coacervation, and hydrolytic degradability in water. The polymers were synthesized through a scalable route involving room-temperature polycondensation, photoinduced thiol–ene click modification, and counterion exchange to yield ammonium-functionalized polyesters paired with either Cl– or BF4 – counterions. The ionizable side groups enable pH-dependent solubility and anion-mediated UCST-type phase separation, while the polyester backbone provides hydrolytic degradability under physiological conditions. By varying polymer concentration, anion type, pH, and the anion-to-cation repeat unit molar ratio (A/CRU), the phase-transition temperature can be precisely tuned over a broad range. Notably, UCST-type transitions induced by NaCl are uncommon, as such behavior typically requires more hydrophobic or mixed anion systems. ,− Salicylate-induced UCST-type coacervation further demonstrates the ability of these materials to respond to pharmaceutically relevant anions, highlighting their potential for molecular sensing and drug delivery related applications. Together with previously reported cytocompatibility results, ,, this work establishes degradable cationic polyesters as a sustainable platform for programmable, anion-induced UCST-type coacervation in purely aqueous media, with potential applications in responsive materials, anion sensing, and delivery systems.

Experimental Section

Materials and Methods

Trimethylolpropane allyl ether (TPAE), adipoyl chloride (ADC), pyridine anhydrous (99.8%), 2,2-dimethoxy-2-phenylacetophenone (DMPA), cysteamine hydrochloride, sodium tetrafluoroborate (98%), sodium salicylate (SAL) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. l-Lactide (Sigma-Aldrich) was purified by recrystallization from ethyl acetate. 1,8-Diazabicyclo[5.4.0]­undec-7-ene (DBU, Sigma-Aldrich) and benzyl alcohol (Sigma-Aldrich) were distilled over calcium hydride before use. Dichloromethane (DCM, 99.5%, Scharlab) was dried over calcium hydride and distilled under a nitrogen atmosphere before use. Methanol (99.9%) and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.5%) were obtained from Scharlab and were used without further purification. Dialysis membranes with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 3500 Da were supplied by Medicell membranes Ltd. and were cleaned in water at 50 °C before use. Milli-Q water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm at 298 K was used for all experiments.

Synthesis of Poly­(trimethylolpropane Allyl Ether-co-adipoyl Chloride) (PE)

The precursor polyesters, bearing alkene side groups, were synthesized via condensation copolymerization of a diol (TPAE) and a diacyl chloride, in the presence of pyridine as the HCl acceptor, following a procedure reported previously. In a typical synthesis, TPAE (5.0 mL, 0.029 mol) and pyridine (5.2 mL, 0.064 mol) were dissolved in 120 mL of freshly distilled DCM in a 250 mL round-bottom flask, under an N2 atmosphere. The flask was immersed in an ice–water bath and adipoyl chloride (4.2 mL, 0.029 mol) was added dropwise over 40 min. After stirring for 20 h at room temperature, the salt formed was removed by filtration, and the solvent was evaporated under reduced pressure. The crude product was then precipitated into an excess of cold methanol to yield 3.8 g of a viscous, light orange oily product.

Synthesis of Polyesters with Positively Charged Amine Side Groups Paired with Chloride Counterion (PE-Cys-Cl)

In a typical experiment, 5.08 g (44.7 mmol) of cysteamine hydrochloride was dissolved in 5 mL of DMF and the solution was stirred for 30 min. Next, 2.54 g PE (8.94 mmol of monomer repeat units) and 0.46 g DMPA (1.79 mmol) dissolved in 7 mL DMF were added to the reaction, and the mixture was stirred for 15 min at RT under an N2 atmosphere. The reaction was then exposed to a hand-held UV lamp (1.8 W, λ = 365 nm) and irradiated for 30 min at room temperature. The resulting product was precipitated in diethyl ether, redissolved in nanopure water and dialyzed against it for 48 h. The final product was obtained in 64% yield (2.28 g) by removing the solvent under reduced pressure.

Synthesis of Polyesters with Positively Charged Amine Side Groups Paired with Sodium Tetrafluoroborate Counterion (PE-Cys-BF4)

In a typical experiment, 77.2 mg PE-Cys-Cl was dissolved in 18 mL of nanopure water at pH 5. Sodium tetrafluoroborate (0.21 g, 1.91 mmol) was added under stirring, resulting in polymer precipitation. The precipitated polymer was isolated, redissolved, and dialyzed against deionized water to remove excess NaBF4 and the released NaCl. The purified polymer was then recovered by lyophilization prior to further characterization.

Synthesis of PLLA Control

Low-molecular-weight poly­(l-lactide) (PLLA) was synthesized as a control sample for the degradation experiments following a previously reported procedure. Briefly, l-lactide (5.00 g, 34.7 mmol) was charged into a flame-dried round-bottom flask under nitrogen. Dichloromethane (DCM, 5 mL), benzyl alcohol (0.670 g, 7.16 mmol), and DBU (0.075 g, 0.50 mmol) were then added, and the reaction mixture was subjected to three freeze–pump–thaw cycles. The reaction was allowed to proceed under a nitrogen atmosphere at room temperature for 1 h. After 1 h, benzoic acid was added to terminate the polymerization. The mixture was concentrated under vacuum and precipitated dropwise into excess cold methanol. The white precipitate was collected, washed with methanol, and dried under vacuum overnight to afford low molecular weight PLLA.

Characterization Techniques

The average molecular weights (M n’s) and the molecular weight distributions (M w/M n’s) of the alkene functional precursor polyesters were determined by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) using an Agilent 1260 infinity II system, equipped with two PL mixed-D columns operated at 35 °C. The eluent was THF, containing 2 v/v% triethylamine, at a flow rate of 1 mL min–1. The system was calibrated using a series of ten narrow molecular weight linear poly­(methyl methacrylate) standards, ranging from 850 to 298,900 g mol–1. 1H NMR spectra were obtained on a Bruker DPX-300 spectrometer, while 13C NMR and two-dimensional 13C, 1H-correlated (HSQC) spectra were obtained using a DPX-500 spectrometer (Bruker, Rheinstetten, Germany). All samples were dissolved in chloroform-d3 or methanol-d 4, depending on their solubility, and the spectra were recorded at 298 K. A UV lamp (Spectroline ENF-280C, Spectronics Corporation, Westbury, NY, USA) was used for the preparation of the amine-functionalized polyesters. The thermal stability of the synthesized polyesters was examined by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) measurements using a PerkinElmer Pyris Diamond TG/DTA Instruments (PerkinElmer, Llantrisant, UK). In a typical measurement, 5–10 mg of the sample were placed in an aluminum holder and were heated under a constant nitrogen flow, from room temperature up to 500 °C, at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The thermal transitions of the synthesized polyesters were examined using a Discovery DSC 250 differential scanning calorimeter (TA Instruments). All measurements were carried out under a nitrogen flow and at a temperature range between −100 and 120 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min. Two heating/cooling cycles were performed in all cases. The glass transition temperature of the polyesters was obtained from the second cycle to eliminate any effect from nonerased thermal history. A Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument equipped with a He–Ne laser (λ = 633 nm) at a 90° scattering angle was used for the dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements.

The ionization of the synthesized polyesters bearing pendant amine groups was investigated as a function of the solution pH, by potentiometric titration measurements. The titration curves of the pH-responsive polyesters in the pH range from 2 to 10 were obtained by monitoring the increase of the solution pH upon the addition of aliquots of 0.1 M NaOH to a 1 wt % polymer solution. The pH was measured using a WTW inoLab pH7110 pH meter. The pH-dependent solubility of the amine-functionalized polyesters was studied by optical transmission measurements at λ = 650 nm using a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer. For this, a 2 wt % aqueous polymer solution at pH 2.5 was prepared and the pH of the solution was gradually increased by the addition of aliquots of 0.1 M NaOH. The transmittance of the solution was measured as a function of the solution pH at room temperature.

Turbidity Experiments

To examine the temperature-dependent solubility of the polymers in water, turbidimetry measurements were performed on a PerkinElmer Lambda 25 UV/vis spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, Llantrisant, UK). A 0.2 wt % - 1.4 wt % polymer solution in nanopure water was prepared and was first heated to a specific temperature to obtain a clear solution. Then, the solution was cooled at a constant rate of 1 °C/min using an external bath, and its optical transmittance at λ = 650 nm was recorded as a function of the solution temperature. Next, a heating cycle was conducted at the same rate. The cloud point (T cp) of the polymers was determined from the normalized transmittance curves by identifying the intersection between the tangent line drawn at the steepest part of the curve and the baseline corresponding to 100% transmittance.

Coacervation Formation

Variable Temperature 1HNMR spectroscopy was employed to investigate the salt-induced phase separation of the cationic polyester at temperatures above and below the UCST. Aqueous solutions of the cationic polyester (PE-Cys-Cl, 8 mg/mL in D2O) containing NaBF4 (200 mg/mL) were analyzed using 1H NMR at 330 K (above the UCST) and 298 K (below the UCST). 1H NMR spectra were obtained on a Bruker DPX-500 spectrometer. In addition, optical microscopy was utilized to directly visualize the formation of the coacervates at the microscale upon cooling. For salt-induced phase separation studies, aqueous PE-Cys-Cl solutions (8 mg/mL, 200 mM NaBF4, and 8 mg/mL, 10 mM SAL at pH 6.9) were deposited onto silicon wafer substrates and imaged below the UCST using an FR optical microscope (Kern Optics). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were performed using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS equipped with a He–Ne laser (λ = 633 nm) at a scattering angle of 90°. PE-Cys-Cl solutions (8 mg/mL, 200 mM NaBF4) were heated above the UCST to obtain a clear solution prior to measurements at room temperature, and data were collected at different time points.

Degradation Experiments

Hydrolytic degradation studies of the amine functionalized polyesters were carried out over a period of 18 days by dissolving 120 mg of PE-Cys-Cl in 10 mL PBS (0.5% w/v) at pH 7.4 and 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals (6, 12, and 18 days), 3.0 mL of the solution was removed and freeze-dried. The degradation products were analyzed by SEC and by 1H NMR spectroscopy. The SEC system used to examine the degradation of PE-Cys-Cl was equipped with a Waters 515 HPLC pump, three PL mixed-D columns operated at 80 °C, and a Shodex RI-101 refractive index detector. DMF containing 0.1% w/v LiCl was used as the eluent at a flow rate of 0.6 mL min–1. The system was calibrated using eight narrow molecular weight linear poly­(methyl methacrylate) standards ranging from 2,680 to 298,900 g mol–1. PE-Cys-BF4 and PLLA were analyzed using the same SEC system at a flow rate of 0.5 mL min–1, calibrated with six narrow molecular weight linear poly­(methyl methacrylate) standards ranging from 1,590 to 138,600 g mol–1. 1H NMR spectra of the degradation products of the PE-Cys-Cl were obtained on a Bruker DPX-500 spectrometer using D2O as solvent.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of Functional Polyesters with Positively Charged Side Groups

Thermoresponsive polyesters containing positively charged amine side groups were synthesized via a two-step procedure. In the first step, a polycondensation reaction between TPAE, an alkene-functionalized diol, and ADC, a diacyl chloride consisting of six carbon atoms, produced a polyester containing alkene side groups (PE) (Figure ai). Subsequently, positively charged amine groups were introduced along the PE chain by grafting cysteamine hydrochloride onto the alkene side groups of PE via a photoinduced thiol–ene click reaction, to yield the cationic polyester (PE-Cys-Cl) (Figure aii). A 5-fold molar excess of cysteamine hydrochloride relative to the alkene groups was used to promote efficient functionalization and minimize competing vinyl-based side reactions.

Synthesis and characterization of cationic polyester. (a) Two-step synthetic route followed for the preparation of the polyesters bearing positively charged amine side groups: (i) synthesis of the alkene functionalized polyester PE and (ii) synthesis of the cationic polyester PE-Cys-Cl. 1H NMR spectra of the cationic polyester in methanol-d 4 before (PE-Cys-Cl) (b) and after the anion exchange with BF4 – (PE-Cys-BF4) (c) (* denotes the solvent peak).

The synthesis of PE was carried out at ambient temperature for 20 h in the absence of any metal-containing catalyst. Following purification by precipitation, the number-average molecular weight and molecular weight distribution of the polyester determined by SEC were M n = 6500 g/mol and M w/M n = 1.45, respectively (Figure S1). The chemical structure of the alkene functionalized polyester was verified by 1H, HSQC and 13C NMR spectroscopies. Specifically, the presence of Ch-Hh and Ci-Hi correlation peaks in the HSQC spectrum of PE, observed at 5.76–5.95/134.2 ppm and 5.10–5.33/116.8 ppm, respectively, corresponding to the vinyl group from the TPAE monomer repeat units, verified the presence of the alkene pendant groups along the polyester backbone (Figure S2). In addition, the presence of a peak at 173 ppm in the 13C NMR spectrum of PE, assigned to the carbonyl carbon of the ester groups formed during the polymerization, confirmed the successful synthesis of the polymer (Figure S3). In the second step, the grafting of cysteamine hydrochloride was performed at room temperature via a photoinduced thiol–ene click reaction, utilizing DMPA as a photoinitiator and a UV lamp emitting at 365 nm (Figure aii). Successful postmodification, to introduce the protonated amine side groups, was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy. The disappearance of the peaks corresponding to the vinyl protons at 5.76–5.95 ppm (Hh) and 5.10–5.33 ppm (Hi), together with the appearance of two new peaks at 2.75–2.86 ppm (Hl) and 3.08–3.19 ppm (Hm), assigned to the protons of cysteamine, confirmed the successful modification of PE (Figure b, Figure S4). Quantitative analysis of the 1H NMR spectrum further confirmed the complete conversion of the alkene groups, as evidenced by the absence of residual vinyl proton signals and the integral ratio of the polyester backbone protons (Ha) over the cysteamine methylene protons (Hl) (Figure S4). Next, the chloride counterions of the PE-Cys-Cl polycation were replaced by BF4 – using an excess of NaBF4 salt. The counterion exchange reaction was monitored by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figure c). In particular, the proton peaks Hm, located next to the protonated amine groups of PE-Cys-BF4, shifted slightly downfield to 3.15 ppm compared to the same protons of PE-Cys-Cl, which appeared at 3.13 ppm.

The thermal stability of the synthesized polyesters was investigated via thermogravimetric analysis (Figure S5a). The parent alkene-functionalized polyester (PE) displayed a single degradation event at 408 °C, corresponding to the backbone decomposition. Incorporation of cysteamine hydrochloride (PE-Cys-Cl) produced a distinct two-step degradation profile. An initial mass-loss transition at 308 °C was attributed to the cleavage of the pendant amine side groups, followed by backbone degradation at 397 °C, indicating a slight reduction in the thermal stability of the polymer chain. Anion exchange to form PE-Cys-BF4 produced a similar two-step profile, with the first transition shifted to 298 °C and the second remaining at 397 °C. These results indicate that counterion replacement from Cl– to BF4 – selectively lowers side chain thermal stability, while leaving the polyester backbone unaffected. Overall, all functional polyesters (PE, PE-Cys-Cl and PE-Cys-BF4) demonstrated sufficient thermal stability, supporting their applicability over a wide temperature range.

The thermal transitions of the synthesized polyesters were investigated by DSC (Figure S5b). All the polymers exhibited a single glass transition temperature below ambient temperature, characteristic of amorphous, low T g polymers. Specifically, the alkene functionalized polyester PE displayed a T g at −56 °C in the second heating cycle. Grafting cysteamine hydrochloride onto the PE backbone increased the T g, reflecting reduced chain mobility imparted by the polar side groups. When isolated from alkaline aqueous solution (pH ≈ 10), where the amines are fully deprotonated, PE-Cys displayed an elevated T g of −41 °C, consistent with hydrogen bonding interactions among the pendant amines. Under acidic conditions (pH ≈ 5), protonation of the amines yielded PE-Cys-Cl, which exhibited a further shift to −9 °C, reflecting strong ionic interactions between the ammonium cations and chloride counterions. Replacement of the chloride ions with the more hydrophobic and polarizable BF4 – counterions (PE-Cys-BF4) slightly reduced the T g to −13 °C, indicating weaker effective ionic associations and enhanced chain mobility, consistent with the counterion-dependent T g trends reported for polyelectrolytes and polymeric ionic liquids. , Overall, these results demonstrate a versatile strategy to modulate the polymer chain flexibility and thermal properties through side-chain chemistry, counterion selection, and pH-dependent ionization.

The synthesized amine-functionalized polyesters behave as weak polybases due to the presence of the ionizable primary amine pendant groups, which undergo reversible protonation–deprotonation in response to the solution pH. At low pH, protonation of the amine groups generates a positively charged polyelectrolyte (PE-Cys-Cl), whereas at high pH, deprotonation renders the polymer uncharged (PE-Cys). Potentiometric titration data revealed a characteristic buffering plateau between pH 6 and 8 (Figure S6a), reflecting the progressive protonation and deprotonation processes along the polymer backbone. Analysis of the titration curve allowed determination of the effective degree of ionization (αeff, 0 < αeff < 1) as a function of pH (Figure S6b), yielding an apparent pK α of 7.8. The fully protonated and fully deprotonated states were reached at pH 5.6 and 9.6, respectively.

The protonation and deprotonation of the pendant amine side groups strongly influenced the solubility of the polyesters in water. Turbidimetry measurements were performed to investigate the pH-responsive solubility of the amine-functionalized polyesters by monitoring the optical transmittance of dilute aqueous polymer solutions at room temperature as a function of the solution pH. As shown in Figure a, the polyesters displayed a sharp solubility transition at pH ≈ 8. Below this point, the solution remained optically transparent, indicating high solubility due to the protonation of the amine side groups, which generates electrostatic repulsion and stabilizes an extended chain conformation. At pH values above ∼ 8, deprotonation decreased the polymer’s hydrophilicity, promoting phase separation (Scheme a). Quantitative analysis identified a critical ionization threshold of ∼ 37%, indicating that protonation of only one-third of the pendant amine groups is sufficient to maintain solubility. To probe self-assembly at elevated pH, DLS measurements were performed at pH 8.1, where the polymer contains both protonated and unprotonated side groups (degree of ionization ≈ 33%). The measurements revealed spontaneous formation of nanoparticles with an average diameter of ∼ 160 ± 80 nm, consistent with chain aggregation above the solubility transition and reflecting the amphiphilic nature of the partially ionized polyester (Figure b).

Effect of pH, Counterion and Salt Type, and Salt Concentration on the UCST-Type Phase Transition

The thermoresponsive behavior of polyelectrolytes is strongly influenced by both the ionic strength and anion type, consistent with the Hofmeister-type effects that arise from charge screening, ion solvation, and specific ion–polymer interactions. , To elucidate these effects, we examined the aqueous solution behavior of the synthesized polycations in the presence of two counterions, chloride (PE-Cys-Cl) and tetrafluoroborate (PE-Cys-BF4). By systematically varying the concentrations of NaCl and NaBF4, we aimed to elucidate the effects of added anions and intrinsic counterions on the anion-induced phase transition and the corresponding cloud point (T cp). To further elucidate the effect of polymer ionization, the amine-functionalized polyesters were examined under two ionization states: at pH 5, where the polymer is fully protonated, and at physiological pH 7.4, where it is partially ionized (∼ 68%). Turbidimetry measurements at a constant polymer concentration (8 mg/mL) revealed T cp values during both heating and cooling cycles (Figures ); the discussion below focuses on the cooling curves. Both PE-Cys-Cl and PE-Cys-BF4 were fully soluble in pure water over the entire temperature range investigated, but underwent reversible phase separation upon salt addition, followed by redissolution upon heating, indicating a salt-responsive UCST-type behavior. For PE-Cys-Cl, UCST-type transitions were observed only at high NaCl concentrations, occurring at approximately 1000 mM NaCl at pH 5 (T cp = 38 °C). In contrast, at physiological pH (7.4), where the polymer is partially protonated, phase separation occurred at a substantially lower NaCl concentration (∼ 450 mM) and at a lower transition temperature (T cp = 29 °C) (Figure a and b). In all cases, the transitions were fully reversible, with turbidity disappearing upon heating and reappearing upon cooling. Notably, small increases in NaCl concentration produced pronounced upward shifts in T cp. For example, increasing the NaCl concentration from 1000 mM to 1100 mM at pH 5 raised T cp from 38 to 71 °C. Even larger shifts were observed at pH 7.4, where T cp increased from 29 to 81 °C as the NaCl concentration rose from 450 mM to 550 mM. Together, these results demonstrate that anions not only trigger phase separation but also enable precise tuning of the UCST window, while the pH serves as an orthogonal control parameter that lowers the salt threshold as polymer ionization decreases.

The effect of NaCl on the UCST-type transition arises from a balance between electrostatic repulsions among the protonated ammonium groups and ion pairing between Cl‑ ions and the cationic side chains of the polyester (Scheme ). At low NaCl concentrations, the chloride ions remain largely dissociated, electrostatic repulsions dominate, and the polymer chains adopt an extended, water-soluble conformation. At higher salt concentrations, increased Cl‑ association screens the positive charges and weakens these repulsions, promoting intra- and intermolecular ion-mediated bridging that drives reversible phase separation (Scheme b). Upon heating, disruption of these weak ionic cross-links restores the polymer solubility (Scheme c). This ion-bridging mechanism also accounts for the dependence of the UCST-type transition on both the solution pH and salt concentration. As the degree of ionization decreases with increasing pH (from fully protonated at pH 5 to ∼68% ionized at pH 7.4), fewer anions are required to reach the critical A/CRU molar ratio necessary for associative phase separation. Likewise, increasing the salt concentration raises the A/CRU molar ratio and strengthens the intra- and interchain pairing, thereby shifting the T cp to higher values. Together, these results establish the A/CRU molar ratio as a key parameter governing the UCST behavior, enabling predictable tuning of the phase transition through independent adjustment of the solution pH and salt concentration.

To isolate the role of anion hydrophobicity and specific ion pairing, BF4 – was selected as a mechanistic model anion because of its weak hydration and high polarizability, enabling systematic evaluation of the anion–polymer interactions governing the UCST-type phase behavior. Replacing chloride with the less hydrated and more polarizable BF4 – counterion further reduced the NaCl concentration required to trigger UCST-type phase separation under both acidic and physiological conditions (Figure c,d). Accordingly, PE-Cys-BF4 exhibited UCST behavior at substantially lower salt concentrations than its chloride analogue, occurring at approximately 900 mM NaCl at pH 5 and 300 mM at pH 7.4, compared to 1000 mM and 450 mM, respectively, for PE-Cys-Cl. This shift reflects the stronger association of BF4 – with the ammonium side groups, which lowers the threshold for anion-mediated cross-linking. Notably, this counterion effect is amplified at higher pH, where reduced polymer ionization increases the effective A/CRU molar ratio. As a result, PE-Cys-BF4 reached a T cp of 64 °C, at only 400 mM NaCl at pH 7.4, whereas PE-Cys-Cl exhibited a T cp of just 29 °C at a higher salt concentration of 450 mM (Figure e, Table ). Across all conditions, the T cp increased approximately linearly with the NaCl concentration, with no indication of saturation within the explored range, highlighting the predictable and highly tunable nature of the anion-induced UCST response of these polyesters.

Thermoresponsive Properties of PE-Cys-Cl and PE-Cys-BF4 as a Function of the Solution pH and the NaCl/NaBF4 Concentration

Having established the enhanced salt responsiveness of PE-Cys-BF4 in NaCl, we next examined its phase behavior in the presence of NaBF4 to directly probe the effect of the added anion (Figure ). Remarkably, PE-Cys-BF4 underwent a UCST-type phase separation at NaBF4 concentrations as low as 100 mM at both pH 5 and 7.4, nearly an order of magnitude lower than the salt concentrations required for the corresponding chloride salt. This pronounced anion effect reflects the high affinity of the weakly hydrated, highly polarizable BF4 – anions for the ammonium side groups along the polymer backbone, which more effectively promotes ion pairing, intermolecular bridging, and ultimately phase separation. Consistently, replacing 300 mM NaCl with the same molar concentration of NaBF4 at pH 7.4 increased the T cp from 24 to 55 °C. The stronger BF4 –-ammonium association requires higher thermal energy to disrupt these interatcions, leading to substantially larger shifts in T cp at comparable anion concentrations. Figure c summarizes the combined effects of pH, salt type, and salt concentration on T cp, demonstrating that these orthogonal parameters enable precise and predictable tuning of the UCST response. As observed for NaCl, T cp increased approximately linearly with NaBF4 concentration at both pH values, rising from ∼ 8 to ∼ 50 °C, at pH 5 and from ∼ 25 to ∼ 55 °C, at pH 7.4 as the NaBF4 concentration increased from 100 to 300 mM. Moreover, at a fixed NaBF4 concentration, increasing the pH systematically elevated the T cp, reflecting a reduced polymer ionization and the corresponding increase in the A/CRU molar ratio. For example, at 100 mM NaBF4, the T cp increased from 8 to 25 °C as the pH was raised from 5 to 7.4, corresponding to an increase in A/CRU from 5 to 7.6. The higher A/CRU molar ratio promotes more extensive BF4 –-mediated ionic bridging, thereby stabilizing the associated state and elevating the T cp. Overall, these results demonstrate that weakly hydrated, highly polarizable anions such as BF4 – enable UCST-type transitions at low ionic strength, particularly under physiologically relevant conditions. Anion type, pH, and salt concentration, therefore act as orthogonal and quantitative control parameters, allowing precise modulation of the UCST behavior through adjustment of the A/CRU molar ratio. Importantly, selected systems exhibited T cp values close to or below 37 °C at physiological pH, illustrating the broad tunability of the UCST response. These findings establish design principles for selecting counterion chemistry, pH, and ionic strength to program the transition behavior of application-specific PE-Cys-based UCST systems.

Effect of an Organic, Anionic Model Drug on the UCST-Type Phase Separation

To evaluate the influence of ion hydrophobicity and specific ion pairing on phase behavior, the aqueous solution behavior of PE-Cys-Cl was investigated in the presence of sodium salicylate (SAL), an aromatic and pharmaceutically relevant anion (Figure S7). SAL efficiently induced a UCST-type phase separation over a wide pH range, from mildly acidic to physiological conditions, demonstrating that these cationic polyesters can directly respond to biologically relevant anionic molecules. Remarkably, SAL triggered UCST behavior at concentrations as low as 7 mM at physiological pH (T cp ≈ 46 °C), nearly an order of magnitude lower than those required for NaBF4 (100–300 mM) and more than two orders of magnitude lower than NaCl (900–1000 mM) (Figure d). Although SAL followed the same qualitative trends as inorganic salts, with the T cp increasing with both anion concentration and pH, its combined electrostatic, hydrophobic, and cation-π interactions with the polymer backbone enabled associative phase separation at dramatically reduced concentrations. In addition, SAL produced a broader phase-transition window compared with NaCl or NaBF4, consistent with stronger and more cooperative anion–polymer interactions. Although the T cp observed with salicylate at physiological pH remains above 37 °C, the pronounced response to SAL highlights the potential of this platform for selective anion sensing and bioactive-anion-triggered coacervation.

Coacervate Formation

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the anion-induced phase transition, variable temperature 1H NMR spectroscopy experiments were performed in D2O in the presence of 200 mM NaBF4 (T cp = 30 °C). Spectra recorded above the UCST (47 °C, homogeneous solution) and below the critical temperature (25 °C, turbid state) revealed pronounced peak broadening upon cooling (Figure a). Importantly, all proton resonances remained detectable in the phase-separated state, albeit significantly broadened, indicating reduced chain mobility rather than complete immobilization. The observed downfield chemical-shift changes reflect deshielding of the proton environments, consistent with partial dehydration and enhanced intermolecular and intramolecular interactions accompanying polymer association. This behavior is distinct from classical coil–globule transitions, in which extensive dehydration leads to the near-complete disappearance of 1H NMR resonances due to polymer collapse and severely restricted segmental motion. In the present system, the retention of proton signals below the UCST therefore provides direct spectroscopic evidence for liquid–liquid phase separation via coacervate formation, rather than macroscopic precipitation or chain collapse. Similar observations have been reported for other polymeric coacervate systems, further supporting this assignment.

To further probe the nature of the phase-separated state, the morphology and dynamics of the polymer-rich phase were examined by optical microscopy. Upon addition of 200 mM NaBF4, the polyester formed well-defined, polydisperse micron-sized droplets (6.88 ± 2.60 μm; range: 0.930–14.6 μm) characteristic of self-coacervates (Figure b,c). These droplets exhibited liquid-like behavior, undergoing fusion and coalescence over time to form larger domains (10.14 ± 2.940 μm; range: 3.17–17.3 μm) (Figure d,e). The spontaneous droplet growth and coalescence provide strong evidence for a liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) mechanism driven by anion-mediated associative interactions, consistent with classical coacervate-forming systems.

Time-resolved dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements further confirmed the coacervate dynamics. At pH 5 and 200 mM NaBF4 (below T cp), the hydrodynamic diameter increased from approximately 2.0 ± 0.3 μm to 4.0 ± 0.9 μm over time (Figure S8), accompanied by a progressive broadening of the size distribution. This temporal evolution reflects the fusion and coalescence of smaller droplets into larger liquid-like domains, consistent with an LLPS mechanism rather than irreversible aggregation or precipitation. Anion-mediated UCST-type phase separation of the polyester was further investigated in the presence of the organic anion salicylate (SAL) using optical microscopy. At pH 6.9, the addition of 10 mM SAL produced polydisperse, micron-sized droplets with an average diameter of 1.34 ± 0.70 μm (range: 0.75–5.03 μm), characteristic of self-coacervates (Figure f,g). These observations demonstrate that pharmaceutically relevant aromatic anions can not only modulate the UCST-type transition but also directly drive the formation of polymer-rich coacervate phases.

Effect of Polymer Concentration on the UCST-Type Transition

To further elucidate the role of polymer concentration in governing UCST behavior, the thermoresponsive properties of PE-Cys-BF4 were examined at pH 5 under a fixed salt concentration (300 mM NaBF4). Temperature-dependent transmittance measurements were performed during cooling and heating for polymer concentrations ranging from 2 to 12 mg/mL (Figure S9), and the corresponding T cp values extracted from the cooling curves are summarized in Figure a.

Although the salt concentration was fixed, increasing the polymer concentration systematically reduced the A/CRU molar ratio, introducing a competing effect that modulates the phase behavior. Under these conditions, T cp exhibited a modest, but nonmonotonic dependence on polymer concentration, varying between 43 and 50 °C. Specifically, the T cp increased with polymer concentration up to 8 mg/mL, reaching a maximum of 50 °C, and then decreased at higher concentrations (Figure a). This behavior reflects a balance between two opposing factors. On the one hand, increasing polymer concentration enhances interchain contacts and multivalent interactions which favor associative phase separation. On the other hand, the concomitant decrease in the A/CRU molar ratio limits the availability of anions required to mediate effective ionic cross-linking. At higher polymer concentrations, reduced anion availability becomes dominant, weakening ion-mediated association and lowering the T cp.

Quantitatively, as the polymer concentration increased from 2 to 8 mg/mL, the T cp rose from 43 to 50 °C while the A/CRU molar ratio decreased from 67 to 17, indicating that ionic bridging remained sufficiently supported within this range. In contrast, a further increase of the polymer concentration to 10 and 12 mg/mL reduced the A/CRU molar ratio to 13 and 11, respectively, and was accompanied by a decrease in T cp to 49 and 45 °C. These results demonstrate that at higher polymer concentrations, reduced anion availability becomes the dominant factor, limiting ionic cross-linking and consequently lowering the T cp.

To decouple the effects of polymer concentration and anion availability, the UCST behavior was next examined at constant A/CRU molar ratios (10, 20, and 40) over polymer concentrations ranging from 2 to 14 mg/mL at pH 5 (Figures S10–S12). The corresponding T cp values, obtained from both the cooling and heating cycles, are summarized in Figure b. In contrast to the fixed-salt case, T cp increased monotonically with polymer concentration at all A/CRU ratios. At A/CRU = 40, T cp increased from 28 to 91 °C as the polymer concentration increased from 2 to 10 mg/mL. At lower A/CRU molar ratios of 20 and 10, the T cp increased from 27 to 80 °C (4–14 mg/mL) and from 19 to 55 °C (6–14 mg/mL), respectively. At a fixed polymer concentration, increasing the A/CRU molar ratio consistently elevated the T cp, reflecting a higher density of ion-mediated interchain associations that require greater thermal energy to disrupt. For example, at 6 mg/mL, the T cp increased from 19 to 42 °C and 68 °C as the A/CRU molar ratio increased from 10 to 20 and 40, respectively. Moreover, higher A/CRU molar ratios shifted the onset of UCST-type phase separation to lower polymer concentrations, occurring at approximately 2 mg/mL for A/CRU = 40, ∼ 4 mg/mL for A/CRU = 20, and ∼ 6 mg/mL for A/CRU = 10. Overall, these results demonstrate that the T cp can be precisely and predictably tuned over a wide temperature window through independent control of polymer concentration and the A/CRU molar ratio, further underscoring the central role of anion-mediated ionic bridging in governing the UCST behavior.

Reversibility of the Anion-Induced UCST-Type Phase Transition

The reversibility of the thermoresponsive behavior was evaluated by monitoring the optical transmittance of an 8 mg/mL PE-Cys-BF4 solution containing 150 mM NaBF4 over four consecutive cooling–heating cycles (Figure c). The polyester displayed fully reversible UCST-type transitions, with minimal cycle-to-cycle variation (∼ 0.7 °C). Within each cycle, the T cp values obtained during cooling and heating differed by less than 2 °C, demonstrating excellent thermal reversibility and high stability of the phase transition (Figure d).

Polyester Degradation

Polyesters undergo hydrolytic degradation through cleavage of hydrolytically labile ester bonds, yielding oligomers bearing carboxylic acid and hydroxyl end groups. Accordingly, the degradability of PE-Cys-Cl was evaluated by incubation in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 18 days, with aliquots collected after 6, 12, and 18 days and analyzed by SEC and 1H NMR spectroscopy. As shown in Figure a, the SEC traces progressively shifted toward longer elution times, indicating a decrease in molecular weight with increasing incubation time due to continuous chain scission. Quantitatively, the number-average molecular weight (M n ) decreased by ∼6% after 6 days, followed by more pronounced reductions of 27% and 54% after 12 and 18 days, respectively (Figure b).

(a) Evolution of SEC chromatograms over time during hydrolytic degradation in PBS (pH 7.4, 37 °C) and (b) changes in the number-average molar mass (M n) as a function of degradation time under physiological conditions.

The degradation behavior of the BF4 – analogue was also examined in PBS. PE-Cys-BF4 exhibited limited solubility in the buffer solution, likely due to the more hydrophobic and weakly hydrated nature of the BF4 – counterion. SEC analysis performed prior to degradation revealed a lower apparent molecular weight for PE-Cys-BF4 compared to that of PE-Cys-Cl (Figure S13), which was attributed to counterion-dependent differences in polymer–solvent interactions and chain conformation in the SEC eluent. In contrast to PE-Cys-Cl, PE-Cys-BF4 showed only minor changes in the SEC traces over 18 days, with no pronounced time-dependent shift toward lower molecular weights. These results demonstrate that the counterion type can modulate the polymer hydration and hydrophobicity, thereby providing a direct handle for tuning the hydrolytic degradation behavior of this cationic polyester platform.

As a benchmark against conventional aliphatic polyesters, the degradation behavior of PE-Cys-Cl was compared with that of low molecular weight poly­(l-lactide) PLLA (M n ≈ 4000 g mol–1) under identical conditions. Unlike PE-Cys-Cl, PLLA exhibited no significant change in its SEC traces over the investigated period (Figure S13b), consistent with the slow hydrolysis typically observed for hydrophobic polyesters such as PLLA and PCL. − This comparison highlights the unusually rapid degradation of PE-Cys-Cl, which is attributed to its hydrophilic, cationic character, promoting water uptake and facilitating ester-bond cleavage. Together, these findings identify polymer hydration and counterion type as key molecular parameters governing the degradation behavior of cationic polyesters under physiological conditions.

Further insight into the degradation process was obtained by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figure S14). The 1H NMR spectrum of the pristine polyester (t = 0) displayed the characteristic methylene resonances adjacent to the ester bonds, with Hc at 2.39 ppm and Hj at 4.06 ppm. After 6 days of incubation in PBS (pH 7.4), two new resonances emerged at 2.21 and 3.53 ppm, which were assigned to the methylene protons adjacent to the newly formed carboxylic acid (Hc*) and hydroxyl (Hj*) end groups, respectively, generated via ester hydrolysis. The progressive increase in the integrals of Hc* and Hj* relative to the invariant Ha resonance confirms a time-dependent ester bond cleavage. These spectroscopic changes are fully consistent with the SEC results and further support the gradual hydrolytic degradation of the amine-functionalized polyester.

Conclusions

In this work, we developed degradable cationic polyesters exhibiting programmable, anion-induced UCST-type coacervation in aqueous media. PE-Cys-Cl and PE-Cys-BF4 were prepared by polycondensation, thiol–ene functionalization, and counterion exchange, yielding ionizable polyesters with pH-responsive solubility and tunable thermoresponsive behavior. The UCST transitions were governed by the counterion type, added anion type, pH, polymer and salt concentration, and especially the anion-to-cation repeat unit ratio (A/CRU), enabling T cp tuning from 8 to 91 °C. Weakly hydrated, hydrophobic anions such as BF4 – promoted associative phase separation and markedly reduced the salt concentration required to induce UCST-type behavior. Salicylate induced UCST transitions at substantially lower concentrations compared to the inorganic salts, and served as a representative bioactive anion trigger, highlighting opportunities for selective anion sensing, bioactive-anion-triggered coacervation, and drug delivery related systems following appropriate optimization. Variable-temperature NMR spectroscopy and optical microscopy confirmed the reversible liquid–liquid demixing into dynamic, water-rich coacervate droplets. Hydrolytic degradation studies further revealed that the counterion type controls the polyester stability, by increasing the polymer hydrophilicity and water uptake and thereby rendering the cationic polyesters substantially more degradable compared to conventional low molecular weight PLLA under physiological conditions. Overall, the reported degradable cationic polyesters provide a versatile proof-of-concept platform for aqueous anion-responsive UCST coacervates, with potential relevance to molecular sensing, bioactive-anion-triggered coacervation, smart delivery-related systems, and sustainable responsive materials.
