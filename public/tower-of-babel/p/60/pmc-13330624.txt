Comparison of intra-articular injectable polydactyly-derived chondrocyte sheet-minis with direct transplantation of intact polydactyly-derived chondrocyte sheets in a rat full-thickness cartilage defect model
Authors: Makoto Ogawa, Eriko Toyoda, Shiho Wasai, Tatsumi Tanaka, Ryoka Uchiyama, Miki Maehara, Tadashi Akamatsu, Masahiko Watanabe, Masato Sato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPolydactyly-derived chondrocyte (PD) sheets are being developed as an allogeneic cell sheet therapy for cartilage defects associated with knee osteoarthritis. Although intact PD sheets can promote hyaline cartilage repair, transplantation requires invasive open procedures. Injectable fragments of PD sheets (PD sheet-minis) may reduce invasiveness. However, their in vivo repair capacity after intra-articular administration remains unclear.MethodsPD cells were isolated from discarded cartilage tissue obtained during polydactyly surgery. PD sheets and PD sheet-minis were fabricated for 14 days using temperature-responsive culture inserts and RepCell plates, respectively. PD cells, PD sheets, and PD sheet-minis were compared by cell counting, viability assays, flow cytometry, soluble factor assays, and quantitative polymerase chain reaction. A full-thickness cartilage defect was created in the femoral trochlea of male athymic nude rats. The animals were randomized by body weight into vehicle, PD cell suspension, PD sheet-mini suspension, or intact PD sheet transplantation groups (n = 6 each). Hindlimb weight-bearing was assessed after surgery, and cartilage repair was evaluated histologically 4 weeks after transplantation.ResultsThe viable cell numbers administered per knee were comparable between PD cells, PD sheet-minis, and intact PD sheets. The PD sheet-minis and PD sheets showed similar surface marker profiles, whereas PD cells showed significantly lower CD26 and higher CD146, CD49a, and CD166 expression. Compared to PD cells, PD sheet-minis secreted more melanoma inhibitory activity and dickkopf-related protein 1 and less transforming growth factor-β, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, and matrix metalloproteinase-3. Gene expression analysis revealed lower COL1A1 and RUNX2 expression and higher MMP3 expression in PD sheet-minis than in PD cells; COL2A1 and COL10A1 were not detected. Weight-bearing showed no sustained intergroup difference. Histologically, intact PD sheets produced consistent Safranin O-positive, type II collagen-positive, and human vimentin-positive repair tissue, whereas PD cells and PD sheet-minis mainly produced fibrous repair tissue. The mean Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scores were 11.4 ± 5.4, 10.7 ± 6.2, 9.3 ± 7.1, and 4.4 ± 3.2 in the vehicle, PD cell, PD sheet-mini and PD sheet groups, respectively.ConclusionsPD sheet-minis retained several sheet-like in vitro characteristics; however, single intra-articular administration did not reproduce the cartilage repair achieved with intact PD sheets. These findings suggest that lesion-specific retention and persistence, rather than injectability alone, may be important determinants of structural cartilage repair by PD cell-sheet products.

Introduction

Articular cartilage provides a low-friction, load-bearing surface; however, its intrinsic capacity for repair is limited because the tissue is avascular, aneural, and sparsely cellular. Osteoarthritis (OA), particularly knee OA, is therefore a major cause of pain, impaired mobility, and disability in aging populations [1]. In Japan, radiographic knee OA is highly prevalent in older adults. The ROAD study reported a prevalence of 35.2% in men and 57.1% in women aged 60-69 years [2].

The current clinical management of knee OA includes analgesics, intra-articular injections, exercise therapy, unloading osteotomy, and arthroplasty. These approaches are important for symptom control and mechanical correction, but they do not reliably restore the hyaline articular cartilage in early- or middle-stage disease. Therefore, cell-based and tissue-engineered strategies have been developed to address the biological limitations of cartilage repair [3]. Autologous chondrocyte implantation is an established cell-based approach for focal cartilage defects [4]; however, its application to OA-associated defects is limited by the requirement for healthy cartilage harvest, donor-site morbidity, variable cell quality after expansion, and the broad, degenerative nature of OA lesions.

Cell sheet engineering is a scaffold-free strategy for cartilage repair. Temperature-responsive culture surfaces allow confluent cells to be harvested without proteolytic enzymes, preserving cell–cell junctions, extracellular matrix (ECM), and surface proteins [5,6]. These properties are particularly attractive for cartilage repair, because a sheet can be placed directly onto a defect, providing both cellular and matrix components at the lesion site.

Clinical and preclinical studies support the translational potential of chondrocyte sheet therapy. In an initial clinical study, autologous chondrocyte sheet transplantation combined with conventional surgery improved magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), arthroscopic, histological, and patient-reported outcomes in patients with knee OA [7]. In immunodeficient rat models, layered human chondrocyte sheets and human juvenile chondrocyte-derived sheets promoted hyaline-like cartilage repair, and transplanted human cells remained detectable within regenerated tissue, supporting both the efficacy and tissue persistence of sheet-based repair [8,9].

Our group has focused on polydactyly-derived chondrocytes (PD cells) as an allogeneic source of juvenile chondrocytes. PD sheets fabricated from surgical waste cartilage have a proliferative capacity, sheet integrity, and soluble factor profiles suitable for cartilage repair [10]. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses have further suggested that PD sheet efficacy is associated with ECM-, skeletal development-, and angiogenesis-related pathways and with secreted factors such as melanoma inhibitory activity (MIA) and dickkopf-related protein 1 (DKK-1) [11]. Clinically, PD sheet transplantation combined with high tibial osteotomy has been reported to improve cartilage properties in patients with knee OA and cartilage defects [12]. In parallel, vitrification methods that preserve chondrocyte sheet structure and viability after rewarming have been developed, highlighting active progress toward clinically practical manufacturing and distribution [13].

To reduce the invasiveness of sheet therapy, injectable PD sheet-minis were developed using RepCell culture dishes containing 3 mm × 3 mm grid walls on a temperature-responsive surface. These sheet-minis are designed to be deliverable through a fine needle, and their structural preservation after passage through a 23-gauge needle was demonstrated in our previous study [14]. These constructs can be delivered through a needle and retain several of the characteristics of conventional PD sheets. However, it remains uncertain whether PD sheet-minis can achieve cartilage repair after intra-articular administration, as dispersion in the synovial fluid and insufficient retention at the defect site may limit its efficacy. Recent studies on juvenile chondrocyte sheets from different donors have also demonstrated substantial donor-dependent variation in histological repair efficacy, emphasizing the importance of evaluating both formulation and potency when new sheet formats are developed [15].

Materials and methods

Ethics statements

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Institutional Animal Experiment Guidelines of Tokai University. The animal protocol was approved by the Tokai University Animal Experiment Committee (approval no. 211045). The use of human cartilage tissue derived from polydactyly surgery was approved by the Tokai University School of Medicine Clinical Research Review Committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the Japanese Ethical Guidelines for Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects, and informed consent was obtained from the tissue providers or their legal guardians, as appropriate. Privacy and confidentiality were maintained throughout this study.

Animals

Twenty-four 12-week-old male athymic nude rats (F344/NJcl-rnu/rnu; CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan) were used in this study. The animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with two rats per cage and free access to standard rodent chow and water. The rats were acclimatized for 6 weeks before surgery. To reduce measurement stress during pain-related behavioral testing, the rats were trained on an incapacitance tester (Linton Instrumentation, Diss, Norfolk, England) every other day for 2 weeks before surgery. Isolation and culture of PD cells

Cartilage tissue was obtained from the discarded surgical tissue of patients undergoing polydactyly surgery at Tokai University Hospital. Cartilage was minced with scissors and digested for 1.5 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air using Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium/Ham's F-12 (DMEM/F12) (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (SAFC Biosciences, Lenexa, KS, USA), 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (AB) (Gibco) and 5 mg/mL type I collagenase (CLS1; Worthington, Mannheim, Germany). The resulting cell suspension was washed and filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer (BD Falcon, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Isolated chondrocytes were seeded at 1 × 104 cells/cm2 in six-well culture plates (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) in DMEM/F12 containing 20% FBS and 1% AB and were designated as PD cells at passage 0. Ascorbic acid (100 μg/mL; Nissin Pharmaceutical, Yamagata, Japan) was added from day 4, and the medium was changed every 3 or 4 days. When the cells reached subconfluence, they were collected by trypsinization and cryopreserved in STEM-CELLBANKER (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) as PD cells at passage 1. For subsequent experiments, cryopreserved cells were thawed, expanded, and used to prepare PD sheet, PD sheet-mini, or PD cell suspensions. The passage number was incremented by one for each trypsinization step.

Fabrication of PD sheets and PD sheet-minis

PD sheets and PD sheet-minis were fabricated using temperature-responsive culture inserts (CellSeed Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and RepCell plates (CellSeed Inc.), respectively. RepCell is a temperature-responsive culture plate based on UpCell technology with 3 mm × 3 mm grid walls on the culture surface [14]. The culture surface areas were 4.2 cm2 for the temperature-responsive insert and 8.8 cm2 for the RepCell plate. Thawed PD cells (passage 3) were expanded once and divided at passage 4 into three portions for PD sheet fabrication, PD sheet–mini fabrication, and comparator PD cell preparation. For PD sheet fabrication, PD cells seeded onto temperature-responsive culture inserts at 0.75 × 104 cells/cm2. The medium was changed every 3 days from day 4, and the cells were cultured for 14 days. For PD sheet collection, the culture inserts were maintained at room temperature for 30 min to promote sheet detachment. For PD sheet-mini fabrication, PD cells were seeded onto RepCell plates at 0.75 × 104 cells/cm2, cultured for 14 days with medium changes every 2 days from day 4, and detached by keeping the plates at room temperature for 30 min. For preparation of PD cells used as the cell-suspension comparator, PD cells (passage 4) were expanded in 150 cm2 flasks (Corning) at a density of 1 × 104 cells/cm2, and passaged twice upon reaching subconfluence and subsequently cryopreserved until use. Before use, the thawed cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and suspended in the same vehicle as the PD sheet-minis.

Cell number and viability

The PD sheet-minis were washed with PBS, incubated with trypsin for 15 min at 37 °C, centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, incubated with collagenase P (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) for 30 min at 37 °C, and centrifuged again. The PD cells prepared during PD sheet-mini fabrication were collected by trypsinization. Immediately after recovery, the PD cell and PD sheet-mini suspensions were adjusted to obtain comparable total cell numbers. Cell number and viability were measured using trypan blue exclusion. To evaluate the stability after preparation for injection, PD cell and PD sheet-mini suspensions were mixed 1:1 with hyaluronic acid, kept at room temperature for 6 h, washed with PBS, dispersed as described above, and counted again.

Flow cytometry

After cell counting, isolated cells were washed with DPBS containing 0.2% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 1 mmol/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Gibco). Approximately 5 × 106 cells were incubated with the antibodies listed in Supplementary Table S1 for 90 min at 4 °C. Fluorophore-conjugated mouse IgG1, rat IgG2a, rat IgG2b, rat IgG3, and rat IgM antibodies were used as negative controls. The stained cells were analyzed using a FACSVerse cell sorter or FACSLyric flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Five donors were analyzed, and each donor was measured once.

Soluble factor assays

PD sheet-minis and PD cells were prepared with comparable cell numbers and suspended in 0.3 mL saline. Each suspension was dropped onto an adherent culture dish, and 3 mL of DMEM/F12 containing 5% FBS and 1% AB was added. After 72 h of culture, supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min to remove cell debris. transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)1, MIA, endothelial cell-specific molecule 1 (ESM-1), DKK-1, matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-3 and MMP-13 were measured using single-sample enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits: human TGF-β1 Quantikine ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA); MIA ELISA (Roche); and human ESM-1, DKK-1, MMP-3 and MMP-13 ELISA kits (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) was measured using the Human MCP-1 Flex Set (BD Biosciences) and a FACSLyric flow cytometer. Signals detected in blank medium containing 5% FBS were subtracted to adjust for FBS-derived proteins. Four donors were analyzed. Each donor was measured at least twice, and the mean value was used. Soluble factor production was calculated as the amount corresponding to one intra-articular injection dose.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

PD sheet-minis cultured for 14 days and PD cells passaged twice during sheet-mini fabrication were placed in TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and homogenized using a SHAKE Master Neo device (Bio Medical Science, Tokyo, Japan). Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Complementary DNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen) with incubation at 42 °C for 15 min and 95 °C for 3 min on a SimpliAmp Thermal Cycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using an Applied Biosystems 7300 real-time PCR system or QuantStudio 3 system (Applied Biosystems/Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan). The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Four donors were analyzed; each donor was measured twice, and the mean values were used to calculate the relative expression in the PD sheet-minis versus PD cells. GAPDH was used as the reference gene.

Preparation of transplant materials

For the in vivo study, the cell source was donor-matched across the PD cell, PD sheet-mini, and intact PD sheet groups so that the three preparations within comparison originated from the same donor-lot. A representative donor-lot of PD cells at passage 4 were divided into three parts and each used to prepare PD cells, PD sheet-minis, and intact PD sheets as described in Section 2.4. For intact PD sheet transplantation, one half-dish equivalent of a PD sheet (2.1 cm2) was used per knee. The insert membrane was transferred to a dish and then cut in half with a scalpel prior to in vivo transplantation. For PD cell and PD sheet-mini groups, because these formulations did not form homogeneous suspensions in saline alone, suspensions of PD sheet-minis or PD cell expanded to passage 6 were prepared in a 1:1 mixture of saline and hyaluronic acid (10 mg/ml, Chugai Pharma). Specifically, 15 μL saline contained a cell number equivalent to that in 2.1 cm2 of PD sheet; 15 μL hyaluronic acid was added to achieve a total injection volume of 30 μL. The vehicle group received the same saline/hyaluronic acid mixture without cells.

The viable cell number of PD sheet was not determined directly from the implanted sheet. Instead, it was estimated from a companion PD sheet fabricated in parallel from the same donor lot under identical culture conditions, which was enzymatically dissociated (trypsin followed by collagenase P) and counted by trypan blue exclusion, as detailed in Section 2.5. The reported value represents an estimate derived from a companion sheet and sheet-minis rather than a direct count of the transplanted materials. Rat full-thickness cartilage defect model and transplantation

Rats were anesthetized using isoflurane and oxygen inhalation. The right knee was shaved, disinfected, and opened under sterile conditions using a medial parapatellar approach. The patella was laterally dislocated to expose the femoral trochlea. A full-thickness cartilage defect, 2 mm in diameter and 1 mm in depth, was created on the patellar surface of the femur using a manual drill. Slight bleeding from the defect bed was confirmed before transplantation.

Rats received one of four treatments: vehicle only, PD cell suspension containing approximately 8.81 × 105 cells in 30 μL vehicle, PD sheet-mini suspension containing approximately 9.74 × 105 cells in 30 μL vehicle, or intact PD sheet containing approximately 9.44 × 105 cells. In the intact PD sheet group, the sheet was placed directly on the defect before closure. In the vehicle, PD cell, and PD sheet-mini groups, The patella was anatomically repositioned, and the quadriceps muscle and tendon were sutured, and the relevant solution was injected intra-articularly through a 23-gauge needle before skin closure. After surgery, the animals were returned to their cages without splinting or immobilization.

Pain-related weight-bearing assessment

Hindlimb weight-bearing was evaluated using an incapacitance tester (Linton Instrumentation, Diss, Norfolk, England). Measurements were obtained preoperatively and on postoperative days 1, 4, 7, 14, 21, and 28. The weight-bearing ratio of the operated hindlimb was expressed as a percentage of the total hindlimb weight-bearing; the normal distribution was approximately 50%.

Histology and immunostaining

Four weeks after transplantation, the rats were euthanized by an overdose of anesthesia, and the distal femur of the operated right knee was harvested. Specimens were fixed in 20% neutral-buffered formalin for 1 week, defatted in acetone for 1 day, decalcified in 10% EDTA (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) for 4 weeks at 4 °C, and embedded in paraffin. Sections 3 μm thick were cut parallel to the femoral shaft through the center of the cartilage defect. Sections were stained separately with hematoxylin and eosin, 0.1% Safranin O/0.08% Fast Green, or 0.05% toluidine blue, using standard protocols.

For type I collagen immunostaining, sections were antigen retrieved in 0.01 mol/L citrate buffer at 98 °C for 10 min, washed, treated with 0.3% H2O2/methanol for 15 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity, blocked with ImmPRESS normal horse serum for 60 min, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-type I collagen antibody (1:100; 1310-01; Southern Biotech). Sections were then processed using the ImmPRESS Anti-Goat Ig Reagent Kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) and visualized with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB; Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Industries). For type II collagen immunostaining, sections were treated with 0.4% pepsin in 0.2 mol/L hydrochloric acid at 37 °C for 30 min, blocked as above, incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-type II collagen antibody (1:100; F-57; Kyowa Pharma Chemical), processed with ImmPRESS Anti-Mouse Ig Reagent Kit (Vector Laboratories), and visualized with DAB.

For human vimentin staining, sections were antigen-retrieved in 0.01 mol/L citrate buffer at 98 °C for 10 min, washed, blocked with 5% normal goat serum for 10 min, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-human vimentin antibody (1:100; SP20; abcam). Sections were then treated with goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 for 60 min in the dark and mounted using VECTASHIELD containing DAPI. Images were acquired using a All-in -One Fluorescence Microscope (BZ-9000; Keyence Corp., Osaka, Japan).

Histological scoring

Histological repair was evaluated using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) histopathology scoring system and International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) II histological assessment system [16,17]. Sections were assessed independently in a blinded manner by two orthopaedic surgeons (SW and TT) and one researcher experienced in articular cartilage evaluation (ET). The mean score across the evaluators was used in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Two-group comparisons for flow cytometry, qPCR, and soluble factor assays were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by the Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cell number and viability were maintained after preparation of PD cell and PD sheet-mini suspensions

PD cell and PD sheet-mini suspensions were prepared that contained cell numbers comparable to those in half of an intact PD sheet. The estimated viable cell numbers prepared per knee were 9.44 ± 3.01 × 105 cells for intact PD sheets, 9.74 ± 1.29 × 105 cells for PD sheet-minis, and 8.81 ± 1.27 × 105 cells for PD cells (see Table 1). Immediately after recovery and after 6 h in the hyaluronic acid-containing vehicle at room temperature, the total cell number, viable cell number, and cell viability did not differ significantly between PD cells and PD sheet-minis. These findings indicated that preparation in the injection vehicle did not cause a marked short-term loss of cells or viability.

PD sheet-minis showed a surface marker profile closer to PD sheets than to PD cells

Flow cytometry showed that PD cells, PD sheets, and PD sheet-minis were negative for the hematopoietic and endothelial markers CD45 and CD31. All three preparations were positive for mesenchymal stromal cell-associated markers CD29, CD44, CD73, CD81, CD90, and CD105. Markers potentially associated with chondrocyte differentiation differed between the cell suspensions and sheet-based preparations. CD26 expression did not differ between PD sheets and PD sheet-minis but was significantly lower in PD cells. Discussion

PD sheet-minis are designed to be deliverable through a fine needle, and their structural preservation after passage through a 23-gauge needle has been demonstrated in our previous study [14]. In addition, cartilage repair following intra-articular injection was evaluated in a rat full-thickness cartilage defect model. Under the present single intra-articular delivery conditions, PD sheet-minis did not reproduce the structural cartilage repair achieved by directly transplanted intact PD sheets, indicating that delivery route and lesion-specific retention — rather than the intrinsic properties of the sheet-mini alone — may be important determinants of in vivo performance. PD sheet-minis retained several sheet-like in vitro characteristics but did not reproduce the robust cartilage repair achieved by directly transplanted PD sheets after a single intra-articular administration. The intact PD sheets generated consistent Safranin O-positive, type II collagen-positive, and human vimentin-positive repair tissue, whereas the PD cells and PD sheet-minis mainly produced fibrous repair. These results suggest that local retention at the defect site are essential for translating the favorable biological properties of PD cell sheet products into structural cartilage repair.

The in vitro results support the concept that sheet-based cultures modify the characteristics of PD cells. Flow cytometry showed that PD sheet-minis more closely resembled intact PD sheets than PD cell suspensions, particularly for CD26, CD146, CD49a, and CD166. Changes in CD26 and CD49a expression are noteworthy because cell surface markers have been used to monitor chondrocyte dedifferentiation during monolayer expansion [18,19].

The lower COL1A1 and RUNX2 expressions in the PD sheet-minis compared with that in the PD cells also suggests a less fibroblastic or osteogenic shift. COL2A1 and COL10A1 were not detected in either PD sheet-minis or PD cells under the present assay conditions, indicating that these expanded preparations did not maintain a mature articular chondrocyte transcriptional phenotype at the mRNA level. Nevertheless, directly transplanted intact PD sheets, which are reported to be negative for type II collagen under culture condition, generated type II collagen-positive repair tissue in vivo. This apparent gap may reflect redifferentiation of PD sheets within the joint environment, as indicated by the coexistence of type II collagen–positive repair and the persistence of human vimentin-positive cells. Furthermore, paracrine signaling from the transplanted PD sheets may contribute to chondrogenic differentiation of resident progenitor cells and cause type II collagen-positive repair.

The soluble factor profile further suggests that PD sheet-minis have biological properties that are potentially favorable for cartilage repair. MIA and DKK-1 secretion was higher in PD sheet-minis than in PD cells, whereas MCP-1 and MMP-3 secretion was lower. Previous studies on layered chondrocyte and PD sheets have implicated secreted factors, including MIA and DKK-1, in hyaline cartilage regeneration, sheet efficacy prediction, and skeletal development-related mechanisms of action [11,20]. Together with the present flow cytometric findings, these data suggest that PD sheet-minis retain part of the biologically active state induced by sheet culture. However, qPCR and protein secretion did not align for MMP3/MMP-3; PD sheet-minis showed higher MMP3 mRNA but lower secreted MMP-3 protein levels. A possible explanation for the divergence between increased MMP3 transcript and decreased soluble MMP-3 protein is that sheet-like culture alters the partitioning of MMP-3 between matrix-associated and soluble fractions. In a sheet-minis, MMP-3 may continue to be transcribed while an increasing proportion of the protein is sequestered within or bound to the ECM, reducing the detectable secreted fraction despite ongoing transcription. Although matrix-bound MMP-3 was not measured directly, this interpretation is consistent with the matrix biology associated with cell-sheet-based preparations. Importantly, MMP-3 is not simply a catabolic marker in this context; secretion of MMP-3 by expanded chondrocytes has been reported to predict ectopic cartilage formation capacity in vivo [21]. Therefore, MMP-3 results should be interpreted carefully and should not be reduced to a binary favorable/unfavorable marker.

Despite these in vitro advantages, PD sheet minis did not achieve consistent cartilage repair after a single intra-articular injection. The most plausible explanation for this is insufficient retention at the defect site. In previous orthotopic models, layered human chondrocyte sheets and juvenile chondrocyte-derived sheets were directly applied to the defect, yielding human cell-positive hyaline-like repair tissue [8,9]. Clinical studies of autologous and PD chondrocyte sheets likewise used direct placement onto prepared cartilage lesions rather than free intra-articular administration [7,12]. In the present study, intact PD sheets were similarly placed onto the full-thickness defect before closure and remained locally detectable as human vimentin-positive tissue at 4 weeks. In contrast, PD cells and PD sheet-minis were injected into the closed joint cavity in a hyaluronic acid-containing vehicle. Even if the injected constructs were viable, they could disperse throughout the joint, become trapped in recesses, adhere to synovium, or be cleared from the defect surface before forming stable cell-matrix interactions. Thus, the injection feasibility did not ensure lesion-specific delivery.

The contrast between the PD sheet-minis and intact PD sheets also emphasizes the functional importance of sheet architecture. Intact sheets preserve a contiguous ECM, cell-cell junctions, and adhesive surface, allowing immediate coverage of the defect and sustained paracrine activity at the repair site. Fragmentation into 3 mm × 3 mm sheet-minis may preserve some sheet-like characteristics and secretory characteristics; however, the fragments still require an effective delivery method to contact and remain on the lesion. The early trend toward improved weight-bearing in the injected groups, despite limited histological repair, suggests that transient effects after injection may arise from short-term paracrine factors with analgesic effects include anti-inflammatory cytokines such as TGF-β [20] or exosomes, whereas durable structural repair likely requires sustained lesion-specific retention and continued cell–matrix interaction at the defect surface. Intra-articular injection alone may be better suited for diffuse symptom-modifying effects than structural reconstruction of a focal full-thickness defect. In addition, donor-dependent variability in juvenile chondrocyte sheet efficacy has been reported in athymic rat models [15], suggesting that the delivery format and lot-specific potency may influence the final regenerative outcome.

These findings refine the development pathway for injectable PD sheet-minis. Rather than abandoning the concept, the data indicate that delivery and retention must be optimized. Potential strategies include image-guided local deposition onto the defect, repeated dosing, combination with adhesive or self-assembling biomaterials, use of hydrogels that gel in situ, and arthroscopic placement of sheet-minis under direct visualization. In addition, recent progress in chondrocyte-sheet vitrification suggests that the logistical disadvantages of intact-sheet therapy may be mitigated by improved preservation and transport technologies [13]. Future studies should include early biodistribution analysis after injection, quantitative assessment of retention, dose-response experiments, and longer observation periods to determine whether repeated or locally retained sheet-minis can approach the regenerative effect of intact PD sheets.

This study had several limitations. First, it used a xenogeneic orthotopic model with human PD cells in athymic nude rats; immune interactions, joint size, and loading conditions differ from the intended human allogeneic setting. Second, the sample size was small, and donor-to-donor variation in PD cell properties was not fully addressed. This issue may be important because donor-dependent variability in juvenile chondrocyte sheet repair efficacy has been previously documented [15]. Third, the observation period was limited to 4 weeks; therefore, long-term maturation, durability, and integration of repair tissue were not evaluated. Fourth, the study did not directly quantify the early biodistribution or local retention after injection, which is central to the proposed mechanism of limited efficacy. Fifth, mechanical testing, MRI, and quantitative histomorphometry were not performed. Finally, only single-dose administration was examined. These limitations should be addressed before clinical translation of intra-articular PD sheet-mini therapy.

Conclusions

PD sheet-minis preserved several in vitro characteristics of intact PD sheets and differed from PD cell suspensions in terms of surface marker expression, soluble factor secretion, and gene expression. However, single intra-articular administration of PD sheet-minis did not reproduce the cartilage repair achieved by direct transplantation of intact PD sheets in a rat full-thickness cartilage defect model. These findings suggest that lesion-specific retention and persistence, rather than injectability alone, may be important determinants of structural cartilage repair by PD cell-sheet products. Optimization of delivery methods, local retention strategies, and dosing schedules is necessary before injectable PD sheet-minis can be advanced as a less invasive regenerative therapy for cartilage defects.
