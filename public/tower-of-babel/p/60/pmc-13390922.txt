Global surveillance quality gaps in antimicrobial resistance monitoring: a composite-index and inequality analysis of 85 countries
Authors: Oluwaseun E Agboola, Samuel S Agboola, Margaret A Adekanle, Ajibola O Akinloye, Zainab A Ayinla, Bolade A Fatukasi, Temitope C Aribigbola, Aniefon S Umoekam, Oluwatoyin A Oyeniran, Victoria O Adenigba, Anuoluwapo B Shaleye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, antimicrobial resistance surveillance generates extensive data, yet the quality of the infrastructure producing it is rarely assessed systematically, even though this is essential for interpreting resistance trends and targeting improvements.MethodsWe evaluated surveillance across 85 countries using WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System bloodstream infection data from 2018 to 2023. A composite Surveillance Quality Index combined six dimensions weighted by principal component analysis. We quantified testing-coverage inequality with the Gini coefficient, used a detection-probability model to assess relative ascertainment of resistance, applied a random forest to identify which dimensions drive the index and used multi-criteria optimization to rank countries for intervention.ResultsThe index averaged 33.3 (range 9–67). Testing coverage was highly unequal: the top 20% of countries performed about 85% of global testing (Gini 0.783, 95% CI 0.690–0.832). Surveillance quality and reported resistance were not significantly associated (r = 0.13, 95% CI −0.09–0.34, P = 0.23); the weak positive direction is consistent with, but does not establish, detection bias, whereby stronger systems ascertain more resistant cases. The detection model identified, in ordinal terms only, the weakest systems as least able to ascertain resistance. Because the index is a composite of its dimensions, we used the random forest descriptively: temporal consistency accounted for 66.4% of the index and sample adequacy for 22.6%. Multi-criteria optimization identified 30 countries in greatest need of strengthening.ConclusionsGlobal surveillance capacity is highly unequal, with the largest quality gaps in high-burden settings; targeted strengthening would address most of the disparity.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance is among the foremost threats to modern medicine. The Global Burden of Disease analysis led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation estimated that 1.27 million deaths were directly attributable to bacterial resistance in 2019, a toll exceeding that of HIV/AIDS or malaria, and this burden is projected to grow without sustained, coordinated intervention spanning diagnostics, stewardship and infection control.1 Resistant bloodstream infections are especially lethal, with mortality exceeding 40% even where supportive care is available.2 International programmes now monitor resistance patterns, but the quality of the surveillance infrastructure that produces these data is seldom evaluated in a systematic way.

National laboratories submit resistance data to international platforms, principally the WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS), which consolidates prevalence estimates across countries and pathogens.3 Since its launch in 2015, GLASS has enrolled more than 120 countries and assembled the most comprehensive resistance database available.4 These platforms describe where and when resistance occurs, but they do not evaluate whether a given system can detect resistance accurately. Because laboratory capacity varies widely, reported prevalence can diverge substantially from the true burden, and low-quality systems may miss emerging organisms or underestimate their prevalence until containment is no longer feasible.5,6 The reliability of surveillance therefore governs how early and how equitably resistance can be managed. This dependence was made vivid during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the capacity of surveillance systems to detect and track a pathogen rapidly proved decisive for outbreak control.7

Surveillance performance depends on several interacting factors. Laboratory capacity constrains how many pathogen–antibiotic combinations can be tested; automated susceptibility testing and trained personnel are unevenly distributed; and competing health priorities limit funding in lower-income settings.8,9 Representative sampling across large or heterogeneous territories adds further difficulty. These interacting determinants call for composite metrics and analytic tools that can weight them appropriately. Methods developed in other fields offer a path forwards. Inequality metrics, such as the Gini coefficient, quantify how unevenly a resource is distributed10,11; machine learning predicts system behaviour from observable features and can flag systems at risk of failure12; and multi-criteria optimization reconciles competing objectives, such as cost, impact and feasibility to guide resource allocation.13

Despite the centrality of surveillance to resistance control, no widely adopted framework quantifies surveillance quality itself, separates genuine epidemiological signal from detection artefact or translates quality gaps into ranked, actionable priorities. This study addresses that gap. Using GLASS bloodstream infection data from 85 countries spanning from 2018 to 2023, we (i) develop a composite Surveillance Quality Index from six measurable dimensions, (ii) quantify testing-coverage inequality, (iii) test whether low-quality systems systematically underestimate resistance and (iv) build a multi-criteria tool that ranks countries for targeted strengthening. Rather than cataloguing resistance prevalence, this analysis evaluates the measurement apparatus that produces prevalence estimates and identifies where investment would most improve the equity and reliability of global monitoring.14,15

Methods

Data sources

We analysed bloodstream infection data from the WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System for the 2018–23 reporting period.16 The dataset contained resistance rates, testing volumes, year-on-year reporting and antimicrobial susceptibility testing results submitted by national reference laboratories. After applying the GLASS quality criteria, 85 countries were retained for analysis.

Surveillance quality index development

For each country, we computed six quality dimensions. Data completeness was the percentage of the 64 possible pathogen–antibiotic combinations for which interpretable susceptibility results were reported. The 64 combinations comprised the eight GLASS priority bacterial pathogens (Acinetobacter spp., Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, non-typhoidal Salmonella spp., Shigella spp., Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pneumoniae) crossed with eight antibiotic groups, so that a value of 100% would denote reporting of every combination. Temporal consistency was the proportion of the six surveillance years (2018–23) in which a country submitted data, expressed as a percentage, so that reporting in all six years scored 100% and reporting in a single year scored about 17%. Sample adequacy was the average number of isolates tested per combination. Pathogen coverage was the number of priority pathogens reported relative to the eight-pathogen panel. Antibiotic panel breadth was the number of antimicrobial agents tested. Data quality used the coefficient of variation of reported resistance as a consistency proxy.

We standardized the six dimensions and applied principal component analysis, using the first principal component loadings as weights normalized to sum to unity, to form the composite Surveillance Quality Index.17,18 Bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations generated 95% CIs for the index scores, and internal consistency was assessed with Cronbach’s alpha. Coverage gap analysis

We measured testing-coverage inequality with Gini coefficients computed from the total isolates tested per country.19,20 Lorenz curves plotted cumulative testing volume against the cumulative proportion of countries, and bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations provided CIs. Countries with coverage below 25% of the median were classified as blind spots requiring intervention.

Surveillance–resistance relationship

We examined whether surveillance quality was associated with reported resistance, quantifying the association by the Pearson and Spearman correlations with 95% CIs and a two-sided P value. Separately, detection probability was modelled as a monotone (logistic) function of the quality score to describe the relative ascertainment of resistance across systems. Because no external measurement of true prevalence is available, this model has no anchor for its absolute scale: only the ordering of ascertainment across countries is identifiable, not any percentage-point quantity. We therefore report relative, ordinal under-ascertainment only and apply no numerical correction to observed prevalence. Bootstrap resampling generated intervals for the ordering.21,22

Decomposition of the Surveillance Quality Index

Because the Surveillance Quality Index is a principal component composite of the six dimensions, a model relating the index to those same dimensions recovers a deterministic function of its own inputs and cannot be read as out-of-sample prediction. We therefore used a random forest descriptively, to quantify by permutation importance how much each dimension contributes to the index, not to predict an external outcome.23,24 As a genuine test of predictive value, we separately used the six dimensions to predict two quantities independent of the index, testing coverage and reported resistance, with 5-fold cross-validated R-squared.

Multi-criteria prioritization

We constructed a composite priority score from three criteria: the surveillance quality gap (100 minus the current score), resistance burden (mean prevalence) and intervention feasibility (proxied by current testing volume).25,26 The criteria were standardized and weighted 0.4, 0.4 and 0.2, respectively, on the basis of expert judgement. We ranked countries by priority score to identify those most in need of intervention.

Statistical analysis

We performed all analyses in Python 3.10 with NumPy, Pandas, SciPy, and scikit-learn. Tests were two-tailed with a significance threshold of 0.05, and we report 95% CIs where applicable. All code and analytic methods are available from the corresponding author.

Results

Surveillance quality patterns

The Surveillance Quality Index varied widely across countries (Figure 1a). The mean score of 33.3 indicates that most national systems operate well below the maximum observed capacity, with scores ranging from 9 to 67. Bootstrap analysis produced narrow CIs, supporting the stability of the scores. The first principal component explained 53.7% of the variance, supporting the use of a single composite index.

Global surveillance quality landscape. (a) Country rankings by Surveillance Quality Index for the highest-scoring countries, with bootstrap 95% CIs. (b) Regional profiles across the six quality dimensions. (c) Distribution of index scores with the mean and quartiles.

The dimensions behaved differently (Figure 1b). Temporal consistency varied most, because some countries reported in all 6 years while others reported in only one. Data completeness and sample adequacy also differed substantially across countries. Regional analysis showed within-region variation comparable to the differences between regions (Figure 1c).

Country rankings separated clear leaders from laggards (Figure 1d). The top performing systems scored above 60, while the bottom quintile averaged below 20. Quality clustered geographically rather than being randomly distributed.

Coverage inequality

The Gini coefficient for testing coverage was 0.783 (95% CI 0.690–0.832) (Figure 2a). Lorenz analysis showed that the top 20% of countries performed about 85% of all testing, while the bottom 20% performed less than 1%. This concentration means that a small number of well-resourced laboratories account for most global testing.

Testing-coverage inequality. (a) Lorenz curve of testing volume with the Gini coefficient. (b) Surveillance quality against testing volume with a fitted trend. (c) Geographic blind spots with the lowest testing volumes.

Coverage and quality were positively correlated (Pearson r = 0.58, 95% CI 0.42–0.71, P < 0.001; Spearman rho = 0.83) (Figure 2b). Countries with stronger infrastructure tested more specimens and reported broader pathogen panels, reinforcing their advantage.

We identified 21 countries as geographic blind spots, with testing volumes below the detection threshold (Figure 2c). These settings tested at low volume and reported at irregular intervals, which prevented reliable prevalence estimation. Most blind spots occurred in regions with high infectious disease burden, producing systematic monitoring gaps where they matter most.

Surveillance quality and reported resistance

Surveillance quality and reported resistance were not significantly associated (Pearson r = 0.13, 95% CI −0.09–0.34, P = 0.23; Spearman rho = 0.18, P = 0.10) (Figure 3a). The direction was weakly positive, with stronger systems tending to report somewhat higher resistance. We present this as a hypothesis consistent with detection bias, whereby better-resourced systems test more specimens and use more sensitive methods and so ascertain a larger share of resistant cases, rather than as an established association; the data neither confirm nor exclude the null, and we do not interpret the relationship as causal.

Surveillance quality and reported resistance. (a) Reported resistance against surveillance quality; the association is weak and non-significant (r = 0.13, P = 0.23). (b) Modelled detection probability as a monotone function of quality, identifiable only up to ordering. (c) Relative (ordinal) ascertainment across the quality range. (d) Countries with the lowest estimated ascertainment.

A detection-probability model expressed ascertainment as a monotone increasing function of surveillance quality (Figure 3b). Because no external measurement of true prevalence is available, only the ordering of ascertainment is identifiable from these data, not its absolute scale; we therefore report relative ascertainment and no percentage-point quantity. On this ordinal basis, the weakest-quality systems are estimated to ascertain the smallest fraction of resistant cases and the strongest the largest, so reported prevalence in low-quality, high-burden settings is likely to understate the true burden in direction, though by an amount these data cannot fix (Figure 3c).

Countries with the lowest estimated ascertainment tended to report only moderate resistance (Figure 3d). Because the weakest surveillance often coincides with the highest burden, reported prevalence may understate the true scope of resistance precisely where it is greatest; we frame this as a measurement hypothesis to be tested against external prevalence benchmarks rather than as a quantified correction.

Drivers of the Surveillance Quality Index

Because the Surveillance Quality Index is a composite of its six dimensions, a model relating the index to those dimensions necessarily recovers a near-deterministic function of its own inputs (apparent cross-validated R-squared 0.966); this reflects the construction of the index rather than genuine prediction, and we therefore report it only as a descriptive decomposition (Figure 4a). When the same six dimensions were instead used to predict quantities independent of the index, cross-validated performance was weak (testing coverage R-squared −0.28; reported resistance R-squared 0.11), confirming that the dimensions summarize reporting structure rather than forecasting external surveillance outcomes.

(a) Apparent model fit when the index is related to its own six constituent dimensions, shown as a descriptive decomposition rather than prediction. (b) Contribution of each dimension to the index, dominated by temporal consistency. (c) Reconstructed versus observed index values. (d) Separation of high- from low-index countries.

Within this decomposition, temporal consistency was the dominant contributor, accounting for 66.4% of the index, followed by sample adequacy at 22.6%; the remaining dimensions contributed little, and pathogen coverage did not vary across the bloodstream subset analysed and therefore contributed negligibly (0.0%) (Figure 4b).

Index values reconstructed from the six dimensions necessarily tracked the composite closely, reflecting this deterministic relationship rather than independent predictive validity (Figure 4c and d); we therefore make no claim that surveillance quality can be gauged without measurement of its dimensions.

Policy framework

The analysis yielded evidence-based recommendations (Figure 6a). Strengthening low-quality systems received the highest priority, followed by expanding testing coverage in high-burden settings, with interventions ranked by expected impact and feasibility.

Policy framework. (a) Phased implementation schedule from 2025 to 2030. (b) Modelled resource allocation summing to 100%. (c) Distribution of the 30 priority countries across WHO regions.

The projected implementation schedule proceeds in stages (Figure 6b): immediate actions from 2025 target about 30% of current quality gaps through training and equipment; short-term investments in 2026 expand service availability; medium-term work in 2027–28 focuses on sustainability; and long-term targets for 2029–30 aim for 90% of countries meeting basic standards.

Resource allocation modelling assigned 35% of funds to infrastructure, 25% to training, 20% to quality assurance, 15% to data systems and 5% to coordination (Figure 6c), balancing immediate needs against long-term capacity building.

Discussion

This analysis reveals substantial global disparities in antimicrobial resistance surveillance quality and provides computational tools to support evidence-based improvement. Three findings stand out.

First, surveillance quality varies more than is generally recognized. The roughly 7-fold range between the best and worst performers exceeds the variation seen in most health system indicators.27,28 These differences reflect divergent, policy-driven investment in surveillance.29 Countries with sustained temporal consistency typically have dedicated funding and political commitment,30 whereas those reporting at irregular intervals often lack the institutional framework to sustain monitoring.31 The link between temporal consistency and overall quality suggests that continuous funding outperforms one-off capacity-building efforts.

Second, surveillance quality and reported resistance were not significantly associated in our data, so any surveillance–resistance paradox must be treated as a hypothesis rather than an established pattern. The weak positive direction we observed is consistent with detection bias, in which better systems detect more resistance because they look harder,32,33 but our data neither establish this association nor exclude the null.34 If detection bias operates as hypothesized, low-burden countries with strong systems could appear more affected than high-burden countries with weak systems, and resources tied to documented problems could systematically miss settings whose surveillance cannot document them.35 Testing this hypothesis would require external prevalence benchmarks that these data do not provide; in practical terms, greater standardization of measurement and reporting would make resistance estimates more comparable across countries.36

Third, the decomposition of the index clarifies what it measures rather than offering a predictive shortcut. Because the index is a composite of its six dimensions, the high apparent accuracy of a model relating the two is a property of that construction, not evidence that quality can be inferred without measurement; accordingly, we do not claim that surveillance capacity can be gauged without audits. What the decomposition does show is that temporal consistency and sample adequacy dominate the index, so sustained multi-year reporting and adequate per-combination sampling are the dimensions whose improvement would most raise measured quality.37

Several limitations apply. The data cover bloodstream infections only, although the patterns may extend to other infection types with similar surveillance requirements.38 Country-level aggregation hides within-country variation, which can be large in geographically diverse nations.39 The 6-year window provides reasonable but not definitive temporal coverage; longer series would strengthen trend estimates. Quality metrics rely on reported data and cannot capture systems that never reported. In addition, pathogen coverage did not vary across the analysed bloodstream subset and so did not contribute to the composite, and the ordinal detection-bias ranking depends on the assumed monotone detection model and should be read as indicative of direction only, with no numerical correction applied.

The framework extends readily to other infectious disease surveillance systems. Tuberculosis, HIV, and vaccine-preventable disease monitoring face similar quality challenges,40,41 and our methods could evaluate their infrastructure systematically. The multi-criteria optimization approach generalizes to any resource allocation problem with measurable outcomes and constrained budgets.42

The policy implications follow directly. Because quality gaps concentrate in high-burden settings, targeted investment offers large returns.1 Allocating resources across the 30 priority countries would address most of the current inequity, and the requirements are modest relative to the disease burden, with many improvements achievable through existing WHO technical assistance. The principal constraint is political will rather than technical or financial capacity.

Generalizability deserves emphasis. Because the framework ranks only the 85 countries that met GLASS reporting criteria, the absence of a country from these rankings reflects insufficient or absent reporting rather than adequate surveillance. Such countries fall outside the analysis precisely because their data are missing, and they may warrant even more urgent attention than those that appear. The priorities should therefore be read as relative rankings among reporting countries, not as an exhaustive global list.

Future work should extend the temporal window and validate the findings prospectively, tracking whether countries achieve quality gains and whether those gains translate into more accurate resistance estimates.3 Integrating genomic surveillance would show whether quality gaps delay detection of emerging resistant lineages.4 Cost-effectiveness analysis would quantify the returns on surveillance investment relative to other options.

In summary, global antimicrobial resistance surveillance shows large quality differences, with the weakest monitoring concentrated in high-burden settings. Computational assessment enables evidence-based decisions about where to strengthen infrastructure, and targeted investment in the identified countries would yield the greatest gains in global monitoring capacity.

Conclusions

Antimicrobial resistance surveillance quality varies substantially across countries, with systematic gaps concentrated in high-burden settings. Computational methods reveal evidence-based priorities for strengthening infrastructure. Targeted investment in the 30 identified countries could resolve most of the inequity in monitoring capacity, directing improvement to where it is most needed.
