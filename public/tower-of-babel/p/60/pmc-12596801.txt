Homogeneous groups are required to assess the effect of unilateral thalamotomy by Gamma Knife radiosurgery on refractory trigeminal neuralgia
Authors: Josef Finsterer
Published: 2025

Dear Editor

We were interested to read the article by Leal-Isaza et al. on a retrospective observational study of 50 patients with trigeminal neuralgia (TN) treated with Gamma Knife radiosurgery (GKS) of the ipsilateral thalamus, the contralateral thalamus, or the retro-ganglionic portion of the trigeminal nerve.[1] A satisfactory response to GKS was described by 82% of patients with a reduction in pain on the visual analogue scale from 10 to 6.[1] The study is appealing, but some points should be discussed.

The first point is that in 82% of the 50 patients, not only a thalamotomy was performed using GKR, as the title suggests but also a rhizolysis using GKR.[1] In the cases in which rhizolysis was performed in addition to contralateral thalamotomy, it is not possible to decide whether thalamotomy or rhizolysis or both were responsible for the positive effect. To assess whether homolateral or combined contralateral thalamotomy plus rhizolysis is effective, these two different approaches should be analyzed separately.

The second issue is that it is not reported how many of the 50 patients received additional therapy for TN during the follow-up period after thalamotomy or rhizolysis. To assess whether thalamotomy or rhizolysis alone or in combination with other therapies for TN (microvascular decompression, percutaneous management, radiofrequency gangliolysis, Gasser ganglion block, sphenopalatine block, peripheral facial block, glycerol gangliolysis, peripheral electrical nerve stimulation, or glycerol rhizolysis) were beneficial, it is essential to provide this information.

The third point is that 24% of patients had already had a GKR before the intervention was assessed for the study.[1] Since the previous GKR was ineffective in a quarter of the patients, it should be explained why it was more effective the second time. Were structures other than the thalamus subjected to radiosurgery in the first GKR? We should also know how many of the patients in whom the first GKR was ineffective had a positive effect with the second GKR.

The fourth point is that the study had a retrospective design, which means that some of the required data may no longer be available. We should, therefore, know how missing data was handled and to what extent missing data influenced the results.

With reference to Table 1 in Isaza-Leal’s paper, we should know why “neurovascular conflict” was not categorized as a secondary cause of TN.

To summarize, this interesting study has limitations that put the results and their interpretation into perspective. Addressing these limitations could strengthen the conclusions and reinforce the message of the study. All open questions need to be clarified before readers can uncritically accept the conclusions of the study. To assess whether unilateral thalamotomy by GKR is effective in refractory TN, it is essential to study a homogeneous group of patients in whom only the thalamus and not the trigeminal nerve or sphenopalatine ganglion is treated and who have a negative history for GKR.
