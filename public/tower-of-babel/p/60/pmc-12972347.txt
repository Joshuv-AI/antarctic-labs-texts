Estimating opium use prevalence at the national and provincial levels in Iran: a modelling study
Authors: Saeed Nemati, Mehdi Hatami Goloujeh, Hossein Poustchi, Maryam Hadji, Hamideh Rashidian, Farhad Pourfarzi, Ali Fakhari Dehkharghani, Mehdi Shahmoradi, Farhad Moradpour, Mojtaba Farjam, Mohammadreza Naghipour, Nader Saki, Mohammad Reza Fattahi, Farid Najafi, Abbas Rezaianzadeh, Parvin Khalili, Fereshteh Ghorat, Moluk Hadi Alijanvand, Mahmood Moosazadeh, Rahim Khalilzadeh, Javad Harooni, Hossein Fallahzadeh, Nour Mohammad Bakhshani, Ali Akbar Haghdoost, Afarin Rahimi-Movaghar, Farin Kamangar, Reza Malekzadeh, Mahdi Sheikh, Kazem Zendehdel

ABSTRACT
AbstractOpium consumption was recently classified as carcinogenic to humans by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC). With Iran having the highest global prevalence of opium consumption, understanding the national and provincial prevalence of opium use is critical for cancer control efforts. This study integrated multiple data sources to estimate the prevalence of opium use across Iran. Using population-based cohorts and case-control studies we calculated opium use prevalence for 17 provinces of Iran. A regression model was then developed to explain geographical variation in opium use prevalence, incorporating demographic, socioeconomic, and opium-related national enforcement variables. The model was trained and validated using leave-one-out cross-validation for the 17 provinces with individual-level data, and was subsequently used to predict prevalence in the 14 remaining provinces lacking individual-level data. The age-standardized predicted prevalence of opium use among Iranian adults aged > 30 years was estimated at 5.93% (95% CI 2.76–8.20%), with a significant difference between men [10.40% (95% CI 4.69–14.30%)], and women [1.46% (95% CI 0.83–2.09%)]. Older age, lower socioeconomic status, unemployment, and higher quantities of seized opium were associated with higher opium use prevalence at provincial levels (all P-values < 0.05). Significant geographical variations in opium use prevalence were observed, with the highest rates estimated for the eastern provinces bordering Afghanistan, the world’s largest producer of opium, and the lowest rates in the northwestern provinces. The highest rates were calculated for Sistan and Baluchestan (15.00%), Golestan (13.43%), and Qom (13.07%), while the lowest were in Markazi (1.30%), Ilam (1.30%), and Zanjan (1.32%). Although the overall prevalence differed substantially between men and women, the geographic distribution patterns were similar for both. Opium consumption is highly prevalent among Iranian men and in populations residing in the eastern and southeastern provinces. Targeted public health interventions are urgently needed to reduce opium use, particularly in high-risk regions. Introduction

Opium is an addictive narcotic obtained from the poppy plant. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) monograph has recently classified opium consumption as “carcinogen to humans” with sufficient evidence linking it to cancers of the lung, larynx, and bladder, and limited evidence for cancers of the oesophagous, pancreas, pharynx, and stomach1. Around 40% of the world’s opium is consumed in Iran, making it the country with the highest prevalence of opium use globally2. Annually, an estimated 5000 incident cancer cases in Iran are attributable to opium use, accounting for 5% of all new cancer cases3. These cancers predominantly include those with poor prognoses, which impose considerable costs on the health system4. A recent study, estimated that a 10–50% reduction in the prevalence of opium use in Iran could, respectively, potentially prevent between 9000 and 50,000 new cancer cases by the year 20355.

The high prevalence of opium use and its significant impact on cancer occurrence in Iran underscore the critical importance of monitoring and controlling opium consumption in the country. However, there is no valid estimate regarding the prevalence of opium use at the national and provincial levels in Iran. Several national surveys have been conducted in this regard, but they have reported significantly lower opium use prevalence compared to rates obtained from population-based cohorts and case-control studies, some of which validated self-reported opium use data using urinary morphine metabolites6–10. Underreporting of opium use in national surveys is expected due to the illegal nature of opium use in Iran and the significant social stigma attached to it11,12.

While the results of previous epidemiologic studies have shown significant geographical variation in the prevalence of opium use in Iran, data on the prevalence of opium use remain lacking for many other provinces7. In the current study, we used a modelling approach that incorporated data from multiple population-based epidemiologic studies, as well as demographic, socioeconomic, and opium-related enforcement data across various provinces of Iran, to estimate the prevalence of opium use in provinces lacking individual-level data. This approach aims to generate more precise estimates of opium use prevalence at the national and provincial levels in Iran, which could help inform public health strategies and disease prevention policies, including cancer control.

Materials and methods

Study design

The current study consists of three phases: (i) cross-sectional analysis of individual-level data from case-control and cohort studies to obtain opium prevalence estimates for various provinces; (ii) generating, training, and validating a linear regression model with the obtained opium use prevalence estimates on a set of provincial-level demographic, socioeconomic, and opium-related enforcement data to explain the estimated inter-provincial variations in opium use prevalence; and (iii) using the developed model to predict opium use prevalence in provinces lacking individual data on opium use from epidemiologic studies. Inclusion criteria were population-based cohort and case–control studies that investigated current opium use among the adult population.

We excluded cross-sectional surveys, studies that did not include a question on opium consumption status (i.e. current vs. former consumption), and studies conducted among populations younger than 20 years.

Data sources

To estimate opium use prevalence at the provincial level for 17 provinces of Iran, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis using baseline data from six distinct large-scale population-based epidemiological studies originating from these provinces. These studies include the Golestan Cohort Study13, Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN Cohort)14, the Iranian Study of Opium and Cancer (IROPICAN)10, Pars Cohort Study15, Keram coronary artery disease risk factors study (KERCADRs)12, and the Khuzestan Comprehensive Health Study (KCHS)16. Supplementary Table 1 provides detailed information about location, gender distribution, age group, recruitment years, percentage of urban population, and raw prevalence of opium use in each included epidemiologic study. In all included studies, opium use was assessed using self-reported questionnaires. Notably, the validity of self-reported opium use was evaluated against urinary opium metabolites in two studies, the Golestan Cohort Study and the IROPICAN study, and both showed that self-reported data provided valid and reliable estimates of opium use10,17.

To explore provincial-level variables that could explain the inter-provincial variations in the prevalence of opium use, we extracted data from various national reports published by the Ministry of Interior Affairs and the National Statistics Center of Iran. These variables covered different socioeconomic and demographic aspects such as the number of divorces, welfare index18, poverty index19, average age20, unemployment rate21, human development index (HDI)18, and literacy rate22. To further enrich our analysis, we incorporated information from the National Anti-Narcotics Committee report23. This included data on the quantity of seized drugs, the number of individuals apprehended in connection with drug trafficking and consumption, and the count of people in prisons related to drug offences23. We also extracted data on the prevalence of current opium use from the latest wave of the Iranian Mental Health Survey6, and tobacco smoking prevalence from the last round of the stepwise approach to surveillance (STEPS) survey in Iran24. Additionally, we extracted national statistics data, encompassing age and gender distribution, population size, and number of urban residents for each province. These data were extracted from the last round of national census in 2016 in Iran20.

Ethics approval

All study participants in the included epidemiologic studies had provided written informed consent prior to their enrolment in these studies. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Reference: IR.TUMS.IKHC.REC.1402.378). All research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Estimation of opium use prevalence for provinces with individual-level epidemiologic data

From each epidemiologic study, we extracted information on current use of opium, defined as having smoked or ingested opium in the past month. To calculate the prevalence of opium use, we initially computed the crude rates separately for rural and urban populations in each province across various studies. These point estimates were then adjusted by applying weights based on the province’s urban and rural population sizes to address any selection bias related to the place of residence. In instances where multiple estimates for a given province originated from different sources, we assigned weights to these estimates proportional to the size of the respective study. Moreover, when a province encompassed more than one city, we further weighted the estimated point prevalence based on the population size of each city. For example, in the province of Fars, where we had three estimates from Pars Cohort15, PERSIAN Cohort14, and the IROPICAN study10 which were conducted in Shiraz, Baladeh, and Fasa cities, respectively, we initially standardized the estimates based on the rural and urban population sizes of the Fars province, and then applied additional weights based on the population size of each city. If the samples from different studies were drawn from the same city within a province, we applied weights solely based on the size of each study. We performed direct age standardization using the Iranian population from the 2016 national census as the standard population. Age- and gender-specific prevalence estimates of opium use were post-stratified using the population size within each age-gender stratum for each province. Since our study population included only individuals aged 30 years and older, the standardization process was also restricted to the ≥ 30-year-old population. Specifically, we used the age and gender distribution of the Iranian population aged 30 and above (from the 2016 census) as the reference population to compute standardized prevalence estimates.

Generating, training, and validating a regression model for predicting opium use

We used a linear regression model to explain the obtained inter-provincial variations in the prevalence of opium use in 17 provinces where individual-level epidemiological data were available. In this model, the estimated point prevalence of opium use from the previous phase was fit as the dependent variable, and a set of provincial-level demographic, socioeconomic, and opium-related enforcement variables were fit as the covariates. A linear regression model was employed, where all potential covariates were initially included in a simple regression model. Variables with a P-value less than 0.2 were retained for further analysis and subsequently entered into a multiple linear regression model.

The final model for men included the following variables: unemployment rate, welfare index, population size of province, average age of the province, regional divisions, and amount of seized drugs. For women, the final model included HDI, smoking prevalence (from STEPS survey), average age, geographical region, urbanization rate, estimates on prevalence of opium use (from Iranian Mental Health Survey), and amount of seized drugs. We included several socio-demographic predictors to explore provincial variations in opium use. Both HDI and the Welfare Index were extracted from Afghe (2020)18, which estimated provincial HDI values using a fuzzy logic model based on health, education, income, and living standard indicators. The HDI theoretically ranges from 0 to 1; in our data, provincial values ranged from approximately 0.68 to 0.82. The Welfare Index, used as one of the components of the HDI model in this study, reflects household-level living conditions and was reported separately for each province.

The models were trained using data from 17 provinces, where we had estimated opium prevalence in the first phase of the study. For validation, we used the leave-one-out-cross-validation (LOOCV) approach, where the regression model was repeated 17 times and each time one observation was dropped. We tested the validity of the model for predicting opium use prevalence using LOOCV R-square, mean-square error, and bias errors, separately among men and women.

Predicting opium use prevalence in provinces lacking individual-level epidemiologic data

We used the data and models that were obtained from the previous two steps to predict the prevalence of opium use in provinces lacking individual-level epidemiologic data. Predicted values were constrained between 1% and 25% for men and 0% to 5.0% for women. These upper limits were selected based on our individual-level analysis of illicit drug use, which identified Sistan and Baluchestan as having the highest prevalence of opium use in Iran. Epidemiologic data showed an opium use prevalence of 25% among men and 5.0% among women, which were consequently used as the upper limit to avoid generating values higher than those observed in this province. The lower limit was set at 1% for men and 0.01% for women to prevent the generation of negative values. To calculate the 95% confidence interval, a simulation method was employed, wherein repeated draws for gender-specific prevalence estimates were generated to account for uncertainty in this domain. We replicated the simulation 1000 times for each gender-specific prevalence in every province.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and Python version 3.11.

Results

Provincial prevalence of opium use based on individual-level epidemiologic data

The overall prevalence of opium use, from individual-level epidemiologic data, was estimated at 6.75% (95% CI: 4.38–9.11), with a notable gender disparity (12.14% among men vs. 1.35% among women). The highest overall prevalence rates were obtained for Sistan and Balouchestan (16.21%), followed by Golestan (13,66%), Kerman (12.13%), and Khorasan Razavi (9.64%). Conversely, the lowest prevalence rates were obtained at 1.41% in East Azerbaijan, followed by Ardabil (1.50%), Kermanshah (1.58%), and Khuzestan (1.60%). These findings underscore the regional variations in opium use in Iran, with certain areas showing notably higher prevalence rates than others (Table 1).

Factors associated with the prevalence of opium use

Among men, in the linear regression model, the welfare index demonstrated a strong inverse correlation with opium use (β = -187.21, 95% CI: -215.71 to -158.67), while residing in larger cities, including medium-sized cities (β = 8.92, 95% CI: 5.84 to 11.99), and megacities (β = 6.84, 95% CI: 4.12 to 9.57), unemployment rate (β = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.40 to 2.34), and the amount of drugs seized (β = 3.12, 95% CI: 0.50 to 5.74) exhibited a significant positive correlation with opium use prevalence. Furthermore, older provinces were associated with higher probabilities of opium consumption. Each one-year increase in the average age of provinces corresponded to a 2.84% increase in the prevalence of opium use (95% CI: 2.15% to 3.53%). We observed positive association between estimates from national surveyes and predicted values (β = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.15). (Table 2).

For women, a lower HDI emerged as the primary predictor of opium use prevalence. Provinces with the highest levels of HDI had significantly lower opium use prevalence compared to other HDI categories (β = −2.58, 95% CI −3.28 to −1.88). The amount of drugs seized showed a significant correlation with the prevalence of opium use (β = 0.86, 95% CI 0.70–1.02) (Table 2). National and provincial prevalence of opium use predicted by the regression model

Using the regression model to predict opium use for all 31 provinces, the overall prevalence of opium use among Iranian adults aged over 30 years was estimated at 5.93%, with a significant difference between genders (10.40% vs. 1.46% among men and women, respectively). Among men, the highest opium prevalence rates were obtained for Sistan and Balouchestan, and Lorestan (each at 25%), followed by Golestan (21.85%), Qom (21.14%), and Kerman (20.40%). On the other hand, the lowest rates of opium use prevalence among men were obtained for Tehran, Zanajan, Ilam, Markazi, and Semnan (each at 2.5%), followed by Ardabil (2.69%), and East Azerbaijan (2.83%). These results reflect the significant regional variations in opium use among men in Iran.

For women, while opium use also showed regional variations, the prevalence rates remained under 1% in most provinces. In Sistan and Balouchestan, South Khorasan, Qom, and Golestan, however, the rate prevalence rate reached 5.0%. Other provinces with high prevalence rates of opium use among women were Hormozgan (4.15%), Qazvin (3.40%), and Kerman (3.38%) (Table 1).

Interestingly, there was a clear geographic pattern for opium use prevalence in Iranian provinces, with opium use being notably prevalent among both men and women in the eastern provinces of Iran, bordering Afghanistan, and less prevalent in the western provinces (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study aimed to provide estimates on the prevalence of opium use among Iranian adults aged over 30 years at both national and provincial-levels, utilizing data from multiple sources including large-scale population-based epidemiological studies, as well we provincial level demographic, socioeconomic, and opium-related enforcement information from national data sources. The results show an age-standardized prevalence of opium use at 5.93% for both genders, with 10.40% for men and 1.46% for women. Older age, lower standards of living, higher unemployment, and residence in larger cities were identified as the main predictors of opium use prevalence in Iran. We also found a clear geographical pattern for opium use at the provincial level, with the highest rates observed for eastern provinces. The prevalence rates declined gradually, forming a gradient, with the lowest rates found in the western provinces.

We obtained a higher prevalence of opium use compared to the latest published national survey in Iran. The most recent national survey reported the prevalence of current opium use at 1.97%, which is significantly lower than the estimated prevalence of 6.04% in the current study7. It is important to note that our study focused on the population aged over 30 years, while the population-based surveys include those over 15 years6–8. Epidemiologic studies that included similar age groups such as the IROPICAN (14.0%)10, Tehran cohort (5.60%)25, and Golestan cohort (17.0%) also reported higher rates for opium use than the national surveys26. Therefore, the observed difference in opium prevalence rates between our study and the national surveys could partly be due to the variations in the age groups studied. However, underreporting in national surveys may have also contributed to the observed differences, as opium use is illegal in Iran and it carries a social stigma in this society11, particularly among younger age groups27. Given the household-based nature of the available national surveys, they could be more prone to underreporting bias than epidemiologic studies that have validated the self-reported opium use against urinary morphine metabolites13,28. As an example, a recent analysis in the IROPICAN study showed that underreporting opium use is significantly more prevalent among neighbourhood controls than hospital-visitor controls28.

A notable geographical pattern was observed in the provincial prevalence of opium use, with the Eastern provinces of Iran exhibiting the highest prevalence rates. This pattern is expected, as these regions share borders with Afghanistan, the largest producer of opium globally, and are known to be the major transit points for drug trafficking. Additionally, these provinces have low literacy rates, low Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita, and high unemployment rates, ranking them among the most deprived provinces in Iran29. A similar geographical patterns were also reported in previous national surveys in Iran7.

The study showed a significant association between socioeconomic status and opium use. More developed provinces, characterized by higher literacy rates, lower deprivation indices, higher standards of living, and lower unemployment rates, exhibited a lower prevalence of opium use. The deprivation index and lower standard of living can impact the prevalence of opium use through several pathways. Lower education levels are strongly associated with reduced health literacy and awareness regarding the negative health outcomes of opium use, potentially serving as the initial driver of opium use in such individuals30. Furthermore, residing in deprived areas is linked to limited access to healthcare services31. Consequently, individuals living in such areas are more likely to turn to opium as a painkiller for health-related issues and may resort to traditional medicine, where opium is frequently used as an analgesic. Furthermore, less developed areas mainly suffer from higher rates of unemployment, leading to financial hardship for many residents. These circumstances could increase the likelihood of participation in the drug trafficking process, thereby providing more convenient and less expensive access to opium in such provinces that could lead to a higher prevalence of opium use. Our findings align with previous studies conducted in the US32, which demonstrated that poverty and unemployment could increase the odds of heroin use and opioid misuse32. These studies have indicated that financial hardship and deprivation could serve as triggers for opioid misuse. Introducing economic support programs for unemployed individuals and those experiencing poverty has been suggested as a harm reduction strategy for controlling opium use32–34.

Strengths and limitations

The current study is unique in its utilization of data from multiple large-scale national epidemiologic studies in Iran, which recruited a substantial number of participants from various provinces and ethnic groups, providing the possibility to obtain opium use prevalence data across 17 out of the 31 provinces of Iran. To our knowledge, this study is the first that uses a prediction model to estimate opium use prevalence at the national and provincial levels in Iran, a country that accounts for around 40% of the global opium consumption. The integration of epidemiological data with a wide range of provincial-level national data, alongside the stringent statistical methods used for training and validating the prediction model, are key strengths of this study. However, our findings must be interpreted considering the limitations of this study. A major limitation of the current study was the unavailability of individual-level epidemiologic data from several major provinces such as the capital Tehran and Isfahan. Also, for some provinces, data were collected from small cities, which may not be fully representative of the entire province. Furthermore, due to the limited number of high-quality studies assessing the prevalence of opium use in adults aged ≥ 30 years, we included all eligible studies that reported such estimates to enhance the comprehensiveness of our analysis. The minimum age of participants in these studies ranged from 20 to 40 years, which may have slightly influenced prevalence estimates specific to adults aged ≥ 30 years. Nevertheless, including all adult populations and applying a multi-step weighting approach to generate provincial estimates, allowed us to obtain multiple provincial estimates from different data sources, improving the precision and generalizability of our findings. Although we tried to minimize reporting bias by incorporating data from several large observational studies, some of which validated self-reported opium use against urinary opium metabolites, most studies relied solely on self-reported data without external validation. Consequently, we cannot exclude the possibility of reporting bias in some cohorts, which may have led to underestimation of opium prevalence in certain provinces.

Conclusion

In summary, the prevalence of opium use in Iran appears to be significantly higher than the previously reported estimates from national surveys. The eastern provinces of the country have emerged as primary centers for opium consumption with the highest prevalence of opium use. To address and mitigate this issue, it is essential to implement comprehensive public health policies against opium use, such as strengthening enforcement against drug trafficking, improving public awareness about the health hazards of opium use, and addressing the socioeconomic conditions, particularly in the most deprived areas in the eastern provinces of Iran. These strategies may help reduce the prevalence of opium use, and subsequently decrease the burden of cancer and other chronic health conditions associated with opium use in Iran. Furthermore, given the recent classification of opium use as a human carcinogen, these findings lay the groundwork for cancer research and preventive measures as priorities for the comprehensive cancer control programs in Iran, highlighting the inclusion of opium control as a critical component, particularly in provinces with the highest prevalence of opium use.
