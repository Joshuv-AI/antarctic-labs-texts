Evaluation of pediatric patients diagnosed with psoas abscess over the last 20 years in a tertiary care hospital in Türkiye
Authors: Fatma Tuğba Çetin, Ümmühan Çay, Ömer Kaya, Duygu Özgül Özesen, Buğra Kundakçı, Asena Ünal, Gökçe Oğuz, Göksu Başargan, Özlem Özgür Gündeşlioğlu, Derya Alabaz, Nazlı Totik

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPsoas abscess is rare in children. Its nonspecific clinical presentation poses diagnostic challenges. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical and demographic characteristics, as well as the diagnostic and therapeutic processes, of 28 pediatric patients diagnosed with psoas abscess, and to provide a broader perspective for the literature.Materials and methodsThe records of 28 patients treated at a tertiary care hospital between January 1, 2005, and December 1, 2025, were reviewed. Age at admission, presenting complaints, clinical and radiological findings, and treatment regimens were analyzed retrospectively.ResultsA total of 28 pediatric patients were included over 20 years. The male-to-female ratio was 1:1. Nine patients (32.1%) were younger than 5 years of age. An underlying condition was identified in 18 patients (64.3%). Hip pain was one of the most common findings and was present in all primary cases. Fever was observed more frequently in secondary cases (54.5%) than in primary cases (16.7%). A high index of suspicion is required for diagnosis. Early initiation of antibiotic therapy and timely drainage are among the most important factors influencing prognosis. There are limited studies on this condition in children, and we believe that our study will contribute to the existing literature.Keywords: Psoas abscess, Pediatric, Treatment, Management

Introduction

Psoas abscess (PA) is a serious infection characterized by the accumulation of pus along the iliopsoas muscle [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of primary PA remains unclear. Primary psoas abscesses usually develop through hematogenous spread from an infection focus in another part of the body. The etiology of primary PA commonly includes intravenous drug use, diabetes, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, alcoholism, malnutrition, and malignancy. Secondary psoas abscesses usually develop as a result of direct spread from a neighboring infection focus. In some cases, it cannot be clearly determined whether the involvement of a neighboring structure is the cause or the consequence of the psoas muscle abscess [3].

The diagnosis of PA is challenging because its clinical presentation is variable and nonspecific [4, 5]. Although it is known to present with a classic triad of symptoms, including fever, back pain, and limping, this triad is observed in only approximately 30% of patients [6]. The clinical onset of symptoms is usually subacute, and diagnosis may be delayed for days or even weeks due to confusion with benign musculoskeletal conditions [3, 6, 7]. For the differential diagnosis of PA, a detailed medical history should be obtained, followed by a thorough physical examination and appropriate laboratory and radiological investigations.

The most common bacterial cause of primary PA is Staphylococcus aureus. In endemic regions, Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis) and Brucella spp. should also be considered in the etiology of PA [6–9].

With advances in imaging technologies, the number of reported PA cases worldwide has increased significantly. These advancements have substantially improved clinicians’ ability to diagnose PA early and manage it effectively [10]. Nevertheless, studies on PA in the pediatric population remain limited [11]. This study aims to provide a broader perspective by evaluating the clinical and demographic characteristics of patients diagnosed with PA, a rare condition associated with serious complications, as well as the diagnostic and treatment processes.

Materials and methods

Study population and data collection

Patients aged 0–18 years who were diagnosed with and followed up for psoas abscess at the Pediatric Infectious Diseases and Orthopedics and Traumatology Clinics of *** University *** Hospital between January 1, 2005 and December 1, 2025 were retrospectively included in the study. Demographic characteristics, clinical findings, laboratory and radiological data, microbiological results, treatment methods and outcomes were examined from medical records. Patients with insufficient medical records or those whose psoas abscess was not confirmed radiologically and/or surgically were excluded from the study.

Diagnosis - radiological evaluation

The diagnosis of psoas abscess was confirmed by radiological imaging and/or surgical drainage findings along with consistent clinical findings. Patients were radiologically evaluated by experienced radiologists using ultrasonography (USG), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Both CT and MRI are valuable diagnostic tools for psoas abscesses, providing useful information for early diagnosis and precise localization. However, USG may not always detect psoas abscesses [12]. Therefore, our patients were evaluated not only with ultrasound but also with MR or CT scans. The largest psoas abscess detected on CT and/or MR was measured by an experienced radiologist. Material aspirated during surgery was inoculated into abscess culture and also examined pathologically. Depending on the epidemiological history and underlying disease, tuberculin skin test or interferon-gamma release test, Brucella agglutination test, anti-human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and anti-HCV tests were performed. Threshold values were defined as white blood cell count 15,000/mm³, C-reactive protein (CRP) 8 mg/L, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 20 mm/hour.

Outcome definitions

In this study, primary psoas abscess was defined as an abscess developing as a result of hematogenous spread from an infection focus located elsewhere in the body, while secondary psoas abscess was defined as an abscess developing as a result of direct spread from an adjacent infection focus [3, 11]. Clinical remission was defined as the absence of the need for additional antimicrobial treatment or drainage after planned treatment and the disappearance of clinical signs and symptoms associated with psoas abscess. Sequelae were defined as persistent clinical, functional, or radiological abnormalities associated with psoas abscess after completion of treatment and resolution of active infection.

During the follow-ups, the first control was 7 days after discharge, the second control was 14 days later, and subsequent controls were monthly.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the research was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the **University Faculty of Medicine** Hospital, meeting number 163, dated 6.2.2026.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD when analyzed with the independent-samples t-test and as median (IQR25–75) when analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as n (%). For comparisons between two groups, independent-samples t-tests were used for approximately normally distributed continuous variables, whereas the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test was applied when expected cell counts were small. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 28 pediatric patients were included in the study over a twenty-year period. The male-to-female ratio was 1:1. Nine patients (32.1%) were younger than 5 years, and 19 (67.9%) were 5 years or older. An underlying condition was identified in 18 patients (64.3%). Immunodeficiency was detected in 6 patients (21.4%), kidney anomalies in 5 patients (17.8%), benign hematological diseases in 2 patients (7.1%), acute myeloblastic leukemia in 1 patient (3.6%), Ewing sarcoma in 1 patient (3.6%), benign bone cyst in 1 patient (3.6%), epilepsy in 1 patient (3.6%), and heart disease in 1 patient (3.6%). None of the patients presented with the classic triad of symptoms (fever, back/lower back pain, pain with hip movement). Initial symptoms included fever and limited mobility in 4 (14.3%) patients, hip pain in 11 (39.3%) patients, limited mobility in 11 (39.3%) patients, and abdominal pain in 2 (7.1%) patients. The most common findings, in descending order of frequency, were limited range of motion (82.1%), hip pain (75%), and fever (46.4%). Acute phase reactants were elevated in most patients. Anti-HIV tests were negative in all patients.

Diagnostic examinations were performed upon initial consultation. The median symptom duration for patients diagnosed with PA was 18.0 (IQR25-75: 5.0–45.0) days, and the median length of hospital stay was 38 (IQR25-75: 21.0-57.5) days (Table 1).

The median duration of intravenous treatment for patients was 38(21-58.8) days. During follow-up, 19 (67.8%) patients received oral sequential treatment. The median duration of oral treatment was 15.0 days (IQR25-75: 14.0–30.0). The total treatment duration (median) was 48.0(33.8–79.0) days. Twenty-four patients (85.7%) received treatment for at least 28 days.

The most commonly used treatment regimens included glycopeptide plus third-generation cephalosporin in 5 patients (17.8%), glycopeptide plus carbapenem in 10 (35.7%), oxazolidinone plus carbapenem in 4 (25%), oxazolidinone in combination with other agents in 3 (10.1%), carbapenem monotherapy in 1 (3.6%), rifampicin-containing combinations in 4 (14.3%), and antifungal therapy in 8 (28.6%). Four patients (25%) received anti-tuberculosis treatment. Surgical intervention was not performed in 9 patients (32.1%). During surgical procedures, additional interventions included nephrostomy in one patient, corpectomy in one patient, and removal of a urethral stent in one patient. In one patient (3.6%), M. tuberculosis polymerase chain reaction (PCR) positivity was detected in the pathology specimen, and in three patients (10.7%), pathology findings were consistent with tuberculosis. Therefore, four patients (14.3%) received anti-tuberculosis treatment for 12–18 months. Osteomyelitis developed in six patients (21.4%). Sepsis related to PA was observed in four patients (14.3%). Among these, three had underlying conditions, including two with immunodeficiency (one with Bare Lymphocyte Syndrome, one with Immunodeficiencies with Hypereosinophilia) and one with acute leukemia. In the patient without an underlying condition who developed sepsis, polymicrobial growth (A. baumannii, S. mitis, S. lugdunensis) was detected in the abscess culture. No mortality related to PA was observed.

Comparisons were made according to age groups (< 5 years and ≥ 5 years) and etiology (primary and secondary). This age limit was chosen because the immune system development is completed around the age of 5. No significant difference was found in sex distribution between age groups or between primary and secondary cases. There was no significant difference in hospital stay length between age groups, and the duration of symptoms before hospitalization was similar between the two groups. However, serum procalcitonin (PCT) levels were significantly higher in the < 5 years group compared to the ≥ 5 years group (0.6 [0.2–62.0] vs. 0.09 [0.06–0.39], p = 0.016). No significant differences were observed in other laboratory parameters. Fever was more common in the < 5 years group, whereas hip pain was more frequent in patients aged ≥ 5 years. The distribution of inguinal pain, limitation of movement, decreased appetite, weight loss, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain was similar across age groups, with no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05).

When analyzed by etiology, hospital stays were shorter in primary cases than in secondary cases, although the difference was not statistically significant. The duration of symptoms before hospitalization was comparable between primary and secondary cases, with a median of 29.5 (5.75–50.0) days in primary cases and 18.0 (4.75–41.25) days in secondary cases (p = 0.764). White blood cell counts were higher in secondary cases compared to primary cases, although the difference was not statistically significant. Similarly, absolute neutrophil counts were higher in secondary cases than in primary cases.

In comparisons by etiology, most clinical findings were similar across groups. Fever was more frequently observed in secondary cases (54.5%) than in primary cases (16.7%). Hip pain was one of the most common findings, present in all primary cases but less frequent in secondary cases (68.2%). Other clinical features, including inguinal pain, back pain, limitation of movement, weight loss, nausea or vomiting, abdominal pain, erythema, and history of trauma, did not differ significantly between primary and secondary groups (all p > 0.05). Discussion

Psoas abscesses are not common in children. Studies involving pediatric patients are limited in the literature [11]. Therefore, we believe that our study will contribute to the literature. In our study, the male-to-female ratio was 1:1, and nearly half of the patients were in the 12–18 age group. In the study conducted by Perez-Lopez et al., 12 children with psoas abscess were evaluated, and 58.4% of the patients were male; similarly to our study, the mean age was 9 years [11]. In another pediatric study, the sex distribution was similar to ours; however, unlike our study, no patients were older than 12 years [13]. Although Crohn’s disease constitutes a significant etiological factor for PA in adults, our study showed that in children, PA was more commonly observed in patients with immunodeficiency and hematological-oncological conditions [14, 15].

Although the classic clinical triad is reported in approximately 30% of PA cases, none of our patients exhibited this triad [8]. Instead, the most common findings, consistent with the literature, were limitation of movement, hip pain, and fever [15]. In another study evaluating 23 pediatric patients, inguinal pain, limitation of movement, and fever were reported as the most frequent findings [13].

Similar to the literature, Staphylococcus species were the most common pathogen in our study [15–18]. In their study, Xu et al. identified methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) as the most common pathogen [15].

All patients with polymicrobial growth had secondary PA. Although polymicrobial infections are generally associated with secondary PA, as observed in our study, some studies report a higher frequency in primary PA [15, 17]. This discrepancy may be related to differences in age groups and the heterogeneity of underlying conditions in pediatric and adult populations. Among the three patients with polymicrobial growth, one had immunodeficiency, and another had vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) with a urethral stent. We consider that the polymicrobial nature of PA in these patients may be related to recurrent urinary tract infections and VUR obstruction, as well as to immune system defects in patients with immunodeficiency. The literature also reports cases of psoas abscess secondary to urinary tract infections [19].

Our findings also indicate that atypical pathogens may cause psoas abscess. In one case of psoas abscess caused by Candida albicans, advanced immunological evaluation led to a diagnosis of immunodeficiency due to CARD9 deficiency. This finding suggests that the detection of Candida in cultures may serve as a warning sign for underlying primary immunodeficiency (Fig. 1). Half of the patients receiving anti-tuberculosis treatment had immunodeficiency. Tuberculosis should be considered in differential diagnosis, particularly in endemic regions, and not only culture but also pathology and advanced molecular methods, such as PCR, should be performed. Our results strongly emphasize that when atypical pathogens are identified, especially in pediatric patients, underlying immunodeficiency should be thoroughly investigated.

Among these patients who developed sepsis, one had acute lymphoblastic leukemia, one had bare lymphocyte syndrome, one had unclassified immunodeficiency, and one had polymicrobial growth. Although not statistically significant, we believe that both the underlying disease and the polymicrobial infection may be associated with sepsis.

The distribution of right- and left-sided PA was similar in our study. The literature also indicates comparable rates of right- and left-sided involvement. Although bilateral PA is rare, some case series have reported an increasing number of bilateral abscesses [20].

Both USG and CT are considered reliable for diagnosing PA. USG may be preferred as an initial modality due to its accessibility, low cost, and lack of radiation exposure. However, in cases where the abscess extends into the pelvis or when detailed evaluation of its extent and relationship with adjacent structures is required, CT may be necessary in pediatric patients [13, 21]. More recently, MRI has also been shown to be useful for diagnosing PA [13]. In our study, USG was used as the initial imaging modality, and additional evaluation with CT and/or MRI was performed to increase diagnostic accuracy and enable prompt diagnosis in this pediatric population.

In treatment, some authors suggest that PA with a diameter up to 30 mm, and others up to 60 mm, may be managed with specific antimicrobial therapy alone. However, the mainstay of treatment is appropriate antibiotic therapy combined with abscess drainage [22, 23]. Although percutaneous drainage guided by USG or CT is considered an appropriate initial approach in adults, it is not always feasible in pediatric patients [15, 21]. In our study, all patients were initially treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics, which were later adjusted based on culture results. No intervention was performed in nine patients (32.1%), all of whom had abscesses smaller than 60 mm in diameter. In a recent study by Khalil et al., it was suggested that PA may be managed with antimicrobial therapy alone in patients with small abscess size, normal or mildly elevated white blood cell and CRP levels, absence of comorbidities, and stable clinical condition. The same study also reported that high CRP levels, immunosuppression, and chronic renal failure were associated with poor prognosis, whereas early diagnosis, prompt antibiotic therapy, and abscess drainage were associated with favorable outcomes [24]. In our study, although 85.7% of patients had elevated CRP levels and 21.4% had immunodeficiency, no mortality was observed due to early diagnosis and timely treatment.

There is no clear consensus on the optimal duration of PA treatment. Treatment duration should be determined based on factors such as underlying disease, abscess size, white blood cell and CRP levels, presence or absence of drainage, and the overall clinical condition of the patient [3, 24].

When comparing patients aged < 5 years and ≥ 5 years, a significant difference in procalcitonin (PCT) levels was observed in the < 5 years group. Of the four patients who developed sepsis, three were younger than 5 years, and two had symptoms lasting longer than 7 days. This may be explained by factors such as the limited ability of younger children to express their symptoms, the immaturity of their immune systems, and the higher likelihood of unrecognized trauma in pediatric patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and relatively small sample size limited the ability to identify independent predictors of clinical outcomes. Second, microbiological confirmation was not available for all patients, particularly those managed without abscess drainage. In addition, anaerobic cultures and advanced molecular diagnostic methods were not routinely performed during the study period. Another important limitation is the temporal heterogeneity of the cohort, as diagnostic modalities, microbiological techniques, and treatment strategies may have evolved over the extended study period. Nevertheless, considering the rarity of psoas abscess, we believe that this study provides valuable additional data to the existing literature.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PA is a rare but serious infection that typically presents with nonspecific symptoms. A high index of suspicion is required for diagnosis. Early diagnosis is even more challenging in pediatric patients. Early initiation of antibiotic therapy and timely drainage are the most important factors affecting prognosis. When atypical pathogens are identified, the possibility of underlying severe immunodeficiency should be considered. Our study provides current data on the etiological agents, clinical and radiological features, and treatment outcomes of psoas abscesses. However, due to the limited number of studies in pediatric patients, more prospective studies are needed.
