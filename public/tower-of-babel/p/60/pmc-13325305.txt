Assessment of temporomandibular joint morphological alterations after bilateral sagittal split ramus osteotomy - A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Rasha Azees, K Sarika, Anju James, Parvathy Ghosh, NK Sapna Varma, VV Ajith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrthognathic surgery corrects skeletal deformities and restores facial and functional balance. This systematic review evaluated the effects of bilateral sagittal split ramus osteotomy (BSSRO) on temporomandibular joint (TMJ) morphology, including joint spaces, condylar height, axial angle, disc position, and muscle changes.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, a comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar was conducted. Two authors independently performed critical appraisal and data extraction. Standardized mean differences with 95% confidence intervals were used to evaluate surgical effects on TMJ morphology.ResultsA total of 26 studies were included, with ten contributing to meta-analysis. Most studies showed no significant postoperative changes in joint spaces in either malocclusion group. Two long-term studies showed a significant reduction in posterior joint space in Class II patients [SMD: 0.97 (95% CI 0.61, 1.33)], with no change in Class III patients. In Class III cases, two studies showed a significant long-term decrease in condylar height [SMD: 0.37 (95% CI 0.08, 0.66)], and three studies demonstrated a reduction in axial condylar axis angle [SMD: 0.77 (95% CI 0.44, 1.10)]. Non-pooled studies reported largely unchanged disc positions, occasional improvement in Class III cases, and inconsistent postoperative changes in masseter and pterygoid muscle morphology.ConclusionsBSSRO produces malocclusion-specific TMJ adaptations, with posterior joint space reduction in Class II, condylar remodeling in Class III, and stable disc position in both groups.Keywords: Orthognathic surgery, Temporomandibular joint, BSSRO, Joint space, Condylar adaptation, CBCT

Introduction

Orthognathic surgery plays an important role in contemporary orthodontics, offering a pivotal solution for correcting complex maxillofacial deformities.1 Its development traced back to Hullihen's report in 1849,2 with significant advancement achieved in 1955 through the introduction of the bilateral sagittal split osteotomy (BSSO) by Obwegeser and Trauner.3,4 Subsequent refinements by Dal Pont (1961) and Epker (1977) led to the evolution of the bilateral sagittal split ramus osteotomy (BSSRO), in which osteotomy cuts were confined to the mandibular ramus. This modification enhanced the alignment of the mandibular condyle and improved the integration of the proximal and distal segments of the mandible, thereby significantly advancing the outcomes of the procedure.5

The BSSRO involves a precise osteotomy of the mandibular ramus, performed above the mandibular foramen, to enable the repositioning of the mandible in a forward or backward direction. When combined with rigid internal fixation (RIF), this technique has become the predominant approach for treating mandibular skeletal deformities.6,7 This technique has resulted in marked improvements in both the functional and aesthetic outcomes for patients, addressing complex orthodontic needs.5,8

While the BSSRO technique is highly effective, it does carry certain risks. Complications associated with BSSRO include misalignment of the proximal segment, post operative condylar resorption, and exacerbation of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) symptoms.3 Changes in TMJ morphology may contribute to the development of temporomandibular disorders (TMD), including pain, joint sounds, and functional limitations. In addition, improper condylar positioning or unfavorable remodeling may predispose to postoperative skeletal relapse and occlusal instability. Adaptive condylar remodeling is generally considered a physiological response to altered biomechanical loading; however, excessive changes may result in condylar resorption. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of TMJ morphological changes is essential for optimizing surgical planning, ensuring postoperative stability, and minimizing potential complications3,9, 10, 11, 12, 13.

The effect of BSSRO on TMJ morphology remains uncertain, as previous studies have reported inconsistent findings. In Class III mandibular setback procedures, postoperative changes in condylar morphology and axial rotation have been variably reported, with some studies noting reductions in condylar height, width, and axial angle 14, 15, 16, while others found no significant alterations.17 Similarly, joint space changes after setback surgery have shown mixed results, with some reporting short-term increases in superior joint space 18 and others demonstrating no significant differences.19,20

In Class II mandibular advancement procedures, immediate postoperative condylar displacement patterns have varied, including anteroinferior,21 posteroinferior,21, 22, 23 and vertical distributions of displacement.22 However, most studies have reported minimal or no significant long-term changes in joint space dimensions.24 Disc position findings also suggest limited postoperative alteration, with evidence indicating that BSSRO does not substantially modify preoperative disc position.23,25 Whereas Song et al. concluded in his study that orthognathic surgery results in a particularly significant improvement in the functional movement of the anterior disc displacement with reduction (ADDwR) of the TMJ. In addition, it improves TMD in patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion and has a positive effect on improving the position of the joint disc position.26

A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Al-Moraissi et al.27 reported that orthognathic surgery resulted in a reduction of TMD symptoms in a considerable proportion of patients who were symptomatic prior to surgery, while a smaller subset of previously asymptomatic patients developed new symptoms following the procedure. Another systematic review by Soltani et al.28 has summarized the changes in disc position followed by BSSRO. However, there is no comprehensive review, which has assessed the morphological changes in TMJ and its associated structure after BSSRO using three-dimensional imaging technique. Hence aim of this comprehensive review is to assess the changes in TMJ and its associated structure including joint space changes, condylar height, condylar axial angle, disk position changes, and masseter/lateral pterygoid muscle changes following BSSRO in patients with skeletal Class II and III malocclusions by employing advanced three-dimensional diagnostic tools, to provide a more understanding of these postoperative changes.

Research question

The systematic review was conducted to address the research question: Does bilateral sagittal split ramus osteotomies affect the TMJ morphology and its associated structure in the adult population?

Method

The present systematic review was conducted by the PRISMA 2020 guidelines.29 The review protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42024501715).

Minor amendments to the registered protocol, including clarification of outcomes and contributor details, were updated in the PROSPERO record during the review process. Glenoid fossa changes were registered as an additional outcome; however, limited and heterogeneous data prevented meaningful synthesis or meta-analysis. Therefore, the review focused on consistently reported TMJ outcomes.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The following eligibility criteria (based on PEOS) were considered for searching the studies.

PEOS analysis

Population (P): Adult patients undergoing orthognathic surgery for skeletal Class II or Class III malocclusions, Exposure (E): BSSRO performed for mandibular advancement in skeletal Class II patients, mandibular setback in skeletal Class III patients, or in combination with maxillary orthognathic procedures. Studies involving both surgery-first and conventional (orthodontics-first) treatment protocols were included, Outcome (O): Morphological changes in the TMJ assessed using three-dimensional imaging before and after surgery, including joint space changes, condylar height changes, condylar axial angle changes, disk position changes, masseter/lateral pterygoid muscle changes, Study design(S): Cross-sectional studies, quasi experimental studies and cohort studies were included. Only full-text, peer-reviewed articles were considered. Studies published in languages other than English were included if a reliable English translation was available.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they involved subjects with facial asymmetry or pre-existing TMJ disorders, or if they were case series, case reports, reviews, editorials, or expert opinions. Studies lacking full-text availability, presenting incomplete data, or demonstrating unclear methodology were also excluded. Grey literature, including conference abstracts, theses, preprints, and clinical trial registries, was not considered due to limited data reporting and absence of peer review. Furthermore, studies not specifically evaluating TMJ morphological changes following BSSRO were excluded. Randomized controlled trials were not included owing to their limited availability in this field and the focus of the review on observational evidence assessing TMJ morphological changes.

Search strategy

An electronic search was performed in four databases: PubMed, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. Additionally, Google Scholar was used with results limited to the first 100 relevant articles. The search strategy included a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms with Boolean operators (AND, OR). The following keywords were used: (“Adult”) AND (“Bilateral Sagittal Split Osteotomy” OR “Bilateral Sagittal Split Osteotomies”) AND (“Condylar Displacement” OR “Condylar Changes” OR “Temporomandibular Disk Position” OR “TM Disk Position” OR “Temporomandibular Joint Space Changes” OR “TM Joint Space Changes” OR “Masseter Muscle Changes” OR “Lateral Pterygoid Muscle Changes”). The search included studies from inception to May 15, 2024, and was updated on March 1, 2026. The search strategy was first developed for PubMed and adapted for other databases. (supply file 2: search strategy).

Screening and selection

Duplicate records were removed using Rayyan (v2020, Cambridge, MA, USA). Two reviewers (RA and SK) independently screened titles and abstracts. Full-text articles were retrieved for potentially eligible studies and evaluated based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer (PG).

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers (RA and SK) evaluated the quality of each included study using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists for analytical cross-sectional studies, cohort studies and quasi-experimental designs. Each criterion was scored as “Yes” (1), “No” (0), or “Unclear/Not applicable” (0). For quasi-experimental design, final scores were converted to percentages and Classified into High quality: at least 80%, moderate quality: 50–80%, and low quality: less than 50%.30 For analytical cross-sectional studies, score: 0–3, low quality; 4–6, moderate quality; and 7–8, high quality.31 And for cohort studies scoring ≥80% were considered to have high quality, those scoring from 60 to <80% were of moderate quality, and those scoring <60% were of low quality.32 The results of the quality assessment are summarized in Table 1, Table 2, Table 3.

Data extraction and study selection process

Two independent reviewers (RA and SK) extracted data using separate Microsoft Excel sheets. A third reviewer (PG) cross-verified the information. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Extracted data included: author names, publication year, study location, mean age, sex distribution, number of subjects, imaging modality used, surgical techniques, study indications, outcome measures, and pre- and post-operative TMJ assessment results, funding of the study and conflict of interest. Follow-up durations were also recorded for all included studies and categorized as short-term (<1 year) or long-term (>1 year) to allow outcome comparison across different postoperative timeframes.

Data analysis

Quantitative data, wherever possible were pooled in a random-effect model using JBI SUMARI, 2022 (https://sumari.jbi.global/). Results were expressed as standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical heterogeneity of the included studies was assessed using χ2, Tau2, and I2 tests. Pooled estimates were derived using inverse variance techniques. Prediction intervals were calculated separately using R software with the metafor package. When statistical pooling was impossible, the findings were presented in descriptive form.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach and ranged from low to very low across outcomes. The evidence was downgraded due to risk of bias (Cross sectional studies, cohort studies and quasi experimental study designs), inconsistency (high heterogeneity among studies), and imprecision (wide confidence intervals). Therefore, confidence in the estimated effects is limited, and further high-quality studies may substantially change the current estimates of TMJ morphological alterations following bilateral sagittal split ramus osteotomy. Certainty was assessed at the level of key morphological domains and categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low.49 A Summary of Findings (SoF) table was developed to present the certainty ratings (supply file 3).

Results

Study selection

After an elaborate database search, 1190 records were initially found. After eliminating 304 duplicate records, the titles and abstracts of the remaining 886 records were screened. The full texts of 49 potentially relevant articles were reviewed, leading to the final inclusion of 26 articles for data extraction (Fig. 1). Most of these studies (92%) were conducted in Asia. The final set of reviewed studies was primarily published between 1992 and 2026, with only two studies appearing before the year 2000. The findings from the included studies are outlined in (supply file 4). Among 26 studies, ten studies were included for meta-analysis, which assessed anterior, posterior, and superior joint spaces, as well as axial condylar axis angle and condylar height, with follow-ups ranging from 1 month to 1 year or more. The excluded studies and the corresponding reasons for exclusion are summarized in (supply file 5).

Flowchart showing the screening, selection and inclusion of article in this systematic review.

Study characteristics

The study characteristics are summarized in (supply file 4). There were variations in total sample size, ranging from 10 to 72 patients, with a combined total of 872 patients across all studies. The age range of participants spanned from 20 to 32 years. Gender distribution was reported in several studies, totaling 273 females and 204 males, while some studies did not specify gender breakdowns. The included studies evaluated patients with skeletal Class II and Class III malocclusions. Class II malocclusion comprised 179 patients, either exclusively or as part of mixed cohorts, and imaging assessment in these studies primarily used CBCT (2 studies) and CT (6 studies). Class III malocclusion constituted the larger study population with 568 patients, with most studies focusing exclusively on Class III cases. Imaging in Class III studies predominantly employed CBCT (7 studies), followed by CT (6 studies) and MRI (5 studies). Orthognathic surgical treatments were a primary focus, with specific procedures such as BSSRO. Common exclusion criteria of the included individual studies where, patients with craniofacial syndromes, severe facial asymmetry (midline deviation >3 mm), cleft palate, history of trauma, degenerative joint diseases, or poor imaging quality.

Assessment of risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment for this review is summarized in Table 1, Table 2, Table 3. The research studies were evaluated based on eight key quality assessment questions for analytical cross-sectional studies, nine for quasi-experimental design, eleven for cohort studies and their overall scores were categorized accordingly, Table 1, Table 2, Table 3.

The JBI critical review tools were used to look at a total of 26 studies. There were 11 cross-sectional analytical studies, 8 quasi-experimental studies and 7 cohort studies. Of the cross-sectional analytical studies, 9 were classified as high quality, achieving scores between 87.5% and 100%, whilst 2 studies had a score of 75%, signifying moderate quality. Within the quasi-experimental studies, 7 studies received high ratings, attaining 100% and 1 earning 77.8%. Among included cohort studies all included 7 studies showed high quality attaining 80% and above. The majority of the included studies were rated as high quality, with only three studies assessed as moderate quality and none as poor quality. This distribution suggests that the overall body of evidence is predominantly of high methodological quality, supporting the reliability of the findings.

Joint space changes

Superior joint space changes

Eight studies18, 19, 20,24,36, 39, 46, 47 assessed the changes in the superior joint space before and after BSSRO. Six studies 18, 19, 20,24,46,36were considered for meta-analysis, while two studies39,47 were excluded. Overall, no significant difference was observed between the pre- and postoperative values.

In Class II cases with short-term follow-up within one month, analysis demonstrated no statistically significant change in superior joint space following BSSRO (SMD: −0.35; 95% CI: −1.72 to 1.02; I2 = 93%), with a wide prediction interval ranging from −2.67 to 1.97 (Fig. 2a). At one-year follow-up, analysis similarly showed no significant difference (SMD: 0.46; 95% CI: −0.08 to 1.00; I2 = 59%), with a prediction interval ranging from −0.34 to 1.27 (Fig. 2b).

Anterior joint space changes

Six studies18, 19, 20,36, 39, 46 assessed the changes in the anterior joint space before and after BSSRO. Three studies 18, 19, 20 which analyse Class III cases were considered for meta-analysis, while three 36, 39, 46studies were excluded. There is no significant difference in the pre- and post-surgery values. Three studies were excluded from the meta-analysis.

In Class III cases, short-term follow-up within one month showed no significant change in anterior joint space (SMD: −0.03; 95% CI: −0.38 to 0.33; two studies; 61 participants), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4a). Similarly, follow-up at 6–8 months demonstrated no significant difference (SMD: −0.21; 95% CI: −0.83 to 0.41; three studies; 87 participants), although substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 74%), with a prediction interval ranging from −1.27 to 0.84 (Fig. 4b).

Axial condylar axis angle

Three studies14,19,35 evaluated axial condylar axis angle changes in Class III cases, and all were included in the meta-analysis. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in axial condylar axis angle (SMD: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.44 to 1.10; three studies; 76 participants), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4c).

Condylar height changes

Three studies14,36,44 assessed condylar height changes in Class III cases, and all were included in the meta-analysis. Overall, long-term follow-up demonstrated a significant reduction in condylar height.

Short-term follow-up within one month showed no significant change (SMD: 0.12; 95% CI: −0.17 to 0.40; two studies; 96 participants), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4d). However, long-term follow-up at one year demonstrated a significant reduction in condylar height (SMD: 0.37; 95% CI: 0.08 to 0.66; two studies; 93 participants), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4e).

Assessment of publication bias using funnel plots was not performed because a minimum of 10 studies is recommended for reliable interpretation. As each pooled outcome in this review included fewer than 10 studies, funnel plot analysis was not considered appropriate.

Condylar changes following BSSRO

The effects of BSSRO on condylar changes vary across different studies. Ren et al.33 found that the extent of condylar displacement correlates with the degree of surgical advancement, with greater advancement leading to more displacement. A study by Kawamata et al.40 observed, the changes are typically within 2 mm. The study by Yin et al.,34 discussed that post-surgical condylar positions are often moved outward, backward, and downward in cases of mandibular retrusion and prognathism. The study by Ma et al. 16 reported that condyles move posteriorly, superiorly, and laterally post-surgery. Research by Kim et al. and Hupp et al.24,35 indicated that the condylar head moves from an anterior position before surgery to a more centered position after surgery. Conversely, Ueki et al.17 found no significant condylar position changes following the surgery. According to Gulcek et al. 14 after orthognathic surgery, condylar depth and condylar width decreased significantly.

Temporomandibular disc position changes following BSSRO

Among the studies reviewed, six 15,23,36,38, 43, 45 investigated changes in disc position. Four of these studies23,36,45,41 reported no significant alterations in disc position following BSSRO. In contrast, in Class III malocclusion treated with BSSRO and mandibular setback, the study by Takahara et al.15 observed improvements in disc position and anterior disc displacement (ADD). Another study, by Kim et al.38 found an increase in the distance between the disc and condyle in the open-mouth position after surgery, although no change was noted in internal disc derangement (IDD). No significant postoperative changes were observed in disc length 23 and morphology.15

Muscular changes following BSSRO

Evidence regarding muscle changes remains limited. Ueki et al.42 reported a reduction in the width, length, and angle of the masseter muscle following surgery in Class III subjects, while no significant change was observed in its cross-sectional area. Lekroengsin et al.37 demonstrated a decrease in the volume of both the masseter and medial pterygoid muscles following BSSRO in Class II patients. Additionally, Ueki et al.43 reported that muscle angle was significantly greater in Class II patients compared to Class III patients, both preoperatively at the lower level and at one-year follow-up at both upper and lower levels (P < 0.05).

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to provide a comprehensive analysis of postoperative morphological changes in the TMJ and its associated structures—including joint space dimensions, condylar height, axial condylar angle, disc position, and masticatory muscle adaptations—following BSSRO in adult patients with Class II and Class III malocclusions. As these malocclusions are commonly corrected through mandibular advancement (Class II) or setback (Class III), understanding TMJ responses to each surgical movement is clinically relevant.

Most of the included studies were of high quality or low risk of bias. Three studies were assessed as having moderate quality. The primary sources of bias were mainly related to the retrospective design of several included studies and incomplete reporting of surgical details, particularly where some studies evaluated isolated mandibular procedures while others assessed combined maxillary and mandibular surgeries.

Present study shows, no statistically significant changes in superior joint space following BSSRO in both Class II and Class III malocclusions across short-term and long-term follow-ups. These findings indicate overall stability of the superior joint space despite variability in individual study outcomes.

In contrast, Mavreas et al.18 reported an increase in immediate postoperative superior joint space in Class II patients, which may be attributed to factors such as the unopposed pull of the lateral pterygoid muscle, postoperative edema displacing the condyle, intraoperative manipulation, splint use during fixation, or temporary changes in muscle tonus. Reduced activity of certain masticatory muscles during intermaxillary fixation has also been noted. Whereas, Chen et al.46 reported a reduction in superior joint space, possibly due to the influence of the temporalis muscle on the proximal segment or rotation of the condylar fragment. However, Hupp et al.24 found no significant postoperative differences.

Regarding Class III subjects, the findings of the present study result is consistent with those of Sobouti et al.,20 whereas Mavrease et al.18 reported an increase in post operative joint space. This variation can be due to the difference in surgical procedure as well as other factors including patient posture, masticatory muscle tension, the approach for locating the proximal segment, and fixation methods.

Analysis of posterior joint space revealed no statistically significant short-term changes in both Class II and Class III cases. However, long-term follow-up demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in posterior joint space in Class II subjects, whereas Class III cases showed no significant changes across all follow-up periods. This suggests a differential adaptive response between malocclusion types.

These findings are consistent with several studies reporting no immediate postoperative changes in Class II cases.19,20,46,36 Whereas Hupp et al.24 have documented an immediate postoperative reduction in the posterior joint space. In the present study long term follow-up in Class II subjects showed a significant reduction in posterior joint space. The long-term reduction in posterior joint space in Class II subjects likely reflects posterior–superior condylar repositioning and adaptive remodeling of the condyle–fossa relationship following mandibular advancement.

Mavreas et al.18 reported an increase in posterior joint space measurements in Class III subjects. In contrast, most other studies 19,20,47 demonstrated no significant postoperative change in Class III cases, which is consistent with the findings of the present meta-analysis.

Analysis of anterior joint space in Class III cases demonstrated no statistically significant postoperative changes at both short-term and intermediate follow-ups, indicating overall stability of the anterior joint space following BSSRO.

Although Kim et al.19 reported an increase in anterior joint space at 6–8 months, studies by Mavreas et al. and Sobouti et al. reported no significant alterations, which aligns with the findings of the present analysis.18,20 Luo et al.48 suggested that posterior positioning of the condyle and reduction in condylar thickness due to resorption may contribute to increased anterior space in some cases.

From the available evidence, it is noteworthy that joint space alterations following BSSRO are primarily observed in the immediate postoperative period, with a tendency to stabilize and approximate preoperative values within one year. Even in asymmetric cases, joint space volume demonstrates a similar pattern post-surgery.50 Post-operative changes in TMJ joint spaces following BSSRO result from a combination of surgical, biomechanical, and biological factors. Intraoperative variables such as condylar positioning, fixation technique, and surgical manipulation can alter the condyle–fossa relationship. Post-surgically, changes in occlusal loading and neuromuscular adaptation modify joint biomechanics, while skeletal relapse or remodeling further contributes to spatial variations. Additionally, adaptive bone and soft-tissue remodeling, together with patient-related factors including malocclusion type, age, parafunctional habits, and pre-existing TMJ pathology, may influence the extent and direction of joint space alterations.

Analysis of axial condylar axis angle demonstrated a statistically significant reduction following BSSRO, indicating consistent rotational changes of the condyle across the included studies. Rotational changes of the condyle in Class III subjects following BSSRO have been documented by Takahara et al.15 and Ma et al.39 Zuo et al.51 suggested that larger rotational displacements may predispose patients to TMJ symptoms. However, Ueki et al.7 and Kaur et al.52 emphasized that minor rotational changes are generally well tolerated when passive seating is achieved, and minimal linear or angular changes do not contribute to TMD postoperatively.

Analysis of condylar height demonstrated no statistically significant changes in the early postoperative period, whereas a significant reduction was observed at one-year follow-up, indicating time-dependent adaptive remodeling of the condyle.

These findings are consistent with previous reports by Kim et al. and Ueki et al., where they recognized the condylar remodeling, including slight resorptive changes, as a physiological adaptation rather than a pathological process.19,41 Studies by Chen et al. 46 also demonstrated that minor reductions in condylar height post-surgery did not significantly compromise TMJ function or stability. The observed reduction over time may be attributed to changes in mechanical loading patterns on the condyle after mandibular repositioning, promoting adaptive bone remodeling. A Study by Hoppenreijs et al.53 has reported more pronounced condylar resorption postoperatively, particularly in patients with pre-existing TMD or skeletal discrepancies.

The muscular changes seen after BSSRO are likely related to the altered position of the mandible and the resulting changes in muscle function. In Class III patients treated with mandibular setback surgery, the backward repositioning of the mandible may reduce tension and functional demand on the masseter muscle, leading to adaptive decreases in muscle dimensions over time.54 In contrast, mandibular advancement in Class II patients places the muscles in a more stretched position, which may influence muscle angulation and functional adaptation during postoperative recovery. These differences between Class II and Class III cases suggest that the direction of surgical movement plays an important role in postoperative muscular remodeling and neuromuscular adaptation.55

The included studies demonstrated a degree of heterogeneity, which may be attributed to variations in study design, surgical techniques, fixation methods, imaging modalities, and follow-up durations. To account for this variability, a random-effects model was applied in the meta-analysis, allowing for both within-study and between-study differences. Furthermore, subgroup analyses based on malocclusion type (Class II and Class III) and follow-up duration (short-term and long-term) were performed to improve the interpretability of the results. Prediction intervals were also incorporated to reflect the potential range of true effects across different clinical settings. Despite these methodological approaches, some degree of heterogeneity persisted, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.

The findings across multiple studies suggests that these TMJ morphological changes are expected biomechanical response to surgical intervention. The surgical repositioning of the mandible induces dynamic shifts in TMJ loading, prompting adaptive changes in condylar orientation as part of the body's self-regulating process to restore functional equilibrium. Anatomical variability, surgeons experience, fixation techniques, and postoperative orthodontic management could all contribute to the differences noted across studies.56 Furthermore, variations in imaging modalities and measurement protocols may influence the reported extent of morphological changes. Overall, the present findings suggest that BSSRO, when carefully executed in adults without prior TMJ dysfunction, results in minimal adverse changes in condylar height and tolerable rotational adjustments. These morphological adaptations are unlikely to impair long-term joint function or stability.

Strength of the study

This systematic review is strengthened by its comprehensive assessment of TMJ morphology and associated structures following BSSRO, encompassing joint spaces, condylar morphology, disc position, and muscular adaptations. The exclusive focus on BSSRO minimized procedural variability, while adherence to PRISMA 2020 guidelines with PROSPERO registration ensured methodological transparency. Additionally, the use of advanced three-dimensional imaging modalities, including CBCT and MRI, enhanced the accuracy and reliability of TMJ assessments.

Challenges and gaps

Despite considerable progress in understanding TMJ adaptations after orthognathic surgery, several challenges remain in the current body of evidence. First, variability in imaging modalities and protocols continues to hinder cross-study comparisons, making it difficult to establish standardized reference for postoperative condylar remodeling.57 Second, limited evidence exists that specifically isolates the effects of a particular orthognathic surgical procedure on TMJ, thereby restricting conclusions about their independent influence on TMJ outcomes. Third, the absence of standardized outcome measures which reduces the clinical applicability of findings across diverse patient populations. Finally, the scarcity of long-term, multicenter studies limits generalizability and leaves unanswered questions regarding population-specific variations and relapse risk. Addressing these gaps will be essential for developing evidence-based guidelines that improve both diagnostic accuracy and surgical outcomes in orthognathic practice.

Clinical implications and future directions

Early postoperative alterations, including joint compression and condylar repositioning, are often transient, whereas long-term remodeling contributes to restoration of stable joint function.52,58 From a clinical perspective, individualized treatment planning, long-term TMJ monitoring, individualized postoperative rehabilitation, and timely occlusal adjustments to minimize adverse outcomes is needed.59,60 Recent advances in virtual surgical planning (VSP), CAD/CAM-assisted fixation, and patient-specific positioning devices have improved intraoperative precision and control of condylar alignment.60,61 Parallel innovations in three-dimensional superimposition and AI-assisted CBCT/MRI analytics provide more reliable tools for detecting postoperative remodeling, thereby enhancing both research reproducibility and clinical decision making.

Future research should prioritize multicenter longitudinal studies, integration of standardized patient-reported outcome measures, and application of biomechanical modeling to refine our understanding of condylar remodeling. In addition, exploration of biomarkers and regenerative strategies may provide novel approaches for predicting or preventing condylar resorption, thereby improving the safety and predictability of orthognathic surgery.

Limitations

This systematic review has certain limitations that should be considered while interpreting the findings. The geographic concentration of the included studies, with a majority originating from Asia, may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations. Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies in terms of follow-up duration, imaging modalities, and assessment protocols, which may affect the comparability of outcomes. Because of which only a subset of studies could be included in the meta-analysis due to incomplete or non-comparable data.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis assess how BSSRO influence TMJ morphology in adult patients. The results suggest that most TMJ parameters remained stable, with selective remodeling observed in specific subgroups such as:

•In Class II patients, superior and anterior joint spaces showed minimal postoperative variation, but a consistent long-term reduction in posterior joint space indicated gradual posterior condylar repositioning; disc position remained largely stable, and condylar morphology showed no major changes.

In Class II patients, superior and anterior joint spaces showed minimal postoperative variation, but a consistent long-term reduction in posterior joint space indicated gradual posterior condylar repositioning; disc position remained largely stable, and condylar morphology showed no major changes.

•In Class III patients, superior, anterior, and posterior joint spaces remained largely unchanged, while significant long-term reductions in condylar height and axial condylar axis angle reflected notable condylar remodeling; disc position also remained stable, with only occasional improvements in disc–condyle spacing and no meaningful changes in disc length or morphology.

In Class III patients, superior, anterior, and posterior joint spaces remained largely unchanged, while significant long-term reductions in condylar height and axial condylar axis angle reflected notable condylar remodeling; disc position also remained stable, with only occasional improvements in disc–condyle spacing and no meaningful changes in disc length or morphology.

•Masseter and pterygoid muscle adaptations showed variable patterns depending on malocclusion type, with some studies reporting reductions in muscle dimensions and volume following BSSRO; however, due to limited and heterogeneous evidence, no definitive conclusion could be established.

Masseter and pterygoid muscle adaptations showed variable patterns depending on malocclusion type, with some studies reporting reductions in muscle dimensions and volume following BSSRO; however, due to limited and heterogeneous evidence, no definitive conclusion could be established.

•Overall, condylar space alterations were more evident in Class II cases, whereas condylar remodeling was more pronounced in Class III cases, with disc position remaining mostly unaffected across groups.

Overall, condylar space alterations were more evident in Class II cases, whereas condylar remodeling was more pronounced in Class III cases, with disc position remaining mostly unaffected across groups.

These findings support individualized surgical planning and long-term TMJ monitoring, and highlight the need for standardized imaging and assessment protocols in future research.
