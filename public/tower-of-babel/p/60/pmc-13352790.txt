The effects of a fully subsidized behavioral weight management program among healthcare workers with pre-diabetes and diabetes
Authors: Katherine Tomaino Fraser, Jacquelyn Sullivan, Lauren Toppenberg, Patrick Masseo, Adey Tsega, Laurel Prokopow, Craig Willingham, Nevin Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluated the impact of a fully subsidized behavioral weight management program (WW; formerly WeightWatchers®) among adults with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes enrolled in the 1199SEIU Benefit Funds, a self-administered labor–management fund providing health coverage to over 360,000 union members, retirees, and their families.MethodsUsing a quasi-experimental mixed-methods design, we analyzed clinical claims data and focus group transcripts. Quantitative outcomes included HbA1c, body mass index (BMI), and program engagement. A one-to-one propensity score–matched control group was constructed to compare pre-post changes over a minimum six-month exposure period. Qualitative analysis explored barriers and facilitators to program participation.ResultsAmong participants living with type 2 diabetes, those enrolled in WW experienced significant reductions in BMI and significant improvements in HbA1c control, while those with pre-diabetes demonstrated BMI reductions and no significant change in HbA1c levels. Higher engagement with WW, particularly through food tracking, activity tracking, and workshop participation, was associated with greater BMI reductions. GLP-1 medication use in the study sample was independently associated with reductions in HbA1c, and the combined effect of WW participation and GLP-1 use on BMI warrants further study given that interaction effects were non-significant in adjusted models. Qualitative findings highlighted key barriers and facilitators to engagement, including time constraints, family dietary preferences, and program accessibility.ConclusionThese findings provide real-world evidence supporting employer- or payer-sponsored behavioral weight management programs as a strategy to improve diabetes-related outcomes. Integration with pharmacotherapy warrants further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27708-3.Keywords: Diabetes, Pre-diabetes, Health promotion, Weight Watchers, WW, Behavioral weight management intervention, Public health, GLP-1, Wellness

Introduction

In the United States, 38.1 million adults (14.7% of the adult population) have diabetes and 97.6 million adults (38%) have pre-diabetes [1] Key risk factors for having or developing type 2 diabetes in the U.S. include having overweight or obesity, having family members with diabetes, and living a sedentary lifestyle [2]. While diet and physical activity can be effective tools to manage diabetes [3], challenges to successful diabetes and pre-diabetes management include access to nutritious foods [4], awareness of treatment options, and difficulty maintaining self-management practices [5].

Diabetes related direct and indirect costs in the U.S. were $412.9 billion in 2022 and care for people diagnosed with diabetes accounts for 1 in 4 healthcare dollars [6]. Direct costs include hospitalization, medical care, treatment and supplies, and indirect costs include reduced workforce productivity, absenteeism, and disease-related disability [7, 8]. Households and communities experience the effects of reduced earnings, while higher taxes and insurance premiums affect the entire population [6].

Lifestyle interventions such as physical activity, healthy eating, and weight management are effective at preventing and managing diabetes and have demonstrated effectiveness comparable to pharmacologic therapy in some populations [8, 9]. There is also evidence that glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1s) can be effective in diabetes treatment [10–12], and clinical guidance recommends both lifestyle modifications and GLP-1 use for treatment [13, 14]. Several studies indicate that lifestyle interventions can prevent and mitigate type 2 diabetes [7, 9, 15, 16]. However, fewer studies have evaluated the real-world impact of fully subsidized behavioral weight management programs implemented at scale within employer-sponsored insurance settings.

WW (Weight Watchers) is a behavioral weight management program that offers in-person activities and virtual tools, including a dietary points tracking system (known as Points), serving as a diabetes management intervention [17]. The program includes accountability mechanisms, including meetings for engagement, motivation, and knowledge exchange. Evidence demonstrates program effectiveness at facilitating weight loss. WW members using the Points Program lose 3.5 times more weight than those receiving standard nutrition guidance [18]. Members with type 2 diabetes achieve 5.7% weight loss and 0.75 HbA1c reduction (a long term indicator of diabetes health) after 6 months [19].

This study evaluated the effects of a fully subsidized behavioral weight management program among healthcare workers with diabetes or pre-diabetes. The primary objective was to quantitatively determine the intervention effects on glycemic control and weight loss and to to evaluate the association between WW participation and changes in glycemic control and weight outcomes among members. A secondary objective was to qualitatively identify barriers and facilitators to program enrollment and participation.

Methodology

Intervention

The WeightWatchers (WW) Program is an evidence-based behavioral weight management program [19–26] that guides members toward their weight health goals through weekly behavioral nutrition and activity targets. The WW program is based on recommendations by national and international guidelines to form the foundation for a healthy pattern of eating [27, 28]. The WW program includes ZeroPoint™ Foods (a list of foods that can be eaten in moderation without tracking), as well as the proprietary Points system, where foods and beverages are assigned a points value based on their caloric and nutritional content (protein, fiber, unsaturated fat, saturated fat and added sugar). Using a member’s age, height, weight, and sex, a daily points value is calculated, as well as a weekly points target, and members are encouraged to track their food and beverages, with the goal of staying within their daily and weekly points target. The Diabetes Program provides a modified ZeroPoints Foods list that is tailored to the unique needs of members living with diabetes. The WW app supports behavioral self-monitoring, including food, activity, water, and weight tracking, barcode scanning, and recipe analysis. The WW program, whether in RCTs or single arm trails, has been shown to produce statistically significant and clinically meaning reductions in weight and glycemic control among those with pre-diabetes [25].

The 1199SEIU Benefit Funds are self-administered labor-management funds providing health coverage for over 360,000 union members, retirees and families [29]. Funded by employer contributions, they aim to provide quality, cost-effective healthcare with minimal out-of-pocket expenses. In 2023, the Funds partnered with WW to offer tiered-cost services: no-cost access for members, spouses or dependents with pre-diabetes or type 2 diabetes; $8 / month for members without these conditions; and $29/month for other spouses and dependents (compared to full price WW membership of approximately $45 per month as of March 2025.) Member eligibility was determined using the Funds’ clinical claims data with this study focusing specifically on the fully subsidized cohort.

Study design

Investigators from the CUNY Urban Food Policy Institute and 1199SEIU Benefit Funds designed a quasi-experimental mixed methods study to assess how WW participation affects health outcomes and chronic disease management among fully subsidized participants. The design combines quantitative clinical claims data measuring three key outcomes: HbA1c (%), BMI (kg/m2) and proportion of participants with controlled HbA1c (%) with qualitative participant perspectives from focus groups. The evaluation included both a quality improvement component conducted by 1199SEIU Benefit Funds to assess program implementation and outcomes within routine operations, and a research component conducted by CUNY investigators to generate generalizable knowledge regarding participant experiences and engagement with the program.

Sampling frame and propensity score matching

CUNY researchers led the design of the sampling frame and recruitment approach. Eligible participants were aged 18+, enrolled in 1199SEIU Benefit Funds healthcare coverage, with confirmed diabetes or pre-diabetes diagnosis within 3 years before the study period. Individuals with specific comorbidities (advanced heart failure, advanced chronic kidney disease) or pregnancy were excluded (Fig. 1).

Study enrollment flow chart. *Note: Baseline and follow up survey data is not presented in this manuscript for brevity, but details are available upon request

The Funds promoted the fully subsidized WW opportunity to eligible members, spouses and dependents through multiple channels: email, postcards, in-person recruitment, group presentations, online communications, and newsletter promotion. In the first year, 2,771 individuals enrolled in the fully subsidized option.

This quasi-experimental study evaluates pre-post measures in 1199SEIU Benefit Fund members with diabetes or pre-diabetes who participated in free WW programming. A propensity score-matched control cohort consisted of eligible participants who received identical communication materials for enrollment but did not enroll. Propensity score matching included the following co-variates: age, gender, relationship to fund, fund type, HbA1c category, BMI, Charlson Comorbidity Index category, chronic pulmonary disease status, obesity status, severe obesity status, GLP-1 medication use). Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), calculated on the full matched analytic sample. Pre-post measures were assessed over a minimum 6-month intervention period.

Target minimum sample size was calculated based on a study population of approximately 54,000 1199SEIU Benefit Funds members with diabetes or pre-diabetes (defined by confirmed clinical diagnosis) who were offered an opportunity to participate in WW at no cost.

A power analysis1 was conducted based on hemoglobin HbA1c as the primary outcome of interest, which determined a minimum of 554 1199SEIU members was needed for this analysis. Based on historical 1199SEIU Benefit Funds data of overall annual loss to follow up (related to primary medical coverage eligibility loss in 2022) of 15%, the final study sample targeted 640 participants, including 320 1199SEIU Funds members with diabetes and pre-diabetes participating in WW (intervention) and 320 members with diabetes and pre-diabetes who were not participating in WW (control). Power calculations assumed α = 0.05 and were based on detecting a clinically meaningful change in HbA1c.

Due to non-randomized program design, propensity score matching identified comparable controls by calculating assignment probability based on observed covariates. The dependent variable was WW enrollment status. A total of 954 WW enrollees (570 with type 2 diabetes, 384 with pre-diabetes) were matched one-to-one with controls using a propensity score model with backward elimination to retain significant predictors. Variables with excessive missing values not correctable through imputation were excluded. Matching occurred without replacement using a nearest neighbor approach starting at 5 decimals, without common support restrictions. The covariates representing potential confounders (associated with both intervention and outcome) and potential outcome predictors included in the propensity matching are: Age at baseline (< 25, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–65, 65 + years); Gender (female, male); Relationship to Fund (member, spouse, dependent); Fund type (NBF, GNY, Home Care); HbA1c category (5.7–6.4, 6.5-7.0, 7.1-8.0, 8.1-9.0, 9.0+); BMI category (< 25, 25–29, 30–34, 35+); Charlson Comorbidity Index category (no, low, medium, high comorbidity); Chronic Pulmonary Disease (yes, no); Obesity status (yes, no); Severe obesity status (yes, no); GLP-1 or weight loss prescription in last 3 months (yes, no). Covariate balance after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values less than 0.1 indicating acceptable balance between treatment and control groups.

Instrument design and data sources

1199SEIU Benefit Funds and CUNY collaborated on an iterative instrument design process for qualitative focus group tools. Qualitative measures included knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors related to diet, physical activity, and chronic disease management, and degree of WW program enrollment and participation and recommendations for improvement. The questionnaire was developed specifically for the purposes of this study and has not been previously published. The full English-language version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Material (Supplementary File A).

1199SEIU Benefit Funds contributed clinical data, including HbA1c and body mass index. These clinical measures, along with other data (diagnosis history, drug prescription history, etc.) were extracted from administrative records available from each individual’s medical claims experience. The original Charlson Comorbidity Index which uses ICD 9 diagnosis codes, was adapted to generate a comparable index using ICD 10 diagnosis codes.2

Recruitment: focus groups

Focus group recruitment was managed by 1199SEIU Benefit Funds, who contacted intervention and control groups via email and phone. Focus groups were offered exclusively to the intervention group, with recruitment materials providing direct links and QR codes for Zoom or in-person session registration using email addresses.

Data collection process

Focus groups were conducted by CUNY between May - October 2024, both online via Zoom and in person, with each session lasting approximately 60 min. Participants in Zoom focus groups received a $25 gift card, while in-person participants were entered into a drawing to receive a hand-held automated massage tool valued at approximately $100. Focus groups were audio-recorded for transcription. Automatic, de-identified AI transcripts were generated through Zoom, and were exported for cleaning (including checking for fidelity and accuracy) and analysis by CUNY staff.

Data preparation

Validation checks of the clinical data were conducted using descriptive statistics and graphical depictions to identify the sample distribution and determine whether parametric or nonparametric tests were appropriate. Based on sample size and distributional assessment, parametric tests were deemed appropriate. Missing or incorrect data were excluded from analysis.

Clinical data analysis

Given variable WW enrollment timing, individualized baseline and follow-up periods were defined for each participant. The baseline period spanned from six months pre-enrollment to three months post-enrollment. The baseline measurement window was defined based on standard HbA1c testing intervals, which reflect average glycemic control over approximately three months, while also accounting for the real-world timing of routine clinical care and laboratory testing. In practice, HbA1c measurements are often obtained during regularly scheduled primary care or endocrinology visits, which may not align precisely with program enrollment dates; therefore, a flexible baseline window was used to capture clinically relevant measurements obtained as part of routine care. WW enrollment occurred between 1/3/23 and 11/16/2023, establishing a baseline period from 7/1/22 to 2/16/24. All treatment group individuals had a minimum 6-month intervention exposure. For controls, matching enrollment dates from their propensity-matched treatment counterparts determined equivalent baseline and follow-up periods.

Differences across demographic and outcome variables were calculated to identify changes within and between the treatment and comparison groups before and after the WW intervention. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard error, and comparisons are made within pre-post groups using paired t-tests and between groups using independent t-tests. Categorical variables are expressed as absolute (number) and relative frequencies (percentage), with proportions compared between groups using the Chi-Square test.

Based on the pre/post correlation, we conducted paired t-tests to measure pre / post differences in HbA1c and BMI measures in the treatment and control groups. We also analyzed outcomes based on quartiles of engagement with WW. Engagement scores were based on cumulative engagement across four engagement categories tracked in the WW application: food tracking, activity tracking, weight tracking and workshop attendance. 1199SEIU Benefit Funds provided this data quarterly. As a sensitivity test, we conducted analyses using independent t-tests that included individuals with pre but not post data. Results were slightly weaker, but followed the same patterns of directionality and significance, and can be shared upon request.

We conducted unadjusted and adjusted ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analysis to explore the relationship between WW participation and changes in the participant clinical outcomes of HbA1c and BMI. Adjusted models included control variables: age, Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) score, GLP-1 usage3, and an interaction term between WW enrollment status and GLP-1 usage. We included the CCI as a proxy for comorbidities [30, 31]. Due to the clinical implications of diagnosis status, we separately considered individuals with diabetes and pre-diabetes when analyzing each treatment group.

Focus group analysis

Focus group transcripts were de-identified, verified for accuracy by the CUNY team, and uploaded to Dedoose, a qualitative data analysis platform, for qualitative and mixed-methods analysis. An initial codebook based on the focus group question script guided coding. Two researchers independently conducted thematic analysis, with codes iteratively refined through collaboration as themes emerged. Interpretation followed data preparation, with thematic saturation achieved during analysis.

The project included both a quality improvement component (led by 1199SEIU Benefit Funds, involving claims analysis under HIPAA compliance) and a research component (conducted by CUNY Graduate School of Public Health and Health Policy researchers, involving focus groups). All research activities received CUNY Institutional Review Board approval (Protocol #2022 − 0818).

Table 2 also shows HbA1c and BMI differences by WW engagement level among those with diabetes. Participants in the highest engagement quartile demonstrated greater HbA1c reductions, although statistical significance was marginal.

Effect size estimates indicated that most observed changes were small in magnitude. Among participants with diabetes enrolled in WW, the reduction in BMI corresponded to a small-to-moderate effect size (Cohen’s d≈-0.33), while the reduction in HbA1c corresponded to a small effect (d≈-0.16). In contrast, changes in the diabetes control group were negligible (BMI d≈-0.05; HbA1c d≈-0.02). Engagement-stratified analyses showed somewhat larger BMI effects among participants with higher engagement levels, with effect sizes ranging from approximately − 0.35 to -0.46 across engagement strata. HbA1c changes across engagement groups were generally smaller, consistent with the modest magnitude of glycemic change observed over the study period. Among those with pre-diabetes, no-engagement showed trend-level HbA1c increases (p < 0.08), while medium and high engagement groups exhibited significant BMI decreases (p < 0.05).

At baseline, controlled diabetes rates were comparable between groups (treatment: 52.6%, control: 47.7%, non-significant) (Table 3). At follow-up, the treatment group showed significantly higher controlled diabetes rates (56.7% vs. 48.2%, p < 0.05).

Focus groups

Fourteen individuals participated in 3 focus group discussions about their experiences with WW and their perceptions of the program’s impact on their health and well-being.

Theme 1. Participants generally understood healthy living habits to be related to eating well, being physically active, and having a balanced lifestyle, but experienced individual and external barriers to fully adopting these habits

One participant said:

“[Healthy living] means doing your best to get the best foods that will help your body to thrive and … be filled nutritionally. It also means… being active so that… the body can be optimal, it can function to its best use. It’s…not just eating the best foods for your body, or exercising or being active or incorporating movement into your daily life…. but also managing stress.” (FG #4).

Despite understanding healthy habits, participants faced both individual and external barriers. Individual challenges included difficulty breaking cultural food traditions, insufficient knowledge of healthier alternatives, and tendency to consume unhealthy comfort foods during celebrations or stress. External obstacles included greater availability and convenience of unhealthy options, higher costs of healthy foods, and work-related time constraints that hampered both nutritious meal preparation and physical activity. One participant said:

“You’re so mentally, physically exhausted from the [work] commute that you don’t really want to do anything, you know… So now I’m noticing that’s…one of my biggest barriers in trying to stay healthy, as healthy as I could.” (FG #1).

Theme 2. The desire to improve both physical health, manage health conditions, and lose weight without extensive restrictions were the primary motivators for adopting lifestyle changes through WW

Many WW participants described experiencing health concerns prior to enrolling with WW, and indicated those concerns were motivation to participate in the program.Most WW participants had diabetes or pre-diabetes, some with family members living with diet-related health complications. Additionally, and often related to these diagnoses, dissatisfaction with weight motivated participants to commit to change. Whether due to physical health or aesthetics, weight loss was a top reason participants joined WW as a method to change their lifestyle. One participant said:

“I don’t wanna get diabetes. That’s the point. I have it in my family, so I know the risk and I know it’s really scary to get diabetes. So, with the increasing HbA1c [pre-diabetes diagnosis], it scared me. I know that I have to stop drinking soda, [eating] fries and carbohydrates, and it’s hard for me to do it. That’s my problem, that’s why I need help.” (FG #5).

Theme 3. WW food and activity tracking and WW community engagement online and in person were the most useful elements of the program, and there are opportunities to improve the food tracking library and expand meeting availability

Food and activity tracking were among the most used WW program features. The point system for food tracking promoted accountability and heightened awareness of dietary choicesWhile food tracking was more common than activity tracking, users of the activity feature found it motivational and beneficial for goal setting.

Participants also valued the community aspects of WW, including virtual and in-person meetings where members shared experiences, recipes, and strategies. One participant said:

“For me, this is helpful. Just knowing that other people are, you know, we’re all trying together…[hearing other people offer] recipes and things that work for them…. It’s always helpful. There’s always some type of nugget that you could write down and use.” (FG #8).

While participants valued in-app tracking and community engagement, they suggested enhancements. Despite being the most popular feature, food tracking needed an expanded food library, particularly for cultural foods—participants acknowledged previous expansions but reported ongoing difficulties logging specific dishes. Participants also requested more accessible meetings (online and in-person) with additional time options to accommodate busy schedules and better location distribution across neighborhoods. Focus group participants emphasized that these tracking and meeting improvements would boost overall WW engagement.

Theme 4. 1199SEIU aids WW participation through reducing barriers to access, motivational communication and by curating a supportive community; participant support structures at home are more variable and may aid or complicate participant success

Participants reported that 1199SEIU supported WW participation by reducing financial barriers and providing motivational communication. Many noted that the subsidized cost made the program accessible and encouraged continued engagement. One participant said:

“For me, too, because I’m diabetic 1199 is paying for Weight Watchers, so why not take the opportunity to look into it and work through it? If our company is helping us to take care of ourselves through … Weight Watchers, why not use it?” (FG #3).

Although 1199SEIU support proved crucial for participant success, varying home support levels affected program participation. Some participants benefited from encouraging family members who embraced accountability and lifestyle changes. Others faced resistance from family members reluctant to adopt healthier diets, forcing these participants (often primary meal providers) to prepare multiple meal versions—requiring additional time and money. The presence of unhealthy foods for family members also complicated point system adherence. Nevertheless, 1199SEIU support remained fundamental to participants’ success regardless of home environment challenges. One participant expressed:

“I’m glad that they’re able to do something. It makes me feel happy that I work here because they’re offering something like this…[it shows]that they care, they care enough to do this, to think of the employees.” (FG #7).

Theme 5. WW program participants experienced positive impact on physical and mental health, including increased knowledge related to healthy eating, improved diet, achievement of weight loss goals, and reported improved management of health conditions

Physical health improvements were related to physical functionality, as participants noted how their bodies moved and felt better. Mental improvements were related to mood, with many participants seeing improved energy and motivation. One participant said:

“Yeah, WW has been great. Honestly, I feel like I could walk faster, I feel healthier, I feel not as tired, not as sluggish, more alert.” (FG #7).

Participants reported enhanced nutritional knowledge through the WW point system, helping them improve diets and discover healthier alternatives. They gained confidence in food selection when shopping or dining out. These dietary improvements combined with increased physical activity supported weight loss goals and better health management. Participants also reported increased self-confidence and improved management of chronic conditions, including improved clinical indicators and greater pride in achieving weight-loss goals. One participant said:

“I lost weight and it got me back into working out and moving. And yeah, it just encouraged me to do that. And then, you know, people say, ‘Oh, wow! You look great. You lost weight’ that helps, you know, motivate you to stay on it and do it.” (FG #2).

Theme 6. 1199SEIU participants plan to continue and increase their engagement with WW, but recommended several app-based and programmatic improvements to better their experience

There was strong general agreement among participants that they would continue their engagement with the WW program. A small, less active subset felt motivated to increase their engagement following focus group discussions. While there was general positive feedback about the WW program and 1199SEIU administration, focus group participants described several recommendations related to the app and the program itself that could improve the experience, including expanded meeting availability, improved app functionality, and additional opportunities for in-person community activities.

Participants also recommended program implementation improvements for 1199SEIU. They would like to see greater use of the 1199SEIU campus workspace for WW activities, including more in-person meetings and in-office weigh-ins. An expansion of WW meeting locations and times was requested, along with more opportunities to engage with 1199SEIU health coaches. In addition, rather than communicating with participants via emails that can be easy to overlook, personal outreach via mobile device or in person may be more effective. Participants were generally interested in more 1199SEIU community engagement, with hopes for more health focused events for staff. There was a great appreciation for 1199SEIU in offering access to WW. Adopting some programmatic changes would be beneficial in maintaining participation and improving the overall effectiveness of the WW program. One participant said:

“It’s just a great program, you know. I hope they continue to offer it…maybe expanding [and] offering…community-based events…that combine the community [and] physical activity. Maybe we could have some more competitions. Those are always fun.” (FG #6).

Overall, focus group findings highlighted the importance of program accessibility, social support, and practical behavioral tools in supporting participant engagement with the WW program.

Discussion

This study provides real-world evidence of a behavioral weight management program’s effectiveness for diabetes management, combining clinical data and focus groups from 1199SEIU Benefit Funds members with diabetes or pre-diabetes enrolled in a fully subsidized WW program, alongside matched controls. By integrating health outcomes with lived experiences, it offers comprehensive insights valuable for employer-sponsored health initiatives and benefit providers seeking to reduce chronic disease burden. The study presents a replicable, rigorous quasi-experimental methodology for evaluating large-scale public health interventions, explores how varying engagement levels influence clinical outcomes, and identifies participation barriers and facilitators to optimize program effectiveness for target populations.

This study contributes a third party examination of WW’s contribution to individual health outcomes to the broader literature describing the impact of WW on weight loss and chronic disease outcomes. Like the Apolzan et al. (2023) study [19], this study documents reductions in body weight and HbA1c for individuals with diabetes participating in WW, advancing the evidence highlighting effective interventions (specifically, WW food and activity tracking and WW workshop community engagement online and in person) and noting potential areas for improvement such as expanding the WW food tracking library and broadening in person and online meetings for participants. While some observed changes did not reach conventional thresholds for statistical significance, the magnitude and direction of the HbA1c and BMI changes may still represent clinically meaningful improvements in metabolic health, particularly given the relatively well-controlled baseline HbA1c levels in this population.

This study adds to what is known about the impact of behavioral weight management intervention programs on HbA1c and BMI by accounting for several social and clinical factors that influence those outcomes. This study has clinical implications, demonstrating that individuals with diabetes participating in a structured behavioral lifestyle intervention can improve clinical markers of diabetes control (HbA1C < 6.5) and BMI. Further, this study suggests that greater engagement in such a program may be linked to variations in the degree of clinical health outcomes.

Findings indicate participating in WW over a minimum 6-month intervention was associated with reductions in BMI for individuals with diabetes and pre-diabetes. Findings also indicated participating in WW for at least a 6-month intervention supports increase in HbA1c control in people with diabetes and maintaining HbA1c for those with pre-diabetes. It could be that those with pre-diabetes experience less urgency to change their habits as significantly as those with diabetes due to the severity of disease state. Outcomes are similar in direction and magnitude to those from a 2016 randomized controlled trial of a WW intervention for adults with type 2 diabetes by O’Neil et al. [26].

Further, we document the differential impact of different levels of engagement with WW on these outcomes. Specifically, WW participants with diabetes in the highest quartile of WW engagement demonstrate a trend for significant reduction in HbA1c from pre to post measures. WW participants with diabetes demonstrated significant BMI reductions at every level of engagement. Higher levels of engagement in WW (medium and high engagement specifically) is associated with significant reductions in BMI for participants with pre-diabetes. Previous studies such as those by Carey et al. [32] and Johnson & Wardle [33] have described how varying engagement levels with social behavioral weight management programs can be responsible for variance in clinical and weight loss outcomes.

Further, after a minimum of 6 months of behavioral weight management intervention, a statistically higher proportion of individuals (p < 0.05) in the WW treatment group had controlled diabetes with HbA1c ≤ 6.5 (56.7%) compared to the control group of individuals with diabetes who did not participate in WW (48.2%). This finding is in line with data presented by Boyd et al. [34] that demonstrates that combining GLP-1 medications with intensive behavioral lifestyle interventions and digital tracking can improve disease status for patients with diabetes.

Regression analyses highlight the complex relationship between WW participation and GLP-1 medication use. WW participation alone was not independently associated with significant reductions in HbA1c among individuals with diabetes or pre-diabetes, whereas GLP-1 use was significantly associated with lower HbA1c in both groups. The loss of statistical significance for WW participation in models including GLP-1 use and the interaction term likely reflects overlapping contributions of behavioral and pharmacologic interventions rather than statistical collinearity. In this context, the interaction model was intended to explore whether GLP-1 therapy modifies the relationship between WW participation and glycemic outcomes. The non-significant interaction term suggests that the combined effects of these interventions require further investigation in larger samples.

Among participants with diabetes, both WW enrollment and GLP-1 use were associated with reductions in BMI in separate models, though these effects were no longer significant when both variables were included together. Among participants with pre-diabetes, WW enrollment was not associated with significant BMI change, while GLP-1 use was linked to significant BMI reductions.

Overall, GLP-1 medication use emerged as the strongest predictor of HbA1c improvement in this study population. The non-significant interaction between WW participation and GLP-1 use suggests that the combined effect of behavioral and pharmacologic interventions warrants further study. While pharmacologic therapy appears to play a central role in glycemic control, behavioral interventions such as WW may provide complementary benefits by supporting healthy eating, physical activity, and sustained lifestyle changes. Integrating behavioral programs with pharmacologic treatment may therefore represent a more comprehensive approach to long-term diabetes management.Qualitative findings on engagement barriers and facilitators provide crucial context for quantitative variability. Focus group participants identified food/activity tracking, peer support, and structured accountability as key participation motivators, aligning with quantitative data showing higher WW engagement correlated with BMI reductions. Participants cited limited time, family dietary preferences conflicts, and meeting attendance difficulties as participation barriers, explaining both low improvement rates in some intervention group members and control group reluctance to register.

In addition to the measurable clinical impact of WW participation on BMI and HbA1c, participants reported experiencing a positive impact of WW in nuanced ways; participants generally cited healthful changes in their lives from the program, such as improved physical functionality (participants noted positive changes in the way their bodies moved and felt) and mental improvements related to mood (such as improved energy and motivation.) These self-reported impacts highlight how active engagement in a behavioral lifestyle weight management intervention can create a positive feedback loop that motivates ongoing participation and deepens impact. This concept was underlined by strong agreement among participants that they intended to sustain and increase their participation in WW for the foreseeable future.

Qualitative findings also align with previous health promotion research through the Knowledge, Attitudes, Behavior model, which indicate that “correlations between information level and overt behavior or between attitude and overt behavior are generally positive though low” [35]. While many WW participants expressed positive impact from program participation, including increased knowledge related to healthy eating, improved diet, achievement of weight loss goals, and reported improved management of health conditions, they also described both individual and external barriers to fully adopting WW informed habits and the ways in which variable participant support structures at home may complicate participant success. Participants compared WW to other health and weight management methods, and concluded that WW was more flexible. Most had tried other health programs or attempted to lose weight on their own by following diets such as the ketogenic diet or attempting to reduce calories. They noted that WW offers more support and is less restrictive than alternative weight loss solutions.

While a formal cost-effectiveness analysis was not conducted, the program’s full subsidy structure and the potential for reducing long term healthcare expenditures is notable. Improved diabetes management among program participants suggest employer- or payer-sponsored behavioral weight management interventions may represent a promising strategy for improving outcomes, warranting future cost-effectiveness evaluation. For healthcare providers considering similar no cost behavioral lifestyle interventions, critical elements for success include: food tracking, activity tracking, healthy recipes, and social support through in person and virtual meetings as well as positive social pressures to engage. Such programs should reduce participation barriers by offering financial incentives for engagement milestones (such as achievement rewards and gamification) and supporting competition among participants for extrinsic motivation. Similar programs should also expand flexible program meeting times and locations (including virtual options) and consider how to target support to participants with unsupportive social or home environments, Finally, the cultural relevance of program materials is an important facilitator to success of such interventions; similar programs should prioritize simplifying food tracking systems that include diverse cuisines.

This study has several limitations. Using a non-randomized, quasi-experimental design limits causal inference. To minimize bias and increase rigor, we employed propensity score matching to create balanced treatment and control groups based on observable characteristics. While demographic variables like race and income weren’t available, we included relevant clinical measures supporting our key outcomes analysis Small baseline differences remained between groups in the diabetes cohort, including obesity prevalence and GLP-1 medication use. To address this, GLP-1 use was included as a covariate in adjusted regression models and examined through interaction terms with WW enrollment. Further, sensitivity analyses and the inclusion of multiple covariates in regression models strengthened the credibility of our analysis. However, residual confounding remains possible, and self-selection bias may exist, as 1199SEIU members who enrolled in WW may differ from non-enrollees.

The qualitative component of the study included 14 WW participants who volunteered to participate in focus groups. While this sample size is modest, thematic saturation was achieved during analysis, through a dual-coder process, with recurring themes observed across discussions. Nevertheless, the relatively small number of participants limits the breadth of perspectives captured and may not fully represent the experiences of all program participants. These qualitative findings provide contextual insights into program interest, motivation, and engagement factors.

Another limitation is the relatively short follow-up period of approximately six months. While this timeframe allowed for detection of short-term changes in BMI and HbA1c, lifestyle interventions often demonstrate initial improvements that may diminish over time without sustained support. The current study design did not allow for evaluation of long-term maintenance of behavioral changes or clinical outcomes. Future studies should examine longer follow-up periods and explore strategies to sustain engagement and health improvements over time. Our design accounts for varying WW engagement by stratifying participants based on WW application activity tracking data, allowing us to describe relationships between intervention engagement levels and clinical outcomes.

Participants with diabetes had relatively well-controlled glycemia at baseline, with a mean HbA1c of approximately 7.0%. While this reflects typical clinical targets for many patients with type 2 diabetes, it may limit the generalizability of findings to individuals with poorer glycemic control. Because participants began the intervention near recommended HbA1c targets, the potential magnitude of improvement attributable to the behavioral intervention may have been constrained. Future research should examine the impact of similar interventions among populations with higher baseline HbA1c levels to better understand potential benefits across the full spectrum of disease severity.

A final limitation relates to the broader intervention context, which included the use of GLP-1 medications. While the WW program was fully subsidized for participants, GLP-1 medications are associated with substantial costs for employers and health systems. Because this study did not conduct a formal cost-effectiveness analysis and medication costs were not included in the evaluation, the economic implications of GLP-1 use remain an important consideration when assessing the real-world scalability of similar interventions. In many healthcare settings, access to GLP-1 medications is limited by cost or coverage restrictions, particularly for individuals with pre-diabetes. Future research should examine the cost-effectiveness of combined behavioral and pharmacologic interventions to better inform employer and payer decision-making. Other limitations include the potential for residual confounding, reliance on claims-based measures for clinical outcomes, and measurement of program engagement primarily through application-based activity metrics.

This study has several strengths, including the use of a large real-world population within an employer-sponsored health system, a propensity score–matched comparison group, integration of quantitative and qualitative methods, and stratification of outcomes by program engagement. The study population of 1199SEIU Benefit Fund members also provides insight into a diverse, working population, allowing examination of how a fully subsidized behavioral weight management intervention functions in a real-world setting. The diversity of the study population highlights the potential of employer- or payer-sponsored behavioral weight management programs to support health equity by expanding access to culturally relevant, fully subsidized interventions for individuals who may face systemic barriers to diabetes management.

Conclusion

In this quasi-experimental evaluation of a fully subsidized behavioral weight management program within a large labor–management health fund, WW participation was associated with improvements in BMI and glycemic outcomes among adults with diabetes and BMI reductions among those with prediabetes. Engagement level appeared to influence the magnitude of observed benefits. Integrating clinical data and focus groups, it highlights the role of behavioral and social factors in improving health outcomes. Findings also reveal the interaction between WW participation and GLP-1 use, where GLP-1 medications significantly reduce HbA1c and BMI. Further research on the combined effect of WW and GLP-1s on BMI and HbA1c is warranted. Beyond clinical impact, participants reported improvements in physical and mental well-being, reinforcing the program’s motivational benefits. This study informs employer-sponsored health initiatives and suggests future research should explore long-term engagement patterns and integration of behavioral and pharmacological treatments for optimizing diabetes care.
