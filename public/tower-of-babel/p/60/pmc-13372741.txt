Impact of crystal symmetry lowering on proton tunneling
Authors: S. S. Das, T. Ozawa, T. Kawauchi, H. Nakanishi, K. Fukutani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Considerable research has focused on the technological applications of vanadium-based materials for hydrogen (H) storage and separation. However, a fundamental understanding of H diffusion, particularly the role of crystal symmetry in its quantum behavior, remains incomplete. Here, we investigate H diffusion in an epitaxial vanadium (V) thin film through a combined approach involving structure analysis, hopping rate measurements, and quantum calculations. Nuclear reaction analysis of H depth profiles and lattice-site occupancy shows that H migration initiates around 70 K between the octahedral (Oz) sites in the uniaxially strained β-phase. The time evolution of electrical resistance, driven by changes in H distribution, enables quantitative estimation of the diffusion coefficient. Above 65 K, H exhibits thermally activated hopping with an activation energy of 148 meV. In contrast, in the cubic α-phase, H hopping remains significantly faster even at lower temperatures. Quantum state calculations reveal that, in the α-phase, H ground states are delocalized over tetrahedral sites due to tunneling, whereas in the β-phase, uniaxial strain suppresses tunneling, leading to localization around the Oz sites. These findings demonstrate that crystal symmetry plays a crucial role in quantum tunneling and, together with H-induced modulation of the potential energy landscape, governs phase-dependent H hopping behavior.

Introduction

Hydrogen (H) is the smallest and lightest of all elements and experiences minimal steric hindrance, making it easy to penetrate metal lattice and modify their physical properties. Whereas metal hydride systems offer a promising H storage medium in view of their high volumetric energy density and safety1,2, the metal-hydrogen interaction remains complex and a rich field in physics with many uncovered physical phenomena3–7. A fundamental understanding of the diffusion of hydrogen isotopes in metals and their alloys holds paramount importance for the viability of the metal hydrides for H-related applications2,4,8,9. At low temperatures, where the thermal over-barrier hopping process is suppressed, nuclear quantum effects (NQEs) such as zero-point vibration and quantum tunneling6,9–12 are expected to significantly influence the diffusion process.

Hydrogen atoms in a body-centered cubic (bcc) metal generally reside at the tetrahedral (T) and/or octahedral (O) interstitial sites as schematically shown in Fig. 1a. According to the Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin approximation13, the tunneling phenomenon is more prominent for a light particle through a narrow and shallow potential barrier. Since bcc metals possess a larger volume and a shorter distance between the neighboring interstitial sites in comparison with the face-centered cubic (fcc) metals9,12, the contribution of quantum tunneling to H diffusion is believed to be noticeable in bcc metals. Recent theoretical studies indeed suggested that NQEs play an important role in H diffusion in bcc metals14,15. Vanadium (V) possesses a bcc lattice and exothermically absorbs H. Although there are several works on the H diffusion at thermal regimes in the V–H system2,16, the NQEs on the diffusion is still poorly understood. Vanadium undergoes several phase transformations with the H concentration17,18. At low H concentrations, V forms a solid solution phase (α-phase) with a bcc structure. In the α-phase, H atoms preferentially occupy the T site (Fig. 1a), and the H hopping occurs along the direct T–T path with an activation barrier of 43–80 meV above 140 K19–21, which is consistent with the energy barriers of 74–136 meV obtained by Density functional theory (DFT) calculations22–25. With increasing H concentration, V forms two intermediary phases (β-phases) of VH0.5 and VH1 with a body-centered tetragonal (bct) structure and VH2 (γ-phase) with an fcc structure. In the β-phase, the 12 T and 6 O sites within a unit cell become inequivalent due to uniaxial strain, resulting in two kinds of inequivalent sites: Txy and Tz for the T sites, and Oxy and Oz for the O sites (Fig. 1b). It is revealed that H atoms reside at the energetically stable Oz sites between the V atoms along the z-axis26,27. DFT calculations suggest a crossover in the H site occupancy from T to Oz sites for c/a > 1.04322. The H diffusion is proposed to occur indirectly between the Oz sites via a network of Tz and Txy sites, as depicted schematically in Fig. 1b19,27–29. Molecular dynamics simulation studies show that the diffusion barrier in the β-phase is 252 meV28, while experimental studies report values in the range of 120–410 meV27,29–31. Despite extensive studies of H diffusion in V, however, the NQEs on the H hopping in both α- and β-phases have scarcely been discussed due to a lack of experimental data on H diffusion at low temperatures.

Here, we report a comprehensive study on the H diffusion and structure in an epitaxial V film at low temperatures. To observe the H hopping at low temperatures, we focus on the high sensitivity of electrical resistance to H migration. By hydrogenating the sample through H ion implantation at low temperatures, we first grow a nonuniform hydride and subsequently analyze the change in the distribution by nuclear reaction analysis (NRA) and its dynamics by probing the time evolution of resistance. The H diffusion rate is successfully derived from the resistance change in comparison with random walk simulations. In order to further examine the NQEs on the H diffusion, we calculate the quantum states of H by solving the Schrödinger equation for H in a three-dimensional potential derived by DFT calculations. It is shown that the H diffusion is fast in the α-phase, which is attributed to the quantum tunneling between the T sites. In contrast, the H diffusion in the β-phase is significantly slower. This diffusion occurs via thermal hopping between the Oz sites, where the quantum tunneling is suppressed due to the lowered crystal symmetry induced by uniaxial strain.

Results and discussion

H depth distribution and structure

An epitaxial vanadium film deposited on an MgO(001) substrate with a thickness of 40 nm was hydrogenated by means of H ion irradiation with an energy of 500 eV at 35 K. The (001) epitaxial growth and the cubic structure were confirmed by reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns shown in Figs. S1 and S2. Figure 2 shows the H depth distribution obtained by NRA at different temperatures (35, 70, and 200 K) after the hydrogenating with an H ion dose (Himp) of 10 mC. The H depth profile of the vanadium hydrides (VHx) film at 35 K shows a nonuniform distribution with a maximum x (=[H/V]) of ~1 at 8 nm up to a depth of 20 nm followed by a long tail with a uniform distribution (x ~ 0.05) into the bulk of the film (highlighted region). The latter is ascribed to the α-phase. The H depth distribution calculated by the TRIM (Transport of Ions in Matter) simulation, assuming the H implantation into V with an energy of 500 eV is also shown in the figure. The H depth profile at 35 K is consistent with the simulation, implying that most of the implanted H atoms remain at the depth at which they were implanted. In contrast, the H depth distributions at 70 and 200 K in Fig. 2 reveal an almost uniform distribution over the entire thickness (40 nm) of the sample with an average H concentration of x = 0.29. This indicates that the diffusion of H in a relatively large concentration region is limited below 70 K. On the other hand, the long, uniform tail extending into a deeper region with x ~ 0.05 at 35 K suggests fast H diffusion in the dilute α-phase even below 35 K. The integrated H amount remains almost unchanged at 35, 70, and 200 K, suggesting that neither H2 desorption nor migration into the substrate occurs below 200 K within the experimental sensitivity. Furthermore, the obtained nearly uniform depth distributions above 70 K suggest negligible interface effects for H trapping. Diffusion and desorption of H in VHx

The dynamics of H in VHx were investigated through simultaneous in-situ measurements of the resistance and thermal desorption spectroscopy (TDS) for mass 2 by heating the H-implanted samples. Figure 4a, b shows the TDS and electrical resistance data for the V film with different H implantation doses. The H implantation in the desorption measurements was carried out around 85 K to suppress the background H2 desorption signal. The sample was fully dehydrogenated upon heating to 540 K, and the initial resistance state (prior to the implantation) of the sample was recovered upon cooling, as also confirmed by NRA. The TDS data show that at lower doses (Himp <10 mC), apparent H2 desorption starts around 240 K with a desorption peak around 330 K. With increasing Himp dose, the desorption onset temperature decreases, and an additional peak appears around 220 K. The average H concentration at a dose of 20 mC at which the additional peak starts to appear is estimated to be ~0.5, as determined by NRA. According to the first-order desorption formula, using a frequency factor of 1013 s−1 and a heating rate of +0.3 K/s, the apparent activation energies for the two desorption peaks were roughly estimated to be ~1.0 and ~0.7 eV, respectively, neglecting the influences of internal diffusion and surface conditions. Therefore, the high- and low-temperature peaks are ascribed to the β1-phase (VH0.5) and β2-phase (VH1.0), respectively, based on the phase diagram of VHx systems, which exhibits a miscibility gap between the β₁- and β2-phases. These two desorption peaks are accompanied by a decrease in resistance as depicted in Fig. 4b, in the range of 200–350 K. Apart from this resistance decrease, an increase in the resistance was also apparently observed below 150 K. Considering the change in the H depth distribution observed below the nominal temperature of 70 K, as shown in Fig. 2, this increase in resistance is ascribed to the H migration into deeper regions of the film, resulting in a uniform H depth distribution. The transitions from nonuniform to uniform hydrides and then to pure V are depicted schematically in Fig. 4c along with their corresponding resistances. In the nonuniform hydride state, the sample consists of both a pure metallic film with low resistance (r) and a hydride phase with higher resistance (RH) in a parallel configuration. Measurements of H diffusion

Here, we examine temporal changes in resistance at various temperatures, which reflect the H diffusion in V films. The H-implanted sample at 35 K was brought to a desired temperature, and the resistance data were captured over time. The H ion dose was set to 10 mC, which corresponds to an average H concentration of 0.29. Representative time evolutions of the resistance are presented in a normalized form, R/R0(T), in Fig. 5, where R0(T) is the resistance at the starting point of the relaxation measurement. The data show two distinct regimes: one with resistance evolution in the upward direction for the temperature range below 120 K and the other with R/R0(T) in a downward direction for T > 240 K. The former regime is attributed to the transition from nonuniform to uniform distributions by H diffusion, and the latter to the transition from VHx to V due to H2 desorption. Note that the apparent upward resistance changes in the R-T measurements in Fig. 4 were observed above ~120 K, whereas the upward relaxations were observed even below 120 K in the resistance relaxation measurements in Fig. 5. This discrepancy arises from kinetic effects since the heating rate of +0.3 K/s used in the R-T measurements was significantly faster than the time constant of the resistance evolution at temperatures below ~120 K. The upward resistance relaxation data corresponding to H diffusion in the β-phase is analyzed based on a random walk numerical simulation, which is conceptually related with Fick’s second law of diffusive transport32,33. A detailed mathematical derivation of the random walk model equation from Fick’s second law is provided in the Splementary Information. In the random walk model, individual H atoms in the system are considered to hop randomly from one site to neighboring sites with a hopping probability. Here, we divide the entire V film into a finite number (N) of equal blocks within the framework of a one-dimensional diffusion model, where each block represents a thin V sheet. Note that we assume H is uniformly distributed in the in-plane direction within each sheet, because H atoms are implanted uniformly over the entire sample area. The time evolution of the H concentration of the n-th block (cn(t)) is simulated following Eqs. (S1)–(S3) as a result of the instantaneous H jump between the neighboring blocks. The initial concentration of each block (cn(t = 0)) was taken from the H depth profile at 35 K obtained by NRA in Fig. 2. A typical simulation result with N = 40 is shown in Fig. S4a.

In contrast to the slow diffusion in the β-phase, the uniform tail observed in the depth distribution at 35 K in Fig. 2 suggests fast diffusion in the α-phase at low temperatures. Although resistance changes at a lower Himp corresponding to x = 0.03 were monitored, no resistance relaxation was observed, showing that the H diffusion in the α-phase is sufficiently fast to reach the film thickness of 40 nm within the resistance measurement time scale of a few seconds (D > ~ 1 × 10−12 cm2/s). Note that the diffusion length of H in 100 s at 35 K is estimated to be 0.1 nm by extrapolating the thermal-diffusion coefficients at higher temperatures in the α-phase based on a previous paper34. Since this value is considerably smaller than the film thickness, it is suggested that the fast diffusion in the α-phase is caused by quantum tunneling.

Quantum-mechanical analysis for hydrogen state

To clarify the NQEs on the diffusion behavior of hydrogen in V, the quantum states of H(D) were calculated by solving the Schrödinger equation of the nuclei on the three-dimensional potential energy surface (PES) obtained by DFT calculations. For the calculations of the PESs, V54H(D)1 supercells (3 × 3 × 3 conventional unit cell) with a cubic (c/a = 1.00) and distorted (c/a = 1.10) structures were used, which emulate the α- and β-phases, respectively, and lattice relaxation of V atoms around an H atom was taken into account. Figure 7a, b shows the obtained three-dimensional PESs in the respective conventional unit cells. Stabilization around the Oz site was found in the distorted cell, consistent with a previous work22. Figure 7c, d shows the wavefunctions of the ground states calculated for H. Within the conventional bcc unit cell, the energetically lowest 12 eigenstates with degeneracies of 1, 2, 6, 2, and 1 with wavefunctions delocalized over the T sites were observed (Fig. 7c), suggesting the contribution of quantum tunneling. The calculated eigenenergies and their degeneracies are tabulated in Table S1, and the corresponding wavefunctions are shown in Fig. S8. In a bcc structure, there are 12 T sites per conventional unit cell, each having 4 first-nearest-neighbor T sites and 2 second-nearest-neighbor T sites. In the presence of tunneling between first-nearest‑neighbor T sites and between second-nearest‑neighbor T sites, with tunneling matrix elements t and t’, the 12-fold degenerate states with an eigenenergy of E0 are split into five levels of E0 + 4t + 2t’, E0 + 2t + 2t’, E0 − 2t’, E0 − 2t + 2t’ and E0 − 4t + 2t’ with degeneracies of 1, 2, 6, 2, and 1, respectively, which are obtained by diagonalizing a 12 × 12 tight‑binding Hamiltonian. By mapping the 12 numerically obtained eigenvalues onto the analytical expressions, the tunneling matrix elements were determined to be t = −0.61 (−0.06) meV and t’ = −0.06 (0.00) meV for H (D), respectively. The numerical fits exhibit a high degree of precision, with residuals consistently below 0.01%. The tight-binding model up to the second-nearest neighbor hopping successfully reproduced the characteristic asymmetric spacing observed in the eigenvalues of H (Table S1), suggesting that the model accurately captures the underlying potential landscape and the quantum interference of the wavefunctions within the system. While the absolute values of potential energy for the hydrogen motion may be subject to the inherent precision limits of DFT calculations, the calculated eigenvalues strictly adhere to the symmetry requirements of the 12 T sites in the bcc unit cell. The precise reproduction of the expected 1: 2: 6: 2: 1 degeneracy pattern demonstrates the high fidelity of the calculations in capturing relative energy splittings. This internal consistency justifies the use of these numerical results for the robust extraction of the hopping parameters t and t’. In contrast, in the bct structure, 2 degenerate lowest-energy eigenstates were obtained, each localized around a different Oz site (Fig. 7d), and an energy splitting was not observed. Note that the number of the Oz sites is 2 in the conventional unit cell. Importantly, the absence of an energy splitting indicates that the tunneling between the Oz sites is vanishingly small and practically absent within the numerical uncertainty of the present calculations. The first excited states of H(D), which are localized around the Oz sites, were found at an energy of 34(21) meV above the ground states, corresponding to the vibrational-excitation energy. No evidence of degeneracy lifting due to tunneling was found for these excited states either, indicating a strong suppression of tunneling in the distorted cell. Note that the deviation of the vibrational energy from the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sqrt{2}$$\end{document}2 scaling for the isotopes expected for a harmonic potential suggests anharmonicity of the potential shape around the Oz site, as seen in Fig. 7b. These calculations are in agreement with the present experimental findings: the fast H diffusion in the α-phase is attributed to the quantum tunneling between the T sites, and in contrast, in the β-phase, the tunneling probability between the Oz sites is negligibly small.

Based on the derived tunneling matrix elements t in the bcc structure, the hopping frequencies ν between neighboring T sites were estimated to be 3.0 × 1011 and 2.9 × 1010 s−1 for H and D, respectively, using the relation ν = ΔE/h proposed by Hund35 for the coherent tunneling timescale in a double-well potential, where h represents the Planck constant and the tunneling splitting ΔE is assumed to be 2t. According to the relation of the diffusion coefficient D with the hopping frequency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D=\,\nu {L}^{2}/6$$\end{document}D=νL2/6), where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L$$\end{document}L (= 1.06 Å) represents the distance between the T sites, the diffusion coefficients D were estimated to be 5.5 × 10−6 and 5.5 × 10−7 cm2/s for H and D, respectively. The corresponding one-dimensional diffusion lengths per 1 s were estimated to be ~33 and ~10 μm for H and D, respectively, which are much larger than the film thickness. It is worth emphasizing that the diffusion length estimated from the tunneling matrix element is consistent with our experimental observations that a uniform H distribution in the α-phase was achieved even at low temperatures in NRA, and that no resistance relaxation was observed at low temperatures in resistance relaxation measurements.

The mechanism of these different contributions of NQEs in the two phases can be explained by the influence of the hopping distance, barrier height, and energy level matching in a tunneling process. Figure 7e–h illustrate the potential energy curves in minimum energy paths obtained by DFT calculations using the nudged elastic band (NEB) method for H migrating along the direct T–T path in the cubic structure, and the direct Oz-Oz and indirect Oz-Tz-Txy-Tz-Oz and Oz-Tz-Oxy-Tz-Oz paths in the distorted structure. In the α-phase, the T sites are all equivalent, and the T–T direct path is the minimum energy path with an estimated barrier height of 0.122 eV. In contrast, the direct Oz-Oz path in the β-phase exhibits a significantly higher barrier of 1.025 eV, and its geometric distance is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2\sqrt{2}$$\end{document}22 times longer than that between the T sites, thereby making the tunneling almost unfavorable. Although the indirect Oz-Tz-Txy-Tz-Oz path offers a lower barrier of up to 0.228 eV, the quantum energy levels at the respective sites are mismatched, which reduces the tunneling probability.

Finally, we discuss the influence of many-body effects of hydrogen on its diffusion, which may become important at appreciable H concentrations, particularly in the β-phase. Blocking effects arising from site occupancy can reduce the effective jump frequency by a factor of (1–P), where P denotes the local H occupancy. Importantly, this effect primarily modifies the prefactor of the diffusion rate and does not contribute to the activation energy. Therefore, blocking effects are unlikely to be the dominant mechanism responsible for the higher H diffusion barrier and the suppressed tunneling in the β-phase. Regarding H–H interactions, although they are not explicitly included in our calculations using a distorted cell in a dilute limit, such hypothetical modelling has been adopted in a previous theoretical study and has demonstrated its validity in analyzing H behavior in the β-phase22. Moreover, the presence of multiple H atoms would tend to further lower the local symmetry of the system, potentially leading to even stronger suppression of tunneling. From these perspectives, while our single-impurity model does not fully incorporate all possible many-body contributions, it is nevertheless sufficient to clarify the essential physics governing the suppressed quantum nature in the β-phase from a crystal-symmetry viewpoint.

In conclusion, we have investigated the diffusion of hydrogen in a thin epitaxial V film through NRA, TDS, and resistance relaxation analysis. We achieved the formation of a nonuniform hydride phase by hydrogenation at a low temperature through low-energy ion irradiation technique. In the β-phase, H diffusion occurs above ~70 K as confirmed by NRA, and the desorption is primarily observed above 200 K by TDS. The electrical resistance change was observed due to the uniformization of the H distribution. The channeling NRA measurements experimentally revealed the Oz-site occupancy by H in the β-phase. We analyzed the H diffusion rate from the time evolution of resistance by random walk simulations. The temperature dependence of the H diffusion in the β-phase suggests thermal hopping between the octahedral (Oz) interstitial sites. In contrast, fast diffusion occurs in the α-phase at low temperatures. Quantum-mechanical calculations show significant quantum tunneling of H between the T sites in the α-phase, while such tunneling is not found between the Oz sites in the β-phase. The phase-dependent diffusion observed in V at low temperatures is a consequence of the NQEs of H, combined with the H-induced potential modulation associated with crystal symmetry. These results highlight the importance of the hydrogen-host metal interactions in shaping the nuclear quantum behavior of hydrogen.

Methods

Sample fabrication

A vanadium thin film sample with lateral dimensions of 8 × 8 mm and a thickness of 40 nm was fabricated on an MgO(001) substrate by electron beam evaporation from a high-purity (4 N) V source in a UHV system with a base pressure of 5 × 10−8 Pa. Prior to the sample growth, the substrate was heated in an oxygen gas (5 × 10−2 Pa) at a temperature of 900 K for 10 min. The deposition of V was performed at a rate of 0.32 nm/min at a sample temperature of 530 K, and the evolution of the film growth was monitored by RHEED (Fig. S1), confirming the (001) epitaxial growth. A lattice spacing along the [110] direction was estimated to be 4.32 Å from the RHEED pattern, corresponding to 3.06 Å along the [100] direction. This is close to the bulk value of 3.027 Å, implying a cubic structure within the accuracy of the present measurement. The crystal structure of the sample was also analyzed by XRD (Fig. S2), showing the (001) epitaxial growth. The lattice parameter was estimated to be 3.031 Å from the XRD pattern, consistent with the RHEED result. These results indicate that the epitaxial V film was considered to have a symmetric lattice structure in the absence of H.

Experimental characterization of hydrogen structure and diffusion

A four-probe technique was adopted to measure the resistance (R) of the sample in a separate UHV chamber with a base pressure of 5 × 10−8 Pa. The film was hydrogenated by means of H ion irradiation with an energy of 500 eV at 35 K to form a non-uniform H distribution. Note that vacancy formation due to the H implantation at 500 eV was calculated to be negligibly small (Fig. S6). Resistance measurements and TDS using a quadrupole mass spectrometer (QMS) were performed simultaneously by heating the H-implanted sample at a rate of +0.3 K/s to investigate H2 desorption and the corresponding change in resistance. In resistance relaxation measurements, the sample hydrogenated at 35 K was set to a desired temperature, and subsequently, the time evolution of its electrical resistance was captured. After each measurement, the sample was heated to 540 K to desorb hydrogen and refresh its state. Before the experiments, the sample was subjected to a few cycles of H loading-unloading until the H-induced resistance change became reproducible36. Surface-cleaning treatments such as ion sputtering or sufficient annealing were not applied.

Analyses on the H depth distribution and identification of its lattice location were performed by NRA via the 1H(15N,αγ)12C reaction at MALT, The University of Tokyo. An accelerated and monochromatized 15N2+ ion beam with a typical current of ~10 nm is delivered to a sample. The γ-ray emitted from the nuclear reaction is measured with Bi4Ge3O12 scintillators for NRA, and the ion signal backscattered from the sample is measured with a solid-state detector for RBS. A more detailed explanation of the NRA and RBS techniques can be found in refs. 37,38. In the present study, the H depth profile was obtained by repeating 20-s measurements with the beam on, followed by ~60-s beam-off intervals. Assuming that the sample temperature is primarily determined by the MgO substrate (10 × 10 × 0.5 mm) and is uniform throughout both the substrate and the 40-nm-thick V film, the temperature rise caused by the beam irradiation during the 20‑s acquisition period can be roughly estimated to be on the order of 10 K based on the heat capacity of MgO substrate and the beam current of ~10 nA. Although accurate determination of the actual temperature at the beam spot is difficult due to uncertainties in thermal conduction between the sample and the sample stage, as well as possible local heating on the beam spot (3 × 3 mm), the temperature rise is unlikely to significantly exceed this order of magnitude. Notably, this temperature uncertainty is present only in the NRA measurements and does not affect the H diffusion analysis. The 2D mappings of V-RBS and H-NRA in the channeling experiments were obtained after a three-point moving average of the yields in θ and φ directions.

The PES for an H atom in V is computed by DFT calculations using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) codes40,41 following the projected augmented wave method42. The generalized gradient approximation with Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof43 is used for the exchange-correlation function. The k-point meshes for the Brillouin zone were obtained using the special point sampling technique of Monkhorst and Pack44 with (6 × 6 × 6) sampling meshes. The cutoff energy for the wave functions is 600 eV. A 3 × 3 × 3 supercell of the conventional unit cell is employed with a lattice constant (a) of 3.00 Å, consisting of 54 V atoms and a single H atom.
