Persistence-Directed Testing of Chemicals Discharged from Offshore Oil Platforms Combined with Nontargeted Analysis
Authors: Mette T. Møller, Heidi Birch, Karina K. Sjøholm, Stefano Papazian, Aina C. Wennberg, Bénilde Bonnefille, Pia M. Kronsbein, Malcolm A. Kelland, Jonathan W. Martin, Philipp Mayer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Persistent chemicals discharged into the sea can pose long-term, irreversible risks. New approaches are needed to elucidate the number and type of persistent chemicals, particularly in complex mixtures and high-volume discharges. We introduce a novel “Persistence-Directed Testing” approach that combines environmentally relevant biodegradation tests with nontarget analytical methods. Complex produced waters from two offshore oil platforms in the North Sea served as case studies. In the biodegradation tests, produced waters were diluted 1:200 with seawater from the site of discharge (inoculum). Biotic test systems and abiotic controls were incubated at 9 °C. At day 60 nontarget analyses were performed by Solid Phase Microextraction coupled to GC-MS and Solid Phase Extraction coupled to LC-HRMS. Primary biodegradation and persistence were determined based on biotic/abiotic peak area ratios. Over 600 chemicals passed the quality control filtering criteria, and the persistent fraction of chemicals discovered by GC-MS was 4% and that discovered by LC-HRMS was 32–44%. Spectral library matches and computational modeling of the LC-HRMS data annotated several persistent chemicals as N-substituted aromatic ring structures. These findings demonstrate the value of a persistence-directed approach in uncovering the hidden burden of unidentified persistent chemicals in environmental discharges and highlight the urgent need for enhanced treatment of produced water discharges.

Introduction

The ocean provides many services that are crucial to life on earth. It generates half of the oxygen we breathe and all the seafood that so many people are dependent on. The ocean faces multiple stressors, including chemical pollution from commercial marine activities and industrial resource extraction, inadequate wastewater treatment, runoff from fields, and industrial discharges. Produced water, a byproduct from offshore oil and gas production, is an example of a large-volume industrial discharge. It is a complex chemical mixture that consists of production chemicals, petroleum-derived residues, other chemicals present in the formation water, and recycled injection water. Produced water is either discharged to the sea or reinjected into the reservoir. More than 240 million m3 of produced water are discharged to the North Sea and Norwegian Sea every year, including 3400 tonnes of dispersed oil and 160,000 tonnes of chemicals (data from 2020 to 2022). Some of the discharged chemicals will rapidly degrade and thus pose a local risk only near the discharge point. However, the continuous discharge of persistent chemicals is more problematic, since it may lead to increasing environmental concentrations over time, thereby increasing the probability of adverse effects in wildlife or humans. − Moreover, once persistent chemicals are present in the environment, removing them and reversing their risks is extremely difficult. , In order to limit chemical pollution and improve ocean health in the future, society should aim for zero persistent chemicals being discharged to the ocean. Produced water discharged to the North Sea is regulated under the OSPAR Convention (Convention for the Protection of the Marine Environment of the North-East Atlantic), restricting the discharge of dispersed oil to 30 mg/L (flow-weighted monthly average) and working on phasing out hazardous production chemicals. In OSPAR and for chemicals allowed on the European market, persistence is assessed for each chemical individually, typically based on laboratory biodegradation tests. , In the EU a chemical is considered persistent if the half-life in marine water is higher than 60 days. In 2023 more than 200 million chemicals were included in the CAS Registry, and new registrations are increasing with 10–20 million per year. It is likely that hundreds to hundreds of thousands of these new chemicals registered in CAS are persistent. With this vast increase in new chemicals, it is not possible to assess their risk individually. One study has estimated that of 95,000 industrial chemicals, biodegradation half-lives were available for only 216. As a society, we are therefore most likely discharging highly persistent chemicals without knowing, but the extent of this problem lacks empirical data and study techniques. In addition to standardized single-substance biodegradation tests, we urgently need new persistence testing approaches that can determine the number and ideally the structure of persistent chemicals in complex discharges such as those in produced water from offshore oil and gas production.

In this study, we introduce “Persistence-Directed Testing”, a concept inspired by effect-directed analysis (EDA). In EDA toxic chemicals in complex mixtures from contaminated environments are singled out by combining fractionation procedures, biotesting, and sensitive chemical analytical methods to identify toxicants. , In persistence-directed testing, persistent chemicals in complex discharges are singled out and potentially identified by combining biodegradation tests, sensitive chemical analytical techniques, and focused data treatment schemes.

The biodegradation testing method is an advancement of a recently developed biodegradation kinetic testing method, a modified OECD 309. The method was developed for chemicals in mixtures at low concentrations, including hydrophobic and volatile chemicals. , In recent years the method has continuously been advanced from testing composed mixtures to testing samples of unknown or variable composition, complex reaction products, or biological materials (UVCBs), such as petroleum mixtures and essential oils. , In the most recent progression of the method, it was used for testing the biodegradation kinetics of chemicals in complex mixtures being discharged to the environment, using native microorganisms in the receiving environment as inoculum. With this method, biodegradation kinetics were successfully obtained for more than a hundred chemicals discharged from an offshore oil platform measured with solid-phase microextraction (SPME) gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS).

The novelty of the persistent-directed testing in the present study is the design of the test and data treatment for persistence rather than biodegradation. This was achieved by combining a discharge sample (complex chemical mixture) and a seawater sample (native microorganisms), for determining the number, fraction, and, if possible, also the identity of those chemicals that are not degraded at the end of the test and are thus found persistent. The experiments were analyzed with both SPME GC-MS and nontarget solid-phase extraction (SPE) liquid chromatography-high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS), in order to extend the chemical space coverage and achieve a more comprehensive persistence assessment.

Nontarget analysis (NTA) techniques have developed and advanced rapidly in recent years. , It is now possible to detect thousands of chemicals in a sample without predetermining targets. By analyzing the sample with HRMS, the exact mass of both the precursor and molecular fragments of each feature detected can be obtained, which can be used for identification or annotation at various confidence levels. Limitations of NTA are that exact identification (confidence level 1) and quantification is difficult and time-consuming. In recent years, new developments of NTA workflows and software tools, such as in silico structure predictions , and feature-based molecular networking (FBMN), , allow increasingly more automated and higher-throughput molecular annotations (Level 2), structural elucidation (Level 3), and molecular formula assignments (Level 4). This has been successfully showcased for environmental applications. −

In the present study, we combine biodegradation testing with NTA to directly enable the simultaneous persistence assessment of a large number of chemicals, while providing identification information across varying levels of confidence.

The hypotheses were: 1) environmentally relevant biodegradation tests combined with advanced analytical methods can reveal persistent chemicals in complex discharges, and 2) based on a recent feasibility study, we expect a much higher fraction of persistent chemicals in produced water within the group of polar substances detectable by LC, compared to nonpolar chemicals detectable by GC. We also anticipate that NTA, combined with advanced computational workflows, can allow to reach a deeper molecular-level understanding of the persistent chemicals discovered in complex discharges.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Seawater was sampled in sterile 1 L PET bottles from Bürkle (Mikrolab Aarhus, Denmark) and produced water from offshore oil platforms (discharged chemical mixture) was sampled in DURAN 0.5 L glass bottles with a polybutylene terephthalate cap and a polytetrafluoroethylene-faced silicone liner. Ultrapure water (Milli-Q water) was produced using an Elga Purelab flex water system (Holm & Halby, Denmark). Glass syringes were rinsed with methanol (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥99.9%), ethanol (VWR Chemicals, 99.8%) and Milli-Q water. Methanol (VWR Chemicals, LC-MS grade) was used for solid-phase extraction. Sodium chloride (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥99.0%) was used for laboratory-prepared saltwater. Some petrochemicals were quantified in produced water by analyzing a reference mixture from LGC “ASTM Method D5769 Aromatics in Finished Gasoline, Calibration Level 1 without internal standard” (DRE-GS09000763, Middlesex, UK). The content of the reference mixture was: benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, m-xylene, p-xylene, o-xylene, isopropylbenzene, n-propylbenzene, 3-ethyltoluene, 4-ethyltoluene, 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene, 2-ethyltoluene, 1,2,4-trimethylbenzene, 1,2,3-trimethylbenzene, 1,4-diethylbenzene, n-butylbenzene, Indane, 1,2-diethylbenzene, 1,2,4,5-tetramethylbenzene (durene), 1,2,3,5-tetramethylbenzene (isodurene), naphthalene, 2-methylnaphthalene, and 1-methylnaphthalene.

Persistence-Directed Testing Approach

An overview of the persistence-directed testing approach is shown in Figure . Samples of produced water were received from two offshore oil platforms, referred to as Platform A and Platform B located <25 km apart. Seawater (native microorganisms) was sampled at Platform A and served as the inoculum for both Platform A and Platform B experiments. Experimental test systems consisted of produced water diluted in seawater (biotic) or in laboratory-prepared saltwater (abiotic). High dilution ratios were chosen to account for the high dilutions occurring in the sea and avoid inhibition of the microorganisms in the seawater while still ensuring a rich analytical data set. Before the biodegradation experiments were started, 30 min ecotoxicological tests of the produced water samples were performed to determine the necessary dilutions for avoiding inhibition. Parallel biodegradation experiments were then conducted, testing dilutions of produced water in seawater of 1:60, 1:200, and 1:500 for Platform A and 1:20, 1:60, and 1:200 for Platform B. Test systems were incubated at 9 °C according to EU regulation on biodegradation simulation tests for the marine environment. The incubation time was 60 days, since a chemical with a half-life exceeding 60 days in marine water is classified as persistent in European regulation. Chemical analyses consisted of GC-MS and LC-HRMS (separate vials). Peak area ratios (biotic peak area/abiotic peak area) were used to assess the persistence of each chemical. Peak area ratios close to 1 confirmed that the chemical was not biodegraded in the test and was therefore flagged as persistent in the ocean. On the other hand, peak area ratios close to 0 confirmed primary biodegradation of the chemical in the test. Tentative identifications of chemicals in the produced water mainly focused on substances flagged as persistent and were achieved by integrating different approaches, such as 1) suspect screening, 2) spectral library matching, and 3) FBMN combined with in silico structural predictions.

Overview of the comprehensive Persistence-Directed Testing Approach utilized in the current study. Seawater and produced water samples were received from offshore oil platforms and used for the experimental setup of biotic (seawater) test systems in parallel to abiotic (laboratory-prepared saltwater) test systems. After 60 days of incubation, test systems were analyzed by SPME GC-MS and SPE LC-HRMS. Data were interpreted based on biotic/abiotic peak area ratios, and chemicals were categorized as biodegraded or persistent. The number and fraction of persistent chemicals were determined for each platform, and some persistent chemicals were tentatively identified.

Sampling and Transportation: Produced Water and Seawater Inoculum

Samples were received on June 14, 2023, from two offshore oil platforms, Platform A and Platform B, both located in the North Sea. Seawater was sampled from Platform A (4 L) and was used as inoculum in all biotic test systems. The two platforms are located less than 25 km apart, and due to the rapid mixing and transport of seawater in the area, the seawater sampled at Platform A was considered sufficiently representative for the Danish section of the North Sea oil field. The seawater was sampled from an injection water pipe. At this sampling point, seawater is continuously pumped to the platform from 21.5 m below the seawater surface level, and the sampling point is located before any addition of chemicals or treatments. Additionally, 2 L of produced water was received from both platforms, sampled from a tap on the platforms after all treatment processes before it is being discharged to the sea at a depth of 26 m. All samples were taken under stable process conditions. Seawater was collected in bottles with headspace to ensure aerobic conditions, whereas produced water was collected without headspace. The samples were transported to shore by a helicopter in cooling boxes. At the shore, cooling elements were added. The total transportation time from the sampling time to arrival at the laboratory was 12–14 h for both platforms. The transport temperature of the seawater was tracked with an EasyLog USB temperature logger and was 16 °C ± 1 °C during helicopter transport and 14 °C ± 1 °C during car transport. The sampled seawater was stored at 9 °C (incubation temperature) and the produced water at 4 °C until the experiments commenced within 40 h from the sampling time. Before the experiments were started, produced water and seawater samples were filtered through a 25 μm (pore diameter) cellulose paper filter (Whatman) to avoid particles in the test systems. The biodegradation experiments were started without any additional pretreatment or preservation of the samples.

Water Quality: Produced Water, Seawater, and Laboratory-Prepared Saltwater

At Platform A the produced water sample had a pH of 7.0, and a temperature of 34.5 °C, and the temperature of the seawater sample was 11 °C before shipment. At Platform B the produced water had a pH of 8.9 and a temperature of 44.4 °C.

Laboratory-prepared saltwater (ocean strength) was prepared in sterile PET bottles by mixing sodium chloride in Milli-Q water. In the laboratory the salinity of all samples was measured with a WTW portable conductivity meter (3310) and found to be 52.7 g/L for produced water from Platform A, 45.5 g/L for produced water from Platform B, 34.3 g/L for the seawater sample, and 35.6 g/L in the laboratory-prepared saltwater.

The nonvolatile organic carbon (NVOC) of the seawater was 2.1 mg/L (standard deviation (SD) ± 0.3 mg/L, n = 3), the total organic carbon (TOC) in produced water from Platform A was 203 mg/L (SD ± 7 mg/L, n = 3), and the TOC in produced water from Platform B was 373 mg/L (SD ± 3 mg/L, n = 3). The total Nitrogen in produced water from Platform A was 38.6 mg/L (SD ± 0.5 mg/L, n = 3) and from Platform B, it was 45.8 mg/L (SD ± 0.8 mg/L, n = 3).

Subsamples of produced water from both platforms, seawater, and laboratory-prepared saltwater were stored at 4 °C, and 9 days after sampling they were filtered through 0.2 μm membrane filters (Millipore) for DNA analysis. The filters were sent to DNASense (Aalborg, Denmark) for DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. Details of the filtered volumes and methods are presented in SI 1. The purpose of the DNA analysis was to characterize the seawater and produced water samples. DNA results are presented in SI 1. Since the samples were stored for 9 days, possible changes to the microbial abundance and diversity must be considered. Raw sequencing reads are available on GenBank under BioProject PRJNA1311330.

Ecotoxicological Tests of Produced Water

Ecotoxicological tests of the produced water samples followed a modified ISO 11348-3 test, testing inhibition of luminescence in marine bacteria (Aliivibrio fischeri) (microtox test) as described in Møller et al. (2024). Filtered (25 μm) produced water samples were tested at dilutions of 1.25%, 2.5%, 5%, 10%, 20%, and 40% (v/v).

Inhibition of the Inoculum by the Produced Water

The day after sampling, subsamples of 10 mL of produced water (filtered, 25 μm) from each platform and 300 mL of seawater (filtered, 25 μm) were shipped in a cooling box to NIVA, Oslo, for parallel experiments, testing the inhibition of the inoculum by the produced water through measurements of bacterial cell count (method described in SI 2). Glass vials (20 mL) with screw caps were sterilized by autoclaving. Seawater and produced water were added at the same dilutions as those for the biodegradation experiment, with three replicates per treatment. The test systems had a total volume of 9 mL and were incubated with horizontal shaking at 9 °C in the dark. Sampling was done on day 0, 3, 6, 10, 12, 14, 28, 47, and 60 by pipetting 3 × 10 μL from each vial for cell count by flow cytometry. Inhibition was assessed based on cell count compared to the nonexposed control (seawater).

Preparation of Biodegradation Test Systems

Preparation of test systems followed the procedure described in Møller et al. (2024). In brief, at the start of the experiment, biotic test systems (seawater containing native microorganisms) and abiotic test systems (laboratory-prepared saltwater without microorganisms) were prepared in 20 mL amber glass gastight autosampler vials (ML VSH2018RA, Mikrolab Aarhus, Denmark) with gastight stainless-steel screw caps and a PTFE-lined silicone septum (SU860101, Supelco, USA). Seawater and laboratory-prepared saltwater were transferred to the vials with an Eppendorf multipipette. Produced water (discharged chemical test mixture) was spiked to both types of test systems with gastight Hamilton or SGE glass syringes (Trajan). Dilutions of 1:20, 1:60, 1:200, and 1:500 were spiked with 750, 250, 75, and 30 μL of produced water respectively to a total volume of 15 mL in the test system. Abiotic test systems served as controls to correct for abiotic losses; however, as small amounts of unsterilized produced water were used, they were not fully sterile. The expected impact on the results is discussed in Section . The vials were closed and shaken directly after spiking. Aerobic conditions in the test systems were ensured by having 5 mL of headspace in the vials, which was verified by oxygen concentration measurements during incubation (see SI 9). The vials were incubated on a benchtop laboratory roller at ∼30 rpm for 60–64 days at 9 °C. One set of vials for analysis consisted of triplicate biotic and triplicate abiotic test systems (n = 6). One set of vials was prepared for each tested dilution (3 dilutions), for each platform (2 platforms), and for both GC-MS and LC-HRMS measurements (2 analytical methods). In total, 72 test systems were prepared for the main experiment. Blanks consisted of 15 mL of laboratory-prepared saltwater and were included in all analytical sequences.

Additional test systems of dilution 1:200 for platform A and 1:60 for platform B were prepared for LC-HRMS analysis on days 14 and 28. The results for platform A are presented in SI 11, the 1:60 dilution for platform B was above EC10 and the results are therefore not included.

Sample Preparation and Chemical Analysis

Solid-Phase Microextraction Coupled to GC-MS

After 60 days, test systems were transferred directly from the incubator to the GC autosampler (PAL RSI 85; CTC Analytics AG, Switzerland) without any pretreatment or conservation. Automated SPME, with a 30 μm PDMS fiber (Supelco, USA), was applied to unopened test systems by direct immersion. The SPME was thermally desorbed on the GC column (122-5562UI DB-5 ms Ultra Inert column, 60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent), where the analytes were separated by the GC (7890B; Agilent Technologies, USA). They were then detected by the MS (5977A MSD; Agilent Technologies, USA), in electron ionization full-scan mode (m/z 50–500) to include a large number of nonpolar chemicals in produced water. The analytical method is described in detail in Møller et al. (2024). The 1:200 dilution from both platforms was analyzed on day 60. On the following 4 days, the remaining dilutions from both platforms were analyzed, due to the number of samples and the analysis time per sample. Each GC-MS analysis run consisted of triplicate alternating biotic and abiotic test systems at one dilution from one platform, with blanks at the beginning and the end. The biotic and abiotic test systems analyzed directly after each other were used as pairs for calculating the peak area ratios.

Solid-Phase Extraction

Samples for LC-HRMS analysis were preserved on day 60 by adding 800 μL of methanol (≈5%, approximately 2 times higher than the EC50 of methanol reported for microorganisms in activated sludge) and the samples were then stored at 2 °C (less than 10 days). Each sample was then spiked with a mixture of isotope-labeled internal standards (see SI 3) to a total concentration of 0.38 ng/mL and extracted with solid-phase extraction (SPE) (HLB Oasis, 150 mg, 30 μm). SPE cartridges were first conditioned with 5 mL of methanol (MeOH; VWR Chemicals, LC-MS grade) and then equilibrated with 5 mL of Milli-Q water. For each sample, 5 mL of the solution was loaded onto the SPE, washed with 3 mL of 5% MeOH in water, left to dry for 30 min under vacuum, and eluted with 5 mL of MeOH at a rate of approximately 1 drop per second. The eluents were stored at −18 °C. Two weeks after the experiments were stopped, the MeOH eluents were transferred to 5 mL Eppendorf tubes with screw cap (part number: 0030 122.305) and shipped to the National Facility for Exposomics, SciLifeLab (Sweden). Here, the samples were concentrated to a nominal volume of approximately 1 mL under a gentle nitrogen stream (0.6 L/min) using a TurboVap (Biotage) with a thermal bath set at 30 °C for 1 h. The final concentrates were vortexed, split into two 500 μL aliquots, and centrifuge-filtered (0.2 μm nylon, Thermo Fisher) for 10 min at 10,000 rpm and 4 °C. One set of the 500 μL aliquots was then spiked with a volumetric standard of diuron-d6 (final concentration of 1 ng/mL), transferred into an LC analytical vial with an insert (PTFE/silicone septa; Thermo Fisher), and stored at 4 °C until analysis by LC-HRMS. The remaining aliquots were stored at −20 °C, for archive. A quality control (QC) sample was also created by transferring 10 μL from each extract (n = 10, including procedure blanks) into a separate LC vial.

LC-HRMS Analysis

Analyses by LC-HRMS for nontarget qualitative profiling were performed using a Vanquish Horizon system interfaced with heated electrospray ionization (HESI) to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 (Thermo Fisher). Each sample was acquired twice using ionization in positive (HESI+) and negative (HESI−) modes. Clean extracts were injected (25 μL) on a C18 Waters Acquity column (UPLC BEH C18, 3.0 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm particle) combined with an Acquity precolumn (BEH C18, 2.1 mm × 5 mm, 1.7 μm). Reverse-phase chromatography was performed using LC-MS grade (Optima) mobile phases (A) Milli-Q water with 1 mM ammonium fluoride (NH4F), and (B) 100% MeOH. Spectral acquisition alternated between full-scan MS1 (m/z 90–1000, ∼1.5 Hz, nominal resolution 240,000 at m/z 200) and nontarget data-independent (DIA) MS2 acquisition using four sequential precursor isolation windows of 250 m/z (i.e., m/z 89–341, 339–591, 589–841, 839–1091). Additional details are presented in SI 3.

Data Preprocessing

Raw data from both GC-MS and LC-HRMS were preprocessed using the open-source software MS-DIAL (v.4.9) allowing chromatographic alignment across samples, spectral deconvolution, peak area integration, and extraction of feature lists (detailed MS-DIAL processing parameters are reported in SI 4 and SI 5). The feature list for the GC-MS data set included a retention time (Rt), quantitative mass (base-peak ion), associated EI deconvoluted mass spectra, and the peak area for each sample analyzed. For the LC-HRMS data set, the feature lists included Rt, accurate MS1, deconvoluted (DIA) MS2 spectra for both HESI+ and HESI– modes, and a peak area for each sample analyzed. Features were exported from MS-DIAL to excel using a filter criterion of one sample peak area >5 times (GC-MS) or >10 times (LC-HRMS) higher than the average of the peak area in blanks. Blanks included procedural and experimental blanks (incubated seawater and incubated and nonincubated laboratory-prepared saltwater) for the GC analyses (n = 12), and instrumental, procedural, and experimental blanks (MeOH, SPE, and incubated and nonincubated laboratory-prepared saltwater) for the LC analyses (n = 12). LC-HRMS data were normalized by internal standards to correct for instrumental variation. The GC sequence was set up with biotic and abiotic test systems analyzed in pairs, in order to account for minor losses and instrument drift.

Quality Control Filter and Peak Area Ratios

Only features originating from produced water were included in the data treatment. This was ensured by initial filtering, selecting features present in a high-concentration sample of produced water (1:10 for LC-HRMS, 1:60 for GC-MS) with an RSD < 20% of replicates and a peak area above the blank area cutoff (eq ). The LC-HRMS data set was curated by blank subtracting the average peak area measured in two procedural blanks (MeOH eluted on SPE). Primary Biodegradation and Persistence Assessment

A feature was categorized as persistent if all triplicate peak area ratios were >0.5. If all triplicate peak area ratios were <0.5 the feature was categorized as (primary) biodegraded and if none of the criteria above were fulfilled it was categorized as ambiguous. Annotation primarily focused on the features categorized as persistent in at least one of the produced water samples.

Molecular Annotations of the GC-MS Data Set

Tentative annotation of SPME GC-MS features was performed with the software MassHunter Unknown Analysis (Version 10.2/Build 10.2.733.8.8, Agilent Technologies). Processing parameters are reported in SI 8. Triplicate analysis of a high concentration (1:20 dilution) of the two produced water samples was used for the annotation of features categorized as persistent by comparing measured mass spectra to the reference spectral library NIST17.

Molecular Annotations of the LC-HRMS Data Set

A high-concentration sample of produced water (1:10 dilution) was analyzed in triplicate by LC-HRMS for both platforms and used for the annotation of molecular features. Three different methods were used to assign molecular structures: 1) suspect screening of known production chemicals from a precompiled list, 2) spectral library matching, and 3) FBMN combined with in silico structural elucidation, in addition to molecular formula calculations.

Suspect Screening of Production Chemicals

Multiple information sources were used to compile a comprehensive suspect list (i.e., “top-down” annotation) of production chemicals known to be used on offshore oil platforms and potentially being discharged to the sea. Documents from the Danish EPA were requested, resulting in a list of products approved for use on offshore oil platforms. Chemical constituents in these products were retrieved from safety data sheets (SDS), though for many products, the SDS were not publicly available or reported only a few constituents. Chemical suppliers were approached by presenting the project at a quarterly meeting in the European Oilfield Specialty Chemicals Association (EOSCA) in Aberdeen, September 2022, and subsequently meetings with individual chemical suppliers. The chemical supply market is very competitive, and most information is therefore confidential, but a list of 8 compounds was retrieved. Expert counseling by Malcolm A. Kelland provided additional information on which chemicals are typically applied at offshore oil platforms, and these were added to the suspect list. The complete suspect list consisted of 55 chemicals and contained both production chemicals expected to be either persistent or biodegradable (SI 7). Exact masses of the production chemicals from the suspect list were compared to exact masses (±H) measured with LC-HRMS. Potential matches were considered for a mass deviation of a maximum of 0.005 Da. For each match, information on the suspect production chemical was compared to the measured feature in SIRIUS, evaluating the match of in silico predicted molecular formula, predicted structure, and MS2 fragments. Annotations were reported with confidence Level 2b based on Schymanski et al. (2014).

Spectral Library Matching

Spectral library matching annotations were based on MassBankEU (https://massbank.eu/), MassBank of North America (MoNA; https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/), and GNPS (https://gnps.ucsd.edu/). Annotations were reported with confidence Level 2a based on Schymanski et al. (2014), considering an accurate MS1 mass deviation of a maximum of 0.001 Da between the detected precursor ion and the library record, and an MS2 spectral matching with a total identification score assigned by MS-DIAL of >60 (i.e., corresponding to dot-product scores >60, or reverse dot-product scores >75). All spectral matches were manually inspected for diagnostic fragments with MS2 accurate mass.

Molecular Networking and In Silico Structural Elucidation

Processed LC-HRMS results from Platforms A and B were exported to the Global Natural Product Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) web platform (https://gnps.ucsd.edu) and clustering of molecular features was performed via the FBMN workflow (version 28.2). The network was constructed with a tolerance setting of 0.02 Da for both MS1 and MS2, and edges between nodes (features) represented spectral similarity for a minimum cosine score of 0.65, with at least two shared MS2 spectral peaks. Results were visualized using the software Cytoscape (v3.10.3), whereby each node (feature) was color-coded in red if it was categorized as persistent in at least one platform, according to the criteria described in Section . Analysis of the molecular networks focused on clusters containing at least three persistent features. The MS1 and MS2 data of these features were further analyzed with the software SIRIUS (v.5.8.6) for in silico structural prediction via compound database search (CSI:FingerID module), de novo structure generation (MSNovelist module), and chemical class prediction (CANOPUS: Superclass) (CANOPUS module). Putative structures were considered within the top-ranked (maximum top-6) in silico candidates matching the top-ranked (top-1) molecular formula, calculated within a maximum of 3 ppm mass error. In this process (i.e., “bottom-up” annotation) closely related formulas and structures of neighboring nodes within the same FBMN cluster were taken into account. In addition, molecular formulas were calculated in SIRIUS for the entire LC-HRMS data set (batch analysis). SIRIUS processing parameters are reported in SI 6.

Tested Concentration Range

Reference petrochemicals were measured by SPME-GC-MS to generally be in the μg/L range for produced water and in the ng/L range in the actual biodegradation tests (calibrations with the LGC reference mixture). The TOC in the produced water was 203 mg/L for Platform A and 373 mg/L for Platform B and the produced water thus contributed with approximately 1 and 2 mg/L to the tests at 1:200 dilution, respectively.

Results and Discussion

Ecotoxicity and Dilution Effect

We previously highlighted the importance of selecting an appropriate dilution factor when determining the biodegradation and persistence of chemicals in discharges: too high a dilution (low concentration) will reduce the number of chemicals that can be measured even with sensitive analytical techniques, whereas too low a dilution (high concentration) will inhibit the microorganisms. Here, the results of a 30 min microtox test were used to determine test dilutions in the biodegradation experiments.

We show the inhibition of the marine bacteria (microtox) by the produced water in relation to the tested dilutions in the biodegradation experiments (Figure A and SI 10). In addition to the fast (30 min) single-species microtox test, the inhibition of the natural seawater bacteria exposed to different dilutions of produced water was compared to a nonexposed seawater control during the 60 days incubation (Figure B and SI 2). Bacterial growth was observed in all produced water dilutions on day 0–6, although samples at the highest dilution (lowest concentration) displayed a relatively higher growth compared to the samples at the lowest dilution (highest concentration). This indicates that the produced water contained both growth-promoting factors, and some factors that inhibited growth at higher concentrations. The inhibition at high concentration increased from day 10. No inhibition was observed at a dilution of 1:200. This method measures total cell count, which might mask the differences in responses between subpopulations that were inhibited and others that grew fast. A greater variation in total cell count between triplicate test systems was observed after day 14 (SI 2).

At the start of the experiment, the cell count in produced water was 0.76 (Platform A) and 1.9 (Platform B) times that of seawater (Figure B), and the microorganisms in produced water were expected to be adapted to higher temperatures than 9 °C. In the environment, rapid dilution of produced water after discharge means that the biodegradation of chemicals in produced water is expected to be driven by the microorganisms in the seawater. The high dilution ratio of 1:200 of the produced water in the seawater ensures that also in the experiment the microorganisms primarily originated from the seawater. The microorganisms from the produced water in the test systems are not expected to influence the results categorized as persistent because potential specific degraders present in the produced water were added to both biotic and abiotic test systems, and features degraded by them would be removed in the quality control filters.

The DNA results showed that the 4 most abundant classes in seawater were Gammaproteobacteria, Bacteroidia, Campylobacteria, and Alphaproteobacteria. Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria are known marine hydrocarbon-degrading bacteria classes. Additional DNA results are presented in SI 1.

The unfiltered GC-MS data set consisted of 492 features at Platform A and 935 features at Platform B. The complete LC-HRMS data set consisted of more than 49,000 features, considering all samples from both platforms. After quality control filters, the overall number of features was drastically reduced but ensured high quality of the features assessed for persistence. More features could be measured at lower dilution (higher concentration) across both platforms (Figure C,D). At a dilution of 1:200, the number of features passing the quality control filters was at Platform A: 144 GC-MS features and 191 LC-HRMS features and at Platform B: 281 GC-MS features and 416 LC-HRMS features.

Primary Biodegradation and Persistence Assessment

Primary biodegradation and persistence were assessed at a dilution of 1:200 based on peak area ratios (biotic/abiotic) of test systems analyzed (GC) or extracted (LC) on day 60. Features were categorized as persistent, when all triplicate peak area ratios were greater than 0.5, corresponding to less than 50% biodegradation (see Section ). Biodegradation and persistence assessment of chemicals in produced water from Platform B at 1:200 dilution from mean (n= 3) peak area ratio. A) Features measured with GC-MS and B) Features measured with LC-HRMS. Green = primary biodegraded, yellow = ambiguous, and red = persistent (see Section ).

Assessment of the GC-MS data resulted in 3–4% persistent features for Platform A (6/144 features) and Platform B (10/279 features) (Figure A and SI 11), consistent with previous GC-MS results from a biodegradation experiment conducted one year earlier with produced water from Platform A, where the persistent fraction was also 4%. In the present study, additional assessment performed on LC-HRMS data yielded 32% persistent features (61 of 191 features) for Platform A and 44% persistent features (183 of 416 features) for Platform B (Figure B and SI 11). Although the chemical space covered by the two analytical methods has only minor overlap, some chemicals might still be detected by both GC-MS and LC-HRMS, as for instance was shown for the potentially persistent chemical N-butylbenzenesulfonamide, in our previous feasibility study. The large extent of biodegradation (>95%) observed in the GC-MS data set shows that microorganisms in the test systems were active (degrading).

In the GC-MS data set, 20 of the petrochemicals in the reference mixture were identified in the produced water samples from both platforms, and they were all biodegraded (benzene and toluene elute too early to be included). No spectral library matches were found for the few persistent features in the GC-MS data sets, which thus remained unidentified. In comparison, many more persistent features were detected by LC-HRMS, and the high-resolution MS data was leveraged for tentative identification of these chemicals using a combination of different workflows.

Annotation of Persistent Chemicals by Spectral Library Matching

A total of 515 features measured by LC-HRMS from both Platforms A and B passed the quality control filter. Hereof, 15 were detected in both positive and negative modes, and duplicates were discarded. Of the 500 features, 38 chemicals (7.6%) were annotated at level 2a (SI 12). In total, 211 features were flagged as persistent, of which 18 of these could be annotated at level 2a (15 unique names), while 91.5% were unidentified persistent chemicals. Spectral libraries are records of reference compounds that have been previously measured. They are therefore biased toward chemicals that are frequently detected in the environment and for which reference standards are available, e.g., pharmaceuticals. Based on publicly available information on the typical use of the chemicals and based on expert judgment, the list of 15 annotated chemicals was evaluated, and 11 chemicals with a plausible relation to offshore oil platforms are presented in Figure B (the full list is presented in SI 12).

Feature-Based Molecular Networking and In Silico Structural Predictions

The results of the FBMN analysis showed that 7 clusters (5 clusters in HESI+ and 2 in HESI−) had at least 3 persistent features (nodes). These 7 clusters contained a total of 46 persistent features, of which nearly all (43) could be assigned a molecular formula and chemical class. Based on information from all the persistent features, the clusters were categorized as hydrocarbon derivatives and organic nitrogen compounds (7 clusters), organic oxygen compounds (6 clusters), and benzenoids (2 clusters).

In silico molecular structure predictions gave information about potential candidates for 36 persistent chemicals (SI 6). Zero Persistent Discharges to the Sea

This study validates the hypothesis that a much higher fraction of persistent chemicals was found within the group of polar (LC) relative to nonpolar (GC) chemicals in produced water discharges. Initially, it was expected that the GC-MS analysis would primarily cover petrochemicals and LC-HRMS would primarily cover production chemicals. However, the number of chemicals measured by LC-HRMS is much larger than the number of chemicals generally added during production. It is therefore clear that most chemicals measured by LC-HRMS are petroleum-related chemicals. Especially annotations of N-substituted aromatic ring structures categorized as persistent were observed in both the FBMN analysis and the spectral library matches. Examples are N-methylquinolines, previously reported to be methylquinoline transformation products and present at tar oil-contaminated sites. This information points to the need for an increased focus on the less-investigated nitrogen heteroatoms that might be overlooked in analytical methods optimized for the nonpolar fraction of petroleum. , Overall, the high number and fraction of persistent chemicals measured by LC-HRMS are very concerning, considering the large volumes of produced water discharged from offshore oil platforms. The continuous discharge of persistent chemicals into the environment poses a significant risk, and long-term effects are highly unpredictable. While regulation of production chemicals with the aim to substitute chemicals of concern is important, treatment optimizations to remove polar chemicals in produced water are also needed to achieve the goal of zero persistent chemicals being discharged to the sea.

Unidentified Persistent Chemicals

Only a few persistent chemicals were tentatively identified. A large fraction of unidentified chemicals is a common challenge in NTA. Hollender et al. (2019) suggested an increased openness from regulators and industry on tonnage, MS spectra, and possibly even reference materials of marketed chemicals. This could facilitate a more comprehensive suspect screening and a higher number of chemicals identified in future NTA studies.

The lack of detection of production chemicals in the produced water at the 1:200 dilution level illustrates the trade-off between biodegradation testing at high concentrations, where a higher number of chemicals can be tracked, and the testing at low concentrations, where inhibition of microorganisms is avoided and environmental conditions are simulated more realistically. It highlights the importance of sensitive analytical methods and suggests that targeted methods might be needed to further evaluate the presence and persistence of production chemicals in produced water. This would require increased openness from the chemical industry and platform operators to disclose the composition and use of production chemicals.

Retrospective analysis of NTA data is possible, especially if the raw data is archived in public repositories. , An advantage of this study is that since exact masses and mass spectra are available (see Data Availability) and have been assessed for persistence (SI 13), retrospective analysis could allow the identification of currently unidentified persistent chemicals if more identification information becomes available.

Persistence-Directed Testing

This study introduces persistence-directed testing and validates the hypothesis that environmentally relevant biodegradation tests combined with advanced analytical methods can reveal persistent chemicals in complex discharges. A limitation of this study is that the chemicals categorized as “biodegraded” only cover primary biodegradation and not potential persistent metabolites. More research is needed to assess persistent metabolites, e.g., by analyzing peaks that are significantly more abundant in biotic test systems compared to abiotic test systems after 60 days of incubation. If persistent metabolites are found, backtracking the parent molecule in the discharge can be challenging but is possible to approach using predicted degradation pathways, e.g., from specific mass shifts (e.g., 15.996 Da for oxygen attachment) as suggested by Tisler et al. (2022), or using dedicated software such as BioTransformer (https://biotransformer.ca/) or EnviPath (https://envipath.org/).

To improve the method, future studies could use sterilized inocula and discharge samples as abiotic controls. Furthermore, as analytical sensitivity increases, future studies can potentially come closer to investigating how the biodegradation process is affected by the extreme dilutions occurring in the ocean, and thereby improving environmental relevance even further.

The persistence-directed testing approach introduced in this study can be used to assess the extent of the problem of persistent chemicals in various types of discharges. An advantage of the approach is that a complex mixture of chemicals can be assessed in one biodegradation study, while the highly time-consuming work of feature identification can be narrowed down by focusing on persistent features. If persistence-directed testing approaches are adopted more widely and identification of the persistent fraction becomes easier with the very rapid development of the NTA field, it will become more and more difficult to unnoticed discharge large quantities of persistent chemicals. The approach can also lead to the identification of new groups of persistent molecules and an improved understanding of persistence in the environment.
