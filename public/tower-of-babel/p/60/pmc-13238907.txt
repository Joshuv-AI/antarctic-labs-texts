Missed Clinic Appointments for Human Papillomavirus‐Related Oropharyngeal Cancer
Authors: John M. Vieth, Anthony A. Mangino, Melina J. Windon, Joseph Valentino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMissing appointments delay treatment affecting patient outcomes and healthcare resource utilization. Adherence to scheduled appointments has not been studied in patients with HPV‐related oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HPV‐OPSCC).MethodsWe collected data from 36,690,000 scheduled medical outpatient encounters at the University of Kentucky Health Care from 2012 to 2021. A total of 36,579 of these encounters were from 334 patients with HPV‐OPSCC. We performed regression analysis of no‐show appointments by demographics, tumor characteristics, and insurance factors.ResultsThe no‐shows rate for appointments was significantly higher for the HPV‐OPSCC cohort (4.6% vs. 2.2% p < 0.001). The HPV‐OPSCC cohort missed 36% of their appointments. Black, Hispanic, younger, those who have ever smoked and patients with advanced tumor stage all had statistically significantly higher no‐show rates (p < 0.01).ConclusionsTreatment delay impacts cancer treatment efficacy and no‐show appointments burden medical systems. Further research into targeted interventions is needed to lower barriers for attending appointments.

Introduction

Kentucky has among the highest incidence and mortality rates of head and neck cancers in the United States and is a geographic hotspot for HPV‐related oropharyngeal cancer (HPV‐OPSCC) [1, 2, 3]. Importantly, missed clinic appointments are well known to contribute to delayed treatment initiation, increased healthcare costs, and worsened cancer survival outcomes [4]. We have concerns that poor outcomes among patients with HPV‐related oropharyngeal cancer may follow if this observation is correct, necessitating a more rigorous examination of the scope and contributing factors associated with appointment non‐attendance in this patient population.

Treatment delays can lead to upstaging of HPV‐OPSCC prior to treatment initiation or may lead to missed early detection opportunities at recurrence, each of which can not only impact oncologic outcomes, but can also add to the financial health care burden of this disease [5]. Oropharyngeal cancer has now surpassed cervical cancer as the most common HPV‐related malignancy in the United States [6, 7]. The financial burden of HPV‐OPSCC treatment is significant and estimated to be 1.9 billion dollars per year from 2014 to 2018 in the United States [8]. Improving appointment attendance can drive improved healthcare efficiency and potentially better serve our population. Timely, efficient, effective treatment for this at‐risk population could result in health care system cost savings [9].

Factors influencing appointment attendance can guide targeted intervention development that may lead to improved patient outcomes and optimized healthcare services. Demographic and socioeconomic factors have been extensively studied for their implications in appointment nonattendance in academic otolaryngology practices. Younger age [10, 11] and female gender [11, 12] have been shown to be significant predictors of missed appointments across general practice. Additionally, lower income and lack of private insurance are commonly associated with higher no‐show rates, indicating that financial barriers significantly impact patients' ability to attend appointments [10, 13]. These insights emphasize the need for tailored strategies to address the specific needs of these groups to improve attendance rates. Implementing policy changes and support programs to address these barriers could enhance appointment adherence among disadvantaged cancer populations.

Dayan et al. reported that missed appointments in oral cancer lead to decreased quality of care and subsequently worse health outcomes, highlighting the need for continuous and timely care [4]. Missed appointments lead to delayed initiation of treatment, allowing for upstaging of disease. Missed appointments after completion of therapy may jeopardize early detection of recurrent cancer at the risk of poorer cancer outcomes. This underscores the importance of interventions aimed at reducing missed appointments to improve patient health and survival rates. Although appointment adherence has been studied in otolaryngology overall and linked to worse survival in lung cancer, previous research lacks data specific to HPV‐OPSCC appointment adherence [10, 14].

This study aims to evaluate the factors influencing appointment completion in patients with HPV‐OPSCC at an academic otolaryngology cancer center. By examining for associated variables such as patient demographics, topography, cancer stage, insurance type, and admission season, this research seeks to identify key predictors of non‐adherence in hopes of directing targeted interventions to improve appointment adherence and patient outcomes.

Methods

Design and Population

This retrospective study examines scheduled appointment attendance rates in adult patients seen in the outpatient clinic at the University of Kentucky between January 1, 2013, and April 1, 2023. Among this cohort, adult patients with p16 positive HPV‐OPSCC were identified through the University of Kentucky Tumor Registry. Data for this study was obtained from three sources: Allscripts Electronic Health Record (AEHR), EPIC electronic health record, and the Markey Cancer Center Tumor Registry. The scheduled outpatient visits data were extracted for all patients seen at the University of Kentucky Medical Center for the 10‐year period as well as information about the status of the appointment. We then extracted demographic and clinical data from patients identified as having HPV‐OPSCC from the two health record systems. The abstracted data were merged and stored in a REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) file for analysis. REDCap is an electronic data capture tool hosted at the University of Kentucky Center for Clinical and Translational Science [15, 16]. REDCap is a secure, web‐based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies, providing (1) an intuitive interface for validated data capture; (2) audit trails for tracking data manipulation and export procedures; (3) automated export procedures for seamless data downloads to common statistical packages; and (4) procedures for data integration and interoperability with external sources.

No‐Show Appointment

No‐show appointments for the purpose of this study were defined as an appointment where individuals neither attended nor canceled/rescheduled a scheduled appointment. Attendance for appointments was classified as the dichotomous variable, show or no‐show. We collected the appointment status for visits as completed, canceled, or no‐showed for all outpatient scheduled visits to UKMC during the study period.

Risk Factors

The following information was abstracted from the electronic medical records of the HPV‐OPSCC cohort: tumor stage (categorical variable with three levels: early‐stage (Stages 1 or 2), advanced stage (Stages 3 or 4), or unknown/unstaged), tumor topography (categorical variable with two levels: base of tongue, tonsil), season (categorical variable with four levels: winter, spring, summer, and fall), insurance type (categorical variable with six levels: Blue Cross Blue Shield, commercial, Medicaid/replacement, Medicare/advantage, other/Tricare, self‐pay), age (continuous variable measuring age in years), sex (dichotomous variable for male or female), race (categorical variable with two levels: White, Black/African American), ethnicity (categorical variable with two levels: Hispanic, non‐Hispanic), and tobacco use status (categorical variable with three levels: ever use, never used, unknown).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics both for the overall sample and stratified by no‐show status were calculated using mean and standard deviation for age, counts and percentages for categorical variables. Unadjusted analyses between appointments accounted for and no‐shows included t‐tests for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were done in R programming language, version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The principal analysis consisted of two parts: logistic regression utilizing the above‐listed risk factors and generalized estimating equations (GEEs) as a sensitivity analysis to account for the within‐patient correlation across encounters. The within‐patient correlation across encounters was 0.062, which indicates sufficient evidence that the odds of a patient not showing for any single appointment are associated with their odds of not showing for another appointment [17].

Results

The UKMC outpatient visit encounter records revealed 809,569 no‐shows out of 36,690,000 total recorded appointments, constituting 2.207% of all scheduled appointments. The overall UKMC rates of missed appointments (no‐show visits and canceled visits) were 11% (n = 33,888,419).

The HPV‐OPSCC cohort contained 36,579 recorded appointments across 334 unique medical record numbers. Overall, 64.4% (n = 23,871) of the scheduled encounters were attended (completed) by the HPV‐OPSCC population, while 36% of the scheduled encounters were canceled (11,081, 31%) or no‐showed (1627, 4.556%). Compared to the entire UKMC cohort, the HPV‐OPSCC cohort had a significantly higher no‐show rate (p < 0.001), as indicated by a one‐proportion z‐test.

When examining variables associated with no‐show appointments, we found that tumor stage; those with advanced stage disease were more likely to no‐show appointments than those with early‐stage disease (p = 0.031), while those with tonsillar tumors were more likely to no‐show than those with base of the tongue tumors (p < 0.001) (Table 1). In terms of patient demographics, younger patients were found to no‐show appointments more often (p < 0.001) as well as male patients being more likely to no‐show appointments than their female counterparts (p = 0.037). Considering race and ethnicity, Black patients were more likely than White patients (p = 0.007), while Hispanic patients were more likely than patients of non‐Hispanic ethnicity (p < 0.001) to no‐show appointments. Interestingly, in relation to tobacco use status, patients who reported ever having a history of tobacco use were more likely than patients who have never used tobacco to no‐show appointments (p = 0.007). Appointment season was not found to be significantly associated with appointment no‐shows in this study. Finally, analyzing the cohort in terms of insurance type, we found that patients who no‐showed appointments were more likely to have Medicaid/Replacement than Medicare/Advantage (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study's objective was to evaluate appointment attendance in patients with HPV‐OPSCC. This Kentucky patient population clearly demonstrated a higher rate of visit nonattendance than the general outpatient population, with cancellation and no‐show rates respectively 3 times and 2 times higher. Taking our cohort's no‐show and appointment cancellation rates together, over one‐third of all scheduled appointments were not completed. Extensive evidence demonstrates that delays in the diagnosis‐to‐treatment interval (DTI), time to treatment initiation (TTI), and prolonged overall treatment package time are consistently associated with decreased overall survival and increased cancer‐specific mortality in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), particularly in oral cavity, oropharyngeal, and laryngeal subsites [5, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. Additionally, treatment delays between surgery and the start of adjuvant radiotherapy, as well as prolonged total treatment package duration, have been independently linked to worse oncologic outcomes [18, 23]. These findings underscore that missed appointments may contribute directly to inferior survival outcomes, especially in cancers where timely multimodal therapy is critical. Consequently, improving appointment adherence in HPV‐OPSCC is not merely a matter of administrative efficiency, but a clinical imperative with direct survival implications.

The magnitude of these findings and potential impact upon cancer outcomes and healthcare delivery costs underscore the need to understand associated risk factors for missed appointments. Our multivariable analysis demonstrated several demographic factors as significant predictors of appointment nonattendance. As recently demonstrated in other studies of general otolaryngology practices, younger age is associated with appointment non‐adherence [10, 11]. Race and ethnicity were also independently associated with visit no‐shows, with Black and Hispanic patients found to have significantly higher no‐show appointment rates, reflecting trends previously described in similarly urban‐located otolaryngology clinics [24]. Mitigation of disparities in cancer care access and outcomes in specific population groupings likely requires multi‐level interventions in healthcare literacy, insurance access, and timely referrals.

Systemic barriers to healthcare access in these racial/ethnic groups exist including economic, transportation, geographic provider availability, health literacy, and bias. In addition, specific health care attitudes and beliefs may impact visit completion. Zhang et al. described racial disparities in adaptive coping strategies, attitudes, and beliefs that may directly impact the priority placed upon getting to a scheduled cancer visit [25]. Multifactorial barriers such as these are important to address because, though the incidence of HPV oropharyngeal cancer has been historically highest among non‐Hispanic White men, it is now rising in racial/ethnic minority groups [26], which have been shown to have a poorer prognosis [27].

The higher rates of no‐shows among the patients with Medicare and Medicaid based plans imply a possible role of the plan's health care delivery processes and coverage. By extension these health coverages are frequently a pseudo‐marker of decreased income. Economic deprivation creates financial and logistical barriers as well as the barriers inherent in select insurance carriers, such as difficulty navigating referrals [28], lack of transportation [29], and higher rates of economic instability [30] that may contribute to missed appointments in this patient population. Addressing these disparities through enhanced patient outreach, transportation assistance programs, and streamlined referral processes could help mitigate non‐adherence among Medicaid‐covered patients and ensure equitable access to timely cancer care.

Appointment adherence was also associated with tumor site, stage, and tobacco use status. Interestingly, patients diagnosed with tonsillar HPV‐OPSCC had higher no‐show appointment rates than those with base of tongue tumors. More predictably, patients with advanced stage cancer were more likely to miss appointments than those with early‐stage disease. Patients with un‐staged cancers have survivals similar to that of patients with advanced stage disease; and for our cohort, the no‐show rates are similar to that with advanced stage disease [31]. It is possible that differences in symptom burden, treatment side effects, or patient‐perceived severity of disease contribute to variations in patients' ability to attend scheduled appointments. The role of symptom burden, including dysphagia and pain, has been found to significantly impact adherence to treatment regimens among head and neck cancer patients [32]. However, tongue base tumors are relatively asymptomatic longer than tonsil cancer, and late stage disease typically is more symptomatic than early stage disease. The paradox may implicate attitude differences in those with symptomatic cancer that require further study. Tobacco users have higher rates of no‐show appointments. These findings may be reflective of financial barriers to healthcare access, due to the well‐described lower socioeconomic status associated with tobacco use [33]. This population has very high rates of clinical depression and substance abuse that may have an impact. Rieke et al. demonstrate significant increasing rates of depression as tumor stage increases [34]. Furthermore, tobacco users are more likely to have co‐substance abuse [35]. These attitudes and mental illness may underscore distinct challenges making it to clinic follow‐up appointments.

Interventions aimed at improving follow‐up in HPV‐OPSCC patients should be multidimensional—addressing structural, financial, and patient‐centered barriers to care. Patient navigation programs have long proven effective in reducing appointment non‐adherence by addressing multiple barriers to care [36]. These programs provide individualized support, such as appointment scheduling assistance, transportation coordination, and patient education, which can be particularly beneficial for HPV‐OPSCC patients with high risk predictive factors facing complex treatment regimens. Studies in oncology settings suggest that patient navigators can significantly lower no‐show rates by offering culturally competent guidance and reducing disparities in healthcare access [37, 38].

Patient education, counseling, and symptom management could also play a role in improving adherence. Addressing mental illness, social, racial, ethnic, and economic disparities is vital in the efforts to enable cancer center appointment attendance.

This data analysis has limitations. The data set and its retrospective nature are extracted from different software programs. Further, we present a single‐institution study that needs to be evaluated in other centers to improve its generalizability. While the data analysis clearly raises a critical issue of missed appointments, the actual impact on care delay and survival is implied and, in the future, could be more directly assessed.

Future studies should also evaluate the effectiveness of specific interventions, such as telemedicine follow‐ups, financial assistance programs, community outreach initiatives, and patient navigators, in improving adherence among high‐risk populations. We must look beyond traditional clinic‐based interventions. Innovative strategies that bring healthcare to patients, rather than relying solely on patient‐initiated engagement, should be considered in future interventions. Telehealth offers a promising means of bridging geographic, transportation, and socioeconomic barriers, especially for follow‐up care and treatment coordination in oncology populations. Surveillance can be further augmented with emerging circulating tumor HPV‐DNA assays that can be collected locally for earlier detection in those who can't get to the cancer center for assessment.

Conclusions
