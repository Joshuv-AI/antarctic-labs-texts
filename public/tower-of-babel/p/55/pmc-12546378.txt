Enhancing Anticancer Effects: Targeted Doxorubicin Delivery Using UiO-66-NH2-FA Metal-Organic Framework Combined with Autophagy Inhibition by Hydroxychloroquine in Colorectal Cancer Cells
Authors: Adeleh Saffar, Tahereh Rohani Bastami, Amir Ebrahimi, Sonia Iranpour, Mohammad Hasan Mollaei, Ahmad Reza Bahrami, Maryam M Matin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of targeted drug delivery systems to accumulate medications in cancer cells, along with the simultaneous application of multiple drugs, can facilitate the administration of optimal doses, leading to more efficient treatment as well as reduced side effects. We fabricated zirconium-based UiO-66-NH2 metal-organic framework (MOF) nanoparticles (NPs) with folic acid (FA) conjugated onto their surface for targeted delivery of doxorubicin (DOX), and smart drug release within tumor cells. Following the physicochemical characterization of the prepared NPs, the drug release profile was investigated in simulated media with pH 5.4 and 7.4. Subsequently, the internalization and anticancer effects of the NPs were evaluated in HT-29 and HEK-293 cells to assess their selectivity. Simultaneous treatment of HT-29 cells with FA-decorated NPs and hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), an autophagy inhibitor, was performed to sensitize cancer cells. The synergistic effects of combined treatment were assessed through MTT assay and autophagy flux detection. UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX NPs with a surface area of 323 m²/g and a high loading capacity of 36.25% showed a pH-dependent release with a substantial increase in acidic condition. Higher uptake of targeted NPs in HT-29 cells led to higher cytotoxicity and apoptosis. The combination of HCQ and targeted NPs increased cytotoxic effects against HT-29 cells compared to treatment with targeted NPs alone. Acridine orange staining revealed different patterns of autophagy flux in the co-administered drug groups. This study suggests that our DOX-loaded targeted nanocarrier enhances the therapeutic efficacy through localized drug delivery and reduced potential side effects compared to conventional DOX treatment. Its combination with HCQ may offer a promising strategy for safer and more effective colorectal cancer therapy by enabling dose reduction of both agents. However, further in vivo studies are necessary to validate these findings.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most prevalent malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. The poor clinical outcomes of conventional treatments, such as chemotherapy, are attributed to non-specificity, systemic toxicity, and the development of multiple drug resistance. These challenges underscore the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies. Impressive progress in designing nanoscale drug delivery systems (DDSs) has made it possible to overcome some drawbacks of chemotherapeutics, such as decreased solubility and bioavailability, poor membrane permeability, and low stability in biological fluids [2, 3]. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), as hybrids of inorganic metal ions/clusters connected by organic linkers, with outstanding advantages such as high surface area and porosity, high drug loading capacity, and customizable functionalization, are considered ideal candidates for drug delivery [4]. UiO-66 is a zirconium-based MOF, named after the University of Oslo, where it was first developed [5]. It consists of octahedral [Zr₆O₄(OH)₄] clusters coordinated by benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (BDC) linkers, and is considered an excellent drug delivery system due to its high biocompatibility and biosafety [6–8]. Moreover, the unique structure of UiO-66-NH2 MOFs confers a pH-responsive nature in acidic tumor microenvironments, effectively preventing the premature release of entrapped drugs, facilitating on-demand release, and enhancing drug accumulation at desired sites [9]. To achieve targeted drug delivery, employing an active targeting strategy that relies on the interaction between ligand-equipped nanoparticles and cellular targets can enhance the uptake of drug-containing NPs and improve the therapeutic index. Folic acid (FA), known for its low immunogenicity, low cost, and high availability, is a widely used small targeting moiety. Nontoxicity and lack of immune response make it suitable for clinical applications. In this regard, FA-decorated DDSs can selectively bind to overexpressed FA receptors on the surface of cancer cells, increasing local drug concentration and reducing off-target effects [10, 11]. Given the high adaptability of MOFs for surface modification, conjugating FA ligands to the MOF surface enables precise targeting of CRC cells with abundant FA receptors [12].

To address the limitations of a single therapeutic modality and mitigate the potential for drug resistance, clinical research has increasingly adopted combination therapy over monotherapy. Combination therapy, which involves the co-administration of at least two chemotherapeutic agents with different pharmacological activities, is a common therapeutic strategy to overcome genetic heterogeneity and the complexities of signaling pathways in tumors by simultaneously targeting multiple oncogenic pathways [13, 14].

Autophagy is a highly conserved process that plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis in normal cells. Under stress conditions, such as nutrient deprivation, autophagy primarily functions as a cytoprotective mechanism by removing damaged organelles and misfolded proteins, thereby preventing the activation of apoptosis and promoting cell survival. However, if homeostasis cannot be restored, apoptosis may be initiated. Thus, the crosstalk and potential polarization between autophagy and apoptosis, depending on the nature of the stimulus, ultimately determine the fate of the cell [15, 16]. In contrast, in cancer cells, this relationship is often dysregulated [17]. Autophagy can reduce the propensity of cells to become tumorigenic, but in established tumors, it may serve as a requirement for survival in harsh environments, including starvation, hypoxia, and exposure to chemotherapeutic drugs [18]. Several studies have shown that autophagy and apoptosis regulatory pathways are closely related and interdependent. The main machinery regulating the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways of apoptosis can also control autophagy, and autophagy regulators can influence apoptotic signaling. Autophagy can inhibit apoptosis by keeping caspase activity below the threshold required for cell death. If the inhibition of apoptosis allows tumor cells to survive until the damage caused by an antitumor agent is repaired, more resistant cells may regrow in the long term [19]. Building on this evidence, the combination therapy using chemotherapeutic drugs along with autophagy inhibitors may represent a promising strategy for treating CRC. Recent research has demonstrated the potential of the antimalarial drug hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) in cellular autophagy inhibition. Pre-clinical studies on cancer treatment with HCQ have proved promising, and there are phase I/II clinical trials underway to determine its efficacy and safety in CRC treatment when combined with other chemotherapeutic agents [18, 20].

HCQ inhibits autophagy in the late stage, preventing the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes and the formation of autolysosomes by deacidifying lysosomes, thereby blocking nutrient recycling [21]. Accumulating evidence suggests that inhibiting autophagy can enhance the antitumor immune response, as increased autophagy in cancer cells often impedes immune function [22]. Furthermore, studies have highlighted the function of HCQ as an immune regulator, inducing the transition of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) from the M2-like tumor-promoting phenotype to the M1 macrophage state, thereby boosting CD8+ T cell immunity [23]. Consequently, the combined use of HCQ and chemotherapeutic drugs may enhance the chemosensitivity of cancer cells, enabling more effective therapy with lower doses and reduced side effects due to their synergistic effects [24].

Given the benefits of combination therapy, this study investigated the synergistic effects of co-administring DOX and HCQ in CRC cells. To achieve this, we developed a UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticle to encapsulate DOX and subsequently surface-modified the NPs with FA for targeted delivery and controlled release of DOX in CRC cells. Following the characterization of the physicochemical properties, the efficiency of the targeted nanocarriers (UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX) was compared with non-targeted ones (UiO-66-NH2@DOX) through in vitro experiments, including cellular uptake, cytotoxicity, and apoptosis induction, using HT-29 and HEK-293 cells, as folate receptor-positive and -negative cells, respectively. Furthermore, we investigated the synergistic effects of the simultaneous administration of FA-ligated DOX-loaded NPs and HCQ on the increased susceptibility of CRC cells to lower concentrations of DOX and autophagic flux inhibition.

Experimental section

Materials

Zirconium dichloride oxide (ZrOCl2•8H2O), 2-amino benzene dicarboxylic acid (NH2-H2BDC), dimethylformamide (DMF), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), FA, and all other chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Corporation (Germany). Doxorubicin hydrochloride (DOX), with detailed properties in Table S1, was acquired from Azista Industries Pvt. Ltd. (India). Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI 1640) medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), penicillin/streptomycin, and other cell culture reagents were bought from Gibco (Scotland). FITC-annexin V apoptosis detection kit with propidium iodide (PI) was supplied by BioLegend (USA). 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) and trypsin-EDTA were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany). HT-29 and HEK-293 cells were provided by Ferdowsi University of Mashhad.

Synthesis of UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticles

For the synthesis of UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticles, a solvothermal procedure was used following a previously published method by Katz et al. [25]. In brief, 321 mg of ZrOCl2•8H2O and 25 ml of DMF were mixed to form mixture A. Additionally, 90 mg of NH2-H2BDC was dissolved in 25 ml of DMF as mixture B. The two solutions were then combined and placed in an ultrasonic bath for about 30 min at room temperature. The final mixture was transferred into a Teflon-lined steel autoclave at 120 °C for 24 h. The obtained solution was cooled, centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min, washed several times with DMF, and then dried in a vacuum oven overnight at 60 °C to remove the solvents.

Synthesis of FA-UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticles

The FA-UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticles were prepared by dissolving 1 mg of dried UiO-66-NH2 in 1 ml of deionized water under sonication for 30 min. The FA-targeting molecules were dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 1 mg/ml and added to the UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticle solution under vigorous stirring for 24 h at room temperature. Subsequently, FA-UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticles were collected using centrifugation and washed to remove excess FA molecules from the mixture [26].

Preparation of doxorubicin-loaded UiO-66-NH2 /FA-UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticles

To load anticancer DOX molecules into both UiO-66-NH2 and UiO-66-NH2-FA, 10 mg of each nanoparticle was dispersed in 10 ml of DOX solution (2 mg/ml) and stirred in darkness for 48 h. Then, the prepared UiO-66-NH2@DOX and UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX were subjected to centrifugation at 1000 g for 15 min and freeze-dried for further use. Physicochemical characterization

The crystallinity of the UiO-66-NH2 and FA-UiO-66-NH2 powder samples was evaluated using X-ray diffraction (XRD; Philips PW1730, Netherlands). Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was performed using the AVATAR 370 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Nicolet spectrometer, USA) to characterize the functional groups of UiO-66-NH2 and FA-UiO-66-NH2. The surface area and pore volume were measured by the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) and Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) methods, respectively (BELSORP MINI II, Japan). The prepared nanoparticles were also physically evaluated for particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and surface charge which were analyzed by dynamic light scattering (DLS; VASCO-3, France) and zeta potential analyzer (Zeta Compact, France), respectively. Finally, the morphological features of the FA-UiO-66-NH2 nano-platform were evaluated using field emission-scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; TESCAN MIRA, Czech Republic) and atomic force microscopy (AFM; JPK Nano Wizard II, Germany).

Assessment of DOX release from UiO-66-NH2-FA NPs

The DOX release behavior from UiO-66-NH2-FA was evaluated in triplicate using a dialysis membrane with a 1000 Da molecular weight cutoff under both acidic and physiological conditions (pH 5.4 and 7.4). In this procedure, 2 mg of the targeted nanoparticle was dispersed in 1 ml PBS, placed into dialysis membranes, and soaked in 30 ml of PBS with different pH values. At specific time intervals (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h), 2 ml of the released solution was withdrawn and replaced with 2 ml of fresh buffer to keep a constant volume. The samples were analyzed using UV/Vis spectroscopy at 480 nm, and the cumulative amount of released DOX was determined [28].

In vitro cellular internalization

The cellular uptake of different experimental groups was compared in both FR-positive and FR-negative cells using flow cytometry. HT-29 and HEK-293 cells were individually seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and grouped as follows: (1) control, (2) free DOX, (3) UiO-66-NH2@DOX, and (4) UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX. After a 5-hour incubation period, the cells were washed twice with PBS, trypsinized, and collected by centrifugation at 400 g for 15 min. Cells were then resuspended in 500 μl of PBS and subjected to quantitative analysis by flow cytometry (BD Accuri C6, USA) in the FL2 channel. FlowJo V.7.6 software was used to analyze the results.

Cell viability assay

The antiproliferative effects of various formulations were assessed using a standard MTT assay [29] on two cell lines: FR-positive HT-29 and FR-negative HEK-293 cells. The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Evaluating the cytotoxicity of various nanoparticle-based treatments

HT-29 and HEK-293 cells were seeded in two separate 96-well plates at a density of 7 × 103 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were treated with different concentrations of free DOX, UiO-66-NH2@DOX and UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX (3.12 to 100 μg/ml; equivalent concentration of DOX) for 24, 48, and 72 h. At the end of the incubation periods, each well received 20 μl MTT solution (5 mg/ml in PBS), and the cells were further incubated at 37 °C in dark condition for 3-4 h. Afterward, the supernatants were carefully removed, and resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 150 μl DMSO. Finally, the optical density (OD) of each well was measured at a wavelength of 545 nm in an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader (ELISA reader; Awareness Technology, USA).

Evaluating the cytotoxicity of combination treatments

The anticancer activity of various concentrations of HCQ in combination with nanoparticles was evaluated by determining the IC50 values. For this aim, both FR-positive and FR-negative cells were seeded in two separate 96-well plates as described in Section 2.8.1. HCQ and nanoparticles were combined in a fixed ratio of doses, corresponding to 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 μg/ml, and incubated with cells for 24, 48, and 72 h. The MTT assay was further conducted to evaluate the possible synergistic effects of the combination.

In vitro apoptosis analysis using flow cytometry

To examine the apoptotic effect of different experimental groups on HT-29 cells, the FITC-annexin V/PI kit was used. Cells were plated at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h. After overnight incubation, HT-29 cells were treated with free DOX, UiO-66-NH2@DOX, UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX, and HCQ, with the final concentration of DOX and HCQ at 3 μg/ml, and further incubated for 24 h. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the cells were harvested and stained with FITC-annexin V and PI at room temperature for 15 min. Finally, apoptosis was analyzed using flow cytometry (BD Accuri C6, USA), and the data were processed using FlowJo V.7.6 software.

Assessment of autophagy

To further confirm cellular autophagy, we evaluated the effects of HCQ as an autophagy inhibitor. For this aim, HT-29 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 7 × 103 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with HCQ, UiO-66-NH2@DOX, UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX, UiO-66-NH2@DOX/HCQ, and UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX/HCQ for 24 h. Treated cells were then washed with PBS and stained with acridine orange (AO) at a concentration of 5 µg/ml at 37 °C. The stained cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51, Japan), and the presence of acidic vesicular organelles in HT-29 cells was regarded as an indicator of autophagy. Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software version 9.3.0 (San Diego, CA) and presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons, depending on the experimental design. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results and Discussion

Characterization results validated successful synthesis of nanocarriers

The UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticles were synthesized using solvothermal methodology. Afterward, the presence of uniformly distributed amino groups on the UiO-66-NH2 facilitated the covalent conjugation of the carboxyl-containing targeting moieties, FA, allowing for the development of a targeted DDS to selectively deliver DOX to HT-29 cancer cells with overexpressed cognate folate receptors. The characterization analyses were employed to investigate the successful synthesis and functionalization of the nanoparticles.

The XRD patterns of UiO-66-NH2 and UiO-66-NH2-FA confirmed the crystal structure of the nanoparticles consistent with previous reports [27, 30]. As shown in Fig. 1A, the presence of characteristic diffraction peaks near 2θ values of 7.5°, 8.5°, and 25.8°, corresponding to (111), (002), and (006) crystal planes, respectively, revealed the successful formation of pure UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticles. Moreover, the post-modification of the FA molecules enhanced the peak intensities, while position of reflection planes remained largely unchanged, indicating that the structural integrity of the framework was preserved. An additional peak at around 18° appears in the XRD pattern of the FA-functionalized nanoparticles, which may be attributed to the presence of folic acid, as a similar peak has been reported by Naglah et al. [31]. To study the surface properties, the FT-IR spectra of UiO-66-NH2 and UiO-66-NH2-FA were also employed to analyze the functional groups (Fig. 1B). In the case of UiO-66-NH2, the peaks around 3450 and 3368 cm−1 were attributed to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of N–H bonds of the amino terephthalic acid organic linker [30, 32]. The peak observed at 1385 cm−1 was identified as the stretching vibrations of C-N group [33]. The peaks at 766 and 656 cm−1 were associated with the μ3-O stretching of Zr-O groups [34], indicating successful synthesis of UiO-66 containing amino groups. All other absorption peaks were consistent with previous studies. Additionally, the detected peak at 1609 cm−1 indicated the bending vibration of the N-H bond in FA [35]. Furthermore, the reduction in the intensity of the characteristic peak of UiO-66-NH2 around 3450 cm−1, related to the NH2 group, can be explained by the consumption of amine groups during the conjugation with the terminal carboxylic group of the targeting ligand FA [32], clearly demonstrating the successful modification of UiO-66-NH2 by FA molecules. MOFs are regarded as effective nanocarriers for drug encapsulation due to their extensive surface area and inherent porosity, which confer a high loading capacity. In this context, the comparison of N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms before and after DOX loading, as presented in Fig. 1C, D, showed that the BET surface area and monolayer pore volume of UiO-66-NH2-FA were determined to be 323 m2/g and 16.7 cm3/g, respectively. However, the surface area and pore volume for UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX decreased to 265.5 m2/g and 11.65 cm3/g, respectively, illuminating the successful drug loading. The loading capacity of UiO-66-NH2 and UiO-66-NH2-FA for DOX was measured to be 42% and 36.25%, respectively. The observed reduction in loading capacity of targeted NPs compared to non-targeted ones may be a consequence of steric hindrance introduced by the surface targeting ligand [32]. The size of nanocomposites is crucial for effective therapeutic delivery to cancer cells. Using DLS, we evaluated the size distribution and hydrodynamic diameter of the UiO-66-NH2 and UiO-66-NH2-FA NPs before and after loading with DOX in an aqueous solution. The UiO-66-NH2 NPs before and after DOX loading showed the average size of 177.55 nm, with a PDI of 0.22 and 167.80 nm with a PDI of 0.29. The UiO-66-NH2-FA NPs before and after loading of DOX exhibited average hydrodynamic sizes of 147.31 nm (PDI = 0.27) and 124.27 nm (PDI = 0.19), respectively. High hydrodynamic diameter confirms the strong interaction of water on the nanoparticle surface. Due to hydrophobic character of DOX, the hydrodynamic size of nanoparticles decreased by the addition of DOX.

Zeta potential analysis serves as a method for assessing the surface charge characteristics of nanoparticles within a colloidal solution. Nanoparticles possess a surface charge that creates a thin layer of ions with opposing charges, which are adsorbed onto the nanoparticle surface, alongside a double layer of ions that resides in proximity to this surface. At neutral pH, UiO-66-NH2 NPs before and after DOX loading were measured with negative values of −13.72 mV, and −17 mV, respectively. In addition, the zeta potential of UiO-66-NH2-FA NPs before and after loading with DOX exhibited −13 mV, and −16.19 mV, respectively. Consistent with our findings, Ibrahim et al. reported that the zeta potential of UiO-66-NH2 changes from positive to negative values at pH levels above 5.6. The positive charge at acidic pH can be attributed to the protonation of free amino groups on the UiO-66-NH2 surface [36]. In this regard, Dastneshan and colleagues reported a negative zeta potential of −27.63 ± 1.75 mV for UiO-66-NH2 [37].

The structural characteristics of UiO-66-NH2-FA were examined using FE-SEM and AFM imaging. The SEM image revealed that the UiO-66-NH2-FA NPs possess a uniform, well-defined octahedral morphology, which is characteristic of UiO-66-NH2 MOFs. The NPs displayed minimal agglomeration and a smooth surface, confirming the successful synthesis of highly crystalline and homogeneous UiO-66-NH2-FA MOFs (Fig. 1E). Similarly, the AFM analysis of UiO-66-NH2-FA NPs exhibited a uniform surface morphology with well-dispersed and regularly spaced nanoparticles. The even distribution across the 5 µm × 5 µm scan area underscores a high degree of homogeneity (Fig. 1F).

Intelligent drug release from prepared nanoparticles in response to acidic pH

The ability of the prepared nanocarriers to trigger the intelligent drug release at the desired sites is a pivotal factor in the development of targeted DDSs. Considering the acidic pH of the tumor microenvironment in many solid tumors, the use of pH-responsive nanoparticles facilitates the triggered delivery of drugs [38, 39]. To this aim, the DOX release profiles of UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX NPs were examined at both pH values of 7.4 (physiologic medium) and 5.4 (endosomes) to assess the DOX release behavior. As seen in Fig. 2, a decrease in pH from 7.4 to 5.4 led to a substantial increase in the release of DOX from nanoparticles, confirming a pH-dependent release rate. The results demonstrated that 9.26% ± 0.41 and 36.25% ± 0.19 of DOX were released from the UiO-66-NH2-FA nanoparticles within a time period of 96 h at pH values of 7.4 and 5.4, respectively. Ligand protonation of UiO-66-NH2-FA at lower pH results in structural instability and selective release, and therefore increased toxicity toward tumor cells. Moreover, the release of DOX may be accelerated due to the disruption of coordination bonds between the protonated hydroxyl groups in DOX and Zr sites under acidic condition [13, 40].

Increased uptake of FA-conjugated NPs in CRC cells

Improved internalization of therapeutic agents within cancer cells is paramount for effective drug delivery, resulting in the administration of lower doses and thereby reducing potential side effects and off-target toxicity. One of the most effective approaches to enhance accumulation in tumor tissues is the surface modification of nanoparticles with targeting moieties that attach to overexpressed receptors on cancer cells [32]. In the current study, the targeting efficiency of the FA-conjugated formulation compared to UiO-66-NH2@DOX was evaluated in FR-positive (HT-29) and FR-negative (HEK-293) cells using flow cytometry analysis. The comparisons were performed based on the fluorescence intensity of the internalized DOX in different treatment groups. As shown in Fig. 3A, targeted NPs demonstrated higher internalization compared to non-targeted ones in HT-29 cells, as evidenced by the higher fluorescence intensity of UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX NPs, indicating FA receptor-mediated endocytosis. A notable decrease in the cellular uptake of FA-conjugated NPs compared to non-targeted NPs was observed in FR-negative cells (HEK-293) (Fig. 3B). The remarkable disparity in the fluorescence intensity of targeted NPs between HT-29 and HEK-293 cells demonstrated the targeting specificity of UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX NPs. Cellular uptake of free DOX was greater than other groups in both cell lines, which is attributed to the nonspecific passive diffusion of small molecules through the cell membrane. However, this also highlights the need for higher dose administration of the free drug to achieve effective outcomes due to its unselective biodistribution, which in turn increases the risk of systemic toxicity [41]. In contrast, the targeted delivery of DOX via UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX NPs enabled selective accumulation in cancer cells and reduced uptake in normal cells, thereby potentially minimizing the side effects and enhancing treatment safety. Our results highlight the pivotal role of folic acid as a targeting ligand in enhancing cellular uptake efficiency in HT-29 cells. Active targeting and sustained drug release can amplify the potential of UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX NPs for precise and effective CRC therapy. Our findings are in agreement with previous studies that used FA ligands for the functionalization of various NPs to enhance their specificity [42–44].

Targeted NPs exhibited more pronounced selective toxicity

Given the increased cellular uptake of targeted NPs in HT-29 cells, facilitated by the interaction of FA-functionalized NPs with folate receptors on the cell surfaces, UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX NPs were expected to exhibit greater toxicity on these cells. Therefore, the MTT assay, a widely used method for evaluating cell viability, was employed after incubating HT-29 and HEK-293 cells with varying concentrations of free DOX, UiO-66-NH2@DOX, and UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX (3.12-50 μg/ml) for 24, 48, and 72 h. Based on the obtained results, all treatment groups induced growth inhibition in a concentration- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 4A, B). As anticipated, the UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX formulation exerted more toxic effects on HT-29 cells compared to the non-targeted UiO-66-NH2@DOX NPs, which must be due to higher receptor-mediated uptake of the targeted NPs in these FR-positive cells. Free DOX exhibited overall higher cytotoxicity in both cell lines because of passive diffusion and lack of targeting of this drug (Fig. 4A). The enhanced toxicity of the targeted NPs compared to the non-targeted ones was further confirmed by their lower IC50 values at the same time points (Table 1). However, FA-conjugated nanocarriers demonstrated negligible toxicity on HEK-293 cells as FR-negative cells compared to HT-29 cells (Fig. 4B and Table 1). The aforementioned findings confirmed that the targeting ligand (FA) decorating the UiO-66-NH2@DOX surface could selectively enhance antitumor activity against HT-29 cells. The MTT results align with the observed differential cellular uptake by FR-positive and FR-negative cells. This consistency has also been demonstrated in other studies investigating various MOFs as drug carriers functionalized with FA for targeting FR-overexpressing cancer cells [43, 45].

HCQ in combination with targeted NPs potentiated the antitumor effects

Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) is considered a potential therapeutic agent in cancer treatment, effective both as a monotherapy and in combination with other therapies [46]. Our results of single-agent HCQ treatment (at concentrations of 3.125 to 50 μg/ml) on HT-29 cells for 24, 48, and 72 h demonstrated a remarkable reduction in cell viability, consistent with increasing drug concentration and treatment duration (Fig. 5A and Table 1). In addition, as shown in the previous section, encapsulating DOX in UiO-66-NH2-FA enhanced cytotoxicity in FR-positive HT-29 cancerous cells, while exhibiting lower toxicity in FR-negative HEK-293 cells. This indicates selective uptake by tumor cells, leading to greater drug accumulation at the tumor site and reduced systemic toxicity. As a result, targeted DOX-loaded nanoparticles not only improve therapeutic efficacy but also enhance the safety profile compared to free DOX, which displays higher nonspecific cytotoxicity. Therefore, to further improve therapeutic outcomes, the co-administration of DOX-loaded NPs, including UiO-66-NH2@DOX and UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX, in combination with different concentrations of HCQ, was investigated (Fig. 5B, C). The synergistic anticancer activities of DOX-loaded NPs and HCQ exhibited an increasing trend in both treatment groups as HCQ concentration and exposure time increased. The combination of targeted NPs with HCQ resulted in greater toxicity on HT-29 cells compared to either agent alone, indicating that HCQ likely acts as a chemo-sensitizer in combination therapy. For instance, after 24 h of treatment, the IC50 values of targeted nanoparticles and HCQ alone were 20.36 μg/ml and 16.31 μg/ml, respectively, each corresponding to approximately 50% cell viability. In contrast, their combination at lower doses, 12.5 μg/ml for targeted NPs and 6.25 μg/ml for HCQ, reduced cell viability to 45%, demonstrating enhanced efficacy accompanied by a potentially safer therapeutic profile owing to the lower drug concentrations used (Fig. 5C). Notably, higher concentrations of HCQ were required in combination with non-targeted NPs to induce cytotoxicity comparable to that achieved by the combination of lower concentrations of targeted NP and HCQ. Comparison of the corresponding IC50 values of simultaneous administration of targeted and non-targeted NPs with HCQ approximately corroborated the potential of UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX for more effective cell growth inhibition at all concentrations of HCQ and all time points than the other group (Fig. 6A–C and Table 2). These findings suggest that UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX NPs, with their active targeting capability, combined with HCQ, improve antitumor efficacy against HT-29 cells. Previous reports have shown that HCQ treatment increases lysosomal pH and reverses lysosome function in the sequestration of therapeutic drugs, leading to the chemo-sensitization of tumor cells. Employing strategies to enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells enables superior efficacy with low-dose drugs and minimizes adverse side effects [23].

Targeted NPs increased the apoptotic cell population in HT-29 cells

The extent of apoptosis as a potential pathway contributing to cell death was examined using the FITC-annexin V/PI dual staining technique in conjunction with flow cytometry. Our findings demonstrated a significant reduction in the viable cell population (Q4) in HT-29 cells treated with free DOX, UiO-66-NH2@DOX, and UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX, with percentages of 37.3%, 55.7%, and 43.2%, respectively, compared to 92.9% in the control cells. Additionally, the percentage of early and late apoptotic cells (Q3 + Q2) from 2.73% in the control increased to 59.98%, 38.64%, and 55.89% in the free DOX, UiO-66-NH2@DOX, and UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX groups, respectively, when treated with an equivalent dosage of 3 μg/ml DOX for 24 h (Fig. 7). Flow cytometry analysis, consistent with MTT assay results, further corroborated the greater cytotoxic effects of UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX NPs on HT-29 cells compared to non-targeted ones by inducing apoptosis. Indeed, FA-arming of UiO-66-NH2@DOX NPs increased their cellular uptake and therapeutic efficiency.

Tumor cells have high levels of autophagy to maintain cell survival [47]. Investigating the potential of HCQ as an autophagy inhibitor to induce apoptosis in HT-29 cells revealed a decrease in live cells to 44% and an increase in the apoptotic population to 48.87% (Fig. 7). These findings align with other studies that reported the ability of HCQ to induce apoptosis by inhibiting autophagy [48]. In this context, Chen et al. have proved that the inhibition of autophagy by HCQ leads to the accumulation of necrotic and aging organelles, resulting in increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The elevated ROS levels ultimately trigger apoptosis [49]. Collectively, our data suggest that the tumor-targeting capability of UiO-66-NH2-FA-DOX NPs, combined with the autophagy inhibitory effect of HCQ, may synergize to enhance cytotoxicity against HT-29 cells through the induction of apoptosis.

HCQ increased the number of acidic vesicular organelles in DOX-loaded NP-treated CRC cells

Hydroxychloroquine is an autophagy inhibitor that prevents the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes, resulting in the accumulation of autophagosomes due to interrupted autophagic degradation [50]. We investigated the potential of HCQ, in combination with DOX-loaded nanoparticles, to inhibit autophagy in HT-29 cells. To assess the inhibition of autophagic flux, the accumulation of cytosolic acidic vesicular organelles (AVOs), indicative of blocked autophagosome-lysosome fusion, was examined using fluorescence microscopy after staining the cells with acridine orange. Acridine orange is a hydrophobic green fluorescent dye that stains acidic vacuoles, including autophagosomes and autolysosomes, within cells. In acidic vesicles, acridine orange emits bright red fluorescence, making it a valuable tool for studying autophagy. In Fig. 8A, it is evident that the HCQ combination treatment resulted in a notable increase in the number of red-fluorescent cytosolic AVOs compared to the HT-29 cells not treated with HCQ. This increase can be attributed to autophagosome accumulation due to the inhibition of autophagic flux and blockage of autophagic degradation by HCQ at the late phase of autophagy, after autophagosome formation. Figure 8B illustrates the quantitative analysis of AVOs across the various groups. Similar results were reported by Sasaki et al., demonstrating that treatment of HT-29 cells with 5-fluorouracil and chloroquine (CQ) increased the formation of AVOs in the cytoplasm [51]. Moreover, existing research on breast cancer cells has shown that an increase in the number of AVOs induced by nanoparticles containing HCQ and DOX was associated with an increase in LC3–II (an autophagosome formation marker), implying autophagosome accumulation due to a cessation of autophagy at a later stage [21]. Taken together, our results suggest that HCQ can potentiate the anticancer effects of DOX-loaded NPs in HT-29 cells by inhibiting autophagy.

Conclusions

This study leveraged the versatility of MOFs as drug carriers, with a particular focus on their capacity for pH responsiveness and surface modifications to enable targeted drug delivery. Amine-functionalized UiO-66 enhanced the controllability of loading and release through hydrogen bonds, attributed to its multiple amine groups. Considering the pKa values of DOX and its cationic form under most conditions [52], efficient loading on nanoparticles with a negative zeta potential was attained. By functionalizing UiO-66-NH2 nanoparticles with folic acid, we achieved targeted delivery to cancer cells overexpressing folate receptors. This targeted approach not only enhanced drug uptake by CRC cells but also minimized off-target effects, given the relatively low expression of folate receptors on normal cells. Additional in vitro evaluations consistently confirmed the superior performance of targeted nanocarriers, demonstrating higher cytotoxicity and enhanced apoptosis induction. To enhance therapeutic efficacy by inhibiting multiple targets, combination therapy pairing UiO-66-NH2-FA@DOX with HCQ was employed. This approach aimed to synergistically inhibit the proliferation of CRC cells by blocking autophagy, hindering cancer cells from leveraging autophagy as a survival strategy, and ultimately increasing the sensitivity of CRC cells to DOX treatment. As expected, the killing potency of targeted NPs combined with low doses of HCQ was more pronounced than that of targeted NPs used alone. According to our results, it appears that this elevated cell death is due to autophagy flux inhibition and apoptosis induction, as evidenced by the high level of apoptosis following HCQ treatment. The detailed understanding of cellular mechanisms underlying the synergistic effects of this combined therapy would highlight its promising potential in effective cancer treatment, which requires further research for translational application in the future. Compared to conventional doxorubicin therapy, which often suffers from poor selectivity and systemic toxicity, our targeted and pH-responsive MOF-based delivery system, especially when combined with autophagy inhibition, demonstrated enhanced delivery specificity, minimized off-target effects, and improved therapeutic efficacy. These features highlight the novelty and potential clinical value of our approach in advancing more effective and safer treatments for colorectal cancer.

While these findings demonstrate a promising therapeutic strategy, it is important to recognize that in vitro conditions do not fully capture the complexity of biological systems. Interactions with the immune system, nanoparticle biodistribution and clearance, as well as the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of the tumor microenvironment, can significantly influence the fate and efficacy of nanocarriers. Future studies incorporating primary normal cells instead of immortalized HEK-293 cells, alongside in vivo experiments, will provide more comprehensive insight into the physiological relevance and clinical applicability of this approach. Additionally, systematic evaluation of the long-term stability and biocompatibility of UiO-66-based carriers will be essential for their future development.
