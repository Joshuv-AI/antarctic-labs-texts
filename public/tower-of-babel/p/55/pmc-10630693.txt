Current status of female urologists in Korea
Authors: Seo Yeon Lee, Sang-Don Lee, Sun-Ouck Kim
Published: 2023

INTRODUCTION

Traditionally, urology has been recognized as a domain of male doctors. However, the number of female urologists has gradually increased worldwide. In the United States, the percentage of women practicing as urologists will continue to rise from 7.7% in 2014 to 11.6% in 2022. In 2022, the total number of practicing female urologists in the United States was reported to be 1,619 compared to 12,357 male urologists [1]. In Korea, the first female doctor started urology training in 1995 and received urology board certification in 1999. Since then, female doctors have continued to apply for urology. As of 2022, there are 54 female residents and specialists. As the number of female urologists gradually increased, the Korean Urological Association (KUA) began to take interest in female urologists, a minority group in the urological society. In the 2010s, meetings between the KUA president and female doctors were held several times irregularly during the annual meeting period. In 2019, KUA created a new committee, the Diversity Promotion Committee and appointed a female doctor as director. In 2021, the name of the Diversity Promotion Committee was changed to the Women’s Policy Committee to more actively express interest in female doctors. To date, there has been no domestic published data on the number of female urologists, work area, work type, or subspecialty. This brief report summarizes the current status of female urologists in South Korea.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The total number of Korean board certified male and female urology specialists and residents, number of annual new Korean board-certified male and female urologists and of annual male and female residents were collected from the database of KUA. As part of a project of the Women’s Policy Committee of the KUA, working status, including the name of the workplace and subspecialty of female urologists, was obtained by telephone survey in February 2021. Questions about subspecialty were classified into urological oncology, non-oncology, and both so that they could be selected. This information was updated in September 2022. For specialists who had previously provided information, we first checked the website of their workplace based on the existing information, and then checked by telephone if there were any changes. For women who had just become urology specialists, we confirmed their workplace and specific major over telephone.

The geographic distribution of female urologists and residents was confirmed using a telephone survey at the workplace. Workplace type was categorized as university hospital, citizens’ medical center, private clinic, pharmaceutical company, and others.

The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed in this study.

RESULTS

The total number of women in the urology was fifty-four, 40 of whom were specialists and 14 were residents. In Korea, the duration of urology residency training is four years. Six of the fourteen residents were in their first year of residence. There were two chief residents and three residents in the second and third years, respectively. The number of female urology residents has been increasing over the past five years, and the proportion of female residents among all residents has increased from 0.9% to 8.4% (Fig. 1).

Between 1999 and 2022, a small number of female doctors obtained urology board each year (Fig. 2). Female urology specialists account for approximately 1.4% of all board-certified urologists (40 out of 2,950). The proportion of female urological residents was calculated to be 8.5% of all residents (14 out of 165). More than 50% of female specialists and residents work in metropolitan areas (Seoul and Gyeonggi), and there are female urology doctors in each province, except Gangwon (Fig. 3). About a half of the female urology specialists are working in the university hospital and 27.5% of the female urology specialists are working in the private clinic (Table 1). Of the three doctors marked as “others” in Table 1, two were on leave, and one was working abroad as a doctor in another department. Among the female specialists working at citizens’ medical centers and university hospitals, three were female doctors specializing only in urological oncology, and the rest selected their subspecialty in non-oncology or both.

DISCUSSION

An increasing number of women are entering medical school. In Korea, the ratio of female students among medical school admissions nationwide was 10% in the 1970s and the 1980s, 20.1% in 1990, and steadily increased, reaching a record high of 38.6% in 2000. In 2022, female students account for 34.75% of medical students in Korea [2]. Accordingly, the proportion of female surgeons has gradually increased. In 1995, the first female resident in Korea started training in urology. Now, the proportion of women urologists is 1.4% of all urology board. In Japan, which has a similar medical environment to Korea (doctors per population, share of female doctors), the number of female doctors increased from 8.2% in 1998 to 20.4% of all doctors in 2014. The number of female urologists increased from 2.2% of all urologists in 1998 to 5.3% of all specialists in 2014 [3]. The proportion of female urologists in Korea is also expected to increase in future, similar to that in Japan. It is unclear why the present proportion of female urologists in Korea is lower than in Japan. In Japan, medical treatments such as hemodialysis are performed not only by nephrologists but also urologists [4], so urology might be more accessible to female doctors who prefer non-surgical field than in Korea. Additionally, with the large number of female urologists and diverse role models, it can be assumed that applications from junior female doctors have increased.

Urology has traditionally been seen by the public and the medical profession as a specialty focused on men’s health; however, in practice, it encompasses both men’s and women’s health [5]. Positive aspect of female urology doctors is patient preference. In a study conducted in the US, female surgeons operated on a significantly higher percentage of female patients than their male counterparts (54% vs. 33%; p<0.01). Female surgeons also performed more female-specific procedures, including sling procedures, than their male counterparts (18 vs. 10 per year; p<0.001) [6]. Additionally, women tended to prefer treatment with female surgeons. We conducted a study on the gender preferences of urologists in Korea [7]. When female patients were individually questioned as to whether they preferred a particular gender surgeon, 62.3% preferred female urologists, 36.4% had no preference, and 1% preferred male urologists. Male patients seemed more indifferent and minded less about their doctors’ sex [7]. Considering the demand for female patients, there were intermittent applications by female doctors in urology during the first ten years, and the first training of female residents at each university hospital began in Korea.

Several female urologists were produced from 2005–2012. Since then, as the popularity of urology departments declined, the number of female applicants also decreased. Recently, as the number of urology applicants has increased, the number of female residency applicants has also increased rapidly.

The rapid increase in the number of female trainees can be attributed to the diversity of role models. Most female doctors who majored in urology in the early days served as faculty members at university hospitals after undergoing training. As the number of female urologists increases, they are becoming active in various fields, such as city medical centers, private clinics, and pharmaceutical companies. This diversity in career paths can help female doctors choose urology without hesitation.

Pregnancy, childbirth, and parenting are important issues for young women and are also concerns for female doctors [8]. For doctors of childbearing age, urology has certain weaknesses. This is due to exposure to potential risks from radiation, teratogenic and cytotoxic drugs such as 5α-reductase inhibitors and mitomycin, iodine hand scrubs, infections, and long working hours [9]. However, this is not a weakness unique to urology, and also applies to other surgical fields. All surgeons are inevitably exposed to iodine hand scrub. Orthopedic, vascular surgery, neurosurgery as well as urology also have potential risk of radiation exposure. The advantage of urology compared to thoracic surgery, general surgery, orthopedics, neurosurgery, and obstetrics and gynecology is that the time required for each surgery is relatively short, except for the oncology part. Another advantage of urology is that while orthopedics, neurosurgery, and vascular surgery cannot avoid radiation exposure during surgeries or procedures, urology can avoid radiation exposure if the endourology field is excluded. Additional questionnaires are needed to know the reason of the application of urology by female doctors.

Female urologists were recruited nationwide. Half the participants worked in metropolitan areas (Seoul and Gyeonggi). Their distribution was similar to that of the Korean population by region. Gangwon and Jeju are the exceptions. The population of Gangwon is twice that of the Jeju Island [10]. However, there are no female urologists in Gangwon, although Jeju has two female urologists. The reason there are more female doctors in Jeju than in Gangwon is thought to be that the population density of Jeju is four times that of Gangwon [11].

In this survey, subspecialties were roughly divided into oncology, non-oncology, and both. Since most female urologists specialize in non-oncology, such as female urology, voiding dysfunction, urinary incontinence, and pediatric urology, this survey focused on whether female urologists specialize in oncology rather than which subspecialties in the non-oncology field. As the number of female urologists increased, their practice areas expanded and diversified. It is necessary to continuously produce female urologists so that their strengths of female urologists, such as sex preferences, can be demonstrated in various fields within the urology department. To this end, continuous interest and support from governments and academic societies are required.

A limitation of this study is its simple design. This is because it was based on a status survey that did not use significant personal information. Several studies on female doctors were conducted in countries where female urologists were present before female urologists were present in Korea. The studies used questionnaires regarding age, years since obtaining a specialty, type of work, location of work, hours worked per week, marital status, and specific specialization. Most of these surveys were conducted with more than 100 women and at least 50 female urologists [5,12,13,14]. Continuous updates on the status of female urologists are needed in the future. In addition, surveys on more diverse items and systematic analysis studies are needed. In the future, it will be necessary to investigate whether the number of female urologists will be sufficient to treat patients and whether the number of male and female urologists in each region is appropriate for the regional population and gender ratio. Additionally, providing more detailed information on the subspecialties of female doctors rather than dividing them into only oncology and non-oncology will help future female doctors choose their career paths.

CONCLUSIONS

As of 2022, there are 54 female urologists in South Korea. Female urologists were evenly distributed across the country, following the population distribution in Korea. Female urologists are employed in various fields. More female urologists chose non-oncology and double majors as subspecialties than they chose oncology. Recently, the number of female doctors who want to pursue urology has increased. It is necessary to pay attention to female urologists, who form a minority within the KUA, so that they can be continuously produced and actively engaged in various fields.
