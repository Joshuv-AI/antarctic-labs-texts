Rheumatological Diseases and Lower Extremity Edema
Authors: Michal Ad, Arin K. Greene
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
“Lymphedema” is commonly used as a generic term to describe any form of leg swelling; 25% of patients referred to our center with “lymphedema” do not have the condition. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the lymphatic function of subjects with rheumatologic disease. Five individuals with a rheumatologic disease and leg edema underwent a lymphoscintigram. All individuals had normal lymphatic function. Patients presenting with lower extremity swelling and a rheumatologic condition do not have lymphedema. The etiology of their leg swelling is by mechanisms other than lymphatic dysfunction.

Introduction

Lymphedema results from an error in lymphatic development (primary) or injury to a normally established lymphatic system (secondary). Primary lymphedema is rare, affecting 1/100,000 people; approximately 30 mutations are known to cause the disease.1 Patients with primary lymphedema typically present with lower extremity edema during infancy, childhood, or adolescence. Secondary lymphedema is responsible for most cases of lymphedema and usually results from lymphadenectomy or radiation to lymph nodes for cancer treatment. Other common causes of secondary lymphedema include parasitic infection and massive obesity (obesity-induced lymphedema).2 Patients with lymphedema have an increased risk of infection in the affected extremity and the limb can increase in size because of subcutaneous fibroadipose deposition.

The term “lymphedema” is commonly used as a generic description to describe any patient with extremity swelling and/or overgrowth. Lymphedema is a specific condition where edema is the direct result of inadequate lymphatic function. Twenty-five percent of patients referred to our Lymphedema Center do not have lymphedema.3,4 Many conditions can cause lower extremity edema and be confused with lymphedema (eg, venous stasis, renal/hepatic/cardiac disease, inflammatory conditions, lipedema, trauma, obesity).5 Rheumatologic diseases also have been hypothesized to be possibly associated with lymphatic dysfunction.6,7 The purpose of this study was to evaluate the lymphatic function in patients with known rheumatologic disease who were referred to our center with a diagnosis of “lymphedema.”

Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Boston Children’s Hospital. Our Lymphedema Program database of 960 patients was reviewed for patients who were referred with a diagnosis of lower extremity lymphedema and had a history of rheumatologic disease. Patient’s age, sex, past medical history, history of infections of the limb, physical examination, body mass index, rheumatologic condition, and lymphoscintigram result were recorded.

Results

Five of 960 (0.5%) patients in our database met inclusion criteria. Three patients had fibromyalgia, 1 subject had Sjogren syndrome, and 1 individual had psoriatic arthritis (Table). Average age at presentation was 40 years (range: 19–51 years). All patients were female, mean body mass index was 33 (range: 31–35), and none had a history of infection in the affected extremity. All patients had normal lymphatic function by lymphoscintigraphy (Figure).

Discussion

Many conditions can cause lower extremity swelling that are not the result of lymphatic dysfunction. The lymphatic system is resistant to injury and it is difficult to cause secondary lymphedema. After lymphadenectomy and radiation to axillary or inguinal lymph nodes, only 1/3 of patients will develop lymphedema.8 Rheumatologic conditions can result in leg edema, which has been hypothesized to potentially be the result of lymphatic dysfunction.6,7 Our study shows that patients with 3 types of rheumatologic conditions referred to us with a diagnosis of “lymphedema” have normal lymphatic function. Lymphoscintigraphy is the gold-standard assessment for lymphatic function and is 96% sensitive and 100% specific for lymphedema.9 It is possible that rheumatologic diseases not studied in our cohort could be associated with lymphedema. However, this is unlikely because patients with other rheumatologic disorders have never been referred to our center for lymphedema diagnosis and management.

The distinction between swelling caused by lymphatic dysfunction and other causes is important because the natural history and treatment are different. Patients with lymphedema have a significantly increased risk of developing an infection in the affected limb.10 Lymphedema also is a progressive condition caused by subcutaneous adipose deposition. Since individuals with rheumatologic diseases do not have lymphatic dysfunction, they are counselled that they may not respond to swelling treatments commonly used for lymphedema (eg, garments, pneumatic pumps, and decongestive therapy). Patients with rheumatologic conditions are informed that their edema is secondary to their underlying medical condition and that maximizing pharmacological control of their disease is the primary method to control their swelling.

Conclusions

Lower extremity edema associated with rheumatologic disorders is not due to lymphatic dysfunction.
