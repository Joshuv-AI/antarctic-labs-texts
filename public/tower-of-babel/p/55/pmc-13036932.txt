Air pollution and lung cancer: an investigation of county-level residual incidence rates with smoking eliminated
Authors: Douglas Myers, Srijana Mainali, Sanzida Afrin, David Kriebel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmoking is the leading cause of lung cancer and this can make it difficult to focus public attention on other preventable causes, like air pollution. To provide easily understandable quantitative information on the relative importance of smoking and air pollution as contributors to lung cancer, we used a two-stage regression method to simulate what would happen to county-level lung cancer rates if smoking were completely eliminated. Residual lung cancer rates would vary substantially among counties after eliminating smoking; the objective of this paper was to identify air pollutants that drive high lung cancer rates post smoking elimination.MethodsWe used multilevel negative binomial regression to model 2019 lung cancer incidence data, provided by the Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) Program, at the county level (n = 1,078), to estimate incidence rates with observed smoking prevalence (lagged 20 years) and with smoking eliminated. In a second step, age-standardized county rates were dichotomized into those above and below the top 20th percentile; logistic regression was used to model the effects of specific air pollutants on the odds of having a high residual lung cancer rate after smoking was eliminated. Using 1999 U.S. EPA data on mean concentrations of carcinogenic air pollutants (lagged 20 years) we evaluated the contributions of: PM2.5, diesel exhaust particulate, benzene, polycyclic organic materials (POM), and radon (chosen a priori) to high county lung cancer rates.ResultsConsistent with previous literature, mean PM2.5 concentrations and radon were important contributors to high lung cancer rates after accounting for smoking. Additional lung cancer determinants were mean concentrations of diesel exhaust particulate, benzene, and POM. The contribution of PM2.5 to lung cancer appeared to be largely explained by the 3 more specific pollutants diesel exhaust, benzene, and POM.ConclusionsThe study suggests there are geographic inequities in focusing cancer prevention only on tobacco control because places with high concentrations of carcinogenic air pollutants would still have disproportionately high lung cancer rates, even if smoking could be completely eliminated. These results can be used to identify counties where control of carcinogenic air pollutants might have a particularly important impact on cancer prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12940-026-01277-9.

Introduction

Despite notable declines in recent years, lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer morbidity and mortality in the U.S. and many other countries [1]. Much of the improvement has been attributed to effective public health campaigns aimed at reducing tobacco consumption, the number one preventable cause of cancers of the lung and many other sites. The tobacco – lung cancer link has long been cited as the best example of a “lifestyle” factor which can, and should, be modified by individuals to improve their health, both for cancers [2] as well as for other noncommunicable diseases [3]. Other such factors include obesity, physical exercise, diet and stress. But lung cancer and many chronic diseases also have social and environmental determinants with spatial and temporal variations which are much more difficult for individuals to control. How important are environmental factors compared to “lifestyle” factors in driving the risk of lung cancer? Epidemiologists have debated the relative importance of these different risk factors for cancer and other chronic diseases for many years, pointing out the limitations of the standard methods for quantifying the “attributable risks” from different causes [4].

In two previous papers, we took a novel approach to addressing the question of the relative importance of tobacco smoking compared to environmental factors as causes of cancer [5, 6]. Using U.S. county-level incidence data for the 12 smoking-related types of cancer, and conventional multi-level regression models, we simulated what the incidence rates would have been in a sample of U.S. counties under a counterfactual scenario in which smoking was entirely eliminated. Unsurprisingly, the results showed that county-level cancer rates would have fallen dramatically – by about 39.8% overall. But because our approach estimated the cancer incidence reduction separately in each county, we found that eliminating smoking would have led to only very modest decreases in some counties, despite having relatively high smoking prevalences. The counties that would have benefited the least from smoking elimination were urban and had higher levels of air pollution, as measured via the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA) Air Quality Index (AQI), a composite measure that includes several pollutants [7].

In the current investigation, we hypothesized that counties whose lung cancer incidence rates decreased very little after we used regression models to simulate eliminating smoking would be those with high levels of air pollutants containing lung carcinogens. This study refined the previous work in several important ways. First, we focused only on cancers of the lung and bronchus rather than on the heterogeneous collection of all 12 types of cancer caused by tobacco smoking. Second, we used a much larger sample of U.S. counties with the most recent high quality cancer incidence data (1,078 counties that, in 2019, contributed to the SEER program). And thirdly, we took advantage of more specific U.S. EPA data to evaluate the contributions of five individual air pollutants chosen a priori because they are suspected or known to be lung carcinogens (PM2.5, diesel exhaust particulate, benzene, polycyclic organic matter (POM) and radon). This study had two primary objectives: first, to characterize air pollution in counties which were found to derive only modest benefits in lung cancer prevention from eliminating smoking; and second to identify those air pollutants that appear to be the most important contributors to the reduced benefit from smoking elimination. In selecting air pollutants to evaluate, we were guided by the substantial existing epidemiologic literature on air pollution and lung cancer [8, 9].

Methods

We used a two-stage method to analyze the effects of air pollution on lung cancer incidence rates. First, we used multilevel mixed-effects negative binomial regression to model the effects of age, sex, and the sex-specific county-level prevalence of daily smokers (those who smoked at least one cigarette every day, and lagged 20 years) on lung cancer incidence. This model was used to generate predicted county-level incidence rates under two conditions: first, using the actual prevalence of daily smokers, and second the counterfactual scenario of no smokers. Simulating the complete elimination of smoking is a well-defined counterfactual intervention for the purposes of epidemiologic modeling [10]. The appropriate, population-level measure of smoking for such a simulation is the prevalence of smokers, rather than an individual-level smoking metric like packs/day smoked. By comparing the two modeled lung cancer incidence rates for each county – under actual prevalence and zero prevalence – we could identify counties whose lung cancer incidence rates decreased relatively little, post-smoking elimination. In the second stage, we used unconditional logistic regression to explore the question: why would some counties experience only modest decreases in lung cancer incidence rates if no one smoked? Specifically, we investigated mean county-level lung carcinogenic air pollution concentrations (five pollutants, each lagged 20 years). More modeling details are provided below.

Cancer data

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program of the National Cancer Institute (NCI) is a centralized publicly available database for cancer incidence data which meet the highest standards of data quality and completeness [11]. This study used incidence data for 2019 for lung and bronchus cancer (ICD-O-3 code C34) [11]. The SEER*Stat software was used to extract all incidence, US population, and patient demographic data [12]. This analysis included nineteen registries across fifteen states (CA, CT, GA, IA, ID, IL, KY, LA, MA, NJ, NM, NY, TX, UT, and W) which included 1,078 counties covering approximately 36% of the 2019 US population (only Washington state lacked complete statewide coverage, and instead included only 13 of 39 counties in the Puget Sound/Seattle area). Patient demographics and population data are also provided by the SEER program. County was the unit of analysis; the SEER Program provides county of residence information. Since all data were publicly available and not personally identifiable, Institutional Review Board approval was not required. However, the SEER cancer incidence data require a signed research data use agreement [13].

Demographic, smoking, and environmental data

Sex and age data were available for each case. We examined age in 5-year categories (20–24, 25–29…, 85+). Race was not included because of the small numbers of non-whites in many of the SEER counties; results therefore represent all races combined. Reliable smoking data were not available at the individual level. Age-standardized sex-specific county-level smoking prevalence estimates, obtained from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation [14], were used to estimate the effects of smoking on incidence rates. These smoking data were derived from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System for ages 18 and over [15]. The smoking variable represented “prevalence of current daily cigarette smoking” by sex for each county. Estimates for 1999 were used to account for a 20-year lag in analyses of the 2019 SEER cancer incidence data.

Data for county mean PM2.5 (µg/m3) estimates were obtained from the Center for Air, Climate, and Energy Solutions. These estimates were derived using spatially decomposed v1 empirical models, as described in [16].

Other air pollutants chosen for this investigation were: diesel exhaust particulates, benzene and polycyclic organic matter (POM). County-level data on the mean concentrations (in µg/m3) of these pollutants were available from the U.S. EPA National-scale Air Toxics Assessment (NATA) for 1999 [17]. These data were developed by the US EPA using a computer model including total emissions, the number of emissions sources in a particular area, weather patterns and pollution source characteristics. For details on the measurement of these data see [17]. Data for all five air pollutants were available for 1999 allowing an investigation of 20-year lags in exposure when modeling the 2019 SEER incidence data. Additionally, we assessed more recent trends in air pollution exposures by comparing 1999 to 2014 data.

Diesel exhaust particulates are generated from diesel engines, primarily in trucks, automobiles and other mobile heavy equipment. Benzene is produced during combustion of hydrocarbon fuels and many industrial processes that involve hydrocarbons. POM is defined by EPA as a large class of chemical compounds including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon compounds (PAHs), many of which are known or suspected to be mutagenic or carcinogenic. These include for example benzo[a]pyrene, benz[a]anthracene, chrysene, benzo[b]fluoranthene, benzo[k]fluoranthene, dibenz[a, h]anthracene, and indeno[1,2,3-cd]pyrene [15]. They form during combustion of almost any organic matter including fossil fuels, but also in wildland fires, agricultural burning, residential wood burning, and hazardous waste sites. EPA also estimates exposures to several other carcinogenic air pollutants – arsenic, beryllium, bis(chloroethyl) ether, and chromium, but we did not investigate these. The primary sources of these pollutants are a limited number of large emitters, often in industrial zones where other pollutants are also produced. For most counties, the concentrations of these were extremely low or zero.

Radon data were also obtained from the EPA [18]. Radon is of concern indoors where it can build up to hazardous levels because it is formed through radioactive decay of certain types of underlying rock. For these analyses, a three-level radon exposure score created by EPA was dichotomized – Radon Level 1 (the highest level) was compared to Level 2 and 3 combined. Since regional radon exposure is relatively stable over time lagging was not necessary.

U.S. Department of Agriculture data were used to distinguish metropolitan from non-metropolitan counties [19], based on the standard Rural-Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC). The dichotomous metropolitan/non-metropolitan coding (variable RUCC2) was applied.

Statistical analyses

Modeling actual and simulated cancer rates

Multilevel mixed-effects negative binomial regression models were used to estimate the association between smoking prevalence and lung cancer incidence. Rates were modeled by using observed cancer counts as the dependent variable and population as the offset. A negative binomial distribution was used based on the presence of overdispersion; incidence rate ratios (IRR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were generated using Stata’s menbreg command. Age, sex, and county-level age-adjusted and sex-stratified daily smoking prevalence, lagged 20 years, were included as independent variables. We assumed the effect of smoking prevalence would be constant across counties; therefore, smoking prevalence was modeled as a fixed effect. Random intercepts by county were included so that residual county differences in cancer rates around the fixed smoking effect could be used to account for variability in individual county rates.

Stata’s predict command was used to generate two modeled counts of lung cancers: one was derived using the actual smoking prevalence values (lagged 20 years), the second was generated under the assumption that smoking was completely eliminated (county prevalence of daily smokers = 0 twenty years prior to the incidence year). Both the fixed- and random-effects components of the model were used to generate predicted values. Estimated values were converted to the expected county rates using county population data. Each county’s predicted lung cancer rate was age standardized to the sex-specific SEER population distribution for 2019 to allow for comparison across counties.

Analyzing environmental predictors of low-benefit counties

To investigate factors which might explain county variability in lung cancer rates after eliminating smoking, we labeled counties in the top 20% of this distribution as “low benefit” counties. These counties would have experienced lower reductions in lung cancer rates, on average, if smoking had been completely eliminated (the remaining counties were referred to as “high benefit” counties).

Difference in demographics, incidence rates, and pollutant levels between low and high benefit counties were first evaluated using T-tests. Next, multivariate unconditional logistic regression models were used to further identify determinants of being a low benefit county. Mean county level air pollutant exposure concentrations (µg/m3) were log transformed due to the extreme skewness of these variables. Model fit was compared via Akaike’s information criteria (AIC). Finally, average exposure measures recorded in 1999 were compared with those from 2005 to 2014 to quantify decreases in air pollution exposure over time; correlation coefficients across time were also generated.

As a check on our two-stage modeling approach, we fit a multilevel mixed-effects negative binomial regression model as above but added to it the county mean PM2.5 concentration lagged 20 years. We hypothesized that this model should yield a similar magnitude of association between lung cancer incidence rate and PM2.5 conditional on age, sex and smoking prevalence as we observed in the logistic regression model for determinants of being a low benefit county as just described. Stata 18.0 was used for all analyses [20].

Results

There were 89,843 lung and bronchus cancer cases identified in 1,078 SEER counties in 2019. The population in these counties was 115,534,442, representing 35.2% of the 2019 US population.

Counties varied considerably in their expected lung cancer rates after eliminating smoking (Fig. 1). Low benefit counties (those in the upper 20%) had a 2019 lung cancer incidence rate greater than 59.2/100,000 person-years after smoking was eliminated.

The effect of completely eliminating smoking would be substantial: the actual age-standardized mean county lung cancer rate in 2019 was 96.8/100,000 person-years, and after removing the effect of smoking, the rate would fall by 53% to 45.3/100,000 person-years. But not all counties would see such a substantial reduction in lung cancer (Table 2; Fig. 1). We chose to focus on the 20% of counties with the highest “residual” lung cancer rates after smoking elimination (other cutpoints yielded qualitatively similar findings). The top 20%, or “low benefit” counties (n = 216), would have an average age-standardized lung cancer incidence rate nearly twice as high, after smoking elimination, than in the other 80% or “high benefit counties” (72.7/100,000 versus 38.4/100,000, p < 0.001). Another way to see this is that the average reduction in lung cancer incidence rates due to the elimination of smoking in low benefit counties would be half that experienced by the other counties (30.9% vs. 59.5%, p < 0.001).

The low benefit counties were different from the high benefit counties in several significant ways. They were much larger in population, and much more likely to be metropolitan than high benefit counties (Table 2). The low benefit counties also had significantly higher mean concentrations of carcinogenic air pollution. The 5 candidate air pollutants (PM2.5, diesel exhaust particulate, benzene, polycyclic organic matter (POM) and radon) chosen a priori as potential explanatory factors for the higher lung cancer rates in low benefit counties, all had higher mean concentrations in low benefit counties, although the difference for radon was small (p = 0.3).

Multivariate logistic regression models enabled us to investigate confounding amongst the potential determinants of which counties would expect low benefit from smoking elimination (Table 3). Metropolitan counties were much more likely than non-metropolitan counties to be low benefit (OR = 8.8, 95% CI [6.1, 12.6], Table 3, Model 1). As expected, the presence of high levels of radon in a county also affected its likelihood of being a low benefit county (OR = 1.4, 95% CI [1.0, 2.1]). The importance of PM2.5 in determining whether a county would be a low benefit county was also expected (OR = 3.7, 95% CI [2.0, 7.0], Table 3, Model 2), given the considerable existing evidence that urban fine particulate matter is a lung carcinogen [21].

When variables representing the mean air concentrations of specific carcinogenic pollutants were added to the logistic regression model as determinants of being a low benefit county, three were found to be important predictors (Table 3, Model 3): diesel exhaust particulates (OR = 1.9, 95% CI [1.2, 2.9]), benzene (OR = 7.6, 95% CI [4.3, 13.5]), and POM (OR = 1.3, 95% CI [1.0, 1.7])). Interestingly, adding these three variables substantially reduced the importance of PM2.5 (OR = 1.4, 95% CI [0.6, 3.3]), which is a broad aggregate measure of complex chemical mixtures that vary in composition over time and space.

It appeared that the above three carcinogenic air pollutants (and a fourth, radon) together explained much of the reason why we observed that metropolitan counties were more likely than non-metropolitan counties to have low benefit from smoking elimination (Table 3, Model 5).

The final model indicates that low benefit counties were those with much higher exposures to four carcinogenic, air pollutants: diesel exhaust, benzene, POM and radon. As shown in Table 2 for the first three, the mean air concentrations of each was twice as high in the low benefit counties. The difference for radon was not as large (15% higher categorical score) in the low benefit counties.

Because of effective national air quality rules, all 4 of the regulated air pollutants in these models have fallen nationwide in recent decades. On average PM2.5 fell by 34%, diesel exhaust by 62%, benzene by 59% and POM by 87%, between 1999 and 2014.

When the logged county mean PM2.5 concentration was included in the multilevel mixed-effects negative binomial regression model instead of the second stage logistic models, the association between PM2.5 and lung cancer incidence was (IRR = 1.13, 95% CI [1.0, 1.20]) per 10 µg/m3 which was consistent with the findings in a previous study of lung cancer and PM2.5 (see Discussion) [21].

Discussion

Tobacco smoking is undoubtedly the single most important preventable cause of lung cancer [2], and this analysis found that, even at the county level with a simple average population measure of smoking prevalence, the powerful role of smoking was clear (Table 1). In addition, lung cancer incidence dropped by over half with the simulated elimination of smoking (Table 2).

The key to our analytic approach was to use a multi-level model which could estimate the variability in the individual county lung cancer rates. The model specified a “fixed” or common effect of smoking, but also “random” or individual intercepts for each county. The latter captured the variable impacts of smoking elimination in each county. This model enabled us to observe the key finding that the drop in lung cancer incidence due to the elimination of smoking would not be equal across all counties (even after accounting for each county’s prevalence of daily smokers).

Remigio and colleagues recently used a similar two-stage regression method to identify county-level associations between wildland fire (WF) exposure and lung cancer mortality [22]. Much like our approach, they first fit a regression model including county smoking prevalence, and then used residuals from that model in a second stage to identify lung cancer mortality associated with WF. They argued that this method made sense given the strong contribution of smoking to lung cancer, and the ecologic nature of their data, very much analogous to what we have done.

With this modeling strategy we were able to confirm the dominant role of tobacco as the primary preventable cause of lung cancer, while also highlighting the substantial impacts that carcinogenic air pollutants have in polluted urban areas. This leads to the conclusion that since the effects of eliminating cigarette smoking were not equal across counties, the relative importance of tobacco control depends on local environmental exposures. The elimination of smoking would have less impact in places with high concentrations of carcinogenic air pollutants and in these places especially, it may be appropriate for cancer control programs to prioritize reducing these exposures.

This investigation confirmed the role of radon as a risk factor for lung cancer, while at the same time finding its contribution notably less important than PM2.5, diesel exhaust, benzene and POM. Radon’s lung carcinogenicity is well established [23]. Human epidemiologic evidence initially came from studies of underground miners, and later from large case control studies using in-home radon gas monitors for exposure assessment. Initial studies using ecologic radon data failed to find a positive association with lung cancer incidence or mortality, but the American Cancer Society’s 1.2 million member Cancer Prevention Study-II confirmed a clear excess lung cancer risk among participants whose residential addresses were predicted to have high indoor radon levels [24].

The effect of PM2.5 on lung cancer is well established [25] and our results confirmed this. County average PM2.5 concentration was a strong predictor of the odds of being a low benefit county (Table 3). However, the inclusion of diesel exhaust, benzene, and POM reduced the effect size of PM2.5, and dropping the latter variable from the logistic regression model for determinants of low benefit had no effect on model goodness of fit.

Diesel exhaust particulate is a well-established lung carcinogen [26]. Most of the epidemiologic evidence comes from occupational studies, where exposures were probably higher than these county average levels, and so this finding may be a useful contributor to the evidence base for setting diesel exhaust environmental standards. The carcinogenic effect of benzene has also been known for decades [27], although most of the epidemiologic literature has focused on the association between benzene and hematopoietic cancers. A recent pooled analysis of 14 case-control studies found “…consistent and robust associations between … occupational benzene exposure and lung cancer after adjusting for smoking…” [28]. Our findings support this association, as benzene exposures were predictive of low benefit county status in each model containing it.

Polycyclic organic matter (POM) is a complex and highly variable mixture, mostly combustion by-products, which as noted above is expected to contain many carcinogenic compounds [17]. It is thus not surprising that counties with high mean POM concentrations would benefit less from smoking elimination. The 3 carcinogenic air pollutants, diesel exhaust, benzene and POM are expected to co-occur in urban air, and we did not necessarily expect that their individual contributions would be quantifiable in these models. However, despite their correlation, these 3 appeared to better explain low benefit from smoking elimination than the aggregated measure, PM2.5.

This study has several limitations. First, we necessarily used an ecologic design and therefore were unable to control for individual-level risk factors or pollution exposure. The associations we observed between lung cancer incidence and pollutant exposures may differ from individual-level associations. Our study was unable to control for all potential risk factors for lung cancer incidence including potential confounders such as occupational and indoor residential exposures. In addition, the ecologic nature of the study prohibits an investigation of the possible synergistic interactions between smoking and air pollutants, for example if counties with high levels of air pollution benefited more from eliminating smoking. Despite this, previous studies, some using individual-level exposure and confounder data, and others using substantially similar methods to ours, have found comparable associations for cancer incidence or mortality and PM2.5 [21, 29]. As noted above, the associations we report with diesel exhaust, benzene and POMs have not been previously reported from county-level geographic studies.

We checked the validity of the two-stage modeling approach by testing a version of the first-stage model (as in Table 1) but added the logged county mean PM2.5 concentration lagged 20 years. The association between PM2.5 and lung cancer incidence from this model was IRR = 1.08 per 10 µg/m3. Coleman and colleagues also studied the relation between PM2.5 and lung cancer incidence in SEER counties, although a smaller set (n = 607) and for an earlier period (2002–2011) [21]. After controlling for smoking and a set of socioeconomic indicators, they reported IRR = 1.16 per 10 µg/m3 (95% CI [1.08,1.23]) for a 15-year lagged moving average exposure, which seems in reasonable agreement given the differences in datasets.

Using a dichotomous outcome (above/below the 20th percentile) might be problematic since annual cancer counts could push small counties with very few cases above or below the cut point due simply to random variation. To investigate whether our results were sensitive to the instability of using only one year of incidence data, we also constructed second stage models using linear rather than logistic regression to identify determinants of the residual lung cancer rate in every county after eliminating smoking rather than the classification of above/below the cut point (Supplemental Table 1). Results were very similar, identifying the same pollutants as the important contributors. But we chose to highlight results from the dichotomous models rather than those using the continuous outcomes to draw attention to the subset of counties where air pollution may be of particular concern.

Our study was also limited by using mean county-level air pollutant concentrations, which use averages of U.S. Census block-level modeled estimates. These models do not account for the full range of spatial or temporal variability, nor do they deal with the effects of housing, and movement within and between counties. We chose to use a fixed 20-year lag, which may not be optimal for lung cancer incidence for each of the various exposures. Prior studies support lags of a decade or two, but there remains considerable uncertainty around the optimal choice. Coleman [21] found that a 15-year lag for PM2.5 and lung cancer incidence fit better than shorter lags, but did not test 20 years.

This investigation used cancer incidence data from 2019. But because of the 20-year lag inherent in studying initiators of solid tumors like lung cancer, the air pollution measures were based on 1999 data. In the meantime, air pollution levels have fallen substantially, and so we expect, will future lung cancer rates. However, we note that reductions in air pollution are not the same everywhere, and there are counties which continue to experience higher than average levels, although quantifying this is beyond the scope of the present paper.

Conclusion

There are geographic inequities in focusing cancer prevention only on tobacco control because places with high concentrations of carcinogenic air pollutants would still have disproportionately high lung cancer rates, even if smoking could be completely eliminated. These results can be used to identify counties where prioritizing control of carcinogenic air pollutants might have a particularly important impact on cancer prevention.
