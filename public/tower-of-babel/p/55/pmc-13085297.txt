Plasma proteomic biomarkers for predicting chemoradiation response in locally advanced cervical cancer
Authors: Raphatphorn Navakanitworakul, Pongsakorn Choochuen, Jitti Hanprasertpong, Suthat Liangpunsakul, Kanokwan Obaun, Kittinun Leetanaporn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA significant proportion of patients with locally advanced cervical cancer (LACC) experience treatment failure following concurrent chemoradiation therapy (CRT). Identifying patients who are unlikely to respond to CRT may inform personalized treatment strategies.MethodsWe evaluated plasma proteomic profiles of 39 patients with LACC stratified by CRT response: complete response (CR), partial response/stable disease (PR/SD), and progressive disease (PD). The primary objective was to identify protein differences between complete responders (CR) vs. non-responders (PR/SD and PD). Secondary objectives included subclass analyses (CR vs. PR/SD vs. PD). Nano-liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry with data-independent acquisition (nano-LC MS/MS-DIA) was used and verified by parallel reaction monitoring. Differential expression between CRT response groups was assessed using a Bayesian moderated t-test with minimal probabilistic imputation. RNA-seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas were analyzed using DESeq2, with functional enrichment performed via clusterProfiler and coexpression networks built with NetworkAnalyst. Nested t-test with ridge regression with 2,000 bootstrap iterations was used to evaluate predictive performance, and model discrimination was assessed by AUC using R.ResultsWe identified 507 protein groups. Of these, 407 were consistently detected in > 50% of samples. The primary analysis identified eight proteins significantly downregulated in non-responders involved in inflammatory response pathways. Secondary subclass analysis demonstrated distinct proteomic dysregulation in patients with PR/SD compared to CR groups, but not in patients with PD, with enrichment of pathways related to complement activation, NF-κB signaling, and lipid metabolism. Targeted verification of secondary findings using parallel reaction monitoring confirmed a panel of 13 proteins that robustly distinguished PR/SD from CR, yielding an optimism-corrected area under the curve of 0.832 (95% confidence interval of bootstrapped models: 0.789–0.957). Additionally, exploratory integration with tumor RNA-seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas revealed coordinated dysregulation in pathways involving hypoxia inducible-factor 1 signaling, proteoglycan and choline metabolism, and the complement and coagulation cascade.ConclusionsBaseline plasma protein profiles found only modest differences between overall responder and non-responders. Nevertheless, secondary analyses suggested exploratory potential of proteins in predicting PR/SD to CRT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04410-5.

Background

Cervical cancer (CC) remains a significant global health burden for women. Although its incidence has substantially declined due to screening and vaccination program improvements [1, 2], it remains among the leading causes of cancer regarding incidence and mortality among women [3]. A large proportion of cases originate from locally advanced cervical cancer (LACC), particularly in low- and middle-income countries [4–6]. Notably, for decades, concurrent chemoradiation therapy (CRT) has been the standard treatment for LACC [7]. Nonetheless, several patients experience treatment failure, necessitating limited efficacy second-line treatment options, or eventually resulting in disease progression [8]. Therefore, identifying patients who are less likely to respond to CRT, thereby enabling more personalized and effective treatment strategies, is warranted.

Circulating proteins, particularly those in plasma, offer valuable insights into systemic physiological states and tumor-derived signals, potentially reflecting the cancer-host environment [9]. Although several plasma-based proteomic studies have examined CC diagnosis [10–12], a limited mechanistic understanding of how these proteins influence the CRT response in LACC remains. Some research has demonstrated that circulating markers, including squamous cell carcinoma antigen, are associated with oncological outcomes in patients with squamous cell LACC [13]. Nevertheless, markers are subtype-specific and may have limited evidence to predict CRT response directly. Recent advances in mass spectrometry-based proteomics have significantly improved the accuracy and protein identification depths [9], thereby supporting the use of plasma-based protein markers to uncover molecular signatures and identify potential predictive biomarkers of CRT response in LACC.

Here, we characterized the differences in the pretreatment plasma proteomic profiles between CRT responders and non-responders using a nano-liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)-based proteomic approach. Our analysis provides insights into how the pretreatment systemic status of patients may influence their response to CRT. Furthermore, we identified potential biomarkers for personalized treatment strategies.

Methods

Sample collection

Leftover peripheral blood samples from patients with LACC who underwent CRT at Songklanagarind Hospital were used. Eligibility criteria included a confirmed diagnosis of CC stage IIB-IVA by the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) 2018 criteria, no prior history of hysterectomy, and completion of the full course of CRT. The exclusion criteria were suspected distant metastasis on computed tomography (CT) or clinical examination at diagnosis, presence of two primary cancers, human immunodeficiency virus infection, or pregnancy. Peripheral blood samples were collected before CRT protocol initiation, as previously described [14]. Within 2 h of collection, the samples were centrifuged at 2,500 × g for 15 min, then centrifuged again at 4 °C to obtain plasma. The resulting plasma samples were stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. The study cohort initially included 40 patients with CC. Following exclusion of one patient due to insufficient chromatographic data, samples from 39 patients (20 responders [complete response; CR] and 19 non-responders [14 partial response/stable disease; PR/SD and 5 progressive disease; PD]) were included in the final analysis. The response status in every patient was determined at 3 months post-CRT via pelvic examination by gynecologists and the contrast-enhanced CT result using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) [15] as assessed by independent radiologists, with pathological confirmation performed in cases wherein the tumor was accessible. All study procedures were approved by the relevant Institutional Review Board (REC 67-373-38-1).

Protein digestion

Protein digestion was performed using a protein aggregation capture protocol, as previously described [16]. Briefly, 1 µL of plasma was diluted in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) containing 2% SDS and then heated at 95 °C for 10 min. The samples were subsequently incubated with 20 mM TCEP and 10 mM CAA at room temperature for 30 min to reduce and alkylate disulfide bonds. Next, 250 µg of MagReSyn® Hydroxyl beads (ResynBio, Gauteng, South Africa) were added to each sample. The final working volume was adjusted to 50 µL before inducing protein aggregation by increasing the acetonitrile (ACN) concentration to 70%. The aggregated proteins were washed twice with 80% ethanol and once with 100% ACN. On-bead digestion was performed using trypsin at a 25:1 (protein: enzyme, w/w) ratio and Lys-C at a 100:1 ratio in 50 mM TEAB buffer, followed by incubation at 37 °C overnight. Finally, the digested samples were acidified with 1% formic acid (FA) prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Samples were analyzed using a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A total of 400 ng of tryptic digest was injected into an Acclaim PepMap C18 trap column (5 μm, 300 μm × 5 mm). Peptide separation was conducted using a PepMap C18 analytical column (2 μm, 75 μm × 500 mm) with a flow rate of 0.3 µL/min and an electrospray voltage of 2 kV. A linear gradient from 2% to 40% solvent B (solvent A: 2% acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid; solvent B: 80% acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid) was applied for 90 min, followed by a 10-min wash at 95% solvent B. Each injection batch consisted of eight samples followed by one blank injection. The column temperature was maintained at 60 °C. MS1 scans were acquired over an m/z range of 400–1000 with a resolution of 120,000 and an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 300%. MS2 spectra were acquired using data-independent acquisition (DIA) with optimized window placement. DIA parameters included 20 m/z isolation windows, 30,000 resolution, an AGC target of 1000%, and a normalized collision energy of 28% using higher-energy collisional dissociation. The radiofrequency lens was set to 50%. For parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) analysis, peptides were separated using the same LC gradient as DIA. MS1 scans were acquired over an m/z range of 400–1000 at 60,000 resolution with an AGC target of 100%, followed by time-scheduled targeted MS2 acquisition at 15,000 resolution with 100% AGC.

Proteomic data processing

Raw data from DIA experiments were analyzed using DIA-NN version 2.1.0 [17]. The data were searched against the UniProt Homo sapiens proteome (UP000005640) with the following parameters: allowance of one missed cleavage; N-terminal methionine excision; carbamidomethylation of cysteines as a fixed modification; generic scoring; MS1 and MS2 mass accuracies set to 5 ppm and 10 ppm, respectively; QuantUMS quantification; retention time-dependent normalization; and enabled match-between-runs functionality. Precursor and protein identification were filtered using a 1% false discovery rate (FDR). For the PRM data analysis, the predicted peptide library generated from the DIA search results was imported with the specified target peptides. The PRM results were integrated and analyzed using the Skyline software platform [18].

RNA-seq data acquisition

RNA-seq data from pretreatment cervical cancer tissue were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma cohort using the TCGAbiolinks R package. The dataset included raw RNA-seq counts and the corresponding clinical information. The inclusion criteria for this analysis were the presence of LACC, no history of hysterectomy, and completion of CRT. Treatment outcomes were based on the “Primary Therapy Outcome Success” variable was analyzed as combined “Partial Remission/Response” and “Stable Disease” vs. “Complete Remission/Response”. Raw count data was filtered to retain genes with at least 10 counts in a minimum number of samples equal to the smallest group size, and further refined to include only those genes with evidence of plasma protein expression, as referenced in the Human Peptide Atlas database [19]. The detailed pipeline of TCGA data was presented in Supplementary Method 1.

Statistical analysis

Outcomes were analyzed according to two objectives: the primary objective was to identify protein differences between complete responders (CR) vs. non-responders (PR/SD and PD); the secondary objectives included subclass analyses (CR vs. PR/SD vs. PD). Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical data are expressed as percentages. For DIA data, differential expression analysis was conducted using a Bayesian moderated t-test implemented in the limma package. The missing values were imputed by random sampling from the first quantile of a Gaussian distribution (minimal probabilistic imputation) due to the non-randomness missing value nature of DIA data. Differential gene expression analysis of RNA-seq data was performed using a negative binomial model in the DESeq2 package. Significant differential expressions were defined by an absolute fold change ≥ 1.2 and an FDR < 0.05. Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using over-representation tests against the Reactome and KEGG databases via the clusterProfiler package, applying an FDR cut-off of < 0.2. The data were visualized using the ggplot2 package. Network coexpression analysis was performed using the NetworkAnalyst web-based platform [20] as detailed in Supplementary Method 2. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.3 (Vienna, Austria).

For predictive modeling of protein biomarkers using PRM data across different response categories, we performed nested bootstrap resampling with t-test for feature selection followed by ridge regression. Supplementary analyses using LASSO and elastic net regression were also conducted for comparison. Discriminatory performance was evaluated using the average area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) across bootstrap iterations, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. An optimism-corrected AUC was reported to mitigate potential overestimation due to overfitting. Complete details of the modelling process are presented in Supplementary Method 3.

Results

Cohort description and association of clinicopathologic factors with treatment response

A total of 39 patients were included in the analysis (20 responders [CR] and 19 non-responders [14 PR/SD and 5 PD]). A summary of the workflow is presented in Fig. 1. The clinicopathological characteristics of the cohort are shown in Table 1 (Supplementary Table 1). The median age was 41 years (interquartile range: 36.5–47.5 years). No significant associations were observed between the response status and clinical variables, including age, BMI, stage, histologic subtype, or tumor grade. Notably, PD was exclusively observed in patients with lymph node involvement (stage IIIC); however, this trend was not significant (Fisher’s exact test p = 0.1402).

Proteomic profiling and differential expression analysis in CRT response

Across the experiment, we identified 6,867 peptides corresponding to 508 protein groups (either unique proteins or indistinguishable groups; Supplementary Table 2). Of these, 407 proteins were consistently detected in > 50% of the cohort. Principal component analysis revealed no discernible batch effects across the sample runs (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, no clear clustering was observed with respect to disease stage or tumor grade. In contrast, moderate clustering patterns emerged when responders and non-responders were compared (Fig. 2A). Correlation analyses of continuous clinical variables indicated that several proteins were strongly associated with age and BMI (Supplementary Figs. 2and 3). Consequently, age and BMI were included as significant covariates and adjusted in the downstream analyses.

For the primary objective, differential protein expression analysis identified eight proteins that were significantly downregulated in non-responders (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 3). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these proteins were predominantly involved in pathways related to inflammatory response regulation, including lipoprotein assembly and remodeling, platelet activation, clotting cascade formation, and retinoid metabolism (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 4).

Proteomic subgroup analysis and differential expression among response categories

We further performed a secondary analysis by characterization based on expression patterns of proteins across response statuses (CR, PR/SD, and PD) using k-means clustering. Notably, we found that patients with a PR/SD formed a distinct cluster separate from those with a CR, whereas patients with PD did not show a clear separation (Fig. 3A). Differential protein expression analysis was performed in the three response groups. We identified 41 dysregulated proteins in patients with PR/SD following CRT (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table 5). Functional pathway analysis indicated that, beyond pathways associated with non-response described previously, patients with PR/SD exhibited decreased expression of proteins involved in cytoskeletal protein cleavage, biotin transport, extracellular matrix organization, and nuclear receptor signaling. Patients with PR/SD also exhibited significant upregulation of proteins related to complement cascade activation, NF-κB signaling, and peroxisomal lipid metabolism (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table 6).

Conversely, only one protein associated with vitamin D transport was significantly downregulated in patients with PD compared to that in patients with CR (Supplementary Table 7). These findings suggest that while plasma signature showed only a modest difference between overall responder and non-responder, the secondary analysis found proteomic distinction between PR/SD and CR subgroup following CRT.

Targeted proteomics verification and predictive modeling of response biomarkers of CR and PR/SD

To verify the robustness of the finding of differential protein expression between PR/SD and CR groups, we performed targeted proteomics using PRM of proteins that were significantly differentially expressed between the groups in the DIA experiment. The top peptides were selected based on the following criteria: (1) significant differential expression in the DIA data; (2) precursor charge states of 2 + or 3+; (3) precursor, peptidoform, and protein group Q-values < 0.01; and (4) absence of missed cleavages (Supplementary Table 8). This approach consistently identified 37 of the 41 proteins across all samples.

Furthermore, we evaluated the predictive potential of the biomarker proteins to discriminate PR/SD from CR. Using nested t-test with a ridge regression model within 2,000 bootstrap resampling iterations, the models achieved an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.832 (95% CI of bootstrapped models: 0.789–0.957; Fig. 4A), demonstrating satisfactory discriminatory performance between patients with PR/SD and CR. Thirteen proteins were presented in > 50% of the bootstrapped models (namely, ALBU, PROC, IPSP, A2GL, SAA1, APOA1, CO9, APOD, TRFE, LCAT, KAIN, LBP, and PEPD; Fig. 4B; Supplementary Table 9). We also performed elastic-net and LASSO modeling as additional approaches and found comparable performance (Supplementary Fig. 4). Moreover, we also performed t-test for CR vs. PD group but observed no significant differences.

Exploration of possible pathways associated with PR/SD via integrated coexpression network analysis of tumor genes and plasma proteins

To gain deeper insight into the dynamic interactions between the tumor microenvironment and systemic host response, we performed an additional exploratory coexpression network analysis integrating tumor gene expression and dysregulated plasma proteins between patients treated with CRT exhibiting primary treatment success of combined “Partial Remission/Response” and “Stable Disease” vs. “Complete Remission/Response” in the TCGA cohort (Supplementary Table 10). After filtering based on plasma protein annotations from the Human Peptide Atlas, 4,677 genes representing plasma gene signatures were identified. Differential expression analysis revealed 26 genes that were significantly dysregulated in Partial Remission/Response and Stable Disease groups than in those in the Complete Remission/Response group (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Table 11). This gene set was combined with previously differentially expressed plasma proteins from PRM for an integrated network coexpression analysis. Interactions between tumor genes and plasma proteins were mediated by multiple intermediate genes (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Table 12). KEGG pathway enrichment revealed that this network was involved in critical cancer-related pathways, including involving hypoxia inducible-factor 1 signaling, proteoglycan and choline metabolism, and the complement and coagulation cascade. (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Table 13). These findings suggested that mechanistic dysregulation of these pathways may contribute to CRT resistance in locally advanced cervical cancer.

Discussion

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy remains the standard care for LACC; nevertheless, treatment failure persists in some patients. Here, we identified distinct proteomic differences between responders and non-responders. Age and BMI were incorporated as significant covariates in the differential protein expression analysis, as we observed that these factors were associated with multiple proteins and influenced plasma protein abundance across the lifespan [21, 22]. Regarding chemoradiation response, our primary analysis demonstrated that non-responders exhibited significant downregulation of proteins involved in the regulation of inflammatory responses, thereby corroborating previous findings from prior laboratory-based clinical investigations demonstrating that decreased levels of anti-inflammatory proteins, such as transferrin, high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-associated apolipoproteins, and albumin, are correlated with poor oncological outcomes [23–25].

Upon secondary findings, we observed distinct plasma proteomic profiles between patients with PR/SD and those with PD. Notably, systemic protein dysregulation in patients with PR/SD appeared more extensive and complex than that observed in patients with CR. Specifically, proteins associated with the complement cascade, including C-reactive protein (CRP) and complement component 9, were significantly upregulated in the PR/SD group. These proteins are well-established markers of systemic inflammation. Additionally, serum amyloid A (SAA) family proteins, known activators of the NF-κB signaling pathway, are elevated in patients with PR/SD, suggesting a heightened chronic inflammatory state [26]. We also identified a reduction in members of the NR1H-related protein subfamily, which are key regulators of HDL homeostasis and lipid metabolism [27]. This downregulation may further contribute to the proinflammatory milieu observed in patients with PR/SD.

Conversely, the proteomic profiles of patients with PD exhibited minimal differences compared to those of patients with CR. Nonetheless, a clinical review revealed that all PD cases were associated with lymph node involvement, although this trend was not significant. Previous studies have established lymph node metastasis as a prognostic indicator of poor outcomes in patients with cervical cancer undergoing CRT [28, 29]. Therefore, in contrast to PR/SD, tumor burden, rather than systemic proteomic dysregulation, may be the predominant driver of treatment resistance and disease progression in patients with PD.

During the verification process, we employed PRM as a targeted proteomic strategy. Unlike traditional immunoassays, PRM offers advantages in terms of time efficiency and multiplexing capability while circumventing the need for antibody-based detection [30]. Using this approach, we orthogonally verified candidate plasma proteins as potential predictors of PR/SD, where 13 proteins were consistently dysregulated in patients with PR/SD. Collectively, this protein panel achieved an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.832, demonstrating good discriminatory potential for predicting PR/SD and might be suitable for future validation in larger independent cohorts.

Beyond the identification of plasma proteomic signatures, we integrated these differentially expressed proteins with tumor-derived RNA sequencing data to explore possible tumor–systemic interactions. A coexpression network analysis revealed that the observed dysregulation may linked to pathways involving chronic inflammation and hypoxia-inducing mechanisms. These findings extend previous observations by highlighting that both pro- and anti-inflammatory states before treatment may influence the efficacy of CRT. Nevertheless, these protein–RNA interaction findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory findings rather than direct validation, given differences in response definitions and intracohort variability.

This study has several strengths. First, we employed orthogonal verification using the PRM, which confirmed the robustness of protein detection and minimized the influence of technical variability. Second, we used a bootstrapping approach and to construct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, providing an internal verification of the predictive performance of the identified protein panel within the cohort. Despite these strengths, this study presents some limitations. Missing values in DIA data were imputed using a standard left-censored approach to model the limit of detection. This strategy may influence variance-based analyses; nevertheless, key findings were independently supported by subsequent targeted PRM verification. Moreover, the relatively small sample size and lack of longitudinal sampling indicate that proposed protein panel model is not yet suitable for immediate clinical implementation, but rather represents a preliminary framework for further validation in larger independent cohorts, with serial time-point sample collection.

Conclusions

This study found modest baseline plasma proteomic differences between CRT responders and non-responders among LACC patients. However, secondary findings found 13 proteins that showed exploratory potential for distinguishing PR/SD from CR. These findings require validation in larger cohorts to elucidate the potential utility of plasma proteomics for pretreatment risk stratification in the future.
