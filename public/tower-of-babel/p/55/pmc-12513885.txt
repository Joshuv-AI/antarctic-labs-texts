Picroside II Alleviates the Progression of Alzheimer’s Disease via the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD Pathway
Authors: Shuyue Li, Ya Gao, Yidan Zhang, Jian Zhang, Yuan Zhao, Cui Chang, Xuan Gao, Jungang Zhang, Guofeng Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD), an irreversible, degenerative disorder, affects the central nervous system. However, its accurate pathology remains unclear, and studies on treatment modalities are ongoing. Picroside II (PII) is an active compound in the medicinal herb Rhizoma coptis. It has strong effects, including antioxidation, anti-inflammatory, antiapoptotic, and neuroprotective effects. In this study, we analyzed how PII affects cognitive impairment in mice with AD and its underlying mechanism. PII at doses of 20 or 40 mg/kg was given to APP/PS1 mice through intraperitoneal injection for 2 months. Moreover, we carried out the Morris water maze test to evaluate cognitive function. Immunofluorescence analysis was performed to observe cortical Aβ plaque deposition, neuronal loss, and inflammatory cell expression. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed to measure the levels of the cortical inflammatory factors tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-6, and IL-1β. Western blotting and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) were performed to measure NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD, and caspase-1 expression. PII improved cognitive function, reduced Aβ plaque deposition and glial activation, and alleviated cortical neuronal loss in APP/PS1 mice. Furthermore, PII decreased the levels of cortical inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β). In addition, it suppressed NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD, and caspase-1 expression at the mRNA and protein levels. PII enhances the cognitive function of APP/PS1 mice by reducing inflammation and pyroptosis via the suppression of the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway. Therefore, PII is a candidate anti-AD therapeutic agent.

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia, a condition primarily characterized by degeneration of the central nervous system. AD is insidious at onset, difficult to diagnose, and incurable; this places a substantial financial and medical burden on individuals and society (Wang et al., 2022a). Unfortunately, no medication or treatment can significantly delay the AD development process (Tian et al., 2021). Therefore, identifying novel anti-AD therapeutic targets is urgently warranted.

Neuroinflammation is essential for AD occurrence because it can exacerbate the pathologies of amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau (Kosyreva et al., 2022). Astrocyte and microglial activation and inflammatory factor production are associated with chronic inflammatory responses within the AD brain, hampering the environment around neurons and leading to neuronal damage (Onyango et al., 2021). Immunohistochemical analysis of the postmortem brains of patients with AD revealed reactive microglia and astrocytes, which are strongly correlated with AD pathology (Munoz-Castro et al., 2022). In addition, studies have revealed that proinflammatory cytokines are upregulated in the brain, serum, and cerebrospinal fluid of AD cases (Appleton et al., 2025; Pelkmans et al., 2024; Taipa et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022b). Anti-inflammatory treatment improves cognitive function and Aβ deposition within the brain in AD mice (Shen et al., 2023; Zakaria et al., 2016). Therefore, developing drugs that target neuroinflammation is a possible anti-AD treatment.

Neuronal death and dysfunction are characteristic pathological changes observed in AD. Studies on cell death, a significant area of research, have revealed the involvement of multiple pathways, including apoptosis, necrosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and autophagy-dependent cell death (Galluzzi et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2021). Recently, researchers have reported the pivotal role of pyroptosis, an inflammatory form of programmed cell death, in neuronal death (Moujalled et al., 2021). In particular, neuronal pyroptosis resulting from the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome during AD has garnered increasing attention. Studies have revealed that Aβ intervention can induce NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated neuronal pyroptosis in mouse cortical neurons in vitro (Piao et al., 2020); this phenomenon has also been noted in the hippocampus and cortex of senescent mice (Li et al., 2020). Structurally, the NLRP3 inflammasome comprises NLRP3, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein (ASC), and procaspase-1. When the NLRP3 inflammasome is activated, procaspase-1 is converted into cleaved caspase-1, subsequently cleaving gasdermin D (GSDMD) and releasing the N-terminal structural domain (GSDMD-NT). This domain translocates to the cell membrane, where it forms a pore that helps discharge cellular components, ultimately resulting in pyroptosis (Jia et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022). The pyroptosis pathway can also promote the production and maturation of proinflammatory factors, including interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-18 (Hu et al., 2024).

Picroside II (PII, Fig. 1a) is an active compound isolated from the medicinal herb Rhizoma coptis (Li et al., 2007). Several studies have revealed that PII can stimulate anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic mechanisms in major organ injuries throughout the body (Li et al., 2016; Noh et al., 2015; Piao et al., 2017, 2020; Wang et al., 2015); in particular, its neuroprotective effects in cases of cerebral ischemia and ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) have been highlighted (Guo et al., 2010; Pei et al., 2012). In addition, research has revealed the neuroprotective properties of PII against the oxidative toxicity induced by glutamate and aluminum chloride in in vitro and in vivo models (Li et al., 2007). Methods

Drug Preparation

High-purity PII (> 98% as determined by HPLC) was obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (China). PII powder was dissolved in a 0.9% NaCl solution, resulting in a final concentration of 1 mg/mL.

Animals and Treatments

Five-month-old male transgenic APPswe/PS1dE9 mice (weighing ~ 30 ± 2 g) were obtained from Beijing HFK Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Age-matched C57BL/6 J mice were procured from the same company in Beijing, China. The animals were kept in a room at 23 °C ± 1 °C with 55% relative humidity and a 12-h light/dark cycle; they were allowed access to food and water freely. The research protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University (approval number: 2023-AE018) and adhered to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Thirty APP/PS1 mice were classified into three groups (n = 10 each): the transgenic control group (Tg), the transgenic group pretreated with low-dose PII (Tg, PII-L), and the transgenic group pretreated with high-dose PII (Tg, PII-H). In addition, 20 wild-type C57BL/6 J mice were randomized into two groups (n = 10 each): the non-transgenic group (nTg) and the non-transgenic group pretreated with high-dose PII (nTg, PII-H). The mice in the Tg and nTg groups were given an intraperitoneal injection of 0.9% NaCl at equivalent amounts. The PII-L and PII-H groups were intraperitoneally injected with 20 and 40 mg/kg PII dissolved in 0.9% NaCl, respectively. Behavioral tests were conducted after 2 months, as indicated in the experimental schedule illustrated in Fig. 1b.

Morris Water Maze (MWM) Test

We carried out the MWM test as follows: water (23 ± 1 °C) was added to a circular pool (diameter, 120 cm), in which a platform (diameter, 12 cm) was placed 1 cm beneath the water surface. Five experimental groups, with 10 participants in each group, received training on spatial learning tasks (lasting ≤ 60 s) four times a day for 5 consecutive days. On the 6th day, a 60-s detection trial was conducted without the hidden platform. The duration required to discover this platform (escape latency) was documented. The maintenance of spatial memory was assessed by calculating the platform crossing frequency and the duration spent in the designated quadrant.

Brain Tissue Preparation

After this test, mouse euthanasia was completed, followed by rapid removal of brain tissues. Sagittal brain sections were prepared on ice. Sagittal section slices encompass structures such as the cortex and hippocampus. Subsequent immunofluorescece imaging and quantitative analysis are conducted specifically on the frontal cortex region. One hemisphere of the brain was fixed for 24 h in 4% paraformaldehyde. At 4 °C, the tissue was dehydrated overnight with 30% sucrose and subsequently embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound. Finally, a Leica cryostat (CM1850) was used to prepare 8-μm-thick sections, which were subjected to immunofluorescence analysis of the cortex. The cortex of the other hemisphere was separated and stored at -80 °C for subsequent biochemical assays.

Immunofluorescence Labeling

A Leica cryostat was used to prepare 8-µm-thick transverse sections of OCT-embedded brain tissues. Five sections from each animal group were washed three times for 10 min with 10% phosphate-buffered saline, and later incubated for 30 min with 1% Triton X-100. After blocking for 1 h with 5% bovine serum albumin, the sections were incubated with rabbit anti-NeuN (1:100, ABN78, Sigma-Aldrich), anti-Iba1 (1:300, Wako), anti-GFAP (1:200, 16,835–1-AP, Proteintech), and anti-Aβ1-42 (1:500, 14,974, Cell Signaling) antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Then, an Alexa Fluor 594-linked secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA) was added to the sections, which were probed for 1 h. Fluorescence analysis was conducted using the Zeiss Vert. A1 fluorescence microscope from three randomly selected fields of every section.

Measurements of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6

Cortical tissues were homogenized on ice using a commercially available NP-40 Lysis Buffer (P0013F, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). According to the manufacturer's specifications, this buffer contains 50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, as well as sodium pyrophosphate, β-glycerophosphate, sodium orthovanadate, sodium fluoride, EDTA, leupeptin, etc. To ensure complete inhibition of proteolytic activity, 1 mM PMSF (ST506, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and a 1 X protease inhibitor cocktail (P1006, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) were added to the lysis buffer immediately before use. Mechanical homogenization was performed using an IKA T10 basic homogenizer. The homogenization process was performed on ice with three cycles of homogenization (10,000 rpm for 15 s each), with 30-s intervals on ice between cycles to prevent overheating. The 1% NP-40 detergent in the lysis buffer effectively disrupts cell membranes, thereby releasing intracellular proteins, including the cytokines of interest. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to pellet cellular debris and insoluble material. The resulting supernatant was carefully collected. Immediately use the BCA Protein Assay Kit (PC0020, Beijing Solarbio Science &Technology, China) to determine the protein concentration of the supernatant. Based on the measured concentration, dilute the sample with PBS to the same concentration and then conduct the ELISA test.This protocol ensures the extraction of total soluble tissue content, measuring the combined soluble pool of both intracellular and extracellular cytokines. Commercial kits from ABclonal (Wuhan, China) were used following specific protocols to measure IL-1β (RK00006), IL-6 (RK00008), and TNF-α (RK00027) levels in brain tissues.

Western Blotting

A total protein extraction kit (P1205, Applygen Technologies, Beijing, China) was used to extract mouse cortical proteins, ensuring complete cell lysis and the extraction of all intracellular proteins, including both soluble and insoluble fractions, for the analysis of total expression levels and the activation status of intracellular signaling molecules such as NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD-NT, and caspase-1 p20. The protein content was measured with a BCA kit and subsequently adjusted to 6 μg/μL. The loading amount for each well was set at 30 μg. Then, sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis was conducted for protein separation prior to transfer onto 0.22-µM polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Following 1 h of blocking with 5% defatted milk at ambient temperature, the membranes were probed with anti-NLRP3 (1:500; GB114320, Servicebio), anti-ASC (1:500; WL02462, Wanlei Biosciences), anti-GSDMD-NT (1:1000, AF4012, Affinity Biosciences), anti-caspase-1 p20 (1:500, GTX101322, Gene Tex), and anti-GAPDH (1:5000, Bioworld Technology, MB9231) primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After being rinsed three times with TBST (10 min each), the membranes were probed with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies for an additional 1 h at ambient temperature. Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents were used for band visualization. The band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. The expression level of each target protein was normalized to that of GAPDH as the loading control.

Real-Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

A total RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (RN28, Aidlab Biotechnologies, Beijing, China) was used as recommended by the manufacturer to extract total cortical tissue RNA. The total RNA had an OD260/280 ratio of 2.1–2.2, and subsequent experiments were performed. A SureScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (GeneCopoeia™) was used for reverse transcription. The following PCR primers were used: NLRP3 (MQP095964, Genecopoeia), ASC (MQP032988, Genecopoeia), GSDMD (MQP034774, Genecopoeia), caspase-1 (MQP028800, Genecopoeia), and GAPDH (MQP027158, Genecopoeia). GAPDH was used as a reference, and mRNA expression was normalized by the 2–ΔΔCT approach.

Statistical Analysis

The data are expressed as the means ± standard errors of the means. SPSS 23.0 (SPSS, Inc.) software was used for statistical analysis, with one-way ANOVA combined with Tukey’s post hoc analysis. A P value of < 0.05 indicated a significant difference.

Discussion

In clinical settings, AD is pathologically characterized by progressively decreased cognition via Aβ deposition, neuronal loss, and glial cell-mediated neuroinflammation. In the present study, we confirmed that PII mitigated memory impairment and decreased Aβ plaque deposition within the cerebral cortex in APP/PS1 mice. Furthermore, PII treatment inhibited NeuN + neuronal loss, decreased astrocyte and microglial activation, and suppressed inflammatory factor production in the cerebral cortex. Collectively, these results suggest that PII is a candidate anti-AD therapeutic agent.

PII, a glycoside derivative, has been widely studied for its therapeutic effects on preventing and treating different diseases, including organic I/R injury, liver injury, inflammation, tumor metastasis, and angiogenesis. According to in vitro and in vivo experimental results, PII decreases the levels of proinflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) and the activities of the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB (Huang et al., 2017). However, studies on the role of PII in the pyroptosis pathway, particularly its role in AD, are limited.

The role of neuroinflammation in AD has garnered increasing attention (Heneka et al., 2013; Huang et al., 2017). Aβ induces astrocyte and microglial activation during AD, resulting in neuroinflammatory damage (Sarlus & Heneka, 2017; Sumsuzzman et al., 2022). The levels of proinflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, TGF-β, and TNF-α) are increased in the brains of AD model mice (Shao et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023). Our findings revealed that these factors were upregulated in the Tg group relative to those in the nTg group, with an increase in the proportion of Iba1 + and GFAP + cells within the frontal cortex. The above results demonstrate the role of inflammation in AD pathogenesis. PII treatment not only alleviates Aβ deposition but also suppresses astrocyte and microglial activation and decreases inflammatory factor production. Therefore, PII exerts neuroprotective effects by suppressing inflammation.

Neuronal degeneration and loss are considered major contributors to cognitive dysfunction in both patients with AD and APP/PS1 mice (Raefsky et al., 2018; West et al., 1994). The activation of hippocampal NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis is associated with neuronal damage, cognitive decline, and neuronal death (Hanslik & Ulland, 2020; Heneka et al., 2013; Ising et al., 2019; Venegas et al., 2017). Aβ1-42 can induce pyroptotic cell death via the GSDMD protein, with the NLRP3-caspase-1 signaling pathway mediating GSDMD cleavage, thereby playing an essential role in Aβ1-42-induced neuronal pyroptosis (Han et al., 2020). NLRP3 or caspase-1 knockdown in AD mouse models has yielded promising results in decreasing Aβ accumulation, improving spatial memory, and alleviating other AD-related symptoms (Heneka et al., 2013). These findings underscore the importance of targeting NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis as a candidate anti-AD treatment. Compared with those in the nTg group, the proportion of NeuN + cells within the frontal cortex in the APP/PS1 group was decreased, resulting in decreased memory performance in terms of escape latency and target quadrant residence time. Furthermore, after PII treatment, the NLRP3, ASC, GSDMD, and caspase-1 mRNA and protein levels were markedly decreased in APP/PS1 mice. Consequently, PII exerts anti-pyroptotic effects in AD through the suppression of the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pathway, thereby mitigating cognitive impairment in AD cases.

Based on our findings, we hypothesize that PII alleviates cognitive impairment via anti-inflammatory and anti-pyroptotic mechanisms, possibly by inhibiting the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD signaling pathway (Fig. 6). Under pathological conditions, abnormal Aβ aggregation triggers an inflammatory response, thereby activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and subsequently producing activated caspase-1 (Woo et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2021). This cascade results in the production of IL-1β and IL-18, which contribute to inflammation. In addition, activated caspase-1 degrades GSDMD, generating the GSDMD-NT domain that induces pyroptosis by penetrating cell membranes; this results in cell death and the release of proinflammatory factors (Liao & Su, 2021; Wang & Hauenstein, 2020; Zhao & Shao, 2016). This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, because of the pathological characteristics of APP/PS1 mice at 7 months of age, this study focused solely on pathological changes in the frontal cortex, omitting the hippocampus, a critical brain region associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD). Consequently, these findings may not fully represent the effects of PII on AD-related brain regions. Second, limitations in the experimental period include the following: the intervention period was restricted to 5–7 months, preventing an exploration of the long-term effects of PII on hippocampal pathology in older mice (such as those aged 8 months and above), where hippocampal pathology is typically more pronounced. Third, certain assessments were omitted: the study did not include histological or immunofluorescence assessments of the hippocampus, thereby hindering the ability to ascertain whether PII exerts regulatory effects on hippocampal function and pathology. Additional experiments are warranted to investigate this further.

Conclusion

We observed that PII administration for 2 months attenuated cognitive impairment, decreased cortical Aβ plaque deposition, and reduced inflammatory responses and cortical neuronal pyroptosis in APP/PS1 mice. PII has significant anti-inflammatory and antipyroptotic effects via the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD signaling pathway. These findings reveal the potential of PII as a treatment option for AD.
