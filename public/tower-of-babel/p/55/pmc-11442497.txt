On the suitability of photocuring-assisted DIW for manufacturing complex prosthesis from commercial dental composites
Authors: Florencia M. Nogales, Óscar Borrero-López, Antonia Pajares, Pedro Miranda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 3-D printing method to produce dental prostheses of complex shapes from a commercial, photocurable resin-ceramic slurry is developed and optimized. The microstructure, mechanical properties and wear behavior of the resulting material are evaluated and compared with a conventional/control sample and other ceramic-polymer dental composites. Commercial resin-ceramic dental slurries can be successfully extruded and appropriately photocured in a low cost 3-D printing system to produce cost-efficient complex dental parts that could be used in indirect restorations. The printing process does not appreciably introduce defects in the material and the 3-D printed composites exhibit mechanical properties (hardness, elastic modulus) and wear resistance comparable to the control material and analogous, conventional dental composites. Introduction

Ceramic-polymer composites are widely used in prosthetic dentistry, both in direct and indirect restorations. They offer a number of advantages compared to alternative dental prosthetic materials, namely good aesthetics, low cost and, especially, ease of processing, fitting and reparability (all conducted chairside in a single session in the case of direct restorations) [1]. However, composite materials have worse resistance to fracture and wear than human enamel and dental ceramics, which can limit the durability of the prostheses, especially in posterior restorations and/or patients with bruxism [2–4].

There are two basic types of dental composites: flowable resin-filler, and rigid CAD/CAM (computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing) composites. The former are commonly used in direct restorations. They typically consist of a relatively high load of ceramic particles (fillers), with sizes ranging from nm to tens of μm, dispersed in a photocurable resin, and the viscous suspensions are delivered manually from a syringe, and subsequently polymerized in-situ using a suitable UV-VIS lamp. The resulting materials have relatively low stiffness (between units and tens of GPa), hardness (between high hundreds of MPa and low units of GPa), and resistance to fracture (between high tens and low hundreds of MPa) [4] and sliding-wear. Of all the basic contact configurations in engineering wear tests (i.e., axial, rolling, rotating, etc.), sliding is the one that more closely resembles tooth-tooth contacts in mastication processes. Wear is typically dependent on the specific tribosystem and loading conditions, however, provided the contact conditions pertain to the same wear regime, results of sliding-wear tests can be compared, with the necessary caveats, via the specific wear rates, SWR, defined as the wear volume divided/normalized by the product of applied load and sliding distance. Nevertheless, there is still a large scatter in the reported values of in-vitro SWR∼10−3–10−5 mm3/Nm [5–8], attributable to the specific contact conditions employed (applied load, sliding speed and lubricant), the choice of antagonist and material microstructure. These SWR values are generally greater than those reported for alternative, CAD/CAM composites (polished) [3]. Albeit more expensive and complex, CAD/CAM technology has the additional advantage of being fully automated, which allows for greater control and precision in the manufacturing of parts with the challenging morphologies that are required in dental prosthetics, and thus can be used in indirect restorations.

Recent technological advances in materials processing by solid freeform fabrication (SFF) techniques have also reached the dental sector, and hold promise for the development of a new generation of prosthetic materials [9, 10]. Compared to CAD/CAM technology, which involves subtractive machining, SFF components are manufactured additively, i.e. by adding material where desired layer-by-layer, and thus neither produce material waste nor contain strength-degrading, machining defects. Direct Ink Writing (DIW) technique, also known as robocasting, uses particulate suspensions as precursors, with suspension rheology being a key parameter controlling the printing process [11, 12]. DIW has been successfully used in dental applications ranging from maxillofacial reconstruction [13] to enamel microstructure emulation [14].

Recently, photocuring-assisted DIW, which combines conventional DIW methodology with photocuring upon deposition, has been proposed as method for producing complex ceramic structures from photocurable resins suspensions [15], and even its combination with near-field electrospinning has been explored as a means to produce thin fibers and scaffolds [16]. However, to the best of our knowledge there has been no studies aimed at determining the suitability of photocuring-assisted DIW for the additive manufacturing of dental restorations from commercially available dental composites. Consequently, the present work investigates the use of an as-received, commercially available resin-filler suspension, which is already optimized and indicated for use in dental restorations, as precursor in a modified 3-D printer suitable to perform photocuring-assisted DIW, with a view to developing cost-efficient strategies for the additive fabrication of indirect dental prosthesis of complex shape. Besides providing a ‘proof of concept’ of such cost-effective dental restoration fabrication technology, this project seeks to establish structure-property relationships at the relevant length-scales of the resulting materials, without aspiring to provide a clinically relevant assessment of such biomaterials in practical applications. Accordingly, special attention is paid to the microstructure, mechanical properties and sliding-wear resistance of the material developed, the latter being employed as a proxy of long-term durability. Results are discussed within the broader context of ceramic-polymer dental composites.

Materials and methods

Starting material

A commercially available, photocurable slurry (Ceram.X Spectra ST LV, Dentsply Sirona, NC, USA) was employed. This is a popular dental composite, delivered in a syringe and curable in the wavelength range of 440–480 nm. According to the supplier, it consists of 57–60 vol% of spherically shaped filler granules (SphereTECTM), comprising barium-aluminum-borosilicate glass, prepolymerized resin and ytterbium fluoride, embedded in resin containing additional smaller primary filler particles. The total amount of inorganic fillers is 48–50 vol%, with particle size ranging from 0.1 to 3.0 μm, and the organic resin has a complex formulation based on methacrylate modified polysiloxane and dimethacrylate resins. This material is clinically indicated for both direct and indirect dental restorations [17].

Photocuring-assisted DIW

Photocuring-assisted 3-D plotting was conducted in a Delta Wasp 2040 printer (WASP, Massa Lombarda, Italy), adapted to extrude ceramics slurries instead of traditional filaments and with a removable structure around the extruder supporting the blue LED lights (LUXEON Rebel, Lumileds Holding B.V. CA; λ = 460 nm, 480 mW max. power) required for photopolimerzation. A schematic diagram of the in-house developed printing head is shown in Fig. 1, together with a close-up image of the photocuring-assisted DIW printing process. The commercial dental composite was too viscous to be readily extrudable at room temperature and, therefore, the temperature inside the printer chamber was maintained at 40–50 °C to decrease its viscosity and facilitate extrusion. Following preliminary attempts, the nozzle diameter, layer height and deposition print speed were adjusted to 0.41 mm, 0.2 mm and 3.5 mm/s, respectively. An extremely low retraction distance of 0.2 mm and a retraction speed of 7.5 mm/s was enough to prevent dripping because of the composite’s high viscosity. Adhesion between the first layer and the Al2O3 printing plate was ensured by establishing a higher initial layer height (0.3 mm) and by applying hairspray to the build plate. To use as control group, an additional material was fabricated from the same commercial slurry, but employing a conventional method (i.e., not 3-D printing). A circular plastic mould with dimensions of 11 mm (radius) × 5 mm (depth) was previously created. Material was manually added to it, from the bottom up, using the as-received syringe containing the slurry, layer by layer, following manufacturer’s guidelines and ensuring that each layer was less than 2 mm in thickness. Each layer was cured immediately upon deposition using a blue LED Light Curing (Bader Europe Group S.L, Spain) with 2000 mw/cm2 and a wavelength of 420–480 nm, for a duration of 10 s. Once the entire specimen was formed, it was carefully extracted from the mould.

Materials characterization

Microstructural characterization of the printed material was undertaken by scanning-electron microscopy (FE‐SEM; Quanta 3D FEG, FEI, The Netherlands) using backscattered-electrons at 20 kV. Specimens were observed both uncoated and gold coated.

Depth-sensing indentation tests were performed (Nanotest, Micro Materials Ltd., Wrexham, UK) in parallelepipedal specimens (polished) using a diamond Berkovich tip, in load control mode, at a loading rate of 150 mN/s, up to a maximum load 3000 mN that was held for 20 s before unloading at the same rate. Nine tests, separated from each other a distance of 1 mm, were performed on the same parallelepipedal, polished specimen. The effective elastic modulus, E* = E/(1 − υ2), with υ being the material’s Poisson ratio, and hardness, H, values of the printed composite were determined using the Oliver-Pharr method [18]. An attempt was made also to evaluate the fracture toughness (K) of the resulting materials using the indentation method [19]. However, none of the indentation marks produced at different loads exhibited any radial cracks, which are necessary to estimate K by this method, indicating that the produced materials are tough and not prone to fracture, leaving wear as the potentially more probable failure mechanism for this type of indirect restorations.

Sliding-wear tests were conducted, using a pin-on-disk configuration, widely employed to simulate mastication processes in-vitro [20], on a dedicated tribometer (High Temperature Tribometer: THT, Anton Paar GmbH, Austria). Mirror-finished zirconia spheres of radius R = 3 mm (Goodfellow Cambridge Ltd., UK), simulating a hard prosthetic antagonist, were used as pin, and polished parallelepipedal specimens as disk. The normal load applied was F = 10 N, which is within the range of cuspal contact loads in actual mastication processes [21]. The radius of the circular wear track was 3 mm. The sliding speed was 10 mm·s−1. The total sliding distance was L = 150 m, corresponding to 7958 cycles. Tests were conducted at room temperature under lubrication with artificial saliva (Lacer S.A., Barcelona, Spain), of viscosity 3.22 mm2⋅s−1 at 20 °C. For each material, tests were performed in three different specimens (one test per specimen). The wear scars at the conclusion of the tests were examined by optical profilometry (Profilm, Filmetrics, San Diego, CA), optical microscopy (Epiphot 300, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and SEM using secondary electrons accelerated at 10 kV. For each test, the wear volume of the disk was computed as the cross-sectional area of the wear scar, obtained from the scar profile using the optical profilometer, multiplied by its circumference length. On each wear scar, three separate 2-D profilometry measurements, as depicted in Fig. 3, were conducted perpendicular to the sliding direction to get statistically representative average values.Fig. 3Example of a profilometric measurement performed on the scar left after wear testing the material obtained from the commercial resin-filler slurry. Fundamental micro-indentation experiments were conducted to assess particle-matrix adhesion, of the composites. Tests were performed in both axial/normal and sliding/scratch modes (Revetest RST3, Anton Paar, Graz, Austria). The load was applied using Rockwell-C diamond indenters. All tests were run at ambient conditions without lubrication. For the axial tests, a maximum normal load of 50 N was applied with a 200-μm-radius tip, at a loading rate of 50 N/min. For the sliding tests, a 20-μm-radius tip was used, and the load was increased progressively from 1 N to 25 N, at a loading rate of 25 N/min and a sliding speed of 5 mm/min. Each test was repeated three times. The surface damage generated was inspected using optical microscopy.

Regarding the tribomechanical behavior of the 3-D printed composite, hardness and effective elastic modulus, measured from the depth-sensing indentation tests, are H = 0.87 ± 0.01 GPa, and E* = 12.64 ± 0.08 GPa, respectively. The values obtained for the conventional (control) composite are H = 0.82 ± 0.01 GPa, and E* = 12.68 ± 0.06 GPa. Therefore, static mechanical performance was not compromised at all by the printing procedure.

The pin-on-disk experiments showed that, in all tests, after a very brief, initial run-in, a stationary state was reached at a sliding distance of ≈5 m; thence onwards, the coefficient of friction remained stable at a relatively low value of ≈0.18. The profilometry measurements at the conclusion of the tests (after 150 m of sliding) yielded a calculated wear volume (V) in the 3-D printed disks of 0.06 ± 0.02 mm3. This results in an average specific wear rate SWR = (4 ± 1)·10−5 mm3/N·m, which pertains to severe wear conditions, according to the tribological classification. By contrast, the zirconia counterspheres showed no visible scar (only light surface scratches), and thus negligible wear. For the conventional (control) material, the average steady-state coefficient of friction was ≈0.19, and the V and SWR values obtained were, respectively 0.06 ± 0.01 mm3 and (3.9 ± 0.8) ·10−5 mm3/N·m. As with the static mechanical performance, these values indicate that the printing procedure does not degrade the wear resistance.

Discussion

The results from this work demonstrate the feasibility and ease of obtaining parts with complex shapes and a high resolution by photocuring-assisted DIW of dental resin-filler slurries. The images of the transversal sections showed that even under the microscope it is impossible to distinguish individual printing lines or layers, neither through the presence of internal printing defects/pores nor even through the stair-step effect typically observable at the external surface of 3-D printed samples. This attests to the quality of the printing process and the correct fine tuning of the selected printing parameters, but is also an indication of the excellent self-wetting properties of the commercial dental composite. In turn, this paves the way for obtaining cost-efficient indirect restorations from starting materials which are conventionally employed for direct restorations.

While 3-D printed dental composites are free from manufacturing defects at the mesostructural scale, they contain flaws at the microstructural scale (Fig. 5), which could result in decreased adhesion and/or weaker interfaces between the matrix and the filler granules that may in turn degrade the strength and wear resistance. Such defects in resin-filler composites could arise from the contraction of the resin upon curing [22], or be a result of processes employed to incorporate the particles to the resin to improve viscosity, e.g., atomization-produced aggregates as in this particular case. Indeed, they are also present in the conventional/control material obtained from the same slurry (Fig. 6). Development of novel slurries specifically conceived for 3-D printing should be aimed at avoiding their occurrence.

From the mechanical point of view, the 3-D printed material is relatively soft, however the ceramic reinforcements result in a greater stiffness than that of non-reinforced polymers, as is required in dental applications. Thus, the evaluated modulus roughly triples the values (2.9–4.7 GPa) of available 3-D printed dental resins employed in provisional dental restorations [23]. The measured hardness and modulus values are comparable to those reported for conventional (i.e., not 3-D printed) dental composite materials obtained from similar slurries (0.3–0.9 GPa and 7–20 GPa, respectively [24]). In fact, the mechanical properties of the 3-D printed composite pertain to the mid-upper range of the above literature values, which adds to the quality of the parts produced through the proposed AM method. For comparison, CAD/CAM dental composites are somewhat harder and stiffer, with values oscillating between 1.1–2.3 GPa (H), and 13–38 GPa (E) [3]. As baseline, human enamel has a hardness of 3.5–4 GPa, and a modulus of 70–100 GPa. Clearly, there is a large mechanical property mismatch between healthy enamel and all composite prosthetic materials. A lower hardness than enamel means that composite prostheses can be potentially abraded by antagonist dentition—which is arguably preferrable to prosthesis causing wear to the opposing healthy tooth. Indeed, the minimal damage observed on the zirconia counterspheres after the wear tests confirms that the 3-D printed composites are ‘antagonist friendly’.

Under the tested conditions the 3-D printed composite shows an SWR of comparable order of magnitude than previously reported values for similar dental resin-filler composites containing- sub-micron glass particles [5–8], with no significant differences between 3-D printed and control materials. While such SWR value pertains to severe wear conditions, it falls within the lower end of the range of values reported in the literature. This can be attributed to the use of a highly polished zirconia sphere as antagonist (pin) in our tests. A smooth zirconia counterbody is well known to minimize two-body wear of opposing dental materials [20, 25, 26]. This contrasts with the use of antagonists such as steatite glass or glass-ceramic materials in most previous studies. The latter easily release sharp glass particles, which remain trapped under the contact and typically produce very aggressive three-body abrasion, thus increasing wear of the composite specimens. The relevant takeout is that 3-D printed dental resin-based composites are not necessarily less wear resistant than the conventional materials. As with the mechanical properties, this is promising because the 3-D printing process can be further optimized, as discussed below.

The microscopic examination of the scars obtained at the conclusion of the pin-on-disk tests (Fig. 7) reveals the main wear modes and mechanisms. The primary wear mode is two-body abrasion, caused by the significantly harder zirconia countersphere, and effected at the asperity level. The main microwear mechanisms are plastic deformation and particle pull-out. Moreover, sharp individual asperity-composite microcontacts cause more intense, localized plastic deformation, and part of that deformed material is extruded out of the microcontacts leading to material loss [27] (schematic in Fig. 7A, inset), as evidenced by the presence of flaky debris. Plastic deformation is greatly favored in resin composites largely due to the soft polymer matrix. Even in composites with high filler loads, weak particle-matrix interfaces result in large relative displacements under shear stress, adding to permanent deformation. The relatively poor bonding between the ceramic particles and the matrix also leads to wear by particle pull-out. This causes more severe damage than plastic deformation. Particle detachment is in principle favored in defective regions, i.e., within the vicinity of the granular flaws/microstructural discontinuities shown in Fig. 5D, where the bonding could be further impaired. Pulled-out ceramic (hard) particles trapped under the contact may contribute to further wear by three-body abrasion. However, because filler particles in the suspension employed are mostly sub-micrometric, the extent of such three-body abrasion is expected to be relatively low (i.e., polishing), thus constituting a secondary wear mode.

To assess whether the 3-D printed material contains weaker particle-matrix interfaces compared to the conventional/control material, fundamental microindentation tests were performed in both axial and sliding modes (Fig. 8). Under axial loading, both materials exhibit a ring-like crack well outside the contact zone. The relatively tortuous paths suggest failure along particle-matrix interfaces induced by the Hertzian stress [28], with no significant differences observed between 3-D printed and control composites. Under sliding at progressively increasing load, initial damage by plastic deformation is followed by particle pull-out at intermediate loads. The macroscopic wear observed on the surface of the 3-D printed material after the pin-on-disk tests is, therefore, chiefly the result of cumulative microscopic/asperity microwear processes under the sliding zirconia sphere. In ceramic-based dental materials, it has been demonstrated that the macrowear volume can be obtained by summation of all microwear events under the macro contact, and follows Archard’s law [29, 30]:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V=K\frac{{FL}}{H}$$\end{document}V=KFLHwith K a dimensionless wear coefficient, and H material hardness. Within this framework, the relatively large macrowear volume on the 3-D printed dental composite is thus due to its low hardness, which reflects the ease of permanent deformation processes in this material.

In the general context of dental prosthetic materials, ceramic-polymer composites are very attractive, and indeed find widespread use, due to their good aesthetics and ease of processing. However, composites indeed have the problem of a low hardness/wear resistance compared to alternative materials such as zirconia and some glass-ceramics, and even human enamel [3]. This in turn limits their durability, especially in posterior restorations or bruxist individuals. The inherent microstructural aspects which contribute to the low hardness/wear resistance of all ceramic-polymer composites (both obtained from resin-filler suspensions, and CAD/CAM) are: (i) low hardness of the polymer phase, and (ii) weakness of filler-matrix interfaces. In addition, there are extrinsic defects that can degrade further the wear resistance of some composites. In particular, CAD/CAM materials typically possess relatively large surface defects, introduced during the subtractive machining. As previously mentioned, additively manufactured resin-filler composites are free from machining defects, but contain other defects, albeit at smaller, microstructural scale. In particular, the composite printed from a commercial slurry investigated here shows spherical defects, arising during processing, which can worsen particle adhesion. Being already fully polymerized, CAD/CAM composites presumably avoid the latter type of defects. Encouragingly, despite the presence of such defects, 3-D printed composites do not experience more severe wear than conventionally produced resin-based dental composites.

Compared to CAD/CAM technology, additive manufacturing techniques such as 3-D printing offer room for improvement and optimization. One aspect that could be investigated in greater deal is the printing pattern, which in principle would allow directional control of processing defects and interfaces. In turn, this would pave the way for engineering microstructures in ways which improve strength and/or hinder material removal processes at relevant length-scales and allow for further improvements in wear resistance, well beyond what could be expected based on the mechanical properties of the constituting phases of the composite material [31].

In summary, 3-D printing holds promise as a cost-effective, potentially very versatile technique to produce customized dental prostheses parts with complex shapes. It brings all the advantages of additive manufacturing, especially the avoidance of material waste and machining defects. The resulting 3-D printed materials from commercially available suspensions have properties at least comparable to those of conventional materials obtained from similar slurries, but the technique can be optimized further to improve some properties, such as strength and/or wear resistance, via microstructural engineering. This warrants subsequent investigations. Also, the authors acknowledge that the preliminary results presented here are statistically insufficient and therefore lack clinical validity. Once all processing issues are solved and the resulting microstructures are fully optimized, extensive statistical analyses of results obtained under clinically relevant settings should be undertaken, in collaboration with clinicians and biostatisticians with relevant expertise before the proposed technologies cand be incorporated to the dental clinical practice.

Conclusions

The use of an optimized 3-D printing method to produce dental prostheses of complex shapes from a commercial, photocurable resin-filler slurry was investigated. The microstructure, mechanical properties and wear behavior of the resulting material were evaluated and compared with a conventional/control material obtained from the same slurry and other ceramic-polymer dental composites. The following conclusions can be obtained:As-received commercial resin-filler dental slurries can be successfully extruded in a 3-D printing equipment and subsequently photocured to produce cost-efficient dental parts of complex shapes, which could in turn be used as indirect restorations.3-D printed composites can be produced free of machining surface defects, but the use of a commercial slurry leads to defects at smaller scales, which are also present in a conventional/control material.3-D printed composites exhibit the same mechanical properties (hardness, elastic modulus) and wear resistance as a conventional/control composite obtained from the same slurry, and at least similar properties compared to other dental composites obtained from comparable slurries.Under sliding contact against a hard antagonist, the main mechanisms of material removal of 3-D printed composites (and the control material) are plastic deformation at the asperity level and particle pull-out, resulting from the composite’s soft polymer matrix and interfacial weakness.
