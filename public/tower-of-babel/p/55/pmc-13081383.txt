Challenges of being left-handed in a right-handed world of clinical dentistry: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Rumaisa Shahid, Aqsa Choudhry, Tasbiha Sehar, Muneeb Ahmed Lone, Maham Muneeb Lone, Taimoor Malik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the global relevance of handedness influencing clinical performance, there is dearth of research that has explored the experiences of left-handed dental practitioners (LHDPs) within Pakistan. Very few studies have reported the challenges and difficulties that left-handed dental students and practitioners may face in our setting. The objective of this study was to assess the self-perceived ergonomic and clinical challenges encountered by LHDPs in dental institutes across Sindh, Pakistan.MethodsThis cross-sectional survey-based study was conducted from March - June 2025 in 17 public and private dental colleges of Sindh to evaluate clinical practices and self-reported challenges faced by left-handed dental students and practitioners. Data collection was carried out by a structured, self-administered questionnaire. The questions assessed the clinical experience and challenges faced by left-handed participants when practicing dentistry. The questionnaire also assessed institutional accommodation and ergonomic considerations in the clinical setting. The questionnaire was disseminated by a non-probability snowball sampling technique because of the limited number of LHDPs. Data was analyzed with SPSS v. 23.0.ResultsA total of 100 left-handed participants completed the survey. Mean age of the participants was 26.2 ± 5.9 years with 72 females and 28 males. Majority of the participants had 0–2 years (n = 70) of clinical experience. The most frequently adopted chair position while working on right-sided dental units was 1 o’clock (n = 37), and 12 o’clock (n = 30). More than half of the respondents (n = 51) identified the second quadrant as the most difficult to access when working on a right-handed dental unit. Seventy-three (73%) participants reported experiencing difficulties while handling various instruments during clinical procedures; this concern decreased significantly with increase in clinical experience (p = 0.007). As clinical experience increased, perception of quality of work being diminished due to handedness also decreased (p = 0.042). A significantly greater number of participants with more clinical experience used their non-dominant hand compared to the less experienced ones (p = 0.002). Sixty participants (60%) believed they were at a higher risk of developing musculoskeletal problems due to the right-sided configuration of dental chairs.ConclusionsThis study highlights significant challenges faced by left-handed dental practitioners practicing in the public and private dental colleges of Pakistan. LHDPs perceive that they are inadequately equipped, lack guidance and have an unfavorable ergonomic clinical environment. To improve practice of LHPDs, availability of left-handed dental units and instruments and integrating left-handed clinical guidance in the undergraduate curriculum may help improve their clinical performance and address the global inequalities that they face. These findings are however, based on self-reported perceptions, so it may limit the generalizability of the study.

Background

To perform meticulous execution of dental procedures, complete mastery over clinical skills is required by dentists when performing them [1]. Excellent manual dexterity developed during the undergraduate training years is important in perfecting dental procedures for optimal results [2]. Successful treatment outcomes depend on various factors, including proper adaptation to instruments and dental units while performing a procedure. For effective treatment outcomes, dentists must possess a clear understanding of dental anatomy, use suitable instruments with precise angulation, and maintain proper posture [3].

In the general population, the average percentage of left-handed people is around 10% [4]. This number is far less for dental practitioners. Reflecting this disparity, there is a dominance of right-handed practitioners in dentistry because of which most dental equipment and clinical setups are designed to suit right-handed ergonomics [5]. This universal standard creates structural barrier for the minority of left-handed dental practitioners (LHDPs). As a result, their needs and difficulties are overlooked, that may lead to poor performance and reduced efficiency. This is of great significance, as the quality of care provided to patients largely depends on the practitioner’s performance, which in turn is tied to their psychomotor skills [2].

This systemic bias also extends into dental education and institutional framework. Majority of dental education and operating procedures of dental schools, including standard operating protocols, techniques as well as resource allocation, are designed favoring right-handed dental practitioners [1, 6]. The limited manufacture and demand of left-hand dental equipment in the market limits its availability to the practitioners [5]. Consequently, LHDPs are forced to adapt to environments not supporting their dominant hand, leading to discomfort, inefficiency, and potential compromise in clinical performance [7]. The physical strain that may be triggered by constrained positioning, incompatible instruments, and visual limitations in the operative field increases the ergonomic burden on LHDPs [8]. These risks can be further compounded by prolonged static posture, repetitive motion, and lack of adequate rest periods [9].

A survey across four dental colleges in Saudi Arabia revealed that 51% left-handed students had difficulty in performing clinical procedures. Around 84% stated that their colleges were inadequately equipped for left-handed practice [1]. In a study by Tezel et al., Turkish dental students reported a higher prevalence of neck pain (67% vs. 43%) and shoulder pain (78% vs. 58%) in left-handed students compared to their right-handed peers [10]. Similarly, a Tunisian study also reported that a very large majority (82.5%) of left-handed students struggled working with right-handed instruments and had significantly more back and neck pain (77.5%) [8]. These findings highlight that ergonomic neglect is not confined to only a region, rather is a global issue in dental education and practice. In Pakistan, however, the experiences of LHDPs remain largely overlooked; a situation which is made more challenging by lack of infrastructure and limited resources.

Even though the ergonomic burdens faced by right-handed dental practitioners have been extensively studied, little work has been done on specific challenges faced by left-handed dental students and practitioners in Pakistan. As a result of this evidence gap, institutions in Pakistan continue to rely on right-hand oriented training models, compromising both the quality of clinical training and the long-term health of LHDPs.

As of yet, even though there is substantial data present on the ergonomic challenges faced by general practitioners, there is still minimal work regarding the consistent difficulties encountered by left-handed dental students and practitioners in Pakistan. This minimal work is either limited to a single institute or only explores the challenges and not the institutional support available [5]. The aim of this study is twofold. First, to explore the self-perceived clinical and training challenges encountered by LHDPs in dental institutes across Sindh, Pakistan. Second, to assess how institutional infrastructure and academic frameworks accommodate their needs.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted over a period of 04 months from March to June 2025. It collectively included 17 public and private dental colleges of Sindh to assess clinical barriers and training limitations faced by left-handed dental students and practitioners. The study population included left-handed third and fourth-year undergraduate students, postgraduate residents, house officers, and faculty members working in the clinical departments of the dental colleges of Sindh. Participants unwilling to participate or had acquired handedness because of any underlying reasons, such as trauma, paralysis, or any other factor, were excluded from the study.

Ethical approval was obtained from the ethical review board of Jinnah Sindh Medical University (Ref # JSMU/IRB/2024/970). Sample size was calculated using Open Epi calculator. The literature does not have any data on the local prevalence of LHDP, hence 6.6% was assumed based on the study by Johanny et al. [1], because of its demographic similarity. With a finite population of 1660, margin of error set at 5%, confidence level at 95%, and power at 80, the minimum sample size came out to be 90 participants. We recruited 100 participants to ensure incorporation of broader experiences.

Data collection was carried out using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. The questions were adapted and altered from previous studies that explored lapses in left-handed dental practice [1, 11]. The questionnaire assessed the self-perceived clinical experiences and challenges faced by left-handed participants along with accommodation of institutional framework serving to their needs, i.e. availability of left-sided dental units and other ergonomic considerations in the setting. The questionnaire was developed and subsequently underwent a validation process. It was reviewed by four experienced dental academicians to ensure content validity. Suggestions and feedback were integrated to the final version of the questionnaire. Face validity and internal consistency were established during the pilot phase where it was tested among 10 left-handed dental practitioners (excluded from final analysis).

The questionnaire was disseminated to participants fulfilling the inclusion criteria across the participating dental colleges. A non-probability snowball sampling technique was used because of the limited number of left-handed practitioners, with an aim for a proportional representation in various institutes. To address the selection bias, we initiated recruiting the participants from all the years and levels of faculty to ensure a broad demographic inclusion of participants. In the first section, informed consent of the participants was sought. The second part of the questionnaire consisted of six questions related to the demographics and 18 questions that assessed the clinical experience, institutional accommodation and self-perceived challenges faced by left-handed participants when practicing dentistry. The investigators were present at all times to clear any ambiguities the participants may have, and the forms collected right afterwards to maximize response rate.

Only the principal and co-investigators had access to the data. In accordance with the institutional data policy, data has been stored in password protected files that will be kept for five years post publication and will be deleted afterwards. Data was analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS v. 23.0). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the responses and chi-square was used to assess the association between clinical experience and designations with reported challenges. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 100 left-handed participants from public and private dental colleges in Sindh, Pakistan completed the survey. Out of these 100 participants, 57 were from public dental colleges and 43 from private ones. The mean age of the participants was 26.2 ± 5.9 years. There were 72(72.0%) females and 28(28.0%) males within the study sample. Experience years skewed towards 0–2 years (n = 70;70.0%) of experience; 12(12.0%) participants reported 3–4 years, 09(9.0%) reported 5–10 years, and 09(9.0%) had 10 + years of clinical experience. There were 31(31.0%) house officers, 19(19.0%) final-year students, 15(15.0%) third-year dental students, 14(14.0%) post-graduate residents, and 21(21.0%) consultants/ faculty members.

When inquired about the chair position they use while working on the right-sided dental units, the most preferred position was 1 o’clock (n = 37;26.6%), followed by 12 o’clock (n = 30;21.6%). Frequency of other chair positions adopted while working are mentioned in Table 1. More than half the respondents (n = 51;51.0%) identified the second quadrant as the most difficult to access working on a right-handed dental unit, while almost one-third respondents (n = 36;36.0%) cited difficulty in working on the first quadrant. Only a minority had difficulty in accessing the third (n = 23;23.0%) or fourth quadrants (n = 15;15.0%).

The most commonly reported challenge for LHDPs working on right-sided units was performing root canal treatment (n = 48;31.0%), followed by extractions (n = 42;27.0%) and crown preparation (n = 32;21.0%) while a small number (n = 15;10.0%) reported difficulty with scaling and bracketing and wire placement (n = 11; 7.0%) as shown in Fig. 1. Seventy-two practitioners (72.0%) indicated they had made adaptations to their workspace to accommodate their handedness. Even though a significant majority (n = 73;73.0%) reported experiencing difficulties while handling various instruments during clinical procedures, this concern decreased significantly with increase in clinical experience (p = 0.007). When asked about the use of the non-dominant hand while working on right-sided dental units, (n = 36;36.0%) participants stated that they sometimes used their non-dominant hand, while (n = 31;31.0%) reported using it often, and (n = 9;9.0%) always resorted to this practice (Fig. 2). Our results also revealed that a significantly greater number of faculty members with more clinical experience used their non-dominant hand compared to the less experienced house officers and dental students (p = 0.002).

Discussion

The results of our study report the experiences and self-perceived challenges of left-handed dental practitioners in Sindh, Pakistan that mirror findings from diverse international studies. Our study results reveal the systemic ergonomic barriers and adaptation strategies that LHDPs of our population face. The study participants were predominantly early-career practitioners (70.0% with ≤ 2 years) who were heavily reliant on improvisation, such as chair rotation, cross handed work, or standing, to compensate for right-handed dental units. Similar practices were documented among Malaysian undergraduates, many of whom reported workspace adaptations and a sense of clinical inefficiency due to non-ergonomic infrastructure [12].

A striking 73.0% of the study participants perceived difficulties in handling instruments, often switching to their non-dominant hand for clinical procedures. These adaptive behaviors are similar to those reported by LHDPs working in India, Malaysia, UAE and Saudi Arabia, who also reported frequently resorting to working around techniques due to dental settings designed for right-handed individuals [1, 11–13]. A study on LHDPs in India revealed that 41.0% of the participants faced problems while performing clinical procedures. In the same study, 93.0% of the participants reported that they preferred working on dental chairs designed specifically for LHDPs [11]. A study in UAE reported that 36.9% dental practitioners were forced to change their handedness because of lack of facilities catering to their needs. Although these studies differ methodologically, their findings remain consistent with ours regarding perceived operational challenges among LHDPs working in right-handed environments.

Our cohort’s high prevalence of self-reported musculoskeletal pain (64.0%) parallels findings with the study conducted in Saudi Arabia [1] supporting the results that LHDPs are more prone to developing musculoskeletal disorders. Similar to our findings, a Tunisian study reported that left-handed dental students experienced higher rates of neck and lower back pain (77.5%) when adapting to right-handed equipment [8].

It has been noted in our study that only 18% of participants reported having left-handed dental units available in their institutes, 70% never learned left-handed ergonomic approaches, and 80% lacked LHDP specific instructors. This is consistent with a study conducted in UAE by Azim et al., that revealed 61.5% of their participants never received any guidance to navigate challenges that comes with being a LHDP [13]. This also parallels findings from Saudi Arabia and Malaysia, where LHDPs reported a lack of accommodating infrastructure and guidance [1, 12]. Kassim et al., in a study on Malaysian dental students reported that majority dental schools train their students according to right-handed equipment neglecting training needs of LHDPs [12]. A qualitative survey “I am a lefty in a right-handed world” poignantly captured the feeling of neglect associated with the absence of left-sided dental setups [14]. It mentioned that all participants reported difficulty while working with right-sided equipment and dental chairs. These barriers, coupled with negligent guidance available, negatively affect the confidence levels of students in their early clinical years [14].

More than half of the participants (51%) in the present study reported the 2nd quadrant, while 36% reported the 1st quadrant to be the most challenging to access when working on right-sided dental units. This pattern is similar to results of a study on Indian left-handed dental students, where 41.7% also reported the upper-left quadrant to be the most difficult, followed by the right-upper quadrant (29.8%) [11]. These findings reflect a consistent ergonomic disadvantage in accessing these quadrants from the standard right-sided chair setup.

In our study, the most reported difficult procedure was endodontic treatment, followed by extraction and tooth preparations for fixed prosthesis. All these procedures require fine motor skills and visual accuracy, thereby were a challenge for LHDPs to perform when working on right-sided units. Similar to these findings, a large majority (70.0%) of Tunisian left-handed dental students also identified endodontic treatment to be the most challenging procedure to perform [8]. Similarly, LHDPs in India reported discomfort and ergonomic strain when performing clinical procedures in the posterior teeth of 2nd and 4th quadrants. In their opinion, visualization and instrument orientation were compromised in these quadrants when working in right-handed setups [11]. It is therefore the need of the hour to train left-handed students according to their handedness in institutional settings.

A significant proportion of early career left-handed participants (0–2 years of experience) reported that right-sided dental chairs negatively impacted their performance. This perception became less frequent and more neutrally distributed among mid-career and senior practitioners (p = 0.042). This pattern suggests that clinicians develop personal adaptation strategies over time and adapt to the infrastructure and resources available to them. Although no studies were found in literature that directly compare efficiency by experience level, LHDPs in a qualitative study by Al-Lawati et al., describe an initial feeling of inefficiency and lack of guidance in early years of clinical practice [14]. They characterized low confidence and discomfort due to right-handed equipment in their early clinical years, but reported improved adaptability and self-reliance despite institutional limitations with experience [14]. Interviews in a qualitative study conducted on final year left-handed students in Australia revealed similar results [2].

Our study findings that a large majority of LHDPs (75.7%) believe that right-handed equipment decreases their work quality aligns with existing research indicating that LH students often face moderate to severe difficulties in early clinical practice. These difficulties can primarily be due to unnatural positioning and restricted visibility [9]. As clinical experience increases, however, this negative perception reportedly dissipates. This shift is most likely the result of professional adaptation; as experienced practitioners develop compensatory motor skills or even achieve a certain degree of ambidexterity [1].

Although nearly half of our study participants was aware of dental tools designed specifically for left-handed clinicians, only 18% of them had access to left-sided dental chairs. This large disparity between awareness and availability suggests a limitation of unavailable infrastructure. Future studies should be conducted to explore this resource disparity that exists and what effect is has on the quality of patient care provided by left-handed clinicians. To date, very scanty literature could be found that explores this cause and effect. Collectively, the lack of institutional support, equipment, and absence of formal training is an oversight in dental education globally [12, 15]. The literature establishes that handedness bias in dental ergonomics has clinical, safety, and career-long health implications [16]. Additionally, equipment-specific ergonomic factors, such as use of light-curing units, can impact both the operator posture and treatment efficacy [17].

This study, the first in our region, highlights the self-reported clinical as well as ergonomic challenges faced by LHDPs in a predominantly right-handed institutional setup. The findings emphasize an evident gap in academic teaching, institutional infrastructure and training strategies. Similar to international studies, our study also reports that left-handed practitioners face the challenge of working in less than optimal environments. Highlighting and rectifying these concerns will not only improve the clinical competencies of LHDPs, but also align us with the latest evidence-based practices for dental education in Pakistan.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of our study is its heterogeneous sample; including students, consultants, and faculty from both public and private institutions of Sindh. Inclusion of this diverse population has helped in capturing changes in perception, adaptation, and stress that the LHDPs deal with over time.

Some major study limitations include self-reported data, which could be subject to recall or social desirability bias, a frequent issue that is faced in such surveys. The small sample size recruited by snowball sampling technique may have missed a few potential participants.

Future recommendations

The reported difficulties by left-handed dental practitioners highlight the need for organizational reform at the undergraduate as well as the postgraduate level. Local regulatory bodies such as the Pakistan Medical and Dental Council (PMDC) should acknowledge handedness as a legitimate factor that can influence clinical performance of dentists. Recommendations can be put forward for establishment of government and institutional policies focusing on ambidextrous dental units or left-handed units. Establishment of simulation laboratories and left-handed instructors should be encouraged to improve left-handed learning during pre-clinical years. Ergonomic training modules, including those focused on left-side dental techniques and posture instructions can also be included in the undergraduate curriculum. Without these changes, the burden of adaptation will always be on the practitioner, affecting their efficiency, comfort, and musculoskeletal health. Since our study provides essential preliminary insights, it can serve as a pilot for future large scale studies. These can generate robust data substantial enough to develop standardized ergonomic policies and curriculum amendments for LHDPs.

Conclusions

This study highlights significant challenges faced by left-handed dental practitioners practicing in public and private dental colleges of Pakistan. Since the clinical infrastructure is dominantly equipped for right-handed practitioners, LHDPs believe that they are inadequately equipped, lack guidance specific to their handedness, and are forced to practice in an unfavorable ergonomic environment. They perceive their handedness can cause a high prevalence of musculoskeletal pain and compromise their clinical efficiency. Easier availability of left-handed dental units, instruments and integrating clinical guidance specific to left-handed practice in the undergraduate curriculum can be an important first step in improving their clinical efficiency. Implementation of these strategies may not only enhance their clinical performance, but may also help address the global inequalities that are felt by LHDPs in current clinical setups.
