Future‐Making From Afar: Digital Twins in Medicine
Authors: Elisa Elhadj, Maiju Tanninen, Ine Van Hoyweghen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDigital twins in medicine are packaged as inevitable, disruptive technologies that will revolutionise healthcare, yet their integration into clinical practice remains slow. In this article, we extend current research on digital twins by shifting attention to the actors behind the technology and their visions. Drawing on multi‐sited fieldwork, we identify from where and how the In Silico medicine community envisions the future of medicine and trace embedded ideas and logics. Our analysis reveals a friction between the narrowing of visions of medicine, the human body and clinicians and the technical features of digital twins, which are intended to open futures and possibilities through virtual interventions. We show how digital twins are developed at a distance from the very practices they seek to revolutionise and how the field attempts to navigate this distance through conversion and training. As with other digital health technologies, the burden of adjustment is placed on clinicians. By attending to how distance between modellers and clinical settings plays out in practice, we show how this “future‐making from afar” constrains but also enables specific modes of engagement. It offers a starting point for demystifying digital twins in medicine and reflects on the kinds of futures the field promotes.

Introduction

On a piece of paper, a hot air balloon floats into the sky, carrying in its basket actors representing the future of medicine: patients, computers, medical tools, modellers and the digital twins that they develop. A clinician is pictured on the lower half of a ladder hanging out of the basket, struggling to get into the hot air balloon that seems to be escaping. The picture conveys a striking message: the clinician is struggling to catch up with the future of medicine and should urgently get on board. It is a simple sketch drawn during a workshop by a small group of engineers, modellers and other professionals, intended to summarise a two‐hour‐long conversation on In Silico technologies. Yet it offers a clear visual metaphor, one that raises questions about by whom and how the future of healthcare is imagined.

Across various industries, computational modelling and simulation (CM&S) or the so‐called In Silico methods, have become standard tools for predicting and planning. They “involve the use of numerical tools to create virtual models of complex systems to better understand processes and predict effects of different interventions” (Lesage et al. 2023, 2). Digital twins, typically defined as virtual representations of a physical entity or system, fall under that umbrella. This definition is often extended to emphasise that the model is interactive and continuously updated, mirroring the current state and behaviour of its physical counterpart (Korenhof et al. 2023). Aerospace and aeronautical engineers rely on such simulations to design aircraft, ships and rockets before a prototype is ever built. Similarly, climate scientists use large‐scale modelling to predict weather patterns decades into the future. This article focuses on the field of medicine, where efforts to model and ‘twin’ human bodies are slowly gaining traction through the development of In Silico medicine techniques. By combining data‐driven approaches, such as machine learning, with knowledge‐driven modelling based on established scientific knowledge (Quarteroni et al. 2025), these techniques strive to develop medical digital twins that would enable personalised care: a virtual version of us that could guide more accurate diagnoses and treatment decisions, opening the door to a ‘new era of personalised medicine’ (Coveney and Highfield 2023).

The prevalent expectation within In Silico medicine, a multi‐disciplinary field of science which includes disciplines such as computer science, mathematics, biomedical and biomechanical engineering, is that the successes of digital twins in other application areas can be replicated in medicine. An outspoken aspiration of the In Silico field is to holistically model the human body as “an integrated multiscale, multi‐time and multi‐discipline representation of quantitative human physiology and pathology” (EDITH consortium et al. 2025, 26). Rhetoric highlighting the revolutionary potential of digital twins often portrays them as a key and scalable technique for enabling a new kind of data‐driven controllability in medical practice (Green and Vogt 2016). However, for the time being, the modelling of the entire human body remains more of a vision than a reality. For now, most efforts focus on single‐organ digital twins, such as those of the heart or liver or on digital twins of specific cells, processes or diseases and combinations thereof. Of these smaller‐scale digital twins, only a handful have made it to the market and into medical use (Lesage et al. 2023).

There is also still no consensus on the exact definition of digital twins in medicine, with a variety of terms being used interchangeably, including ‘virtual patient’, ‘virtual twin’, ‘digital patient’, ‘digital avatar’ and others (Fuller et al. 2020). Digital twins in medicine differ from those in other sectors that use the same term, as the maturity level of the models in healthcare is lower. For instance, real‐time data loops (e.g., via sensors) and continuous updates remain mostly absent in medicine, although there is interest in developing and experimenting with them. To make the idea of a digital twin more concrete, one can consider the case of a single‐organ cardiovascular model, which is often treated as an aspirational case for the field. FEops HEARTguide 1 is a procedure planning tool designed to generate predictive insights into how an implanted device may interact with a patient's individual anatomy. It uses CT scans as input data to construct a digital copy of the heart and incorporates not only the geometry of the relevant cardiac structures, but also information about tissue characteristics. Surgeons can then run virtual tests on the digital twin, experimenting with different sizes and placement positions. This enables ‘what if’ scenarios that can help anticipate different possible outcomes (e.g., will the chosen device fit properly or might there be a leak). This means that digital twins are not mere representations, but they create a space to experiment with different possible futures before acting in the physical world (Emmert‐Streib 2023). Such models are what the field wants to amplify into a holistic digital twin of the whole human body.

With the current buzz around digital twins, within and beyond medicine, they are starting to receive more scholarly attention (see Borbach et al. 2025 for an overview). However, many studies from within the social sciences that focus on digital twins in medicine have been studying them in a theory‐driven and abstract manner, often presenting broad and anticipatory socio‐ethical assessments based on possible future capabilities of digital twins and drawing on the implications of related technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI). Still, this literature plays an important role as it initiated dialogues within the social sciences on digital twins and provides a foundation we build on. This tendency is also due to how digital twins remain difficult to access and study empirically. As we mentioned earlier, only a handful have made it to the market, limiting opportunities for researchers to observe them in clinical practice.

We seek to enter into dialogue with the (growing) literature within the social studies of digital twins by presenting an empirically grounded account that brings into view the actors and practices behind digital twin technologies in medicine. This allows us to depart from the techno‐centricity and abstract nature of existing debates and make visible the human labour and effort behind this emerging technology (Pink et al. 2022).

We ask the following research question: How do actors in the In Silico field envision the future of medicine and its execution? In doing so, this paper aims to demystify digital twins by analysing the visions and practices of actors in In Silico medicine, rather than speculating about the potential effects of medical digital twins. Our analysis draws on multi‐sited fieldwork among In Silico professionals, the so‐called in silico medicine community, conducted over 3 years and across multiple European countries. It uses the notion of distance as an analytical lens, which emerged from the empirical material as actors are grappling with it. Distance, here, is understood as “the extent or amount of space between any two things” (Cambridge Dictionary, n.d.). Although distance can refer in part to spatial arrangements (e.g., engineers located at a distance from the clinical setting), we use this term more broadly to signal forms of epistemic and relational proximity or distance and (dis)connect. This falls in line with a Science and Technology Studies (STS) and Actor‐Network Theory (ANT) approach, as we treat distance as something relational that can be followed through networks and connections. Distance (or proximity) is not approached as a simple dichotomy; something can be near or far in different respects and from different perspectives, as will be illustrated empirically later on. More broadly, distance, here, provides the lens through which we examine how processes of translation unfold within innovation. Looking within the distance allows us to observe how a scientific community manoeuvres and responds to translational challenges.

We discuss how the observed digital twins are developed at a distance from the very field they aim to revolutionise and how the In Silico community we studied seeks to navigate this distance through the practices of training and conversion, their efforts fuelled by techno‐solutionism. Through our analysis, it becomes visible that distance not only constrains, but it also enables certain forms of engagement, which can be productive for the field. In addition, although digital twins are designed technically to open possibilities, we also observe a certain narrowing of medicine, bodies and clinicians. We offer an empirical and situated account of the ambivalences that lie in the distance as well as to what kinds of futures this field promotes and from where. This, we argue, requires reflexivity. It is of sociological interest to explore how speculative futures and emerging technologies are shaped through the values, aims and aspirations of involved actors. After all, digital twins are not ready‐made technologies, but complex socio‐technical systems that develop over time and require ongoing efforts from those who are co‐implicated with them.

The article proceeds as follows: we begin by contextualising digital twins in medicine within broader debates of digital health and position our paper in relation to existing research on digital twins. Next, our approach and methodological choices are presented. Moving on to the analysis, we first trace the In Silico field's visions and what they contain. From there, we dissect what lies within the distance and how it is navigated. We, then, reflect on how distance not only constrains but also enables and why this matters. We conclude with reflections and recommendations.

Situating Digital Twins and Our Approach

In the last decades, digital health technologies and the preventive, predictive and personalised care they supposedly enable have been positioned as the solution to the grand challenges facing contemporary healthcare systems, such as the ageing population, workforce shortages, rising healthcare costs and the soaring burden of chronic disease. An impressive body of social science research has examined the expansion of digital health and its social importance, showing that these technologies are not merely technical tools but are embedded in broader sociotechnical arrangements (Marent and Henwood 2023; see also Mayer‐Schönberger and Cukier 2013; Ruckenstein and Schüll 2017). Studies have examined the emergence of digital health technologies across a wide range of empirical settings. They have focused, e.g., on market and policy implementation, the evolution of public values within these arrangements, patient engagement and changes in clinical practice and have highlighted from a socio‐technical perspective, the challenges of translating these innovations into everyday use (e.g., Hoeyer 2023; Hogle 2016; Petersen 2019; Geiger and Gross 2017; Lupton 2016; Marent and Henwood 2021; Lievevrouw et al. 2024; Engel 2020). From an STS perspective, which we situate ourselves in, such translational challenges are not simply understood as technical issues of implementation, but as socially constituted processes through which digital health technologies are reshaped and made workable in specific contexts (Faulkner 2008; Aarden 2016; Engel et al. 2014).

Digital twins have recently emerged within this context as yet another transformative technology, extending previous predictive logics through the promise of personalised simulations of health and disease (see Braun 2025; Green and Vogt 2016). As Amoore (2025) points out, unlike earlier data‐driven approaches that draw primarily on past data, digital twins combine “data of multiple selves and others, human and machinic, real and synthetic” to anticipate how an individual body might respond. Thus, instead of merely estimating the likelihood of an outcome, this form of prediction makes it possible to model several potential responses to an intervention, allowing different results to be studied and visualised virtually (Emmert‐Streib 2023).

Digital twins are increasingly receiving scholarly attention from the social sciences, as reflected in the recent publication of a special issue (see Borbach et al. 2025) and a collection in AI&Society “Digital Human Twins – our future data selves”, which offer both reflective and empirical insights into what lies behind the buzzword and hype. However, digital twins in the field of medicine remain considerably under‐examined from a social science perspective. This is likely partially due to the current state of digital twin medical innovations as only few have made it to the market and implementation in clinical settings remains low (Lesage et al. 2023). Existing research has largely taken the form of socio‐ethical assessments that map potential benefits and risks in an anticipatory manner (e.g., Bruynseels et al. 2018; Popa et al. 2021; Braun 2021; Braun and Krutzinna 2022). These socio‐ethical assessments also tend to be formulated based on the implications of related technologies such as AI (see Iqbal et al. 2025). Although digital twins in medicine can utilise machine learning and other AI techniques, bundling digital twins and AI together runs a risk of creating a sort of “AI blur” overlooking the specific qualities of digital twins and weakening the analysis of their (potential) consequences. The In Silico community itself has published a white paper describing the role of AI within In Silico medicine to clarify this matter in more detail. However, these boundaries tend to shift. Sometimes strong efforts are made to demarcate digital twins from AI and other times, it is deemed beneficial to latch on the AI hype. This heightens the importance to tread carefully around this topic.

A small body of work has attended to the specific socio‐technical features of digital twin technologies in medicine, raising concerns about the relationship between digital twins and the people they attempt to replicate. For example, Braun (2021) shows how digital twins represent novel forms of representation that act and stand in on behalf of people's bodies and health and in doing so raises ethical questions around who controls these representations and how they are used. Korenhof et al. (2021) complement this by arguing that digital twins are not merely representations but also help steer and shape the very things they represent. Thus, their analysis highlights the assumptions and politics that become embedded in the design of digital twins (Korenhof et al. 2021; see also Amoore 2025). Korenhof et al. (2023) investigate the data required to build digital twins and show how certain choices influence reliability, representation, and what is left out. Taken together, the existing literature provides a useful backbone for making sense of digital twins in medicine in their early stages. Yet there is a lack of empirically grounded studies of the practices through which digital twins in medicine are enacted. Little attention has been paid to the actors and networks developing the technology, which has a long tradition in STS (Latour 2005; Callon 1984; Law 1992).

In conversations on algorithmic systems and as a response to techno‐centricity, scholars have also highlighted the need to look at the people who bring technologies into being. Seaver (2019), for example, describes algorithmic systems as being “dynamic arrangements of people and code” (p.419) reminding us how socio‐cultural effects do not just come from the technology itself. After all, humans are essential for algorithmic functioning. This aligns with Ruckenstein's (2023) call to re‐humanise technologies by focusing, within our empirical enquiries, on the human involvement: where they are, what they do and what influences they exert—seeing them as “critical and creative agents” (p.1241). We follow these calls in our approach to digital twins. Through its unique access to the In Silico medicine community leading the progress of digital twins in health, this article seeks to demystify digital twins and encourage more nuanced conversations that avoid both hype and fear debates. Instead of beginning with anticipatory questions about how digital twins might transform medicine, we focus on how these technologies are talked about and enacted by the people developing them. In doing so, we can support grounded sociological reflections.

To this end, we attend to the visions of the field. The analytical fruitfulness of looking at how techno‐scientific futures are described and negotiated in the present has long been pointed out by scholars, including within the sociology of expectations and STS. There is a wide range of studies on expectations (Brown and Michael 2003; Borup et al. 2006), (socio‐technical) imaginaries (Jasanoff and Kim 2019; see Hendriks et al. 2025 for an overview) promises (Van 2000), hype (Pollock and Williams 2026) and visions (Hilgartner 2015) which show how such future‐oriented narratives can act as self‐fulfilling prophecies, pointing to their generative and preformative qualities (Beckert 2013). As Joly (2021) puts it, speaking about the power of socio‐technical promises and how they constrain the imagination, “the future colonises the present” (own translation, originally in French). In scholarly debates, these concepts have been used to make visible the “gap between what is and what might, could or even should be” (Taussig et al. 2013, 5). More specifically, these studies have brought forward how promissory narratives can be used to mobilise funding, attract attention, orient (policy) agendas and are also used to legitimise emerging fields and create markets.

Therefore, these future‐oriented narratives put forward by the people behind the technology merit detailed attention as they are crucial to understand the ongoing developments. Following Hilgartner (2015), we approach visions as situated and not fully settled projections, which relate to and feed into broader socio‐technical imaginaries. The visions discussed in this paper can thus be understood as part of a broader socio‐technical imaginary of data‐driven medicine and technological progress (for details on the distinctions between visions and imaginaries, see Hendriks et al. 2025). In this way, attending to the visions of the In Silico community allows us to explore how the future of medicine is imagined by the In Silico community and how they are executed and made action‐able in the present. In other words, it is not only the visions themselves that are interesting to examine, but also the ways in which they are translated into the here and now.

Methods

This article is based on extensive, multi‐sited fieldwork conducted between 2022 and 2025 across Belgium, Italy, the Netherlands, Germany, France and Sweden. Our methodological approach combines participant observation (in engineering labs, seminars, workshops and events) with document analysis. Taken together, these methods enable us to address our research question.

The actors behind digital twins are not a coherent whole, but fragmented formations. One such cluster, a tightly connected network we call the “close‐knit community”, is composed of researchers, policy advocates and institutions who explicitly identify with terms such as “digital twin” and “In Silico medicine” and who position themselves as steering this movement. Most of them are based in or closely affiliated with academic and research institutions. They actively work to define their boundaries, are part of the scientific community shaping the story of digital twins as it currently circulates, and participate in lobbying efforts through actors such as the Virtual Physiological Human Institute (VPHI) and the Avicenna Alliance, nonprofit organisations dedicated to the promotion of In Silico and predictive medicine. Alongside this group are researchers and institutions that do not necessarily adopt the terminology of “digital twins” but are developing related hybrid models. They do not consistently attend the same events, participate in the same networks or contribute to the same position papers, yet they remain part of the broader making of digital twins in healthcare.

In this article, we focus specifically on the “close‐knit community” of In Silico actors, which we first had the opportunity to study from within the Horizon 2020‐funded In Silico World project (2021–2024), in which two of the authors (EE & IVH) were involved. Our involvement in this project gave us access to meetings, project discussions and internal events that we otherwise would not have been able to attend. Moreover, it created opportunities for further engagement with the field. Being involved in this project was our entry point into the In Silico medicine community. From there, the different ‘rooms’ (Calvert 2024) where knowledge is negotiated became accessible to us, both as observers and as active participants. This allowed us to study the field both from within and from the outside, a dual position that came with access to an emerging field but also posed difficulties, as we have discussed elsewhere (Elhadj et al. 2024; Elhadj 2025).

As part of this project, we organised three focus groups as well as one workshop with the project consortium. Although the results of these activities are not included in this article, they have fed into our broader understanding of digital twins. This work was further developed with colleagues through several events, such as a workshop on trust in In Silico healthcare technologies organised in Belgium in October 2024, where some of the quotes and the prologue presented in this article were collected.

In addition to the workshops where we were in a more active role, we followed the field and its actors through different events, such as conferences, technical workshops, seminars, policy events and webinars. Our analysis is also informed by informal conversations, such as those held over coffee in these events. Such data (solely present in Section 5) is relevant as the informal setting, away from hierarchies and power dynamics, encouraged junior scholars to speak more openly about their field. The fieldnotes we collected within this frame included notes on presentations, interactions, informal exchanges, as well as contextual details (e.g., physical setting), nonverbal behaviour and reflexive notes.

To supplement these data, we examined documents published by the VPHI, focusing in particular on its news archive from September 2011 to January 2025. Out of more than 1600 news items, 410 were selected as relevant to this research and thematically analysed. Selection was carried out in a structured manner using an Excel file; each news item was reviewed and those relating to specific themes connected to the development and uptake of digital twins were selected, inductively coded and analysed (Charmaz 2006). These materials helped us gain a better understanding of the promises of digital twins. We also analysed a wide range of publicly available documents, including white papers, position papers, mission statements, websites and other promotional material such as videos and podcasts.

The analysis was carried out in an iterative and collective manner. We first ordered and coded thematically our fieldnotes, collected documents and workshop transcripts and conducted situational mapping exercises (Clarke 2003), which helped trace recurring actors, themes and tensions.

From these early exercises, the theme of a persistent gap (spatial, relational and epistemic) between In Silico actors and the clinical setting systematically began to stand out. It was, through the repeated emergence of distance from our data, that we decided to make use of the notion of distance as an analytical lens, inspired by STS/ANT approaches where the line of inquiry is guided by the methodological principle of “following the actors” and their networks (Latour 2005). Writing itself became part of this analytical process as we collectively refined our analysis and tested how distance (in all its forms) could be used to frame our observations.

As with all participant observation, our account is partial and situated. Although we refer to a European context, our fieldwork was limited to a handful of Western and Northern European countries, leaving other regions where such models are also being developed – outside our scope. Consequently, our focus on the dominant, self‐identified actors limits our ability to capture the range of visions and practices that exist beyond it. Still, the “close‐knit community” we analyse is particularly relevant, having played a central role in shaping the field's broader direction.

Findings

The Visions: Medicine as Engineering

To ground our analysis, this section first examines the visions guiding the field of In Silico medicine. Within the setting studied, digital twins in medicine are predominantly described as inevitable, disruptive technologies that will revolutionise healthcare on a global scale—following the prominent scalable and universalist logics of emerging technologies (Pfotenhauer et al. 2022). This view is rarely, if ever, problematised in the In Silico community's public discourse. Instead, outreach materials convey inevitability: “the future of medicine is in silico 2 ” and “in silico medicine: not a solution, but a healthcare revolution 3 ”. Such slogans, abundant in project discussions, documents and promotional materials, are often reinforced by aspirational cases and analogies to other sectors. After all, if it works for rockets and aeroplanes, why would it not work for human bodies?

Today around 80% of an aircraft is designed only through computers. In many other industries and scientific areas like climate prediction, oil, atomic energy, automotive, computer modelling and simulation have been successfully applied for decades. Why not healthcare too?

(Chief Technology & Scientific Officer of a spin‐off, The Digital Twin Theory, VPHI Podcast, December 2024)

Given the omnipresence of disruption narratives being far from unique to digital twins (see Geiger 2020), this section focuses on the specific and most prevalent elements that are desired to be disrupted to make digital twins happen. It is within these spaces that the In Silico community carves out room for its technologies and expertise. They first need to highlight a problem of some sort in order to make themselves necessary, a process Callon (1984) calls problematisation.

To Cure Is to Predict

The broader visions of technological inevitability are circulated alongside more specific claims and ideas about clinical practice. Across talks and texts, clinicians are portrayed as being in need of decision‐making support and better predictive tools. A promotional video 4 from one of the early In Silico medicine projects, DISCIPLUS, illustrates the growing pressure on clinicians through a vivid visual metaphor. Faced with more than 800,000 new medical publications each year across hundreds of journals, the clinician's brain is shown struggling to process the overflow of information before eventually exploding. The video then elaborates on CM&S as a solution to this information overload. Digital twins are envisioned as tools that integrate up‐to‐date information from different parts of the body, helping clinicians stay informed about the latest medical insights and view the patient as a whole rather than a collection of fragmented parts. In this way, the video suggests that medicine has become so complex that clinical expertise alone is insufficient and that digital models are needed to build a clear‐er picture from large amounts of data and support decision‐making (see also Van Sint Jan and Geris 2019). This notion of “information overload” and its surrounding narratives appeared in several presentations by the In Silico community that we attended. Paradoxically, however, the claim that clinicians must make decisions without the necessary information is also mobilised to justify digital twins: they are thus envisioned to tackle both information overload and underload, providing parameters and predictions that could support more informed decision‐making.

This focus on predictions is central to the In Silico community's visions and it puts forward a particular understanding of clinical practice. Predictions are seen as a core aspect of clinicians' everyday work, not as something peripheral. The goal, hence, is to support clinicians through models. Here, medicine is cast as a predictive practice, or as one leading figure tends to invoke “to cure is to predict”. Digital twins are positioned as an extension of this logic, promising more informed, ‘objective’ and subject‐specific predictions. This is also intensified in the way that the subjectivity of clinicians is problematised. Digital twins are proclaimed as tools to reduce human fallibility and error and described, for instance, as “useful in reducing the noise and bias of human decision‐making” (unpublished session notes, January 2024). As one cardiologist and In Silico advocate suggested during a workshop, clinicians may become “less flawed and less subjective, more machine‐like” (cardiologist, October 2024). Digital twins, even at this stage, carry forward particular assumptions about what needs fixing. Medicine, in this context, seems to become narrowed into a practice of prediction, within which a space is carved out where the In Silico community's expertise and computational knowledge appear as justified and legitimate.

Making Everything Optimisable

Where the In Silico community positions digital twins in medicine is one question; how the human body is imagined within these visions is another. What quickly becomes noticeable is how the body emerges as something optimisable and codable, much like any other engineered system, as we saw earlier in the aeroplane example. This blurring between mechanical systems and the body creates the path through which digital expertise becomes the entry ticket to all kinds of spheres (Sharon 2021).

Soon a virtual copy of your body will help clinicians predict or it will even prevent us from getting sick at all.

(VPHI, Code & Cure Video, “How Computers are Changing Healthcare”, February 2024)

The imagining of the human body as optimisable and codable is not unique to digital twin technologies (Pols et al. 2019). In these modelling efforts, the everyday and human sensibilities are not treated evenly; instead, the focus remains on whichever aspects of health is solvable computationally (Amoore 2023). Selections are made that influence which layers of representations of the human form are prioritised, bringing to the surface questions about the embedded politics. During a workshop where the first author of this article presented on the limits of holistic modelling, one of the senior modellers used an interesting term to designate (and demarcate) what cannot be included as parameters, as things that are “out of skin”. This provided an interesting example of boundary‐work (Gieryn 1983), making the selectivities visible in terms of what gets included in a model and raising the question of what is left to fall “out of skin”?

With digital twins specifically, it is not just about how the human body is modelled, but also about the relationship between the individual and its digital counterpart. The European Commission (EC), for example, has a page dedicated to Virtual Human Twins, describing them as:

A virtual version of you will be used to test treatments, like a crash test dummy, guinea pig and trial volunteer all rolled into one. Your digital doppelgänger could breathe, blister and bleed. It could be dissected, probed and explored in unprecedented detail, helping to work out the treatments that work best for you

(extract from the EC's page “Shaping Europe's Digital Future”)

Here, it is not only the boundaries of what gets included that are stake, but also the boundaries between human and machines becoming blurred, further carving space for computational modelling expertise.

Constraining Possibilities

In many ways, the promises embedded in these visions echo those associated with other technologies. The tension we think is worth attending to here is between the technical features of the technology itself and the visions presented above. The specificity of digital twins is that it enables alternatives through its virtual interventions, offering a glimpse into what might happen—‘what if’ scenarios. Returning to our earlier example, we saw how a digital twin of a patient's heart does not produce a single prediction. Through “virtual interventions” it can be explored how, if the stent is placed at x angle, y could happen. This means that the specificity of digital twins, including our example, lies in its supposed ability to explore different scenarios computationally before acting on the patient. This can be interpreted as a sort of opening of futures and possibilities rather than a foreclosure. Yet, within the visions, medicine is framed in a narrow (and technical) way, flattening the 'messiness' and multiplicity of clinical practice (Mol 2002). The talks and texts of the In Silico community do not showcase this openness when carving out space for their expertise and tools: medicine is narrowed into a matter of prediction; the human body is viewed from a machinic lens and clinicians are narrowed into subjective experts. In other words, although digital twins in their nature technically inhabit elements of opening futures, in practice and even in this anticipatory state, it carries dynamics of narrowing. The very quality that makes digital twins so particular is in friction with the visions put forward by the In Silico community.

Looking Within the Distance

In the first section, we examined the contents of the visions and the ideas and priorities embedded within them. But these visions cannot remain static; they must travel (Pfotenhauer and Jasanoff 2017) beyond the In Silico community to be taken up in the clinical setting. In this section, we turn to where these visions travel from, what lies within the distance between modellers and clinical settings and how that distance is manoeuvred. Here, distance is not presented as something to be overcome, but rather as a space where specific dynamics, practices and effects appear and is therefore of analytical value.

Relational and Epistemic Gaps

Our interest in distance and from where the In Silico community imagines the future of medicine started during a fieldwork instance in 2023, when the first author had the opportunity to visit a research group working on digital twins based in a local hospital in Sweden. At first glance, there appeared to be at least geographical proximity, as the team members and clinicians entered through the same doors. However, to reach their lab, one must take several turns through quiet corridors, basements, an elevator ride and another hallway. After navigating what feels almost like a maze, their office space seemed distant from the clinical setting. Such a physical arrangement raises questions about what their day‐to‐day collaboration with clinicians looks like. A member of the research group shed light on this during a tour of the lab:

You know, we work on models for months, sometimes years. And then we take them to the clinicians, all excited and they just say, ‘Well, we can’t use this.’ Engineers are really bad at this. We need to get better at thinking about the context of use and I really try.

(postdoctoral biomedical engineer, November 2023)

This example demonstrates, first, that despite the lab's spatial proximity to clinicians, given its location within the hospital, a certain distance persists, one that is epistemic and relational rather than geographical. During a workshop, one professor similarly recalled that even when their team was located next door to clinicians within the hospital, they still struggled to reach them. This points to different modalities of distance. Here, Latour (1996) reminds us of the importance of looking beyond the ‘tyranny of distance': what matters is not how close things are but how they are connected. Geographical proximity is only one mode of connection and often not the most consequential one. The second reason why the above quote is pertinent is how collaboration gets framed and bound to usefulness. When discussing collaboration such as in the conversation above and enquiring about physical and epistemic distance, conversations tended to lead organically towards whether models are useful in the clinical setting.

Another observation from our fieldwork illustrates this well. During an event in Belgium in February 2025, an established senior engineer gave a talk aimed at helping PhD and postdoctoral researchers to better understand how to collaborate with clinicians. He described a moment at an earlier conference that he felt revealed the gap between the fields. An engineer colleague proudly told clinicians in a conversation that they had developed a model capable of predicting how an aortic rupture would tear. However, this had not been met with the expected enthusiasm. The clinicians explained that despite being a great achievement, such a model is of little practical use since survival rates after a rupture are extremely low. What they need is to know before it tears: that's when such a model could make a real difference. In other words, although this biomechanical insight was impressive, it was not deemed clinically relevant. The presenter used this story to describe their own evolution from being—in their own words—“a naïve engineer” to focus on where modelling approaches could be most useful for clinicians.

It is noteworthy to mention that an important number of the modellers working on digital twins within our research rarely and for some, never, set foot in a clinical setting. This suggests that actors central to pushing visions of how medicine should look are, in practice, far removed from clinical practice. This has also been observed from within the field through focusing on the lack of collaboration with clinicians (Lesage et al. 2023). This may help explain why it is so difficult to figure out the precise usefulness of specific digital twins in advance. Usefulness, however, is not a single measure, nor should it be understood in a narrow way. Not all scientific work is meant to be useful in the clinical sense; for example some knowledge is pursued for experimentation to better understand a specific phenomenon. At the same time, what counts as useful for the clinical setting is not a given. Medicine is a complex field, consisting of numerous narrow specialities with distinct focuses and needs. Usefulness, then, functions as an indicator of distance: what is useful in one setting and moment might not be in another; therefore, usefulness is not something engineers can define in isolation or figure out from a distance, it is relational and situated. This is why usefulness becomes even more difficult to pin down across this distance.

A Solution With Demands and Through Conversion

In the visions of the In Silico professionals, digital twins are typically framed as support tools, with clinicians bearing ultimate responsibility. Yet, in their discourses, clinicians are also assigned an expanding list of responsibilities and tasks related to integrating digital twins. This is not a unique phenomenon, as scholars have observed similar tendencies in relation to a broad range of digital health technologies (Hoeyer 2023; Tanninen et al. 2025). Callon (1984) describes this as ‘interessement’ whereby actors are enrolled into specific roles and expectations. In this case, as in many other instances of digital health, clinicians are assigned a variety of tasks, including data collectors for digital twins:

The clinicians need to help us get data. We need data, we need them to get educated and let them understand the benefit and teach them that we are collaborators.

(nonprofit representative, annual event, October 2023)

In their ideal world, the daily tasks of clinicians are reoriented around the needs of the digital twin. The assigned tasks are preconditions for the technology to work: clinicians must first assist and contribute by producing the necessary data, despite uncertainties about whether the tool will deliver on its promises. Clinicians are cast as essential enablers, tasked with integrating an additional experimental layer of work into their already demanding workflows.

But how would this work in practice? According to many voices in the In Silico community, clinicians need training. This expectation was echoed across the settings we observed and was repeatedly framed as a self‐evident requirement: “Digital tools are a challenge for clinicians, I think that doctors need to be trained to deal with these tools” (engineer, project meeting, January 2024). Through training, there is the hope of increasing trust and making clinicians ‘less resistant to innovation’. This simulated or forced proximity is presented as a way to navigate the distance, falling into a classic deficit‐model logic (Wynne 1993). Training in this context is more than just a transfer of knowledge. It can be seen as a script for how their collaboration should work and who is expected to do the adapting.

The clinical setting becomes an obligatory passage point (Callon 1984) through which future visions must pass through. What also appears as a proposed solution to these tensions (and navigate the distance) is a push to ‘adjust’ or ‘convert' the clinicians. A handful of clinicians are present in the In Silico medicine community, but it is a small and familiar group. They have often been involved in the field for quite some time and tend to be cardiologists. After more than 3 years of fieldwork, the same names and faces tend to appear across digital twin events—present to ‘tick the box' of clinician involvement. This lack of variety of involved clinicians has not gone unnoticed by the field and they have been conducting surveys with larger samples of clinicians exploring ways “to recruit them”. Those who are already on board are often asked to give presentations to share insights on what clinicians want, becoming sorts of spokespersons for their professions. At one such event, the moderator referred to a clinician as “one of the converted ones”. The seemingly minor remark points to a wider logic at play: a division between those who have accepted the promise of digital twins and those who have not (yet) been converted.

It also sometimes sounds as if it is simply a matter of pitching convincingly, as the following quote illustrates:

We need to learn how to communicate with the doctors, we need to be simple, not saying they are stupid, but we need to communicate in a few minutes which gets them excited and builds their trust.

(nonprofit representative, conference, November 2023)

This ties into what we have observed here on a broader scale: a narrative of conversion rather than collaboration, where the slow pace of uptake becomes a problem to be solved through persuasion. However, this work of persuasion does not close the distance, or what falls within it; instead, it reveals the gap between the visions and promises of the field and the realities and practices of the clinical setting. Also, attention tends to settle on how the barrier might be lowered, without considerable questioning about what its very presence might imply.

What Distance Enables

Earlier, we established our analytical interest: not whether distance is good or bad, or how it should be managed, but what it does. In the previous section, distance appeared as somewhat of a constraint, which the field desires to overcome through training and conversion. Yet, across our material, we also noticed and started to reflect on how distance enables certain forms of engagement. It has a generative quality to it, which sustains this future‐making from afar.

In the prologue, we introduced readers to a sketch in which a hot air balloon (the future of medicine) was floating away, whereas a clinician was trying to keep up. It could be described as a bold vision, which can bring up a wide array of questions (and even critique), but it is also a moment that invites us to think about how this boldness is fuelled by distance. Would these modellers have such bold visions if they worked in close proximity (in all its dimensions) to the medical setting? Would they still reflect on how to train clinicians and have them do the readjusting? Or would they calibrate their visions and maybe fall into the domestication of critique? After all, it is much more difficult to critique a field to which one is closely positioned to.

Not being in the same room as clinicians also enables the In Silico community to imagine alternatives. As modellers are not embedded within clinical cultures and workflows, they bring different disciplinary perspectives (e.g., biomechanical, computational, mathematical) to the table, offering a way out of siloed thinking (Smye and Frangi 2021; Cummins et al. 2018). During a presentation, one biomechanical engineer reflected on what they considered a productive difference: “what they (clinicians) find interesting is not what an engineer finds interesting”.

Distance also allows expectations and hype about a data‐driven future of medicine in which digital twins play an important role to circulate without being substantially tested. This dynamic is visible in how the In Silico community has maintained its narrative that digital twins will revolutionise healthcare for nearly two decades, despite only a few digital twins reaching the market and being able to revive the hype time and (time) again. The hot air balloon keeps floating. The promises and visions may even seem to become bolder, as there is a revived interest in building a holistic virtual human twin – adding a fresh layer of promises. The future remains promising because it does not yet have to work in practice (Hoeyer 2023).

Distance then enables what we would consider both productive and limiting elements, which equally deserve reflexivity. But its costs, including material, emotional and social, should not be brushed aside, especially considering on whom the burden of adjustment tends to fall with digital health technologies and the politics of future‐making. Throughout our fieldwork, it was consistently clinicians who were expected to adapt to make the technology work in practice. Why is it that clinical practices are being asked to bend and accommodate? Why not the reverse? And what about the patients?

Concluding Remarks

Digital Twins. Visions. Execution. Distance. This article traced the visions of a specific In Silico medicine community and their execution, making visible how distance and proximity and opening and closing can co‐exist. We first saw how the openness of the technical features of digital twins themselves is at odds with the visions that narrow medicine to a matter of prediction and frame the human body and clinicians as optimisable. We then turned our attention to what lies within the distance, not only looking at where gaps and misalignments become visible, but also how the field tries to navigate this distance through training and conversion, putting the adjusting responsibilities on clinicians. Through dissecting distance, we made visible where the frictions and ambivalences lay. This allowed us to not only trace what might get constrained, but also what gets enabled.

What makes this contribution to the social studies of digital twins important is that it draws on our unique access to the field, offering a glimpse into a specific community behind the technology. We attend to their own talks and texts and through that move away from abstract and mystifying debates that overlook their specificity or overstate their novelty—a trap that existing literature on digital twins in medicine has not always managed to avoid. Some of it latches onto technological determinism and hype itself, by, for example, questioning if clinicians will remain the epistemic authority once digital twins are implemented (see, e.g., Iqbal et al. 2025). The current literature is, of course, valuable in providing insights into the potential benefits of the technology and how it could be applied in medicine. Yet, we should be cautious not to succumb to compressed foresight (Williams 2006), and also reflect on how our own sociological accounts become entangled in the stories about technologies.

We deem it important that reflexivity and attention are paid to the present practices that might be carried into the future (Pink et al. 2023) and who and what the In Silico community mobilises to make their ‘hoped‐for future’ happen. By doing so, we begin to see what digital twins are constructed to be and from where they are imagined, rather than what they ought to be. This empirical contribution offers a modest beginning that suggests where further attention and digging might be needed if we wish to explore the social lives of digital twins in medicine. This might be an especially productive approach for this “in the meantime” phase in which we find ourselves in (Durham and Masquelier 2023).

By bringing the community behind the technology into view and its future‐making practices, this article also raises broader questions about whether the future of medicine should be imagined from afar. And how far is too far? After all, distance is a space between worlds that still see each other, isolation is not. There is, however, hope. The In Silico community acknowledges this distance. This is already a crucial first step. However, instead of viewing slow uptake and frictions as hurdles to be overcome, we encourage the community to learn from its successful cases. Hence, what digital twins need, we suggest, is not visions that further romanticise disruption, but a focus on what works, why it works and for whom, while recognising that the most clinically useful models may not be the most advanced, but the most adjusted to their surroundings—bringing different forms of strength together.
