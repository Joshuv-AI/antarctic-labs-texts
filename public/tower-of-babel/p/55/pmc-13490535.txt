Direct Amidation of Alkynes with Nitroarenes via Heterogeneous Semiconductor TiO₂ Nanotubes
Authors: Kashif Hussain, Fukai Xie, Hui Hui luo, Wen Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmides, which are crucial in pharmaceuticals and materials science, are traditionally synthesized via unsustainable condensation reactions. While direct amidation has long been pursued as an atom-economical route, it still relies on toxic reagents and harsh conditions. This work presents a light-driven photocatalytic process that explores a rarely investigated transformation of alkynes and nitroarenes, enabling amide synthesis with excellent yields (up to 95%) using heterogeneous TiO₂ nanotubes. This sustainable approach enables C–N bond formation with a wide range of alkynes and diverse nitroarenes under mild reaction conditions. This method is suitable for the direct synthesis of pharmaceutically relevant amides and offers broad applicability for the late-stage modification of bioactive molecules. A tentative coupling mechanism is proposed based on controlled, mechanistic experiments. Overall, the combination of operational simplicity, ambient-air compatibility, and catalyst recyclability positions this method as a promising tool for green, practical synthesis in pharmaceutical and chemical applications.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Photocatalysis, Catalytic mechanisms

Introduction

Amidation is fundamental to chemistry and life science1–4. Especially in the pharmaceutical field, more than 60% of the top-ranked small-molecule drugs are amide derivatives5,6. The conventional approach to the amide bond is to condense an amine with an activated carboxylic acid derivative; the tedious precursor preparation steps result in low atom economy, and the use of toxic reagents limits their widespread application (Fig. 1a). The landscape of amide synthesis has been continuously expanded over time, and new methods in this field can be broadly categorized into two classes based on their reaction partners, as summarized by Pattabiraman and Bode2. The first class uses amines as key reactants that undergo acylation via catalytic or oxidative pathways. The second class highlights innovative reactant combinations that generate amides via mechanistically unique routes. In the first class, our group established a series of methodologies for the formation of amides from alkylarenes7, alcohols8–10, organosulfur compounds11, and ester12 as precursors of the carbonyl moiety (Fig. 1b). Notably, most of these transformations were achieved in a sustainable, green manner using recyclable heterogeneous catalytic systems. However, the pressurized oxygen atmospheres and, in some cases, high reaction temperatures in such scenarios have consistently motivated us to explore novel amidation schemes as the second class. In comparison with amines, nitro compounds, especially nitroarenes, are often regarded as more economical, accessible, and stable nitrogen sources13,14. Additionally, the anilines employed in amidation are commonly synthesized via metal- or acid-mediated reduction of nitroarenes15–18. Based on these facts, a host of N-aryl amidation protocols that directly leverage nitroarenes have emerged19–24. Nonetheless, intractable difficulties in catalyst recovery and dependence on excess metallic reductants pose major obstacles to widespread application. A newly reported heterogeneous thermal catalytic system forms amide but requires high H2 pressure and temperature25. Recently, owing to impressive advances in photoredox approaches, elegant photoinduced methodologies have been developed for the direct transformation of nitroarenes into amides, employing soluble transition-metal complexes or organic small molecules as catalysts19,26–29. Although these homogeneous photocatalytic systems offer a milder approach to amidation, the persistent and inherent problems of catalyst recoverability and recyclability, the frequent need for additives, and the resulting high costs collectively pose significant barriers to large-scale implementation of these protocols20,27. From both sustainability and practicality perspectives, a novel photocatalytic paradigm for the direct amidation of nitroarenes is urgently needed (Fig. 1b).

Heterogeneous semiconductor photocatalysts, owing to their cutting-edge properties such as ease of separation, recyclability, photostability, and minimization of metal impurities in the final products, have become a powerful and promising tool in the arsenal of photoinduced organic synthesis. Photoexcited electron-hole pairs at the semiconductor surface can serve as an electron bridge, facilitating interfacial electron transfer between two substrates via oxidative and reductive pathways. However, catalytic efficiency is limited by the rapid recombination of these photoexcited charge carriers, and sacrificial reagents are added to trap either electrons or holes. As for the direct transformation of nitroarenes into amides, a suitable and complementary selection of reducing agents will fully utilize this electron bridge under mild conditions without sacrificing any reagents. Alkynes, owing to their unsaturated structures, easy accessibility, and diverse transformation possibilities, epitomize a pivotal feedstock in oxidative coupling chemistry. Moreover, single-electron oxidation of the carbon-carbon triple bond generates a highly reactive alkyne radical cation, offering additional synthetic opportunities30. Grounded in the knowledge, we envision that a hole oxidation of an alkyne, serving as the carbonyl precursor of an amide, could close the heterogeneous photoredox cycle for the direct amidation of a nitroarene. Accordingly, this transformation encounters some significant challenges: (1) the high oxidative potential of alkynes (> +2.0 V)31,32 requires a semiconductor with sufficiently high valence-band maxima (VBMs) to enable single-electron oxidation. (2) Selecting an appropriate heterogeneous photocatalyst should consider both its catalytic performance in this amidation and the redox potential of the substrates involved. (3) There are no previous studies on heterogeneously photocatalyzed direct amidation of nitroarene in an additive-free fashion.

TiO2 is one of the most-studied heterogeneous semiconductors for photocatalytic applications and has been widely used in hydrogen production, environmental remediation, and organic synthesis. Upon photoexcitation, electrons are excited to the conduction band (≈−0.65 V), leaving behind holes in the valence band (≈ +2.55 V)33,34. Conceptually, this electron-hole pair would bridge the redox between nitrobenzene (≈ −0.55 V) and alkyne without any additives. As part of our ongoing interest in developing mild, green, and sustainable synthetic methods using heterogeneous catalytic systems35, we intend to employ TiO2 as a photoredox catalyst to direct the formation of amides from nitroarenes and alkynes.

In this work, we report a heterogeneous TiO₂-based photocatalytic direct amidation of nitroarenes with alkynes under additive-free and mild conditions. This approach uses photoinduced electron–hole pairs on TiO₂ to couple the oxidative and reductive transformations of alkynes and nitroarenes, enabling the direct formation of amides without any sacrificial oxidative or reductive reagents.

Results and discussion

Catalyst characterization

Using established synthetic methods, we prepared TiO₂ nanorods, nanowires, nanoparticles, and nanotubes33,36–38. The direct amidation was carried out with each material, and the resulting products were characterized using multiple spectroscopic techniques. X-ray diffraction pattern confirmed that all TiO₂ nanomaterials exhibited similar diffraction peaks (Fig. 2a), which matched well with the reference data for TiO₂ crystals (JCPDS file #01-084-1286)39. The TiO₂ nanotubes displayed broader and diffuse peaks, indicating a mixed-phase crystalline and amorphous9, and the dominant facets, 101 and 004, were clearly observed, and no impurity peaks were detected. X- ray photoelectron spectroscopy confirmed the presence of Ti and O elements in all TiO₂ nanomaterials. The Ti 2p spectra displayed two characteristic peaks at approximately 464.5 and 459.8 eV, corresponding to Ti 2p₁/₂ and Ti 2p₃/₂, respectively40. A strong peak at around 530.8 eV in the O 1 s spectrum was attributed to Ti-O bond binding energy41, while a shoulder peak near 532.6 eV indicated the O-H bonds, probably due to moisture adsorption42 (Fig. 2b, c). The morphology of the synthesized TiO₂ nanomaterials was verified by TEM and SEM images (Fig. S1 and S2), which revealed that all TiO₂ nanostructures were well-distributed and uniformly dispersed. The HR-TEM image is shown in Fig. 2d. The specific surface area of each TiO₂ nanomaterial was evaluated using nitrogen (N₂) adsorption-desorption isotherms, which exhibited hysteresis loops consistent with porous structures. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas for TiO₂ nanorods, nanoparticles, nanotubes, and nanowires were measured to be 109.6, 220.9, 261.6, and 82.2 m²/g, respectively (Fig. 2e). We speculate that the synergistic effects of high surface area and mixed amorphous and crystalline phases significantly enhance the photocatalytic activity of TiO₂ nanotubes in this reaction43. Variations in surface area across the different TiO₂ morphologies are likely to influence their overall photocatalytic efficiency. The light absorption properties of all TiO₂ nanomaterials were analyzed. The UV-Vis absorption band edges were observed near 360 nm for all samples (Fig. 2f); however, slight variations in peak intensities were observed. These UV-Vis spectra were used to calculate the band gap energies (Eg) via Tauc plots (Fig. 2g)44. The calculated band gaps were 3.16 eV for TiO₂ nanoparticles, 3.19 eV for TiO₂ nanorods, 3.29 eV for TiO₂ nanotubes, and 3.32 eV for TiO₂ nanowires. The XPS valence-band spectrum of TiO₂ nanotubes showed a valence-band maximum of 2.60 eV (Fig. S3). Given the highly positive oxidation potential of alkynes (approximately +2.0 V) and the negative reduction potential of nitroarenes (approximately –0.55 V), TiO₂ was selected as a heterogeneous photocatalyst with these oxidation and reduction potentials. To evaluate the charge-separation performance of the photocatalysts, transient photocurrent response (I-T) measurements and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were performed. Among all tested morphologies, TiO₂ nanotubes exhibited the highest photogenerated charge-separation efficiency (Fig. 2h). Additionally, they showed a smaller arc radius on the Nyquist plot than other TiO₂ nanomaterials (Fig. 2i), indicating TiO₂ nanotubes exhibit superior charge-transfer properties45. The Mott–Schottky plots revealed that the flat-band potential of TiO2 nanomaterials varies only slightly, ranging from –0.69 V to –0.64 V (Fig. S4).

Reaction development and substrate scope

With the optimized reaction conditions in hand (See Supplementary Table 1 and Fig. S7 for detailed reaction optimization studies), we systematically investigated the substrate scope. Initially, we focused on terminal aryl alkynes for direct amidation (Fig. 3). A wide range of functional groups, including halogens, trifluoromethyl, methyl, methoxy, and alkyl-substituted aryl alkynes, afforded the corresponding amide products in good to excellent yields. A more detailed analysis of the structure–activity relationship reveals that the reaction yield is governed by a combination of electronic, positional, and steric effects. It was observed that the electronic effect of the substituents significantly influenced the reaction outcome. Aryl alkynes bearing electron-withdrawing groups such as fluoro, chloro, bromo, trifluoromethyl, and keto produced the desired products in moderate to excellent yields (3a-3l). The enhanced reactivity associated with electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs) is attributed to their ability to increase the electrophilicity of the alkyne-derived intermediate. In contrast, substrates containing electron-donating groups, including methoxy and alkyl moieties, gave products with satisfactory to good yields (3m-3v). The comparatively lower yields for electron-donating group (EDG)-substituted substrates likely arise from reduced electrophilicity of the reactive intermediate, thereby decelerating the key amide-bond-forming step. Furthermore, the position of the substituents also played a crucial role in determining the yield. For aryl alkynes bearing electron-withdrawing groups, meta-substitution generally yielded slightly higher yields than ortho- and para-substitution (3a-3l). Aryl alkynes bearing electron-donating groups at the meta-position exhibited marginally higher yields than their ortho- and para-substituted counterparts (3m-3u). Steric effects were also evident in this transformation. Bulky alkyl-substituted aryl alkynes afforded products in satisfactory to high yields (3r-3w), indicating that steric bulk at a distance from the reactive center does not severely impede the reaction. Additionally, aryl alkyne bearing disubstituted groups delivered the desired amides in acceptable to high yields (3v-3w), suggesting that the present catalytic system exhibits reasonable tolerance to moderate steric congestion. Also, the unsubstituted substrate was obtained in satisfactory yields (3x) (Fig. 3). Furthermore, this reaction system was compatible with heteroaromatic alkynes, such as pyridine and thiophene, and with fused aromatic rings, such as naphthalene, affording the corresponding products (3y-3ac) in satisfactory yields. The successful amidation of heteroaromatic substrates enhances the adaptability of this protocol for broader synthetic applications. Excitingly, beyond electronically activated aryl alkynes, inactivated aliphatic alkynes such as open-chain, alkyl-substituted, and even cyclic alkynes also participated effectively under the same conditions, yielding the desired products in acceptable yields (3ad-3ag) (Fig. 3). CO2 was detected and captured as a byproduct originating from the terminal carbon of the phenylacetylene substrate (Figs. S18 and S19)

The reaction of internal alkynes is typically more challenging due to steric hindrance, which reduces substrate accessibility. Remarkably, our current photocatalytic system also demonstrated effectiveness for both symmetrical and asymmetrical disubstituted internal alkynes. Symmetrical internal alkynes (4a–4d) were well tolerated and underwent efficient oxidative cleavage, providing the corresponding amides in moderate to high yields (45–71%). Furthermore, asymmetric internal alkynes also showed excellent performance, producing two different amide products in good to high yields (4e1, 4e2−4h1, 4h2), further highlighting the broad versatility of this protocol. Notably, this system enables the simultaneous generation of two amide products in a one-pot reaction, with aryl amides generally obtained in higher yields than their alkyl counterparts (Fig. 4). To further expand the reaction scope, we explored a range of nitroarene substrates. A series of substituted nitroaromatic compounds bearing different functional groups, such as halogen, alkyl cyano, methoxy, trifluoromethyl, and cyano, afforded the corresponding amide products in good yields (5a–5h). The reaction was also applicable to heteroaromatic nitroarene (5i) and disubstituted nitroarene (5j), demonstrating the practical potential of this method for commercial applications (Fig. 4).

Reaction scope

To evaluate the practicality of the reaction system and the photocatalyst, we conducted both a gram-scale reaction and recyclability tests. A scale-up reaction using 1.08 g of 1a and 1.14 g of 2a yielded 1.26 g of product 3a (74%) after filtration, solvent evaporation, and flash chromatography (Fig. 5a). The recyclability and stability of TiO₂ nanotubes were also examined through recycling experiments. After each run, the photocatalyst was recovered by centrifugation, washed with acetonitrile, ethanol, and water, and then dried under vacuum at 80 °C. The recovery rate exceeded 99.5% per cycle, with no observable changes. As shown in Fig. 5b, the photocatalyst was reused for at least five cycles without any significant decline in product yield. XRD patterns and TEM images after recycling showed negligible variation (Fig. S5), confirming the excellent structural stability and durability of the TiO₂ nanotubes in this photocatalytic reaction system. The positive outcome of the recycling tests led us to hypothesize that the recovered catalyst could be applicable in various direct amidation reactions. The TiO₂ nanotubes obtained from the previous gram-scale experiment demonstrated excellent performance in direct amidation reactions involving both mono- and disubstituted alkynes with nitroarenes (Figs. 5c, 3b and 4e1, 42). The isolated yields from these coupling reactions using the recycled catalyst were comparable to those obtained with the fresh catalyst in all instances.

To further demonstrate the effectiveness of this reaction system for late-stage transformations of naturally occurring or synthetic bioactive compounds (Fig. 5d), we investigated (i) alkyne derivatives of estrone (A1), triclosan (A2), and the commercially available (ii) nitro-containing antifungal drug flutamide. These substrates underwent amidation successfully, affording the corresponding amide products 6a (53%), 6b (76%), and 7a (58%). Moreover, to evaluate the practical applicability of the reaction system, we synthesized biologically active (iii) antifungal drugs Flutolanil (8a, 58%) and Mepronil (9b, 67%) in a single step (Fig. 5d). This step proved the feasibility of the reaction system for practical commercial use.

Mechanistic investigation

A series of systematic studies was conducted to shed light on the reaction mechanism46. UV–vis analysis identified TiO₂ nanotubes as the sole photoactive components, exhibiting a distinct absorption maximum near 360 nm in the reaction. Upon the addition of 1a and 2a, slight changes in the TiO₂ nanotubes UV-spectrum were observed, further supporting catalyst–substrate interaction under photocatalytic conditions (Fig. 6a). To investigate the specific role of TiO₂ nanotubes as a photoactive catalyst in the SET-driven radical pathway, we performed Stern–Volmer quenching experiments. The incremental addition of p-trifluoromethyl nitrobenzene (2a) efficiently quenched the photoluminescence, whereas variation in the concentration of substrate 1a caused no detectable quenching (Fig. 6b)47. The light-on/off experiments matched the control reaction and showed that no product 3a formed in the dark, directly demonstrating that the photocatalyst initiates the reaction only under illumination (Fig. 6c). A radical capture experiment using 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-oxyl (TEMPO) was performed, and the formation of a TEMPO adduct was detected via HR-MS, indicating the involvement of an acyl radical derived from alkynes (Fig. 6d–I and S10). Several scavengers were employed to inhibit the reaction to identify the reactive species generated during the photocatalytic process (Fig. 6d-ii). The addition of 1,4-benzoquinone, a scavenger for superoxide radicals (O₂⁻·), significantly suppressed the yield of product 3a to 11%, signifying the participation of O₂⁻·. Similarly, the addition of 2,6-di-tert-butyl-4-methylphenol (BHT) reduced the formation of 3a, supporting the role of carbon radical in the reaction. The addition of ammonium oxalate (OA) inhibited the reaction, indicating that photogenerated holes were involved in the oxidation of alkynes.

Conversely, the electron scavenger carbon tetrachloride decreased the yield, suggesting that photogenerated electrons also contribute to the reaction46.

These amalgamation results suggest that the direct amidation reaction proceeds via an O₂⁻· mediated pathway. Further confirmation was obtained by in situ electron spin paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) as a spin-trap agent. The characteristic peaks of the DMPO–O₂⁻ adduct were observed (Fig. 6e), strappingly confirming the presence of superoxide radicals. Overall, findings from the TEMPO experiment, radical-scavenger studies, and EPR spectroscopy indicate that both electron-hole pairs and O₂⁻· radical species play crucial roles in the direct amidation reaction. Furthermore, in the hot filtration test, the coupling reaction between 1a and 2a halted immediately upon removal of the TiO₂ nanotube after 15 h, and no further formation of 3a was detected over the subsequent 16 h. This observation demonstrates that catalysis proceeds exclusively via photogenerated electrons and holes at the photocatalyst surface (Fig. 6f). Moreover, the kinetic profile of the standard reaction between 1a and 2a showed rapid conversion, with no observable induction period (Fig. 6g)47,48.

HR-MS analysis of the reaction mixture revealed intermediates, including benzaldehyde, a nitrosoarene, a benzoin-type compound, trace amounts of aniline, and a carboxylic acid, along with the desired product (Figs. S10–15). To understand the role of these intermediates, they were tested under standard reaction conditions (Fig. 7a). Among all nitroarene analogs, only nitrosoarene gave a relatively high yield with 1a, while N-hydroxy aniline and aniline were completely inactive toward the amidation reaction (Fig. 7a-I). When benzaldehyde was used in combination with nitroarene or its analogs, only nitroarene and nitrosoarene afforded the desired product (Fig. 7a-II). Furthermore, in the system without direct use of the benzoin-type substrate (intermediate 2), the product yield (46.6%) decreases. This might be because the presence of alkynes can quench holes, thereby inhibiting the recombination of photogenerated carriers and efficiently separating electron–hole pairs, facilitating SET throughout the reaction. The same effect was observed when nitrosoarene was added instead of nitroarene. The results of control experiments strongly support the crucial roles of intermediates such as nitrosoarene, aldehyde, and N-hydroxy amide for the amidation reaction. The nitrosoarene and aldehyde, derived from nitroarene and alkynes, likely form an N-hydroxy amide through radical coupling. Another study revealed that photoexcited electrons from TiO₂ reduced nitroarenes to nitrosoarenes, which then reduced to anilines49. Concurrently, we observed that photoexcited holes in TiO₂ were involved in the oxidation of alkynes to benzaldehydes, which were subsequently converted to the corresponding carboxylic acids in the presence of O₂.

To further extend the mechanistic study, isotopic labeling experiments were performed using CD₃CN and D₂O. A trace of a minor deuterium-incorporation product was observed by HR-MS, suggesting the involvement of hydrogen transfer during the reaction (Fig. 7b-I and II, Figs. S16 and S17). The proposed detailed mechanism for the direct amidation reaction is anticipated based on previous literature28,30,50–52 and our experimental results (Fig. 7c). Initially, the excited TiO₂ nanotube photocatalyst generates electron-hole pairs. The valence band potential of TiO₂ nanotubes is substantiated to be capable of oxidizing alkynes31,34 into radical cation I. Simultaneously, the photogenerated electrons reduce molecular oxygen to superoxide radicals (O₂⁻·), and can also reduce nitroarenes to nitrosoarene 153. The O₂⁻·further reacts with water to produce hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) and hydroxide ions (OH⁻)30. The radical cation I is then attacked by ˙OH to generate cationic intermediate II, which subsequently couples with OH⁻ to yield benzoin intermediate 230. Consequently, the intermediate 2 undergoes α-scission to produce the corresponding aldehyde 350,51. Photocatalytic holes further oxidize the aldehyde 3 to form an acyl radical III, which then reacts with nitrosoarene 1 to generate radical intermediate IV28. This is followed by a single-electron transfer process, affording N-hydroxyamide 452. The N-hydroxyamide 4 is reduced to a radical anion V, which releases OH⁻ to form amide radical VI. This radical is further transformed into amide anion VII via SET and finally protonated to furnish the amide product28. In another pathway (Path 2), the acyl radical III directly reacts with nitroarene through a radical addition mechanism to form intermediate VIII29, which decomposes into a carboxyl radical and nitrosoarene 1. Although this pathway is less dominant, we detected trace amounts of carboxylic acid 6 and aniline 8 in GC-MS analyses. The carboxylic acid is produced as a result of acyl radical III addition over nitroarene, followed by path 228.

In conclusion, we have developed a sustainable photocatalytic method for the direct synthesis of amides from alkynes and nitroarenes using heterogeneous semiconductor catalysts. The environmentally friendly and recyclable TiO₂ nanotubes catalyzed the coupling reaction under mild conditions, using readily accessible substrates, i.e., aromatic, aliphatic, and internal alkynes, with nitroarenes. This system demonstrated a broad substrate scope and excellent functional-group tolerance, achieving yields up to 94%. Moreover, the photocatalytic system proved effective for the late-stage amidation of complex bioactive molecules and the direct synthesis of commercially available drugs. To the best of our knowledge, this work represents a rare example of a coupling reaction between alkynes and nitroarenes for amide synthesis, in which C≡C bond cleavage is promoted by a heterogeneous photocatalyst. This method offers a valuable addition to the existing amidation strategies.

Methods

Representative procedure for direct amidation reaction

An oven-dried quartz reaction tube (20 mL) equipped with a magnetic stir bar was charged with 1a 1-ethynyl-arenes (0.3 mmol, 1.0–1.5 equiv.), 2a (1-nitro-4-(trifluoromethyl)benzene, 0.2 mmol, 1.0 equiv.), TiO₂ nanotubes (20 mg), and 2 mL of acetonitrile. The reaction tube was sealed with a rubber septum under an air atmosphere and placed in a photoreactor. Photocatalytic irradiation was performed using a parallel reactor (WATTCAS, WP-TEC-1020SL) equipped with 365-nm LEDs (10 W), with continuous stirring for 72 h. The reaction temperature was maintained near room temperature (~25–26 °C) using a low-temperature circulator (EYELA, CoolAce CCA-1112) set at 25 °C. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was centrifuged to separate the photocatalyst, then concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by column chromatography on silica gel using the appropriate solvent system (Petroleum Ether/EtOAc, typically 7:2) to afford the desired product.
