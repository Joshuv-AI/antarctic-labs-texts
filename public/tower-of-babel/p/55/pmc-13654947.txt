SUV39H2-mediated NCOA4 methylation controls ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Lingxia Liu, Xinyun Pei, Ding Li, Ying Huo, Zhaoting Wang, Qiu Zhang, Yuan Yue, Bao Deng, Minmin Yu, Tong Xu, Lei Zhong, Xianhui Ruan, Xichuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) exhibits iron homeostasis that supports tumor growth and proliferation, yet the regulatory mechanisms controlling iron flux remain poorly defined. Here, we identify a ferritinophagic cargo receptor NCOA4 as a novel substrate of the lysine methyltransferase SUV39H2, uncovering a previously unrecognized mechanism that regulates ferritinophagy and ferroptosis. SUV39H2 directly binds and mono-methylates NCOA4 at lysine 356, a modification that reduces NCOA4 stability. Mechanistically, K356 methylation enhances NCOA4 interaction with the E3 ligase HERC2, promoting its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. This degradation increases FTH1 stability, suppresses ferritinophagic flux, limits iron release, maintains the high-risk iron homeostasis and ferroptosis resistance, ultimately promoting tumor proliferation and chemoresistance. Conversely, genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of SUV39H2 (OTS186935) inhibit NCOA4 methylation, stabilizes NCOA4 protein, enhances ferritinophagy, and triggers ferroptosis. Furthermore, SUV39H2 inhibition sensitizes TNBC cells to chemotherapy in vitro and in vivo, indicating OTS186935 treatment is a feasible therapeutic strategy. Collectively, the SUV39H2-NCOA4-HERC2 axis as a critical regulatory pathway in iron metabolism and ferroptosis, and highlight inhibition of NCOA4 K356 methylation as a promising therapeutic target in TNBC. Subject terms: Breast cancer, Autophagy, Methylation

Introduction

Iron is an essential trace element that supports numerous physiological processes in mammals, including energy metabolism, oxygen transport, DNA synthesis, and enzymatic activity [1]. Owing to its redox flexibility, iron cycles between ferrous (Fe2+) and ferric (Fe3+) states, enabling it to participate in various biochemical reactions [2]. However, the iron level is tightly regulated, as both iron deficiency and iron overload can lead to pathological conditions, including cancer [3–5]. Tumor cells usually exhibit abnormal iron metabolism, which is characterized by increased intracellular iron levels or “iron addiction” [6, 7]. Beyond acting as a nutrient, excess iron catalyzes the formation of ROS via the Fenton reaction, promoting genomic instability, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis, indicating tumor cells have a high-risk iron homeostasis [8–10].

Ferritinophagy, a selective form of autophagy mediated by the cargo receptor NCOA4, is thought to play a key role in maintaining intracellular iron homeostasis [11]. NCOA4 recognizes ferritin heavy chain (FTH1) and delivers ferritin to autophagosomes for degradation, leading to iron release in lysosomes [11–15]. This process regulates systemic and cellular iron availability and is increasingly recognized as a modulator of tumorigenesis [16–18]. Given this role of ferritinophagy in regulating iron homeostasis, enhancing NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagic flux increases vulnerability to ferroptosis [19–21]. Thus, a critical gap remains in understanding the molecular mechanisms by which ferritinophagy maintains intracellular high-risk iron levels without triggering ferroptosis.

Protein post-translational modifications (PTMs) are crucial enzymatic catalytic reactions that covalently regulate protein conformation, stability and cellular activities [22]. Accumulating evidence suggests that ferroptosis and ferritinophagy are tightly regulated by multiple PTMs, including methylation [23–26], phosphorylation [27–29], acetylation [30, 31], and ubiquitination [32]. Among these, protein lysine methylation has emerged as a key regulator of ferroptotic pathways [33]. Histone lysine methylation, such as H3K4me1, H3K4me3, and H3K9me3, has been shown to modulate ferroptosis [34–37]. Given that ferroptosis and ferritinophagy are regulated by many non-histone proteins, such as NCOA4, transferrin, and FTH1, it is highly plausible that lysine methylation of non-histone proteins may also play a significant role in these processes. SUV39H2, also a lysine methyltransferase, methylates histone H3K9 and non-histone proteins [38–40] and has been implicated in cancer proliferation and metastasis [41]. However, its specific role in regulating ferritinophagy and ferroptosis remains to be fully elucidated.

In this study, we identify a link between nonhistone lysine methylation and iron metabolism that modulates ferroptotic susceptibility. Mechanistically, SUV39H2 methylates NCOA4 at K356, promoting its degradation via enhanced HERC2 binding. This degradation suppresses ferritinophagy and ferroptosis, thereby contributing to tumor growth and chemoresistance. Our findings reveal an epigenetic mechanism by which SUV39H2 deficiency disrupts iron homeostasis and enhances ferroptotic susceptibility in TNBC.

Results

NCOA4low-FTH1high ferritinophagy axis maintains iron homeostasis in TNBC

TNBC is characterized by aberrant iron and lipid metabolism, with elevated intracellular iron levels contributing to tumor progression [42]. To validate the presence of high iron accumulation in TNBC, we first measured the iron concentrations in TNBC tissues and adjacent normal tissues using ICP-OES (inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry), colorimetric assays, and Prussian blue staining, which confirmed significantly increased iron levels in TNBC (Fig. 1A–C). Consistently, TNBC cell lines (MDA-MB-231 and BT-549) exhibited higher iron levels than non-tumorigenic mammary epithelial MCF10A cells (Fig. 1D–F and Fig. S1A). We hypothesized that elevated iron level in TNBC might result from altered expression of iron metabolism-related genes (FeMs) (Table S1). Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data identified the expression levels of 42 FeMs in TNBC and normal controls varied significantly (Fig. 1G, H). Given that NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy regulates intracellular iron homeostasis by directing ferritin degradation and iron release based on cellular demand [11], the NCOA4 and FTH1 expressive status may play a critical role in sustaining iron homeostasis in TNBC. To prove this hypothesis, we sought to investigate NCOA4 and FTH1 expressive levels in TNBC tissues and cells, and the results showed that FTH1 as significantly upregulated and NCOA4 as downregulated in TNBC compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1H–J), as well as by Western blot analyses in cell lines (Fig. 1K). Thus, the NCOA4low-FTH1high ferritinophagy axis correlates with elevated iron homeostasis in TNBC.

To further determine whether disrupting this axis could enhance ferritinophagy, thereby inducing ferroptosis, we first stably overexpressed NCOA4 in MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells (Fig. S1B). NCOA4 overexpression led to decreased FTH1 protein levels and increased the expression of autophagic marker LC3-II, which was further enhanced by chloroquine (CQ) treatment (Fig. 1L and Fig. S1C). Subsequently, we performed tandem RFP-GFP-LC3 analysis, and the results showed that NCOA4 significantly increased the number of red puncta (Fig. S1D, E). Notably, the addition of CQ induced a more pronounced increase in yellow puncta accumulation, indicating that NCOA4 promoted autophagic flux (Fig. S1D, E). This was accompanied by increased co-localization of FTH1 with LAMP2 (Fig. 1M) and elevated intracellular iron levels (Fig. 1N, O and Fig. S1F), ultimately promoted ferritinophagy. We next evaluated whether ferritinophagy flux induces ferroptosis. Indeed, the overexpressed NCOA4 led to a significantly increase in ferroptotic biomarkers, including enhanced ROS (Figure S1G-I), elevated lipid ROS (Fig. 1P and Fig. S1J, K), and disrupted mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) (Figure S1L), accompanied by condensed mitochondrial membranes and disrupted cristae (Fig. 1Q). Hence, the NCOA4low-FTH1high ferritinophagy axis contributes to maintaining iron homeostasis in TNBC.

NCOA4 is methylated by SUV39H2 at lysine 356

Previous studies have shown that NCOA4 stability can be regulated by ubiquitination and arginine methylation [43, 44], but whether it is subject to other post-translational modifications has not been explored. To identify potential modification types and sites, we performed mass spectrometry on purified NCOA4 protein and identified mono-methylation at lysine 356 (K356me1) (Fig. 2A). Sequence alignment showed that K356 is highly conserved across species (Fig. S1M). As expected, mutating K356 to arginine (K356R) substantially reduced the lysine methylation (Fig. 2B). To identify potential modifying enzymes, we performed mass spectrometry on the 3×Flag-NCOA4 protein complex and the lysine methyltransferase SUV39H2 was identified as a putative NCOA4-binding protein (Fig. 2C). This interaction was further supported by AlphaFold-based structural modeling (Figure S1N), co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays (Fig. 2D, E) and immunofluorescence assays (Fig. 2F). Direct binding was also validated by GST pull-down using recombinant NCOA4 and MDA-MB-231 cell lysates (Fig. 2G). To determine whether SUV39H2 methylates NCOA4, we performed an in vitro methylation assay, which revealed that SUV39H2 catalyzed methylation of NCOA4 (Fig. 2H). Overexpression of SUV39H2 in MCF10A cells significantly increased lysine methylation of NCOA4 (Fig. 2I), while knockout of SUV39H2 using CRISPR/Cas9 in MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells markedly decreased NCOA4 methylation (Fig. 2J, K). Additionally, a catalytically inactive mutant of SUV39H2 failed to promote NCOA4 methylation (Fig. 2K, L). Despite high sequence conservation between SUV39H1 and SUV39H2 [39], SUV39H1 knockout did not reduce NCOA4 methylation (Fig. S1O), establishing SUV39H2 as the primary methyltransferase responsible for NCOA4 methylation.

To further validate K356 as a direct substrate site of SUV39H2, we generated a K356R mutant of GST-NCOA4 for use in vitro methylation assays. As expected, the K356R mutation substantially reduced SUV39H2-mediated methylation (Fig. 2M). Meanwhile, molecular modeling using the crystal structure of NCOA4 suggested that K356 was located within a core domain predicted to interface with SUV39H2 (Fig. S1P), suggesting that the K356R mutation likely impairs NCOA4-SUV39H2 binding. To prove this hypothesis, we constructed SUV39H2 dimer/NCOA4-M_domain-Wild (K356-in-M_domain) and SUV39H2 dimer/NCOA4-M_domain-K356R simulation systems. Both systems reached structural stable after ~120 ns of simulation and RMSF analysis showed that the K356R mutation markedly increased local flexibility of the NCOA4-M_domain (Fig. S1Q, R). Notably, the mutation also destabilized the SUV39H2 dimer, as reflected by elevated RMSF values (Fig. S1S, T). Hydrogen-bond analysis showed that K356 in the NCOA4-M domain forms stable hydrogen with S182, V184, and E186 in SUV39H2 in the wild-type system, whereas the K356R mutation disrupted these interactions, resulting in reduced complex stability (Fig. 2N). The destabilizing effect was further corroborated by immunoprecipitation assays, which showed that the NCOA4 K356R mutation impaired its binding to SUV39H2 (Fig. S1U). Thus, K356 serves as both a critical methylation site and a key structural determinant for the NCOA4-SUV39H2 interaction.

SUV39H2 promotes NCOA4 degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system

An inverse relationship between SUV39H2 and NCOA4 expression was observed in TNBC tissues and cells. TCGA data analyses, GEO data analyses, immunohistochemistry, and western blot revealed that SUV39H2 expression was markedly higher in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues, whereas NCOA4 levels were lower (Fig. 3A–C and Fig. S2A). Consistent with this, tissues with high SUV39H2 expression also exhibited elevated NCOA4 lysine methylation and reduced NCOA4 protein levels (Fig. 3D). Following this, we performed survival analysis, and the result revealed that SUV39H2 expression alone was not significantly associated with patient prognosis (Fig. S2B). Notably, we identified a patient subgroup characterized by the SUV39H2high/NCOA4low expression pattern, and this signature was correlated with worse clinical outcomes (Fig. S2C, D). We next compared SUV39H2 and NCOA4 protein levels across breast cell lines and found that TNBC cells expressed higher SUV39H2 and lower NCOA4 compared to MCF10A (Fig. 3E). These observations raised the possibility that SUV39H2 regulates NCOA4 protein stability. To this end, we treated MCF10A-SUV39H2 cells with cycloheximide (CHX) to block new protein synthesis or with the proteasome inhibitor MG132. CHX treatment accelerated NCOA4 degradation, while MG132 treatment prevented it (Fig. 3F, G), suggesting SUV39H2 promotes NCOA4 degradation.

Since protein degradation often occurs via ubiquitination, we next investigated whether SUV39H2 modulates NCOA4 ubiquitination. Indeed, NCOA4 showed higher ubiquitination levels in MDA-MB-231 cells relative to MCF10A cells (Fig. 3H). Moreover, SUV39H2 overexpression increased NCOA4 ubiquitination (Fig. 3I), whereas SUV39H2 knockout reduced it (Fig. 3I). Together, these results demonstrate that SUV39H2 promotes NCOA4 degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome system.

NCOA4 K356me1 facilitates HERC2-mediated ubiquitination of NCOA4

To further explore whether SUV39H2 facilitates the recruitment of ubiquitin ligases to NCOA4, we analyzed our LC-MS/MS data and identified HERC2, a known E3 ligase reported to interact with NCOA4[43], as a potential binding partner (Fig. 4A). Co-IP confirmed the in vivo interaction between HERC2 and NCOA4 (Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, HERC2 depletion led to increased NCOA4 protein levels (Fig. 4C), and similarly rescued NCOA4 expression in MCF10A-SUV39H2 cells (Fig. 4D). Moreover, SUV39H2 overexpression enhanced the interaction between HERC2 and NCOA4 (Fig. 4E), while HERC2 knockout reduced NCOA4 ubiquitination (Fig. 4F). Therefore, SUV39H2 facilitates NCOA4 degradation by promoting its ubiquitination via HERC2.

We next asked whether SUV39H2 promotes NCOA4 ubiquitination through K356 methylation. Supporting this, NCOA4 WT exhibited a shorter protein half-life than the K356R mutant (Fig. 4G), and MG132 treatment blocked the degradation (Fig. 4H). Consistently, NCOA4 WT showed markedly increased ubiquitination compared to K356R (Fig. 4I), indicating that methylation at K356 enhances protein degradation. Additionally, Co-IP revealed stronger interaction between HERC2 and NCOA4 WT than with K356R (Fig. 4J). Furthermore, HERC2 knockout decreased NCOA4 WT ubiquitination, while it had little effect on the K356R mutant (Fig. 4K). Thus, NCOA4 K356 methylation promotes its ubiquitination and degradation, in part by modulating its interaction with HERC2.

Inhibition of NCOA4 K356me1 enhances ferritinophagic flux, disturbs iron homeostasis and triggers ferroptosis

Given that SUV39H2 methylates NCOA4, we hypothesized that SUV39H2 might regulate ferritinophagy and ferroptosis. As expected, SUV39H2 knockout led to elevated intracellular iron levels (Fig. 5A, B and Fig. S2E), decreased FTH1 expression, and increased LC3-II levels (Fig. 5C and Fig. S2F). Consistently, the co-localization of endogenous FTH1 with LAMP2 (Fig. 5D) and the number of red puncta (Fig. S2G, H) were increased, further confirming the induction of ferritinophagy. We then assessed the effect of SUV39H2 deficiency on ferroptosis. Knockout of SUV39H2 resulted in increased cellular ROS (Fig. 5E and Fig. S2I), lipid ROS (Fig. 5F and Fig. S2J), a reduction in MMP (Fig. 5G and Fig. S2K), and typical ferroptotic mitochondrial changes (Fig. 5H). These findings indicate that SUV39H2 inhibition induces ferroptosis and ferritinophagy.

To test whether SUV39H2 deletion-induced ferroptosis is mediated by NCOA4, we knocked out NCOA4 in SUV39H2-deficient cells. The results showed that SUV39H2 loss elevated lipid ROS and labile iron, which were reversed by NCOA4 ablation, confirming that these phenotypes depend on NCOA4 (Fig. S2L–N). Having established this, we next investigated the functional role of SUV39H2-mediated NCOA4 K356 methylation in regulating ferritinophagy and ferroptosis. We found that NCOA4 K356R resulted in increased intracellular iron levels, co-localization of ferritin with lysosomes, and autolysosome formation compared to NCOA4 WT (Fig. 5I–K and Fig. S3A–C). Western blot analysis further revealed that the K356R mutation inhibited FTH1 expression and promoted the conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II (Fig. 5L and Fig. S3D), indicative of increased ferritinophagy. To assess ferroptosis, we measured relevant biomarkers and found that the K356R mutation significantly promoted ferroptotic cell death (Fig. 5M–P and Fig. S3E, F). Together, NCOA4 K356me1 suppresses ferritinophagy, thereby promoting ferroptosis resistance.

Inhibition of NCOA4 K356me1 suppresses tumor growth and enhances chemosensitivity in vitro and in vivo

Given our findings that NCOA4 overexpression suppresses TNBC cell proliferation and invasion (Fig. S4A–E), we next examined the functional role of SUV39H2 in TNBC cell growth and invasion. SUV39H2 knockout significantly reduced colony formation (Fig. 6A), cell proliferation (Fig. 6B, C), and cell migration and invasion (Fig. S4F, G). Similarly, expression of the NCOA4 K356R mutant—resistant to SUV39H2-mediated methylation—significantly impaired colony formation (Fig. 6D), growth (Fig. 6E–G), and invasive properties (Fig. S4H–J) compared to NCOA4 WT. Ferroptosis has been implicated in regulating chemotherapy resistance [45]. Therefore, we tested whether K356 methylation modulates chemosensitivity in TNBC. SUV39H2 knockout increased the sensitivity of MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells to paclitaxel (PTX), doxorubicin (ADM), cisplatin (DDP), and vinorelbine (NVB), with the most pronounced effect observed in response to DDP (Fig. 6H and Fig. S5A). We further explored the IC50 values of chemotherapeutic drugs using pRRophetic R software package. Notably, we found that the IC50 value of DDP and PTX was lower in TNBC patients with SUV39H2low/NCOA4high expression (Fig. S5B), suggesting that the NCOA4 K356R mutation may also increase sensitivity to DDP. Consistently, cells overexpressing NCOA4 K356R displayed greater sensitivity to DDP compared to NCOA4 WT-expressing cells or cells treated with other chemotherapeutic agents (Fig. 6I).

To assess the in vivo relevance of NCOA4 K356 methylation in ferroptosis and chemotherapy response, we established a xenograft model using MDA-MB-231 cells stably expressing either NCOA4 WT or the methylation-resistant K356R mutant. And 2 weeks after cell inoculation, DDP was administered every 2 days for 3 weeks (Fig. 6J). Tumor volume and weight were significantly reduced in mice bearing K356R-expressing tumors (Fig. 6K–M). Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed that tumors expressing NCOA4 K356R exhibited reduced Ki67 staining, consistent with suppressed proliferation, and increased iron accumulation and decreased FTH1, consistent with enhanced ferroptosis (Fig. 6N). Notably, the combination of NCOA4 K356R expression and DDP treatment led to a synergistic inhibition of tumor growth, whereas K356R expression alone had a moderate effect (Fig. 6J–N). These findings underscore that methylation-mutant NCOA4 at K356 not only impairs tumor proliferation via ferroptosis but also sensitizes TNBC tumors to cisplatin chemotherapy in vivo.

OTS186935 induces ferroptosis in TNBC by targeting NCOA4 K356 methylation

Having shown that SUV39H2-mediated methylation of NCOA4 at K356 suppresses ferritinophagy and promotes chemotherapy resistance in TNBC, we next tested whether OTS186935, a known SUV39H2 inhibitor[46–48], could restore ferroptosis and chemosensitivity. We performed the RNA-seq on SUV39H2-knockout and OTS186935-treated MDA-MB-231 cells, and results showed highly consistent transcriptional changes, with 1,191 overlapping DEGs (Fig. S6A, Table S2). Expression trends of the top 50 altered genes were also largely concordant (Fig. S6B), supporting OTS186935 as an SUV39H2 inhibitor [49, 50]. Furthermore, treating MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells with OTS186935 markedly reduced NCOA4 methylation and ubiquitination (Fig. 7A–C and Fig. S6C–E). Importantly, OTS186935 significantly inhibited TNBC cell viability, but this inhibition was partially rescued by co-treatment with DFO (iron chelator), Fer-1 (ferroptosis inhibitor), or NAC (antioxidant) (Fig. 7D and Fig. S6F). Consistently, OTS186935 induced hallmarks of ferroptosis (Fig. 7E–H and Fig. S6G–J), which was driven by enhanced ferritinophagy (Fig. 7I–L and Fig. S7A–G). Thus, OTS186935 enhances ferritinophagy and triggers ferroptosis.

Functionally, OTS186935 treatment impaired colony formation (Fig. 7M and S7H–J), reduced cell proliferation (Fig. 7N and Fig. S7K, L), suppressed migration and invasion (Fig. S8A–G). and enhanced the DDP sensitivity (Fig. 7O and Fig. S8H). Furthermore, OTS186935 significantly suppressed MDA-MB-231 xenograft tumor growth, as evidenced by reduced tumor volume, weight, and Ki67 staining (Fig. 7P–T). OTS186935-treated tumors also showed increased iron accumulation and reduced FTH1 expression, consistent with ferroptosis activation (Fig. 7T). Notably, combining OTS186935 with DDP achieved greater tumor suppression than either treatment alone (Fig. 7Q–T). Besides, the OTS186935 and DDP-treated groups showed no evident organ damage or abnormalities were observed, exhibiting no significant toxicity (Fig. S8I, J). To further investigate the clinical translational potential of OTS186935, we conducted pharmacokinetic investigations to assess the absorption and safety profile. Pharmacokinetic profiling indicated effective oral absorption, characterized by a Cmax of 42.0 ng/mL, a Tmax of 0.389 h, and an AUC0-t of 275 hr*ng /mL, corresponding to an absolute oral bioavailability (F) of 17.1% (Table S3). The plasma disappearance half-time was 10.3 h, and at 24 h post-injection, the injected OTS186935 had almost completely disappeared from the blood (Table S3). Together, these results support the favorable oral absorption and safety of OTS186935 in mice.

Discussion

Iron is essential for normal cell survival, functioning as a cofactor in numerous processes, including oxygen transport, oxidative phosphorylation, and enzymatic reactions essential for cell proliferation [51]. In cancer, this regulation is often disrupted, resulting in an enhanced dependence on iron, that supports tumor initiation and growth [52]. Reprogramming iron metabolism to disrupt this iron homeostasis and release excess iron can trigger ferroptosis, presenting a therapeutic vulnerability in certain cancers. To date, the mechanism for maintaining iron levels is still unclear. In this study, we identified a NCOA4low-FTH1high ferritinophagy axis that sustains iron homeostasis in TNBC. This phenomenon is consistent with the previously reported functions of NCOA4-FTH1 in ferroptosis and ferritinophagy [14]. NCOA4 overexpression reactivated ferritinophagy, promoted iron release from ferritin, induced ferroptosis, and suppressed tumor cell proliferation. Mechanistically, SUV39H2 methylated NCOA4 at K356, enhancing its interaction with the E3 ubiquitin ligase HERC2 and leading to NCOA4 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Consequently, ferritinophagy and ferroptosis were suppressed, promoting TNBC progression and chemoresistance.

In addition to the methylation, HIF also serves as a major transcriptional regulator of cellular iron levels [53]. Li et al. further uncovered the molecular mechanisms in which dual HIF1α and HIF2α can directly transcriptionally activate NCOA4 in response to iron demand in hepatocytes [54]. The regulation of NCOA4 involves a multifaceted interplay between the SUV39H2-mediated epigenetic axis and the HIF-driven transcriptional axis. The SUV39H2-mediated epigenetic axis may function as an independent regulatory module that either fine-tunes or counterbalances HIF-mediated NCOA4 activation. This regulatory interplay likely endows TNBC cells with metabolic flexibility. Under normal conditions, it maintains ferroptosis resistance, while allowing rapid iron mobilization to support proliferation when needed.

Fe-S clusters are essential cofactors for numerous enzymes involved in critical cellular processes, including mitochondrial electron transport, DNA repair, and metabolic catalysis[55]. In rapidly proliferating tumor cells, the demand for Fe-S clusters is markedly elevated [18] and the canonical pathway for releasing stored iron to meet this demand is NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy[56]. Our findings that SUV39H2 downregulates NCOA4 protein levels establishes a novel layer of NCOA4 regulation. By dampening ferritinophagy, SUV39H2 may act as a brake on iron release, potentially creating a controlled, steady supply of iron for Fe-S cluster assembly rather than a surge that could lead to iron toxicity and ferroptosis. This restraint could be particularly important in TNBC, where high metabolic activity must be balanced against oxidative stress.

Lysosomes possess a large amount of iron and play a crucial role in iron homeostasis by integrating signals from different subcellular compartments and cell death pathways [57]. Lysosomal iron overload causes lipid peroxide production, leading to ferroptosis in cancer. Consistently, we found NCOA4 overexpression or the inhibition of NCOA4 methylation can reactivate ferritinophagy, promote the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes for iron release from ferritin, and induced ferroptosis. Subsequently, for this property, a small-molecule activator of lysosomal iron—fentomycin-1-were designed to induce the oxidative degradation of phospholipids and ultimately ferroptosis in cancer [58]. Similarly, we discovered the SUV39H2 inhibitor OTS186935 increased intracellular iron, reduced FTH1 levels, enhanced LC3-II expression, and promoted ferritin–lysosome colocalization, collectively indicating robust induction of ferritinophagy and ferroptosis (Fig. 7). These data indicate that pharmacologic activation of lysosomal iron may server a promising therapeutic strategy in cancer.

Several limitations should be noted. First, definitive validation of NCOA4 K356 monomethylation awaits the development of a site-specific antibody, as current evidence is indirect. Second, given the high sequence homology between SUV39H2 and related methyltransferases (SUV39H1, EZH2, G9a, SETDB1), it would be important to assess the biochemical selectivity of OTS186935 against these enzymes. However, due to technical and resource constraints, such experiments could not be performed in the current study. Therefore, we have reclassified OTS186935 as an SUV39H2 inhibitor rather than a selective one.

In summary, we identify that TNBC cells exhibit “iron addiction” through the NCOA4low-FTH1high ferritinophagy axis. NCOA4 K356 methylation promotes NCOA4 degradation and suppresses ferritinophagy; whereas K356R stabilizes NCOA4, reactivates ferritinophagy, and induces ferroptosis. Thus, targeting NCOA4 methylation with OTS186935 represents a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance ferroptosis vulnerability and chemotherapy efficacy in TNBC.

Materials And Methods

Cell culture

All cell lines (MDA-MB-468, MDA-MB-453, MDA-MB-231, BT-549, MCF10A, HEK293T) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2 atmosphere. The medium used for MDA-MB-468, MDA-MB-453, MDA-MB-231, BT-549, and HEK293T cells was supplemented with DMEM, 10% fetal bovine serum, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. MCF10A cells were cultured in complete MCF10A culture medium. Prior to the experiment, these cells were screened for mycoplasma contamination, interspecies cross-contamination, and authenticity.

RNA-seq data and corresponding clinical information related to TNBC were acquired from TCGA data portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in TNBC tissues, compared with normal breast tissues.
