A paired electrolysis for hydroxymethanesulfonate synthesis using sulfide waste as the sulfur source
Authors: Chunyu Zhang, Haozhe Sun, Xiang Liu, Kejian Kong, Tiancong Zhou, Xi Wang, Haohong Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical C–S bond construction represents an attractive route to produce valuable organosulfur compounds, yet current approaches suffer from low reaction efficiency and rely on sulfites (SO32−) as the sulfur source. Here we report a paired electrolysis for hydroxymethanesulfonate synthesis from widespread industrial-waste sulfides (HS−). Specifically, HS− is oxidized to SO32− by in-situ generated H2O2 at cathode, and methanol is oxidized to formaldehyde at anode, with following C–S coupling to form hydroxymethanesulfonate. By matching HS− concentration with current density and strengthening convective mass transfer between intermediates, we achieve efficient hydroxymethanesulfonate production with Faradaic efficiency of 102.8% and productivity of 282 μmol h−1 in a membrane-free H-cell under optimized conditions (50 mA cm−2, 0.05 M HS−, stirring in anodic chamber). By constructing a flow reactor, this paired electrolysis enables conversion of HS−-contaminated wastewater (from <10 ppm to 3000 ppm) with Faradaic efficiencies exceeding 100%, while tolerating coexisting anions and heavy metals in real effluents. It also converts waste H2S gas with >87% conversion and >74% hydroxymethanesulfonate selectivity over 102 hours of stable operation, demonstrating more cost-effective economics to both industrial and previous electrochemical methods.

Introduction

Organosulfur chemicals have found widespread applications in pharmaceuticals and organic synthesis. In particular, hydroxymethanesulfonate (HMS) represents an important compound, serving as a preservative, dispersant, and antioxidant in lubricant oil, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics industries. Conventional preparation of HMS relies on a chemical reaction between saturated bisulfite (e.g., sodium bisulfite) and concentrated formaldehyde solution (HCHO, >20 wt%) at 90 °C1, which is suffering from the utilization of highly toxic and volatile HCHO, as well as from the generation of substantial amounts of wastewater, together posing serious safety and environmental concerns.

Electrosynthesis is considered as a potentially sustainable tool for value-added chemicals production, owing to the mild reaction conditions, decentralized operation and ability to utilize renewable electricity. In recent years, HMS electrosynthesis is receiving increasing attention with benefits over the aforementioned conventional method. The carbon source of HMS can be originated from CO2 that was reduced to alcohol-like intermediate (*CHOH, * denoted as adsorbed form) at cathode (Fig. 1a)2, or from methanol (MeOH) and ethylene glycol (EG) that were oxidized to formaldehyde-like intermediate (*HCHO) at anode (Fig. 1b)3–5. In particular, MeOH, as a key C1 platform feedstock for commodity and fine chemicals production6–8, can also be valorized to HMS. This would expand the valorization pathway of MeOH and avoid the direct use of toxic formaldehyde. However, it is noteworthy that sulfites ions (SO32−) was the only reported sulfur source for HMS electrosynthesis, which serves as a key nucleophile to attack on the preceding carbon-based electrophilic intermediates for C–S coupling. However, SO32− abundance is very low in nature and is primarily obtained as a byproduct in industrial productions of sulfuric acid or thiosulfates2,9, making it impractical as the major sulfur source for large-scale HMS electrosynthesis. Moreover, previous synthetic routes showed low HMS production efficiency. For example, HMS synthesized from CO2 delivered low Faradaic efficiency (FE; 4.1%), owing to the low selectivity of the Cu catalyst for intermediate *CHOH generation. Although HMS synthesized from MeOH or EG showed enhanced performance by selectively producing *HCHO, the reported maximum FE was only 67% (ref. 4). Compared to SO32−, sulfide ions (HS− and S2−) more widely exist in industrial waste streams, such as natural gas extraction, petrochemical processing, and spent caustic, with concentrations ranging from 100 to 10,000 ppm. They also occur in domestic scenarios, such as municipal sewage at low dissolved concentrations (<10 ppm)10,11. Due to the corrosiveness and malodor characteristics, sulfide poses severe harm to industrial equipment and the ecological environment. Considering its higher availability and the necessity to valorize wastes, sulfides seem as an appealing alternative to SO32− for HMS electrosynthesis. One may imagine to electrochemically oxidize sulfide to SO32− at the anode, before the following C–S coupling with in situ-generated carbon-based intermediates taking place. Unfortunately, as widely observed in existing literature12–14, sulfides are incline to be oxidized to element sulfur (S0) species at the anode, which then aggregate to form Sx deposit, showing reluctance to further oxidation to SO32−. Moreover, the competing Sx deposition over the anode surface may deactivate the reaction by preventing carbon source (e.g., MeOH and EG) oxidation to *HCHO at the same electrode.

In this work, we reported a paired electrolysis strategy for HMS synthesis, by using sulfide as the sulfur source and MeOH as the carbon source. The paired electrolysis includes HS−-to-SO32− conversion mediated by in situ generated H2O2 at the cathode, and MeOH-to-HCHO conversion at the anode. Then, C–S coupling between SO32− and HCHO takes place, with the formation of HMS (Fig. 1c). We initially attempted co-oxidation of HS− and MeOH at the anode, but showing low HMS production efficiency (FE: 10.9%, productivity: 25.3 μmol h−1) and Sx deposition. We then demonstrated the paired electrolysis in H-cell without a membrane, attaining significantly improved HMS production (FE: 102.8%, productivity: 282 μmol h−1). Moreover, we demonstrated that matching HS− concentration and current density, and strengthening convective mass transfer of SO32− and HCHO are crucial factors for high reaction efficiency. To demonstrate the practical application of paired electrolysis, a flow reactor was constructed using HS−-contaminated wastewater and gas as the sulfur source. For wastewater conversion, the system enables continuous treatment of sulfide wastewater across a wide concentration range, from domestic (<10 ppm) to industrial (200–3000 ppm) conditions, with HMS FEs exceeding 70% under optimized current densities. Moreover, for real sulfide wastewater coexisting anions and heavy metal contaminants (Cl−, NO3−, SCN−, and Cr3+), a high FE of HMS (>67.9%) was still maintained. For the waste gas conversion, H2S-contaminated gas (a typical pollutant in gas/oil reservoirs) was prepared. This paired electrolysis exhibited stable operation for 102 h, along with nearly 90% H2S conversion, >70% selectivity and >100% FE of HMS. Taking HS−-contaminated waste gas conversion as an example, techno-economic analysis showcased a higher economic benefit of the paired electrolysis compared to industrial and previous electrochemical methods for HMS synthesis.

Results

Anode catalysts screening for MeOH oxidation

To achieve the above-conceived paired electrolysis for HMS synthesis, we utilized MeOH as the carbon source and initially screened different catalysts for selective electrooxidation to HCHO at the anode (Supplementary Fig. 1), a prerequisite for the following C–S coupling. Among the tested catalysts, a commercially available niobium-based boron-doped diamond (BDD, fabricated by depositing BDD films on a niobium substrate) delivers more favorable HCHO production with good FE (>70%) in 0.5 M NaHCO3 electrolyte within a wide range of current density (between 10 and 100 mA cm−2; Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2), in agreement with its promising MeOH oxidation activity15. We anticipate that the electrophilic property of HCHO may facilitate C–S coupling via reacting with sulfur species with nucleophilic features. EG was detected as a byproduct with FE range from 0.8 to 18.5% within the tested current density range, presumably resulting from the coupling between two hydroxymethyl (*CH2OH) intermediates derived from the deprotonation of MeOH16–18. Difficulty of anode C–S coupling using sulfide as the sulfur source

We then demonstrated the possibility of using sodium sulfite (Na2SO3) for HMS electrosynthesis via C–S coupling with MeOH-oxidized HCHO over BDD, the only reported sulfur source in literature. MeOH and Na2SO3 were fed into the anodic chamber of a membrane-divided H-cell under stirring at 500 rpm (Supplementary Fig. 3). To our expectation, HMS was observed as the major product (detected by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), Supplementary Fig. 4a) under a constant current electrolysis at 50 mA cm−2, with 64.7% FE, 73.9% selectivity, and 603.4 μmol h−1 productivity (left columns in Fig. 2b, c), showing comparable performances with previous results3,4. Other minor products include sulfoacetate (SA; FE of 7.3%, detected by 1H NMR), sulfate (SO42−; FE of 9.1%, detected by ion chromatography) and oxygen (O2; FE of 3.9%, detected by gas chromatography). The formation of SA originates from nucleophilic addition of SO32− to EG-oxidized glycolaldehyde (*CH2OHCHO)2,3. Furthermore, we introduced SO32− into the anodic chamber after conducting MeOH electrooxidation over BDD, and as a result, obtained comparable performance to that via co-introducing MeOH and SO32− (Supplementary Fig. 4b), demonstrating that the C–S coupling between SO32− and HCHO occurs mainly in the bulk solution phase.

Then, we replaced Na2SO3 with sodium hydrosulfide (NaHS) as the sulfur source and conducted anodic electrolysis under identical conditions. By evident contrast, HMS production is markedly suppressed (10.9% FE, 25.3 μmol h−1 productivity; right column in Fig. 2b), and S0 exhibits the highest selectivity of 47.3% (right column in Fig. 2c; detected by gravimetric analysis and ion chromatography), with obvious accumulation both in the solution (existing as polysulfide ions, Sx2−) and over the electrode surface after electrolysis (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, time-dependent profile reveals continuous S0 accumulation, while deep oxidation to SO42− was limited (Supplementary Fig. 5). Together with other sulfur-containing products include thiosulfate (S2O32−; detected by ion chromatography) and SO42−, these products account for the majority of the total FE (54.4%), while FE contributions from MeOH-oxidized products (including HCHO, FA, and EG) are minimal. Linear sweep voltammetry curves (Fig. 2e) show that the onset potential of HS− oxidation (purple curve) is much lower than that of MeOH oxidation (blue curve) or oxygen evolution reaction (OER; grey curve), even when MeOH and NaHS co-exist (red curve), suggesting that HS− oxidation is energetically more favorable than MeOH oxidation and OER, thus giving rise to severe S0 deposition.

Based on the above observations, the reason for the difficulty of using HS− as the sulfur source for C–S bond coupling in conventional anodic electrolysis is unveiled. In the presence of SO32− (Fig. 2f), HCHO generated from MeOH oxidation experiences nucleophilic attack by SO32−, leading to efficient C–S coupling with HMS formation. In contrast, in the presence of HS− (Fig. 2g), SO32− generation is suppressed owing to preferential HS− oxidation to S0 deposit, in addition with inhibited MeOH oxidation and thus HCHO generation, together giving rise to low C–S coupling efficiency for HMS generation. As the same time, the accumulated S0 may consume SO32− via comproportionation reaction to form S2O32−. This reaction proceeds faster than the coupling between HCHO and SO32−, resulting in predominant accumulation of S2O32− rather than HMS (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Subsequently, S2O32− is completely oxidized to SO42− (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Even with MeOH’s presence, SO42− is still the major product with minimal HMS formation, suggesting that the anodic oxidation of S2O32− to SO42− may bypass SO32− formation.

Paired electrolysis for efficient C–S coupling using sulfide as the sulfur source

Based on the above findings, we recognize the importance of concurrent HS−-to-SO32− and MeOH-to-HCHO conversions to achieve C–S coupling for HMS production. In recent years, paired electrolysis is emerging as an attractive strategy for chemical synthesis. Typically, the same product can be generated at both electrodes, showing higher energy efficiency than conventional electrolysis, in which the product is produced at singular electrode19,20. More delicately, different transformations can take place at the cathode and anode, with the coupling of reaction intermediates to construct more complicated chemicals21,22. Very recently, we achieved H2S oxidation to SO42− at the cathode, by using in situ generated H2O2 via two-electron oxygen reduction reaction (2e− ORR) over an oxidized carbon nanotube (OCNT)23. During the study, the S0 deposit was not observed, and SO32− was one key intermediate during H2S oxidation. Therefore, we anticipate that if the in situ generated SO32− from HS− oxidation at the cathode can undergo nucleophilic attack on in situ generated HCHO from MeOH oxidation at the anode, C–S coupling to yield HMS might be possible by designing a paired electrolysis, which consists HS−-to-SO32− (oxidized by in situ generated H2O2) at the cathode and MeOH-to-HCHO at the anode.

This paired electrolysis may avoid S0 deposition and is estimated to attain a theoretically maximum HMS FE of 167% (see details in Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). To achieve the paired electrolysis, one prerequisite is to produce H2O2 at the cathode. Thus, we initially evaluated H2O2 generation ability via 2e− ORR at the cathode in a divided H-cell without MeOH feeding. A commercially available gas diffusion electrode (GDE) was first selected as the catalyst based on its reported high activity24–26, and OCNT was investigated later. The electrolysis was conducted in a 0.5 M NaHCO3 electrolyte under different current densities. During electrolysis, 100 ml min−1 of O2 was continuously fed to the cathodic chamber for 2e− ORR. The results show H2O2 FE between 50 and 71%, and increase of H2O2 productivity with current density (from 10 to 100 mA cm−2; Supplementary Fig. 9), demonstrating efficient H2O2 production at the cathodic side.

Then, we carried out the paired electrolysis for HMS synthesis in H-cell without equipping a membrane under stirring at 500 rpm in the anodic chamber (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 10). The electrolysis was performed at the same conditions as those for H2O2 generation. NaHS (0.05 M) and MeOH (6 M) were added to the cathodic and anodic chambers, respectively. To our delight, HMS was successfully synthesized and accumulated over electrolysis time (Supplementary Fig. 11a), and no sulfur was observed either in the solution phase or on the electrode surface after electrolysis (Fig. 3b). Moreover, time-dependent product distribution suggests that HS− may experience a stepwise oxidation to S2O32−, SO32− and then to SO42− in paired electrolysis, during which the presence of HCHO (from MeOH oxidation) may serve as an electrophile to react with SO32− to produce HMS (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c), validating the feasibility of the paired electrolysis strategy. Furthermore, the reaction performances at different current densities are also given (Fig. 3c), showing a volcano-type trend of HMS FE and productivity with current density. The optimal performance is reached at 50 mA cm−2, showing HMS FE of 102.8% and productivity of 282 μmol h−1. Compared with previous results for HMS electrosynthesis in a batch reactor (Supplementary Fig. 12), including anodic C–S coupling from MeOH/EG and SO32−, and cathodic C–S coupling from CO2 and SO32−, the paired electrolysis delivers competitive HMS production performance. More importantly, HS−, with higher abundance, is used as the sulfur source in our work, while SO32− was used in all previous works. Notably, the overall FE at different current densities remains below 200%, with a loss ranging from 25 to 56%. For all the screened NaHS concentrations, similar volcano-type trends between current density and FE/productivity of HMS were observed (Supplementary Fig. 13). Notably, a higher current density is required for reaching the peak of the volcano when the HMS concentration increases, highlighting the importance of synchronous optimization of HS− concentration and current density to achieve highly effective HMS-producing performance. The reaction pathways at different combinations of HS− concentration and current density are proposed (Fig. 3f–h). At low HS− concentrations (e.g., <0.05 M; Fig. 3d), high current densities promote over-oxidation of HS− to SO42−, thereby limiting HMS production. Conversely, at high HS− concentrations (e.g., >0.5 M; Fig. 3e), low current densities result in substantial S2O32− accumulation due to its slow oxidation kinetics at the cathodic side (Supplementary Fig. 14), hindering SO32− formation and ultimately reducing HMS generation. Moreover, S0 formation may occur at anodic chamber due to the excessively high HS⁻ concentration (Supplementary Fig. 13c), which would interfere with C–S coupling reaction. Only when the NaHS concentration and current density are synchronously increased or decreased (Fig. 3f), can the oxidation of HS− and MeOH match with each other with maximized HMS production. Collectively, higher HMS FE and productivity were attained at an optimal combination of 0.05 M NaHS and 50 mA cm−2 for GDE cathode and BDD anode (Fig. 3b).

The effect of MeOH concentration was also investigated (Supplementary Fig. 15). The HMS production firstly increased and then decreased at elevated MeOH concentration, giving optimal performance at 6 M MeOH. Regarding anode catalyst, other materials than BDD were also evaluated for the paired electrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 16), including HS− oxidation-favorable materials (CoSx, NiSx, and Ni(OH)2)27–29, alcohols oxidation-favorable materials (MnO2, Pt/C, and Ru/C)30–32, and dimensionally stable electrodes (PbO2, SnSb/Ti, and IrTa/Ti)33–35. Notably, HMS was produced as the major product over all the examined anodic catalysts, demonstrating the universality of the paired electrolysis strategy. Among the tested catalysts, alcohols oxidation-favorable materials and dimensionally stable electrodes generally exhibit higher HMS-producing performance than HS− oxidation-favorable materials, which is more likely due to their higher efficiency in HCHO formation. Regarding the cathode catalyst, another 2e− ORR catalyst OCNT was synthesized based on our previous work23, showing higher H2O2 FE (approaching 90%; Supplementary Fig. 17a) than GDE. It was also employed in paired electrolysis with 0.05 M NaHS (Supplementary Fig. 17b). Compared to the GDE cathode, a similar volcano-type trend of HMS FE with current density was observed, but over 100% HMS FE was attained at a lower current density (30 mA cm−2) than that for GDE (50 mA cm−2). As a result, the OCNT cathode delivered a lower cell voltage and thus a reduced electrolysis energy consumption (Supplementary Fig. 17c), which are vital for practical application. Since GDE is a commercially more available material, it was still employed as the cathode in the following study. More studies and optimizations based on OCNT as the cathode can be the focus.

Based on the above results and the additional control experiments conducted in H-cell (with/without NaHS and/or MeOH in the same/different chambers, as well as with/without a membrane, as shown in Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. 18–21), we propose the following reaction pathway for HMS production in paired electrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 22). At anode, MeOH is selectively oxidized to HCHO over BDD catalyst. At the cathode, HS− is oxidized to SO32− by the in situ generated H2O2. Then, C–S coupling between SO32− (as a nucleophile) and HCHO (as an electrophile) takes place with HMS production. Meanwhile, SO32− can also react with *CH2OHCHO (an EG-oxidized intermediate) to form another C–S coupling product SA, but its FE was below 10% within the current density range due to low EG production (Fig. 3c). Additionally, SO32− can be further oxidized to SO42− at higher current densities.

Importance of strengthened mass transfer for enhanced performance

Based on the reaction pathway understanding, the rapid mass transfer of SO32− (from cathodic chamber) and HCHO (from anodic chamber) and their mixing in the H-cell is crucial for HMS synthesis. Otherwise, SO32− would be over-oxidized to SO42− without nucleophile ability to attack on HCHO. Indeed, H-cell equipped with a membrane that prevents crossover from anodic and cathodic chambers showed negligible HMS production, in evident contrast with the one without a membrane (Supplementary Table 1, entries 1–2).

In paired electrolysis, the crossover of SO32− towards the anodic side is promoted by electric field potential, concentration gradient, and competitive H2O2 consumption of sulfur-containing species (as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 23). In addition, convection is an important type of mass transfer, we studied the influence of convection of the key reaction intermediates (that are, SO32− and HCHO) on HMS-producing performance. Since the type of convection varies from natural convection to forced convection by exerting rigorous stirring, we designed a series of experiments with/without magnetic stirring for investigations. Note that all the experiments above were conducted with magnetic stirring at anodic chamber with 500 rpm. As depicted in Table 1, the electrolysis results with stirring in anodic (entry 1) or cathodic (entry 2) chambers, stirring in both chambers (entry 3) or without stirring (entry 4) are given. Evidently, the HMS production performance under stirring conditions (entries 1–3, that are forced convection) are much higher than that under non-stirring condition (entry 4, that is natural convection), whereas higher SO42− and HCHO concentration were observed under non-stirring condition (entry 4; Supplementary Fig. 24a). These results demonstrate that forced-convective mass transfer can strengthen reaction intermediates transport, thereby improving the C–S coupling efficiency. Moreover, the HMS production with stirring only in the cathodic chamber (entry 2) is slightly lower than that with stirring only in the anodic chamber (entry 1) or stirring in both chambers (entry 3). These differences may be attributed to the fact that cathodic stirring enhances the convection of HS− from cathodic to anodic side, facilitating S0 formation that interferes with HCHO generation, as evidenced by the formation of soluble Sx2− (the anolyte’s color was changed to yellow) and the sulfur deposition over electrode surface (Supplementary Fig. 24b). Furthermore, the electrolysis under different stirring speeds in anodic chamber were also studied (Supplementary Fig. 25). The reaction system (Fig. 4a), the membrane-free flow reactor’s configuration (Fig. 4b) and corresponding photographs (Supplementary Fig. 26) are shown. Specifically, O2 was fed to the cathodic gas chamber, and 6 M MeOH electrolyte was fed to the middle chamber. To facilitate the mixing of reaction intermediates (that are, SO32− and HCHO), the solution was circulated and stirred in the storage tank. In scenario 1 (Fig. 4a, up), five NaHS solutions with different HS− concentrations typically observed in industrial process were prepared to mimic sulfide-contaminated wastewater, ranging from 200 ppm (the level in refinery wastewater effluents) to 3000 ppm (the levels in spent caustic)36–39. The wastewater was introduced into the MeOH-containing electrolyte, fed into the flow reactor, and then flowed out from the middle chamber of the reactor. The current–voltage (i–V) curve was first tested in 0.5 M NaHCO3 electrolyte containing 6 M MeOH and 3000 ppm sulfide, covering a current density range of 0–200 mA cm−2 (Supplementary Fig. 27). Then, HMS-producing performance under different HS− concentrations was evaluated (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 28). The FE and productivity of HMS generally exhibited a volcano-type trend with increasing current density. For all the tested HS− concentrations, HMS FE can reach >100% at optimal current density. Moreover, the highest FE appeared at a higher current density when the sulfide concentration increased. Specifically, as shown in Fig. 4c, for a low sulfide concentration (200 ppm; grey curve), the highest HMS FE (108%) was observed at a current density of 2.5 mA cm−2, while for a high sulfide concentration (3000 ppm; red curve), the highest HMS FE (101.5%) was observed at a higher current density of 50 mA cm−2. Notably, low-concentration sulfide wastewater commonly exists in domestic scenarios, for example, municipal sewage, which generally contains only approximately 1–5 ppm dissolved sulfide. The paired electrolysis system was further applied to treat such low-concentration wastewater. By decreasing the applied current density, the system still achieved a good HMS FE of >70% (Supplementary Fig. 29). These results indicate the importance of synchronous optimization of sulfide concentration and current density to achieve maximum HMS FE, in agreement with preceding results in the H-cell study (Fig. 3f–h), providing operational guidance for treating wastewater at varying sulfide concentrations.

In addition, the influence of pollutants commonly co-exist in real sulfide-contaminated wastewater on HMS-producing performance was investigated. The examined pollutants covered three major categories: common anions (chloride, Cl−; nitrate, NO3−; and thiocyanate, SCN−), heavy metal cations in tannery wastewater (chromium, Cr3+), and organic matters in paper mills (alkali lignin). Other species, such as cyanide (CN−), thiols and thioethers were not studied in this work owing to their high toxicity, which beyond the permission of the lab study. For the anionic pollutants (Cl−, NO3−, and SCN−), they were introduced into the HS−-containing electrolyte at a concentration of 1000 ppm. The electrolysis results show HMS formation, indicating that its electrosynthesis can tolerate these pollutant ions in real wastewater, although FE decreased from 101.2 to 67.9% (Supplementary Fig. 30a, b). Further analysis of these anion concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 30c, d), combined with control experiments in which each anion was introduced individually (Supplementary Fig. 31), suggest that SCN− could react with MeOH to form new product (4.47 ppm in 1H NMR), thereby interfering with the C–S coupling between HS− and MeOH (the in-depth mechanism is beyond the scope of this work but is worth investigating in the future). Cr3+ ions are typically present at concentrations of 1900–3000 ppm in tannery wastewater40,41. So we added 2000 ppm of chromium sulfate to the electrolyte to mimic actual wastewater system. Under alkaline conditions, insoluble Cr(OH)3 precipitated rapidly (Supplementary Fig. 32a) and was readily removed by filtration prior to electrolysis (this can be achieved by incorporating a filtration step in practical implementation). Consequently, the electrolyte after precipitation removal still delivered a high HMS FE of 94.5% in the subsequent paired electrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 32b), indicating that Cr3+ contamination can be readily managed with minimal impact on performance. For the alkali lignin organic contaminant, a solution with an initial concentration of 1000 ppm was directly added to the electrolyte of the paired electrolysis system. Comparison of the ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectra before and after electrolysis showed a marked decrease in the characteristic absorption at 280 nm (Supplementary Fig. 33a), indicating a reduction of alkali lignin content within the system. This may be attributed to direct anodic oxidation of lignin or indirect oxidation by H2O2 generated at the cathode42,43. Meanwhile, the FE of HMS decreased markedly to 34.3%. To mitigate this inhibition effect, the lignin concentration in the electrolyte was gradually decreased to 10 ppm, and after electrolysis the FE of HMS returned to a high level of 92.9% (Supplementary Fig. 33b). Given the macromolecular nature of alkali lignin, we consider membrane dialysis can be adopted in practical applications as a pretreatment step before paired electrolysis, to remove lignin macromolecular organic species with improved HMS production performance (Supplementary Fig. 33c). Collectively, these above results preliminarily show the feasibility of using a series of real sulfide-contaminated wastewater to produce HMS via the paired electrolysis, although further exploration of more robust system to tolerate the co-existing pollutants is needed.

Then, the capability of full conversion of HS−-contaminated wastewater was evaluated. Under conventional constant-current electrolysis (that is, chronopotentiometry mode; Supplementary Fig. 34), as the sulfide concentration reduces at prolonged time, HMS FE decrease from 101.5 to 61.7% owing to the preceding matching relationship between high (or low) sulfide concentration and high (or low) current density (Fig. 4c). Therefore, to maintain a high FE of HMS, a stepwise-current electrolysis was adopted (Fig. 4d), in which the applied current density was progressively reduced to dynamically fit with the reduced sulfide concentration during long-term electrolysis (0–240 min: 50 mA cm−2, 240–360 min: 25 mA cm−2, 360–540 min: 12.5 mA cm−2). As a result, 99.1% HS− conversion was reached in a 540-min electrolysis, with HS− concentration reducing from 3000 ppm (equivalent to 2912 mg L−1 of S) to 26.6 ppm (equivalent to 25.8 mg L−1 of S), a residual level approaching the current industrial wastewater discharge limit (1 mg L−1 of S)44. In the meantime, HMS was continuously produced with FE >90% throughout the electrolysis, with an accumulated production of 748 mg (Supplementary Fig. 35). Moreover, the post-electrolysis solution remained alkaline, in which sulfide mainly exists in HS− form, avoiding the risk of H2S gas leakage (Supplementary Fig. 36). For real-world applications, we envision that online pH monitoring systems are necessary to reduce this potential leakage risk (as discussed later).

HMS electrosynthesis from HS−-contaminated waste gas

Besides HS−-contaminated wastewater, HS−-contaminated waste gas is another important pollutant source from gas/oil reservoirs, which is worth investigating. In scenario 2 (Fig. 4a, down), H2S gas (5 vol% in Ar gas) was selected to study, considering that it represents a typical H2S ratio in gas/oil reservoirs (Supplementary Table 2). H2S was introduced directly into the cathodic gas chamber with O2 flow, which was readily captured by the alkaline solution (that is, NaHCO3 electrolyte) and then dissociated to HS− ions. The remaining reaction pathways were similar as those for HS−-contaminated wastewater conversion.

The reaction performance was tested at different gas flow rates (Supplementary Fig. 37). Near-complete H2S conversion was achieved across the tested flow rates. As the H2S flow rate increased, a higher current density was needed to maintain a high HMS FE and productivity, in accordance with the matching between sulfide concentration and current density for HS−-contaminated wastewater (Fig. 4c). Under an optimized flow rate (30 ml min−1) and current density (50 mA cm−2), a long-term electrolysis for converting H2S-contaminated waste gas to HMS was conducted. The electrolyte was refreshed every 17 h to maintain the electrolyte’s alkalinity to capture H2S. As a consequence, stable HMS production (FE: 91–102%, selectivity: 74–85%) and cell voltage (3.73–3.88 V) were reached over a total 102-h operation, with a cumulative HMS yield of 13.5 g (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 38).

Moreover, compared with previous reports on flow reactors using SO32− as the sulfur source, the paired electrolysis shows competitive HMS-producing performance (including both FE and productivity) under the same current density (Supplementary Fig. 39). Although the maximum HMS productivity in paired electrolysis (2281 µmol h−1, obtained at 100 mA cm−2) was lower than the state-of-the-art result (4194 µmol h−1, obtained at 1000 mA cm−2)3, we anticipate that the performance can be promoted by further introducing higher concentration of sulfide waste, given the positive correlation between HS− concentration and current density.

Techno-economic analysis and life-cycle assessment of paired electrolysis

To assess the economic viability and environmental impact of paired electrolysis, we performed a techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life-cycle assessment (LCA), respectively, based on the preceding results under conditions of H2S/Ar gas in a flow cell at a current density of 50 mA cm−2 (Supplementary Notes 2 and 3). The full-process model for HMS production comprises of feedstock supply, electrolysis, downstream separation, and purification units (Fig. 5a). Specifically, CO2 is first released from the electrolyte in a neutralizer and subsequently recycled via alkaline absorption. MeOH and most of the water are recovered in a flash tank and recycled. Then, the target product HMS is separated from Na2SO4 by crystallization based on their solubility difference and eventually dried. Taking a daily HMS production scale of 1 ton as an example (Supplementary Fig. 40 and Supplementary Table 3), the total production cost consists of capital, operating, and material (including catalyst and chemical reagent) costs. Among these costs, cell voltage, HMS FE, electricity price, as well as the price and lifetime of the BDD electrode are the key factors (Eqs. 8, 10, 11, 14, and 19 in the Supplementary Note 2). Further univariate sensitivity analysis based on their practically possible fluctuation ranges indicates that the production cost is more sensitive to the HMS FE, BDD price, and its service life (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Table 4). Notably, the cost of the BDD electrode constitutes the largest proportion of the total production cost (Eq. 34 in Supplementary Note 2). Nevertheless, as demonstrated above (Supplementary Fig. 16), the paired electrolysis system is compatible with a variety of anode materials. This flexibility allows the BDD electrode to be replaced with a lower-cost alternative, such as an MnO2 anode, thereby reducing the production cost to well below that of the industrial process and to a level comparable to that of previous electrochemical methods using SO32− as the sulfur source (Supplementary Fig. 41). Furthermore, we evaluated the profitability of the process under varying electrolysis performance parameters specifically HMS FE and cell voltage (Fig. 5c). The results reveal that a higher FE and a lower cell voltage are also crucial for lowering the production cost. Under the experimental condition with high HMS FE (in flow reactor, H2S/Ar flow rate of 30 ml min−1), high profitability can be expected (red star in Fig. 5c), thereby enabling both waste sulfide resource treatment and the high-value conversion of C1 feedstock (that is, MeOH).

Subsequently, we preliminarily calculated the energy consumption of this paired electrolysis system to be 38.4 MJ per kg HMS, which suggests a potential advantage over the conventional chemical HMS synthesis method (39.1 MJ per kg HMS; Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Figs. 42 and 43). Notably, the energy associated with electrolysis still accounts for 44% of the total system energy input (Supplementary Table 5), which is mainly attributed to the high cell voltage (~3.8 V) and the FE of HMS (Eqs. 7, 8, and 10 in Supplementary Note 2). In particular, the elevated cell voltage largely stems from the intrinsically high oxidation potential of the BDD electrode. In the future, the use of alternative metal oxide electrodes in place of BDD, such as MnO2, could reduce the cell voltage (2.42 V) and thus further enhance the energy advantage of the system (Supplementary Fig. 43). In addition, LCA was performed to evaluate the environmental impact of the paired electrolysis system (Supplementary Note 3). The results show that CO2 emission is the most prominent among all environmental impact categories, particularly arising from the separation and purification steps (Supplementary Table 6). These steps rely heavily on fossil fuel-derived heat and coal-fired electricity, contributing over 90% of total carbon emissions (Supplementary Fig. 44a), necessitating comprehensive optimization in future practical implementation. Nevertheless, the paired electrolysis system still offers a marked carbon emission advantage over the industrial method (Supplementary Fig. 44b), due to the use of renewable-powered electrochemical synthesis, underscoring the key role of electrochemical technology in carbon mitigation.

Discussion

In summary, we developed a paired electrolysis for HMS synthesis from HS− and MeOH, different from previous reports in which SO32− was used as the sulfur source. This difference broadens the scope of sulfur source for organic sulfur chemicals synthesis, since HS− can be widely from wastewater and waste gas. Moreover, this paired electrolysis expands the valorization route of MeOH (a key C1 platform feedstock). The paired electrolysis decouples HS− oxidation to SO32− by in situ generated H2O2 at the cathode and MeOH oxidation to HCHO at the anode, enabling C–S coupling to give HMS. This process circumvents S0 precipitation from HS− oxidation and also competition with MeOH oxidation in conventional anodic electrolysis.

In a membrane-free batch H-cell, optimal matching of sulfide concentration and current density, together with enhanced convective mass transfer, enabled efficient HMS production performance, reaching an HMS FE (102.8%) and comparable productivity (282 µmol h−1) under optimized conditions (50 mA cm−2, 0.05 M NaHS, with stirring in anodic chamber). In a flow reactor, it shows practical potential by continuously converting HS−-contaminated wastewater and gas into HMS. Regarding HS−-contaminated wastewater, the system can operate over a wide sulfide concentration range, from domestic conditions (<10 ppm) to industrially relevant levels (200–3000 ppm), with HMS FEs above 70% at optimized current densities. Even when treated with real wastewater containing impurities (including Cl−, NO3−, SCN−, and Cr3+), a good HMS FE above 67.9% was still reached. For H2S-contaminated gas, high H2S conversion (87–93%) and HMS production efficiency (FE: 91–102%, selectivity: 74–85%) and stable operation over 102 h were attained. Moreover, the TEA and LCA conducted on the H2S waste gas conversion case indicated this system showed marked economic and carbon emission advantages over the conventional chemical synthesis method.

Despite these promising results, there is still plenty room to advance and optimize toward more practical applications. First, for scalable operation under fluctuating H2S feeding conditions, electrolyzers should be integrated with online pH and sulfide monitoring, considering the different adsorption efficiency of H2S in electrolyte with different alkalinity and the correlation between sulfide concentration, current density and FE of HMS (Fig. 4c). Then, the electrolyte alkalinity and current density can be dynamically adjusted, by which H2S leakage can be prevented and a high FE of HMS is expected (Supplementary Fig. 45). Secondly, broader scenarios for such paired electrolysis are worthy of exploring, particularly treatment of low-concentration sulfide wastewater with practical significance. Thirdly, for economically viable deployment, the development of more cost-effective and more stable anodic catalysts is critical, and pilot-scale validation should be pursued. Lastly, to establish an idealized low-carbon route for HMS electrosynthesis from MeOH and H2S, besides paired electrolysis, other components in the total process should be taken into account for optimization, including green methanol supply, renewable electricity, and low-energy separation and purification technologies, together making a contribution to establish an idealized full-process low-carbon route.

Methods

Chemicals

All chemicals were used as purchased without further purification. Deionized (DI) water was used throughout the experiments. Sodium hydrosulfide (NaHS, 70%), sodium sulfate (Na2SO4, 99%), sodium sulfite (Na2SO3, 98%), sodium thiosulfate (Na2S2O3, 99%), cerium sulfate (Ce(SO4)2, 99%), N, N-dimethyl-p-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (C8H12N2·2HCl, 96%), and ammonium iron sulfate dodecahydrate (NH4Fe(SO4)2·12H2O, 99.95%) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). Methanol (CH3OH, 99.5%) and concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98 wt%) were purchased from TongGuang Fine Chemicals Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Hydroxymethanesulfonate (HMS, 99%) and sulfoacetate (SA, 99%) were purchased from Macklin Reagent Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3, 99.5%) was purchased from Greagent Reagent Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Oxygen (O2, 99.999%), 5% vol H2S/Ar, and 5% vol H2S/CH4 were purchased from Air Liquide Co. Ltd (Tianjin, China). Gas diffusion electrode (GDE, SGL Carbon Sigracet), bipolar membrane (FBM-PK, thickness 130 μm), and anion exchange membrane (FAA-3-50, thickness 50 μm) were purchased from Shengernuo Co. Ltd. (Suzhou, China).

Preparation of electrodes

The dimensionally stable electrodes (including BDD electrode, PbO2 electrode, SnSb/Ti electrode, and IrTa/Ti electrode), GDE electrode, and Pt sheet were all commercially purchased without any subsequent treatment or modification. The Pt/C was a commercially available catalyst with a Pt content of 5 wt%. For the CoSx and NiSx electrode, their preparation methods followed the synthetic procedures reported in the literature for the anodic sulfide oxidation27,47. Similarly, the preparation of Ni(OH)2, MnO2, and Ru/C catalysts adopted the established methods reported in the literature for the anodic alcohol oxidation4,30,32,48.

Materials characterization

Scanning electron microscopy images were obtained by S4800 microscopy. X-ray diffraction patterns were recorded on Bruker D8-Focus using Cu Kα radiation in the range from 30° to 100° at 40 kV and 40 mA. The ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectra were recorded using a Shimadzu spectrophotometer (UV-3600).

Electrochemical measurements in H-cell

All electrochemical tests in the H-cell were conducted using chronopotentiometry in a three-electrode system. All measurements in this work were recorded without application of iR compensation. For the HMS synthesis via anodic electrolysis, the BDD electrode (1 cm2), Hg/HgO electrode, and Pt sheet were used as the working electrode, reference electrode, and counter electrode, respectively. Both the anodic and cathodic chambers were filled with 20 ml of 0.5 M NaHCO3 electrolyte and separated by an anion exchange membrane (AEM). The solution resistance was determined by the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy technique at open circuit potential in a frequency range from 105 to 1 Hz with a perturbation of 5 mV. The resistance of the 0.5 M NaHCO3 electrolyte in the H-cell reactor was measured as 3.1 ohm (±0.1) on average. The sulfur source (e.g., NaHS, Na2SO3) and carbon source (e.g., MeOH) were added to the anode chamber. Subsequently, various constant currents were applied to drive the oxidation reactions and the subsequent C–S coupling process. After electrolysis, the products were collected and analyzed.

For the HMS synthesis via paired electrolysis, the BDD electrode (1 cm2), Hg/HgO electrode, and GDE (1 cm2) were used as the working electrode, reference electrode, and counter electrode, respectively. Both the anodic and cathodic chambers were filled with 20 ml of 0.5 M NaHCO3 electrolyte without any membrane separation. The stirring with 500 rpm was applied at the anodic chamber. 100 ml min−1 of O2 was continuously fed into the cathodic gas chamber. Sulfur source and carbon source were introduced into the cathodic and anodic chambers, respectively. Subsequently, various constant currents were applied. After electrolysis, the products were collected and analyzed.

For the electrochemical H2O2 production in the H-cell, the BDD electrode (1 cm2), Hg/HgO electrode, and GDE (1 cm2) were used as the working electrode, reference electrode, and counter electrode, respectively. Both the anode and cathode chambers were filled with 20 ml of 0.5 M NaHCO3 electrolyte and separated by a bipolar membrane (BPM). O2 was continuously fed into the cathode chamber with a flow rate of 100 ml min−1. After electrolysis, the concentration of H2O2 was measured by the Ce(SO4)2 titration method using UV–vis spectra at a wavelength of 318 nm.

Electrochemical measurements in a flow reactor

All electrochemical tests in the flow reactor were conducted using chronopotentiometry in a two-electrode system. BDD electrode (4 cm2) and GDE (4 cm2) were employed as the anode and cathode, respectively. O2 was continuously fed into the gas chamber at the cathodic side. The open-circuit resistance of the flow reactor filled with 0.5 M NaHCO3 electrolyte was measured using a multimeter to be 0.9 ohm (±0.1) on average.

For the treatment of simulated sulfide-contaminated wastewater, 100 ml of NaHCO3 electrolyte containing 6 M MeOH and varying concentrations of NaHS was fed into the middle chamber of the reactor. The solution was then continuously circulated and stirred from an external storage tank. During the experiment, various constant currents were applied for 2 h, and the products were collected for analysis. To investigate the effects of co-existing pollutants on paired electrolysis, 1000 ppm of NaCl, NaNO3, and KSCN were introduced into the NaHS solution, and electrolysis was conducted under the same conditions as described above. For long-term continuous electrolysis, a step-current operation approach was adopted; the applied current was progressively decreased with prolonged electrolysis time until the sulfide concentration reached the target level.

For the treatment of H2S waste gas, an H2S/Ar mixing gas containing 5 vol% H2S was selected as the representative pollutant gas. The setup and operating procedures were the same as those for the sulfide-contaminated wastewater treatment system, except for the different sulfides feeding method. In this scenario, H2S and O2 were co-fed through the gas chamber inlet at the cathodic side, where H2S was absorbed by the alkaline solution and then recirculated. For long-term stability test, the electrolyte was refreshed every 17 h of continuous operation to maintain the alkaline environment of the system.

Products quantification

The quantitative analysis methods for various products are presented in Supplementary Fig. 46. The C–S coupling products, including HMS and SA, were detected by 1H NMR spectra (Varian 400) with water suppression: 800 µl of electrolyte was collected and mixed with 100 µl of deuterated water. Then 100 µl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added as an internal standard.

The oxidation products of MeOH, including HCHO and FA, were analyzed by HPLC (Agilent 1200 Infinity Series) equipped with a refractive index detector (RID): 100 µl of electrolyte was collected and diluted by 1000 µl of deionized water. The oxidation product EG was analyzed by 1H NMR spectra, following the same procedure as that of HMS and SA.

The oxidation products of HS−, including S2O32−, SO32−, and SO42−, were analyzed by ion chromatography (ICS-5000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a conductivity detector and AS11-HC column: 20 µl of electrolyte was collected and diluted by 1000 µl of deionized water. The quantification of sulfur (S0) generated at the anodic side comprises two components: solid sulfur deposited on the electrode and sulfur dissolved in the electrolyte in the form of Sx2− ions. For sulfur deposited on the electrode surface, the post-reaction electrode was immersed in deionized water to remove residual soluble species (e.g., NaHCO3), dried, and weighed. The mass difference of the electrode before and after electrolysis was used to determine the amount of deposited sulfur. For Sx2− dissolved in the electrolyte: 2 ml of electrolyte was collected, and excess of 0.2 M Na2SO3 was added. The solution color changed from yellow to colorless within a few minutes, indicating that zero-valence sulfur in Sx2− reacted with SO32− to form S2O32− through a comproportionation reaction. The resulting increase in S2O32− concentration determined by ion chromatography can be used to calculate the amount of dissolved sulfur. The concentration of HS− ions were determined by the methylene blue spectrophotometric method49,50. Thirty microliters of electrolyte was collected and mixed with 10 ml of 0.25 M NaOH solution, then 5 ml of 4 M H2SO4 solution containing 0.01 M C8H12N2·2HCl, 0.5 ml of 0.2 M H2SO4 solution containing 0.2 M NH4Fe(SO4)2·12H2O, and 20 mL of deoxygenated deionized water were sequentially added. After the solution color turning blue, the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 665 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer. O2 generated during anodic electrolysis was analyzed by an on-line gas chromatograph (Shimadzu GC-2014ATFSPL-230C) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector.
