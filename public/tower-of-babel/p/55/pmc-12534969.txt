Dedicated drug-eluting stents versus bare-metal stents for vertebral artery origin stenosis: a real-world single-center experience
Authors: Xiaoxin Sun, Lei Wang, Rui Zhu, Yanli Zhang, Xinxiu Liu, Zhiyong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn-stent restenosis (ISR) remains a major challenge following vertebral artery origin (VAO) stenting. Dedicated rapamycin-eluting vertebral artery stents were developed specifically for vertebral artery. This study aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of dedicated drug-eluting stents (DES) versus bare metal stents (BMS) in this anatomically complex lesion.MethodsIn this observational prospective, single-center cohort study, consecutive patients with symptomatic severe vertebral artery origin stenosis (VAOS) undergoing either dedicated DES or BMS implantation between January 2021 and December 2023 were enrolled. The primary efficacy endpoint was the 1-year ISR rate (> 50% luminal stenosis within or adjacent to the stent). The primary safety endpoint was 30-day stroke/death. Secondary endpoints included stroke occurring between 31 days and 1 year, symptomatic ISR within 1 year, all-cause mortality from 31 days to 1 year, stent fracture, and perioperative complications.ResultsA total of 217 patients were enrolled, including 150 dedicated DESs and 67 BMSs. Compared with the BMS group, the dedicated DES group showed Significantly lower 1-year ISR rates (14 [9.3%] vs. 25 [37.3%]; HR 0.22, 95% CI 0.11 − 0.45; p < 0.001), indicating a 75.1% relative risk reduction. No significant differences were observed in 30-day stroke/death (1 [0.7%] vs. 0 [0%]; p > 0.99) or 31-day to 1-year stroke (6 [4.0%] vs. 5 [7.5%]; OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.12 − 2.82; p = 0.49) between the two groups. Symptomatic ISR occurred exclusively in BMS group (2.6%, 1/39). Stent fracture rates were numerically higher with dedicated DES (13 [8.7%] vs 3 [4.5%], p = 0.60), with ISR occurring in 25.0% (4/16) of fractured versus 17.4% (35/201) of nonfractured stents. Mortality beyond 30 days was similar (1 [0.7%] vs 1 [1.5%], p > 0.99).ConclusionsRapamycin-eluting vertebral artery stents significantly reduced ISR rates compared to BMS in symptomatic severe VAOS, while maintaining comparable short- and mid-term safety. Stent fracture warrants further investigation as a potential modulator of ISR risk.Keywords: Vertebral artery stenosis, In-stent restenosis, Drug-eluting stent, Angioplasty, Prospective cohort study

Background

Posterior circulation ischemic strokes account for 25%−40% of all ischemic strokes and are associated with a mortality rate of 20%−30% [1, 2]. Data from the New England Medical Center revealed that approximately 20% of posterior circulation strokes are caused by lesions at the vertebral artery origin (VAO) [3, 4]. Endovascular stenting is recommended for patients with symptomatic vertebral artery origin stenosis (VAOS) refractory to optimal medical therapy [5, 6]. Despite high procedural success rates and low periprocedural complications, in-stent restenosis (ISR) remains a critical limitation of VAO stenting, with reported rates ranging remarkably from 11 to 67% and often resulting in nonprocedural ischemic events [7, 8].

The two most commonly used types of stents are bare metal stents (BMS) and drug-eluting stents (DES). According to most studies, DES are more effective than BMS at inhibiting intimal hyperplasia and preventing ISR [6, 9]. International guidelines state that DES is the preferred and recommended stenting strategy for patients scheduled for vertebral artery stenting [10]. Notably, since a dedicated stent for the vertebral artery was not available before, coronary stents and peripheral stents have often been used to treat vertebral artery origin stenosis, with relatively satisfactory results [10, 11]. However, off-label use could lead to medical and legal risks, and stent size mismatch cannot be ignored.

The rapamycin-eluting vertebral artery stents Bridge (MicroPort NeuroTech, Shanghai, China) and Maurora (Alain Medical, Beijing, China) were specifically developed for VAOS and were approved by China's National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) in 2020 [12, 13]. These dedicated DES, constructed from balloon-expandable L605 cobalt‒chromium alloy, utilize the high yield and tensile strength of the material to fit the tortuous vertebral artery [14]. Rapamycin’s antiproliferative effects significantly reduce ISR incidence by inhibiting smooth muscle cell migration and delaying endothelialization [12]. Furthermore, these dedicated DESs offer larger diameters of up to 4.5–5.0 mm to fit VAO lesions (typically > 4.0 mm), addressing a key unmet clinical need for larger devices [12, 13]. Despite the increasing use of dedicated DES in clinical practice, postmarket research is still lacking.

This prospective, single-center, real-world study was conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of dedicated DES versus BMS in patients with severe symptomatic VAOS.

Materials and methods

Patients

From January 2021 to December 2023, consecutive patients with symptomatic severe VAOS who were treated with either dedicated DES or BMS at Beijing Geriatric Hospital were enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged ≥ 18 years; (2) stenosis at VAO ≥ 70%, confirmed by digital subtraction angiography (DSA) according to the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial (NASCET criteria) [15]; (3) a history of vertebral territory transient ischemic attack or ischemic stroke within the previous 6 months despite treatment with at least one antiplatelet agent; (4) modified Rankin scale (mRS) score ≤ 2; and (5) at least one atherosclerotic risk factor, such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hyperhomocysteinemia, or smoking history. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) concomitant stenotic lesions in the intracranial vertebrobasilar territory and (2) active neurological disorders such as intracranial tumors, cerebral arteriovenous malformations, aneurysms, dementia, or psychiatric disorders. Baseline demographic data, preexisting vascular risk factors, procedural parameters, and clinical and angiographic follow-up results were retrospectively reviewed.

Procedures

The Neurovascular Intervention Center followed a standardized perioperative management protocol [10]. All procedures were performed by operators with at least ten years of neurovascular interventional experience. Patients received daily doses of 100 mg aspirin and 75 mg clopidogrel for at least 3 days before stenting. A loading dose of 300 mg aspirin and 300 mg clopidogrel was given if the procedure commenced immediately. A thromboelastogram test ruled out drug resistance, with clopidogrel-resistant patients being switched to ticagrelor (90 mg twice daily). Procedures were routinely performed under local anesthesia via a radial or femoral approach. Following puncture, a 6F sheath was inserted, and systemic heparinization achieved an activated coagulation time of 250–300 s.

V1 segment tortuosity (origin to C6 transverse foramen entrance) was assessed intraoperatively on pre-stent DSA by two independent neurointerventionists. Tortuosity included undulation (S-/C-shape), kinking (acute angulation), or coiling (spiral loop) [16, 17]. Disagreements were resolved by third-observer adjudication. This visual qualitative method aligns with standard clinical practice for procedural risk stratification.

The stent type (BMS vs. DES) was selected through shared decision-making between treating neurointerventionists and patients/representatives, incorporating patient-specific factors (economic capacity, bleeding risk/dual antiplatelet therapy suitability) and lesion characteristics (vessel diameter, calcification severity). The dedicated DES types included Maurora (Alain Medical, Beijing, China) and Bridge (Shanghai MicroPort NeuroTech, Shanghai, China). The BMS types included Carbofilm-coated (Alvi Medical, Italy), Trump (MicroPort Medical, Shanghai, China), and Apollo (MicroPort Medical, Shanghai, China). The stent diameter was selected at a ratio of 1.0–1.1 relative to the diameter of the normal lumen distal to the stenosis. The stent measured 4 mm longer than the lesion (2 mm proximal and 2 mm distal). Predilatation with a SacSpeed balloon (Achieva Medica, Suzhou, China) was performed for critical stenosis (< 2 mm), limiting stent delivery. In the case of incomplete stent apposition, optimal stent apposition can be achieved by postdilation. A control angiogram was performed immediately after the procedure. Quantitative angiography was used to calculate the degree of stenosis before and after stenting. Dual antiplatelet therapy was administered for 6 months after dedicated DES implantation and 3 months after BMS implantation.

Follow-up and study endpoints

The primary efficacy endpoint was ISR at 1-year follow-up. The primary safety endpoint was any stroke or death within 30 days after the procedure. The secondary efficacy endpoints included any stroke (ischemic stroke in the target vessel territory; ischemic stroke in other vessel territories; any intracranial hemorrhages) occurring from day 31 to one year postprocedure; symptomatic ISR within 1 year; all-cause deaths occurring between day 31 and 1 year; and stent fracture. The secondary safety endpoint included perioperative complications.

ISR was defined as greater than 50% stenosis of the luminal diameter within or immediately adjacent (within 5 mm) to the implanted stent [18]. The technical success of stenting was defined as residual stenosis < 30% after the procedure. Symptomatic ISR was defined as ISR associated with an ischemic event in the territory.

Statistics

On the basis of a referenced assumption of an ISR rate of 44% in the BMS group and 15% in the dedicated DES group [13], a sample size of 98 patients was calculated to ensure 80% power to detect a statistically significant difference between groups at a two-sided α level of 0.05. Accounting for the anticipated difficulty in obtaining angiograms at the 6-month and 1-year follow-ups, a dropout rate of 20% was assumed. Consequently, a total sample size of 123 patients was needed (62 patients in the dedicated DES group and 61 patients in the BMS group).

Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages). Continuous variables are presented as the means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges. Baseline characteristics and procedural data were compared via Student’s t test for continuous variables and the χ2test for categorical data. Survival curves were constructed via Kaplan‒Meier estimates, and the log-rank test was used to compare between-group differences. For the time-to-event outcomes of ISR and stent fracture, hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated via a Cox proportional hazards regression model. The probabilities of other endpoints were analyzed via multivariable logistic regression, yielding odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to determine risk factors associated with ISR and stent fracture during follow‐up. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

From January 2021 to December 2023, 282 symptomatic severe VAOS patients underwent stenting. After excluding 65 patients (4 with aneurysms, 25 with severe intracranial vertebral or basilar artery stenosis, and 36 lacking angiographic follow-up), 217 patients were analyzed (150 dedicated DESs, 67 BMSs). Mean age was 68.6 ± 7.3 (dedicated DES) vs 68.2 ± 7.3 years (BMS). The baseline characteristics were not significantly different between the groups (Table 1).

Follow-up and study endpoints

Table 3 shows the endpoint comparisons between the two groups. The mean follow-up time was 12.1 ± 1.3 months for the dedicated DES group and 11.8 ± 1.5 months for the BMS group. The ISR rate was significantly lower in the dedicated DES group than in the BMS group (14 [9.3%] vs. 25 [37.3%]; HR 0.22, 95% CI 0.11 − 0.45; p < 0.001) (Fig. 1), representing a 75.1% reduction. There was no significant difference in the rate of any stroke or death within 30 days between the dedicated DES and BMS groups. One patient with internal carotid artery stenosis in the dedicated DES group suffered anterior circulation ischemic stroke due to hypoperfusion. No perioperative mortality occurred.

No significant difference in stroke rates was observed between groups from 31 days to 1 year (6 [4.0%] vs. 5 [7.5%]; OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.12 − 2.83; p = 0.49). Compared with the dedicated DES group, the BMS group presented numerically greater rates of ischemic stroke in the target vessel territory (2 [3.0%] vs 1 [0.7%]) and intracranial hemorrhage (1 [1.5%] vs 1 [0.7%]), although these differences were not statistically significant. Of the 5 ischemic strokes in the BMS group during 31-day to 1-year follow-up, 1 case (20.0%) was causally related to ISR. Notably, this was the only symptomatic ISR among 39 total ISR cases (2.6%) in the entire cohort. By the end of follow-up, one death was related to pulmonary infection in the dedicated DES group, and one was related to intracranial hemorrhage in the BMS group (1 [0.7%] vs. 1 [1.5%]; p > 0.99). Dedicated DESs were associated with a greater stent fracture rate, but the difference was not significant (13 [8.7%] vs. 3 [4.5%]; HR 1.41, 95% CI 0.38 − 5.20; p = 0.60). In addition, the dedicated DES group had one major complication (myocardial infarction) and three minor complications (femoral artery pseudoaneurysm) within 30 days, whereas the BMS group had two major complications (myocardial infarction, pulmonary infection) and two minor complications (femoral artery pseudoaneurysm).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective report of dedicated rapamycin-eluting vertebral artery stents after their launch. Our study demonstrated that both dedicated DES and BMS achieved high technical success rates with favorable periprocedural safety profiles in the treatment of VAOS. However, for the primary efficacy endpoint of 1-year ISR rates, dedicated DES demonstrated significantly lower incidence compared to BMS. Notably, the proportion of patients with symptomatic ISR remained low in both groups. Furthermore, the occurrence of stent fracture represents an important consideration for long-term device performance.

The dedicated rapamycin-eluting vertebral artery stent was specifically designed for VAO anatomy. Unlike coronary stents, the Maurora stent offers extended sizing options including shorter lengths (as short as 8 mm) and larger diameters (up to 5.0 mm), thereby improving anatomical conformity to reduce migration and restenosis risks. The bridge stent uses abluminal groove coating technology, enabling targeted drug release that lowers restenosis rates while avoiding adverse effects on endothelialization. At the 1-year follow-up in our study, the ISR rate was significantly lower with dedicated DES than with BMS (9.3% vs. 37.3%; p < 0.001), corresponding to a 75.1% relative reduction. This reduction exceeds the 66% reduction observed in the premarket trial of the same DES (Maurora stent) by Si et al. [13], which reported rates of (DES 14.5% vs. BMS 43.8%; p = 0.001) in patients with symptomatic intracranial and vertebral artery stenosis. The difference in the magnitude of benefit between our study and the premarket trial is likely attributable to variations in study design and patient inclusion criteria. The 2021 postmarket Surveillance of 665 implanted stents reported a 6% ISR rate [19], which is notably lower than our observed rate of 9.3%. This discrepancy likely reflects our protocol of routine scheduled imaging surveillance for all patients rather than symptom-driven assessment alone.

In a study cohort of 301 patients with atherosclerotic extracranial vertebral artery stenosis, stent implantation achieved a 98% technical Success rate, and periprocedural complications occurred in 9.1% of cases, including primarily arterial dissection and distal embolization [20]. A meta-analysis of 12 studies (n = 1,243 patients) demonstrated no significant differences in symptomatic ISR, technical success rates, mortality rates, or perioperative complication rates between the DES and BMS groups [21]. Our study achieved a 100% technical success rate. The rates of stroke or death within 30 days and other perioperative complications were low in both groups, suggesting that endovascular treatment of VAOS is technically safe.

In patients with stenosis in the intracranial and vertebral arteries, ISR often leads to nonprocedural ischemic events, particularly intracranial artery stent restenosis [22, 23]. In contrast, some studies have shown that most cases of VAO restenosis are asymptomatic [7, 24]. Our study confirmed that while VAOS exhibited high ISR rates, symptomatic ISR occurrence remained relatively low (2.6%). This finding is consistent with the 1.6–8.0% symptomatic ISR range reported in prior studies, with no significant difference across different stent types [21, 25]. Stented-territory infarction following vertebral artery stenting was not associated with ISR but was significantly correlated with metabolic factors, particularly elevated HbA1c levels and preexisting diabetes mellitus. Ryu JC et al. demonstrated that stent-territory infarction following vertebral artery stenting is strongly associated with metabolic disorders but not restenosis [26]. These findings suggest that while symptomatic restenosis is uncommon, uncontrolled metabolic risk may promote both restenosis and recurrent stroke.

The relatively low rate of symptomatic ISR may be explained by the lower thromboembolic risk associated with neointimal proliferation than with atherosclerotic stenosis [27]. Furthermore, stent implantation not only immediately restores blood flow but also allows time for collateral circulation development, providing protection even when restenosis occurs [7]. However, the non-significant difference between the groups may be attributable to the relatively short follow-up period.

In addition to stent type, ISR risk factors in VAOS patients include patient-related, anatomical, and procedural variables [28, 29]. A smaller arterial diameter has been shown to increase ISR susceptibility in some studies [17, 30], but our study revealed nonsignificant differences in stent diameter. Other risk variables, including hypertension, diabetes, renal impairment, stent length, and vessel tortuosity, were not significantly associated with ISR rates.

Compared with stainless steel BMS, dedicated rapamycin-eluting vertebral artery stents adopt cobalt‒chromium alloys, which offer greater fatigue resistance and stability, potentially reducing the risk of stent fracture. Prior studies reported 1-year fracture rates of 4.7% for DES and 5% for BMS [31, 32]. Interestingly, our study revealed a higher 1-year fracture rate in the dedicated stent group (8.7% vs. 4.5%; p = 0.60). This finding has not been previously reported. Additional analysis by stent type was conducted to explore this issue. High V1 segment tortuosity is an established independent risk factor for stent fracture, as chronic stresses from straightening tortuous vessels can lead to strut fractures [16, 17]. Longer stents, overlapping stents, and overexpansion also increase fracture susceptibility [32–34]. Consistent with prior research [34], we found that postdilatation was a significant independent risk factor for stent fracture in the dedicated DES group. In our study, balloon dilatation was performed for incomplete stent apposition. The operators either increased the primary balloon’s dilatation pressure (between the nominal and rated burst pressures) or used a larger balloon. The standard stent Size used in clinical practice and in most studies is 1.0 to 1.1 times the normal lumen diameter [35]. However, a recent study on the optimal coronary stent size Suggested that a stent-to-reference vessel diameter ratio of 1.1 to 1.3 is more suitable, as angiographically estimated diameters are slightly smaller than actual arterial diameters [36]. Therefore, an optimal stent size and reduced postdilation frequency may lower the risk of stent fracture. In this trial, vessel tortuosity and stent length were not associated with stent fracture. This could be due to the use of shorter stents to bypass tortuous segments, thereby weakening the correlation between tortuosity and stent fracture.

Werner et al. hypothesized that stent fractures typically lead to ISR because mechanical disturbances trigger abnormal smooth muscle and endothelial proliferation [37]. However, we found no significant difference in ISR incidence between the fractured stents and nonfractured stent group (25.0% vs. 17.4%). This could be explained by the remarkable inhibitory effect of the rapamycin drug coating on the inflammatory response after stent fracture. The relationship between stent fracture and ISR remains unclear and warrants further research.

This study has several important limitations. First, the single-center observational design inherently limits causal inference and generalizability. Future investigations should involve larger, multi-center randomized controlled trials to validate findings. Second, blinding was not feasible due to the trial design, potentially introducing performance and detection biases. Third, BMS use progressively declined during the study period as evidence increasingly favored DES, resulting in a smaller BMS cohort and potential bias. Fourth, the inclusion of only Chinese patients may limit generalizability to other populations. Finally, while the mean angiographic follow-up of 12 months provides meaningful mid-term data, longer-term surveillance is warranted to assess delayed outcomes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that dedicated rapamycin-eluting vertebral artery stents significantly reduce ISR rates compared to BMS in symptomatic severe VAOS, while maintaining comparable mid-term safety. A certain proportion of stent fractures was observed with both stent types, indicating this risk requires clinical attention regardless of stent choice. These findings support the use of dedicated rapamycin-eluting stents for VAOS, while highlighting the need for careful follow-up. Further prospective, randomized trials with broader populations and longer-term follow-up are needed to assess the durability of the stent and longer-term patient outcomes.
