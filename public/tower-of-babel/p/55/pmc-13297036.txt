Causal Effects on Nonterminal Event Time With Application to Antibiotic Usage and Future Resistance
Authors: Tamir Zehavi, Uri Obolski, Michal Chowers, Daniel Nevo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTComparing future antibiotic resistance levels resulting from different antibiotic treatments is challenging because some patients may survive only under one of the antibiotic treatments. We embed this problem within a semi‐competing risks approach to study the causal effect on resistant infection, treated as a nonterminal event time. We argue that existing principal stratification estimands for such problems exclude patients for whom a causal effect is well‐defined and is of clinical interest. Therefore, we present a new principal stratum, the infected‐or‐survivors (ios). The ios is the subpopulation of patients who would have survived or been infected under both antibiotic treatments. This subpopulation is more inclusive than previously defined subpopulations. We target the causal effect among these patients, which we term the feasible‐infection causal effect (FICE). We develop large‐sample bounds under novel assumptions, and discuss the plausibility of these assumptions in our application. As an alternative, we derive FICE identification using two illness‐death models with a bivariate frailty random variable. These two models are connected by a cross‐world correlation parameter. Estimation is performed by an expectation‐maximization algorithm followed by a Monte Carlo procedure. We apply our methods to detailed clinical data obtained from a hospital setting.

Introduction

Antibiotic resistance, the ability of bacteria to survive in an environment containing antibiotic drugs, is a major public‐health threat, and has been linked to approximately 4.95 million annual deaths [1, 2]. The increasing rates of antibiotic resistance have raised substantial concerns about the future effectiveness of antibiotic treatments [2, 3]. Prior antibiotic usage has been repeatedly correlated with increasing antibiotic resistance rates [4, 5, 6], presumably as a result of evolutionary forces selecting for antibiotic‐resistant bacteria. As these findings might be confounded by many factors, causal effects of antibiotic treatment on subsequent antibiotic resistance are generally understudied.

The WHO AWaRe (Access, Watch, Reserve) antibiotic book [7] offers recommendations on antibiotic selection, dosing, route of administration, and treatment duration for common clinical infections in both children and adults. Access antibiotics have a narrow spectrum of activity, and they generally convey low future antibiotic resistance potential. Watch antibiotics are broader‐spectrum antibiotics, generally leading to higher future resistance, and are recommended as first‐choice options for patients with more severe clinical presentations or for infections where the causative pathogens are more likely to be resistant to Access antibiotics. Finally, Reserve antibiotics are those meant to be mainly used as last‐resort antibiotics for severe infections that are resistant to multiple antibiotics. While the consequences of antibiotic choice on future resistance are widely acknowledged, only a few studies formally study this causal question [8, 9]. Further methodological advances are essential to enable quantitative estimation of the causal effect of prescribing antibiotics.

The comparison between antibiotic treatments with respect to future antibiotic resistance is complicated by the fact that some patients may only survive under one of the antibiotic treatments. We embed this problem within the semi‐competing risks (SCR) data structure, also termed the illness‐death model [10, 11]. SCR data arise when studying times to two events, a nonterminal event and a terminal event. The terminal event can occur after the nonterminal event, but not vice versa. In our motivating example, death is a terminal event that precludes the occurrence of a future resistant bacterial infection. However, death is possible after a resistant bacterial infection, sometimes as a direct consequence of the newly acquired infection. When studying treatment effects on SCR data, challenges arise in defining and interpreting standard causal effects [12], similarly to the closely‐related truncation by death problem [13, 14, 15, 16].

Several regression‐based models have been proposed for SCR data, aiming to extract different information and tackle various challenges [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. These approaches directly model the realized event times distribution, and are not based on a nonparametric, assumption‐free definition of causal effects.

The causal effect of different antibiotic treatments on time to resistant infection is challenging to define. A simple comparison between the cumulative incidence of resistant infections up to a given time point might be misleading due to the treatment effect on the terminal event. For instance, an alleged lower resistance rate might be observed for a certain antibiotic treatment, even if all confounders are properly accounted for, simply due to higher death rates under this treatment.

Recently, approaches anchored within the causal inference paradigm have been proposed [12, 23, 24, 25, 26]. A common theme across this research is the reliance on principal stratification estimands [27]. We review several principal stratification approaches in the context of our motivating study in Section 3. These estimands are based on the notion that within a subset of the population defined by potential event times, causal effects are well‐defined. However, as these subsets are typically not identifiable from the observed data, it is crucial to assess whether the subset is meaningful. We contend that these estimands do not conclusively capture the causal effect of interest, as they overlook patients among whom a well‐defined causal effect exists and, importantly, is of clinical interest.

Therefore, we focus on a different, previously unexplored, principal stratum: the subpopulation of patients who would have been infected or would have survived within one year after treatment initiation under both antibiotic treatments. The one‐year mark is chosen based on subject‐matter considerations, though other time points can also be considered. We then define the feasible‐infection causal effect (FICE) as the causal effect among this subpopulation. We argue that in applications with SCR data, the FICE provides a meaningful effect to address questions about the causal effect on the nonterminal event. In the comparison of Access and Watch antibiotics, the FICE is defined among the subpopulation of patients for which the occurrence of a resistant infection is possible under both antibiotic treatments within the specified time period.

We consider two overarching identification approaches for the FICE. First, we develop a partial identification approach in the form of large‐sample bounds under new order‐preservation (ORP) assumptions. We present bounds under different assumption combinations and discuss the plausibility of these assumptions in our data. As an alternative, we derive FICE identification as a function of an unidentifiable cross‐world sensitivity parameter. Namely, we connect the counterfactual event times using a bivariate frailty random variable, where the cross‐world parameter is the correlation between the two frailty variables. We consider estimation via an expectation‐maximization algorithm (EM) [28], followed by Monte Carlo approximations.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe the motivating dataset. In Section 3, we review and compare existing approaches and estimands in the context of studying causal effects of antibiotic usage, and introduce our proposed estimand. In Section 4, we present the two identification approaches and discuss the plausibility of the identification assumptions. In Section 5, we describe the estimation methods for both proposed approaches. In Section 6, we present a simulation study to study our methods. In Section 7, we apply our approach to the motivating dataset. In Section 8, we offer concluding remarks.

Motivating Dataset

The data were obtained from Meir Medical Center, a secondary university‐affiliated hospital serving a population of approximately 600 000 people. The dataset included all 38 791 adult patients who had a positive bacterial culture based on samples taken from their blood, urine, sputum, or wounds, between 1 January 2015 and 22 October 2022. Each culture was tested for susceptibility to several antibiotic types, and the dataset included all resistance results. The dataset is similar to the dataset analyzed by Chowers et al. [8], only that the data we analyze in this paper covers a longer time period and is used to address different analytic objectives.

The dataset contains hospitalization records, including length of stay, antibiotic treatments, and culture results, along with demographics and comorbidities, and date of death. We define the baseline to be the first instance when a patient received treatment of either amoxicillin‐clavulanate or cefuroxime, two commonly prescribed antibiotics in Meir Medical Center, belonging to the Access and Watch classes, respectively. Patients who received both antibiotics were excluded from the analysis (2.5% of patients). To enhance clarity, we refer from now on to the antibiotics as Access (amoxicillin‐clavulanate) and Watch (cefuroxime) antibiotics. We remark that the Access and Watch groups of antibiotics contain many more diverse antibiotics, and our comparison is between amoxicillin‐clavulanate and cefuroxime only. Therefore, the causal effects we study in this paper compare amoxicillin‐clavulanate and cefuroxime. Such head‐to‐head comparisons are common in the literature of antibiotic treatments and resistance [8, 29, 30]. The treatment as defined in this study is the antibiotic assigned to the patient at baseline. We followed patients regardless of subsequent antibiotic changes and did not adjust for switching or additional courses because these are post‐baseline events that may be affected by evolving severity and could induce bias if conditioned on. Thus, our estimand reflects the effect of the initial antibiotic choice given at baseline (an intention‐to‐treat effect).

The outcome is the time to ceftazidime‐resistant infection, up to one year after baseline. We chose ceftazidime as it can be used as a marker for extended‐spectrum beta‐lactamase, which is a type of antibiotic resistance mechanism with important consequences for hospital infections [8]. A patient is considered to have acquired a resistant infection if they had at least one ceftazidime‐resistant culture result between five days and one year after baseline treatment. We focus on the one‐year mark because the association between antibiotic treatment and resistance decreases over time, and is negligible one year after treatment [6].

The antibiotic treatment was not randomized and may depend on confounders such as age, medical condition, previous antibiotic usage, and more. Our dataset contains only inpatient antibiotic usage, as is often the case with hospital‐based data. We excluded patients whose baseline infection occurred less than six months after the start of data collection, as for those patients prior antibiotic usage in the six months prior to baseline is unavailable. Supporting Information Table A1 describes the distribution of prominent variables among the Watch group (12 750 patients) and the Access group (5372 patients), categorized by various forms of demographic and clinical information. Patients in the Watch group were generally older; more likely to suffer from several medical conditions, such as dementia and chronic renal failure; and more frequently resided in a residential institution prior to admission. These characteristics of the data align with clinical standards, as physicians tend to prescribe Watch antibiotics to higher‐risk patients.

Within one year after baseline, the unadjusted resistant‐infection rates among those treated with Access and Watch antibiotics were 7.2% and 9.8%, respectively. The corresponding unadjusted one‐year death rates were 17.1% and 22.0%. At face value, these results might look puzzling, because Watch antibiotics are expected to be more effective in clearing out the bacteria causing the current infection, and consequently should prevent death. However, as discussed above, this is presumably due to confounding, because Watch antibiotics are typically administered to higher‐risk patients. Cox model‐based standardized one‐year death rates in the overall population for the Watch or Access antibiotics were 20.0% (95% CI: 19.3%, 20.7%) and 25.2% (95% CI: 23.8%, 26.6%), respectively. The resulting estimated risk difference and risk ratio were −5.2% (95% CI: −6.7%, −3.7%) and 0.79 (95% CI: 0.74, 0.84), respectively; see Supporting Information Appendix A for further details.

However, it should be noted that the positivity assumption may be violated in this dataset. Supporting Information Figure A1 shows the distribution of the estimated propensity score (PS), the probability of being treated with the Watch antibiotic conditionally on covariates, among each treatment group. For low estimated PS values, there was almost no overlap between the two PS distributions. Therefore, the above adjusted numbers are only interpreted as descriptive.

To mitigate this positivity violation, we focus on the causal effect among those treated with the Access antibiotic, as in Chowers et al. [8]. To this end, we use matching [31] to adjust for pretreatment differences in observed covariates between the antibiotic groups. Details on the matching procedures we considered are given in Section 7 and Supporting Information Appendix A. Supporting Information Table A1 presents descriptive statistics within the original and matched datasets. After matching, covariate balance improved substantially, as can be seen from the notable decrease in standardized mean differences between the two antibiotic groups.

Table 1 presents the event proportions among the 3722 matched pairs. Resistant infection rates were relatively similar in the full and matched datasets. In line with the results of the adjusted Cox model in the full dataset, death rates in the matched dataset among the Access antibiotic group were higher than those of the Watch antibiotic group.

Setup and Causal Estimands

Notations

Let A be the antibiotic treatment, where A=0 corresponds to the Access antibiotic and A=1 to the Watch antibiotic. Using the potential outcomes framework [32], for each unit i=1,...,n in the sample, let Ti1(a) and Ti2(a) be the potential times to resistant infection and death, respectively, had the antibiotic treatment been set to Ai=a. This formulation assumes there is no interference between units [33]. The units are also assumed to be independent and identically distributed, so we omit the i index when it improves clarity. We set Ti1(a)=∞ whenever a resistant infection would not occur within the one‐year period under treatment level .

Let Si(a)=1{Ti2(a)>1} and Ii(a)=1{Ti1(a)≤min{Ti2(a),1}} be the survival and the resistant bacterial infection indicators, respectively, within one year under treatment level a. Notice that Ii(a)=1 is equivalent to Ti1(a)≤Ti2(a). We term the strata {i:Si(0)=1,Si(1)=1}, and {i:Ii(0)=1,Ii(1)=1} the always‐survivors (as) and the always‐infected (ai), respectively, with corresponding population strata proportions πas and πai. Other subpopulations, defined by posttreatment periods other than one year, might also be considered. For instance, Xu et al. [24] and Comment et al. [23] have defined time‐varying always‐survivors subpopulations. Time‐varying subpopulations could be considered in our framework by redefining Si(a,r)=1{Ti2(a)>r}, and Ii(a,r)=1{Ti1(a)≤min{Ti2(a),r}} and then studying different subpopulations as a function of r. We defer the discussion regarding time‐varying subpopulations to Supporting Information Appendix B.

Existing Approaches and Causal Effects

We now review several approaches that have been proposed to address similar problems. First, if we ignore follow‐up after resistant infections, our application can be described as a competing risks problem, with the competing events being resistant infection and infection‐free death. The total effect Pr[T1(1)≤t]−Pr[T1(0)≤t] [34] contrasts infection rates between the antibiotic groups, without eliminating the competing event, that is, death. However, the applicability of the total effect can be challenging in practice, and its interpretation involves the effect on death. More specifically, low resistant infection rates under a specific antibiotic treatment might be due to high death rates under this treatment. That is, the total effect might quantify changes in infection rates due to mortality differences [34, 35]. Yet, it quantifies the additional number of hospitalized patients with a resistant infection resulting from the use of the Watch antibiotic, which might be useful for resource management, for example, for determining the number of beds and medical staff capacity. The controlled direct effect contrasts infection rates between two antibiotic treatments under the hypothetical intervention that also prevents the occurrence of death. However, such an intervention remains hypothetical, and is impossible to carry out when the competing event is death, as in our study.

Several authors have utilized principal stratification for SCR data to target causal effects among different subpopulations. One approach generalizes the survivor average causal effect (SACE) [13, 14, 15], the effect among those who would have survived regardless of their treatment. To this end, the SACE can be redefined to target time‐to‐event outcomes, while also recognizing that the nonterminal event may occur at different times rather than being measured at a single, prespecified time point. In our context, a possible estimand is SACE(t)=Pr[T1(1)≤t|S(0)=1,S(1)=1]−Pr[T1(0)≤t|S(0)=1,S(1)=1], for t≤1. The SACE(t) compares the resistant infection proportions until time t among the always‐survivors. Xu et al. [24] and Comment et al. [23] extended the SACE(t) to acknowledge that the terminal event may also occur at different time points, giving rise to time‐varying subpopulations (see Supporting Information Appendix B). The SACE was also generalized to certain competing events settings [36].

Recently, Nevo and Gorfine [12] studied the effect of a specific allele on late‐onset Alzheimer's disease (nonterminal event) and death (terminal event) times. They proposed another family of estimands, based on a different stratification. In terms of our application, this stratification divides the population into four strata according to the order of infection and death times. The always‐infected causal effect (AICE) is the causal effect on resistant infection time among the always‐infected (ai), that is, those who would have had a resistant infection within one‐year posttreatment, regardless of the antibiotic type. Formally, the AICE(t) is defined as AICE(t)=Pr[T1(1)≤t|I(0)=1,I(1)=1]−Pr[T1(0)≤t|I(0)=1,I(1)=1], for t≤1. The AICE(t) might be adequate when the main interest lies in the difference in the nonterminal event times, and researchers are unwilling to impose that patients will survive by the end of the study under both treatments. Within the always infected, one could also study the causal effect on the terminal event time [12].

Newly Proposed Estimands

A key issue when discussing effects based on principal or population stratification strategies is the population for which a causal effect is defined. The stratification enables focusing on a well‐defined effect, but if the studied stratum excludes certain subpopulations of interest, this effect might be noninformative. With this in mind, we argue that in our setting, neither the SACE nor the AICE are sufficient to capture causal effects under SCR data. Specifically, in the comparison of Access and Watch antibiotics and their effect on future resistant infection, these estimands exclude from their subpopulation patient types (see below) for whom resistant infection is feasible within the one‐year window.

Define patient type (pt) to be an index pt=1,...,16 representing the quadruple {I(0),S(0),I(1),S(1)}. Table 2 presents all 16 possible pt's; see also Supporting Information Appendix C for a concrete example of potential event times for each patient type. For example, pt=1 stands for patients who would have died without being infected under both antibiotic treatments, that is, {I(0),S(0),I(1),S(1)}={0,0,0,0}.

Both the always‐survivors and the always‐infected exclude patients who would have had an infection and subsequently died within one year under one antibiotic treatment, while surviving free of infection under the other. In Table 2, those patient types are pt=8,9. Clearly, there is a clinically meaningful causal effect among these patients, because one antibiotic treatment causes a resistant infection while the other does not. Furthermore, a resistant infection will lead to death for these patient types, highlighting the importance of appropriate treatment for those patients.

Furthermore, the SACE and the AICE are defined among subpopulations that exclude additional patient types for whom the resistant‐infection endpoint is feasible within one year under both treatment values. Specifically, the always‐survivors stratum excludes patient types pt=10,12,13. Those patients would have been infected during the one‐year span under both antibiotics. Hence, a causal effect can be defined among them, even though under at least one antibiotic, they would have died following infection. The always‐infected stratum includes those patient types, but excludes patient types pt=11,14,15. As these patients would have survived during the one‐year posttreatment period, a causal effect is well‐defined for them.

As a further motivation for studying a larger subpopulation, as targeted by the FICE, note that the one‐year ceftazidime‐resistant infection rates in the matched data were relatively low, with only 6.9% and 10.1% for patients treated with Access and Watch, respectively (Table 1). As we show in Section 7, these proportions suggest that the proportion of individuals in the always‐infected stratum is low. The SACE does not suffer from this specific problem in the motivating dataset. If, however, mortality rates had been high, then the proportion of the SACE‐targeted population would have been low.

To target a more inclusive subpopulation aligned with the clinical question of comparing one‐year resistant‐infection risk, we propose to focus on a new principal stratum. For a=0,1, let ios(a)={i:{Ii(a)=1}∪{Si(a)=1}} be the patient subgroup that would have acquired an infection and/or would have survived within one year after antibiotic treatment A=a. We propose to focus on the stratum of patients that belong to both ios(0) and ios(1), which we term the infected‐or‐survivors (ios). That is, ios={i:i∈ios(0)∩ios(1)}. We also denote πios for the population proportion of this stratum. As can be seen from Table 2, the ios subpopulation is more inclusive than both the always‐survivors and the always‐infected. It contains all patients that belong to at least one of these strata, and in addition, patient types pt=8,9 which are neither always‐survivors nor always‐infected. Looking again at Table 2, we see that the ios stratum covers patient types 8–16. A common feature of the excluded patient types is that for at least one antibiotic treatment a, these patients would have died without a resistant infection, that is, I(a)=S(a)=0.

The ios stratum can be interpreted as the set of patients “at risk” for resistant infection within one year under both antibiotic treatments. They either survive long enough for infection to be possible within the horizon or they experience infection before one year. Patients who would die infection‐free within one year under at least one treatment value are excluded because the resistant‐infection endpoint is precluded under that treatment.

Among the ios stratum, we propose a new estimand, the FICE, defined by FICE(t)=Pr[T1(1)≤t|ios]−Pr[T1(0)≤t|ios], for t≤1. The FICE(t) is the resistant infection risk difference by time t in the ios stratum. Because infection rates are relatively low, we also consider the analogous risk‐ratio scale estimands. For instance, the risk ratio FICE(t) is defined by Pr[T1(1)≤t|ios]/Pr[T1(0)≤t|ios]. In Supporting Information Appendix B, we discuss causal effects among time‐varying subpopulations [23, 24]. We further discuss the policy relevance of the FICE in Section 8.

Comparison Between the Estimands

In this section, we illustrate and compare the characteristics of the various estimands using a synthetic data‐generating mechanism (DGM). The DGM was based on Markov Cox cause‐specific hazard models with patient‐level frailty terms for each antibiotic treatment level [11, 12]. Sections 4.2 and 5 provide further information on these models. An additional scenario and the corresponding parameter values for each scenario are given in Supporting Information Appendix D. The parameter values of the scenario presented here were chosen such that infection and mortality rates would roughly reflect the proportions of the events in the motivating dataset.

We generated datasets with n=30000000 with one continuous covariate. Data were created with Weibull baseline hazards, and a bivariate Gamma frailty (random effect) model, with means one, variances θ, and a parameter ρ indicating the correlation between the frailty random effects under each treatment value. Loosely, ρ=0 means that the event times under different treatments are independent (conditionally on covariates), while values away from zero induce cross‐world dependence. Large θ values capture within‐world dependence between T1(a) and T2(a), and may also affect the cross‐world dependence, depending on the value of ρ.

Figure 1 compares the FICE(t), the SACE(t) and the AICE(t). We considered several ρ and θ values. As ρ increased, both the FICE(t) and the SACE(t) were smaller for all t values. Further, the estimands varied more with ρ at higher θ values. The trajectories of FICE(t) and SACE(t) over time exhibited similar patterns, both demonstrating monotone increases. In contrast, trends in the AICE(t) diverged from those of the other estimands. At t=1 the value of the AICE(t) is zero as expected, because all patients in the always‐infected stratum experience a resistant infection within one year after treatment.

Comparison of different estimands in simulated data over one year following antibiotic treatment.

The similarities in trends and in magnitude between the FICE(t) and the SACE(t), and their distinction from the AICE(t), resulted from the fact that the ios stratum contained more always‐survivors than always‐infected, due to the substantially higher proportion of the former. The always‐infected proportion was approximately 1% – 1.5%, whereas the always‐survivors proportion range was 74%−78%. The ios proportion exceeded that of always‐survivors by roughly 2%−3%. This phenomenon highlights that it is essential for researchers to identify and estimate these proportions and to assess membership overlap across strata when examining the behavior of the estimands.

Identification of the FICE

In this section, we describe our two proposed identification approaches. Our first identification approach constructs large‐sample bounds for the FICE(t) under relatively weak assumptions. We compare different types of assumptions and the obtained bounds in Section 4.1. Our second approach, presented in Section 4.2, provides identification up to an unidentifiable sensitivity parameter representing cross‐world dependence within a bivariate random effects framework. The results we present in this section can be extended and proven for the more general case of time‐varying subpopulations. In Supporting Information Appendix E, we discuss the required adjustments for constructing bounds under the general setup, while identification under the frailty assumptions requires no further adjustments. We also present the results for the risk‐ratio scale estimands and all the identification proofs in that appendix.

We assume consistency, so for each patient i, the observed outcomes are Ti1=Ti1(Ai) and Ti2=Ti2(Ai). In our dataset, follow‐up within the first year after treatment assignment is complete for nearly all patients (censoring rate before the one‐year mark was approximately 8.2%). We therefore focus on the case of no right censoring, and account for it in the estimation. The identification strategy can be naturally extended to allow for censoring, similarly to Axelrod and Nevo [37]. Let T˜i1=min(Ti1,Ti2,1) and T˜i2=min(Ti2,1) be the observed times, and let δi1=1{Ti1≤1} and δi2=1{Ti2≤1} be indicators for the occurrence of a resistant infection and death, respectively, during follow‐up.

When selecting antibiotic treatment, physicians take into account the patient's clinical status. For instance, patients classified as high‐risk are more likely to receive the Watch antibiotic. Therefore, in our motivating problem, we cannot assume that the antibiotic treatment was allocated randomly. We instead leverage the detailed dataset and assume conditional exchangeability (CE), that is, A⊥{T1(a),T2(a)}|X, for a=0,1, where X is a column vector of observed confounders. Similarly to the identification of the SACE and the AICE, which often requires additional assumptions, consistency and CE do not suffice for FICE identification, and additional assumptions are required.

To summarize, the observed data for each patient i are (XiT,Ai,T˜i1,δi1,T˜i2,δi2). For any event 𝒬, let Ψ𝒬(t)=Pr{T1≤t}∪{T2>t}|𝒬 be the conditional probability of being infected by time t, surviving beyond time t, or both. To avoid clutter, we also let Ψ𝒬=Ψ𝒬(1). For j=1,2, let Fj|𝒬(t)=PrTj≤t|𝒬 be the conditional probability of the j‐th event (infection or death), occurring before time t. Finally, denote Sj|𝒬(t)=1−Fj|𝒬(t).

Bounds Under ORP‐Type Assumptions

We start by presenting our partial identification approach, in the form of large‐sample bounds for the FICE. To provide motivation for the different assumptions, we revisit our clinical context. Watch antibiotics are aimed at clearing the more severe infections, and consequently, within the same population, they are expected to increase survival probability, compared with Access antibiotics. Concurrently, this major benefit is not necessarily devoid of associated clinical costs, as Watch antibiotics are also more likely to generate future antibiotic resistance, as a result of ecological and evolutionary forces selecting for antibiotic‐resistant bacteria.

Principal stratum effect identification often relies on monotonicity‐type assumptions. In our notation, the monotonicity assumption is Si(0)≤Si(1), for all i. That is, all patients who would have survived beyond one year under the Access antibiotic would also survive under the Watch antibiotic. Monotonicity within a short time frame, during the hospital stay, might seem plausible; a baseline life‐threatening infection cleared by the Access antibiotic would also be cleared when treated with the more aggressive Watch antibiotic. Monotonicity will exclude, for example, individuals of patient type pt=3 (Table 2), which are indeed not expected to be part of the population. Such patients would have survived only under the Access antibiotic (S(0)=1,S(1)=0), but their death under the Watch antibiotic is not due to a new resistant infection (I(0)=0,I(1)=0).

However, when considering the wider one‐year time window, monotonicity is no longer plausible, because the Watch antibiotic may lead to a subsequent resistant infection, which may lead to death. This would be the case for pt=9, which belongs to the ios stratum. Of note is that for the SACE, monotonicity alone does not imply point identification [13, 14, 15].

Therefore, we consider assumptions of a different nature that also respect the SCR nature of the data. Our approach extends the approach of Nevo and Gorfine [12], who considered the following order preservation assumption (ORP).

In our application, ORP implies that the Watch antibiotic cannot prevent a future resistant infection, including by causing death before such an infection would occur. We focus on bounds under more subtle assumptions for two reasons. First, as previously explained, patients of pt=3 are unlikely to exist in the population, and the ORP assumption does not exclude this patient type. Second, as seen in Table 2, ORP excludes from the population two patient types that belong to the target population (pt=8,14). Although it seems plausible to assume away the inclusion of these patient types in the population, we prefer to avoid assumptions on the target subpopulation itself.

We introduce a new assumption, which we term the infected or survivors order‐preservation assumption (ios‐ORP).

Assumption 2Infected or Survivors Order‐Preservation (ios‐ORP). ∀i:Ifi∈ios(0),theni∈ios(1).

Infected or Survivors Order‐Preservation (ios‐ORP). ∀i:Ifi∈ios(0),theni∈ios(1).

Under the ios‐ORP assumption, every patient that belongs to the ios(0) subpopulation also belongs to the ios(1) subpopulation. Namely, any patient who would have been infected or survived under the Access antibiotic would also have been infected or survived had they received the Watch antibiotic.

As seen in Table 2, the ios‐ORP assumption excludes patient types pt=2,3,6. The logic behind excluding pt=3 has been previously presented. For pt=2,6, a future resistant infection occurs under the Access antibiotic treatment, while under the Watch antibiotic these patients would have died without having a resistant infection. Such scenarios are unlikely, because, as previously explained, the Watch antibiotic is more effective against the baseline infection (so death is less likely), but has higher chances of future infection.

The ios‐ORP assumption can be falsified by the observed data. Under ios‐ORP, Pr({T1(0)<1}∪{T2(0)>1})≤Pr({T1(1)<1}∪{T2(1)>1}). Therefore, under CE, we would expect that EX[ΨA=0,X]≤EX[ΨA=1,X]. If this inequality does not hold in the data, then either CE or ios‐ORP does not hold. Furthermore, this assumption should also hold in any subgroup of X, and thus can be checked within subgroups, as we illustrate in Section 7. Nevertheless, within small groups, the sample size might be limited, making definitive conclusions less likely.

In our data analysis in Section 7, we assume that for a=0,1, γa is a Gamma frailty variable, with a shape parameter θa−1 and a scale parameter θa. The vector γ is then a bivariate Gamma variable, with means one, variances θ0 and θ1, and the sensitivity parameter ρ is the correlation between γ0 and γ1. More generally, researchers can specify other probability distributions for γ.

In practice, researchers should compare estimators (discussed below) under different assumed values for ρ. The value ρ=0 implies cross‐world independence between (T1(0),T2(0)) and (T1(1),T2(1)) while nonzero values model different dependence levels, as captured by the correlation between γ0 and γ1. A ρ=1 specification implies the same frailty variable in the two illness‐death models, that is, γ0=γ1. We therefore recommend comparing the results under ρ=0, ρ=1, as well as values between these two extremes, if disagreement arises.

Semi‐Parametric Model and Estimation

Calculating the large‐sample bounds entails estimation of F1|A=a,X(t) and ΨA=a,X(t), for both a=0,1. This could be done using any regression‐based illness‐death model. Frailty random variables can be used to model dependence between T1(a) and T2(a), without modeling cross‐world independence. We describe one such approach at the end of this section. Notably, bounds estimation requires neither frailty models nor semi‐competing risks approaches, but can be done via the competing risks framework without including a frailty variable. Consequently, bounds estimation can be performed free of any assumptions concerning the underlying frailty distribution. The nonparametric percentile bootstrap is commonly used to calculate confidence intervals (CIs) around large‐sample bounds [38, 39, 40]. In some applications, the coverage rate would not be the desired one due to the kinks created by the maximum and minimum operators. For our bounds, pointwise bootstrap CIs are valid if, in the true DGM, the true bounds are away from these kinks. Developing alternative bootstrap methods when similar conditions fail is an active field of research, and other methods might be possible to adapt [41, 42].

Simulation Studies

We conducted simulation studies to evaluate the performance of the proposed estimator. The DGM closely resembled that of Section 3.4, and consequently the true estimands were similar. We compared the same six parameter specifications as before: θ=1,θ=2, and for each θ value we considered three ρ values: ρ=0,ρ=0.5, and ρ=1. Because the goal of the simulations was to study the estimation itself, we assumed the correct ρ. In each iteration, we first obtained estimates for θ˜,β˜,λ0(·) by the EM algorithm, and then we estimated the causal effects by Monte Carlo simulations. SEs and 95% Wald‐type CIs were estimated by the bootstrap with 200 repetitions. The number of simulation repetitions was 500 for each simulation setting. The sample size of each simulated dataset was chosen to be similar to the matched dataset in the application and was n=4000. More details and additional results are given in Supporting Information Appendix G.

Convergence of the EM algorithm was achieved on average in approximately 140 and 4950 iterations under θ=1 and θ=2, respectively. The corresponding mean computation times were approximately 9 s for θ=1 and 330 s for θ=2.

Application to the Motivating Example

We applied our two identification approaches to quantify the causal effect of using cefuroxime, a Watch antibiotic treatment, compared with amoxicillin‐clavulanate, an Access antibiotic treatment, on future ceftazidime‐resistant infection. We stress that these results pertain only to the comparison between cefuroxime and amoxicillin‐clavulanate and not to a general comparison between Watch and Access antibiotics.

As discussed in Section 2, we first matched each patient in the Access group to a single patient in the Watch group, without replacement. Matching was based on the Mahalanobis distance with a caliper [31, 43] of 0.3 standard deviations on the estimated PS. Both the Mahalanobis distance and the PS model (Supporting Information Appendix A) included all covariates described in Supporting Information Table A1.

Due to the difference in the estimated PS distributions between the two antibiotic groups, only 3772 out of the 5372 (70%) patients who received Access antibiotic had a satisfactory match. Comparing the matched and unmatched patients within the Access group, there are notable differences (Supporting Information Table A2). Those who had no satisfactory matches generally had a “safer” covariate profile. For example, they were approximately ten times more likely to have their culture sampled from a wound (5.0% versus 52.6%), which in turn is strongly associated with better survival (HR^=0.48, 95% CI: [0.37, 0.64], Supporting Information Table A4). Presumably, this explains why these patients were nearly always treated with the Access antibiotic. Therefore, as a result of the matching, the causal effects we study only cover the population of patients who are treated with the Access antibiotic and are candidates for the Watch antibiotic.

Starting with the large‐sample bounds, the quantities needed for calculating the bounds were estimated by two frailty illness‐death models, estimated separately at each treatment group by the EM algorithm. In all analyses described in this section, SEs and Wald‐type 95% CIs were estimated by a matched pair‐level Bootstrap with 200 repetitions. Under the main matching specification, six repetitions (3%) failed to converge. Convergence of the EM algorithm was achieved in 572 iterations, requiring 272.5 s of computation time. Convergence criteria for the EM are described in Supporting Information Appendix H. The parameter estimates obtained from the EM algorithm were similar across different combinations of initial parameter values (Supporting Information Appendix H).

Let E^X(·) be the empirical mean over the distribution of X in the matched dataset. The estimated mean Ψ's were E^X[Ψ^A=0,X]=0.815 and E^X[Ψ^A=1,X]=0.874. The estimated ios stratum proportion under ios‐ORP was 81.5% (95% CI: 80.4%, 82.5%). The bounds for πios under weak‐ORP and without ORP assumptions were identical and estimated to be [68.8%,81.5%]. Because E^X[Ψ^A=0,X]<E^X[Ψ^A=1,X], the ios‐ORP assumption was not falsified by our data when looking at the entire sample. As ORP assumptions are defined at the unit level, we also evaluated the condition above within subgroups of a prominent variable, baseline sample location. The conditional ORP assumption appears to hold in most subgroups, with the possible exception of the patient subgroup where the baseline sample was taken from a wound. For that group, the difference was the lowest and close to zero, and the group was of size 184 pairs (5% of the total number of matches); see Supporting Information Table A8.

We also considered the analyses under ρ=0.5 and ρ=1, reflecting cross‐world dependence between the frailties. In the extreme case of ρ=1, the frailty variables are identical under either treatment value, that is, γ0=γ1 with probability one. The estimated stratum proportions were overall similar to those obtained under ρ=0 (Supporting Information Appendix H).

The full results are given in Supporting Information Figure A11. Generally, the effects were smaller for these ρ values, and the FICE(t) became statistically insignificant (at the 5% level) for later times, and specifically at t=1, but not at t=0.5. Under ρ=0.5, the estimated FICE(t) and the SACE(t) for t=0.5,1 were FICE^(0.5)=1.6% (95% CI: 0.3%, 2.9%), FICE^(1)=1.4% (95% CI: −0.1%, 2.9%), and SACE^(0.5)=1.8% (95% CI: 0.7%, 2.9%), and SACE^(1)=1.6% (95% CI: 0.3%, 2.9%). The estimated total effects were 2.1% (95% CI: 0.9%, 3.3%) for t=0.5, and 2% (95%: 0.7%, 3.3%) for t=1. Under ρ=1, the estimated FICE(t) and the SACE(t) for t=0.5,1 were FICE^(0.5)=1.4% (95% CI: 0.1%, 2.7%), FICE^(1)=1.1% (95% CI: −0.4%, 2.6%), and SACE^(0.5)=1.7% (95% CI: 0.6%, 2.7%), and SACE^(1)=1.5% (95% CI: 0.2%, 2.7%). The estimated total effects were 2.1% (95% CI: 0.9%, 3.3%) for t=0.5, and 2% (95% CI: 0.7%, 3.3%) for t=1. The total effects were about 17%–54% (for t=0.5) and 23%–82% (for t=1) larger than the estimated FICE and the SACE. This gap was more notable under ρ=1, as the estimated FICE and SACE were lower, yet again it illustrates that the total effect captures effects on future resistance that may operate through lower death rates.

We considered two alternative matching procedures: matching on the Mahalanobis distance with a 0.5 SD caliper (instead of 0.3 SD), and matching on the PS solely. The balance achieved for the former was nearly adequate, although not as good as in our main matching specification, while matching solely on the PS resulted in very poor balance (Supporting Information Table A3). Accordingly, the results for the larger caliper were overall similar to the main matching specification, while the results under PS matching only were close to the null, which could be explained via confounding patterns similar to the unadjusted analysis (e.g., higher death rate in the Watch antibiotic group) resulting from the lack of balance; see Supporting Information Tables A3, A9, and A10, and Figures A9 and A10.

In summary, while the large‐sample bounds are more informative under ORP assumptions than the bounds without ORP assumptions, they do not reject the option of a null result. In contrast, our frailty‐based analysis reveals that a causal effect of using Watch instead of Access on future ceftazidime‐resistant infection exists, especially in the first few months post‐antibiotic treatment. The estimated number needed to harm was approximately 54 (95% CI 30, 278). This means that for every 10 000 ios patients treated with the Access antibiotic, had we replaced it with the Watch antibiotic, we would expect about 186 more patients to acquire a resistant infection. The estimated ios proportion was high under both the ORP‐type assumptions and the frailty assumptions. This indicates that this subpopulation is large and well‐aligned with the clinical question of one‐year resistance risk.

Discussion

In this paper, we proposed a principal stratification approach to study the causal effect on the nonterminal event time in SCR settings. Our proposed estimand, the FICE(t), is defined in a new stratum in which a well‐defined causal effect exists and is informative. This stratum is more inclusive than previously proposed subpopulations. We derive FICE(t) bounds under new ORP assumptions, and provide a principal‐stratification oriented examination of the plausibility of these assumptions. As an alternative strategy, we conduct a sensitivity analysis that incorporates a frailty random variable, resembling the approaches of Xu et al. [24] and Nevo and Gorfine [12].

Our approach offers broad applicability to researchers in a variety of disciplines. One particularly urgent, yet understudied, public‐health problem to which it can be applied is quantifying the causal effect of antibiotic usage on subsequent antibiotic resistance. To demonstrate its utility, we apply the proposed method to our motivating dataset, estimating the excess ceftazidime‐resistance burden from Watch versus Access use. While in this application the focus was on the nonterminal event, causal effects on the terminal event within strata covered in this paper can also be targeted.

We used matching to overcome measured confounding. Because matching required adequate covariate overlap, our estimates pertain to the subset of Access‐treated patients for whom Watch therapy would have been a plausible alternative. Unmatched Access patients had a lower‐risk profile (Supporting Information Table A2), so our findings should not be interpreted as directly generalizable to the entire Access‐treated group.

Quantifying the effect of antibiotic choice on future resistance is an important yet challenging task. As a causal estimand, the FICE adds information about the magnitude of the effect, and while it only quantifies an effect in a subset of patients, it provides information on a large and clinically meaningful subpopulation. As is typically the case with principal stratum estimands, it is impossible to identify which of the patients are ios patients, so the FICE cannot be translated directly into a population‐level policy value. Nevertheless, we believe that studying the FICE constitutes a major step towards a causal assessment of the challenging to define and estimate, and clinically important question of the causal effects of antibiotics on future resistance.

Several avenues remain for future research. First, extending the framework to handle repeated or time‐varying antibiotic use. Second, developing full identification strategies for causal effects on the nonterminal event time under alternative assumptions. Specifically, assumptions whose violations can be captured by a sensitivity parameter, similarly to the approaches adopted by Ding and Lu [14] and Zehavi and Nevo [15] for SACE identification. While our methodology was tailored to address the causal effect of antibiotic usage on future antibiotic resistance, we believe it may also constitute a step toward strengthening principal stratification tools for studying causal effects in SCR settings more broadly.
