The use of glucocorticoids in immunoglobulin-resistant Kawasaki disease
Authors: Tong Tong, Yujia Wang, Zhe Lin, Yijing Tao, Jiawen Xu, Xiao Xu, Zhimin Geng, Songling Fu, Wei Wang, Chunhong Xie, Yiying Zhang, Fangqi Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The treatment of intravenous immunoglobulin-resistant Kawasaki disease (IVIGRKD) is still controversial, and few studies investigate using glucocorticoids (GCs) alone as rescue therapy for Kawasaki Disease (KD) patients. This study aimed to investigate the efficacy of using GCs alone as rescue therapy in IVIGRKD patients and to further explore the appropriate GCs dosage. We enrolled 210 IVIGRKD diagnosed in our hospital and conducted a cohort analysis. Patients were divided into the intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) group, IVIG + GCs group, and the GCs group. The GCs group was further divided into GCs A group(10-15 mg/kg/d) and GCs B group(1-4 mg/kg/d). Clinical outcomes and changes in coronary arteries after the treatment during a one-year period were observed. Propensity-score matching and logistic regression analyses were used to make the comparison. After propensity-score matching, the therapeutic response rate in the GCs group was significantly higher than that in the IVIG group (91.1% vs. 69.6%, P = 0.004). And there was no significant difference between the IVIG group and IVIG + GCs group and between the GCs group and IVIG + GCs group. We further compared various doses of GCs (10–15 mg/kg/d and 1–4 mg/kg/d) and didn’t find a significant difference. After logistic regression analysis, it was found that the therapeutic response rate in the GCs group was significantly higher than in the IVIG group (P = 0.001, OR = 2.24, 95% CI 1.37–3.67). Additionally, the therapeutic response rate was significantly higher in the IVIG + GCs group than in the IVIG group (P = 0.01, OR = 4.73, 95% CI: 1.40-16.02). However, there was no significant difference between the GCs and IVIG + GCs groups. Different doses of GCs (10–15 mg/kg/d and 1–4 mg/kg/d) were also not significantly correlated with the treatment efficiency. There was no significant difference in preventing coronary artery lesions (CALs) between different groups. Single-GCs therapy is a viable option for patients who do not respond to IVIG, and a dosage of 1–4 mg/kg/d of GCs demonstrated comparable efficacy to higher-dose regimens and may represent an effective treatment option for IVIGRKD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42083-0.

Introduction

KD) is an acute vasculitis of unknown etiology, mainly affecting medium-sized arteries and multiple organs and tissues during the acute febrile phase, with coronary artery lesions (CALs) as the most severe complication1,2. In high-income countries, KD is the leading cause of acquired heart disease in children. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) combined with aspirin is currently the first-line therapy for KD, reducing the inflammatory response and the incidence of coronary artery disease to approximately 4%3. However, approximately 10–20% of children continue to experience persistent or recrudescent fever at least 36 h and seven days after receiving their first IVIG infusion, a condition known as intravenous immunoglobulin-resistant Kawasaki disease (IVIGRKD)3. The mechanism is unclear, but patients with IVIGRKD are at a higher risk of CALs, and the incidence of medium-to-giant coronary aneurysms remains as high as 3.4% in China4. The treatment regimen for IVIG-resistant patients varies between institutions, and there is currently no consensus regarding the best option. The most commonly used treatments for IVIGRKD currently include secondary IVIG, IVIG + prednisolone(2 mg/kg/d), and infliximab3. However, due to the high price of immunoglobulin and infliximab, it is common to use steroids alone to treat IVIGRKD in our country. The use of glucocorticoids (GCs) as a second-line treatment in KD is controversial3,5. The dose and duration of therapy should be determined by disease severity and the degree of steroid reaction and dependence. Studies evaluating the effectiveness of these different options, including IVIG, steroids, and IVIG combined with steroids, are poor. Furthermore, the optimal steroid dose remains uncertain. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective cohort analysis to determine whether IVIGRKD patients may benefit from additional IVIG or IVIG combined with steroids. Additionally, we compared different doses of GCs, aiming to provide some references for the choice of second-line treatment of KD.

Treatment efficacy

Among the 210 IVIGRKD patients included in this study, the overall treatment efficacy rate to second-line therapy was 80.0% (168/210). The therapeutic efficacy rate in the GCs group was 1.31 times that in the IVIG group (91.1% vs. 69.6%, P = 0.004) (Table 2). The IVIG + GCs combination group showed a numerically higher treatment efficacy rate than the IVIG alone group (34/39 [87.2%] vs. 26/39 [66.7%]); however, this difference was not statistically significant after applying the Bonferroni correction. Additionally, there was no significant difference in treatment efficacy between IVIG + GCs and GCs. Furthermore, there was no significant difference between the GCs A and GCs B groups.

CALs incidence

CALs are one of the most important prognostic indicators of KD. Among the 210 IVIG-resistant patients, 82 (39.0%) presented with CALs. The incidence of CALs in children with effective second-line treatment was significantly lower than that in children with no response to second-line treatment (33.3% vs. 61.9%, P = 0.001). CALs were assessed at multiple time points, including before treatment, within two weeks post-treatment, and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months of follow-up.30.2% (19/63) patients in IVIG group developed CALs, 36.1% (35/97) patients in GCs group developed CALs, 56.0% (28/50) patients in IVIG + GCs group developed CALs, 41.8% (23/55) patients in GCs A group developed CALs and 28.6% (12/42) patients in GCs B group developed CALs. A total of 207 CALs developed during the disease, including 59 in the left main coronary artery (LMCA) (28.5%), 56 in the left anterior descending artery (LAD) (27.1%), 28 in the circumflex artery (Cx) (13.5%), and 64 in the right coronary artery (RCA) (30.91%). A total of 11 patients (5.2%) had giant coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs), involving 19 coronary arteries, including 2 in the LMCA, 11 in the LAD, 2 in the Cx, and 4 in the RCA. Adverse effect

No serious adverse effects occurred in any of the groups during the treatment period. And no significant differences were found in the incidence of adverse effects between IVIG and GCs, IVIG and IVIG + GCs, GCs and IVIG + GCs, and GCs A and GCs B groups.

Logistic regression analysis

Treatment efficacy

According to univariate analysis and clinical experience, whether the treatment was effective or not was taken as a dependent variable, including age, sex, treatments, CALs before treatment, the lowest level of albumin (Alb) , the highest level of aminotransferase (ALT), the highest level of WBC and the highest level of CRP to construct a multivariate binary Logistic regression equation. The variance inflation factors were all < 5, and pairwise comparisons were made among different groups. The results showed that the efficacy rate of using GCs as second-line therapy was significantly higher than that of using IVIG as second-line therapy (P = 0.001, OR = 2.24, 95% CI:1.37–3.67) (Supplementary Table 1). The efficacy rate of using IVIG + GCs was significantly higher than that of using IVIG alone (P = 0.01, OR = 4.73, 95% CI: 1.40–16.02) (Supplementary Table 2). However, there was no significant difference between using IVIG + GCs and GCs (Supplementary Table 3). Also, no significant difference was found between using 1–4 mg/kg/d GCs and 10–15 mg/kg/d GCs (Supplementary Table 4).

CALs incidence

Based on univariate analysis and clinical experience, a multivariate binary logistic regression model was constructed with CAL occurrence as the dependent variable.Independent variables included age, sex, treatment regimen, Alb level, and highest levels of ALT, WBC, and CRP. The analysis revealed that older age was associated with a lower risk of CALs (Supplementary Tables 5–8), while a lower Alb level was associated with a higher risk of CALs (Supplementary Tables 5–8).However, after adjusting for these covariates, no significant independent effect of treatment regimen on CAL incidence was observed (Supplementary Tables 5–8).

Discussion

In developed countries, KD is the leading cause of acquired heart disease among children. The most devastating sequela of KD is CALs6. It is believed that GCs are promising because they can suppress not only the immune response and inflammatory cytokines that IVIG alone cannot fully suppress, but also reduce the need for expensive IVIG re-use and the associated risks of repeated infusions, thereby reducing healthcare costs. However, the optimal treatment regimen for GCs is still undefined. The effectiveness and adverse effects of using GCs alone have been studied in limited numbers of patients, and GC dosage selection is left to individual organizations or clinicians. Our study directly compared the efficacy between GCs and IVIG, both as monotherapy and in combination, and also compared the difficacyof 1 and 4 mg/kg/d GCs versus 10–15 mg/kg/d GCs.

During the study period from January 2020 to December 2021, a total of 1,115 patients were diagnosed with and treated for Kawasaki disease at our center. Of these, 258 patients exhibited IVIG resistance [23.1% (258/1115)]. Numerous studies suggest that the incidence of CALs in IVIG- resistant KD is as high as 22%−45.8% 7; our study showed that 82 of 210 IVIG-resistant patients had CALs, an incidence of 39%. In light of the high incidence of CALs, it is crucial to find a more effective treatment for these children.

A retrospective study results suggest that GCs combined with IVIG for IVIGRKD may reduce the risk for CALs8. A meta-analysis by Chan et al.9 included twelve studies with 372 IVIGRKD patients and concluded that methylprednisolone (MPT) was not significantly more effective than a second IVIG infusion in reducing the incidence of CALs or subsequent treatment resistance, although MPT was associated with better antipyretic effects. On the contrary, Zhouping Wang10 et al. conducted a prospective RCT analysis and found that MPT was associated with a higher incidence of treatment failure and CALs than the IVIG retreatment group during long-term follow-up. Their study only compared the efficacy and safety of retreatment of IVIG therapy with MPT (15 mg/kg/d for three consecutive days, without a subsequent course and taper of oral prednisone). The differences between the above studies might derive from various doses and regimens. In our trial, retreatment with GCs (including 10–15 mg/kg/d and 1–4 mg/kg/d GCs, with a gradually tapering course) did not differ significantly from IVIG retreatment in terms of preventing the development of CALs at 6 months and 1 year.

Compared with normal-dose GCs, pulsed-dose glucocorticoid administration may inhibit cytokine generation and alter the activities of membrane-associated proteins, perhaps explaining why certain rheumatologic disorders can be effectively treated only by pulsed doses11. Previous studies have used a variety of formulations and dosages of GCs, but the effect of different doses of GCs on the clinical outcome of KD remains unclear because only a few studies have conducted such comparisons. Therefore, we compared different doses of GCs. Consequently, no significant difference could be found between GCs A group (10–15 mg/kg/d for three days, then tapering gradually) and GCs B group (1–4 mg/kg/d, tapering gradually), indicating that 1–4 mg GCs may be enough to exert a therapeutic effect without increasing side effects associated with higher doses. Similarly, Okubo et al. conducted a retrospective study and found a shorter length of hospital stay in the high-dose (methylprednisolone 10–40 mg/kg/day) corticosteroid group, with similar risks of CALs, readmissions, and total hospitalization costs between the normal-dose (prednisolone 0.5–4.0 mg/kg/day) and the high-dose corticosteroid groups for patients with KD in initial treatments12.

GCs are associated with several adverse effects, including sinus bradycardia, increased blood glucose, and increased blood pressure13. High doses of GCs may shorten the clotting time and increase platelet count, thereby increasing the risk of thrombosis. In this study, a total of six children developed coronary artery thrombosis, all of which occurred in the anterior descending coronary artery. Among these patients two had received IVIG + GCs, two had received 10–15 mg/kg/d GCs, one had received IVIG, and one had received 1–4 mg/kg/d GCs as second-line treatments. All the six patients received regular thrombolytic therapy. After thrombolysis, the thrombus resolved completely in five cases, while one patients had a persistent organized thrombus. No myocardial ischemia or myocardial infarction occured in the five cases during the one-year follow-up. In addition, the powerful anti-inflammatory effect of GCs may lead to immunosuppressive effects in patients, increasing the risk of infection. These side effects mainly occur in children with long-term and high-dose corticosteroids. In the present study, methylprednisolone was administered as a short-course regimen, and the main adverse effects were infections. No significant difference in adverse effects were observed among the groups, suggesting that the use of GCs does not increase the risk of adverse reactions when administered at appropriate doses.

Beyond the second-line therapies compared in this study, biologic agents represent a promising treatment option for patients with IVIGRKD. As noted in several guidelines, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) inhibitors, such as infliximab, are conditionally recommended for IVIGRKD patients3,5,14. A randomised, multicentre comparative effectiveness trial including 103 patients found that infliximab led to a shorter fever duration, decreased requirement for additional treatments, less severe anemia, and reduced hospitalization time compared with a second IVIG infusion, offering a safe, well-tolerated, and effective therapeutic option for IVIGRKD patients15. Other biologic targets are also under investigation, including interleukin-1 (IL-1) receptor antagonists and agents targeting interleukin-6 (IL-6)16,17. The strategic application of these biologics, particularly for patients at high risk of, or with, coronary artery complications, is an area of active research and represents a critical step towards personalized medicine in KD management.

However, the study has the following limitations. Firstly, as a real-world cohort study, the number of patients with missing data increased as the follow-up period was prolonged. When analyzing the occurrence of coronary artery aneurysms in children at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year, we performed a complete case analysis, including only those participants with complete data. This high attrition rate raises the possibility of selection bias, as the patients who were retained in the study may not be fully representative of the entire original cohort. Additionally, treatment allocation was based on parental preference rather than true randomization, which may have introduced selection bias. In order to analyze patients with similar baseline characteristics, we used propensity-score matching, which resulted in a reduction in the number of patients in both groups. Secondly, our included patients, after matching, did not have serious complications such as Macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) and Kawasaki Disease shock syndrome (KDSS), so in children without serious complications. The comparative effectiveness of GCs versus IVIG in patients with severe complications (such as MAS or KDSS) remains unclear and warrants investigation in future studies. Thirdly, since it was a retrospective study, the ultrasound reports were not reviewed by two individuals and were performed by different reporters, potentially may have led to bias.

To summarize, this study provides novel insights into the treatment strategies by comparing the outcomes between the additional IVIG, IVIG combined with GCs and GCs alone as a rescue treatment. Furthermore, we compared different doses of GCs. As a result, using GCs alone as the rescue therapy for IVIGRKD patients can be safe and effective when we consider their potential costs and possible adverse events, and GCs doses of 1–4 mg/kg/d may be sufficient.

Conclusion

Given that GCs alone achieved a higher response rate and effectively normalized CRP in IVIGRKD patients without serious adverse effects, this approach may be considered for patients who do not respond to initial IVIG therapy. Furthermore, a dosage of 1–4 mg/kg/d of GCs demonstrated comparable efficacy to higher-dose regimens, positioning it as an effective treatment option for this population.

Subject and methods

Subjects

Children diagnosed with IVIGRKD and hospitalized in the cardiology ward in our hospital from January 2020 to December 2021 were enrolled in this study. All the patients were Chinese, and our hospital was in the urban areas of eastern China. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee/Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2022-IRB-219).

Inclusion criteria

All the IVIGRKD patients diagnosed according to the 2017AHA Guideline were included3.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with a history of KD; (2) patients with contraindications for GCs; (3) patients who had received GCs for other diseases; and (4) Patients with incomplete data.

Other definitions in the study

(1) The diagnosis of KD was based on the 2017AHA Guideline3, including classical KD and incomplete KD. CALs were defined as Z-score ≥ 2.0 according to the criteria of Kobayashi Z-score adjusted for body surface area18. The measurements usually include the left main coronary artery (LMCA, theLAD, the Cx, the proximal segment of the right coronary artery (RCA prox), and the distal part of the right coronary artery (RCA dist). (2) The therapeutic response was defined as body temperature < 37.5 °C for 48 h and < 7 days after treatment and the disappearance of acute clinical signs associated with KD. (3) Patients whose fever does not resolve within 36 h after IVIG infusion has been completed were defined as IVIGRKD. (4) Liver function damage was defined as ALT > 50U/L. (5) Hypoalbuminemia was defined as albumin level < 30 g/L. (6) The diagnosis of KDSS and MAS was based on the article by KANEGAYE19 and RAVELLI20, respectively. (7) Adverse effect: Infection was defined as the occurrence of a new febrile illness (body temperature ≥ 38.0 °C) with supporting clinical signs/symptoms (e.g., cough, rhinorrhea, diarrhea) and/or laboratory confirmation (e.g., positive culture, radiological evidence), requiring the initiation of anti-infective therapy.

Study Design and Population

This was a cohort analysis of IVIG-resistant patients who met the enrollment criteria between January 2020 and December 2021(Supplementary Fig. 1). All patients were initially treated with IVIG (2 g/kg as a single intravenous infusion) and oral aspirin (30–50 mg/kg/day in three divided doses, after afebrile followed by 3–5 mg/kg/day, once a day). Patients whose fever did not resolve within 36 h after completion of the IVIG infusion were considered to have resistance to IVIG and subsequently received second-line treatment. The primary endpoints were treatment efficacy and CALs incidence. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of complications and adverse effects were compared as well. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies.

Data collection

This retrospective study included 210 children diagnosed with IVIGRKD at our hospital between January 2020 and December 2021, with disease onset defined as the first day of fever. Data collected encompassed demographic characteristics (sex, age, disease duration before initial and second-line treatment), laboratory findings, complications, treatment response, CALs detected on echocardiography, and adverse effects. Patients were categorized into five groups based on their second-line treatment regimen: the IVIG group received IVIG 1–2 g/kg; the IVIG + GCs group received IVIG 1–2 g/kg combined with GCs (GCs >1 mg/kg/d); and the GCs group was subdivided into GCs A (intravenous methylprednisolone 10–15 mg/kg/d, tapered to 2 mg/kg/d after 3–5 days, followed by a 50% dose reduction every 3–5 days after normalization of body temperature and CRP levels) and GCs B (intravenous methylprednisolone 1–4 mg/kg/d, with a 50% dose reduction every 3–5 days after normalization of body temperature and CRP levels). The groups were compared regarding complications, treatment response rates, CAL incidence, and adverse effects. To adjust for potential confounders and ensure comparability, propensity score matching was employed, and multivariate regression analyses were conducted to further evaluate treatment outcomes.

Laboratory tests

We collected the highest or lowest values of their laboratory tests, including WBC, Hb, PLT, CRP, ALT, Alb levels, before the first IVIG treatment, second-line treatments.

Coronary artery assessment by Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed before second-line treatment, within two weeks after treatment, and at one month, two-to-three months, six months, and one year of the illness.

Statistical analyses

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to identify whether variables had a normal distribution. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for data that were normally distributed, and median and interquartile range (IQR) were used for data that were not normally distributed. Independent sample t-test was used for continuous variables if the data were normally distributed, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used if the data were not normally distributed. χ2 test was used to analyze the categorical variables. Given the differences in the baseline characteristics between eligible participants in those groups (Table 1), propensity-score matching was used to identify a group of patients with similar baseline characteristics. Propensity scores were estimated using a non-parsimonious multivariable logistic regression model that included the following covariates: age, sex, presence of CALs, liver function damage, hypoalbuminemia, and highest WBC and CRP before 1 st IVIG treatment. Matching was performed with a 1:1 matching protocol without replacement, with a caliper width equal to 0.1 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Then, in order to further determine whether different treatments are the independent influencing factors of treatment efficacy and CALs, we carried out multivariate binary logistic regression analyses. For all regression models, a complete-case analysis was performed to handle missing data. Propensity score matching procedure and all statistic analyses were performed using SPSS version 26. All tests were two-sided. When comparing between IVIG group, GCs group, and IVIG + GCs group, Bonferroni correction was used, and a P value < 0.017 was considered statistically significant. When comparing between GCs A and GCs B subgroups, a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
