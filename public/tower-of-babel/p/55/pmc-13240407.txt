Breaking Barriers Between Species: Integrated Multi‐Species PK Models Improve Human PK Predictions
Authors: Miriam S R Happ, Jens M Borghardt, Charlotte Kloft, Hermann Rapp, Achim Sauer, Anneke Himstedt, Bettina Gerner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe accurate prediction of human pharmacokinetics (PK) is critical for compound selection and determining the human efficacious dose in drug discovery. Besides predicting the key PK parameters clearance (CL) and steady‐state distribution volume (Vss), the prediction of exposure metrics, e.g., maximum and trough concentrations (Cmax, Ctrough), are essential to evaluate efficacy and toxicity. Still, methods for predicting further PK‐shape‐defining parameters like absorption rate constant (ka), central and peripheral distribution volume (Vc, Vp), and intercompartmental clearance (Q) remain underexplored. Thus, this work aimed to improve the prediction of respective parameters, building up on a published PK‐prediction‐framework. The updated framework leveraged a multi‐species PK model, enabling the joint prediction of shape‐defining parameters by incorporating allometric scaling, while keeping established mechanism‐based prediction methods for bioavailability (F) and CL. The performance of the multi‐species model was tested for 40 small molecules with known human PK profiles and benchmarked against the literature framework, published alongside the data. The new predictions showed a reduction in geometric mean fold‐error (GMFE) compared to the benchmark for both the predicted PK profile and its shape, associated with an improved prediction of Q and late concentrations (C24h). The overall improvement was primarily driven by compounds, exhibiting inconsistent PK model structures across species when applying the benchmark method. Meanwhile, the prediction performance on other compounds remained comparable, making the integrated multi‐species PK‐model framework a promising approach for human PK prediction, especially in cases where difficulties in individual species model integration are faced or accurate trough concentration prediction is a priority.Keywords: compartmental modeling, cross‐species fitting, drug discovery, human PK prediction, pharmacokinetics, preclinical prediction, translational modeling

Introduction

The prediction of human pharmacokinetics (PK) is a critical step in the evaluation of new drug candidates, as it directly impacts the prediction of the human efficacious dose (HED) and the design of early clinical studies. Besides an accurate prediction of the key PK parameters clearance (CL), bioavailability (F), and steady‐state volume of distribution (Vss) [1, 2, 3], defining the overall drug exposure and the mean residence time (MRT), the prediction of the shape of the PK profile plays a pivotal role for drug efficacy and toxicity. By driving metrics such as the maximum concentration (Cmax) and trough concentrations (Ctrough), the profile shape relevantly impacts metrics commonly used for PK profile‐based predictions of drug effect or toxicity (see Figure 1). These include, for example, the area under the effect curve (AUEC) [4] or many classical pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) indices [5], commonly used for anti‐infectives (e.g., the time that the free concentration is above the minimum inhibitory concentration (fT > MIC) used for the majority of beta‐lactam antibiotics) [6].

A comprehensive overview on processes involved in the human PK prediction for new chemical entities (NCEs) alongside a suggested standardized compartmental modeling workflow has recently been published by some of the authors and evaluated against other published prediction approaches [2]. While the suggested framework led to high accuracy in prediction of the key PK parameters CL and Vss, opportunities to improve the prediction of the profile shape and thereby the prediction of related parameters remained.

The shape of the PK profile after extravascular administration, described by a multi‐compartment model (ncompartment ≥ 1), is characterized by the number of compartments and its parameters, including the absorption rate constant (ka), intercompartmental clearance(s) (Q), central and peripheral volumes of distribution (Vc and Vp(s)), and CL itself [7]. While prediction methods for CL and Vss from preclinical data have been extensively discussed [1, 2, 3, 8, 9, 10, 11], approaches to predict parameters relevant for the detailed shape of the plasma‐concentration‐time profile, such as Q, Vc, and Vp, remain largely unexplored. Therefore, this work places particular emphasis on improving the prediction of these parameters.

Within published frameworks of preclinical to clinical PK translation, human PK predictions are typically performed only as a second step following initial species‐by‐species assessment, by combining the per species derived PK parameters into the human predictions. This is current practice independent of the use of non‐compartmental [12], compartmental modeling [12, 13] or PBPK approaches [14, 15]. Within these approaches, drug absorption and distribution processes are generally assumed to be conserved/scaled across species and therefore extrapolated, e.g., by allometric relations depending on the body weight [16, 17, 18]. However, scaling species‐specific parameters does not account for the uncertainty associated with individual parameter estimation in the different species, which can introduce bias into the predictions for humans. For example, parameters estimated with high uncertainty due to sparse sampling in relevant profile sections, variability in measured concentrations, or limitations in bioanalytical sensitivity (e.g., a high lower limit of quantification (LLOQ)) may contribute disproportionately to the final prediction. Another challenge in this two‐step approach arises from potential application of different compartmental model structures. This either leads to difficulties in combining models with different structures, or biased/imprecise parameters from models with non‐optimal fits to the data, i.e., oversimplifications of the applied model for specific species (ignoring key distributional phases) or over‐parameterization. These challenges highlight the need for more robust approaches to integrate preclinical in vivo data and predict human PK parameters.

To address these limitations, we hypothesize that using an integrated multi‐species PK model, for which parameters are simultaneously estimated based on data from all available preclinical species, could improve the prediction of shape‐related PK parameters. This approach may reduce bias introduced by both data‐based uncertainty in specific PK processes and bias rooted in integration of different compartmental structures. The potential advantage of pooling data from multiple species into a single model has been previously recognized for the use case of “sparse and imbalanced” [19] data from preclinical in vivo experiments [19, 20, 21]. Furthermore, simultaneous multi‐species PK analysis based on interspecies scaling has been used for non‐modeling approaches established by Dedrick and Wajima et al. [16, 22] Various examples on multi‐species models for both PK of NCEs [19, 21, 23] and large molecules [24, 25, 26, 27], as well as physiological kinetics of glucose homeostasis [28] have been published in the past. While some of these investigations included an evaluation of the models' translational capabilities by comparing the predicted human PK against PK measured in human [19, 24] or other “unseen” species [23, 28, 29], these evaluations were conducted for single compounds. According to our knowledge, there is no systematic evaluation of the predictiveness of such an approach in the context of a first in human dose prediction across multiple compounds available in literature. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to:

Evaluate the feasibility of obtaining stable parameter estimation using a single multi‐species PK model across up to five preclinical species.

Incorporate and benchmark the integrated multi‐species PK modeling approach with the previously established framework by Himstedt et al. [2] and assess its performance, with a special focus on the predicted shape of the human PK profile for 40 NCEs.

By addressing these objectives, this study aims to provide the scientific basis for a complementary approach to predict human PK profile shapes, aiming to improve the accuracy of key PK metrics such as Cmax and Ctrough, thereby supporting better‐informed decisions in drug development.

Methods

Data Base

The integrated multi‐species model was developed and benchmarked on preclinical in vivo data of 40 NCEs, previously evaluated by Himstedt et al. [2] The dataset included plasma‐concentration‐time data from at least three different preclinical species per compound, typically comprising one rodent and two non‐rodent species, including peroral (p.o.) and intravenous (i.v.) administration routes, as well as in vitro experiments, i.e., hepatocyte stability and plasma protein binding. The dataset comprised a broad range of orally administered compounds from Boehringer Ingelheim's NCE pipeline, covering BCS classes I–IV, molecular weights between 250 and 750 g/mol, and plasma protein binding ranging from 28.4% to 99.1%. A detailed characterization of these compounds was published by Himstedt et al. [2] Compound numbering was kept consistent with the previous publication throughout this work.

In addition to the available information from preclinical data, results from non‐compartmental analysis (NCA) of human PK data collected in early clinical trials, alongside a pooled PK model of the respective clinical data, were utilized to evaluate the predictive quality of the multi‐species models.

Integrated Multi‐Species Model and Human Parameter Prediction

The integrated multi‐species model parameter estimation was performed using Phoenix WinNonlin (Version 8.5.2, Certara USA Inc., 2024), applying a PK model to the pooled data from all available preclinical species (see Figure 2, right part).

Schematic overview on differences between individual species model‐based human PK prediction as performed by Himstedt et al. [2] (left) and integrated multi‐species model‐based prediction method (right).

Allometric scaling principles [18] were applied for Vc and Vp (fixed exponent of 1) as well as Q (fixed exponent of 0.75 [9]) to obtain a single parameter estimate across all species (i.e., a single Q, Vc, Vp, and ka instead of e.g., three species specific Q values from the three species). While ka was generally assumed to be consistent across species, prior experience revealed that including minipig data in the estimation of absorption kinetics led to systematic mispredictions of ka [2]. The misprediction associated with minipig was therefore considered in the multi‐species PK model by fitting ka as a single parameter across all species except for the minipig, for which a separate ka parameter was estimated, that was later excluded from the human PK prediction. The estimated PK parameters from the preclinical species were extrapolated to humans by allometrically scaling Vp, Vc and Q to an average human body weight of 70 kg. No further scaling method was applied to the estimated ka (see Table 1).

Estimating a joint CL and/or F was considered scientifically not meaningful due to the differing biotransformation pathways and enzymatic expression across species [30]. Therefore, the prediction workflows for CL and F were kept consistent with the published method (described in detail by Himstedt et al. [2]), (see Table 1 and Figure 2, left part) while within the multi‐species model, CL and F were estimated separately for each species. These estimates were later not incorporated into the human PK prediction (for full model code example see Data S1).

Classical one‐, two‐ and three‐compartment model structures combined with different error models were explored during the fitting process for each compound. The best model fit was chosen based on the Akaike Information Criteria (AIC), parameter plausibility and precision (indicated by relative standard error (RSE)) and visual inspection of the model predictions to the preclinical data, based on species profile prediction and standard goodness of fit plots. Whenever insights from preclinical in vivo or in vitro data indicated the presence of more complex PK processes, such as non‐linear elimination or target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD), these processes were also incorporated into the multi‐species PK model while estimating related parameters individually for each species.

Benchmarking of Integrated Multi‐Species‐Based Human PK Prediction

To assess the quality of the multi‐species model‐based prediction, the predicted human PK was first compared to the observed human PK on a parameter level using fold error (FE) summary metrics,

and median fold error (MFE). Additionally, the percentage of predictions within 2‐, 3‐, and 5‐fold of the clinically observed values was calculated, alongside the bias

and the coefficient of determination (R2) of the observed and predicted parameters. To evaluate the impact of the integrated multi‐species method on PK profile shape prediction, both predicted and observed human PK profiles were simulated over 24 and 48 h (see Figure S1 and Figures 3), with analyses focusing on the profile GMFE. In addition, as this work also aimed to better understand whether the shape was correctly predicted, the respective profiles were also normalized by the average concentration (Caverage) to derive a so‐called shape GMFE. The overall shape of the PK profile is a result of multiple factors including the underlying compartmental structure as well as the interplay of Vc, Vp, Q, and ka. Therefore, a certain misprediction of parameters, such as Vc and Vp might not always have the same relevance, and instead the shape GMFE was considered to be a better measure for evaluating the quality of the prediction. The shape GMFE was calculated from the profiles by calculating the GMFE over all Caverage‐normalized concentrations for all simulated timepoints [2] (to, for instance, exclude the impact of systematic mispredictions of F).

Finally, the performance of the multi‐species PK model‐based prediction was benchmarked against the previously published framework by comparing simulated PK profiles and predicted parameters as well as the key PK metrics Cmax and Ctrough and total mean residence time (MRTtot) derived from both approaches.

Results

Across the 40 investigated NCEs (specified as #1 ‐ #40), convergence was successfully achieved for all estimated candidate models, enabling the identification of suitable PK models for all compounds. In total, two one‐compartment, 34 two‐compartment, and four three‐compartment models were identified by applying the multi‐species fit (in comparison to five one‐, 34 two‐, and one three‐compartment models with the original method). In most cases, parameters were estimated with an RSE below 50%. Only 17 parameters across all selected PK models were estimated with an RSE > 50%, with ten of these estimates not contributing to the human PK predictions (i.e., minipig ka, F or CL). The remaining seven estimates were distributed across six compounds (#5, #12, #14, #17, #21, #27) with most of the respective RSEs being < 60% except for one estimate of Vp (#5) that was associated with an RSE of 126%.

Overall, improvements in the prediction of the full human PK profile were observed, as indicated by moderate decreases in both profile GMFE and shape GMFE across the investigated compounds (see Table 2). While for 36 compounds, the average quality of the 24‐h profile predictions was comparable to the previous method (see Figure 3), 10% of the 40 compounds, specifically #10, #21, #25, and #33, showed substantial enhancements in the prediction of the 24‐h profile shape. This was accompanied by an overall more accurate and precise prediction (based on R2, AAFE, GMFE, MFE) of concentrations at 24 h (C24h) (see Figures 3 and 4 and Table 2). Furthermore, the comparison of predicted versus clinically observed PK parameters demonstrated a significant improvement in the Q/F prediction based on the integrated multi‐species approach (see Figure 4, top). This was reflected in a large reduction of AAFE from 9.22 to 3.27, alongside a large increase in the proportion of predictions falling within the 2‐, 3‐, and 5‐fold error ranges, and a moderate to small reduction for GMFE and MFE by 1.19 and 0.34, respectively (see Table 2).

Generally, the predicted MRTtot was consistent with values derived from the observed human PK. Vss predictions were similarly accurate as with the previous method, except for compounds #1, #5, #18, #20, and #32, which showed an increase in FE. From these exceptions, only #1 and #20 showed a relevant increase in predicted MRTtot FE, and while compound #1 did not show much change in profile GMFE and shape GMFE, compound #20 even improved in shape GMFE while the profile GMFE was slightly increased but remained within the 2‐fold range. Similar observations were made for Cmax, for which AAFE, GMFE, and MFE indicated comparable predictivity (see Table 2).

Some of the 36 compounds, that showed similar results applying the new method, had already been predicted with high accuracy using the previous method, therefore leaving little room for improvement (e.g., #16, #17 or #30). Other compound predictions deviated from the observed profile mainly due to a misprediction in CL/F in the original prediction method, e.g., #5, #9, #10, #18, #32. Thus, no large reduction in the profile GMFE could be expected for the respective compounds as, the prediction method for both CL and F remained unchanged compared to the previous method. The prediction of ka showed comparable performance to the previous method (see Figure 4, Table 2).

Further investigation of the compounds benefiting most from the implementation of a multi‐species model (i.e., #10, #21, #25 and #33, see Figure 3) revealed that differences could be explained by either an improved prediction of Q/F (see Figure 4), e.g., #21 and #25, correct identification of compartmental structure, e.g., #10, or adequate characterization of an identified peripheral distribution compartment (characterized by estimates of Vp and Q), e.g., #33. The underlying causes contributing to these originally worse predictions of the shape could be associated with the previously described methodological limitations when integrating individual species fits for human PK prediction, namely oversimplification or over‐parameterization due to differences in the distributional patterns of included preclinical species. Compound #21 will be used in the following to demonstrate such a case example:

Case Example of Integrated Multi‐Species Model‐Based Prediction for Compound #21

Concentration‐time data for rat, beagle dog, and cynomolgus monkey after both i.v. (see Figure 5A) and p.o. administration (see Figure S2) were available for #21, also known as BI 705564, a covalent, small‐molecule inhibitor of Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) that was investigated as a candidate for treatment of lupus nephritis and systemic lupus erythematosus [31]. A two‐compartment model with linear absorption and elimination and a combined additive and proportional error was chosen as the best fit when applying the multi‐species model to the pooled data of all species. The chosen model showed high precision for most parameter estimates, with only the Vp showing an RSE slightly above 50%, and a good visual fit to the data (see Figure 5A, blue). For the individual species PK, two‐compartment models were chosen for monkey and rat (see Figure 5A, red) as the best fitting model structure leading to high certainty in all parameters for rat and all non‐absorption related parameters in monkey. The p.o. data for the monkey was not used for the human dose prediction as parameters related to absorption (F and ka) were associated with high uncertainty and data were mainly measured below the LLOQ. For the dog, a one‐compartment model exhibited the best model fit and was associated with low RSE < 20% for all parameters, whereas the two‐compartment model was associated with high uncertainty for estimates of Vp, Vc, and Q (RSE > 300%). Nevertheless, the two‐compartment model was used as the final model for the human PK prediction, as both other species had shown a bi‐exponential decline in the concentration time profile.

(A) Preclinical individual (red) vs. multi‐species model fit (blue) depicted as a simulation of single intravenous administration in dog, monkey and rat vs. observed preclinical data after administration of the same dose of compound #21 (BI 705564). (B) Human predicted 24‐h PK profile vs. clinical model based on observed human data (black) for compound #21 (BI 705564) after single peroral administered dose using individual species model‐based prediction by Himstedt et al. [2] (red) and multi‐species model‐based prediction (blue).

The human PK prediction based on the individual species fit was performed by allometric scaling of the mean across species for Q, Vc, and Vss (based on NCA results). Vp was then estimated as the difference between Vss and Vc. Ka was also predicted as mean across species, while different methods for F and CL were chosen in accordance with the previously described workflow (see Table 1). This resulted in prediction of CL based on the human hepatocyte stability corrected by the mean ratio across preclinical species while F was predicted as the product of absorbed and hepatic fraction (Fabs, Fhep) based on NCA and hepatocyte in vitro data [2].

For the extrapolation to human in case of the multi‐species fit, Q, Vp and Vc were allometrically scaled from the model‐based parameter, while the model ka was kept the same. CL and F were predicted in accordance with the individual species fit based prediction from NCA and hepatocyte stability data. Simulation of the predicted human PK profile and comparison with the clinically observed PK model revealed a reduction of the 24‐h profile GMFE from 2.27 to 1.98 and an even stronger improvement in the shape GMFE from 2.73 to 1.28 (see Figure 5B). The prediction of the C24h also improved from a > 40‐fold underprediction to be within 3‐fold and the newly predicted terminal half‐life of 10.4 h is well in line with the reported apparent terminal half‐life of 10.1 to 16.9 h from the single rising dose study in human [31], in contrast to the previously predicted half‐life of 2.23 h. Looking at the parameter level, the prediction of Q/F has improved from 4.5‐fold error to 1.8‐fold error with a much slower Q. The high prediction of Q for the individual species model‐based benchmark prediction was influenced by the very high prediction of Q in dog with uncertainty of > 1300%, that was clearly not supported by the preclinical data for this species (see Figure 5A), but necessary to get the same compartmental structure to integrate the different species. Additionally, as depicted in Figure 5A, the distributional compartment modeled for both dog and monkey in the two‐compartment model differed from one identified in rat. The evaluation of the multi‐species model prediction for monkey and rat relied to a larger extent on the prediction of the data after i.v. administration, as the profiles after p.o. administration in monkey and for late timepoints in the rat were to a large extend below the LLOQ (p.o. fits of individual species models and multi‐species models are provided in Figure S2).

As indicated by the multi‐species model simulation of the PK profile after i.v. administration, measurement of later timepoints would have been necessary to identify such a compartment in dog or monkey. The multi‐species model is able to distinguish between this very early distributional phase observed in dog and monkey and the one in rat and is weighing the rat data more for the identification of respective compartment parameters. In contrast, the individual species model‐based prediction applies an average of both compartments, even if they are likely not representing similar distributional phases and estimates came with a large difference in uncertainty; therefore, resulting in a misprediction of the profile shape.

Discussion

This work presents the first comprehensive evaluation of integrated multi‐species models for predicting full human PK profiles of NCEs. By embedding the multi‐species model into a mechanistically focused PK prediction framework, we combined the strengths of mechanism‐based predictions for CL and F with the benefits of the integrated multi‐species fit. While other methods using simultaneous cross‐species PK analysis, such as the methods established by Dedrick or Wajima et al. [16, 22] rely fully on allometric scaling, the integrated multi‐species method also enables quality predictions for compounds that don't follow allometric principles in elimination. Differences that are observed, e.g., in Dedrick plots can be captured by the mechanism‐based prediction approach for CL and F established by Himstedt et al. [2] (see Figure S3), which has been shown to be superior to full allometric approaches.

Attempts to fit multiple species simultaneously using models incorporating interspecies scaling have been previously published for various therapeutic entities including peptides [24, 26], therapeutic mRNA [25] and small molecules. Some of these models have been evaluated against alternative approaches, such as single‐species PK modeling [26] and the classical two‐step method [20, 21]. However, to our knowledge, a systematic evaluation and benchmarking of this method for human dose prediction across a diverse range of compounds has not yet been published. This study addresses that gap by investigating compounds from different therapeutic areas and across various combinations and numbers of preclinical species.

Overall, the multi‐species PK model‐based prediction facilitated integration across preclinical species and contributed to an improvement in the prediction of the human PK profile, as indicated by lower profile GMFE and shape GMFE across 40 different small molecules. The method demonstrated a large improvement in the prediction of late concentration‐time points indicated by a reduction in GMFE of 23% while achieving similar quality of prediction for Cmax, MRTtot, and Vss. The improvement in profile shape predictions was primarily driven by a drastically improved prediction of Q/F. In addition, the integrated multi‐species approach more frequently identified relevant peripheral distribution patterns (compare increased sum of two‐ and three‐compartment models) by compensating for missing observations in key distributional phases, as has been observed in the case of compound #21 in monkey and dog (see Figure 5A). These changes resulted in improved predictivity of C24h and C48h while the predictivity of Cmax was comparable for both methods, which is in line with a small impact of Q/F on Cmax. This makes the integrated multi‐species model‐based prediction a promising approach, especially when Ctrough is a relevant metric for efficacy or toxicity.

Four compounds primarily contributed to the overall improvement of the predictions across all NCEs, addressing previous mispredictions caused by difficulties associated with integrating different compartmental structures between species or non−/misidentification of relevant peripheral compartment. Except for one compound, individual species fits of these NCEs were previously associated with parameter estimates of high uncertainty, which might provide guidance when this methodology really might result in beneficial predictions. By leveraging the multi‐species model, these uncertainties were accounted for while fitting the model across the entire dataset, resulting in PK models with largely reduced RSEs (see Figure S4). This highlights the challenges of individual species fit integration (differences in compartmental structures and high parameter uncertainties) as an important use case for integrated multi‐species model fitting. While the dataset comprised a broad range of chemical properties, other compound descriptors identifying strongly increased predictivity through multi‐species modeling, such as physicochemical compound properties or number/identity of species investigated, were not apparent. In addition to observing substantial enhancement in the prediction of compounds #10, #21, #25 and #33, the human PK prediction across the remaining 36 compounds was, on average, comparable to the one performed by Himstedt et al. [2], which previously demonstrated superior predictions compared to other available literature benchmarks. Few compounds exhibited slightly worse predictions of the 24 h profile based on profile GMFE, e.g., #4, #7 (see Figure 3). While some (e.g., #4) profiles were still better matched when considering simulations over longer time periods (see Figure S1), the observed variability resulting in slightly better or worse predictions was within a marginal, expected range. As the new method did not differ in the prediction method of CL and F, deviations in the predicted profiles caused by an over‐ or underprediction of CL/F remained, e.g., compounds #5, #9, #10, #18, #32.

Besides regular first‐order drug distribution and elimination kinetics, the investigated dataset included two compounds exhibiting TMDD (#39, #40) and one compound showing non‐linear CL in dogs (#15). Both processes could be accurately integrated into the multi‐species model‐based prediction, leading to equally accurate (#40) or improved predictions (#15, #39) based on shape GMFE and 24‐ and 48‐h profiles (see Figures 3 and S1). While such nonlinear PK characteristics were only exhibited by three compounds, results suggest that application of integrated multi‐species models may be useful even beyond classical NCEs exhibiting linear first‐order kinetics.

The multi‐species model could not improve the prediction of ka. In agreement, profile mispredictions previously caused by large error in predicted ka (e.g., mispredictions for Cmax of #36), remained unchanged. The limited predictive capability of the preclinical data for absorption processes is consistent with the results shown by Himstedt et al. [2] This is likely not a model related issue but caused by different formulations used in preclinical PK studies versus first in human trials, which consequently limits predictive capability of the model for the respective process (as can be observed, e.g., for #36). Additionally, the applied multi‐species model did not account for differences in plasma protein binding. A general correction of concentrations for the fraction unbound (fu) based on predefined conditions (i.e., the reduction of variability of Vss across species after accounting for fu: CVVss<CVVss/fu) introduced an overall bias into the PK prediction including prediction of the parameter MRTtot. Nonetheless, even when excluding the four compounds that benefited most from the multi‐species model approach, the method not including fu‐correction achieved comparable prediction quality as the benchmark, which accounted for plasma protein binding in certain compounds. While this does not negate the potential benefit of accounting for fu in the PK predictions for specific compounds. In fact, such a benefit has been shown for the prediction of both CL and Vss in other investigations [32, 33]. It demonstrated that the method achieved satisfying results even in the absence of such information. Meanwhile, further investigation is needed to determine the circumstances under which such inclusion of plasma protein binding enhances the overall PK profile predictions. Other PK‐relevant processes such as lysosomal trapping or active transport were not mechanistically incorporated in either PK prediction method. Instead, these processes are captured empirically by an increased volume of distribution or a decreased bioavailability. However, it needs to be highlighted that these processes need specific consideration e.g., when the target might be in the brain or the liver.

In conclusion, the integrated multi‐species model has demonstrated to be a valuable tool for human PK predictions of NCEs revealing comparable performance to the best available literature benchmark for most compounds and largely improving PK profile‐shape predictions for compounds associated with structural modeling challenges and parameter imprecision related to distributional parameters. This approach proved particularly useful when accurate prediction of late concentration time points is a priority or in the case that integration of individual species model‐based predictions encounters structural or parameter‐related limitations.
