A hemoglobin concentration dataset derived from triple-wavelength photoplethysmography for machine learning applications
Authors: Tomy Abuzairi, Diva Bintang Maharani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The commonly used method for measuring Hemoglobin (Hb) concentration involves invasive blood sampling, which has drawbacks such as discomfort and pain. To address these limitations, photoplethysmography (PPG) has emerged as a promising non-invasive alternative. PPG device is a non-invasive optical device used to detect blood volume changes within microvascular tissue beds. This device offers the capability to measure hemoglobin levels without the need for invasive procedures. In this article, the dataset consists of PPG signal, Hb value, age, and gender. The PPG signal was measured by a MAXM86161 module sensor that emitted triple-wavelength light (green, red and infrared). Data was collected from 52 subjects (60 % female, 40 % male), with Hb values ranging from 12 to 20 g/dL for males and 8–18 g/dL for females. Acquisition occurred from April to May 2025 at West Java, Indonesia. The public dataset on Mendeley Data includes raw PPG signals, filtered outputs, and peak detection results for each subject. This resource is valuable for researchers advancing non-invasive Hb measurement based on machine learning.

Background

Hemoglobin (Hb) is a critical protein responsible for oxygen transport and often measured invasively. The development of non-invasive and continuous Hb monitoring is crucial for clinical applications, particularly in resource-limited settings or for patient convenience. Photoplethysmography (PPG) is an optical technique used to detect blood volume changes within microvascular tissue beds. It operates by illuminating the skin with a LED light and measuring the intensity of reflected or transmitted light with a photodetector. Changes in blood flow, corresponding to the heartbeat, cause fluctuations in the detected light intensity. These fluctuations produce the characteristic waveform known as the PPG signal, which reflects cardiac cycles, peripheral circulation, and other physiological parameters such as heart rate, oxygen saturation (SpO₂), blood pressure, and Hb levels [[1], [2]]. However, single- and dual-wavelength PPG methods are often hampered by interference from skin tone and local tissue structure. The use of triple-wavelengths provides richer spectral information, which is theoretically necessary to better isolate the Hb component from other tissue variables. Despite this technical advantage, publicly available datasets containing triple-wavelength PPG signals paired with ground truth Hb values are scarce. This data article addresses this gap by providing a transparent and structured dataset specifically designed to accelerate machine learning research in non-invasive Hb estimation.

Data Description

This dataset comprises triple-wavelength PPG signals and corresponding hematological and demographic data from 52 subjects in Depok City, Indonesia, collected between April 14th and May 20th, 2025. PPG signals were recorded at green (λ = 535 nm), red (λ = 660 nm), and infrared (λ = 880 nm) wavelengths. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

PPG device

PPG signals in this dataset were recorded using the PPG device depicted in Fig. 2. The PPG device consists of a MAXM86161 module sensor (Maxim Integrated, California, USA) and 3-D print case. This PPG case is made in the shape of a fingertip with a total size of 26 mm × 104 mm × 32.5 mm using 3-D Print. The 3-D printed fingertip-shaped case houses all electronic components to optimize skin contact, minimize motion artifacts, and cover from ambient light interference.Fig. 2PPG device to measure PPG signal. (a) The PPG device with a user’s finger positioned for signal measurement. (b) Side view of the PPG device. (c) Front view of the PPG device. (d) Internal view of the PPG device revealing the electronic components and sensor MAXM86161.Fig 2

PPG device to measure PPG signal. (a) The PPG device with a user’s finger positioned for signal measurement. (b) Side view of the PPG device. (c) Front view of the PPG device. (d) Internal view of the PPG device revealing the electronic components and sensor MAXM86161.

The MAXM86161 module integrates three LEDs (green at 535 nm, red at 660 nm, and infrared at 880 nm), a high-sensitivity photodetector, a 19-bit analog-to-digital converter (ADC), and a 128-word FIFO buffer for accurate multi-wavelength PPG signal acquisition [3]. It operates at a low power supply of 1.8 V for the sensor and 3.0–5.5 V for the LED drivers, with a typical standby current of <10 µA to ensure energy efficiency. The module is paired with the MAXM86161OSBFLEX board, which includes a high-current LED driver and supports Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) connectivity via a Cypress CY5677 dongle for wireless data transfer. The system is powered by a rechargeable 3.7 V, 100 mAh lithium-polymer battery (LP-401230). Dataset collection

The MAXM86161 sensor emits triple-wavelengths: green (535 nm), red (660 nm), and infrared (880 nm). Fig. 4 illustrates the typical signal pattern measured by the high-sensitivity photodetector and converted by 19-bit ADC. Each recording lasted 90 s at the sampling rate of 100 Hz. The raw PPG signals were transmitted to a microcontroller via I2C protocol, facilitated by the MXM86161OSBFLEX board, which supports real time signal acquisition. Raw PPG signals from 52 subjects were exported and preprocessed into structured CSV format for downstream analysis.Fig. 4Typical PPG signal from MAXM86161 sensor.Fig 4

Typical PPG signal from MAXM86161 sensor.

The data collection for Hb concentration was conducted using a Hb meter, Nesco Multicheck 2® (Bioptik Technology Inc, Miaoli, Taiwan), and the process was performed invasively by taking a small blood sample. The blood sample was obtained by pricking the finger using a lancet. The blood was then applied to a strip provided on the device. After a few seconds, the device will display the Hb concentration value. This value was recorded and saved in CSV format, along with the previously obtained data on the intensity of triple-wavelength from the PPG device.

A consistent data collection protocol was followed for all subjects, involving several sequential steps: (i) providing a comprehensive explanation of the informed consent in the subjects' native language, (ii) obtaining written consent from the subjects, (iii) gathering demographic information, (iv) conducting invasive-based Hb measurement, and (v) recording PPG signals using the sensor.

Preprocessing

Data acquisition involved collecting raw PPG signals from the green (LEDC1), infrared (LEDC2), and red (LEDC3) channels. These raw signals underwent initial cleaning and structuring. This cleaned dataset, serving as the first dataset, contained key information for each participant, including a unique participant identifier, the sampling point number (acting as a timestamp), the raw signal values from the green LED channel, infrared LED channel, and red LED channel, along with their ground truth Hb concentration, gender, and age.

For the filtered dataset, these raw PPG signals underwent a multi-stage preprocessing to enhance quality for advanced analysis and modelling. This involved data imputation, where missing values were filled using both a forward-filling and a backward-filling method to ensure data continuity. Subsequently, a bandpass filter was applied within the frequency range of 0.3–10.0 Hz to effectively remove low-frequency drift and reduce high-frequency noise, such as those caused by motion or respiration. A notch filter at 50 Hz was also applied to eliminate external electrical interference.

Concurrently, the peak dataset was generated by performing peak detection directly on the raw PPG signals. All three datasets, encompassing cleaned raw signals, filtered signals, and extracted peak information, were then paired with Subject ID, Ground Truth, and demographic data (Gender and Age), and consolidated into CSV format. Machine learning

Recent studies leveraging machine learning on PPG features have reported high accuracy in predicting Hb concentration. Chen et al. proposed a novel system utilizing a multi-wavelength PPG approach integrated with machine learning algorithms to achieve accurate Hb estimation from 58 subjects [4]. The multispectral PPG device paired with an AdaBoost regression model demonstrated non-invasive Hb measurement with an MAE of 2.67 g/L (R² ∼0.91) on a cohort of 56 participants [5].

Several researchers also introduced a multi-model approach, leveraging multiple machine learning algorithms to enhance Hb prediction accuracy using PPG signals [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Other novel techniques include using complex network-based transformations of PPG signals: one approach attained ∼93 % accuracy in classifying Hb ranges and a regression mean absolute error of ∼6.7 g/L by extracting visibility-graph features and employing a stacked SVM–LightGBM model from 152 volunteers [10]. Researchers have explored a wide range of algorithms (support vector machines, neural networks, regression trees, etc.) from 68 subjects [11].

The machine learning process involves several stages, as shown in Fig. 9. Initially, model building includes signal acquisition, collecting PPG signals from multiple PPG channels, signal preprocessing, and feature extraction that programmatically extract features from the processed signals. Subsequently, model training and validation are conducted through multiple iterations, where the model learns from the dataset and adjusts parameters to minimize errors. This iterative feedback loop of training, validating, and adjusting continues until the model achieves satisfactory accuracy and generalization. Finally, model testing is performed by comparing predicted Hb values against actual measurements to evaluate the model's performance.Fig. 9Machine learning model for non-invasive Hb measurement.Fig 9

Limitations

The data acquisition process for each participant's green, red, and infrared signal values employed the MAXM86161 sensor, subjected to several limitations that may influence its application in machine learning. First, the sample size is relatively small (N=52), which may constrain the generalizability of machine learning models solely using this dataset. Second, the lack of detailed demographic variables (e.g. ethnicity, skin color classifications) is a recognized limitation, given the influence of skin pigmentation on PPG signal absorption. Third, health status data beyond the Hb concentration were not collected. Therefore, the application of this dataset to subjects with specific medical conditions must be performed with cautions. Finally, the data acquisition process relied on a single type of sensor (MAXM86161 sensor), which introduces potential device-specific bias.

Ethics Statement

Informed consent was obtained from all the individual participants. Data was anonymised, and no personal information, such as phone numbers or email addresses, was requested. They were given the option to withdraw at any point during the study.
