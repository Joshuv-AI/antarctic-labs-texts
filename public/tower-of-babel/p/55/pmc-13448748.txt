Risk assessment of canine periodontal disease: insights from owner‐reported health questionnaires
Authors: A A Jemmett, C Salt, L A Perry, C O'Flynn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify risk factors associated with periodontal disease in pet dogs, based on detailed owner‐reported demographic, health and behaviour survey data.Materials and MethodsA total of 12,753 pet owners completed a one‐time questionnaire that included questions concerning their pet's demographics and health.ResultsOverall periodontal disease prevalence was 50.5% (95% CI 0.50 to 0.51). Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified age, breed characteristics, oral diagnoses, symptoms and oral care habits as key risk factors for periodontal disease. Age was strongly associated with periodontal disease, with dogs aged ≥8 years at highest risk (OR = 1.37 to 2.43) and those under 4 years at lowest risk (OR = 0.30 to 0.64). Several breeds were at significantly increased risk, including American cocker spaniels (OR = 2.52, 95% CI 1.63 to 4.00, P < .001) and Papillons (OR = 2.36, 95% CI 1.56 to 3.65), while English Bulldogs exhibited the lowest odds of periodontal disease (OR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.20). Symptoms such as halitosis (OR = 2.03, 95% CI 1.75 to 2.34) and resistance to head touch (OR = 1.66, 95% CI 1.28 to 2.15) were associated with significantly increased periodontal disease odds.Clinical SignificanceThese findings highlight important risk factors associated with periodontal disease that may contribute to earlier identification and prevention.

INTRODUCTION

Canine periodontal disease (PD) is one of the most common health issues affecting dogs and can significantly impact their overall well‐being (O'Neill et al., 2021; Wallis & Holcombe, 2020). PD encompasses a range of inflammatory conditions affecting the tissues that support the teeth, primarily gingivitis and periodontitis (Martínez‐García & Hernández‐Lemus, 2021). Gingivitis, localised inflammation of the gums and early‐stage periodontitis are reversible if treated early, whereas late‐stage periodontitis involves deeper tissue damage, including the bone and periodontal ligament, and leads to irreversible tissue loss (Kinane et al., 2017). The progression from gingivitis to periodontitis is marked by the formation of pockets around the teeth, where bacteria accumulate, causing further inflammation and damage (Abdulkareem et al., 2023).

The development of PD in dogs is primarily driven by bacterial dysbiosis in the oral cavity (Santibáñez et al., 2021; Wallis et al., 2015), beginning with the accumulation of plaque on the tooth surface (Holcombe et al., 2014). Over time, this plaque matures into complex biofilms that trigger the host's immune response, leading to inflammation (Bowen et al., 2018). However, not all cases of gingivitis lead to periodontitis, and factors beyond bacterial colonisation, such as the host's immune response, play a critical role in disease progression (Harvey, 1998). These factors can be influenced by both modifiable (e.g., dental care) and non‐modifiable (e.g., breed or genetic predisposition) risk factors (Harvey, 2005). Despite the high prevalence of PD in dogs, diagnosis often occurs late in the disease process (Niemiec, 2008), making early detection and prevention strategies critical for effective management.

Research shows that PD prevalence is linked to numerous risk factors including breed (O'Neill et al., 2021), age (Marshall et al., 2014), head shape (O'Neill et al., 2020) and breed size (Wallis et al., 2021). In addition, PD has been linked to other systemic health conditions in dogs and may contribute to broader health issues, either through chronic inflammation or bacterial spread from the oral cavity to other parts of the body. Managing PD in dogs is, therefore, not only important for oral health but may also have implications for overall systemic health (Cunha et al., 2022; Peștean et al., 2024; Trevejo et al., 2018).

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are widely regarded as the gold standard for clinical research due to their ability to randomise subjects into control and intervention groups. Observational studies using real‐world data offer a valuable alternative to RCTs, especially when randomisation is not feasible. These studies collect data in natural settings, allowing for the examination of real‐world outcomes across diverse populations and can provide insights into long‐term effects, rare conditions and understudied groups (Chavez‐MacGregor & Giordano, 2016). Care must be taken in analysis and interpretation, however, due to the lack of randomisation (Grimes & Schulz, 2002), and the inherent possibility of biases in the data.

Studying PD in dogs through owner survey data is particularly valuable as it enables large‐scale data collection, which is crucial for analysing risk factors such as breed, age, diet and dental care practices. Survey‐based data sets offer insights into owner‐controlled variables that would otherwise be difficult or impractical to measure in a research setting. While the precision of such data may not match that of controlled experiments, the breadth and diversity of information obtained from many dog owners can provide valuable insights into canine health, behaviours and outcomes, helping to inform future research.

This study aimed to utilise owner‐reported data from across the United States and Canada to identify risk factors associated with PD in dogs and highlight areas of management and lifestyle that could influence a dog's predisposition to disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data collection

The study population was derived of pet dogs from customers of a fresh pet food and health company, whereby pet owners completed a one‐time questionnaire that included questions concerning their pet's demographics and health. These include single‐choice checkbox, multiple‐choice radio‐button, dropdown and free text responses. PD status, concurrent diagnoses, symptoms, oral care types and preferred diet/treat types were reported with a true, false or unknown indicator and behavioural factors, personality type and time since last veterinarian visit were reported across multiple predefined levels.

This study was carried out in accordance with the recommendations of the Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects, U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. Since the study gathered information from owners focused solely on their dogs, it did not meet the definition of human subjects' research and therefore did not require review by an Institutional Review Board. All survey respondents agreed to a Terms of Service (TOS) consenting to using pet information for research and publication and all data were anonymised.

Data processing

Questionnaires were divided into sections (behaviour, wellness, oral and medical) and completion date for each were reported to allow owners to complete sections at different times. Calendar age was determined by calculating the difference between reported date of birth (DOB) and each completion date. Records were excluded if calculated age was negative (i.e., reported DOB was chronologically later than questionnaire completion date), was less than 3 months, the range of calculated calendar ages across all questionnaires completed spanned more than 3 months, or age could not be determined due to missing data. Records were grouped by age bracket as 0.25 to <2 years, 2 to <3 years, 3 to <4 years, 4 to <5 years, 5 to <6 years, 6 to <7 years, 7 to <8 years, 8 to <9 years, 9 to <10 years, 10 to <11 years, 11 to <12 years and ≥12 years. Biological sex and neuter status were assigned, respectively, as either male or female and either neutered or sexually entire.

Free‐text breed description information was cleaned and mapped to a standard list used by BANFIELD® Pet Hospitals (Banfield), a network of over 1000 primary care veterinary hospitals across United States and Canada, which contained a predefined set of recognised breeds and cross breeds (e.g., Cockapoo [cocker spaniel × poodle]). Cross breeds that were not recognised in this list (e.g., Maltipoo [Maltese × poodle]) were described as “mixed breed”. Entries that were not included in the Banfield list, that could not be accurately interpreted from the free‐text entry, or were missing were described as “unknown”. Breeds with fewer than 50 individuals were grouped as “other”. Breed size was based on a previously published set of breed sizes (Salt et al., 2017), mapping from an individual breed label to the average body weight for each breed based on the Banfield dataset, defined as “toy” (0.0 to <6.5 kg), “small” (6.5 to <9.0 kg), “small‐medium” (9.0 to <15.0 kg), “medium‐large” (15.0 to <30.0 kg) and “large” (≥30.0 kg). Breeds were further mapped from breed to cephalic type (brachycephalic, mesocephalic and dolichocephalic). Breeds, breed sizes and cephalic types are summarised in Supplementary Data.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out in R version 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2021).

The distributions of observations across factor levels were investigated by χ 2 testing, which indicated a significant difference in age between the levels of other independent variables. For example, for breed, the mean age ranged from 5.1 years (French Bulldog) to 11.5 years (Italian greyhound); since age was strongly associated with PD status, breed effects would be confounded by age in an analysis with no correction for this. The proportions of each age bracket across factor levels within each variable were therefore normalised against the distributions of ages in the reference category, using weighting, to determine the effects of the variable of interest on PD status, after the influence of age had been removed. These results are summarised in Supplementary Data.

Dogs were split into cases (PD) and non‐cases (non‐PD) according to whether they were reported by their owner as having had a diagnosis of PD. Univariate logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the relationships between PD status and each risk factor in turn, with no other predictors included. As most risk factors had been shown to be significantly associated with age, weighting was used to balance the age distribution between risk factor levels; specifically, each level was weighted to achieve balance with the reference category for that risk factor. Coefficients from each model were extracted and exponentiated to obtain odds ratios (ORs) to interpret the univariate effect sizes of the different levels of the risk factors on the probability of PD risk factor. A Bonferroni correction was applied when interpreting P‐values to maintain type I error within an overall statistical significance level of .05.

To evaluate their collective impact on the likelihood of periodontal disease, all predictors found to be significant in the univariate analyses at an uncorrected significance level of .05 (a deliberately liberal filter) were included in one of a series of stepwise multivariable logistic regression models, fitted separately within each variable domain (demographics, concurrent diagnoses/symptoms, behaviour/personality factors and oral care). An initial logistic regression model with age as the single predictor was used as the baseline model for each stepwise procedure. Additional predictors were sequentially added to this base model in a forward direction based on their contribution to model fit as assessed by χ 2 testing, thus identifying an “optimal” set of predictors of PD out of the other previously significantly associated risk factors. Significance of variables in the final model was assessed using ANOVA, where statistical significance was set at .05. A final model was then fitted to re‐evaluate variables that were significantly associated with the outcome variable within their separate domains to identify predictors that were most significantly associated with PD overall.

RESULTS

Prevalence and study population

Of the 47,172 questionnaires, 34,419 were excluded from the study as PD status was not stated or unknown. Of the remaining 12,753 records assigned a PD status, overall disease prevalence was reported as 50.5% (95% CI 0.50 to 0.51). Neuter status and sex were approximately evenly distributed across both the population as a whole and within each disease status (Supplementary Data).

A total of 176 breeds were represented in the data. Of these, 35 were represented by at least 50 individuals across all age brackets, and breeds represented by fewer than 50 individuals were grouped as “other”, resulting in a final list of 37 breeds after additional entries “other” and “mixed breed” were included. The 10 most common breeds accounted for a total of 36.4% of the population, the most common of which were Chihuahua (8.9%), shih‐tzu (5.8%), Yorkshire terrier (5.6%), dachshund (3.8%) and Pomeranian (2.5%). The breeds with the highest prevalence of PD were American cocker spaniel (76.4%, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.85), Papillon (72.5%, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.81), dachshund (69.2%, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.73), Yorkshire terrier (68.7%, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.72) and miniature poodle (68.6%, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.76). The breeds with the lowest prevalence of PD were English Bulldog (6.0%, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.11), French Bulldog (16.7%, 95% CI 0.13 to 0.22), American Bulldog (18.0%, 95% CI 0.09 to 0.32), German shepherd (21.1%, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.31) and American Staffordshire terrier (24.5%, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.31).

When separated by head shape 25.1% of dogs were classified as brachycephalic, 33.0% of which reported a PD diagnosis (95% CI 0.31 to 0.35). Dolichocephalic breeds made up 9.4% of dogs and reported 36.6% PD prevalence (95% CI 0.33 to 0.40). Mesocephalic breeds were represented by 65.5% of dogs, 41.9% of which reported a PD diagnosis (95% CI 0.41 to 0.43). The most common breed sizes were toy and small, together accounting for 61.7% (28.9% and 32.8%, individually) of the total population, with a reported disease prevalence of 65.9% for toy breeds (95% CI 0.64 to 0.68) and 55.8% for small breeds (95% CI 0.54 to 0.58). Large breeds were the least common, representing 8.7% of the population with a PD prevalence of 35.5% (95% CI 0.33 to 0.38).

When categorised by age, 41.9% of PD cases were associated with dogs over 12 years of age (95% CI 0.41 to 0.43), and the remaining age categories individually accounted for between 0.5% and 11.0% of the diseased population. The mean age of dogs with PD was 10.8 years (median 11.1, IQR 8.2 to 13.2, range 0.6 to 20.0) and was significantly higher than that of dogs without PD, mean 7.6 years (median 7.5, IQR 4.0 to 10.8, range 0.3 to 20.7; t(12,261) = −46.4, P < .001).

Risk factors: univariate analyses

Univariate analyses were conducted to identify factors that were associated with the presence of PD. Full results are summarised in Supplementary Data.

Demographics

PD status was significantly associated with age, where older pets were more likely to report a PD diagnosis (Fig 1). After applying age weighting, PD status was not significantly associated with sex (OR = 1.00, 95% CI 0.93 to 1.07, P = .942) or neuter status (OR = 0.94, 95% CI 0.82 to 1.08, P = .395) (Fig 1).

Breed characteristics

Relationships between breed characteristics and PD status after weighting to account for age distribution are summarised in Fig 2. Overall, breed was significantly associated with PD status (degrees of freedom [df] = 36, deviance 676.7, P < .001). Individually, 24 breeds showed significantly altered odds of PD compared with the reference category of Mixed Breed. Of these, 10 were associated with reduced odds of PD, those with the smallest odds ratios being English Bulldog (OR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.20, P < .001), American Bulldog (OR = 0.35, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.64, P = .001), German shepherd (OR = 0.41, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.63, P < .001), American Staffordshire terrier (OR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.35 to 0.63, P < .001) and Boston terrier (OR = 0.50, 95% CI, 0.35 to 0.69, P < .001). There were 14 breeds with significantly increased odds of PD: those with the largest odds ratios were American cocker spaniel (OR = 2.52, 95% CI 1.63 to 4.00, P < .001), Papillon (OR = 2.36, 95% CI 1.56 to 3.65, P < .001), Yorkshire terrier (OR = 2.30, 95% CI 1.95 to 2.72, P < .001), Cavalier King Charles spaniel (OR = 2.05, 95% CI 1.49 to 2.84, P < .001) and Pomeranian (OR = 2.02, 95% CI 1.60 to 2.56, P < .001).

Cephalic type was significantly associated with PD status overall (df = 2, deviance = 185.1, P < .001). Compared with mesocephalic breeds, brachycephalic dogs had significantly lower odds of PD (OR = 0.51, 95% CI 0.46 to 0.46, P < .001) while odds were not significantly altered in dolichocephalic breeds (OR = 1.08, 95% CI 0.93 to 1.26, P = .305).

Breed size (df = 4, deviance 284.6, P < .001) was significantly associated with PD status. When using small‐medium as the reference category, the majority (71.1%) of all PD diagnoses were associated with toy (OR = 1.76, 95% CI 1.54 to 2.01, P < .001) and small (OR = 1.19, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.35, P < .001) breeds. Medium‐large breeds had a reduced odds ratio (OR = 0.72, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.85, P < .001) and large breeds had the smallest odds ratio (OR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.66, P < .001).

Oral diagnoses/symptoms

Seven concurrent diagnoses and symptoms were associated with PD status (Fig 3). Odds of PD were increased in dogs with halitosis (OR = 2.32, 95% CI 2.10 to 2.58, P < .001), those that were observed to drop food or chew on a single side of the mouth (OR = 1.34, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.59, P < .001), those that were resistant to head touch (OR = 1.28, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.51, P = .004) and those that never exhibited chewing behaviours (OR = 1.22, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.42, P = .009).

Univariate analysis of the relationship between oral diagnoses/symptoms and PD status. All variables were weighted by age bracket in relation to the age distribution of each reference category (chewing frequency, “occasionally”; all other symptoms, “not present”). Points represent odds ratios, where statistical significance at P < .05 level is denoted by filled points, and error bars represent 95% confidence intervals.

Dogs were less likely to have a PD diagnosis if they were missing teeth due to non‐eruption (OR = 0.36, 95% CI 0.31 to 0.43, P < .001) or trauma (OR = 0.31, 95% CI 0.29 to 0.33, P < .001), reported malocclusions (OR = 0.22, 95% CI 0.20 to 0.24, P < .001) or had broken/discoloured teeth (OR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.89, P = .011). Dental infection, bloody saliva, chattering jaws, excessive lip licking, lumps/bumps/swelling and pained eating sounds were not significantly associated with PD status.

Oral care

The reported use of certain types of oral care altered the odds of PD diagnosis (Fig 4). Reported use of liquid/gel/oil products (OR = 1.53, 95% CI 1.38 to 1.70, P < .001), powder‐based products (OR = 1.89, 95% CI 1.50 to 2.39, P < .001) or toothbrushing (OR = 1.16, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.26, P < .001) was associated with higher rates of PD. Conversely, lower rates of PD were associated with the reported use of chew toys (OR = 0.68, 05% CI 0.62 to 0.74, P < .001), crunchy biscuits (OR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.99, P = .033) or rawhide/bones/animal parts (OR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.63 to 0.75, P < .001). There was no significant association between PD status and of the use of long‐lasting chews, pills/tablets or “other” oral care types.

A dog was more likely to have PD if it had not visited a veterinarian in the past 2 years (OR = 0.63; 95% CI 0.42 to 0.93; P = .021), and an increase in the frequency of dental treatment was significantly associated with PD status. Dogs that had reportedly received treatments one to two times and more than three times before had, respectively, odds 1.83 times greater (95% CI 1.69 to 2.00, P < .001) and 2.02 times greater (95% CI 1.84 to 2.23, P < .001) than dogs that had never had dental treatment.

Diet and treats

Diet type at the time of questionnaire completion was not found to be significantly related to PD status; however, preferred treat type was significant (Fig 5). While no individual treat type was significantly associated with altered odds of PD, dogs were significantly less likely to be reported to have PD if their preferred treat type was cooked hard bones (OR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.86, P < .001), raw bones (OR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.67 to 0.90, P < .001), frozen treats (OR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.67 to 0.91, P = .001), dehydrated meat (OR = 0.85, 95% CI 0.79 to 0.92, P < .001), baked biscuits (OR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.80 to 0.92, P < .001) or dental treats (OR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.96, P = .003). Preferences for fresh food or soft chewable treats were not significantly associated with PD.

Behaviour

Data exploring interactions between behaviour and PD status are summarised in Fig 6. Dogs with PD were significantly more likely to be described as “fearful/timid” (OR = 1.41, 95% CI 1.23 to 1.61, P < .001), “very reserved” (OR = 1.22, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.37, P < .001), “very stressed” (OR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.46, P = .042) and having “very low” energy levels (OR = 1.28, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.51, P = .003). Dogs with reported PD were significantly less likely to be associated with “excellent” overall mood (OR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.92, P < .001) and health (OR = 0.62, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.69, P < .001) and were less likely to have “high” overall energy levels (OR = 0.92; 95% CI = 0.83 to 1.00, P = .040); however, there was no significant association of PD status with overall levels of aggression, eating enthusiasm or food motivation.

Multivariate analyses

As previously described, variables that were significantly associated with PD status in age‐weighted univariate analyses were further analysed by logistic regression modelling. Age group was included in the initial model to which each additional variable was added, stepwise, in a forward direction. Variables were grouped by type and a separate model was constructed for each: demographics, concurrent oral diagnoses/symptoms, behaviour/personality factors, current oral care and diet/treats. Full results from stepwise modelling of demographic factors are summarised in Supplementary Material. The final model contained 20 individual factors that were associated with (Fig 7), relating to 12 of the 20 variables that showed statistical significance in the initial domain multivariate models.

Summary of statistically significant relationships between predictive variables and PD status. Points represent odds ratios, where statistical significance at P < .05 level and error bars represent 95% confidence intervals reported from stepwise modelling with respect to reference categories (age bracket, “5 years”; dental health products, symptoms, “false”; dental treatment frequency, “never”; overall health, “average”).

The odds of PD were significantly associated with pet age, where dogs under the age of 4 were less likely to be associated with PD (OR = 0.30 to 0.64, P < .001 to 0.049), and those over the age of 8 were at higher risk of disease (OR = 1.37 to 2.43, P = .045 to <.001).

PD status was associated with five symptoms and concurrent oral diagnoses. There were significantly increased odds of PD in dogs with halitosis (OR = 2.03, 95% CI 1.75 to 2.34, P < .001) and resistance to head touch (OR = 1.66, 95% CI 1.28 to 2.15, P < .001). Dogs were less likely to be associated with PD if they had missing teeth as a result of trauma (OR = 0.15, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.88, P < .001) or non‐eruption (OR = 0.23, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.30, P < .001) or had malocclusion (OR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.15, P < .001).

The odds of PD increased with the frequency of dental treatments with those undergoing ≥3 treatments having an odds ratio of PD 1.64 times higher than those that had never undergone treatment (95% CI 1.38 to 1.95, P < .001). Dogs with PD were significantly less likely to be described as in “good health” (OR = 0.77, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.90, P = .001) or “excellent health” (OR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.54 to 0.80, P < .001).

Two oral care methods were associated with reduced odds of PD: chew toys (OR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.72 to 0.95, P = .007) and rawhide/animal parts (OR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.74 to 0.97, P = .014). Conversely, two methods were associated with increased odds of PD: liquid gel/oil (OR = 1.28, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.50, P = .002) and powder (OR = 2.21, 95% CI 1.56 to 3.17, P < .001).

Variables that were entered into the final model but did not show statistical significance were breed, cephalic type, chewing frequency, dropping food or chewing on one side and the use of any main diet or treat types.

DISCUSSION

This paper investigated the prevalence of and risk factors for periodontal disease in a cohort of pet dogs across the United States of America and Canada, utilising owner‐completed online questionnaires concerning pet demographics and signalment, alongside health and lifestyle factors.

The overall prevalence of PD in the study population was reported as 50.5% which is comparable to other recent studies, though existing prevalence figures vary hugely (Kortegaard et al., 2008; O'Neill et al., 2021; Wallis & Holcombe, 2020). Reported prevalence of PD is dependent on several factors, such as the type of study (prospective vs. observational) and diagnosis method (anaesthetised vs. conscious assessment) (Venturini et al., 2007). Data in this study were via owner reporting, which has the potential to be biased due to a lack of awareness and underdiagnosis of PD (Fernandes et al., 2012). Pets with or without PD may therefore not be reported as such, though it is predicted that false negatives are more likely to be reported than false positives due to the condition being largely below the gum line and invisible on conscious assessment. Owners are often not veterinarians and therefore depend upon veterinary professionals for diagnoses of conditions such as PD, as such dogs were significantly less likely to have a diagnosis of PD if they had not visited a veterinarian in the past 2 years. This highlights the importance of regular veterinary attention for the diagnosis and monitoring of such conditions.

Demographic variables were highly influential on disease status. There was a significant association between age and PD status, where disease was more likely to be associated with dogs over the age of eight and less likely to be associated with dogs under the age of four. This was reflected throughout all analyses and significance persisted into the final model, supporting extensive previous research (O'Neill et al., 2021; Wallis et al., 2021). The present data suggest a pivot point between 5 and 7 years of age at which point dogs are more likely to have PD than not. PD is an irreversible condition that progressively develops; therefore, these observations may suggest that awareness prior to this age should be increased to improve screening and owner education. The observed relationship between age and PD may be a result of the progressive pathogenesis of the condition which occurs in adult teeth after colonisation of the plaque biofilm (Holcombe et al., 2014), and the eventual trigger of the immune system (Harvey, 1998). As the pet ages, these events become increasingly likely and lead to a further increased risk when combined with the deterioration of tissue. In addition, veterinarians are more likely to identify PD as a pet ages, either through increased suspicion or increased likelihood through accumulated vet visits. Here, we see that owner‐reported PD follows the same logic as existing studies utilising different study tools. We also show that, even after the removal of age as a factor by weighting observations by age, we still see a significant relationship between PD and factors such as oral care types, overall health and behavioural observations such as resistance to head touch.

Univariate analyses highlighted that, of the 13 breeds associated with increased odds of PD (compared to mixed breeds), 10 (76.9%) were classed as “toy” or “small”: Papillon, dachshund, Yorkshire terrier, miniature poodle, Maltese, Pomeranian, Chihuahua, Toy poodle, Miniature schnauzer and Havanese. Conversely, of the nine breeds with reduced odds of PD, seven (77.8%) were classified as “medium‐large” or “large”: Labrador retriever, golden retriever, boxer, American Staffordshire terrier, German shepherd, American Bulldog and English Bulldog. These observations consolidate existing work (O'Neill et al., 2021; Wallis et al., 2021). It is reported that small dogs have proportionally larger teeth than larger dogs (Gioso et al., 2001), which results in a build‐up of plaque and may be a predisposing factor for oral disease. Breed and breed size were not identified as significant factors in the final multivariate model; however, as they did not significantly contribute to the demographic domain multivariate model but may have been excluded from the multivariate model due to a relationship with other variables related to breed size.

Brachycephaly was associated with reduced odds of PD in univariate analysis and demographic multivariate analysis, contradicting reports where brachycephalic breeds were 1.25 times more likely to be associated with PD than mesocephalic breeds (O'Neill et al., 2021). Of the eight brachycephalic breeds represented by at least 50 dogs in the present dataset, univariate analysis identified that five (American Bulldog, Boston terrier, boxer, English Bulldog, French Bulldog) were at significantly lower risk of PD compared with mesocephalic breeds. It is suspected, however, that cases of PD may be under‐diagnosed in these breeds. This is because an in‐depth diagnosis of PD is typically made while the patient is under general anaesthetic (Kim et al., 2013), and brachycephalic dogs are up to 4.33 times more likely to develop anaesthesia‐related complications than non‐brachycephalic dogs (Gruenheid et al., 2018), meaning they may not truly be at lower risk of PD than other cephalic types. Furthermore, dogs diagnosed with malocclusion were reportedly at lower risk of PD; however, 58.3% of these dogs were classed as brachycephalic breeds under the present definition (Niemiec, 2021), and underdiagnosis is suspected. This may be a limiting bias within observational data, and further work investigating the true prevalence of PD in brachycephalic breeds and non‐anaesthetic methods of diagnosis is warranted to ensure that these cases are being identified appropriately.

There may be additional factors independent of physical breed characteristics that predispose dogs to PD and were not assessed in the present dataset, as reports suggest that genetic variations in interleukin‐1 and toll‐like receptor 9 may be linked to disease susceptibility (Silva et al., 2023). Genetic testing of pets with accurate diagnoses of PD in a prospective study may clarify these suggestions and further our understanding of the relationship between breed and PD status.

Reduced odds of PD were consistently associated with fewer teeth resulting from either non‐eruption or trauma. Biologically, this is perhaps due to greater spacing between teeth and a reduction in the surface area required for the formation of biofilms and plaque build‐up that result in disease (Van Dyke, 2009). Alternatively, this could be linked to tooth extractions as a result of existing oral disease: as PD is an irreversible condition, extraction is often a treatment option to mitigate any associated discomfort and prevent further development (Lobprise, 2006). PD was associated with increased frequency of dental treatment in the present study, which may include extractions, though these findings suggest that this does not offer a long‐term solution and that PD requires further, alternative management (Albuquerque et al., 2012). The direction of causality should be considered here, however, due to the increased likelihood of an older dog undergoing dental treatment, increasing the opportunity for a diagnosis of PD, and the likelihood of future treatment.

The uses of oral care methods were analysed and the risk of PD was significantly lower in dogs that used mechanically abrasive treats and objects such as chew toys, crunchy biscuits, rawhide or bones and was higher in dogs that did not exhibit chewing behaviours. PD can be managed by reducing plaque biofilm accumulation (Cunha et al., 2022); therefore, the physical removal of such factors reduces the opportunity for disease progression. The use of liquid, gel or oil oral care products was associated with increased risk of PD, though further details regarding the nature and frequency of use of such were not given. Reports show that 17.5% of dog owners use oral health products (Fernandes et al., 2012), and it is, therefore, hypothesised that the use of oral‐specific products may be more likely to be selected by owners of dogs who already have a diagnosis of PD as a method of slowing disease progression. In contrast, mechanistically abrasive toys and treats may be incorporated into the daily lives of dogs without the owners' realisation of the beneficial effects. Whist abrasive chews and cooked bones may demonstrate efficacy for reduced plaque and therefore PD, they may have deleterious effects regarding tooth fracture or other issues and should be utilised with caution (Pion & Spadafori, 2017).

Despite the low proportion of owners that report awareness of PD (Fernandes et al., 2012), present data suggest a number of behavioural observations that are easily apparent to owners such as halitosis, dropping of food while eating, chewing on one side and avoiding head touch. It could be hypothesised that these noticeable symptoms may encourage an owner to seek veterinary attention; however, reports show that just 2.7% of dogs diagnosed with PD are brought to a veterinary clinic with this as their main complaint and that the majority of cases are identified opportunistically (Fernandes et al., 2012).

Behavioural observations including “fearful” or “reserved” personality type, “high” stress level, and “very low” energy levels were also associated with PD in univariate and domain multivariate analyses; however, reports show that 41.8% of American dog owners would not seek veterinary attention if their dog was inactive or lethargic (Park et al., 2021). This vastly reduces the opportunity for PD diagnosis and may contribute to the underestimation of its true prevalence across populations. Conversely, non‐PD dogs were associated with “high” energy levels and “excellent” overall mood, suggesting that the presence of disease may prevent dogs from entering these positive states. Dental disease has a high impact on welfare (Summers et al., 2019) and has been linked to poor overall health (Bellows et al., 2019), which was reflected in an association of non‐PD dogs with “good” and “excellent” overall health statuses in the present data. Furthermore, PD can progress to more serious conditions such as alveolar bone loss (Wallis & Holcombe, 2020) and systemic consequences including cardiovascular disease (Pereira dos Santos et al., 2019), chronic azotemic kidney disease (Glickman et al., 2011) and hepatopathy (Pavlica et al., 2008). The identification, or preferentially the prevention, of PD is therefore of importance when reducing the risk of the development of such comorbidities and disease complications.

Despite these associations, it should be highlighted that data were derived from voluntary owner questionnaires through a commercial pet health platform, which may introduce selection bias and limit the representativeness of the findings as a result of convenience sampling (Andrade, 2020). These findings are, therefore, generalised to the population that was accessible in the pre‐existing data set and should be validated against a purposive data set. Furthermore, the data presented in this study rely on owner perceptions of their pet's oral health status and lifestyle factors, which may be reflected in reported underdiagnosis of PD and potential information bias.

Owner education is of paramount importance in the prevention of PD onset and management, and it is evident that there is a responsibility of all parties involved in pet care to promote oral health in pet dogs. Efforts should be made to promote vigilance and highlight key risk factors including age and breed factors, while raising awareness of symptoms such as pain responses and withdrawn behaviours and promoting the potential for mechanical abrasion through toy and treat selection to minimise the risk of disease development.

In conclusion, the findings presented here support numerous existing reports regarding canine periodontal disease and its associated risk factors. They also highlight key issues regarding the management of PD linked to methods of diagnosis, owner awareness of symptoms and motivation to seek veterinary attention.
