Recommendations on Drug Selection and Dose Adjustment for Patients with Liver Cirrhosis: Results from a Multidisciplinary Expert Panel
Authors: Katharina Karsten Dafonte, Philipp Lutz, Christian Jansen, Marika Busse, Peter Dietrich, Katrin Farker, Hans-Peter Lipp, Jacob Nattermann, Tilman Sauerbruch, Sven Schmiedl, Dorothea Strobach, Jonel Trebicka, Dario Zocholl, Gunther Hartmann, Ulrich Jaehde, Martin Coenen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesLiver cirrhosis profoundly alters pharmacokinetics and is frequently associated with medication-related problems. Stage-specific dosing guidance remains inconsistent, and prescribing information often lacks clarity. As a result, treatment decisions rely on individual clinical judgement and may differ between professional groups. This study assessed drug-selection and dose-adjustment practices among hepatologists, clinical pharmacologists, and clinical pharmacists, quantified consensus across Child-Pugh stages, and evaluated alignment with prescribing information as a basis for harmonised dosing guidance.MethodsTwelve experts from hepatology, clinical pharmacology, and clinical pharmacy evaluated prioritised drugs for patients with liver cirrhosis across Child-Pugh stages A–C in a structured survey. For each drug, experts indicated whether they would apply no dose adjustment, modify the dose, or avoid administration. Consensus was defined as at least 75% agreement, and interrater agreement was assessed by pairwise percentage agreement. Consensus recommendations were compared with the dosing information provided in each drug’s Summary of Product Characteristics.ResultsConsensus or strong consensus was achieved for only 20% of 177 recommendations, mainly for no dose adjustment or avoidance. Agreement decreased with disease severity (Child-Pugh A 83%; B 47%; C 41%). Of 36 consensus recommendations, 18 matched the summary of product characteristics, while six diverged from it. Pharmacists and clinical pharmacologists more often recommended dose adjustments, especially in Child-Pugh B and C, whereas hepatologists preferred standard dosing or avoidance.ConclusionsExpert recommendations for dosing in cirrhosis showed marked variability and limited concordance with prescribing information, underscoring the need for harmonised interdisciplinary guidance integrating pharmacokinetic evidence and clinical feasibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40261-026-01536-0.

Key Points

Introduction

As the final stage of various chronic liver diseases, cirrhosis leads to impaired liver function characterised by reduced metabolic capacity, decreased protein synthesis, and portosystemic shunting [1]. These alterations markedly affect drug pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD) [2], often requiring dose adjustments. In particular, diminished cytochrome P450 (CYP) activity and altered protein binding contribute to unpredictable drug exposure that is not adequately captured by conventional clinical scores [1]. At the same time, polypharmacy (≥5 daily medications) is common among patients with liver cirrhosis and a high burden of medication-related problems (MRPs) [3, 4].

Although PK alterations in liver cirrhosis are well described, they have rarely been translated into harmonised, stage-specific dosing recommendations for clinical use. Published strategies remain heterogeneous and sometimes contradictory, particularly in patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis [5–7]. Similarly, summaries of product characteristics (SmPCs) often provide only vague or inconsistent guidance, leaving prescribers with considerable uncertainty [8, 9]. This uncertainty is amplified in advanced disease stages, where risk-benefit considerations are most complex and available evidence is limited. In such situations, prescribing decisions often depend on individual clinical judgement rather than standardised frameworks [5, 10] resulting in substantial variability in the prescription of medications. This variability is further amplified when different professionals are involved, such as physicians from various specialties or pharmacists—given the differences in their general approaches to pharmacotherapy [11, 12].

This study therefore aimed to evaluate expert decision making across different disciplines using a structured Delphi survey to analyse similarities and differences between different professional backgrounds across disease stages as a prerequisite for the development of harmonised and clinically applicable dosing guidance. Hepatologists, clinical pharmacologists and pharmacists indicated their recommendations on drug selection and dose adjustments for commonly used drugs in patients with liver cirrhosis across Child-Pugh stages A to C [13, 14]. To our knowledge, no prior study has systematically evaluated whether different professional groups apply distinct dosing strategies in liver cirrhosis. We further compared expert ratings with corresponding information from the SmPCs to assess potential discrepancies.

Materials and Methods

Compilation of the Active Substances

The steps identifying and compiling the active substances provided to the experts for evaluation have been published recently [9]. In brief, we conducted a systematic literature review (SLR) of all peer-reviewed publications available up to October 2020 that provided recommendations for dose adjustments in patients with liver cirrhosis. Identified studies were assessed for methodological quality (PRISMA [15]/AGREE [16]) and evidence level (CEBM [17]). An updated search in July 2025 yielded no additional publications meeting the predefined inclusion criteria. Substances were considered if they had clearly defined dose recommendations from at least two different publications. To ensure clinical applicability, the resulting substances were cross-checked against the list of drugs delivered by the hospital pharmacy to the Department of Internal Medicine I, hosting the Gastroenterology Department of the University Hospital Bonn. Substances not routinely used or were off market in German inpatient care were excluded. The remaining list of 70 substances was evaluated by a three-member expert group consisting of two hepatologists and one clinical pharmacologist. The substances were rated based on (i) frequency of use in cirrhotic patients, (ii) potential for medication-related problems, and (iii) whether these could plausibly be addressed by dose adjustment (dose-dependent side effects) with 1 to 6, resulting in a maximum of 18 possible points. The cut-off of 13.5 points (≥ 75 % of the maximum score) was defined a priori in order to include only substances that scored high across all dimensions and to focus the Delphi procedure on the clinically most relevant drugs. Following this process, 39 active substances qualified for the Delphi procedure. Due to clinically relevant differences in formulation (e.g., oral vs intravenous [IV] or immediate release [IR] vs extended release [ER]) and indication (e.g., thrombosis prophylaxis vs treatment of thrombosis), they were further subdivided into 59 distinct drug-formulation-indication combinations (in the following called “items”), each of which was evaluated separately by the expert panel (Table 1).

Assessment of the Clinical Management

This study represents the first round of a two-round Delphi process designed to resolve conflicting recommendations found in the literature and to develop consensus-based recommendations for drug use in hospitalised patients with liver cirrhosis. A panel of 12 experts was recruited, consisting of six hepatologists, three clinical pharmacologists, and three clinical pharmacists. All expert-panel members were senior clinicians or senior clinical pharmacists from tertiary care centres with a dedicated clinical and scientific focus on hepatology with long-standing clinical experience of over 20 years in the prescription of drugs to hospitalised patients with liver cirrhosis. It was ensured that the clinical and academic expertise across the different specialties was comparable. Their expertise covered the management of liver cirrhosis across disease stages, including complications of advanced liver disease and liver transplantation. The survey process was conducted anonymously between May and October 2022. The list of participants was disclosed to the experts only after the completion of all evaluations. For the survey, the experts were asked to consider an average cirrhotic patient with no atypical comorbidities and to indicate their usual prescribing approach for each drug selecting one of the following categories: (i) “no dose adjustment” (unchanged dose), (ii) complete “avoidance of administration”, or (iii) “dose adjustment”. If selecting “dose adjustment”, they were asked to choose one of the following categories: “dose reduction”, “prolonged dosing interval”, “therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM)”, or an “alternative strategy” supplemented by a proposal for a specific action (see Supplement Fig. S1). Experts were asked to base their responses on their overall clinical judgement and were free to base their decisions on any criteria they considered appropriate, although the Summaries of Product Characteristics (SmPCs) for the respective active substances were provided together with the evaluation form. To ensure clarity and feasibility of the rating process, pre-tests of the survey were conducted with a pharmacist, a clinical pharmacologist, and two hepatologists.

Analysis of Consensus and Divergence of Recommendations

For statistical evaluation, the response options were grouped into three categories: “no dose adjustment”, “dose adjustment”, and “avoidance of administration”. All statistical analyses, including those for interrater agreement and group comparisons, were performed in Python (version 3.11) using the SciPy library (version 1.11), with a significance level of α = 0.05. According to guidelines of the Commission of the Association of Scientific Medical Societies in Germany (AWMF) [18], “strong consensus” was defined as ≥ 96% agreement, “consensus” as ≥ 75% agreement, “majority approval” as agreement between 51 and 75%, and “no majority approval” as ≤ 50% agreement.

Interrater agreement was calculated as a pairwise percentage agreement to assess not only the proportion of identical ratings, but also the distribution of divergent responses across categories, allowing a more nuanced assessment of agreement than consensus definitions based solely on majority ratings. It was assessed at three levels: (i) for each Child-Pugh class, (ii) considering both Child-Pugh class and professional group, and (iii) stratified by Child-Pugh class, professional group, and specified drugs. Interrater agreement was calculated by pairwise percentage agreement across items and professional groups as a pragmatic measure of expert consensus, as reliability coefficients (Fleiss’ and Cohens’ Kappa), were not interpretable due to the “kappa paradox”, which occurs at response distributions with low variance [19, 20]. This descriptive measure of response consistency reflects how frequently raters selected the same response.

“Divergence” was used to describe a substantial lack of agreement in expert responses. Three categories of divergence were defined: (i) no majority agreement (≤ 50%), (ii) a polarised majority with ≥ 4 votes (≥ 33.3%) for each of the two opposing categories at both ends of the scale (“no dose adjustment” and “avoidance of administration”) and (iii) equal distribution between two distinct response options, meaning that the two most frequently chosen categories received the exact same number of votes. Unlike in divergence definition (ii), these responses were not located at opposite ends of the response scale. Categories (i) and (iii) were documented descriptively. Items classified as polarised majority (ii) were first subjected to a Fisher’s exact test and a subsequent post hoc pairwise Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni correction to identify whether specific professional groups differed significantly in their selection of these two extreme response options.

Comparison of Expert Consensus with the SmPC

To evaluate the alignment between expert consensus and the official prescribing information, we compared the recommendations which reached “strong consensus” or “consensus” (≥ 96% and ≥ 75% agreement) with the corresponding specifications of the SmPC. Since the SmPC categories “mild”, “moderate” and “severe hepatic impairment” are not formally defined, the Child-Pugh classification was used as a pragmatic approach to classify hepatic impairment in accordance with clinical practice, as it is the most widely used and clinically established staging system in both regulatory guidelines and clinical practice. Consistency was therefore assessed per Child-Pugh grade and categorised as (a) “full agreement”, (b) “no specific SmPC statement” (interpreted as implicit agreement with "no dose adjustment required"), or (c) “contradictions between expert recommendations and SmPC information”.

Comparison of the Individual Professional Groups

To examine differences in response behaviour between professional groups, a Chi-square test of independence (3×3 matrix: three response categories × three professions) was first applied separately for each Child-Pugh stage and subsequent post hoc pairwise Fisher’s exact tests with Bonferroni correction were performed to identify the specific group (e.g., hepatologists, clinical pharmacologists or pharmacists).

Results

Distribution of Expert Recommendations According to Child-Pugh Stage

A total of 59 drug-formulation-indication combinations (hereafter referred to as “items”) were evaluated separately for each of the three Child-Pugh stages, resulting in 177 individual recommendations. These recommendations were rated by the 12 experts, yielding a total of 2124 expert responses. Table 1 shows the levels of consensus across all 177 recommendations. Preferences of individual expert responses across the three decision categories—“no dose adjustment”, “dose adjustment”, and “avoidance of administration”—shift with increasing severity of liver dysfunction (Fig. 1). In Child-Pugh A, the majority of experts recommended “no dose adjustment” (71.8%), while “dose adjustment” (19.4%) and “avoidance of administration” (8.9%) were less frequently selected. In Child-Pugh B, recommendations for “dose adjustment” (34.5%) and “avoidance of administration” (21.2%) increased, reflecting a greater need for dose modifications. This trend continued in Child-Pugh C, where “avoidance of administration” peaked at 35.6% and “no dose adjustment” declined to 31.1%, highlighting the increasing necessity to restrict certain medications in advanced liver disease. For a detailed distribution by drug, please refer to Supplement Fig. S2. In a subset of cases where avoidance was recommended, individual experts also provided suggestions for alternative substances. These individual statements were documented for 16 recommendations and are summarised in Table 2. Most of the suggested alternatives were independent of the Child-Pugh stage and represent individual expert opinions rather than consensus-based recommendations.

Consensus and Interrater Agreement on Recommendations

Overall, the level of consensus was low, as “strong consensus” or “consensus” only applied to 36 of 177 recommendations (20%), while the majority of the recommendations achieved either “majority approval” (47%) or “no majority approval” (28%) (Table 1). Consensus was primarily achieved for either the administration of the standard dose or the avoidance of the drug and only applied to a single Child-Pugh stage in the majority of cases (70%). For a detailed overview over the assessment of consensus strength, please refer to Supplement Table S1.

The overall “interrater agreement”, reflecting how frequently raters selected the same response was 58% (3894 of 6726 pairwise comparisons), with individual items ranging from 32% to 100% agreement (see Supplement Table S2). Interrater agreement was substantially higher in items related to Child-Pugh A (median agreement: 83%) compared to Child-Pugh B (median of 47%) and Child-Pugh C (median of 41%).

Alignment of Achieved Consensus with the SmPC

Thirty-six recommendations that achieved either “strong consensus” or “consensus” were compared with the corresponding SmPC information (Tables 3, 4, 5). Recommendations without consensus were excluded from this analysis. Of the 36 evaluated recommendations, only 18 were fully consistent with the SmPC. Eleven recommendations were considered as implicit agreement as experts recommended “no dose adjustment” in cases where the SmPC provided no specific information for use in liver cirrhosis. For six recommendations, expert assessments deviated from the information of the SmPC, including carvedilol, metformin, prednisolone and ramipril. More details including citations from the respective SmPCs are provided in Supplement Table S3.

Divergence on Recommendations

Marked divergence in expert opinions was observed in 71 of 177 recommendations. In 50 cases, no single response option reached more than half of the votes, meaning that no clear majority decision could be made (“no majority approval”, Table 1). In 13 other cases, the panel was split between the two extreme options “no dose adjustment” and “avoidance of administration”, with at least four experts choosing each (“polarised majority”). However, Fisher’s exact test showed no statistically significant polarisation of responses, indicating that these differences likely reflected random variation rather than a systematic disagreement between experts. Examples for items with “polarised majority” included ramipril, amitriptyline, and tramadol in Child-Pugh B, and beta blockers, midazolam, and venlafaxine in Child-Pugh C (see Table 1).

Comparison Between the Professional Groups

For all Child-Pugh categories, the overall difference in the distribution of response categories (“no dose adjustment”, “dose adjustment”, “avoidance of administration”) between professional groups was statistically significant (Child-Pugh A: p = 0.0464; Child-Pugh B and C: both p < 0.001).Subsequent post hoc tests revealed that across all Child-Pugh stages, both pharmacists and clinical pharmacologists differed significantly from hepatologists in their response patterns (Child-Pugh A: p = 0.021 and p = 0.012; Child-Pugh B and C: p < 0.001 for both). In contrast, no significant difference was observed between pharmacists and clinical pharmacologists for any Child-Pugh stage. Additional analyses demonstrated that only pharmacists and clinical pharmacologists recommended TDM, in approximately 5% of all responses, while hepatologists did not recommend TDM for any drug. Additionally, for Child-Pugh B and C, pharmacists and clinical pharmacologists more often recommended “dose adjustment”, whereas hepatologists more frequently selected either “no dose adjustment” or “avoidance of administration”. Across all items, in Child-Pugh B, dose adjustment was recommended by 53.6% of pharmacists, 49.7% of clinical pharmacologists, and 23.6% of hepatologists; while avoidance was selected by 10.1%, 14.5%, and 26.4%, respectively. In Child-Pugh C, dose adjustment rates were 52.0%, 45.1%, and 24.1%, and avoidance was recommended by 25.1%, 36.0%, and 34.5% of pharmacists, clinical pharmacologists, and hepatologists, respectively.

Discussion

This study provides an analysis of expert decision making on pharmacotherapy in patients with liver cirrhosis comparing recommendations across three key professional groups. Overall, the consistently low level of consensus and the limited interrater agreement across 177 recommendations emphasise the challenge of aligning pharmacological decisions in this important, but complex population, particularly in advanced disease stages where supporting evidence is sparse and drug therapy is primarily based on individual risk-benefit considerations. In line, consensus was more frequently achieved either for maintaining standard doses in compensated cirrhosis or for complete avoidance of selected drugs in Child-Pugh stage C. In contrast, intermediate options, particularly differentiated dose adjustments, were associated with the highest degree of variability. Consensus was lowest in Child-Pugh C, particularly for CNS-active and cardiovascular drugs, where ratings were polarised between standard dosing and avoidance. These disagreements reflect the therapeutic challenges in decompensated cirrhosis, where PK alterations and a variety of frequent comorbidities combined with limited supporting evidence led to variability in expert judgement. This uncertainty has direct clinical implications, as patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis are highly vulnerable to drug-related harm and yet may derive substantial benefit from effective pharmacotherapy given the poor spontaneous course of the disease [21–23]. Our findings confirm that a structured consensus approach, as previously proposed [6], is an essential tool to develop interdisciplinary recommendations, but the effectiveness remains limited by unresolved interprofessional disagreement.

When comparing expert consensus with the information from the SmPC, major discrepancies became evident. Even among the 36 recommendations that reached consensus, only half were fully consistent with the SmPC. In six cases, expert ratings even contradicted SmPC instructions. Such discrepancies illustrate the gap between regulatory information and clinical practice. Previous analyses have documented major shortcomings in hepatic safety information of SmPCs, including contraindications between different versions and provision of only vague warnings [6, 23]. One reason for these inconsistencies is that patients with moderate or severe liver disease are routinely excluded from clinical trials, leaving SmPC data largely extrapolated from non-cirrhotic populations or from experiences with other drugs and resulting in conflicting or vague hepatic safety information. Moreover, the dose adjustment principles in liver cirrhosis differ fundamentally from those in renal or cardiac impairment, as portal hypertension leading to reduced hepatic blood flow, plasma volume expansion, functional renal failure and reduced protein synthesis alter first-pass extraction and systemic drug exposure in ways that are not addressed by conventional dose-adjustment concepts [2, 7]. In addition, recommendations are rarely differentiated by stage of liver disease. And finally, published PK evidence for dose-adjustment strategies in cirrhosis has not been transferred in systematic controlled studies to clinically validate specific recommendations [24].

Our findings also highlight differences between professionals with different backgrounds. Pharmacists and clinical pharmacologists more frequently recommended dose adjustments, particularly in Child-Pugh B and C, whereas hepatologists more often favoured standard dosing or complete avoidance of a drug.

This is consistent with data from a previous survey showing that pharmacists are generally more cautious in liver cirrhosis, even in the absence of formal SmPC guidance [25]. This pattern suggests different general approaches to pharmacotherapy: Clinical pharmacologists and pharmacists tend to apply a conservative, criteria-based strategy, while hepatologists may favour pragmatic decisions [11, 12]. Practical constraints likely contribute to these differences. It has been shown that complex prescriptions in hospital settings are associated with higher error rates [26] and that inappropriately complicated regimens can compromise patient adherence and understanding [27]. These factors may explain why hepatologists managing complex patients under high time pressure may prefer pragmatic regimens informed by clinical experience and may recommend fewer adjustments, viewing standard dosing as the more feasible option in routine care. In contrast, pharmacists and clinical pharmacologists may focus on the optimisation of pharmacotherapy based on PK considerations, leading to more frequent selection of dose adjustment.

Consistent with these professional differences, TDM was only recommended by pharmacists and clinical pharmacologists. Although dose adjustments are useful for selected drugs in cirrhotic patients [28] and TDM has been shown to be cost-effective in specific populations, its implementation in clinical settings, including hepatology, remains reserved for selected cases [29]. Pilot initiatives integrating TDM on hepatology wards could therefore represent a pragmatic step to evaluate feasibility, safety benefits, and cost effectiveness in this high-risk population.

To our knowledge, this is the first structured Delphi-based assessment comparing prescribing strategies between different professional groups in liver cirrhosis. Our findings highlight the variability and uncertainty in drug prescription in this vulnerable population and emphasise the need for harmonised, interdisciplinary dosing guidance for patients with liver cirrhosis. Such guidance should integrate PK and PD evidence, expert consensus, and feasibility considerations to be clinically applicable. Integration into clinical decision support systems, as already implemented in the Netherlands [24], may improve patient safety, reduce variability, and support shared decision making across professions in this high-risk patient population.

From a methodological perspective, our study highlights the limitations of consensus definitions based on majority thresholds. While they provide a pragmatic categorisation, they are not able to capture the nuances of agreement and disagreement. The additional use of interrater agreement measures allowed us to assess variability more comprehensively, although statistical limitations such as the “kappa paradox” remain relevant [19]. Nevertheless, the combination of consensus thresholds, descriptive divergence analysis, and interrater agreement provide a robust framework for capturing the complexity of consensus procedures.

Our study is limited by the rather small number of substances which were assessed by a rather small number of experts. This may restrict generalisability. However, the substances were selected through a predefined prioritisation process, which targeted the most clinically relevant drugs with frequent use, risk for medication-related problems and potential for dose adjustment. Another potential limitation is the use of the Child-Pugh classification to stratify hepatic impairment. Although it remains the most practical and widely applied framework in regulatory and clinical contexts, it was originally developed to predict postoperative mortality rather than to reflect hepatic metabolic capacity, which limits its suitability for PK stratification [30]. Furthermore, the interpretation of SmPC information is complicated by the lack of standardised definitions for hepatic impairment categories. The terms “mild, “moderate” and “severe” hepatic impairment are used inconsistently across SmPCs and cannot be directly aligned with Child-Pugh stages, which limits their applicability for stage-specific dosing decisions. However, the Child-Pugh score was selected because of its widespread use in regulatory guidance and published clinical recommendations, making it the most practical framework for stratified dosing in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This multidisciplinary study revealed an overall low level of consensus among experts from different clinical backgrounds regarding drug selection and dose adjustment for patients with liver cirrhosis, with greater variability observed in advanced stages of the disease. Consensus, even for frequently used drugs, was limited, highlighting substantial variability and uncertainty in prescribing decisions for patients with liver cirrhosis. Frequent discrepancies were identified between the recommendations of these professional groups and the information provided by the respective SmPCs, indicating gaps and inconsistencies in current guidance. These findings emphasise the need for harmonised, stage-specific dosing recommendations that integrate PK evidence, interdisciplinary expertise, and clinical feasibility to improve medication safety in this vulnerable population.
