Delayed Tension Pneumothorax After Microwave Ablation of Lung Metastasis
Authors: Yuanyuan Wang, Yanting Jiang, Lei Wang, Xiaodong Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrowave ablation, as a minimally invasive treatment, is commonly used for local lung nodules. However, complications such as tension pneumothorax can lead to life-threatening consequences. We present a case of delayed tension pneumothorax that occurred 24 hours post-procedure, which required urgent intervention to prevent further deterioration and reduce mortality risk.Keywords: lung metastasis, microwave ablation, delayed tension pneumothorax

INTRODUCTION

Microwave ablation (MWA) is a viable option for managing pulmonary metastases to prolong the survival of non-surgical patients. However, complications of MWA represent the roadblocks for its application, such as pneumothorax. A delayed pneumothorax may be asymptomatic at the beginning and worsen over time, which affects lung respiration and recoil, leading to ventilation suppression or even respiratory failure. Rapid gas drainage from the chest cavity might result in pulmonary oedema, which has a mortality rate of up to 20%. We report a case of delayed tension pneumothorax, for which an urgent intervention was performed to prevent further deterioration and avoid death.

CASE REPORT

A 60-year-old woman who underwent radical resection of endometrial cancer was admitted to our hospital with a right lung nodule, which was confirmed as adenocarcinoma of endometrial origin via a computed tomography (CT)-guided biopsy. Then, MWA of the lung lesion was performed with the patient under local anaesthesia with 50 W of power for 5 minutes. The MWA device, which was purchased from Nanjing Yigao Microwave System Engineering Co. (Nanjing, China), operated at a frequency of 2450 MHz. Post-procedure CT imaging confirmed the adequate coverage of the ground-glass opacity around the lesion (Figure 1). The woman tolerated the procedure well with no immediate complications such as pneumothorax, bleeding, and pleural effusion detected on post-ablation CT scans. After 24 hours, the woman developed acute chest tightness. Her blood pressure was 88/37 mm Hg in the right upper limb and 122/47 mm Hg in the left upper limb, with blood oxygen saturation at 92%. An urgent bedside chest X-ray demonstrated a nearly 70% collapse of the right lung with tracheal deviation to the left, consistent with tension pneumothorax. Emergency interventions, including bedside thoracentesis drainage and CT-guided thoracentesis, were performed. The water-sealed bottle contained pink fluid, and an urgent lung CT scan ruled out pulmonary oedema, suggesting blood-tinged exudate instead. Subsequently, the patient experienced recurrent low-grade fever and gradual reduction in right-sided chest pain. Follow-up CT imaging showed progressive resolution of the pneumothorax (Figure 2), with improvement in right lung cavitation, pleural effusion, and interstitial fluid accumulation. These complications resolved after 1 month, leaving a residual cavitary lesion in the right lung. Ultimately, the patient recovered fully and resumed normal daily activities without any disruption to her life.

DISCUSSION

Microwave ablation is theoretically superior to radiofrequency ablation due to its ability to generate higher ablation temperatures and larger heating radii in lung lesions, enabling complete tumour eradication within a shorter treatment duration. For lung tumours, the long-term efficacy of MWA critically depends on sufficient ablation margins of at least 5 mm beyond the tumour boundaries.1 Complications associated with pulmonary MWA include pleural effusion, pulmonary necrosis, pneumothorax, and so on. Pneumothorax incidence rates range from 8.5% to 63%.1,2 Emphysema has been recognized as an independent predictor of a pneumothorax, likely due to its impact on pulmonary anatomy and tissue integrity.3 Additionally, repeated punctures and extended ablation zones can lead to pleural defects and tissue damage, resulting in alveolar air leakage into the pleural cavity. Consequently, lesions near the pleura also pose a higher risk of pneumothorax. Furthermore, delayed pneumothorax may occur due to prolonged thermal injury latency, heat-induced pleural necrosis, or late alveolar rupture. No significant difference in pneumothorax rates between high-frequency and conventional low-frequency MWA treatments were noted.4 Nonetheless, intentional induction of an artificial pneumothorax has been utilized to mitigate pain in patients with tumours adjacent to critical structures.5 In this case, the woman did not experience tachycardia, hypotension, or difficulty breathing after chest tube drainage, suggesting no pulmonary oedema secondary to atelectasis. This observation implies that gradual gas drainage may facilitate pneumothorax resolution, which requires further studies to validate. This case report highlights the diagnosis, management, and clinical implications of delayed pneumothorax following MWA. Early recognition and prompt intervention for complications are crucial to prevent adverse outcomes and ensure patient safety.
