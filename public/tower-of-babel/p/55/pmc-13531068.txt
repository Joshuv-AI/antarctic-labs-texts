Papillary Endothelial Hyperplasia Mimicking Right Atrial Infective Thrombus
Authors: Carlos Huerta, Kristopher Aten, Darwin Nguyen, Akram Kawsara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntracardiac masses pose a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, with clinical features overlapping infective endocarditis, thrombus, and primary cardiac tumors. History of Presentation

A 75-year-old man presented with a 3-day history of lethargy and generalized weakness. Three weeks prior, he had undergone Foley catheter placement for urinary retention. On admission, he was hemodynamically unstable, with a blood pressure of 57/45 mm Hg, heart rate of 73 beats/min, respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air. Physical examination was unremarkable, with no murmurs.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of coronary artery disease with prior myocardial infarction and percutaneous coronary intervention to the left anterior descending artery, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, stage 3 chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and dementia.

Investigations

On presentation, the patient was in septic shock requiring intensive care and brief vasopressor support. Laboratory evaluation was notable for leukocytosis (white blood cell count: 30 × 109/L), serum lactate of 6.0 mmol/L, and procalcitonin of 18 ng/mL. Computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated findings consistent with pyelonephritis. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis (ampicillin susceptible). The patient was initiated empirically on vancomycin and meropenem, which was subsequently de-escalated to ampicillin monotherapy guided by susceptibilities. Despite appropriate antibiotic therapy, the bacteremia persisted.

A DENOVA score of 3 prompted transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), which revealed a 2 × 1.2 cm mobile right atrial mass at the superior vena cava–right atrial junction in the region of the crista terminalis (Figure 1). No valvular vegetations were identified, and left-sided cardiac structures were unremarkable. Given the combination of persistent E faecalis bacteremia and the previously described mobile echodensity in the right atrium, the patient met modified Duke criteria for definitive infective endocarditis.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included infected thrombus, right-sided infective endocarditis, bland thrombus, and, less likely, primary cardiac tumor or malignancy.

Management

Given his persistent bacteremia and right-sided vegetation >2 cm, surgical consultation was obtained. Because of the patient's multiple comorbidities, frailty, and lack of high-risk embolic phenomenon, a multidisciplinary team recommended against surgery, with a trial of percutaneous mechanical aspiration using the AngioVac system (AngioDynamics). The procedure was performed under general anesthesia using bilateral femoral venous access, with the AngioVac cannula advanced into the right atrium under combined fluoroscopic and TEE guidance. Partial debulking of the right atrial mass was achieved; however, further aspiration was deferred because of adherence to the superior vena cava. Intraprocedural TEE confirmed the right atrial mass and demonstrated interval reduction following partial debulking (Figure 2).

Outcome and Follow-Up

Histopathological analysis demonstrated branching papillary structures composed of organized fibrin and paucicellular fibrous stroma lined by endothelial cells with flat to mildly plump, nonpleomorphic nuclei (Figures 3A to 3D). No mitotic activity, nuclear atypia, or necrosis was identified. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated strong diffuse CD31 positivity of the lining cells, confirming their endothelial nature, and negative staining for cytokeratin AE1/AE3, excluding epithelial or carcinomatous origin (Figures 3E and 3F). These findings are characteristic of papillary endothelial hyperplasia (PEH), in which reactive endothelial proliferation overlies organized thrombus in a papillary pattern, with bland nuclear morphology and absent mitotic activity excluding malignancy. No histologic evidence of infection or abscess formation was identified. Together, these morphologic and immunohistochemical findings established the definitive diagnosis of PEH. The patient tolerated the procedure without complication and was discharged home in stable condition to complete 6 weeks of intravenous antibiotics.

Discussion

This case describes a rare intracardiac presentation of PEH in the right atrium of a patient with concurrent E faecalis bacteremia, in whom the clinical and echocardiographic features strongly suggested an infected thrombus. The case illustrates 3 important teaching points: inclusion of PEH in the differential diagnosis of right atrial masses, the diagnostic limitations of echocardiography in this context, and the emerging role of the AngioVac system as both a therapeutic and tissue acquisition tool.

Pathogenesis and epidemiology of PEH

PEH, first described by Pierre Masson in 1923, is a benign, reactive, non-neoplastic intravascular proliferation of endothelial cells overlying organized thrombus.1 The lesion is thought to arise from reactive endothelialization of a preexisting thrombus, in which organizing fibrin provides a scaffold for papillary endothelial outgrowth. Three forms have been described: primary (arising de novo in a dilated vessel or aneurysm), secondary (arising within a preexisting vascular lesion), and extravascular (arising in a hematoma), with the primary form most common.2 PEH typically occurs in the skin and subcutaneous tissues of middle-aged adults; intravascular involvement is rare. Fewer than 25 cardiac PEH cases have been reported, most involving right-sided chambers and often associated with antecedent thrombus, endovascular devices, or central venous catheters, supporting thrombus as a nidus for PEH development.2

Diagnostic dilemma: PEH vs infected thrombus

Echocardiographic features of PEH are nonspecific. On TEE, it typically presents as a mobile, echogenic mass that may be pedunculated or sessile. These features overlap substantially with those of bland or infected thrombus, vegetation, or papillary fibroelastoma, rendering echocardiographic differentiation unreliable.2,3 In the present case, a large right atrial mass with persistent E faecalis bacteremia created a compelling clinical picture for infected thrombus or intracardiac vegetation. The DENOVA score is a validated bedside risk stratification tool developed specifically for patients with E faecalis bacteremia to predict the likelihood of infective endocarditis.4 In the present case, the DENOVA score was 3, meeting the threshold for high risk. TEE was appropriately pursued given the persistently positive blood cultures and clinical concern for infective endocarditis. Ultimately, imaging alone was insufficient; definitive diagnosis required histopathology. The differential diagnosis of a right atrial mass is broad and includes thrombus, vegetation, papillary fibroelastoma, cardiac myxoma, angiosarcoma, metastatic malignancy, and, rarely, PEH. Key distinguishing features on echocardiography include attachment site, stalk morphology, surface characteristics, and associated chamber or valvular abnormalities.3 Definitive diagnosis requires histopathological confirmation, as in this case.

AngioVac as a therapeutic and diagnostic tool

The AngioVac system is a percutaneous venovenous extracorporeal bypass circuit designed to aspirate intravascular material, including thrombi, vegetations, and other intracardiac masses. Increasingly used in patients at high surgical risk, it enables minimally invasive debulking under fluoroscopic and echocardiographic guidance.5,6 In this case, AngioVac was an appropriate minimally invasive strategy given the patient's advanced age, multiple comorbidities, and ongoing recovery from septic shock, all of which made him a poor candidate for any open surgical procedure. The AngioVac approach offered simultaneous therapeutic debulking and diagnostic tissue acquisition through a single percutaneous intervention. The extracorporeal filter captured sufficient tissue to permit detailed histopathological and immunohistochemical confirmation of PEH. Several case series and registry studies have demonstrated acceptable procedural success rates with AngioVac for right-sided vegetations and thrombi, with low procedural mortality, supporting its use in carefully selected patients.5,6

A notable limitation of AngioVac is incomplete mass removal, as observed in this case. The procedure achieved partial debulking of the right atrial mass, and whether residual PEH requires repeat percutaneous intervention or expectant management with anticoagulation remains a matter of debate. Given the benign nature of PEH and the patient's comorbidity burden, conservative management with outpatient echocardiographic surveillance was pursued. Ancillary tools, such as snares, were not used during this procedure, as the preprocedural diagnosis favored infected thrombus rather than a tumor. Snare-assisted debulking is typically reserved for pedunculated intracardiac masses with a confirmed or suspected neoplastic etiology, where mechanical disruption of the mass is required before aspiration.7

Conclusions

PEH is a rare but important consideration in the differential diagnosis of right atrial masses, particularly when concurrent bacteremia creates diagnostic ambiguity. Tissue acquisition is essential for definitive diagnosis, even when the clinical picture strongly favors infection. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case in which the AngioVac system enabled both percutaneous debulking and definitive histopathological diagnosis of intracardiac PEH, offering a minimally invasive alternative to open surgery in a patient who was a poor operative candidate.
