AI-powered platform revolutionizing blood cell morphology education for medical students
Authors: Xuekai Liu, Lei Shang, Chen Liu, Yongpei Yu, Donghua Shao, Meilin He, Guowei Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to preliminarily explore the advantages and potential issues of artificial intelligence in the teaching of blood cell morphology to undergraduate medical students, so as to provide theoretical support and practical experience for promoting the intelligent transformation of medical education.MethodsUndergraduate students from the 2021 cohort of the Aerospace School of Clinical Medicine at Peking University were assigned as the experimental group, while students from the 2020 cohort served as the control group. The experimental group utilized the AI platform to study blood cell morphology, whereas the control group relied on conventional teaching methods. We compared the accuracy rates of cell identification between two groups of students. Additionally, we conducted supplementary research through questionnaires, post-class interviews, and classroom observations.ResultsThe experimental group achieved a significantly higher average score in cell identification (87.82 ± 9.63) compared to the control group (74.83 ± 12.41) (P<0.0001). The correct identification rates of metamyelocytes, eosinophils, and monocytes in the experimental group were significantly increased by over 30%.DiscussionAI holds considerable promise in medical education, particularly in the instruction of hematology cell morphology. Nevertheless, further research is required. Traditional microscope-based teaching should not be completely dismissed, as current digital platforms for blood cells do not yet capture all cellular nuances.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07761-z.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Image analysis, Blood cell morphology, Medical education

Introduction

Blood cell morphology is a fundamental course in clinical medicine, primarily focusing on the morphology, quantity, and variation patterns of various cells in the blood. It forms the basis for the diagnosis of hematological diseases and serves as an important pathway for medical undergraduates to study blood system diseases from a pathological perspective.

The traditional teaching model mainly relies on microscope-based instruction and is teacher-centered. However, these methods have numerous limitations [1], such as limited access to microscopes, restricted teaching hours, inefficiencies in instructional delivery, uneven resource allocation, and challenges in standardization, which fail to fully stimulate students’ interest and initiative in learning [2, 3].

In recent years, with the rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI) technology [4–6], significant breakthroughs have been achieved in its application within medical fields such as image analysis and pathological diagnosis, as well as in blood cell morphology [7–10]. In hematology, AI has shown outstanding capability in identifying standard white and red blood cells, achieving an accuracy rate of up to 98%. Furthermore, AI systems have demonstrated impressive sensitivity (95.82%), specificity (99.19%), and accuracy (98.89%) in classifying and counting 13 types of bone marrow nucleated cells. All these results suggest that artificial intelligence can serve as a supplement to the traditional teaching model and probably further transform the teaching method of blood cell morphology [11, 12].

This study was designed to preliminarily explore the advantages and potential challenges of artificial intelligence in the teaching of blood cell morphology within undergraduate medical education. The findings were expected to offer theoretical underpinnings and practical insights, facilitating the intelligent transformation of medical education.

Materials and methods

Optimized grouping criteria

In this study, we carefully selected the students who enrolled in 2021 as the experimental group and those who enrolled in 2020 as the control group. Both groups of students participated in this experiment during their third year of college (The study was conducted with the 2020 cohort in 2023 and the 2021 cohort in 2024). This ensured that the experiment was carried out in the same teaching environment, enabling us to explore relevant research questions more accurately.

Experimental Group (Group A): This group included 27 students from the 2021 cohort of the Aerospace School of Clinical Medicine at Peking University, consisting of 19 students from mainland China and 8 international students.

Control Group (Group B): This group comprised 37 students from the 2020 cohort of the Aerospace School of Clinical Medicine at Peking University, including 19 students from mainland China and 18 international students.

All participants took part in the final examination and classroom observations. Six students were randomly selected for post-class interviews(four mainland Chinese students and two international students.). The questionnaire was administered to the Experimental Group (Group A), among whom one participant abstained from the questionnaire for personal reasons.

Materials

The bone marrow and peripheral blood smears used in this study were all from hematopoietic stem cell transplant donors, and the cell morphology was normal. All of them signed informed consent forms voluntarily donating the remaining samples from hospital tests for teaching purposes. The blood smears had been anonymized, and there was no personal information of the donors.

The microscope model used in the control group was OLYMPUS CX33.

Methods

AI-based platform

The DeepCyto morphology image analysis system (Tianjin, China) integrates machine vision, deep learning, and big data mining technologies to achieve intelligent recognition of blood cell morphology. The strength of the DeepCyto lies in the informative features extraction and is followed by accurate classification. The best accuracy achieved is 97% on the Herlev dataset, 99% on the Sipakmed dataset and 100% on the LBC dataset [13]. (Fig. 1)

The DeepCyto morphology image analysis system

The system primarily consists of three key steps: automatic selection of suitable areas on blood smear slides, cell segmentation, and cell classification. The AI-powered online learning platform offers a realistic simulation of a microscope’s view, creating an immersive learning environment. Upon signing the informed consent form, students provide a username, which, along with a default password, grants them access to the online platform. Students can rest assured that their personal information is solely used for educational purposes.

Within the platform, students had the opportunity to examine any bone marrow or blood smear. By selecting a smear, the platform displayed a categorized list of individual cells on the left and the corresponding microscope view on the right. When a specific cell was chosen, the platform focused on that cell’s microscope view on the right. Students could zoom in and out to explore the cell’s details and navigate the microscope view in any direction to compare adjacent cells. This allowed students to observe and analyze different cell types. When they wished to know a cell’s classification, they simply click on its corresponding box to access the information. (Fig. 2)

Statistical method

Original data was recorded in Excel spreadsheets for subsequent comparison and analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

The academic performance of the two groups of students was a continuous variable. Continuous variables were presented as means (SD), with differences between groups compared using either the independent samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. The correct rate of cells for the students in the two groups was presented in the form of a percentage. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Differences in accuracy rates between groups were calculated, and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals were estimated using Wald’s method. Additionally, a stratified analysis for Chinese mainland students and international students was conducted using the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (CMH) method. A two-sided P-value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We had created a more intuitive Figs. 5 and 6 to illustrate the correct rate of cells.

To measure students’ cell identification accuracy more intuitively and precisely, we use the confusion matrix as an analytical tool. It’s like a “monster-revealing mirror”, clearly reflecting students’ judgment deviations in cell classification. Through the matrix data, we can easily see cases of misclassifying positive and negative classes, and analyze confusion patterns among specific cell categories. This helps us pinpoint learning difficulties and cognitive blind spots, providing crucial evidence for optimizing teaching strategies.

Compared to the control group, the experimental group exhibited a more concentrated range of commonly confused cell types. Although both groups of students showed a consistent trend of cell confusion, the degree of confusion was less pronounced in the experimental group. The control group frequently misidentified monocytes and eosinophils as myelocytes, metamyelocytes, and band cells, a situation that improved in the experimental group. Additionally, the control group tended to confuse myeloblasts with lymphocytes, which was also ameliorated in the experimental group. (Fig. 5)

In the experimental group of mainland students, the most significant improvement in accuracy compared to the control group was observed in the metamyelocytes. For international students, the greatest improvement in accuracy compared to the control group was seen in the promyelocytes and monocytes. International students in the experimental group demonstrated a lower accuracy in plasma cell identification compared to their counterparts in the control group. This discrepancy might be attributed to sampling bias arising from the smaller sample size in the experimental group. (Fig. 6)

We distributed 26 questionnaires to the experimental group and retrieved all 26 of them. The questionnaires revealed that through the course study, students rated the artificial intelligence platform highly, with a satisfaction rate of 100%. The vast majority of students believed that this platform made it easier to learn the characteristics of cells and improved learning efficiency. Additionally, they expressed a willingness to continue using this platform to study the morphology of blood cells. However, 3% (1 out of 26) of the students still thought that the traditional microscope was more acceptable. (Fig. 7)

In the conversations, students noted that, in contrast to traditional microscope - and PPT - based teaching, the artificial intelligence system boasted remarkable advantages. These encompassed a diverse range of samples, high - efficiency learning, sharp - clarity images, and convenient access for learning. However, students also pointed out that the AI platform cannot completely replace traditional microscope teaching because the details of some cell images cannot be fully restored [9]. Meanwhile, students proposed optimization recommendations for the artificial intelligence model. These included incorporating English - language options, establishing a quiz mode, and offering an online question - answering feature. While the AI system is recognized for improving learning efficiency and standardization, the majority of students believe microscope skills remain an indispensable part of medical education. Future teaching models should balance AI-assisted learning with foundational skill training.

Observation

In terms of student engagement, approximately 85% of students could quickly focus at the beginning of the lesson; however, about 15% of students experienced distractions during the later part of the lesson or self - study sessions due to increased content and unfamiliar operations. During group discussions, there was a high level of enthusiasm, with approximately 92% of students participating and sharing insights. Students exhibited a proactive attitude towards asking questions, primarily concerning system usage and the details of cell morphology, and they also expressed skepticism about the classification results of the system.

In terms of learning outcomes, students had a good grasp of the knowledge of common blood cell morphology. For instance, after completing studies on the granulocyte system, when students were randomly selected for questioning or assessment, 80% (8 out of 10) of them could accurately describe the morphological characteristics of cells at various stages. Furthermore, approximately 92% of students could proficiently use the system to locate cell images and accurately identify common cell morphologies.

The classroom atmosphere was lively and filled with a passion for learning, with positive interactions between teachers and students as well as among students themselves. Teachers actively responded to students’ questions and guided their thinking, fostering collaborative exchanges among students. The overall teaching effectiveness was good, stimulating students’ interest in learning, although there remained room for improvement. It was recommended that teachers increase the use of comparative cases for similar cell morphologies, optimize the organization of group discussions, adjust the teaching pace, enhance system operation training, and employ various methods to explain special cell morphologies.

Discussion

In the experimental diagnosis curriculum for undergraduate medical students, hematological morphology holds a pivotal position. It represents both a core teaching focus and a formidable challenge. Currently, undergraduate instruction commonly integrates multimedia resources with microscope slide examination [1]. During the teaching process, educators leverage PPT presentations to methodically elucidate the morphological features and identification criteria of diverse blood cells to students. Subsequently, students directly observe blood and bone marrow smears under low - power and oil immersion microscopes, thereby forming an intuitive comprehension of blood cell morphology. Whenever students encounter ambiguous cell morphologies during their observations, they can consult teachers and teaching assistants, who will offer timely clarifications and guidance.

With technological advancements, artificial intelligence (AI) platforms have increasingly permeated medical education, infusing novel impetus into traditional teaching paradigms [14]. These platforms seamlessly blend teacher - centered and student - centered pedagogical approaches, crafting a more efficient and equitable learning ecosystem for students [1]. In this medical education study, we adopted multi - faceted evaluation strategies to comprehensively assess teaching efficacy from various perspectives [15]. This comprehensive assessment framework not only endows the research findings with greater depth and breadth but also enables a precise reflection of the actual teaching scenarios.

The AI - based blood cell image analysis system is equipped with robust functionality [16, 17]. It houses an extensive repository of normal blood cell images and undergoes continuous updates, significantly expanding the scope of teaching materials. The rich array of cell images facilitates students in broadening their perspectives and more thoroughly grasping the morphological characteristics of different blood cells. Moreover, the adoption of digital images effectively alleviates the workload associated with storing physical slides. This system can accurately identify multiple cell types, and in the event of occasional errors, experts will promptly rectify them. Consequently, all students have access to consistent and accurate teaching resources. This substantially mitigates teaching discrepancies arising from variances in teachers’ expertise in cell morphology, providing a solid foundation for the standardization of teaching and assessment practices. Additionally, students can access this system at any time, both before and after class, to meet their individualized learning requirements. This fosters a student - centric learning environment, effectively stimulates students’ subjective initiative, creates conducive conditions for nurturing practical - oriented medical professionals, and propels the transition of the teaching model from a traditional teacher - dominant approach to a student - centered one [18].

However, post-class interviews have shown that 4 out of 6 students believe that this platform cannot completely replace the teaching method based on traditional microscopes [9]. This view has been corroborated by some research. Compared with digital learning models, hands-on operation of microscopes is often more appealing. Additionally, some medical students have misgivings about artificial intelligence technologies and regard these technologies as “black boxes” due to their incomplete understanding [19, 20]. These concerns have an impact on their receptivity to these technologies. In fact, we should not completely abandon the teaching based on traditional microscopes, because current digital platforms for blood cell analysis are still unable to comprehensively capture all cellular details. Perhaps with the further improvement of the AI platform, for example, by adding a discussion module and perfecting the language system.

Contemporary China has one of the larger medical education systems in the world [21]. Amid the burgeoning development of artificial intelligence, the imperative of maximizing the utilization of these advanced resources to cultivate an increased number of outstanding and qualified healthcare professionals, capable of meeting the escalating health demands of the public, has emerged as a critical issue that necessitates in - depth exploration and resolution within the realm of medical education [22–24].

Limitations

Problems related to language: Upon examining the performance differences between mainland and international students, we found that international students scored lower than mainland students. This course is taught in Chinese, and although the international students have passed the Mandarin proficiency test, some medical terminology remains slightly challenging for them.

Sample Size Considerations: The number of medical students enrolled annually at Peking University varies, as does the allocation of students to each teaching hospital. Since this study represents a preliminary exploration of AI applications in undergraduate medical education, future research will aim to expand the sample size through further investigations, including collaborative studies with additional teaching hospitals.

Collaborations and Sample Limitations: This study was conducted solely with students from our institution, and there is currently no collaboration with other teaching hospitals. Differences in teaching resources, student demographics, and training models across different hospitals result in a narrow research scope. The sample limitations restrict the generalizability of the research findings, making it difficult to reflect the effectiveness of the system’s application under various teaching environments. Future studies should involve more hospitals and expand the sample size to encompass a broader student cohort.

Software Functionality Deficiencies: The artificial intelligence image analysis system lacks a multilingual option, which hinders international students’ learning. Additionally, the system interface does not include a discussion module, preventing students from engaging in real-time discussions about cell images or cases within the system, which affects interactive learning efficiency and the creation of an immersive learning atmosphere.

Traditional Teaching Obstacles: The inertia of traditional microscopy teaching poses challenges to the implementation of this teaching reform. Teachers tend to rely on conventional teaching experiences and fail to fully utilize the system’s capabilities. Some students are accustomed to traditional modes and exhibit resistance to the new teaching model, showing insufficient motivation for self-directed learning. Both teachers and students need to collaboratively adapt to the new teaching paradigm.

Teaching Duration Issue: The teaching duration is relatively short, allowing the project to only reflect preliminary teaching effects. Future research could extend the duration of the study to track students’ usage of the system at different learning stages and collect more performance data, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment of the system’s application in teaching blood cell morphology.

Conclusion

Artificial intelligence has a certain potential in the field of teaching medical blood cell morphology, but this still remains to be further explored. However, the skill of microscope operation remains an indispensable part of medical education. Future teaching models should attempt to strike a balance between AI-assisted learning and basic skills training.
