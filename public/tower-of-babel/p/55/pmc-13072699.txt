Relative circadian disruption in urinary excretion associates with renal function in biopsy-proven primary glomerular diseases: a cross-sectional study using the novel CRDI metric
Authors: Jie Wang, Yisha Li, Xieguanxuan Xu, Guangyan Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe circadian rhythmicity of urinary solute excretion is a hallmark of intact renal physiology, yet its disruption in chronic kidney disease (CKD) lacks a practical quantitative tool. We developed the novel Circadian Rhythm Disruption Index (CRDI) to objectively quantify this disruption from routine clinical data and investigated its association with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in primary glomerular diseases. This cross-sectional study prospectively enrolled 81 inpatients with biopsy-confirmed primary glomerular diseases under standardized hospital protocols. All spontaneous urine samples over 24-hours were collected. The presence of circadian rhythms in urinary excretion was validated by cosinor analysis. CRDI metrics were defined and developed to calculate protein, creatinine, and volume. Multivariable linear regression was used to examine the CRDI-eGFR relationship, adjusting for demographics, pathology, and medications. Clinical utility was assessed using ROC and decision curve analysis. Significant circadian rhythms were confirmed for all biomarkers. The CRDI for creatinine (CRDI_cr) demonstrated the strongest independent inverse association with eGFR after full adjustment (β = −4.45 mL/min/1.73m2 per unit, p = 0.008). A key finding was the pathological specificity of this relationship; it was most pronounced in IgA nephropathy. Incorporating CRDI_cr into a clinical model improved the AUC for identifying eGFR ≤60 mL/min/1.73m2 from 0.748 to 0.800 and yielded a significant net reclassification improvement (NRI = 0.237). The CRDI effectively quantifies relative circadian disruption. CRDI_cr is independently associated with renal function in a pathology-specific manner, highlighting its potential as a novel biomarker for refined risk stratification in specific glomerular diseases, particularly IgA nephropathy.Keywords: Primary glomerular diseases, circadian rhythm, cross-sectional studies, glomerular filtration rate, spot urine

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) poses a significant worldwide health challenge, with primary glomerular diseases being a leading cause of end-stage renal disease [1]. Clinical management of such patients heavily depends on static biomarkers, notably the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and 24-h total proteinuria [2]. Although essential, these combined indicators tend to mask the kidney’s dynamic physiological functions, including the inherent circadian rhythmicity of urinary solute excretion.

Urinary excretion of creatinine, electrolytes, and other solutes displays characteristic diurnal patterns that reflect integrated renal physiological regulation, while these rhythms may be disrupted in patients with CKD [3]. Compelling evidence has linked circadian rhythm disruption to the pathogenesis and progression of CKD [4]. Molecular clocks within renal cells regulate critical functions ranging from podocyte homeostasis to tubular handling, and their disruption has been implicated in renal injury [5]. Moreover, circadian blood pressure dysregulation, such as non-dipping patterns, is a well-established risk factor for progression of diseases like IgA nephropathy [6].

This link extends beyond the kidney, as circadian misalignment is recognized as a contributor to adverse systemic health outcomes [7]. In the context of renal disease, experimental models demonstrate that disturbing circadian rhythms exacerbates kidney injury and accelerates functional decline [8]. Clinically, the attenuation or loss of normal diurnal variation in urinary solute excretion (e.g., sodium, creatinine) is associated with hypertension and target organ damage, serving as a marker of circadian dysfunction [9–11]. Furthermore, conditions characterized by systemic circadian disruption, such as shift work, are associated with an increased risk of CKD development and progression [11]. These lines of evidence underscore that quantifying circadian excretory disruption could provide critical insights into renal health beyond conventional static measures.

However, critical translational gaps persist despite this knowledge. First, there is a conspicuous lack of a quantitative tool to precisely measure the degree of circadian excretory disruption from routine clinical data. Sophisticated research methods like cosinor analysis are ill-suited for widespread clinical application. Second, it remains largely unknown whether such rhythm disturbances exhibit disease-specific patterns across different glomerular pathologies. Third, evidence is scarce regarding whether quantified rhythm disruption holds a direct, independent association with functional renal decline, as measured by eGFR, after accounting for conventional risk factors. Finally, the potential of circadian metrics to serve as prognostic biomarkers remains virtually unexplored, hindered by the absence of practical assessment tools.

To address these gaps, we developed the Circadian Rhythm Disruption Index (CRDI) – a novel, objective metric designed to quantify the relative disruption in urinary excretion patterns. In this cross-sectional study of patients with biopsy-proven primary glomerular diseases, we aimed to: (1) validate the CRDI for urinary creatinine, protein, and volume; and (2) investigate its independent association with eGFR, thereby exploring its potential as a functional biomarker reflective of renal health.

Materials and methods

Study design and study populations

This study employed a single-center cross-sectional design. Data were collected from inpatients enrolled in the Department of Nephrology at the First Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital between July 2024 and September 2025. The sample size was not predetermined by a formal power calculation but constituted a convenience sample including all eligible patients admitted during the recruitment period who consented to participate. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 60 years, (2) fulfilled KDIGO CKD criteria with confirmed G1-G4 stages, (3) proteinuria, defined as a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio > 3.5 mg/mmol or 24-h urinary total protein > 150 mg/24 h, (4) definitive pathological diagnosis confirmed by renal biopsy within the past 6 months, (5) stable renal function (defined as <25% variation in serum creatinine over the preceding 3 months), (6) written informed consent provided. Exclusion criteria included: (1) eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73m2 or current receipt of dialysis or kidney transplantation, (2) ongoing use of diuretics (either oral or injectable), (3) females who were menstruating, pregnant, or within 3 months postpartum, (4) active urinary tract infection (symptomatic or asymptomatic bacteriuria >105 CFU/mL), (5) diabetes mellitus (any type) or hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%, (6) 24-h urine volume less than 400 mL or exceeding 3000 mL, (7) shift workers or individuals with recent transmeridian travel (within 1 month) that could disrupt circadian rhythms, (8) participants who failed to comply with standardized urine collection protocols. All enrolled patients were hospitalized during the study period and underwent a standardized protocol to minimize potential confounders. Specifically, all enrolled patients received a standardized hospital diet designed in accordance with current clinical practice guidelines for non-dialysis CKD management, with the following restrictions: sodium (<5 g salt/day), protein (0.6–0.8 g/kg/day), and fat (<50 g/day) [12,13]. Daily water intake was regulated between 1000 and 1500 mL. A consistent sleep-wake cycle was encouraged, with bedtime between 22:00 and 23:00 and wake-up time between 06:00 and 07:00. This protocol was designed to achieve two primary objectives: (1) to provide a consistent, guideline-adherent background therapy for all participants, minimizing inter-individual variation in renal solute load that could confound circadian excretion patterns; and (2) to standardize the acute dietary milieu, thereby allowing the investigation of the association between intrinsic renal function (eGFR) and circadian excretory rhythms (CRDI) under controlled conditions. We acknowledge that we did not assess individual dietary habits prior to hospitalization; the study focused on physiological relationships under standardized conditions. To minimize the confounding effect of variable physical activity on urinary excretion rhythms, all patients were managed within the stationary ward and were advised to maintain routine in-room activities only, with strenuous exercise prohibited. All randomly voided urine samples over a 24-h period were collected, and the exact time of each urination was recorded. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (Ref: S2024-577-01). All participants provided written informed consent before study participation.

Measurements

All data, including demographic information, occupation, sleep onset time, medical history, medication use, daily water intake, and physical measurements such as height, weight, and blood pressure, were obtained from inpatient medical records and bedside interviews. Drug use was defined as continuous use for more than 3 months prior to hospitalization. We focused on the following classes of medications that may influence kidney disease progression and urinary excretion: angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), SGLT2 inhibitors, glucocorticoids and other immunosuppressants. Fasting serum creatinine levels were measured using standardized enzymatic assays in blood samples drawn the morning following hospital admission. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the CKD-EPI 2009 equation [14]. Blood pressure was measured at the bedside by a nurse between 7:00 and 8:00 AM on the urine collection day using an electronic sphygmomanometer; three consecutive measurements were taken and averaged. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2).

24-h urine collection

Participants were instructed to collect every voided urine sample over a 24-h period. The exact time of each micturition was recorded by the patient or nursing staff using a standardized documentation form. The entire collection process was conducted in a ward setting under the supervision of ward nurses. A research staff member provided standardized instructions to ensure proper collection procedures and minimize omissions or recording errors. At the start of the study, participants were asked to empty their bladder (this initial void was discarded), and all urine produced in the subsequent 24 h was collected. The start and end times of the collection period were defined by the first and last void of the 24-h interval, respectively, and were individually recorded for each participant. To ensure the completeness of the 24-h collection, participants were instructed to completely empty their bladder at the end of the collection period, and this final void was included in the total volume and analysis. The volume of each individual urine sample was measured using a graduated cylinder, and a 15 mL aliquot was extracted as an on-site sample. All samples were stored at −80 °C until batch analysis.

Laboratory examination

In this study, urinary protein was quantified using a modified immunoturbidimetric assay, and urinary creatinine was measured via an enzymatic method, employing a BA400 fully automated specific protein analyzer (BIOSTEC, Spain) at the National Clinical Research Center for Kidney Diseases. Prior to analysis, the instrument was calibrated with standard reference materials. All samples were assayed simultaneously in the same batch. For internal quality control, 10% of the samples were randomly selected and reanalyzed as blinded replicates.

Quantification of relative circadian disruption using the Circadian Rhythm Disruption Index (CRDI)

Given the cross-sectional design of our study focusing exclusively on patients with established chronic kidney disease, we developed the Circadian Rhythm Disruption Index (CRDI) specifically to quantify relative differences in circadian excretion patterns within the CKD population rather than establishing absolute thresholds against healthy controls. This approach acknowledges the clinical reality that circadian rhythms exist along a continuum of disruption in renal disease, with the CRDI serving to stratify patients based on their relative position within this spectrum. The theoretical foundation for CRDI derivation draws upon established physiological principles and clinical evidence demonstrating that CKD progression involves distinct disturbances in both the day-night distribution and intra-day stability of urinary solute excretion [15]. The 24-h collection period for each participant was segmented into four physiologically meaningful intervals: nighttime (22:00–06:00), morning (06:00–12:00), afternoon (12:00–18:00), and evening (18:00–22:00). For each interval, total excretion of urinary creatinine, protein, and volume was calculated by summing the respective analyte amounts from all voiding events within that period. The CRDI was then derived through a multiplicative integration of two complementary components that reflect core aspects of circadian disruption: (1) the nocturnal-to-diurnal excretion ratio (NDR), calculated as nighttime excretion divided by total daytime excretion (morning + afternoon + evening), which reflects the distribution of excretory load across the diurnal cycle. A deviation from the physiological pattern of reduced nocturnal excretion (typically a day/night urine output ratio of 2–3:1 in health) toward a higher NDR indicates a phase or amplitude disturbance analogous to patterns seen in conditions like nocturnal polyuria [16,17]. (2) the peak-to-trough ratio (PTR), determined as the maximum divided by the minimum excretion among the four periods, which quantifies the amplitude or dynamic range of the excretion rhythm. A PTR approaching 1.0 signifies a flattened rhythm with minimal variation, indicating loss of robust circadian oscillation [18,19]. The complete CRDI formula was thus defined as CRDI = NDR × PTR. This multiplicative approach ensures that substantial rhythm disruption requires concurrent abnormalities in both temporal distribution and pattern stability, reflecting the integrated nature of circadian deterioration in advancing CKD. The clinical rationale for this specific formulation is supported by literature demonstrating that nocturnal excretion dominance independently predicts hypertension risk and target organ damage, while blunted daytime variability correlates with tubular function impairment [3].

Statistical analysis

Prior to calculating the Circadian Rhythm Disruption Index (CRDI), significant 24-h circadian rhythms in urinary protein, creatinine, and volume excretion were confirmed using three complementary methods: (1) Cosinor Rhythmometry: A cosinor model was fitted to the time-series data for each analyte to test the null hypothesis of no circadian rhythm (amplitude = 0). The model was specified as: Y = M + A*cos(2πt/24 + φ) + e, where Y is the excretion rate, M is the MESOR (Midline Estimating Statistic of Rhythm), A is the amplitude, φ is the acrophase, and t is the time of day in hours. (2) Mixed-Effects Cosinor Model: To account for inter-individual variability while providing a population-level rhythm estimate, a mixed-effects cosinor model was implemented with a random intercept for each participant. (3) Non-Parametric Testing: The Kruskal-Wallis test was applied to compare analyte excretion across four pre-defined 6-h time periods (00:00–06:00, 06:00–12:00, 12:00–18:00, 18:00–24:00) to validate rhythmicity without assuming a specific waveform. A rhythm was considered significant if p < 0.05 across all three methods.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) based on their distribution (assessed by Shapiro-Wilk test), and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). The associations between CRDI metrics (CRDI_pro, CRDI_cr, CRDI_vol) and eGFR were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation.

Multivariable linear regression models were constructed to evaluate the independent association of CRDI parameters with eGFR, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, pathological diagnosis, and medication use (SGLT2 inhibitors, ARBs, glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants). Interaction terms were included to test for effect modification by sex, pathological type, and medication. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF < 5 considered acceptable).

The discriminative ability of CRDI metrics for identifying patients with eGFR ≤60 mL/min/1.73 m2 was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Area under the curve (AUC) values were compared using DeLong’s method. The Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) and Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) were calculated to compare the baseline clinical model (age, sex, BMI, diagnosis, medication) against the model incorporating CRDI. The optimal cutoff was determined by maximizing Youden’s index. Clinical utility was assessed via decision curve analysis.

All analyses were performed using R software (v4.5.1). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of our primary finding (the association between CRDI and eGFR), we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses: (1) We performed stratified analyses by eGFR level (dichotomized at 60 mL/min/1.73m2). (2) For the association between CRDI and eGFR, we further adjusted for 24-h total urine volume (UV_24h) to examine whether it was independent of the total urine output. (3) We excluded potential extreme values of CRDI_cr (defined as values beyond 1.5 times the interquartile range) to ensure our results were not driven by outliers. (4) To further evaluate the influence of outliers and model robustness, we calculated Cook’s distance (with a conventional threshold of 4/n) and performed robust regression using M‑estimation. (5) Internal validation: To assess the stability of the CRDI_cr‑eGFR association, we performed bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) on the fully adjusted model, estimating the 95% confidence interval of the regression coefficient using the percentile method. All sensitivity analyses employed the same multivariable adjustment strategy as the primary analysis.

Artificial intelligence-assisted analysis

The statistical analyses and visualizations in this study were conducted using the R language. The generation of statistical code was assisted by a generative artificial intelligence tool (Deep-Seek) under the continuous supervision and direction of the investigators. All AI-generated code was meticulously reviewed, validated, and executed in its entirety on the dataset by the investigators to ensure it accurately implemented the pre-specified statistical analysis plan. The final interpretation of the data and the derivation of conclusions were performed solely by the investigators.

Circadian rhythm validation

A multi-method approach confirmed significant 24-h circadian rhythms in urinary protein, creatinine, and volume excretion (all p < 0.001 by cosinor, mixed-effects cosinor, and Kruskal-Wallis tests). Peak excretion occurred nocturnally: protein at 03:00, creatinine at 04:00, and volume at 01:00 (Figure 2).

Association between CRDI and eGFR

Spearman correlation revealed significant inverse relationships between all CRDI metrics and eGFR, with CRDI_vol showing the strongest correlation (ρ = −0.389, p < 0.001), followed by CRDI_pro (ρ = −0.322, p = 0.004) and CRDI_cr (ρ = −0.304, p = 0.006) (Figure 3). Rhythm amplitude did not correlate with eGFR. In multivariable linear regression, CRDI_cr demonstrated a robust, independent inverse association with eGFR across progressively adjusted models (Table 2, Figure 4). The association persisted in the fully adjusted model (β = −4.45 mL/min/1.73m2 per unit increase, 95% CI: −7.65 to −1.25, p = 0.008). However, the association for CRDI_vol was attenuated upon full adjustment (β = −2.90, p = 0.037), and CRDI_pro showed no significant association.

Interactive analysis and subgroup analysis

Multivariable regression models revealed that pathological diagnosis significantly modified the relationship between eGFR and both CRDI_pro (P for interaction = 0.045) and CRDI_vol (P for interaction = 0.038), but did not significantly modify the CRDI_cr-eGFR association (P for interaction = 0.181). Subgroup correlation analyses further elucidated this pathological specificity (Figure 5). In patients with IgA nephropathy (IgAN), CRDI_cr demonstrated a strong inverse correlation with eGFR (n = 39, ρ = −0.529, p < 0.001), whereas no such association was observed in membranous nephropathy (MN, n = 29, ρ = −0.022, p = 0.912). Among the six patients with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), CRDI_cr showed a moderate inverse correlation with eGFR (ρ = −0.429) that did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.419), likely attributable to the limited sample size. No significant effect modification was detected for sex or for any of the medication classes examined (ARB, SGLT2i, glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants; all P for interaction > 0.05). In exploratory sex‑stratified analyses using the fully adjusted model, CRDI_cr exhibited a significant inverse association with eGFR in males (n = 56, β = −3.91 mL/min/1.73m2 per unit, 95% CI: −7.65 to −0.18, p = 0.046). In females (n = 25), the association was directionally consistent but did not attain statistical significance (β = −7.50, 95% CI: −17.4 to 2.37, p = 0.159), and the confidence intervals for the two groups overlapped considerably.

Discriminative performance and clinical utility

For identifying eGFR ≤60 mL/min/1.73m2, the clinical model alone achieved an AUC of 0.748. Adding CRDI_cr increased the AUC to 0.800 and provided a significant net reclassification improvement (NRI = 0.237). Decision curve analysis indicated that adding CRDI_cr provided a clearer net benefit only at higher decision thresholds (>40%) (Figure 6).

Sensitivity analyses

The inverse CRDI_cr-eGFR association was robust in sensitivity analyses. Patients with eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73m2 had higher median CRDI_cr than those with eGFR ≥60 (1.29 vs. 0.77, p = 0.001). Adjusting for 24-h urine volume did not confound the CRDI_vol-eGFR association (Figure 7). Using the 1.5 × IQR criterion, 7 patients (8.6%) were identified as CRDI_cr outliers. The association remained significant in the adjusted analysis (β = −10.25, 95% CI: −18.56 to −1.94, p = 0.017). Formal outlier assessment (Cook’s distance) indicated minimal influence (0% in overall cohort, 2.6% in IgA nephropathy). Robust regression (M-estimation) confirmed significant associations in both groups (all p < 0.05). Bootstrap resampling (1000 replicates) yielded a mean coefficient of −4.52 (95% bootstrap CI: −9.02 to −1.64), consistent with the primary model estimate (β = −4.45, 95% CI: −7.65 to −1.25), confirming the internal robustness of the association.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study introduces the Circadian Rhythm Disruption Index (CRDI) – a novel clinical-data-derived tool quantifying relative circadian disruption severity within CKD populations for internal risk stratification, not absolute deviation from healthy physiological rhythms. Our principal finding is that a higher CRDI for creatinine (CRDI_cr), indicative of greater relative circadian disruption, exhibits an independent and inverse association with eGFR in patients with primary glomerular diseases. Crucially, this association is not uniform but demonstrates marked pathological specificity, being most robust in IgA nephropathy (IgAN) and remaining significant after adjusting for total urine output. This suggests that the CRDI captures a dimension of physiological derangement – specifically, the integrity of the intrinsic renal circadian clock – that is obscured by conventional, aggregate biomarkers. While this interpretation remains, a working hypothesis requiring direct experimental validation, it is biologically plausible given the established role of molecular clocks in regulating renal transport processes [20].

To contextualize the disruption captured by the CRDI, it is useful to consider the established physiological norm in healthy individuals. In healthy adults, renal circadian function is characterized by a robust diurnal variation, with urine output and solute excretion during the nocturnal rest period typically reduced by 50–70% compared to daytime levels, reflecting the integrated action of central circadian pacemakers and intrinsic renal clocks [19,21,22]. The elevated CRDI_cr values observed in our patients, driven by an increased proportion of nocturnal excretion, therefore represent a marked departure from this expected diurnal pattern. This finding quantifies a state of relative circadian disruption within our CKD cohort, characterized by a blunting of the normal day-night variation in renal excretory function. The superior performance of CRDI_cr, compared to its counterparts for protein or volume, is physiologically grounded. Creatinine excretion, governed by both glomerular filtration and tubular secretion, offers a more stable reflection of intrinsic renal function and its circadian regulation [8,23]. In contrast, protein excretion is highly variable and influenced by non-renal factors, while urine volume is acutely dependent on fluid intake.

The striking absence of a CRDI_cr-eGFR association in membranous nephropathy (MN) compared to its strong presence in IgAN suggests that the pathophysiological role of circadian disruption differs fundamentally between these two glomerulopathies. We propose that this dichotomy may stem from the timing and context of circadian perturbation relative to disease progression. In IgAN, circadian disruption may be an early and integral component. IgAN is frequently accompanied by non-dipping hypertension, a manifestation of circadian misalignment that independently predicts renal decline, and is intricately linked with systemic immune dysregulation, which is itself coupled to the circadian clock [6,24]. Furthermore, the intrarenal renin-angiotensin system (RAS) exhibits intrinsic rhythmicity, and its early activation in IgAN could amplify local clock disruption, leading to altered tubular solute handling [25,26]. Thus, in IgAN, CRDI_cr may capture a primary dysfunction of the kidney’s circadian machinery that contributes to eGFR loss. In contrast, MN is primarily driven by podocyte injury and complement activation, with significant tubulointerstitial involvement typically developing only after prolonged, heavy proteinuria [27,28]. Therefore, many MN patients in our cohort may not yet have reached the threshold of tubulointerstitial damage required for circadian excretory patterns to correlate with eGFR. In this context, circadian disruption in MN may be a secondary consequence of advanced disease rather than an early driver. This pathology-specific pattern highlights the potential of CRDI_cr to identify a ‘chrono-phenotype’ in IgAN that could benefit from chronotherapeutic strategies.

An important and unresolved question is whether the CRDI primarily reflects kidney-specific circadian alterations or more systemic chronodisruption. Urinary excretion rhythms are an integrated output influenced by central circadian pacemakers, systemic hormones, and local renal clocks [8,16,22,29]. While our inpatient protocol controlled for key behavioral confounders (sleep-wake cycle, diet), we cannot definitively dissect these contributions. Furthermore, we excluded individuals with overt exogenous circadian disruptors (e.g., shift workers) to minimize confounding. While strengthening internal validity, this represents a missed opportunity to study extreme chronodisruption. Future studies correlating CRDI with markers of systemic circadian alignment (e.g., melatonin, cortisol) or peripheral clock gene expression would be valuable. Similarly, exploring the relationship between CRDI and other solutes known to be clock-controlled, such as sodium or carnitine, could provide deeper mechanistic insight into the contribution of tubular function versus glomerular filtration [30]. Experimental studies have identified increased urinary carnitine excretion in mouse models with tubule-specific deletion of Bmal1, suggesting that urinary carnitine loss could serve as a sensitive readout of altered tubular clock function [30]. Such findings highlight that circadian clock disruption in CKD likely affects multiple renal cell types – including podocytes, tubular epithelial cells, and vascular cells – each contributing to the integrated excretory phenotype captured by the CRDI [5,8,31]. Future studies incorporating these solutes could help bridge the gap between the CRDI and intrinsic renal clock dysregulation.

We also considered the potential influence of glucocorticoids therapy on circadian rhythms. Glucocorticoids are potent regulators of both central and peripheral circadian clocks, acting through glucocorticoid response elements to modulate clock gene expression and synchronize peripheral oscillators [29]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that glucocorticoids coordinate diurnal micturition patterns by regulating bladder peripheral clocks, and clinical observations have shown that alterations in cortisol rhythmicity are associated with disrupted peripheral clock gene expression in critically ill patients [29,32]. Given this strong biological basis, glucocorticoids therapy would be expected to potentially impact CRDI values. However, its modifying effect on the CRDI_cr-eGFR association was not statistically significant in our cohort. Given the small number of patients on glucocorticoids (n = 16), our study is underpowered to detect such an effect, and this null finding should be interpreted with caution.

A major strength of the CRDI is its pragmatic design for internal risk stratification, obviating the need for healthy controls. This, coupled with the significant net reclassification improvement when adding CRDI_cr to clinical models, underscores its potential to refine prognostication using routine data. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the directionality of the CRDI-eGFR relationship; longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether circadian disruption precedes and predicts functional decline. Second, while the controlled inpatient setting enhanced internal validity by minimizing lifestyle confounders (diet, activity, sleep-wake cycles), this very standardization may limit generalizability to outpatient populations living under free-living conditions. Third, our sample size (n = 81), while sufficient for primary analyses, constrained statistical power for subgroup comparisons. The FSGS subgroup (n = 6) was particularly underpowered; the observed moderate inverse correlation (ρ = −0.429) did not reach significance, and whether the CRDI-eGFR relationship extends to other pathologies remains to be established. Fourth, as a single-center study, our findings require external validation in independent, multi-center cohorts with diverse demographic and clinical profiles, although bootstrap resampling (95% CI: −9.02 to −1.64) supports internal robustness. Fifth, urine collection based on natural urge, while yielding high sampling density (mean 7.9 voids/patient), may not have guaranteed complete bladder emptying at precise intervals, potentially affecting period-specific calculations. Sixth, to minimize confounding from exogenous circadian disruptors, we excluded individuals with shift work exposure or recent transmeridian travel. While this decision strengthened internal validity, it also represents a missed opportunity to establish an internal benchmark for severe, externally imposed chronodisruption. Future studies specifically enrolling such populations could provide valuable insights into the full spectrum of circadian excretory disturbance and its renal consequences. Seventh, we did not assess patients’ habitual dietary intake prior to admission. Acute dietary changes – particularly salt restriction in individuals accustomed to high-salt diets – may activate the RAAS and influence circadian excretory rhythms. The absence of RAAS measurements precludes evaluation of this potential confounding pathway, which should be addressed in future studies incorporating dietary history and RAAS biomarkers. Finally, the present study was designed to develop and validate the CRDI from routine clinical parameters; therefore, we did not measure tubular injury markers (e.g., NGAL, KIM-1) or urinary electrolytes, limiting mechanistic insights into whether CRDI_cr reflects glomerular versus tubular contributions to circadian disruption [33]. Future studies incorporating such markers are warranted.

In conclusion, we present the CRDI as a novel functional biomarker that unveils a previously inaccessible layer of pathophysiology in glomerular diseases. Our findings robustly link quantified circadian excretory disruption to renal function in a pathology-specific manner. If validated prospectively, the CRDI could enable refined risk stratification and pave the way for pathology-specific chronotherapy, enriching our understanding of the circadian dimension of CKD.

Conclusion

This study introduces the Circadian Rhythm Disruption Index (CRDI), a pragmatic tool that quantifies relative circadian disruption in urinary creatinine excretion without requiring healthy controls. We demonstrate its independent association with renal function (eGFR) in primary glomerular diseases, particularly in IgA nephropathy. The CRDI_cr, derived from routine data, holds potential as a complementary biomarker for refining risk assessment in specific patient populations.
