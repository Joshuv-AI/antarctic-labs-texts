Development of Peptide Identification System for ToF-SIMS Spectra Using Supervised Machine Learning
Authors: Satoka Aoyagi, Miya Fujita, Hidemi Itoh, Hiroto Itoh, Takaharu Nagatomi, Masayuki Okamoto, Tomikazu Ueno
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTime-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) data interpretation for organic materials is complicated because of various fragment ions produced from each molecule and the overlapping of certain mass peaks from different molecules. Fragmentation mechanisms in SIMS are complex because different sputtering and ionization processes can simultaneously occur. Therefore, a prediction system that can identify materials in a sample is required. A novel prediction system for peptides based on ToF-SIMS and amino-acid-based teaching information (labels) for supervised machine learning was developed. To develop the prediction system for general organic materials, the annotation of materials is crucial to creating effective labels for supervised learning. Peptides are composed of 20 amino acid residues, which can be used as labels. We previously developed a peptide prediction system using Random Forest, a supervised machine-learning method. However, only the amino acids contained in the target peptide were predicted, and the amino acid sequence was unable to be assumed. In this study, the amino acid sequence of the test peptide was determined by adding the information on two adjacent amino acids to the labels. Once the prediction system learned the target peptide spectra, the peptides in the newly obtained ToF-SIMS spectra could be identified. The new prediction system also provides useful information for the identification of unknown peptides. The prediction results indicate that two adjacent permutations of amino acids are effective pieces of teaching information for expressing the amino acid sequence of a peptide.Keywords: SIMS, peptide identification, amino acid sequence, Random Forest

Introduction

Machine learning techniques have been applied to interpret complicated secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) data.1−19 Unsupervised learning methods, such as principal component analysis (PCA),1−7 non-negativity matrix factorization (NMF),2,3,7−9 self-organization maps (SOM),15−19 and autoencoders (AE)8,9,11−13 are useful for extracting important features of samples and interpreting the SIMS data of complex samples, including unknown materials. Although unsupervised learning methods effectively segment SIMS data into important or pure components in the samples, identifying each peak in the segmented SIMS spectra can be difficult, owing to peak overlap and matrix effects. The SIMS spectrum of an organic sample generally contains hundreds to thousands of fragment ions from multiple molecules, which complicates the interpretation of the SIMS spectra. Introducing a mass spectrometer with a high resolving power, such as a tandem mass spectrometer or Orbitrap, helps identify mass peaks in SIMS. However, important mass peaks for the characterization of unknown samples do not always have sufficient intensity for MSMS. In addition, despite a high-resolution mass spectrometer, the SIMS spectra of complex samples may contain mass peaks that are difficult to identify.

In a previous study, we developed a peptide prediction system for time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) using Random Forest.10 The peptides were predicted based on the ToF-SIMS spectra and labels expressing the presence of the 20 amino acids. The prediction accuracy was almost perfect for peptides included in the training data set. In addition, even for an unknown peptide not included in the training data set, the presence of the 20 amino acids could be predicted. However, the previous system could not predict amino acid sequences because the labels for supervised learning did not contain sequence information.

In this study, new labels that express the two adjacent amino acids in the peptides were added to predict the amino acid sequences of the peptides using a peptide prediction system. In addition to the 1038 ToF-SIMS spectra of the six model peptides used in the previous study, new ToF-SIMS spectra of the other two peptides were added. Identification of the test peptides based on the two adjacent amino acid labels was evaluated. Moreover, the important factors in the ToF-SIMS spectra of the peptide samples for peptide identification were investigated by analyzing the ToF-SIMS spectra used for the peptide identification system by utilizing unsupervised learning methods, such as PCA and sparse autoencoder (SAE).

Experimental Details

Samples

The following six peptides10 were used. S1: Physalaemin (amino acid sequence (A. A. S.) ADPNKFYGLM; molecular weight (M. W.) 1155.81, Scrum, Japan). S2: synthesized peptide no. 1 (A. A. S. ESTHQWCK; M. W. 1018.32, Scrum, Japan). S3: Angiotensin II (A. A. S. DRVYIHPF; M.W. 1046.49, Scrum, Japan). S4: Oxytocin (A. A. S. CYIQNCPLG; M. W. 1008.25, Wako-Fujifilm, Japan). S5: Bradykinin (A. A. S. RPPGFSPFR; M. W. 1060.27, Scrum, Japan). Unknown (U): synthesized peptide no. 2 (A. A. S. AEMTHWCK; M.W. 1005.18, Scrum, Japan). Each peptide solution was added to a silicone substrate dropwise and then dried in a vacuum desiccator. The two newly added peptides were S7—Bovine Andrenal Medulla Dodecapeptide (BMA-12P) (YGGFMRRVGRPE, M.W. 1424.6, Fujifilm, Peptide Lab)—and S8—(Arg8)-Vasotocin (CYIQNCPRG, M.W. 1050.2, Fujifilm, Peptide Institute, Inc.). Table S1 in the Supporting Information demonstrates the amino acid patterns for the eight peptides.

ToF-SIMS

Six peptide samples were analyzed by the VAMAS project participants from 25 institutions, using 27 machines. The participants were from Japan (20), Germany (2), China (1), Korea (1), UK (1), and the USA (2). Ga+, Bi+, Bi3+, Bi32+, C602+, and Ar1000+ ion beams were primarily used. A total of 1038 ToF-SIMS spectra were collected for the six peptides (S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, and U),10 and 45 spectra for the new samples (S7 and S8) were subsequently added. The final number of peptide spectra was 1083.

Data Analysis

Because the conversion of the ToF-SIMS spectra has been described in a previous study,10 the conversion processes are briefly described here. The intensities of the peaks ranging from m/z 14 to 1214 were converted based on the peak list composed of 4230 peaks. The same peak lists, which mainly distinguish inorganic and organic peaks (the bin was approximately 0.3 u), were prepared for SurfaceLab by ION-TOF GmbH (Münster, Germany) and for TOF-DR or WinCadence by ULVAC-PHI (Chigasaki, Japan). The peak intensities were normalized to the total ion count of each spectrum.

In addition to the basic label set expressing the presence of each amino acid (20 patterns), two adjacent amino acids, such as AA and AR (400 patterns), were added to the labels. The permutations of the two adjacent amino acids are listed in the Supporting Information (Table S2a). One-hot encoding was used to express the labels.

The training data set included the spectra of all the peptides for interpolation and did not include the spectra of the test peptides for extrapolation. Seven reference peptide spectra (S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S7, and S8) were included in the training data set for evaluating extrapolation, and the spectra of the peptide samples U and angiotensin I (DRVYVHPFHL) were used for the test data set.

RandomForestClassifier and MultiOutputClassifier from Scikit-learn as well as Python 3 were used to predict the amino acid labels of the peptide samples. There were 150 estimators, and the maximum ratio of the features used for the decision trees (max_features) was 0.1. The Python 3 program is available upon request.

The descriptors (mass peak intensities) were also analyzed using PCA (PLS toolbox, Eigenvector Research Inc.) and SAE (Deep Learning Toolbox, Matlab, Mathworks). The relationship between some of the eight peptides and the important mass peaks used to identify each peptide or peptide group was evaluated based on the PC scores and loadings of the PCA, encoded features, and encoder weights of SAE.

Results and Discussion

Table 1 lists the main prediction results of the test data for two peptides (AEMTHWCK and DRVYIHPF), where the training data set contained the following peptide spectra: ADPNKFYGLM (S1), ESTHQWCK (S2), DRVYIHPF (S3), CYIQNCPLG (S4), RPPGFSPFR (S5), AEMTHWCK (U), YGGFMRRVGRPE (S7), and CYIQNCPRG (S8). There were 148 and 23 spectra for the AEMTHWCK and DRVYIHPF test samples, respectively. The data in Table 2 indicate the number of spectra that provided a label representing each amino acid or two adjacent amino acids. The labels supported by the majority of the test spectra were used to identify the test peptides. Labels with data numbers greater than 120 and 10 were used to predict the test peptides. The complete results for Tables 1 and 2 are presented in Tables S3 and S4, respectively, in the Supporting Information, AEMTHWCK and DRVYIHPF, respectively.

In the test data of the AEMTHWCK spectra, AE, CK, EM, HW, MT, TH, and WC and all of the individual amino acids contained in the test peptide sample (AEMTHWCK) were indicated by the analysis system. Moreover, the amino acid sequence was suggested by the adjacent amino acids AE, EM, MT, TH, HW, WC, and CK. Similarly, the amino acid sequence of another test peptide (DRVYIHPF) was accurately predicted by using the indicated labels of the two adjacent amino acids: PF, DR, RV, VY, YI, IH, and HP. When the spectra of the same peptide as that of the test peptide were in the training data set, the amino acids contained in the test peptide as well as the amino acid sequence were able to be predicted for other peptides as well. As conventional ToF-SIMS using a Bi-cluster ion beam strongly detects mass peaks of less than several hundred, the fragment ions from two neighboring amino acids may be present in the ToF-SIMS spectra. Therefore, the labels of two adjacent amino acids are effective for identifying the amino acid sequences. Moreover, it indicates that the information regarding the two adjacent amino acids is adequate to determine the amino acid sequence of a peptide and that the information on more than two adjacent amino acids is not important for the identification of peptides based on the ToF-SIMS spectra.

The number of labels for the permutation of the two adjacent amino acids (400) can be reduced to 210 by considering the combination without the order of the two amino acids (Table S2b in the Supporting Information). The predicted results of the amino acid sequences using the 230 (210 combinations of the two adjacent amino acids and 20 individual amino acids) labels (Table 2) were similar to those obtained using 420 (400 permutations of the two adjacent amino acids and 20 individual amino acids) labels (Table 1). The 420-label set was more useful than the 230-label set for directly assuming a sequence. In addition, fragment ions from two adjacent amino acids may differ depending on the order of the amino acids, which may reflect the effectiveness of the labels. The structure of a peptide fragment ion can depend on the terminal type remaining in the fragment.25 For example, peptide fragments are frequently generated through cleavage of the peptide bond. Fragments with the C-terminal side have an NH group on the right end, while those with the N-terminal side have a CO group on the left end. These characteristics can potentially influence the ToF-SIMS spectral patterns with the generation of distinct fragment ions observed for the same combination of two amino acids in different orders.

When peptide spectra not included in the training data set were analyzed by the prediction system, the identification was not perfect, but a part of the sequence was assumed. For the prediction of the test peptide AEMTHWCK(U), one of the suggested sequences was ESTHQWCK (S2), because more than 124 (70%) spectra suggested that this test peptide contained the amino acids ES, ST, TH, HQ, QW, WC, and CK labels (Table 3). Although this sequence was incorrect, part of the sequence was indicated, which helped to identify completely unknown peptides. Peptide DRVYVHPFHL was not perfectly predicted, although its amino acid sequence was similar to that of one of the peptides in the training data set (S3: DRVYIHPF). There were 23 spectra for the test peptide DRVYVHPFHL. The labels indicated by more than 70% of the spectra (more than 16) suggested that this test peptide contained the amino acids D, R, V, Y, H, P, and F, as well as the adjacent R and V (Table 4). The complete results for Tables 3 and 4 are presented in Tables S5 and S6, respectively, in the Supporting Information.

The descriptor (mass spectra) part of the data set was analyzed using PCA and SAE to investigate why the AEMTHWCK test peptide was predicted to be ESTHQWCK (S2). The first three principal components (PCs) have more than 85% of the information in the data (Table 5), and PC1 represents the common factor of the eight peptides because Figure 1 shows that the PC1 scores of all of the samples have positive values. The positive score values of PCs 2 and 3 indicate the relationship of S1, S3, and S5 and that of S2 and U, respectively. The relationship between PC2 and PC3 in Figure 1 clearly shows that the S2 and U scores overlap. The mass peaks with high values of PC3 loading mainly contained amino acid fragment ions from many of the common residues of S2 and U, H (m/z 81 and 110), W (m/z 44, 84, and 130), E (m/z 56, 81, 102, and 110), K (m/z 56), and T (m/z 74). The fragment ions from the homopolymers of an amino acid1,20−23 are summarized in the Supporting Information of the previous study (Supplementary Table 8).10 Generally, fragment ions from amino acid residues are frequently detected in ToF-SIMS spectra;1,20−25 therefore, these common amino acid fragment ions of S2 and U may have strongly influenced the prediction of both peptides.

The highly contributing mass peaks of feature no. 3 (F03), mainly representing S5, contained mass peaks higher than those of F01. For example, m/z 598, 323, and 527 were Bradykinin (S5) fragment ions from RPP (a3), RPPGF (a5), and RPPGFS (da6), respectively (a3, a5, and da6 indicate the type of peptide fragment ion25). They are peptide fragment ions that retain their N-termini.

The PCA and SAE results indicated that the identification of a test peptide was influenced by the training peptides with structures similar to those of the test peptide or those that produced the same mass peaks as the test peptide. Both PCA and SAE extracted features that included the common factors ESTHQWCK (S2) and ESTHQWCK. Therefore, the AEMTHWCK (U) test peptide was predicted to be ESTHQWCK (S2). The peptide prediction via RF depends on the amino acid fragment ions and fragment ions related to more than one amino acid residue. The detection of such fragment ions at m/z values of several hundred using ToF-SIMS is important for the identification of peptides using the prediction system.

When the spectra of the same peptide as that of the test peptide were included in the training data set, the test peptide was almost perfectly identified. The current training data set containing the spectra of the eight peptides helps suggest amino acids and parts of sequences of unknown peptides whose information is not included in the training data set, although the prediction of such unknown peptides is limited. A training data set containing the spectra of additional peptides may be useful for correctly predicting more types of unknown peptides. The current prediction system is effective for the ToF-SIMS spectra of the pure peptide samples. Further studies on the prediction of peptides in the ToF-SIMS spectra of peptide mixtures are needed to extend this prediction system to general multipeptide samples.

Conclusions

The peptide prediction system with 420 labels, which expressed the presence of 20 amino acids and the permutations of two adjacent amino acids, determined the amino acid sequences of the test peptides when the ToF-SIMS spectra of the same peptide were included in the training data set. This prediction system is also useful for evaluating unknown peptides and provides helpful information for the identification of unknown peptide identification. The prediction results indicated that two adjacent amino acid permutations are powerful tools for expressing the amino acid sequence of a peptide. The amino acid sequence of a test peptide can be identified as long as for pure peptide samples. Further investigation of peptide prediction based on ToF-SIMS spectra of multipeptide samples is required.
