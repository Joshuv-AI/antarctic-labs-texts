Introduction

The omphalomesenteric duct (OMD) is an embryological remnant of the vitelline duct, a structure that connects the developing fetus to the yolk sac and is responsible for the nutritional support of the fetus during the early embryological days [1]. Typically, during the 5th–10th week of gestation, following the establishment of placental nutrition, it turns into a thin fibrous band that spontaneously obliterates and separates from the intestine [2]. Partial or complete failure of closing and reabsorption of the vitellointestinal duct (VID) may lead to diverse types of congenital intestinal malformations, comprising Meckel's diverticulum (MD), vitelline cord, umbilical sinus, enteric fistula, and hemorrhagic umbilical granuloma [3].

The VID is a relatively rare congenital condition that occurs in approximately 2 % of the general population, with the most common presentation being MD [4]. It is usually asymptomatic (detected incidentally), though symptoms can be seen in a few patients, most commonly in MD [5]. Though a patent vitellointestinal duct (PVID) is the least common form of OMD anomalies, it is the most symptomatic of all [6]. The patient may present differently depending on the specific type of anomaly. The presentation could be due to the anomaly itself or complications secondary to the anomalies, including acute abdomen, gastrointestinal bleeding, intestinal obstruction, intussusception, or umbilical discharge. Once a diagnosis of a POMD is made, the management will be surgical exploration [7].

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [8].

Case presentation

Patient information

We reported a female neonate born to an Ethiopian para II mother after 8 months of amenorrhea. Her last normal menstrual period was unknown, and didn't have an early ultrasound. The mother had antenatal care follow-up at a local health center, and antenatal obstetric ultrasound (U/S) was done twice; both were reported to be normal. Delivery was via spontaneous vaginal delivery, and the baby had a birth weight of 1900 g. She presented to our hospital on the fourth day of postnatal life after she was referred from the health facility where she was born.

She has no known allergies, and there is no reported family history of similar illness.

Clinical findings

At physical examination, the grossly preterm baby (34–36 weeks from Ballard score) was acutely sick-looking in cardiorespiratory distress. She had gross dysmorphic features, including small size, short neck, prominent occiput, and low-set ears. The feet were clubbed and had rocker-bottom deformity. She was cyanotic and had a single heart sound on precordial examination. There was an umbilical area defect with herniated bowel covered with a sac. At the dome of the sac, there was a herniated bowel tissue with exposed mucosa having a T-shaped appearance (Fig. 1). Diagnostic assessment

Blood workups were normal, but C-reactive protein was 52. Abdominal U/S showed herniated bowel contents through an umbilical defect, likely an omphalocele; otherwise, other solid organs were formed and in their anatomical position and were not part of the sac. Echocardiography showed a double outlet right ventricle with an atrial and ventricular septal defect. There was also a small patent ductus arteriosus.

Therapeutic intervention

With the assessment of preterm (24–36 weeks) + low birth weight (LBW) + small for gestational age (SGA) + Edward syndrome + early onset neonatal sepsis (EONS) + major omphalocele + patent omphalomesenteric duct with bowel prolapse + cyanotic CHD (congenital heart disease), the baby was kept nil per os (NPO) on maintenance fluid (MF), intranasal oxygen 1 l/min flow, catheterized and a nasogastric tube was inserted for decompression. Ampicillin, gentamycin, and metronidazole were initiated as per local protocol for the treatment of neonatal sepsis. In the meantime, the mother was counseled about the critical condition of the baby, the possible poor outcome, and the next management plan.

After a consensus was reached with the mother, she was prepared for surgical correction of the abdominal defect and bowel prolapse. Under general anesthesia and endotracheal tube ventilation, the patient's abdomen was explored through a Smiley peri-omphalocele surgical incision. There was a patent omphalomesenteric duct at the distal ileum, with the ileum prolapsing through it (Fig. 2). The bowel distal to the POMD was tiny (likely due to disuse atrophy) (Fig. 3). Normal saline solution was administered via a nasogastric tube to check for patency, and it was patent to the rectum and anal canal. After reducing the prolapse, the POMD was wedge excised, and the ileum was anastomosed. The resected tissue was sent for biopsy. The alimentary tract from the proximal stomach to the rectum was inspected, and there was no proximal atresia, duplication, or perforation. The bowel was returned to the abdominal cavity, and the abdomen was closed. Follow-up and outcomes

The patient was extubated on the operating room table and transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). In the NICU, she was kept NPO on MF and intranasal oxygen 2 l/min.

She deteriorated further in the early post-operative days and died on the second postoperative day. Death was concluded to be from decompensated heart failure due to the complex cyanotic CHD. Otherwise, the abdomen was soft and had no signs of anastomotic leak or sepsis.

Discussion

Vitelline duct malformations comprise a wide spectrum of anatomical structures and associated symptoms. They may range from a completely patent vitelline duct at the umbilicus to a variety of lesser remnants, including cysts, fibrous cords connecting the umbilicus to the distal ileum, granulation tissue at the umbilicus, umbilical hernia, or MD [9]. Symptoms may include fecal fistulas at the umbilicus, intussusception/prolapse of the ileum at the umbilicus, intestinal obstruction from various causes, melena and anemia, abdominal pain and inflammation, among others. Although symptoms occur most frequently during the childhood years (especially in the first 2 years of life), they may also occur throughout the adult years. Although these malformations are found with equal frequency among the sexes, a significantly greater incidence of symptoms is encountered in males. Although MD is the most common VD anomaly (97 %), PVID is the most common symptomatic presentation [10].

PVID is a rare occurrence, and diagnosis is based on clinical and radiological findings. Clinical findings may be atypical in some cases. Symptoms usually appear before the age of 2 years [11]. PVID may present itself as continuous or intermittent discharge through the umbilicus. However, discharge from the umbilicus is a ubiquitous presentation in the pediatric age group, and umbilical granuloma is the most common differential diagnosis in such patients. It is commonly managed by simple thread ligation of the granuloma or by chemical cauterization. PVID should be ruled out in such patients to avoid catastrophe [12]. When the defect is wide enough, it may lead to partial or total prolapse of the intestine through the patent duct. Double intussusception may lead to intestinal obstruction, strangulation, and gangrene of the prolapsed intestinal loop [13,14]. Hence, surgical intervention is necessary as an emergency [13,15]. Delay in seeking treatment for such cases was associated with considerable morbidity and mortality; therefore, the PVID should be excised at the earliest [16]. During surgery, the possible options available are resection of the loop of intestine near the patent duct, followed by primary anastomosis if the bowel is healthy, or exteriorization of the suspected bowel loop if the patient arrives late, with the viability of the intestinal loops in question [13,14]. In addition, primary closure of the VID following reduction of the prolapse can be considered if the patient arrives early without any gross edema over the intestinal loops [14]. But there is a risk of ectopic gastric or pancreatic mucosal retention if the patent duct is not completely excised.

Compared to other similar previous reports, we unfortunately had a fatal outcome. We believe she had multiple reasons for the unfavorable outcome, including late presentation due to delayed referral, being a syndromic baby with serious congenital anomalies, including a serious congenital heart defect.

Strengths and limitations

Our case report describes a rare finding that will be informative for both clinicians and researchers. However, the patient was syndromic with multiple congenital abnormalities, which led to early demise, making long-term outcome assessment difficult.

Conclusion

In conclusion, though a patent OMD is rare, it should be suspected whenever there is discharge via the umbilicus. It can be complicated with bowel tissue prolapse, which is an emergency that needs prompt diagnosis, surgical reduction, and repair of the defect. In addition, clinicians should not overlook umbilical discharge, as it may indicate an underlying serious surgical condition.
