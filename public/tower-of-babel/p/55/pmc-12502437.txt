Effectiveness of a self-determination theory-based module to improve interaction styles of physiotherapy students: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Hesters Laura, Compernolle Sofie, De Craemer Marieke, De Cocker Katrien
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare professionals’ (HCPs) interactions with care recipients influence care recipients’ self-management skills. Since HCPs cultivate these skills during education, enhancing interaction styles in healthcare education is crucial. Therefore, the primary aim of this study is to evaluate the effects of a Self-Determination Theory (SDT)-based course module on physiotherapy students’ (de)motivating interaction styles. The secondary aim is to assess its impact on students’ SDT-based beliefs and self-management support competencies.MethodsA nonequivalent pre-test post-test control group design was employed with students from the Master of Science in Rehabilitation Sciences and Physiotherapy at two universities: one was the intervention group (n = 148), the other the control group (n = 108). The intervention was an SDT-based module embedded in the curriculum with a theoretical and a practical part. The theoretical part was delivered during classes, and the practical part was implemented during internships. When internships were not possible (e.g. lack of consent from care recipients or no internship placement), students completed role-play simulations instead. During that practical part, they observed and reflected on a personal video-recorded treatment session. Students in both the intervention and control group reported twice on their (de)motivating interaction styles (autonomy-support, structure, control, chaos), SDT-beliefs (effectiveness and feasibility beliefs) and self-management support competencies (self-efficacy and performance subscales) via validated questionnaires. Interaction-effects (Group*Time) were analyzed using general linear mixed models.ResultsFor the primary aim, the intervention group showed a significantly larger increase in autonomy-support (mean difference = 0.28, p = .002, 95%CI=[0.11, 0.45]) and a significantly larger decrease in both chaos (mean difference=-0.84, p < .001, 95%CI=[-1.10, -0.58]) and control (mean difference=-0.98, p < .001, 95%CI=[-1.23, -0.72]), compared to the control group. Regarding the secondary aim, SDT-effectiveness belief (mean difference = 0.31, p < .001, 95%CI=[0.18, 0.44]) and the six subscales of self-efficacy in self-management support (mean differences = between 0.27 and 0.37, p < .012, 95%CI=[0.09, 0.58]) increased significantly more in the intervention group compared to the control group.ConclusionsThe integration of an SDT-based course module in physiotherapy education may enhance physiotherapy students’ (de)motivating interaction skills, SDT-effectiveness beliefs, and self-efficacy in self-management support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07805-4.

Background

The way healthcare professionals (HCPs) interact with people living with chronic diseases is crucial for supporting them to adequately self-manage their health conditions [1, 2]. Consequently, it is essential to train HCPs’ interaction styles during healthcare education [3]. The Self-Determination Theory (SDT) offers an appropriate framework for HCPs (in training) to understand how to interact with care recipients [4] and to cultivate a (more) supportive healthcare environment that can promote motivation and healthier behaviors [5–7]. A large group of HCPs are physiotherapists, who have an important role as promoter, preventer, and rehabilitator [4]. As effective theory-based education programs focusing on (de)motivating interaction styles for physiotherapy students are limited [4, 8], the aim of the present study was to evaluate the effectiveness of an SDT-based course module on the (de)motivating interaction styles (primary outcome), and SDT-based beliefs and self-management support competencies (secondary outcomes) of physiotherapy students.

HCPs’ interaction styles and self-management support

According to SDT, a widely used motivational theory [9], HCPs have different ways to interact with their care recipients. They can support or thwart care recipients’ basic psychological needs: autonomy (i.e. feeling empowered and having choice), competence (i.e. feeling capable and competent), and relatedness (i.e. feeling close to and valued by others) [9]. In alignment with the classification of these needs, SDT offers guidance on how HCPs should interact with care recipients. In healthcare settings, four interaction styles can be distinguished based on theory and empirical evidence [9–11]: two motivating styles, i.e. autonomy-support and structure, that support care recipients’ basic psychological needs and are associated with adaptive outcomes in care recipients, and two demotivating styles, i.e. control and chaos, that thwart these needs and are associated with maladaptive outcomes. The theory outlines that autonomy-supportive interactions mainly support care recipients’ need for autonomy, while structuring interactions mainly foster the need for competence. In contrast, controlling interactions specifically thwart care recipients’ autonomy and chaotic interactions undermine their competence [9, 12, 13].

Autonomy-supportive HCPs identify, nurture and develop care recipients’ interests, preferences and personal goals, by offering meaningful choices, using inviting language and accepting care recipients’ input [14, 15]. A retrospective study indicated that autonomy-supportive physiotherapists positively predict care recipients’ treatment motivation and rehabilitation adherence [12]. Structuring HCPs inform care recipients about what to do and how to achieve the desired outcomes by offering help, guidance, and feedback. No empirical studies were found focusing on care recipient outcomes of a structuring style, but it is theoretically known to promote care recipients’ self-efficacy, competence in managing their chronic conditions and achieving health behavior change [9]. In contrast, controlling HCPs ignore care recipients’ perspective and pressure to control their actions, thoughts, or feelings [14, 16, 17]. Care recipients in a controlling healthcare climate reported feelings of obligation, less persistence, and negative emotional experiences (e.g. pressure, frustration, boredom) [13, 15, 16]. Finally, chaotic HCPs leave care recipients on their own, making them confused about what to do and how to achieve their goals. No studies have already focused on care recipient outcomes of a chaotic style, but chaos is theoretically linked to thwarting care recipients’ needs [9, 16].

Consequently, to positively affect care recipient outcomes, HCPs are recommended to apply autonomy-supportive and structuring interaction styles, while avoiding controlling and chaotic styles. Currently, few SDT-related studies have examined all four interaction styles of HCPs. Nurses (in practice and in training) reported relatively high levels of autonomy-support and structure (3.76 and 3.59, respectively), and rather low levels of control and chaos (1.87 and 1.64, respectively) on a 0–5 scale [14]. Despite these rather optimistic self-reported figures, recent research revealed that 51% of care recipient complaints are about HCPs’ communication, often due to controlling (e.g. mocking, discrimination) and chaotic (e.g. impersonal interactions, impatience, ignoring) behaviors [18]. This might underscore the need to improve HCPs’ (de)motivating interaction styles.

Related to the interaction styles of HCPs, their actions to support care recipients’ self-management are also important [1, 2, 19]. To effectively support self-management, HCPs need to master five behavioral competency categories as described in the 5As model of behavior change: assess (i.e. assessing care recipients’ motivation and beliefs), advise (i.e. providing information and instruction), agree (i.e. shared decision-making and mutual goal setting), assist (i.e. assisting care recipients with overcoming barriers in daily living), and arrange (i.e. follow-up care) [20]. However, recent studies showed that physiotherapists’ competencies to support self-management are not optimal [21–24]. Consequently, future HCPs need to be trained to optimize their knowledge, skills, beliefs and competencies needed to effectively facilitate self-management support.

Educating interaction styles and self-management support

Given the importance of HCPs’ (demotivating) interaction styles and their role in supporting care recipients’ self-management, the question arises how these competencies can best be developed. Since the foundations of effective communication and self-management support are laid in professional training, educational programs play a crucial role in shaping (future) HCPs’ ability to interact in a motivating way. Ideally, this should start during healthcare education [25, 26]. Although SDT has been widely applied in general education and sports contexts, its use in healthcare training remains limited [5, 8, 16, 27]. Some evidence is available for in-service HCPs. An experimental study conducted among nurses (51%), general practitioners (11%), occupational therapists (7%), and physiotherapists (4%) showed higher levels of motivating and lower levels of demotivating styles after following an SDT-based intervention, involving a 10-hour workshop for HCPs on counseling people with chronic diseases [27]. Additionally, a previous SDT-intervention aimed at enhancing physiotherapists’ self-management support and communication strategies (i.e. autonomy-support, structure, and interpersonal involvement) has shown improvements in their effectiveness beliefs in applying SDT-based strategies [28]. However, studies investigating the effects of such SDT-based interventions during pre-service healthcare education are lacking.

Systematic and scoping reviews on effective educational methods to train future HCPs recommend incorporating tutorials, interactive sessions (e.g. role-play), learner-centered approaches (e.g. active groups or one-to-one learning), the use of feedback, hands-on elements, and reflection [8, 29, 30]. For instance, video recordings can facilitate professional development by allowing self‐reflection and providing a practical means for feedback, particularly in improving communication skills [8, 30, 31]. This approach has shown promise in health care, for example, with rheumatologists valuing it for reflection on their practice [31] and general practitioners in training perceiving it as invaluable for bridging theory and practice, thereby solidifying their knowledge [32]. Consequently, a learner-centered approach incorporating video recordings, observations, and feedback is recommended to improve the interaction styles of HCPs in training. At this stage, evidence is missing regarding the use of these educational methods within theory-based training aimed at enhancing future HCPs’ (de)motivating interaction styles and self-management support.

Present study

Physiotherapists play an important role in care recipients’ self-management support [1, 2], but care recipients often complain about HCPs’ interactions [18]. Therefore, it is crucial to educate physiotherapy students to interact in a motivating (i.e. need-supportive) way, while reducing demotivating (i.e. need-thwarting) styles. This requires optimizing physiotherapy educational programs to equip future therapists with both the theoretical knowledge and practical skills necessary for motivating and effective person-centered care. The intervention in this study consisted of an SDT-based course module, integrating a theoretical part [27, 28], which was reinforced with a tailored practical part that promotes self-reflection and peer-feedback [8, 29, 30] aiming to optimize the interaction styles of graduating physiotherapists. The present study examined the effects of this SDT-based module embedded in the education of physiotherapy students. The study’s primary outcomes were the motivating and demotivating interaction styles, as the main focus of the course module is on SDT-based interaction styles. It is expected that the motivating interaction styles of the future physiotherapists will increase, while the demotivating interaction styles will decrease after receiving the course module (versus no change in the control group). The secondary outcomes included SDT-based beliefs and their self-management support competencies as it is assumed that the module might implicitly impact students’ effectiveness and feasibility beliefs of SDT-based strategies and their self-efficacy and performance in self-management support [33]. It is expected that the SDT-based beliefs and their self-management support competencies will increase after the SDT-based module (versus no change in the control group). These findings have the potential to inform the education of physiotherapists and possibly other healthcare professions, ultimately enhancing the quality of care provided.

Methods

Procedure and participants

A nonequivalent pre-test post-test control group design was used among students in the first year of the Master of Science in Rehabilitation Sciences and Physiotherapy of two universities in Flanders, Belgium (Ghent University and Hasselt University). Students at Ghent University, serving as the intervention group, received an SDT-based module embedded within the course ‘Exercise is Medicine’ as part of their educational program. Conversely, students at Hasselt University comprised the control group, having a similar educational program as the intervention group, without an SDT-based component in their courses. Throughout the semester, both groups kept participating in their regular education, including attending other class-based courses, conducting research for their master’s thesis and participating in internships. Data collection occurred in two waves, corresponding to the second semester of academic years 2021–2022 and 2022–2023. The baseline measurements were conducted in February, before the start of the course, the post-measurements three months later (see Fig. 1). The control group only participated in the second wave (i.e. academic year 2022–2023) also in the second semester.

Study procedure

The study was explained to the physiotherapy students during class. In the intervention group, this was done by the teacher who was responsible for the SDT-based course module, while in the control group the main researcher did so during a class of a teacher who agreed to spend some time on this. Participation in this study was voluntary and students were explicitly informed that participation would not affect their course scores. Participants provided written or digital consent – depending on the participating method – before completing the baseline questionnaire. In the intervention group, the online questionnaire was administered via Pointerpro (i.e. software platform to create online surveys) at the start of a class, while the control group completed a paper-and-pencil version of the questionnaire at the end of a class. To maximize participation, students in the intervention group received a general reminder via the official digital learning environment of the university to make sure that students not attending the class were also able to fill out the questionnaire and participate in the study. The questionnaires were completed in a confidential manner and data collection was pseudonymized. Participants’ gender, birth year and month were processed in such a manner that made individual identification impossible. To link the baseline and post-measurement, linking variables were used (i.e. house number, last three digits of phone number, and shoe size). No names were recorded. The data file was accessible to the main researcher and principal investigator only. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Ghent University Hospital (BC-11016).

Of the 700 eligible physiotherapy students (589 intervention group, 111 control group), 256 completed the baseline measurement (148 intervention group, 108 control group). This corresponds to response rates of 25.1% and 97.3%, respectively, and an overall response rate of 36.6%. The baseline sample characteristics and homogeneity results are provided in Table 1. Most of the participants had no work experience (92.2%) outside of their academic program. An independent samples t-test showed no significant differences between intervention and control group for age and a Chi²-test showed no difference in work experience. However, Chi²-test for gender revealed a different distribution between both groups (p <.001). Specifically, the control group exhibited a higher proportion of male participants (48.1%) compared to the intervention group (23.6%).

Comparison of the outcome variables of both groups at baseline is provided in detail in Appendix A. In brief, students in the intervention group reported lower levels of structure and performance in “assess”, “advise”, “agree” and “assist” compared to the control group. However, the intervention group had higher scores on SDT-based feasibility beliefs, and self-efficacy and performance of “overall competence” than the control group.

At the post-test, 159 students (110 in the intervention group, 49 in the control group) completed the measurement. The missing post-measurement data were primarily due to the participants’ busy educational schedule and limited allocated class time for completing the survey. The dropout-rate was significantly higher (ꭕ²(1) = 22.24, p <.001) in the control group (54.6%) than in the intervention group (25.7%). Overall, a higher proportion of male students dropped out (x²(1) = 6.36, p =.012), though, the gender difference was not significant within each group separately. Additionally, independent samples t-tests showed that baseline-only participants reported higher scores on structure (p =.014) and control (p =.002). No other significant differences were found between baseline-only participants and those who completed measurements on both time points.

Intervention SDT-based course module

The SDT-based module was embedded into an existing course and comprised of a theoretical and a practical part (see Fig. 1 and Appendix B). The theoretical part covered behavior change theories relevant to health care, such as the social cognitive theory [34] and the ASE-model (i.e. attitude, social norm and self-efficacy) [35]. Special attention was given to SDT, focusing on HCPs’ (de)motivating interaction styles, care recipients’ basic psychological needs, and motivation for treatment adherence. Students explored how interaction styles can either support or thwart care recipients’ need satisfaction, and the importance of care recipients’ need satisfaction and motivation. The theoretical content was delivered through lectures, written cases (to encourage reflection on behavior and its impact) and video examples (to illustrate physiotherapists’ behavior and concretize abstract concepts). This was followed by exercises and practical guidance on encouraging motivating and reducing demotivating interaction styles in clinical practice. All content was tailored to the physiotherapist-care recipient context. In wave 1, students followed three on-campus classes (two of 3 h, one of 1.5 h). In wave 2, the same content was provided via a self-paced online learning path. Participation in the SDT-based module was mandatory.

After the theoretical part, students completed the Situations in Self-management support-(SIS-)HCP questionnaire (see 2.3. Measurements) to receive their personal interaction profile (see Fig. 2). Students used this profile to reflect on their interaction styles and prepare for their upcoming treatment session. To facilitate this reflection, they answered three self-reflection questions, followed by three forward-looking questions (see Appendix C for the questions). Students then selected a care recipient during their internship and video-recorded two treatment sessions of their choice. If no internship was ongoing, they recorded a role-play simulation with a volunteer (e.g. a family member). In all cases, the care recipient or relative granted permission to film the treatment by signing an informed consent. To protect confidentiality, the camera was positioned behind the care recipient and all persons appearing in the session were rendered unrecognizable by the student before sharing it with peers and the supervising instructor. Access to the recordings was strictly limited to the student, peer-students involved and the supervising instructor, the researchers did not have access to the video material.

Example of a personal interaction profile

Students observed both their own and a peer’s video-recorded treatment using the valid and reliable Coding and Observing Need-Supportive Counseling in Chronic Care Encounters (COUNSEL-CCE) tool [36]. This tool provides a structured approach for observing and analyzing the recorded treatment, thereby fostering constructive feedback. The COUNSEL-CCE assesses HCPs’ (de)motivating interaction styles and thus serves as a suitable tool for students to reflect on their own and others’ interaction and communication, as well as its potential impact on the care recipient. Students received a manual to use the COUNSEL-CCE tool optimally. After completing the COUNSEL-CCE form, students sent their observation form to the peer-student, and vice versa, to reflect on their behavior. Next, to further enhance their understanding and insights, students utilized the reflection cycle developed by Korthagen and Vasalos [37] to look back on their experiences during the treatments. They were taught how to use this framework by means of a(n) (online) lecture. Students were given two months to go through this comprehensive practical part.

The control group received no SDT-related training. However, reflection on their communication was part of their internships. Their internship mentors provided feedback and scores using the CanMEDS competency model, focusing on the seven professional roles, including the ‘communicator’ role [38]. This role emphasizes clear, transparent, effective, and efficient communication during therapy, using verbal, non-verbal and written methods. Students were introduced to these roles through an informational session and a descriptive guide. The intervention group was not introduced to this model.

Measurements

All measures used in this study were self-reported Dutch questionnaires.

Primary outcome (de)motivating interaction styles

The primary outcomes of the study are the four distinct (de)motivating interaction styles, as this was the main focus of the SDT-based course module. Physiotherapy students’ interaction styles were measured using the SIS-HCP questionnaire. This vignette-based questionnaire [11] allowed physiotherapy students to report how they perceive their own interaction styles when counseling people with chronic diseases. Although originally designed for nurses, the SIS-HCP has been slightly adapted to better represent the situation faced by physiotherapists (Supplementary File S1). In the SIS-HCP questionnaire, participants scored ten physiotherapy situations, each with four reactions, on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from zero (‘does not describe me at all’) to five (‘completely describes me’). The SIS-HCP for nurses has good construct validity, internal consistency, and test-retest reliability [11]. In the current sample, McDonald’s omega (ω) ranged between 0.86 and 0.89 at baseline and between 0.83 and 0.90 for the post-measurement, corresponding with a good internal consistency [39].

Secondary outcomes SDT-based beliefs and self-management support

To assess the integration of SDT-based beliefs, participants’ beliefs about SDT-based strategies were assessed using 15 situations in physiotherapy practice. They rated each situation in terms of effectiveness (effectiveness beliefs: agreeing with the statement) and feasibility (feasibility beliefs: finding the statement feasible) when implementing that situation in counseling and treatment [10]. Both aspects were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from one (‘not at all’) to five (‘completely’) for both effectiveness and feasibility. The effectiveness beliefs (ωbaseline = 0.84, ωpost = 0.86) and feasibility beliefs (ωbaseline = 0.81, ωpost = 0.86) of SDT-based strategies were measured separately by means of the 15 situations each.

Finally, to assess self-management support (as part of promoting health change of care recipients), the Self-Efficacy and Performance in Self-management Support (SEPSS-36) was used. The SEPSS-36 questionnaire is valid and reliable to assess competencies to support self-management [40]. The 36-item questionnaire measures physiotherapy students’ (i) beliefs in their ability to apply the key competencies of self-management support to change care recipients’ behavior (self-efficacy) and (ii) perceived performance in these competencies (performance). The SEPSS categorizes six subscales, based on the 5As model: assess (i.e. assessing care recipients’ motivation and beliefs), advise (i.e. providing information and instruction), agree (i.e. shared decision-making and mutual goal setting), assist (i.e. assisting care recipients with overcoming barriers in daily living), arrange (i.e. follow-up care) [20], and overall competencies for self-management support (i.e. like a partnership approach). Those subscales reflect the skills required throughout each phase of care recipients’ self-management support [20, 40]. Each subscale comprises six situations rated for self-efficacy (SE; ‘I think I can do this’) and for performance (Perform: ‘I do this’) of self-management support in daily practice on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from zero (‘not at all’) to four (‘good’) and from zero (‘never’) to four (‘always’), respectively [40]. The subscales scores were calculated by averaging their corresponding six items (ωbaseline−SE = 0.78-0.89, ωpost−SE = 0.83-0.87; ωbaseline−Perform = 0.77-0.90, ωpost−Perform = 0.82-0.87) [39].

Data-analyses

In the preliminary analysis, the kurtosis and skewness indicate normal distribution of continuous data, which is consistent with previous research [10, 41, 42]. Furthermore, as in previous research, the Likert scales were treated as continuous variables [10, 41, 42].

To answer the research questions, general linear mixed models were conducted to assess intervention-effects (Group*Time) on physiotherapy students’ interaction styles, SDT-beliefs, and self-management support competencies. The analyses were controlled for gender due to its unequal distribution (see Table 1). Group (i.e. intervention and control group) and Time (i.e. baseline and post-measurement) were considered as fixed, while participants were defined as a random-effect factor. Linear mixed models were chosen since they compare the changes over time between groups while maximizing the use of all obtained data through maximum-likelihood estimation, which effectively handles missing data [43]. To assess the sensitivity of these intention-to-treat analyses (i.e. including all participants who completed baseline measures, n = 256), additional ‘complete case’-analyses were performed (i.e. only including participants who completed both baseline and post-measurements, n = 159).

Next, to take the delivery mode of the theoretical part of the module into account, additional general linear mixed model analyses were conducted with three groups (i.e. intervention wave 1, intervention wave 2, and control group). As these analyses showed no different intervention-effects for the two waves, the findings were not reported in this paper.

To account for type-1 errors due to the multiple models, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used [44]. It ranks the individual p-values and compares them to progressively less stringent cutoffs. All analyses were performed using RStudio 4.2.2 with the significance level consistently set at p <.05 (two-tailed) and considering p <.10 as marginally significant.

Primary outcome (de)motivating interaction styles

The Group*Time effect was significant for autonomy-support and marginally significant for structure (see Table 3). The intervention group showed a significant increase in autonomy-support and structure after the SDT-based module, while the motivating interaction styles did not significantly change in the control group (see Table 3). For the demotivating interaction styles, significant Group*Time effects were found, showing that the intervention group reported significant decreases in both chaos and control, while the control group reported significant increases over time in these styles (see Table 3). These results were in line with the results of the complete case analyses, except that the marginally significant Group*Time effect for structure was no longer significant in the sensitivity analyses (see Appendix D).

Secondary outcomes: SDT-based beliefs and self-management support

A significant Group*Time effect was found for effectiveness beliefs of SDT-based strategies. The intervention group showed a significant improvement in these beliefs compared to no change in the control group (see Table 3 and Appendix D). There was no significant Group*Time effect for feasibility beliefs of SDT-based strategies. This was in line with the results of the complete case analyses, except that the significant Group*Time effect for effectiveness beliefs was marginally significant in the sensitivity analysis (p =.052, see Appendix D).

Regarding self-efficacy in self-management support, significant Group*Time effects were found across all subscales. The intervention group improved significantly on all six competencies, while no changes were found in the control group (except for an increase in the subscale “advise”). When analyses were restricted to complete cases, the Group*Time effects of the subscales “advise” and “agree” were only marginally significant, and no significant Group*Time effect was observed for “arrange” (see Appendix D). Regarding performance of self-management support, significant Group*Time effects were observed for the subscales “assess” and “overall competence”, and marginally significant for “agree”, with significant increases over time in the intervention group and (marginally) significant increases in the control group, except for “overall competence” (see Table 3). When considering only complete cases, the significant Group*Time effects were marginally significant for the subscales “assess” and “overall competence” and not significant for the subscale “agree” (see Appendix D).

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

This study explored the impact of an SDT-based course module on physiotherapy students’ (de)motivating interaction styles (primary outcome), and SDT-based beliefs and self-management support (secondary outcomes). Current findings offer valuable insights into enhancing healthcare education towards improving care recipients’ self-management. This quasi-experiment showed positive Group*Time effects for self-perceived interaction styles, effectiveness beliefs of SDT-based strategies, and self-efficacy in self-management support.

More specifically, physiotherapy students who received an SDT-based course module reported increases in autonomy-support, while students who did not receive the module reported no change in autonomy-support. This means that after completing the module, students in the intervention group reported offering more meaningful choices and using more inviting language [9, 16]. For care recipients, these improved autonomy-support may lead to more positive treatment motivation, better rehabilitation adherence, and less negative emotions [9, 12, 16]. It should be acknowledged that, at baseline, the intervention group reported slightly lower levels of autonomy-support compared to the control group, potentially leaving more room for improvement. However, at post-intervention, the intervention group level of autonomy-support increased more over time (even above the levels of those who did not receive the module), indicating a meaningful impact of the intervention beyond baseline differences. Similar results appeared for the structuring interaction style (increase in intervention group, no change in control group), however, this Group*Time effect was only marginally significant after applying the Benjamini-Hochberg correction. The lack of Group*Time effect for the structuring interaction style is surprising and contrasts with a previous SDT-based study involving HCPs in practice, where both motivating styles were higher and both demotivating styles were lower in the intervention group compared to the control group [27]. However, an important distinction is that the earlier study only reported post-measurement group differences, whereas the present study’s inclusion of baseline measurements provides a more robust picture of the intervention’s effectiveness. Several factors may help explain the lack of Group*Time effect for structure. First, students may find autonomy-support more intuitive, easier to implement, or more recognizable to reflect on than structure. Second, the intervention may not have emphasized all interaction styles equally, as students were free to choose which motivating style to focus on (see Appendix C). Unfortunately, the researchers had no access to the reflections, making it impossible to clarify which student focused on which motivating style. In the future, it might be good to provide students with more guidance during their reflection and ask them to pay attention to both motivating interaction styles. Lastly, implementing a more structuring interaction style during internships may be more challenging due to, for example, contextual barriers such as time constraints or lack of role models [45].

Regarding demotivating interaction styles, the interaction-effects followed the expected direction, with the intervention group showing greater decreases over time in control and chaos compared to the control group. A remarkable finding of the present study is the increase in both demotivating styles in the control group over time, showing more controlling and chaotic interactions from baseline to post-measurement. Several explanations may account for this. One possibility for the unexpected increase in self-reported demotivating interaction styles in the control group is that these students may have completed the questionnaire more thoughtfully or became more self-critical over time, particularly as they gained more internship experience. Alternatively, in the absence of training in motivating interaction styles, it may lead students by default to less supportive and even need-thwarting styles. Contextual factors during internships (such as limited supervision, time pressure, or a lack of role models) may also have contributed to the increase in demotivating styles in the control group [35, 45]. Moreover, it is possible that students did not perceive their need-thwarting behaviors as detrimental to care recipients, and thus did not consciously avoid them. Nevertheless, research showed that these behaviors do thwart care recipients’ basic psychological needs and result in negative emotional experiences [9, 13, 16]. This detrimental evolution in the control group highlights the need to educate future HCPs with practical tools and guidance to avoid demotivating interaction styles and to develop and maintain motivating interaction styles throughout their internships.

Regarding the secondary outcomes, as expected, significant Group*Time effects were found for effectiveness beliefs of SDT-based strategies, all six subscales of self-efficacy in self-management support, and the subscales “assess” and “overall competence” of performance in self-management support. However, in contrast to the hypothesis, there was a lack of Group*Time effects for feasibility beliefs and some subscales of performance in self-management support. Several factors may explain these findings. First, the group that did not receive the SDT-based course module also improved over time in feasibility beliefs, self-efficacy in “advise” and performance in “assess”, “advise”, “agree”, “assist”, and “overall competence”. These improvements align with most of the outcomes that did not show significant Group*Time effects. This might suggest that also without an SDT-based course module, physiotherapy education has a positive impact on feasibility beliefs of SDT-based strategies. Second, the course module’s primary focus on the SDT-framework – without explicitly targeting the 5As model of behavior change for self-management support competencies – may also have contributed to the lack of additional Group*Time effects. Additionally, the higher self-efficacy scores compared to performance (in both groups) suggest that students may face challenges in translating self-efficacy or knowledge into practice – a common issue during education and in the field [42, 46, 47].

Strengths and limitations

A first strength of this study is the fact that the intervention was grounded in theory, here SDT, an empirically based framework, suited for investigating and promoting (de)motivational interaction styles in healthcare settings [9, 15]. SDT’s emphasis on the universal importance of basic psychological needs offers a strong foundation for designing interventions aimed at enhancing self-management, behavior change and therapy adherence [9, 15]. However, at the same time, we acknowledge the fact that other theories or frameworks can be useful to support self-management [48, 49] or behavior change [50], and SDT is not free of limitations or criticism [51, 52]. For example, the experiences of care recipients’ needs and interaction styles may be personal and context-related (versus universal), leading to variability across situations. This may highlight the importance of other individual circumstances and factors that shape care recipients’ self-management and therapy adherence, such as family support and the reason for therapy [53, 54], next to the role of the HCP [55]. Secondly, the course module was strengthened by practical components that required future physiotherapists to engage in both self- and peer-feedback, in which they received information on how to use it. This approach encourages developing feedback skills and fosters self-awareness, and reflection on both personal and peer behavior. Thirdly, addressing an important gap in the literature [30], methodological strengths include that this study’s design was controlled, and that reliable and valid measurements were used [11, 40].

However, some limitations need to be mentioned too. First, the reliance on self-reports may have caused socially desirable answers, particularly because the post-measurement of the SIS-HCP was part of the intervention group’s curriculum and incorporated into the course’s grade for a small part. To address these considerations, future research could use care recipient reports or external rating of the interaction styles. Accordingly, future studies could explore changes in care recipients’ experiences such as the satisfaction and frustration of their basic psychological needs, their motivation, therapy adherence, behavior change, and self-management. A second limitation is the short-term post-measurement period. Future longitudinal studies are important to measure long-term behavior change of HCPs, which could enrich the sustainability of the education process. Such studies can examine whether the observed improvements in physiotherapy students’ outcomes extend beyond internships and are transferred to real clinical practice. Third, interaction styles were measured as separate dimensions. It is important to consider that HCPs do not adhere to a single interaction style exclusively but exhibit a combination of styles [14, 33]. Fourth, although including a control group at the same stage of their master’s program (i.e. second semester of the first master year) was a strength, random allocation was not feasible for practical reasons. This may have contributed to some baseline differences between the groups. However, these baseline measurements were taken into account in the analyses to minimize their potential impact on the results. Further, it is important to acknowledge that students’ usual education continued alongside the intervention, with different accents in the universities’ programs and curricula. Nevertheless, both programs align with the same national graduation outcomes, ensuring comparable educational standards and similar academic trajectories, including a master’s thesis, internships (including self-reflection and mentor feedback) and standard physiotherapy courses. Still, minor differences in content may have occurred due to variations in scheduling, instructors, or elective choices. Fifth, more dropouts occurred in the control group than the intervention group, likely due to the non-optimal timing of the post-measurement and the initially high response rate in the control group at baseline. However, sensitivity analyses (only including participants with complete baseline and post-intervention measures) revealed similar results as the intention-to-treat analyses (maximum use of data including all participants who completed baseline measures). Sixth, since only two universities of the same educational stage and profession were included in this study, at this stage, the findings cannot be generalized to other health professions (such as occupational therapy, speech language therapists, dietitian, nursing), different educational stages, or other universities or colleges. However, future research can address these limitations.

Practical implications

This study has several implications for healthcare education. First, the results indicate that focusing on improving physiotherapy students’ interaction skills and self-management support is welcome, as students scored higher on control and lower on autonomy-support, self-efficacy (except for subscales “agree” and “assist”) and performance (except for “agree” “assist”, and “arrange”) in self-management support on the reported outcomes at baseline, compared to nurses, as reported in previous studies [14, 41]. Second, the results of this study support the integration of an SDT-based course module into physiotherapy education. This SDT-based course module holds considerable potential to enhance future HCPs’ ability to support people with chronic diseases by strengthening their interaction skills essential for supporting care recipients’ basic psychological needs [9, 10] and motivation [12], which can have a positive impact on therapy adherence and clinical practice [12].

The module was embedded in a course programmed in the first masters’ year and focused on developing students’ knowledge, skills and competencies, using methods such as in-class lectures, online learning, self-assessment, self-reflection, and peer-observation. The findings suggest that applying this SDT-based course module during healthcare education has added value for educating those competences [8, 30]. The module could also be effective when integrated and linked to the clinical internship component of the educational program, which starts in the bachelor’s years. Given the structured format of the module and the online delivery mode in wave 2, the intervention is adaptable to online platforms and requires minimal training for educators, increasing its applicability across programs. Although this study did not focus on between-wave comparison (i.e. in-class versus online delivery mode of aspects of the theoretical part), the similar Group*Time effects (data not shown) suggest that both teaching methods are effective. This finding aligns with recent studies reporting comparable outcomes between in-person and online courses [56–58]. Still, to gain deeper insight into how students engage with and respond to specific delivery methods or particular components of the SDT-based course module, future research could incorporate student engagement as an additional variable or compare the effects of different (delivery) methods and teaching activities.

It should be acknowledged that recording a treatment session posed challenges as ensuring care recipients’ privacy is essential under all circumstances. Hence, the implementation of that part of the module can be experienced as a barrier. Solutions should be found so that peer-observation can still be facilitated. Another way to address privacy concerns related to video recordings could be to ask the care recipient to provide feedback after the therapy session regarding physiotherapy students’ interaction styles and self-management support. A short anonymous or identified online survey – depending on wishes of care recipients – can be developed on which the HCP (student) can reflect on. This can be developed and tested in the future.

Although beyond the scope of this study, there are possibilities to broaden this SDT-based module to, for example, other educational programs and HCPs in practice. To make the module more suitable for them, content adaptations could be recommended. The SDT-component needs to be adapted to situations relevant for their profession (written and video cases). When targeting HCPs in practice, high workload might pose a barrier to participate in a training program [59]. However, this should not hinder opportunities in lifelong learning and efforts to improve the quality of care. To increase feasibility, it might be valuable to develop a less intensive version of a program tailored to working HCPs. Given their practical experience, the applied components of the training could be integrated into their job.

Conclusion

This study enriches the SDT-based literature on optimizing healthcare education by evaluating the effectiveness of an SDT-based course module in physiotherapists’ curriculum. The findings suggest that integrating an SDT-based module can improve graduating physiotherapists’ (de)motivating interaction skills. Positive Group*Time effects were found for self-perceived autonomy-support, control and chaos, as well as for effectiveness beliefs of SDT-based strategies, self-efficacy in self-management support, and the subscales “assess” and “overall competence” of performance in self-management support. While these results are promising, conclusions regarding effectiveness and generalizability should be drawn with caution due to the specific and controlled educational setting.
