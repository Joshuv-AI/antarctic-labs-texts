Sex-independent visual evoked potential complexity
Authors: Łukasz Lisowski, Łukasz Łabieniec, Jolanta Lisowska, Andrzej Andrejczuk, Dominik Zalewski, Joanna Konopińska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveVisual evoked potentials (VEPs) provide a non-invasive measure of visual pathway function, traditional analyses focusing on peak latency and amplitude may overlook the dynamic complexity of cortical processing. This study examined whether VEP signal complexity differs between sexes using a non-linear fractal approach based on the correlation dimension (D₂).MethodsEighty-eight healthy adults (39 females, 49 males; mean age 37 years) underwent pattern-reversal VEP testing for each eye using two checkerboard field sizes (0.808° and 0.258°), preferentially engaging magnocellular and parvocellular pathways. The complexity of averaged VEPs was quantified using the Grassberger–Procaccia algorithm with optimised embedding parameters. Sex and eye effects were analysed separately for each stimulus condition.ResultsNo significant main effects or interactions of sex or eye were observed for either checkerboard size. Estimated D₂ values ranged from 3.3 to 3.6 across conditions. Although females showed numerically higher P100 amplitudes, particularly for small-checkerboard stimulation, and a subtle tendency toward longer response latencies for left-eye stimulation, these differences were not reflected in D₂.ConclusionsThe fractal dimensionality of early visual cortical responses is stable across sex, eye, and stimulus parameters.SignificanceCorrelation dimension (D₂) analysis can be applied to averaged visual evoked potential (VEP) data from healthy adults without stratifying participants by sex.

Introduction

Visual evoked potentials (VEPs) are electrophysiological signals generated in response to visual stimuli, reflecting the functional integrity of the visual pathway from the retina to the occipital cortex (Baiano and Zeppieri, 2025). Conventionally, VEP analysis focuses on characteristic peaks, such as N75, P100, and N135, with emphasis on their amplitude and latency (Šuštar Habjan et al., 2025). These parameters are sensitive to both technical and biological factors (Kothari et al., 2016). Technical factors include stimulus parameters (contrast, reversal rate, check size, luminance, and viewing distance), electrode placement and impedance, signal filtering, ambient lighting conditions, and electrical noise level (Marcar and Wolf, 2021; Marmoy et al., 2023; Yap et al., 2020). Contrastingly, biological factors encompass age, sex, refractive status and visual acuity, ocular dominance, state of retinal adaptation, level of attention, fatigue, and individual differences in visual cortex thickness and excitability (Dziadkowiak and Podemski, 2019; Ekayanti et al., 2021; Gentile et al., 2021). Complementary spectral analyses, such as Fourier or wavelet transforms, allow for assessment of the frequency composition of VEPs, providing additional insight into their temporal dynamics and signal complexity (Boon et al., 2008, Boon et al., 2009; Cheker et al., 2024; Gutowitz et al., 1986; Hassankarimi et al., 2020; Johansson and Jakobsson, 2000, Johansson and Jakobsson, 2006; Quian Quiroga et al., 2001; Sawaguchi and Ogawa, 1998).

Beyond these conventional measures, non-linear methods provide tools for quantifying the complexity of VEP signals. One such approach is the correlation dimension (D2), which is a fractal-based metric that estimates the dimensionality of the underlying dynamics in a time series (Boon et al., 2008). Early research showed that fractal dimension can detect transient events in electroencephalography signals, including evoked potentials, without prior assumptions about waveform morphology (Arle and Simon, 1990). Subsequent studies applied D2 to VEPs, showing that it varies with stimulus properties, perceptual thresholds, and linear VEP measures, and may reveal subtle features of visual processing not captured by conventional peak analyses.

VEPs should be considered as the output of a dynamical system to interpret D₂ in the context of electrophysiological data. Many dissipative dynamical systems—particularly those exhibiting non-linear or chaotic behaviour—tend to evolve toward characteristic patterns in phase space known as attractors. These attractors may display irregular or fractal geometry in highly complex system dynamics and are referred to as strange attractors (Grassberger and Procaccia, 1983a, Grassberger and Procaccia, 1983b). A key property of such attractors is that their fractal dimensionality is typically lower than the number of physical degrees of freedom in the system, reflecting how the long-term dynamics occupy only a constrained subset of state space.

When a VEP signal is embedded into phase space, the resulting trajectory reveals such an attractor structure, representing the organisation of neural activity over time. The D2 provides a way to quantify the effective dimensionality of this attractor—capturing how densely or sparsely the trajectory fills the space. Therefore, D2 offers a measure of the ‘complexity’ or ‘richness’ of the neural response: lower values indicate more regular, coordinated activity, while higher values suggest more variable, less predictable dynamics. In this framework, differences in VEP complexity may reflect variations in cortical synchrony, network interactions, or developmental and physiological factors that are not apparent in traditional amplitude or latency measures. For example, children and adults exhibit differences in VEP complexity at low-contrast chromatic stimuli, suggesting that D2 can reflect developmental or physiological variations in visual processing (Boon et al., 2009).

Previous research from our group (Lisowski et al., 2026–in press) established normative VEP values for the healthy Polish population and demonstrated sex-related differences in P100 amplitudes, particularly for small field (0.258°) checkerboard stimuli. These amplitude differences raise the question of whether sex also influences the dynamical organisation of the transient visual response. Given that non-linear measures can be sensitive to subtle neurophysiological factors—including network synchrony and pathway-specific cortical integration—it is plausible that D2 might exhibit sex dependence analogous to that observed for amplitude.

Building on this foundation, the present study examined whether the fractal complexity of VEPs—quantified using D2 analysis—differs between women and men and whether it depends on the stimulated eye or on checkerboard field size, which preferentially engages magnocellular (0.808°) versus parvocellular (0.258°) pathways. We complemented the non-linear analysis with conventional measures, including Fourier power, signal-to-noise ratio, and N75/P100 morphology, to evaluate whether any sex-related differences in VEP amplitude correspond to differences in dynamic complexity.

Consistent with previous findings, we formulated two competing hypotheses. First, D2 would differ between sexes, mirroring known amplitude differences in the P100 component, and stimulation conditions engaging magnocellular and parvocellular pathways would modulate D2, potentially interacting with sex. Alternatively, if the D2 reflects stable, pathway-independent properties of early cortical visual processing, D₂ should remain invariant across sex, eye, and stimulus parameters.

Testing these hypotheses provides insight into whether fractal measures capture an additional dimension of inter-individual variability in VEP organisation or whether they constitute a robust, sex-independent descriptor of early visual cortical dynamics.

Methods

Participants

All healthy volunteers were examined in the visual electrophysiology laboratory of the Department of Ophthalmology between 2019 and 2024. They underwent a comprehensive ophthalmological evaluation before the electrophysiological VEP examination. Visual acuity was assessed using standard Snellen charts, colour vision was tested with Ishihara plates, and intraocular pressure was measured by applanation tonometry. The anterior segment of the eye was also examined using direct slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and the fundus was evaluated with a Volk wide-field lens.

Only healthy volunteers with full visual acuity (1.0 on the decimal scale), no history of ocular disease or ophthalmic surgery, a refractive error not exceeding ±2.0 dioptres, and not taking neuroactive medications were included in the study. All examinations were performed at the same time of day, between 12:00 and 14:00, to ensure consistent physiological conditions. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Bialystok, Poland (Resolution No. R-I-002/477/2019, dated 24 October 2019). All participants provided written informed consent prior to the procedure.

VEP recording

Recordings were performed according to the International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision standards using an Espion E3 (Diagnosys LLC) system. Gold cup electrodes were positioned at Oz (active), Fz (reference), and Cz (ground), with impedance maintained below 5 kΩ. Checkerboard stimuli (luminance 100 cd/m2) were reversed at 2 Hz. Two field sizes were tested as follows: (1) large field: 0.808° (dominantly magnocellular), and (2) small field: 0.258° (dominantly parvocellular). Each eye was tested separately, with at least two averaged responses of 80 sweeps each. Signals were sampled at 1000 Hz (1 ms resolution), and each VEP epoch was extracted from −20 to +299 ms relative to stimulus onset. All tests were conducted in the same electrophysiology laboratory, under consistent lighting conditions.

Correlation dimension analysis

The dynamical properties of the visual evoked potentials (VEP) were assessed using the Grassberger–Procaccia algorithm to estimate D2, which quantifies the effective number of degrees of freedom underlying the observed time series. Because the VEP signal is one-dimensional in time, a state-space reconstruction was performed prior to dimensionality analysis.

Let xt denote the continuous-time VEP signal amplitude. The experimentally recorded signal was sampled at a constant sampling interval ∆t, yielding a discrete time series xii=1N, where(1)xi=xti,ti=i∆t,and N is the total number of samples.

State-space reconstruction was performed using the time-delay embedding method. Each reconstructed state vector Xi was formed from the discrete time series as follows:(2)Xi=xixi+τxi+2τ…xi+m−1τ,where xt denotes the VEP signal amplitude in time t, m is the embedding dimension, and τ is the time delay. The embedding dimension was set to m=5 and time delay to τ=4 ms based on previously published methodological recommendations (Boon et al., 2016) for signals recorded with similar sampling characteristics.

For the reconstructed trajectory Xi, the correlation integral Cr was computed as follows:(3)Cr=limN→∞1N2∑i=1N∑j=1NΘr−Xi−Xj,where N is the number of reconstructed state vectors, ∥Xi−Xj∥ denotes the Euclidean distance, and Θ is the Heaviside step function. Practically, a Theiler window W (Theiler, 1986) was applied to prevent spurious near-neighbour correlations caused by temporal autocorrelation, excluding state-vector pairs with i−j≤W:(4)C^r=1M∑i,j=1i−j≤WNΘr−Xi−Xj,where M is the number of admissible vector pairs. W was estimated individually from the first zero-crossing (or 1/e decay) of the autocorrelation function of each VEP trace. For sufficiently small radius values r, the correlation integral follows a power-law scaling:(5)Cr∼rD2

Practically, D2 was estimated as the slope of a linear least-squares fit to the segment of the logCr versus logr curve that exhibited high linearity (R2≥0.99) and minimal slope variation. Following Tsonis (2009) and Henry et al. (2001), the search for the plateau was restricted to the dynamical scaling range logrϵ−1.0−0.5 which excludes both the small-radius noise regime and large-radius correlation saturation region. Within this range, sliding linear regressions were performed over windows of 10 consecutive points. A segment was considered a valid scaling region if it exhibited high linearity (R2≥0.99) and a stable local slope (coefficient of variation <10%). The longest contiguous segment meeting these criteria was taken as the scaling plateau, and D2 was defined as the mean slope of this segment.

For group-level comparisons, the plateau was first identified on the group-mean logCr versus logr curve, and the same radius interval was subsequently applied to all subjects within the condition. Individual D2 values were retained for statistical analysis.

In addition to the non-linear analysis, amplitudes and latencies of the N75, P100, and N135 components were also summarised, to allow comparison with previously reported normative data from our laboratory.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of D2 values were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. If the data met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted with sex (female versus male) as a between-subject factor and eye (left versus right) as a within-subject factor, performed separately for small- and large-checkerboard stimulation conditions. Non-parametric alternatives were applied when normality assumptions were violated: the Mann–Whitney U and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for between-group (female versus male) and within-subject (left versus right) comparisons, respectively. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation, presented both in tabular form and as box-and-whisker plots illustrating group differences in D2. Eye-related comparisons were treated as within-subject contrasts, and paired statistical tests were applied accordingly.

Across all conditions, the obtained amplitudes and latencies fell within the expected physiological range and closely matched previously published normative values from the same cohort (Lisowski et al. 2025–in press). As in that earlier study, females exhibited numerically higher P100 amplitudes than males—particularly under small-checkerboard stimulation—while latencies showed minimal sex-related variation. None of these group differences reached statistical significance in the present dataset (all p > 0.05), consistent with the sex-independent character of D2 observed in the non-linear analysis.

Importantly, no systematic interocular differences were found for any peak (Wilcoxon paired tests, all p > 0.05), confirming the bilateral symmetry of the recorded responses and supporting the robustness of the VEP averages used for D2 estimation. These results demonstrate that the VEP waveforms in this sample were typical both in morphology and magnitude, and that the absence of sex or eye differences in D2 cannot be attributed to atypical amplitude or latency patterns. Although a slight left–right latency asymmetry was visible in the grand-averaged waveforms, it did not translate into differences in the non-linear complexity measures.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether the fractal complexity of VEPs, quantified using D2, differs as a function of sex, the stimulated eye, or checkerboard field size. Across all conditions, D2 remained stable, and no significant effects were observed. These findings indicate that the geometric complexity of averaged VEP waveforms is preserved across these factors. Moreover, these findings suggest that sex-related differences in VEP amplitude do not necessarily translate into differences in the geometric complexity of the averaged cortical response waveform, as quantified by D2.

The estimated D2 values (approximately 3.3–3.6) are consistent with previous studies applying state-space reconstruction and correlation-integral scaling to averaged VEP signals (Grassberger and Procaccia, 1983a; Henry et al., 2001; Quian Quiroga et al., 2001; Sawaguchi and Ogawa, 1998). In the context of the present study, fractal dimensionality reflects the effective dimensionality of the reconstructed averaged VEP waveform. Higher D2 values correspond to a more complex reconstructed waveform trajectory, whereas lower values reflect a more regular and constrained averaged response. The relative invariance of D2 across conditions suggests that the reconstructed geometry of averaged transient VEP responses is stable across sex and stimulation parameters that preferentially engage magnocellular versus parvocellular pathways.

The comparison with traditional VEP measures further supports this interpretation. As previously reported in this cohort (Lisowski et al., 2026–in press), females tend to show higher P100 amplitudes than males, particularly under parvocellular-biased (small-checkerboard) stimulation. The absence of corresponding differences in D2 in the present study indicates that waveform magnitude and dynamical complexity are complementary but dissociable features of the neural response. Although amplitude reflects the degree of synchronous activation within thalamocortical pathways, D2 captures the richness of temporal interactions among distributed neural generators.

A Fourier transform was performed, in addition to fractal analysis, to explore spectral characteristics of the VEP signals. However, the frequency-domain results should be interpreted with caution due to the short 300-ms post-stimulus window, which limits spectral resolution and precludes reliable estimation of power at the 2-Hz reversal frequency and its harmonics. Instead, the Fourier spectra primarily reflected background physiological activity and environmental electrical interference, including a prominent occipital alpha component at approximately 10 Hz and peaks at 50 Hz and its harmonics associated with mains and display refresh artefacts. Such residual oscillatory components may affect the absolute magnitude of D₂ by altering the phase-space structure; however, their comparable presence across conditions suggests that relative group comparisons remain valid. Although averaging and filtering substantially reduce noise, non-linear measures remain sensitive to low-level artefacts that can subtly alter the geometry of the reconstructed phase-space trajectory, potentially inflating the apparent complexity of the signal. Therefore, minor contributions from alpha activity or mains interference should be considered when interpreting absolute D₂ values and group comparisons.

Consequently, the Fourier analysis in the present study was not well-suited to detect stimulus-locked periodic oscillations. While this does not undermine the interpretation of the transient VEP waveforms in the time domain, it emphasises that steady-state neural entrainment cannot be evaluated from these data. Future studies employing longer continuous stimulation windows or SVEP paradigms will be necessary to characterise the frequency-specific cortical responses associated with magnocellular and parvocellular processing.

Finally, the stability of D2 across checkerboard field sizes suggests that although diverse visual pathways are differentially engaged depending on stimulus spatial scale, the higher-order cortical dynamics that shape the emergent VEP remain conserved. This supports the view that D2 constitutes a pathway-independent descriptor of averaged VEP waveform complexity that can be applied uniformly across healthy subjects.

This study has some limitations. Averaged VEPs were analysed rather than single-sweep responses, which may underestimate trial-to-trial temporal variability and reduce sensitivity to subtle neural dynamics. This choice reflects a deliberate translational emphasis: diagnostic interpretation is routinely based on averaged VEP waveforms in clinical neurophysiology, and using the same signal enhances comparability between the present findings and clinical practice. However, it should be emphasised that the estimated correlation dimension primarily reflects the geometric complexity of the averaged evoked response rather than the full trial-by-trial variability of the underlying neural dynamics. Averaging improves the signal-to-noise ratio and corresponds to standard clinical VEP interpretation, but it may also suppress inter-trial fluctuations and mask subtle alterations in neural complexity. Consequently, similar D₂ values in averaged recordings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of preserved cortical dynamics, particularly in pathological cohorts. Future studies should therefore include single-sweep or trial-by-trial analyses to determine whether D₂ is sensitive to disease-related changes that may be obscured by signal averaging. Individual differences in visual acuity and fixation stability could have contributed to variability in D₂ measures, although these factors could not be fully controlled in the present paradigm. Another methodological limitation is the temporal window of the recordings: the 300 ms post-stimulus segments capture only transient responses and do not include a full cycle of the 2-Hz stimulation. Consequently, the spectral resolution was insufficient to isolate stimulus-locked frequency components, limiting the interpretability of Fourier-based analyses. Therefore, the frequency-domain results primarily reflected endogenous rhythms (e.g. approximately 10 Hz alpha) and line-noise harmonics, rather than stimulus-driven periodic activity. Future investigation should extend this analysis to longer continuous recording windows, single-trial data, and clinical or pathological cohorts, where differences between transient and steady-state responses may be more pronounced.

From a clinical perspective, the application of D₂ analysis to VEPs may provide a valuable complement to traditional amplitude- and latency-based measures. Although conventional VEP parameters are sensitive to alterations in neural conduction and synchronisation, they may fail to detect more subtle disturbances in the dynamic organisation of cortical responses that precede overt abnormalities of the P100 waveform. As a measure of effective signal complexity, D₂ may reflect early changes in cortical integration, network synchrony, or compensatory neuroplastic mechanisms. Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of fractal and non-linear analyses in visual and electrophysiological signals, including steady-state and transient VEPs in neurodegenerative contexts (Schmeisser, 1993), as well as in conditions involving demyelinating pathology and altered neural dynamics (Rahatabad et al., 2021; Seifi et al., 2018). Fractal analysis may reveal functional alterations that are not apparent in conventional VEP recordings in clinical settings, such as neurodegenerative disorders, demyelinating neuropathies, toxic–metabolic damage to the visual pathway, or disorders of visual information processing. Consequently, D₂may have future potential as a functional biomarker supporting early diagnosis and longitudinal monitoring in ophthalmic and neuro-ophthalmic diseases, but its diagnostic sensitivity must first be confirmed using trial-by-trial analyses and clinical cohorts.

Conclusions

The D₂ of averaged VEP waveforms was consistent across sex, the stimulated eye, and checkerboard field size. This finding indicates that the geometric complexity of clinically averaged VEP responses is stable across stimulus configurations that differentially engage magnocellular and parvocellular pathways. Therefore, within the context of healthy adults and averaged VEP recordings, D₂ appears to be a robust, pathway-independent descriptor of VEP waveform complexity. Further trial-by-trial studies in clinical populations are required before its diagnostic sensitivity can be established.
