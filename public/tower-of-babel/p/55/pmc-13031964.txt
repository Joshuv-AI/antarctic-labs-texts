Deep learning framework with interpretable feature selection for accurate SUMOylation site prediction
Authors: Aasem N Alyahya, Salman Khan, Naqqash Dilshad, Nijad Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMOs) are crucial protein regulators influencing diverse biological processes through covalent modifications or non-covalent interactions. SUMOylation, a key post-translational modification (PTM), plays a vital role in cellular regulation. This study presents Hybrid-Sumo, a deep learning-based model integrating protein structural and sequence features to predict SUMOylation sites. Hybrid-Sumo combines three advanced feature extraction techniques: Half-Sphere Exposure (HSE), Position-Specific Scoring Matrix with Discrete Wavelet Transform (PSSM-DWT), and Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT). The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) algorithm is employed for optimal feature selection, while a Deep Neural Network (DNN) serves as the classification model. Extensive 10-fold cross-validation confirms the effectiveness of Hybrid-Sumo, achieving 99.74% accuracy on benchmark datasets and 96.15% and 95.83% on balanced and imbalanced independent datasets, respectively. These results surpass existing models, improving training accuracy by 1.45% and testing accuracy (both balanced and imbalanced) by 1.90% and 0.25%, respectively. These findings highlight Hybrid-Sumo as a robust computational tool for accurate prediction of SUMOylation sites, accelerating research on protein function and modification analysis.Keywords: Deep learning, SHAP, SUMOylation, BERT, Half Sphere ExposureSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) regulate protein function, influencing their activity, localization, and interactions1. The PTM mechanism encompasses over 450 modifications, including methylation, nitrosylation, proteolysis, phosphorylation, lipidation, glycosylation, ubiquitination, acetylation, and SUMOylation, thereby significantly expanding the diversity of protein structures and functions2,3. Studying these modifications reveals changes in protein mass and provides critical insights into biological functions. Among these modifications, SUMOylation is a significant PTM process in which Small Ubiquitin-like Modifier (SUMO) proteins covalently attach to lysine residues of target proteins4. Transport between the nucleus and the cytoplasm, regulation of transcription, DNA repair, and protein interactions are all regulated by this change in the cell. Further, SUMOylation has been linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s, where protein misfolding and cellular homeostasis disruptions contribute to pathogenesis.

Furthermore, SUMOylation plays a crucial role in gene expression, protein stability, and genome replication, serving as a key modulator of eukaryotic protein function. Recent research has identified over 450 distinctive protein modifications, including SUMOylation, which affects protein interactions, intracellular distribution, target protein activity, and longevity. Given its widespread impact, SUMOylation has attracted increasing attention due to its vital role in post-translational protein regulation5. The importance of SUMOylation extends to functional proteomics, where it influences protein recycling, degradation, intracellular localization, and enzymatic regulation. Research has established strong links between SUMOylation sites and diseases, particularly neurodegenerative disorders6. To better understand SUMOylation, several bioinformatics tools have been developed for structural analysis and characterization. The integration of sequential and structural bioinformatics has advanced drug discovery and computational biology, underscoring the importance of identifying SUMOylation sites. Accurately predicting these sites is essential for understanding fundamental biological processes and developing potential therapeutic targets for cancer and other diseases.

Recent studies highlight the growing role of computational models in bioinformatics, particularly for predicting post-translational modifications such as SUMOylation and other biological properties using machine learning techniques7–12. Some tools, such as SUMOsp13, GPS-SUMO14, and SUMOsp 2.015, employ phosphorylation-based scoring algorithms to assess peptide similarity. Alongside experimental screening methods, computational prediction tools, including MusiteDeep13, ResSUMO14, SUMO_LDA15, and JASSA16, provide valuable insights for identifying SUMOylation sites and SUMO-interacting motifs (SIMs). More recent, more sophisticated models, such as SumoPred-PLM17, use machine learning techniques, including Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and decision trees, to enhance accuracy. The SUMOPre and SUMOHydro models, which utilize a Support Vector Machine (SVM) for SUMO site categorization, were proposed in recent works by Xu et al.18 and Chen et al.19, respectively. Similarly, the pSumo-CD predictor was introduced by Jia et al.6 using a universal PseAAC method and SVM. In subsequent work, Sharma et al.17 proposed HseSUMO, which predicts SUMOylation sites by combining the half-sphere exposure method with a decision tree. Recently, the multi-layer Deep Neural Network (DNN) and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), i.e., Deep-Sumo20, PSSM-Sumo21, and XGBoost-Sumo22, were introduced and achieved impressive accuracies of 96.47%, 98.71%, and 99.68%, respectively, outperforming earlier methods. However, existing models still struggle with reliability and generalization; despite their success, they require further refinement, particularly to overcome the constraints of feature extraction and linear dataset dependencies. Most rely on sequence-based encoding that computes residue composition without preserving sequence order. Others use traditional evolutionary feature descriptors, which are computationally expensive. Additionally, many models are trained using conventional machine learning methods, which limits their effectiveness. Alternative approaches are needed to better distinguish Sumo and non-Sumo sites, thereby enhancing prediction accuracy and throughput.

This study proposes a computational model for precisely identifying SUMOylation sites. Our approach enhances prediction performance in distinguishing Sumo and non-Sumo sites by focusing on four essential phases: (i) designing three novel feature extraction algorithms, (ii) employing a new tree-based feature selection method, (iii) developing a deep learning-based classification model, and (iv) evaluating generalization performance on both balanced and imbalanced samples. Overall, the proposed model integrates discriminative features from three novel feature extraction techniques, i.e., Half Sphere Exposure (HSE), BERT, and PsePSSM-DWT. To optimize hybrid features and enhance interpretability, we employ SHAP, and a DNN is then explicitly designed for classification. Using 10-fold cross-validation, Hybrid-Sumo demonstrates its strong performance, achieving 99.74% accuracy on benchmark datasets and 96.15% and 95.83% on balanced and imbalanced independent samples, respectively. This study makes three primary contributions to SUMOylation site prediction:

A novel hybrid feature representation integrating Half Sphere Exposure (HSE), BERT, and PsePSSM-DWT is introduced to capture complementary structural, contextual, and evolutionary characteristics of SUMOylation sites, improving training accuracy by 1.45% over existing state-of-the-art predictors.

Benchmark dataset

A legitimate and trustworthy dataset is crucial for developing an effective computational model23,24. We selected a reliable benchmark dataset from25 for this study to train and validate the proposed model. In machine learning and bioinformatics, using a trustworthy dataset is crucial for making reliable prediction models. The dataset used in this study was sourced from the Compendium of Protein Lysine Modification (CPLM)26, which includes details of 12 types of lysine PTMs. The original dataset contained 780 SUMOylation site sequences (positive samples) and 21,353 non-SUMOylation site sequences (negative samples), resulting in an imbalanced dataset. This imbalance can lead to classification bias towards the majority class. Various strategies, such as oversampling and under-sampling, address this issue27. In this study, we applied the Near-Miss under-sampling technique28 in Python to reduce the number of majority-class samples and balance the dataset. Finally, we obtained a balanced dataset of 780 positive and 780 negative samples, ensuring the reliability of our model training and analysis.

Furthermore, we conduct two independent validations, balance-independent and imbalance-independent, to better represent real-world settings and the model’s generalizability. First, for the balanced independent validation, One-fifth of the benchmark data was set aside for independent verification. Our sample set consisted of 312 balanced cases, comprising 156 positive and 156 negative cases. Secondly, we obtained a dataset (i.e., 500 samples, 200 positive and 300 negative) from26 for imbalance-independent validation to better assess the model’s generalizability. Crucially, the absence of repeated samples from the training data in the test set does not guarantee an objective assessment of the model’s performance.

To prevent data leakage and overly optimistic performance caused by homologous protein sequences, sequence redundancy reduction was conducted using CD-HIT before dataset partitioning. Sequences sharing more than 30% pairwise identity were clustered, and redundant samples were removed. The dataset was then split into training and independent test sets, ensuring that no sequences from the same cluster appeared in both sets. Consequently, the maximum sequence identity between any training and testing samples remained below 30%. This procedure ensures that Hybrid-Sumo is evaluated on biologically distinct proteins and that the reported performance reflects genuine generalization rather than memorization of similar sequences.

Feature encoding schemes

Most predictive machine-learning methods need numerical input due to the complexity of peptide sequence representation29–33. To ensure the model works, we’ll use feature encoding techniques here; selecting the right features while preserving the sequence’s important structure and attributes is key. Additionally, the classifier training procedure is hindered by the datasets used in our study, which do not follow a uniform distribution of peptide sequence lengths34–37. Peptide sequences with lengths between four and fifty-three amino acids make up the dataset used in this study. The majority of sequences have publicly accessible PSSM profiles; however, for a small number, additional parameters, such as the iteration count and E-value retrieval threshold, are required. For the remaining brief sequences, we supplemented the peptide sequence with non-standard alphabetic characters, such as the hyphen (-), that do not match amino acids. These dashes (-) do not affect the peptide’s physical or functional properties. Additionally, we made sure that the original peptide sequences were preserved in the expanded sequences. Take the sequence “--- HDEF—” as an example; the original “HDEF” is still front and centre.

Pseudo-PSSM-discrete wavelet transform (DWT)

PSSM (Position Specific Scoring Matrix), introduced by Gribskov et al.38, is a powerful tool for aligning distant protein relatives by capturing sequence-based similarities and structural features. It has been widely applied to predict various protein types, including hormone-binding and membrane proteins. Generated via PSI-BLAST, PSSM encodes evolutionary details, with substitution scores reflecting conserved or rare amino acid replacements, as mathematically defined in Eq. 2. The PSSM matrix H represents residue frequencies for amino acid pairs in a peptide, with rows denoting residues for 20 amino acids across a sequence of length L.

In this paper, we applied pseudo and DWT methods to PSSM to derive evolutionary descriptors. While PSSM provides evolutionary insights, it overlooks sequence order and residue correlations, challenging ML models with variable-length sequences. To address this, PsePSSM generates a consistent L×20 vector by integrating PSI-BLAST with the Swiss-Prot database, enabling diverse applications in bioinformatics and proteomics. Half sphere exposure (HSE)

In this study, we employed HSE as the feature-extraction approach, which is a two-dimensional measure of solvent exposure introduced by Hamelryck44. Unlike alternative solvent exposure metrics such as RD45, rASA46, ASA47, and CN48, the HSE method offers distinct advantages. Specifically, HSE demonstrates superior capabilities across multiple critical parameters, including conservation patterns across different structural folds, computational efficiency, prediction of protein stability, sensitivity to structural variations, predictive accuracy, and processing speed compared to traditional exposure measurement techniques.

As seen in Fig. 2, the HSE technique splits the sphere of a residue into two halves. The first half is called HSEβ or HSE-up, while the second half is called HSEα or HSE-down. The HSE-up and HSE-down are the top and bottom spheres of the residue’s chain. Intriguing features for describing the spatial neighborhood of the residue are shown by these two half spheres, which have different geometries and energy profiles. To determine the measurements, we used a sphere with a radius of rd = 13 Å for all residues. This yielded a 124-dimensional feature vector representing the protein sequence as a discrete model.

Bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT)

Recent advances in natural language processing (NLP) have been primarily attributed to transformer-based models, such as BERT, which capture contextual and semantic relationships49. Lin et al.50 treat amino acids like words and apply the BERT architecture to peptide sequences to extract characteristics. Our method utilized ProtBERT-BFD51, which enhances feature representation by combining protein-based BERT embeddings with BFD from the Big Fantastic Database. The peptide sequences are tokenized at each amino acid, and a unique “CLS” token is appended to each amino acid to represent the combined characteristics used for prediction. Using PAD, we set the length of each sequence to 200 and use the SEP token to divide them. Using global average pooling, the tokenised sequences are transformed into feature vectors with a dimensionality of 1024. Figure 3 shows the ProtBERT-BFD model’s fundamental mechanism.

Deep architecture

One branch of machine learning that attempts to mimic the way the human brain operates is deep neural networks (DNNs). A DNN architecture consists of an input layer, multiple hidden layers, and an output layer56. For the network to discover patterns and characteristics in data that aren’t visible in the raw data, the hidden layers are crucial. Hidden layer enhancement improves the predictive capacity of mapping complicated patterns, but it also increases complexity, computational costs, and the risk of overfitting57,58. An essential benefit of deep neural networks (DNNs) is feature-extraction; these networks can automatically learn features from data without requiring manual feature engineering, which is particularly helpful when dealing with unlabeled or otherwise unstructured data. The usual methods of learning can do this, as mentioned in59,60. Due to their depth and versatility, deep neural networks (DNNs) are more effective than previous machine learning approaches at addressing complex categorization problems, according to experts. Many different fields utilize deep neural networks (DNNs), including bioengineering, image recognition, audio recognition, and natural language processing61.

The consistently strong performance of all evaluated machine learning methods and their systematic advantage over existing tools can be attributed to several key factors. First, the use of an optimized hybrid feature representation enables these models to leverage complementary semantic, evolutionary, and sequence-based information, which is often absent or only partially exploited in existing tools that rely on single or handcrafted feature sets. Overfitting prevention and generalization analysis

Deep neural network models trained on high-dimensional hybrid representations may be vulnerable to overfitting, in which the model memorises the training data rather than learning generalizable patterns. To explicitly mitigate this risk, multiple regularization and validation strategies were incorporated into the proposed Hybrid-Sumo framework.

First, a rigorous 10-fold cross-validation protocol was adopted to ensure that the model was evaluated across multiple independent data splits rather than a single train–test partition. Second, architectural regularization techniques, including dropout (0.25) and L1 weight regularization (0.001), were applied to reduce neuron co-adaptation and constrain model complexity. Third, SHAP-based feature selection was employed to remove redundant and less informative features, thereby lowering the dimensionality of the hybrid feature space and improving generalization.

Finally, the trained model was validated using completely independent balanced and imbalanced test datasets. The small performance gap between cross-validation accuracy (99.74%) and independent testing accuracies (96.15% and 95.83%) indicates stable predictive behaviour and confirms that the model does not suffer from significant overfitting. These findings demonstrate that Hybrid-Sumo exhibits strong generalisation to unseen protein sequences.

Conclusion

SUMOylation, a reversible post-translational modification, involves the covalent attachment of small ubiquitin-like modifiers (SUMOs) to specific lysine residues in target proteins, influencing key cellular functions such as gene regulation and protein stability. Dysregulated SUMOylation has been linked to various diseases, including Alzheimer’s, cancer, and diabetes, highlighting the need for precise identification of SUMOylation sites. This study introduces Hybrid-Sumo, a novel model integrating optimal hybrid transformers with evolutionary-based features to enhance prediction accuracy. The model’s effectiveness was rigorously evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation, demonstrating strong generalisation in real-world settings. Comparative outperformance accuracy analysis against conventional machine learning models (i.e., 1.18%) and with existing state-of-the-art models (i.e., 1.45%) further underscored Hybrid-Sumo’s superior predictive performance.

Future research will focus on three key directions: (1) validating the model across diverse species and tissue-specific datasets to assess cross-organism generalizability, (2) integrating experimental proteomics data for clinical applications in disease mechanisms such as cancer and neurodegenerative disorders, and (3) leveraging parallel computing and big data platforms to address scalability challenges as NGS-driven genomic data expands exponentially70. Additionally, extending the framework to predict other post-translational modifications (ubiquitination, phosphorylation, acetylation) through transfer learning could establish a unified PTM prediction platform for systems biology and precision medicine66,71.
