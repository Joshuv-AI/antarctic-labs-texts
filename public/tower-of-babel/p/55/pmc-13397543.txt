Protein-Enforced Ligand Environments Reshape Classical Coordination Preferences in Copper Polypyridyl Complexes
Authors: Inseo Choi, Jaehee Lee, Kyohyun Hwang, Sebastian Kunze, Jae Wan Park, Seung Jun Hwang, Jongwoo Lim, Seunghoon Lee, Woon Ju Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Copper coordination chemistry is governed by several well-established features, including Jahn–Teller distortion in octahedral Cu­(II) complexes and the scarcity of high-coordinate Cu­(I) species. Here, we demonstrate that protein scaffolds can function as mechanically active ligand architectures that override these intrinsic electronic and structural preferences. We designed four protein scaffolds that preorganize two or three bipyridine-alanine noncanonical amino acids to generate mononuclear copper sites in either the cupric or cuprous state. These protein-embedded complexes exhibit distinct charge-transfer features, enhanced structural symmetry with significantly reduced Jahn–Teller distortion, an unprecedented octahedral Cu­(I) geometry, markedly cathodic shifts in Cu­(II)/Cu­(I) redox potentials, and rapid self-exchange rate constants consistent with entatic-state behavior. Collectively, this work establishes protein-based multidentate ligands as a general strategy to access nonclassical coordination environments and to expand the chemical space and reactivity of metalloproteins through protein-imposed strains.

Introduction

Proteins are naturally occurring macromolecular ligands that provide an exceptionally diverse spectrum of metal coordination environments. The architectural diversity arising from protein topology, secondary structure, and proximal residue arrangement enables virtually unlimited coordination spheres. Recent advances in understanding protein sequence–structure relationships have enabled increasingly accurate prediction of metal-binding interactions , and the introduction of metal-binding sites within various protein scaffolds. − In these approaches, the preferred coordination geometry of a given metal ion and oxidation state serves as a primary parameter for positioning metal-coordinating amino acids. Beyond the first coordination sphere, proteins uniquely provide well-defined secondary and tertiary coordination environmentselectrostatic and hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen-bonding networks, and oriented local electric fieldsthat collectively modulate the chemical properties of metallocofactors, including Lewis acidity, redox potentials, metal specificity, and catalytic activity. −

In addition to these chemical factors, macromolecular scaffolds also play mechanical roles in defining the overall coordination sphere. − A particularly illustrative and extensively studied example is the blue copper protein. − Although a mononuclear Cu center thermodynamically prefers square-planar and tetrahedral coordination, corresponding to the geometries of Cu­(II) and Cu­(I), respectively (Scheme A), the blue copper protein enforces a unique intermediate geometry, known as the entatic state, minimizing the structural reorganization required during electron transfer and thereby supporting rapid rates. Inspired by these natural ligand architectures, sterically constrained four- or five-coordinate synthetic ligand frameworks have been developed to stabilize intermediate geometries that mimic entatic states. − A diverse array of engineered copper-binding proteins, including blue, red, purple copper proteins and type 0 copper centers, have been reported, underscoring the capacity of protein scaffolds to modulate copper coordination geometry and electronic structure. ,

Additional well-recognized intrinsic features in octahedral copper complexes, relative to those of other first-row transition-metal ions, are their pronounced propensity to adopt lower-symmetry coordination geometries. Incorporation of Cu­(II) into an octahedral ligand field typically results in a Jahn–Teller distortion (Scheme B). , Upon reduction, the resulting Cu­(I) species loses ligand-field stabilization energy, experiences increased electronic repulsion, and frequently undergoes ligand dissociation. Furthermore, Cu­(I) is intrinsically prone to disproportionation into Cu­(II) and Cu(0) in aqueous solution, although the extent of this process depends strongly on ligand environment and reaction conditions. Consequently, to the best of our knowledge, crystallographically characterized octahedral Cu­(I) complexesparticularly those supported exclusively by six nitrogen donorsremain exceptionally rare in both synthetic coordination compounds and natural metalloproteins.

In this work, we designed copper-binding proteins that access coordination environments rarely attainable with strain-free small-molecule ligands. Specifically, we constructed a series of copper polypyridyl complexes through the genetic incorporation and symmetry-adapted multiplication of the noncanonical amino acid (2,2′-bipyridin-5-yl)­alanine (bpy-Ala) within dimeric (Figure A) and trimeric (Figure B) protein scaffolds, yielding tetradentate and hexadentate ligand architectures for mononuclear copper coordination. Spectroscopic, structural, and electrochemical analyses demonstrated that [Cu­(bpy) n ] complexes (n = 1–3) embedded within these protein matrices exhibit coordination geometries and redox-activity distinct from those of their strain-free molecular counterparts across two oxidation states. Most notably, octahedral cupric and cuprous tris-bipyridyl complexes were observed with minimal symmetry lowering and structural reorganization, highlighting protein scaffolds as a platform for reprogramming classical coordination chemistry.

Results and Discussion

Protein Designs

A central protein design challenge in this work was to achieve precise structural control over the local mechanical strain imposed on the metal center while maintaining the intended coordination geometry. To address this, we genetically incorporated the noncanonical amino acid, bpy-Ala (denoted as Z, Figure C), into homo-oligomeric protein scaffolds possessing C 2 (Figure A) or C 3 symmetry (Figure B). Within these assemblies, bpy-Ala residues contributed by each monomer converge to coordinate a single copper ion, thereby forming bis- or tris-bipyridine complexes. This symmetry-adapted multiplication strategy creates a rigid and short covalent linkage between the protein backbone and the metal-binding site, unlike earlier approaches that relied on long, flexible linkers, thereby enforcing direct mechanical coupling between the protein matrix and the metal complex.

Guided by this metalloprotein design strategy, we surveyed the RCSB Protein Data Bank for homodimeric and homotrimeric proteins. Protein scaffolds were selected according to the following criteria: (i) reliable heterologous overexpression in Escherichia coli, (ii) high thermal stability, and (iii) absence of native cofactors or intrinsic metal-binding sites, thereby minimizing background copper coordination and simplifying subsequent spectroscopic analyses (Supporting Information Figure S2). Based on these requirements, we identified one homodimeric protein, DTDP-6-deoxy-d-xylo-4-hexulose 3,5-epimerase from Methanobacterium thermoautotrophicum (PDB: 1EP0), two homotrimeric proteins: the signal-transducing protein GlnK from Thermus thermophilus HB8 (PDB: 1VFJ) and the phosphosulfolactate synthase from Methanococcus jannaschii (PDB: 1QWG) (Figure C). The native functions and organismal origins of these proteins were not considered in the design.

Next, we identified appropriate residues near the rotational symmetry axis to enable coordination of a single copper ion by bpy-Ala residues contributed from each monomer. To guide residue selection, the crystal structures of [Cu­(bpy)2]+ (CCDC 601277), [Cu­(bpy)2]2+ (CCDC 1115158), and [Cu­(bpy)3]2+ (CCDC 2048918) were analyzed to estimate optimal interatomic separations between the Cβ atoms of candidate bpy-Ala positions, as reported previously (Figure S1). The average Cβ–Cβ distances, 7.5 Å for the dimeric bis-bipyridine design and 6.5 Å for the trimeric tris-bipyridine design, were taken as structural benchmarks corresponding to strain-free copper-bipyridine complexes. Deviations from these optimal distances at the selected positions within the protein scaffolds are expected to impose protein-induced mechanical strain on the resulting Cu­(bpy)2 or Cu­(bpy)3 complexes, although local structural flexibility and interactions with surrounding amino acids may also modulate the magnitude of protein-imposed strains.

Based on this structural analysis, four bpy-Ala variants were constructed using the selected oligomeric proteins with distinct Cβ–Cβ separations: the R79Z and R46Z mutants of the homodimeric DTDP-6-deoxy-d-xylo-4-hexulose 3,5-epimerase (designated DP1 and DP2, respectively), the S31Z mutant of GlnK (TP1), and the K26Z mutant of phosphosulfolactate synthase (TP6) (Figure C); the corresponding Cβ–Cβ distances in the wild-type protein structures were 6.6, 8.3, 6.8, and 7.9 Å, respectively (Figure S3). All four bpy-Ala-containing proteins were successfully expressed in E. coli and confirmed by FPLC and LC-MS analyses (Figures S4–S5).

UV–Vis Characterization of the Copper-Bound bpy-Ala Mutants

Proteins containing bpy-Ala alone exhibit an absorption maximum at 280 nm (Figure A). Upon addition of aqueous copper­(II) sulfate under ambient conditions, all four variants (DP1, DP2, TP1, and TP6) displayed an immediate increase in absorbance between 306 and 318 nm, accompanied by a concomitant decrease at 280 nm and the appearance of clear isosbestic points near 295 nm. This red-shift of the π → π* transition is consistent with Cu­(II) coordination to bipyridine ligands, as reported previously. The dimeric variant DP1 reached saturation upon addition of 1 equiv of Cu­(II) per dimer, whereas the DP2 variant required up to 2.5 equiv to reach saturation (Figure S7B).

(B) d-d transition of the Cu­(II)-bound bpy-Ala variants (2 mM). (C) Cu­(I)-bound proteins. (D) Cu-bound TP1 (20 μM) upon addition of dithionite or exposure to air. This behavior suggests that the R46Z site in DP2 has a substantially lower Cu-binding affinity than the R79Z site in DP1, likely due to differences in local structural features, including loop flexibility, Cβ–Cβ distances, and interactions with neighboring amino acid residues. Alternatively, DP2 may form a distinct Cu­(II) bipyridine species, such as a 1:1 Cu:bpy complex, [Cu­(bpy)]2+, as observed previously, whereas DP1 may form a desired [Cu­(bpy)2]2+ species. If the latter scenario applies, only DP1 satisfies our original design objective. In contrast, both trimeric variants, TP1 and TP6, exhibited complete spectral changes upon addition of 1 equiv of Cu­(II) per trimer (Figure S7C,D), suggesting selective formation of [Cu­(bpy)3]2+ complexes within the protein assemblies.

Because d-d transitions are highly sensitive to the ligand-field strength and overall coordination environment at the Cu­(II) center, we recorded UV–vis spectra of the proteins bound to 1 equiv of Cu­(II) at higher concentrations (Figure B). The d-d transitions appeared between 670 and 759 nm, with molar absorptivities ranging from 74.0 to 92.7 M–1 cm–1, assuming complete conversion to a single mononuclear copper species. These values are clearly distinguishable from those of the Cu-bound wild-type (WT) proteins lacking bpy-Ala residues (Table S4), indicating that Cu­(II) preferentially binds to the engineered bipyridine rather than nonspecifically to native residues, and adopts distinct coordination geometries depending on the bpy-Ala-containing protein scaffold.

More specifically, Cu­(II)­DP1 and Cu­(II)­DP2 exhibited d–d transitions (λmax = 683 nm, ε = 84.0 M–1 cm–1; λmax = 759 nm, ε = 92.7 M–1 cm–1, respectively), showing ∼40 nm shifts relative to the in situ-generated [Cu­(bpy)2]2+ complex (λmax = 720 nm, ε = 91.2 M–1 cm–1), suggesting differences in metal-to-ligand ratio and/or structural distortions at their Cu­(II) centers. In contrast, Cu­(II)­TP1 and Cu­(II)­TP6 showed similar d-d transitions at 687 nm (ε = 74.0 M–1 cm–1) and 670 nm (ε = 78.0 M–1 cm–1), respectively, closely resembling the in situ-generated [Cu­(bpy)3]2+ complex (λmax = 684 nm, ε = 71.2 M–1 cm–1), indicating that the three preorganized bipyridine ligands in TP1 and TP6 support an octahedral-like coordination geometry at the Cu­(II) center.

Under anaerobic conditions, addition of tetrakis­(acetonitrile)­copper­(I) tetrafluoroborate to the bpy-Ala-containing dimers and trimers resulted in a red shift of the bpy-derived π → π* transition in the 280–310 nm region, along with the broad bands spanning 350–600 nm (Figure C). The spectral changes in the 280–310 nm region reached saturation upon addition of 1 equiv of Cu­(I) for all proteins except DP2, which required 1.5–2 equiv of Cu­(I) to achieve complete saturation (Figure S8).

Cu­(I)­DP1 exhibited two broad absorption bands centered at 390 and 535 nm, with molar absorptivities of 4310 and 2440 M–1 cm–1, respectively, assuming the formation of a homogeneous mononuclear copper complex. In contrast, Cu­(I)­DP2 displayed a single broad band centered at 400 nm (ε = 3510 M–1 cm–1) with a shoulder extending toward 600 nm. These features were absent in the Cu­(II)-bound species and the Cu­(I)-bound wild-type proteins (Figure S9). Instead, the UV–vis spectra of Cu­(I)­DP1 and Cu­(I)­DP2 closely resemble the metal-to-ligand charge-transfer (MLCT) transitions characteristic of [Cu­(bpy)2]+ analogues, such as [Cu­(dpphen)2]+ (dpphen = 2,9-diphenyl-1,10-phenanthroline), [Cu­(dmphen)2]+ (dmphen = 2,9-dimethyl-1,10-phenanthroline), and [Cu­(bpy)2]+. In these systems, two absorption bands near 450 and 550 nm have been previously assigned to the B2 ← A1 and A2 ← A1 transitions, respectively. Because these bands are sensitive to coordination geometry, particularly showing increased lower-energy band intensity upon flattening toward D 2 symmetry, the distinct spectral features of Cu­(I)­DP1 and Cu­(I)­DP2 may reflect different extents of structural distortion from the typical D 2d geometry of [Cu­(bpy)2]+.

Both TP1 and TP6 also exhibited spectral changes upon addition of Cu­(I) (Figure C) that differ markedly from those of their Cu­(II)-bound forms and from wild-type proteins lacking bpy-Ala (Figures S7 and S9). To the best of our knowledge, UV–vis data for [Cu­(bpy)3]+-like complexes have not been reported, precluding direct comparison. However, the spectra of Cu­(I)­TP1 and Cu­(I)­TP6 closely resemble those of Cu­(I)­DP1 and Cu­(I)­DP2, suggesting that they likewise arise from MLCT transitions. Notably, Cu­(I)­TP6 displays a single broad absorption band centered at 445 nm (ε = 2690 M–1 cm–1) with a shoulder extending toward 750 nm, whereas Cu­(I)­TP1 exhibits two broad absorption bands at 400 and 540 nm, with molar absorptivities of 4160 and 1960 M–1 cm–1, respectively.

The spectral differences between Cu­(I)­TP1 and Cu­(I)­TP6 may originate from two scenarios. First, either protein may form Cu­(I) species in which not all three bpy ligands are simultaneously coordinated, despite their proximal arrangement, paralleling the behavior of protein-free systems where Cu­(I) preferentially adopts a four-coordinate geometry even in the presence of excess ligand; in situ mixing of Cu­(I) with 3 equiv of bpy yielded spectral changes indistinguishable from those obtained with 2 equiv of bpy (Figure S6B), providing no clear evidence for the formation of [Cu­(bpy)3]+ in solution. Alternatively, both TP1 and TP6 may form [Cu­(bpy)3]+-like species, with the MLCT transitions influenced not only by coordination number but also by the extent of structural distortion and metal–ligand covalency imposed by the protein scaffold. Notably, none of the Cu­(I)-added proteins showed detectable Cu­(II)-associated features arising from Cu­(I) disproportionation, suggesting that the Cu­(I) states are sufficiently stabilized within the proteinaceous ligand environment.

It is worthy to note that the intensity of bands in the 400–550 nm region continued to change upon addition of excess Cu­(I) (Figure S10), suggesting that alternative copper species may form depending on the metal-to-protein stoichiometry. Therefore, stoichiometric amounts of Cu­(I) were used for the following spectroscopic characterizations.

The chemical reversibility of the Cu­(II)/Cu­(I) redox couple was further examined by UV–vis spectroscopy. The MLCT features of all Cu­(I)-bound bpy-Ala variants were fully reproduced by chemical reduction of Cu­(II)-bound proteins with 0.5 equiv of 2-electron donor, dithionite, under an inert atmosphere (Figures D and S11). Subsequent exposure to air led to the rapid disappearance of the Cu­(I)-specific MLCT bands and restoration of the Cu­(II) spectral signatures. These observations indicate that the copper-bipyridine complexes embedded within the protein scaffolds undergo reversible interconversion between the +2 and +1 oxidation states while remaining structurally supported by the macromolecular framework.

EPR and NMR Spectra of Copper-Bound bpy-Ala Variants

The Cu­(II) coordination geometries of the protein-bound species were investigated by EPR spectroscopy. Cu­(II)­DP1 and Cu­(II)­DP2, prepared by adding 1 equiv of Cu­(II) per dimer, produced characteristic Cu­(II)-centered EPR signals with parameters that differ substantially from those of the Cu­(II)-treated wild-type protein lacking bpy-Ala residues (Figures A and S12B). Spectral simulation indicates that Cu­(II)­DP1 and Cu­(II)­DP2 comprise at least three and two distinct Cu­(II) species, respectively (Table S5A,B).

The two dominant components observed for Cu­(II)­DP1 (sim A and sim B) are consistent with axial EPR signals reported for cis- or trans-[Cu­(bpy)2(OH2)2]2+-type species. In contrast, the two components observed in Cu­(II)­DP2 exhibit highly rhombic EPR signals, more closely resembling five-coordinate Cu­(II) complexes supported by N-donating ligands and adopting geometries intermediate between square-pyramidal and trigonal-bipyramidal coordination. − Given that DP1 and DP2 derive from the same protein scaffold and differ by only a single mutation, their distinct coordination environments underscore the critical role of residue selection and surrounding residues.

In contrast, both Cu­(II)­TP1 and Cu­(II)­TP6, prepared by adding 3 equiv of Cu­(II) per trimer followed by washing to remove unbound metal and ensure full site occupancy, displayed well-defined axial EPR spectra (Figure B), each fitting a single species. The perpendicular g values were nearly identical (g ⊥= 2.063 and 2.064 for TP1 and TP6, respectively), whereas the parallel components differed slightly (g ∥ = 2.278, A ∥ = 159 × 10–4 cm–1 in TP1; g ∥ = 2.268, A ∥ = 171 × 10–4 cm–1 in TP6). These parameters are similar to those reported for [Cu­(bpy)3]2+ in aqueous solution, , and differ from those of the copper-treated wild-type proteins. The anisotropic EPR signatures of Cu­(II)­TP1 and Cu­(II)­TP6 indicate that Jahn–Teller distortion persists in solution, likely due to structural flexibility of the protein backbone. The subtle but notable difference between Cu­(II)­TP1 and Cu­(II)­TP6, however, suggests that two trimers exert distinct degrees of structural strains on the bound metallocofactor, finely tuning unpaired spin density at the copper nucleus and metal–ligand covalency. ,

The Cu­(I) states of all bpy-Ala variants, prepared by mixing 1 equiv of Cu­(I) per dimer or trimer, were examined by EPR spectroscopy (Figures A,B and S12A). The Cu­(II)-derived EPR signals completely disappeared, consistent with formation of diamagnetic d10 Cu­(I) species. The electronic structures of the Cu­(I) complexes were further characterized by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy (Figures C and S13). The Cu­(I)­DP1 and Cu­(I)­DP2 displayed resonances confined to the 0–10 ppm region, with two singlet peaks at −1.04 ppm and −1.46 ppm. These upfield-shifted signals are characteristic of strongly shielded aliphatic protons positioned within the anisotropic magnetic field of nearby aromatic rings, as reported previously; − analogous shifts were observed in the metal-free protein samples (Figure S13F), confirming the absence of paramagnetic species. Cu­(I)­TP1 and Cu­(I)­TP6 also exhibit 1H NMR resonances exclusively within the diamagnetic region, consistent with closed-shell d10 Cu­(I) species and in stark contrast to paramagnetic standards, such as metmyoglobin and Cu­(II)- or Co­(II)-bound azurins. Collectively, these spectroscopic results demonstrate that the protein-enforced, preorganized ligand environments support stable formation of Cu­(I) species across diverse coordination geometries and protein scaffolds.

X-ray Absorption Spectroscopic Study of bpy-Ala Mutants

Given that octahedral Cu­(I) complexes are rarely reported, we collected Cu K-edge X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra of Cu­(I)­TP1 as a representative case, together with reference compounds, including [Cu­(bpy)2]2+, [Cu­(bpy)3]2+, [Cu­(BCS)2]3– (BCS, bathocuproine disulfonate) (Figure D). For comparison, we also collected the XANES spectrum of Cu­(I)­DP1. Both Cu­(II) complexes, [Cu­(bpy)2]2+ and [Cu­(bpy)3]2+, exhibit intense rising edge with the inflection points at 8996 eV, whereas [Cu­(BCS)2]3– showed the absorption at 8983 eV, consistent with a Cu 1s → 4p transition. −

Both Cu­(I)­DP1 and Cu­(I)­TP1, prepared at a 1:1 Cu­(I)-to-oligomer ratio, exhibit broad yet intense features at 8983 and 8980 eV, respectively, similarly to [Cu­(BCS)2]3–. Notably, the energy of Cu­(I)­DP1 closely matches that of [Cu­(BCS)2]3– and that of other reported Cu­(I) complexes, whereas that of Cu­(I)­TP1 is red-shifted, indicating notable differences in electronic structure or coordination geometry. One possible explanation is enhanced cumulative σ-donation from an additional bipyridine ligand, raising the electron density at the Cu center. Therefore, the XANES spectrum of Cu­(I)­TP1 is consistent with retention of the Cu­(I) oxidation state while supporting a coordination environment distinct from conventional low-coordinate Cu­(I) complexes.

X-ray Crystal Structures of bpy-Ala Mutants

We attempted to determine structures for all four proteins in two oxidation states. Of the eight combinations, we successfully determined seven X-ray crystal structures of the copper-bound bpy-Ala mutants at resolutions ranging from 1.73 to 2.67 Å (Figures and ). For Cu­(II)­DP1, the asymmetric unit cell contains four monomers bearing bpy-Ala at the R79Z positions, revealing four distinct Cu-binding sites (Figures A and S14). These sites exhibit Cu:bpy ligand stoichiometries and coordination geometries that differ from the originally targeted [Cu­(bpy)2]2+ motif. Instead of the expected 1:2 Cu:bpy stoichiometry, each Cu­(II) center is coordinated by a bpy-Ala ligand and one to three molecules of terminally bound water molecules, with the number of water molecules depending on the specific monomer. One of the axially bound water molecules bridges the two Cu­(II) centers, giving rise to a μ-(OH x )-[Cu­(bpy)­(OH x )2]2 species (x = unidentified), where the two Cu­(II) centers are separated by a Cu–Cu distance of 4.73 Å.

Notably, the Cu-to-bpy ratio differs from those observed in the copper titration experiments, where 1 equiv of Cu­(II) per dimer was sufficient for saturation. This discrepancy suggests that the unexpected stoichiometry observed in the Cu­(II)­DP1 crystal structure likely arises from the crystallization conditions, under which excess copper ions were unavoidable.

In contrast, the X-ray crystal structure of Cu­(I)­DP1, obtained by crystallization in anaerobic conditions and flash-freezing, reveals two dimers in the asymmetric unit, each forming the targeted [Cu­(bpy)2]+ coordination environment through a crystallographic 2-fold symmetry operation at the interface between two monomers (Figure B). In contrast to the archetypal [Cu­(bpy)2]2+ and [Cu­(bpy)2]+ complexes, which adopt slightly distorted square-planar and tetrahedral geometries, respectively, − the Cu­(I) center in Cu­(I)­DP1 adopts a coordination environment lying between distorted square-planar and pseudo-octahedral geometries. Consequently, Cu­(I)­DP1 exhibits a dihedral angle of 32.6° between two bipyridine ligands and a τ4 value of 0.34, closely matching those of [Cu­(bpy)2]2+ (35.1° and 0.34) rather than [Cu­(bpy)2]+ (72.5° and 0.69), respectively. This enforced geometry permits weak axial association of two water molecules, with Cu–O distances of 2.85 Å in trans-configuration, along with hydrogen-bonding interactions with surrounding solvent molecules and the D106 side chain.

The Cu–N bond distances observed in Cu­(I)­DP1 (2.07–2.24 Å, average = 2.18 Å) are slightly longer than those observed in Cu­(II)­DP1 and in small-molecule [Cu­(bpy)2]2+ (1.99 Å) and [Cu­(bpy)2]+ (2.03 Å) complexes. This elongation is consistent with the population of an antibonding orbital in the reduction. The surrounding protein environments may also contribute to the observed distances, as previously demonstrated for sterically encumbered bipyridine analogue complexes. ,

The angular parameters are also substantially altered relative to the protein-free complex. The protein-free [Cu­(bpy)2]+ complex shows dispersed interbpy cis N–Cu–N angles, defined between one nitrogen of each bpy ligand, differing by an average of 20.1°. By contrast, the corresponding angle in Cu­(I)­DP1 is substantially reduced: 3° and 16.7° for the Nproximal and Ndistal pairs, respectively (Figure C and Table S11D).

For the DP2 protein bearing bpy-Ala at the R46Z position, only the Cu­(II)-bound structure was obtained (Figure C), with the asymmetric unit containing two monomers. DP2 forms two mononuclear Cu­(II)­(bpy)­(Glu)­(OH2)2 species that adopt a coordination geometry closer to square-pyramidal geometry with a τ5 value of 0.31. Therefore, rather than forming a [Cu­(bpy)2]2+ species, Cu­(II)­DP2 also exhibits a Cu-to-bpy ratio of 1:1, corresponding to 2 equiv of Cu­(II) per dimer. This stoichiometry is consistent with the copper titration results in solution, indicating that the DP2 protein scaffold requires further redesign.

Finally, both TP1 and TP6 structures complexed with Cu­(II) were determined with asymmetric units containing three monomers. Although partial in situ reduction during X-ray diffraction cannot be completely ruled out, both structures exhibit the designed [Cu­(bpy)3]2+ complexes, in which three bpy ligands simultaneously coordinate a single copper center (Figures , S15, and S16). The octahedral geometries observed with Cu­(II)­TP1 (Figure A) and Cu­(II)­TP6 (Figure B) are also consistent with the UV–vis, titration, and EPR parameters. Notably, the designed complexes are present as distinct enantiomeric forms, with TP1 adopting Δ- and TP6 adopting Λ-configuration, indicating that local protein environments can program the formation of specific isomers with metal-centered chirality.

For geometric comparison, we also analyzed previously reported octahedral Cu­(II) complexes (Figures C,D; Table S10A,B). Depending on the counteranion and crystallization conditions, the structures of [Cu­(bpy)3]2+ display considerable variability (CCDC 2048918, 640435, and 654782). One structure displays archetypal Jahn–Teller distortion, whereas the other shows partial Cu–N bond dissociation, accompanied by pronounced distortion of the bipyridine ring. Consequently, the equatorial Cu–N bond lengths range from 2.01 to 2.10 Å (average 2.05 Å), whereas the axial Cu–N bond lengths range from 2.16 to 2.45 Å (average 2.28 Å), corresponding to a maximal deviation of 0.44 Å. The cis-N–Cu–N bond angles also deviate significantly from the symmetric octahedral geometry.

In contrast, Cu­(II)­TP1 exhibits reduced tetragonal elongation relative to synthetic analogs, with Cu–N bond lengths spanning 2.13 to 2.29 Å (average 2.22 Å) and a maximal deviation of 0.26 Å. These distances are longer than the equatorial Cu–N bonds in [Cu­(bpy)3]2+ complexes (2.05 Å) yet shorter than the elongated axial Cu–N bonds (2.28 Å) observed for protein-free [Cu­(bpy)3]2+ or analogous Cu­(II)­N6 complexes (average 2.1–2.2 Å; Δax‑eq 0.2–0.6 Å). These data demonstrate that the preorganized hexadentate coordination within the protein promotes a substantially more symmetrical arrangement around the copper center, thereby geometrically constraining and suppressing the Jahn-Teller distortion.

The effects of protein-imposed strains on the interbpy cis-N–Cu–N angles in Cu­(II)­TP1, however, are minimal. The protein-free [Cu­(bpy)3]2+ complexes exhibit average interbpy cis-N–Cu–N angles of 94.3–94.5°, with deviations ranging from 4.4 to 18.3, whereas the average of the corresponding angles in Cu­(II)­TP1 is 94.6°, with an overall angular deviation of 16.1°.

Next, we determined the structure of Cu­(I)­TP1 by soaking Cu­(II)­TP1 crystals with excess sodium dithionite (Figures A and S15). Upon reduction, the Cu­(II)­TP1 crystals immediately developed the characteristic purple color associated with the formation of Cu­(I)­TP1, which was retained throughout X-ray diffraction data collection. The resulting structure reveals that three bpy ligands remain simultaneously coordinated to the copper center, giving rise to a [Cu­(bpy)3]+ species in a coordination environment approaching octahedral geometry. To the best of our knowledge, a hexadentate [Cu­(bpy)3]+-like complex adopting an octahedral coordination environment has not been reported, precluding direct comparison.

The average Cu–N bond length in Cu­(I)­TP1 (2.22 Å) is indistinguishable from that of the Cu­(II)-bound structure within experimental uncertainty (Figure C). However, the bond-length distribution is slightly broader in the Cu­(I) state (0.27 Å) range, as one bipyridine ligand exhibits noticeably elongated Cu–N bonds (2.32–2.33 Å) relative to the other two ligands (2.06–2.27 Å)a structural feature absent in Cu­(II)­TP1. Furthermore, the average cis-N–Cu–N angle (94.6°) in Cu­(I)­TP1 is comparable to that observed in the Cu­(II)-bound form, although deviations in these angles are smaller (9.4°) (Figure D). These observations suggest that population of antibonding orbitals in the Cu­(I) state partially weakens specific Cu–N interactions while allowing a less twisted coordination environment. Despite this weakening, dissociation of the bipyridine ligand is disfavored, likely owing to geometric constraints imposed by the protein scaffold, thereby maintaining all six coordination interactions.

Analogously, both Cu­(II)­TP6 and Cu­(I)­TP6 structures revealed octahedral [Cu­(bpy)3]+2/+ complexes within the trimers; the Cu­(I)­TP6 structure was obtained by flash-freezing crystals grown under anaerobic conditions (Figure B). The average bond distances and angles of Cu­(II)­TP6 and Cu­(I)­TP6 also remain essentially identical across both oxidation states within the resolution limit, with mean Cu–N distances of 2.16 Å (2.13–2.23 Å, maximum deviation of 0.10 Å) for Cu­(II) and 2.24 Å for Cu­(I) (2.13–2.37 Å of 0.24 Å) (Figure C).

The interbpy cis N–Cu–N angles became widely dispersed with negligible changes between two oxidation states, with average values of 93.9° (maximum deviation of 35.4°) and 92.5° (maximum deviation of 33.5°), respectively (Figure D). The angular deviation was greater than the protein-free complex and TP1, likely reflecting the intrinsic asymmetry of bpy-Ala, which contains two nonequivalent nitrogen donors (Nproximal and Ndistal), as well as local structural features of TP6.

Therefore, despite the structural variations and strain effects, the CuTP1 and CuTP6 structures at both oxidation states collectively demonstrate that protein scaffolds can enforce access to coordination environments that are unfavorable and rare in small-molecule systems. Therefore, this work underscores the capacity of physical strain to expand the accessible landscape of copper coordination chemistry.

Computational Study of Protein-Bound Cuprous Complexes

Using the X-ray crystal structures of pseudosquare planar Cu­(I)­DP1 and octahedral Cu­(I)­TP1 and Cu­(I)­TP6 complexes, we conducted time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calculations (Figure ). The optical spectra experimentally determined in the 350–600 nm region (Figure C) are well reproduced computationally (Figure S17) and assigned to MLCT transitions from the Cu d orbitals, eg for Cu­(I)­DP1 and t2g for Cu­(I)­TP1 and Cu­(I)­TP6, to the bpy ligand 1π* and 2π* orbitals, respectively.

For Cu­(I)­DP1 and Cu­(I)­TP1, all d orbitals have comparable overlap with all π* orbitals, resulting in a relatively uniform distribution of d → π* transition intensities. In contrast, the DFT-optimized geometry of Cu­(I)­TP6 shows an asymmetric torsional distortion of one bpy ligand relative to the others, producing a pronounced asymmetry in its MLCT transitions: the three d orbitals are predominantly directed toward a single 1π* orbital, while overlap with the remaining 1π* orbitals are significantly attenuated; transitions to the higher-lying 2π* remain more evenly distributed. This distortion may selectively preserve d/π* overlap for one π* acceptor orbital while diminishing it for the rest.

Redox Properties of bpy-Ala Mutants

We examined whether protein-enforced coordination geometry and structural distortions influence the redox properties of the copper complexes. The cyclic voltammograms of Cu­(I)­DP1 (Figure A) and Cu­(I)­TP1 (Figure B) were recorded under anaerobic conditions, revealing quasi-reversible responses with E 1/2 values of −142 mV and −205 mV versus Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl) reference electrode, respectively (Figure D). For Cu­(I)­DP2 and Cu­(I)­TP6, reliable cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were not obtained; therefore, we conducted the titration experiments with redox-active dye, methylene blue (E°′= – 220 mV versus Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl)) with sodium dithionite (Figures C,D and S18), resulting in the E 1/2 values of −205 mV and −219 mV versus Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl) for CuDP2 and CuTP6, respectively.

The measured redox potentials of all bpy-Ala-containing proteins are more negative than those of the protein-free [Cu­(bpy)2]2+/+ (−120 mV) (Figure A,D), [Cu­(bpy)3]2+/+ (−120 mV) (Figure B), as well as those of previously reported 1:1 Cu­(bpy)-containing proteins (+180 mV and +250 mV), and those of other natural Cu proteins, such as azurin (+96 mV) and plastocyanin (+166 mV) versus Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl). This behavior parallels a previous study reporting an anodic shift in the Cu­(II)/Cu­(I) redox potential upon substituting 2,2′-bipyridine with 6,6′-dimethyl-2,2′-bipyridine in a 1:2 complex, where steric hindrance suppresses flattening upon oxidation, thereby destabilizing the Cu­(II) center.

Although the X-ray structure of Cu­(I)­DP2 has not been determined, the decreased redox potentials of Cu­(II/I)­DP1, TP1, and TP6 are likely derived not only from the electronic properties of the primary coordination sphere but also from structural factors: protein-imposed structural distortion destabilizes the Cu­(I) state while preferentially stabilizing the Cu­(II) state. These structural factors likely act in concert with contributions from the secondary coordination spheres, as well as local electrostatic interactions, to tune the redox potentials.

Electron Self-Exchange Rate

The rapid electron transfer rates of blue copper proteins are attributed to their entatic state, which minimizes the structural reorganization required upon redox cycling of the metal center. , Given that the crystal structures of our trimeric protein scaffolds revealed a high degree of similarity between the two oxidation states, we sought to determine whether the entatic-state principle extends to our designed system. As a representative case, we measured the electron self-exchange rate of CuTP1 by comparing the transverse relaxation times (T 2) of selected resonances in the fully reduced sample and in a 1:1 mixture of Cu­(I)- and Cu­(II)-bound TP1 (Figures E,F and S19), as reported previously. −

We initially aimed to monitor resonances derived from the bpy ligands; however, reliable analysis in the aromatic region was hindered by a low signal-to-noise ratio. Instead, we followed two prominent peaks that broadened upon addition of Cu­(II)­TP1. Although further NMR studies are required for full assignments, the selected resonances are tentatively assigned to singlet protons of residues near the copper-binding site. From these data, the electron self-exchange rate constant of CuTP1 was determined to be 2.2 × 104 M–1 s–1 on average, based on values of 2.3 × 104 and 2.2 × 104 M–1 s–1 for the resonances at 3.79 and 7.62 ppm, respectively at 298 K. These values are comparable to those reported for blue copper proteins such as plastocyanin (2.0 × 104 M–1 s–1) and rusticyanin (1.7 × 104 M–1 s–1), although slower than azurin (9.6 × 105 M–1 s–1).

The relatively rapid electron self-exchange between Cu­(II)­TP1 and Cu­(I)­TP1 is consistent with minimal structural reorganization at the copper center, suggesting that protein-imposed geometric constraints promote an entatic-state regime. To the best of our knowledge, bottom-up design of metalloproteins exhibiting entatic-state-like behavior remains rare. Given that the surrounding protein environment is not optimized for transient formation of a properly oriented encounter complex, these properties may be further optimized through mutation, for example, by removing charged residues near the surface-exposed metal site.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that protein scaffolds can function as preorganized, mechanically active ligand environments that reshape the classical coordination preferences of copper complexes. By genetically encoding bipyridine-alanine at defined positions within dimeric and trimeric protein architectures, we accessed a diverse set of copper coordination environments across both the Cu­(II) and Cu­(I) oxidation states. Combined structural, spectroscopic, electrochemical, and kinetic analyses reveal that protein-imposed geometric constraints can attenuate Jahn–Teller distortion, enforce unusually symmetric coordination environments, enable hexadentate Cu­(I) complexes, and modulate redox behavior. Notably, stabilization of octahedral [Cu­(bpy)3]2+/+ sites with closely matched geometries in both oxidation states highlights how macromolecular ligand frameworks can compensate for electronic preferences that disfavor such structures in small-molecule systems. Collectively, these findings establish protein scaffolds as versatile platforms and introduce a design principle for engineering artificial metalloproteins and expanding the accessible chemical space of transition-metal complexes.
