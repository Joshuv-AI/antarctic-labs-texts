Percutaneous Lymphatic Contrast-enhanced Ultrasound for Preoperative Localization in Stage 2–3 Lymphedema: A Feasibility Study
Authors: Lanjing Wu, Shufang Yuan, Liping Chen, Chenyang Zhao, Nan Zhuang, Yi Chen, Sirui Liu, Xia Liu, Jingjing Wen, Longbiao Yu, Zhegang Zhou, Desheng Sun, Zhengming Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Lymphaticovenous anastomosis (LVA) is a crucial surgical method for treating lymphedema by reconstructing lymphatic pathways. The success and efficacy of LVA rely on accurate preoperative localization of functional lymphatic vessels. This study investigated the use of percutaneous lymphatic contrast-enhanced ultrasound (PL-CEUS) to identify and localize these vessels in patients with the International Society of Lymphology stages 2–3 before LVA.Methods:This retrospective cohort study included 234 patients with 256 limbs affected by International Society of Lymphology stage 2–3 lymphedema. Preoperatively, a contrast agent was injected intradermally, and functional lymphatic vessels were dynamically visualized and localized by ultrasound. Using surgical findings as the gold standard, the localization accuracy of PL-CEUS was calculated. The diameter and depth of the lymphatic vessels from the body surface were analyzed. Short-term postoperative efficacy was evaluated by comparing changes in limb circumference within 1 week postoperatively.Results:PL-CEUS visualized lymphatic vessels in 218 of 256 limbs (85.2%), identifying 468 vessels. The mean inner diameters were 0.52 ± 0.26 mm (stage 2) and 0.59 ± 0.35 mm (stage 3), with mean depths of 8.61 ± 3.40 and 11.03 ± 4.38 mm, respectively. Surgical verification showed localization accuracies of 96.04% (stage 2) and 93.64% (stage 3). Postoperative limb circumference measurements within 1 week revealed a significant reduction, with a mean reduction rate of 3.73% ± 3.55%.Conclusions:PL-CEUS accurately localizes superficial functional lymphatic vessels and may serve as an alternative or complementary method to indocyanine green lymphography for preoperative lymphatic mapping in LVA.

Takeaways

Question: Can percutaneous lymphatic contrast-enhanced ultrasound accurately map functional lymphatic vessels during preoperative planning for lymphaticovenous anastomosis in stage 2–3 lymphedema patients?

Findings: A retrospective diagnostic study confirmed precise localization of functional target lymphatics using percutaneous lymphatic contrast-enhanced ultrasound (>90% accuracy), with 4% limb circumference reduction observed at 1 week postoperatively (P < 0.001).

Meaning: This technique provides a safer and more effective preoperative localization for stage 2–3 lymphedema patients and could serve as an alternative or complementary approach to ICG lymphography in lymphaticovenous anastomosis surgical planning.

INTRODUCTION

Lymphedema is a chronic disorder resulting from lymphatic system dysfunction. Nonsurgical management primarily involves methods such as positional drainage, compressive bandaging, or manual lymphatic drainage to alleviate limb swelling. In recent years, the emergence of supermicrosurgical techniques has transformed the clinical management paradigm of lymphedema.1,2 “Supermicrosurgery” refers to anastomosis in small blood vessels (0.3–0.8 mm in diameter). In 2000, Koshima et al3 first reported lymphaticovenous anastomosis (LVA) with a diameter less than 0.8 mm, providing an innovative approach for treating lymphedema. Subsequent studies and improvements in surgical techniques have demonstrated the efficacy and safety of LVA in various types of edema.4 Currently, LVA has evolved into a well-established surgical procedure for treating lymphedema and is widely applied in clinical practice.

Preoperative precise identification and localization of functional lymphatic vessels are critical determinants of LVA surgical success.5 Traditional indocyanine green (ICG) lymphography has limited penetration depth, as it can only visualize superficial lymphatic vessels. It is difficult to locate patent lymphatic vessels in patients with advanced (stage 2–3) lymphedema, and their functional status cannot be assessed.6 Although high-frequency ultrasound (US) (15–20 MHz) serves as an alternative to ICG, its diagnostic performance is highly operator-dependent and struggles to distinguish lymphatic vessels from veins or nerves.7 The latest ultra-high-resolution US systems (with frequencies up to 70 MHz) exhibit higher sensitivity and specificity for small structures (such as lymphatic vessels), providing clearer morphology. However, their penetration depth is limited to within 10 mm of the body surface, and image quality remains highly operator-dependent, potentially restricting their application in severely edematous limbs.8

As a radiation-free, real-time dynamic image modality, percutaneous lymphatic contrast-enhanced ultrasound (PL-CEUS) has demonstrated unique advantages in the lymphatic system assessment in recent years. Intradermal injection of microbubble contrast agents enables real-time observation of lymphatic vessel visualization, lymph fluid flow, and valve function. This study used PL-CEUS as a preoperative method to evaluate and identify superficial lymphatic vessels in edematous patients, aiming to explore the potential application value of PL-CEUS in preoperative lymphatic vessel localization for patients with stage 2–3 lymphedema undergoing LVA and investigate its impact on surgical efficacy.

Instruments and Methods

US Instruments and Contrast Agent

The US examination was performed by a US physician with more than 10 years of experience. All data measurements were taken 3 times and averaged to reduce measurement errors. An Aplio i900 US diagnostic system (Canon, Japan) was used, equipped with high-frequency linear array probes (i24LX8 probe, frequency 8–24 MHz; i18LX5 probe, frequency 5–18 MHz). PL-CEUS imaging used low-mechanical index harmonic imaging technology. The US contrast agent used was sulfur hexafluoride (SF6 lyophilized powder; Bracco, Italy). The contrast agent was prepared by mixing 59 mg of SF6 lyophilized powder with 5 mL of normal saline to form a 5.0 mL suspension, which was administered percutaneously at 0.3 mL per injection site. The suspension must be shaken for 30 seconds before each injection.10

Procedure of Contrast-enhanced US Examination

First, a 22G needle syringe was used to inject 0.3 mL of the US contrast agent suspension intradermally at each injection site. For the upper limb, injection sites included the first to fourth interdigital webs, the midpoint of the wrist crease on the radial side, the medial and lateral aspects of the elbow, and the median cubital fossa. For the lower limb, injection sites included the first to fourth interdigital webs, the medial and lateral malleoli, and the medial and lateral aspects of the knee. Local gentle massage was performed for 10–15 seconds to facilitate distal diffusion of the contrast agent.11 Immediately, a PL-CEUS examination was performed. A preset lymphography mode on the US diagnostic system (Aplio i900, Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Japan) was used, with the depth set between 2 and 2.5 cm, the focus located at the base of the target lymphatic vessel, and the mechanical index set between 0.04 and 0.09. An i18LX5 transducer (frequency: 5–18 MHz) was used.

Under CEUS mode, contrast microbubbles taken up by functionally intact lymphatic vessels seemed as focal hyperechoic points extending from the injection site in the cross-section, and as strip-shaped hyperenhancements with linear courses in the longitudinal section (Fig. 2). (See Video 1 [online], which shows the scan starting perpendicular to the long axis of the limb, observing a focal hyperechoic area, then changing the probe direction to parallel to the long axis to reveal a linear hyperenhancement lumen with visible flow of contrast agent [microbubble signals] within the lumen.) Some lymphatic vessels showed abrupt truncation signs (Fig. 3).12 (See Video 2 [online], which shows a truncation sign in one of the small tributary lymphatic vessels.) Functionally intact lymphatic vessels were identified and marked on the body surface with a black marker pen. The probe was then switched to i24LX8 (frequency 8–24 MHz), with instrument parameters adjusted: depth set at 2–2.5 cm, focus at 1 cm depth, and mechanical index at 0.7–1.0, to reconfirm the identified lymphatic vessels and measure their inner/outer diameters and depth from the body surface. Continuing with the i24LX8 probe (frequency 8–24 MHz), superficial small veins matching the lymphatic vessel diameter were sought near the visualized lymphatics; their diameters and depths from the body surface were measured, and they were marked on the body surface with a red marker pen.

ICG Lymphography Protocol

In this study, all patients underwent ICG lymphography before contrast-enhanced US examinations. The procedure was performed using the HH-C-02 device (Harbin Haihong Jiye, China) and conducted by clinicians with extensive experience in ICG lymphographic imaging. The imaging protocol for ICG lymphography was performed as follows: ICG was diluted with 10 mL of 5% dextrose solution (used as the solvent). For upper extremities, the injection sites included the first to fourth interdigital webs and the midpoint of the wrist crease on the radial side; for lower extremities, the injection sites included the first to fourth interdigital webs and the medial malleoli. A total of 0.1 mL of the diluted ICG solution was administered via subcutaneous injection at each site. After the injection, the sites were gently pressed with the thumb to facilitate absorption. Imaging was visualized using an infrared imaging system 5–10 minutes postinjection, and fluorescent images were reevaluated 12–24 hours later. Based on the ICG lymphography findings, lymphatic drainage was staged according to the lymphatic drainage blockage staging system13: stage 0 and stage 1 were defined as “complete imaging visualization,” stage 2 and stage 3 as “partial imaging visualization,” and stage 4 and stage 5 as “no imaging visualization.”

Surgical Validation

All patients underwent LVA surgery within 2 weeks after PL-CEUS examination, performed by the same team of microsurgeons (Z.-G.Z., L.-B.Y., each with ≥5 y of LVA experience and performing more than 100 LVA procedures annually). A 2–3 cm skin incision was made along the CEUS-marked sites, and the surgical field was explored under a microscope (Mitaka, Tokyo, Japan; Model: MM51/YOH). We determined localization accuracy based on intraoperative observations. First, lymphatic vessels need to be visualized during surgery. These vessels must meet 2 criteria: their diameters should be similar to those marked by PL-CEUS, and they should be located within 1 cm of the marked lines. If both criteria were satisfied, the localization was considered accurate.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 25.0. Continuous variables (such as lymphatic vessel inner diameter and depth from the body surface) were expressed as mean ± SD (X ± s), and categorical variables were described by frequency (percentage). Postoperative efficacy was evaluated using paired-sample t tests to compare limb circumference differences between preoperation and within 1 week after surgery. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Limb circumference was measured at 5-cm intervals along the longitudinal axis using the wrist and elbow (for upper limbs) or ankle and knee (for lower limbs) as landmarks. The average circumference was then calculated from the measurements of all segments.14

DISCUSSION

LVA is a minimally invasive surgery that redirects lymphatic fluid to veins, serving as a promising therapeutic intervention for relieving or resolving lymphedema. Accurate identification and localization of lymphatic vessels is crucial for LVA success. ICG lymphography is recognized as the gold standard for assessing lymphedema staging and identifying lymphatic vessels. After intradermal injection, ICG binds to albumin and lipoproteins; when excited by near-infrared light, the resulting fluorescent complex renders the lymphatics clearly visible. However, due to the limited penetration of infrared light through skin, ICG lymphography can only visualize superficial lymphatic structures within 1–1.5 cm of the subcutaneous layer.15 Additionally, literature reports an ICG lymphography visualization rate of approximately 40%.16 In this study, all patients underwent ICG lymphography preoperatively, with a visualization rate of only 12.5%. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that all patients in this study had moderate to advanced lymphedema, classified as the International Society of Lymphology stages 2–3, in whom lymphatic vessel damage is more severe, which might thus limit the application of ICG in such patients. The visualization rate of PL-CEUS was as high as 85.2%, showing a statistically significant difference compared with ICG. PL-CEUS was significantly superior to ICG; thus, for patients with moderate to severe lymphedema, PL-CEUS should be the preferred choice for preoperative lymphatic vessel localization.

The results of this study indicate that PL-CEUS can intuitively and efficiently localize lymphatic vessels, assess their function, and enable real-time dynamic observation of lymph flow dynamics. Because this technique does not require the use of radioactive substances, it offers higher safety and reproducibility. With the advancement and improvement of US technology, PL-CEUS has provided a new prospect for the localization and assessment of superficial lymphatic vessels.17,18

Findings from this study show that PL-CEUS clearly depicts lymphatic vessel distribution and course, identifies lymphatic vessels with wider inner diameters, and evaluates their functional status (Fig. 4), providing clearer surgical anastomosis sites for LVA. (See Video 3 [online], which shows the long strip-shaped lymphatic vessels after intradermal injection of contrast agent, with B-mode US showing obvious peristaltic waves and an inner lymphatic vessel diameter of about 0.37 mm, proving effective lymphatic vessel function.) This improves anastomosis success rates and reduces mean surgical duration per limb to 2.25 ± 0.75 hours (stage 2) and 2.39 ± 0.79 hours (stage 3), significantly shorter than LVA surgery guided by ICG lymphography, thereby enhancing surgical efficiency and reducing anesthesia and infection risks.19 Moreover, PL-CEUS rapidly identifies and assesses functional lymphatic vessels by observing contrast agent flow, greatly improving examination efficiency.17

Following lymphatic vessel localization by PL-CEUS, high-frequency US (i24LX8 probe, frequency 8–24 MHz) was used to identify superficial small veins matching the lymphatic vessel diameters near the visualized lymphatics. This assists microsurgeons in confirming LVA sites more conveniently and efficiently during LVA, thus shortening surgery time. In contrast, ICG lymphography cannot localize small veins that match lymphatic vessels.20

Additionally, because ICG contains sodium iodide, ICG lymphangiography is contraindicated in individuals with iodine allergy.21 The contrast agent SonoVue for PL-CEUS contains sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) as its active ingredient, which is an inert gas rapidly eliminated via the lungs, with no hepatic or renal toxicity.22 Its common adverse reactions include headache, rash, erythema at the injection site, pruritus, and allergy to sulfur hexafluoride or any excipients. According to literature reports, the incidence of severe hypersensitivity reactions to SonoVue is only 0.034%.23 Because it is iodine-free and its allergic reactions are controllable, SonoVue can be safely used in patients with iodine allergy.24 In this study, among 234 patients, 7 patients reported mild pruritus at the injection site, and 15 patients developed skin flushing after the injection of the US contrast agent. All reactions resolved spontaneously within 24 hours; no severe allergic reactions were observed. This indicates that PL-CEUS is a safe procedure.

In patients with lymphedema undergoing preoperative PL-CEUS localization, limb circumference measurements 1 week post-LVA showed a significant circumference reduction (preoperative versus postoperative: 39.65 versus 38.08 cm, P < 0.01), with an average reduction rate of 3.73%, accompanied by improved clinical symptoms. These results suggest favorable outcomes of LVA surgery in patients with preoperative PL-CEUS localization.

Approximately 14.8% of limbs in this study showed nonvisualization of lymphatic vessels by PL-CEUS, potentially related to incorrect contrast agent preparation, excessive injection pressure, overly deep injection sites, lack of contrast agent uptake, and complete obstruction of local lymphatic vessels, warranting further validation. For this subset of patients, we attempted to perform lymphatic vessel localization using high-frequency US (transducer: i24LX8; frequency: 8–24 MHz). However, this is highly dependent on the US physician’s ability to accurately identify the ultrasonic imaging features of lymphatic vessels. Future research will refine study designs and methods, continue exploring PL-CEUS imaging features of lymphatic vessels in lymphedema patients, and evaluate the application of PL-CEUS in assessing postoperative lymphatic vessel recovery and LVA efficacy.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study, which may be subject to selection bias and potentially affect the generalizability of the conclusions. Second, the results lack assessment by multiple observers, which may introduce subjectivity. Third, the assessment of postoperative outcomes is based solely on data at 1 week postoperatively, with a lack of data on long-term efficacy. Fourth, the measurement of the affected limb’s circumference may involve certain errors due to factors such as the operator’s technique, the tightness of the measuring tape, and the condition of the soft tissues. Fifth, due to individual differences in the severity of lymphedema among patients, the number of anastomoses in each LVA procedure is not fixed and is difficult to quantify. Because this variable was not included in the analysis for this study, it may lead to potential bias in the statistics regarding the duration of unilateral procedures.

CONCLUSIONS

Percutaneous lymphatic contrast-enhanced US can accurately localize superficial functional lymphatic vessels and provide optimal anastomosis sites for LVA. This technique is expected to become an alternative or complementary approach to ICG lymphography in preoperative lymphatic mapping for LVA.
