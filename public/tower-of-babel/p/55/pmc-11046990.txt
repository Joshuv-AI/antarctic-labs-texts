Optimising admission to scope time for acute GI bleeding (UGIB): retrospective comparative analysis of the timing of endoscopy for UGIB in a Queensland quaternary centre following international recommendations for best practice
Authors: Anthony Bergin, Michael Christmas, Wei Lian Tan, Yuming Ding, Lei Lin, Myat Myat Khaing, Tony Rahman
Published: 2024

Timing proportions of endoscopy for UGIB.

Background and aim

An acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) refers to GI blood loss occurring proximal to the ligament of Treitz. UGIB is a common gastroenterological emergency, estimated to occur in around 80 to 150 per 100,000 people per year and is associated with high mortality and morbidity. Lau et al found that urgent endoscopy performed within less than 6 hours has not been shown to reduce mortality when compared to endoscopy performed between 6 and 24 hours.1 Best practice suggests that endoscopy now be performed within 24 hours.2 We aimed to undertake a retrospective comparative analysis of timing of endoscopy provided for UGIB at a metropolitan teaching hospital in Queensland prior to and following April 2020.

Method

Data from patients admitted with UGIB from April 2018 to March 2019 (cohort 1) were analysed and compared with data from April 2021 to March 2022 (cohort 2). Endoscopic, administrative, biochemical and radiological data were collected from Provation® MD and other clinical medical databases. Patients admitted from the emergency department with melaena, haematemesis, coffee-ground emesis or suspected UGIB were included. Patients who had been in hospital for greater than 48 hours, outpatients and an endoscopy indication other than bleeding were excluded. For each patient, the age, Glasgow-Blatchford score (GBS), haemoglobin (on admission), endoscopy indication, endoscopic findings, treatment, timing of endoscopy, (all-cause) 30-day mortality and 30-day repeat procedure were noted.

Results

Cohort 1: 84 patients met the inclusion criteria; mean (±SD) age was 65±18 with mean GBS of 10±3.8. Mean timing to endoscopy was 13.9±12.8 hours; 70 (83%) performed within 24 hours of admission. Thirty (36%) performed within 6 hours, eight (10%) at 6–12 hours, 32 (38%) at 12–24 hours, and 14 (17%) after 24 hours. Pathology was detected in 76 (90%) endoscopies; 42 (50%) patients requiring haemostatic treatment. Thirty-day all-cause mortality was five (6%) and six (7%) patients requiring further endoscopic intervention within 30 days. Cohort 2: 128 patients met the inclusion criteria; mean age was 68±16 with mean GBS of 9.5±4.3. Mean timing to endoscopy was 17.8±12.2 hours; 101 (79%) were performed within 24 hours of admission time. Twenty-six (20%) were performed within 6 hours, 15 (12%) between 6–12 hours, 60 (47%) between 12–24 hours, and 27 (21%) after 24 hours. Pathology was detected in 105 (82%) endoscopies; 38 (30%) patients requiring haemostatic treatment. Thirty-day all-cause mortality was eight (6%) with 16 (12%) patients requiring further endoscopic intervention within 30 days.

Conclusion

The timing of endoscopies for UGIB has been noted to have changed, possibly a consequence of international consensus. Longer periods of acid suppression prior to endoscopy have been noted and this has led to a reduction in the need for haemostatic intervention during endoscopy. There has been a reduction in the proportion of endoscopies performed within 6 hours for acute UGIB, and an increase in those within 6 to 24 hours with no change in 30 day-mortality.
