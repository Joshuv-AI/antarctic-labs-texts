A Case of N-Methyl-D-Aspartate (NMDA) Autoimmune Encephalitis in a 34-Year-Old Man Thought to Have Schizophrenia

Introduction

N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) autoantibody encephalitis is a rare autoimmune condition with an annual incidence of approximately 1.5 per million people.1 It is most prevalent in young women and is often associated with tumors, particularly teratomas.1 Symptoms can vary, with manifestations ranging from psychosis to respiratory failure.1 Due to its nonspecific symptoms, which can mimic the acute onset of schizophrenia—including paranoia, delusions, apathy, and avolition2—NMDA encephalitis is often misdiagnosed as a primary psychiatric disorder, leading to treatment delays. Key differentiating features that can steer a diagnosis toward an autoimmune etiology include seizures, autonomic instability, and hyperkinesia.3 This case report highlights the importance of considering NMDA autoantibody encephalitis in patients with a change in mental status, describing a male patient initially misdiagnosed with a psychiatric illness who was ultimately found to have NMDA autoantibody encephalitis.

Case presentation

We present the case of a 34-year-old male transferred to our facility for worsening encephalopathy. Review of available health records spanning approximately 2–3 years prior to admission revealed no significant chronic medical history; prior healthcare encounters were limited to episodic, self-limited conditions including episodes of dysuria and vertigo. No prior psychiatric diagnoses, substance use disorders, or regular outpatient medications were identified. Social history was limited by the patient's incarcerated status, and no family history of psychiatric illness was documented in accessible records. His symptoms began as decreased communication and mild agitation while incarcerated, reportedly in the setting of a hunger strike, for approximately one week prior to first clinical evaluation. This led to his transfer to an inpatient psychiatric facility, where he was diagnosed with new-onset schizophrenia based on auditory and visual hallucinations, avolition, anhedonia, social withdrawal, and signs of catatonia. He was treated at that facility with olanzapine, ondansetron, and linezolid prior to further transfer.

His condition progressively declined, prompting a transfer to an outside hospital for a medical workup. Preliminary tests, including neuroimaging and a lumbar puncture, were unrevealing. As he continued to deteriorate with profound agitation, confusion, leukocytosis, hypernatremia, and acute kidney injury, he was intubated due to concerns he could not protect his own airway and transferred to our facility for further neurologic evaluation.

Upon arrival, he was on mechanical ventilation and intravenous sedation for a persistent catatonic state. Physical examination revealed episodic tonic stiffening of his limbs, upper lip tremoring, and hyperreflexia without clonus. He was unresponsive to commands and did not withdraw from noxious even when sedation was weaned. Initial labs showed a creatinine kinase (CK) of 1524 U/L (normal 55–170 U/L), a renal creatinine (Cr) level of 0.7 mg/dL (normal 0.7–1.3 mg/dL) and a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 24.2 mg/dL (normal <3 mg/dL), indicating a possible inflammatory process. Brain computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were unremarkable for acute pathology such as a stroke, hemorrhage, or mass (Figs. 1 and 2). A fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) scan was not obtained.

A repeat lumbar puncture performed eight days after his initial one at the outside hospital showed increased protein and total nucleated cells but a negative microbiology panel. An autoimmune panel was sent to an outside laboratory. A scrotal ultrasound was unrevealing for a mass. Long-term electroencephalography (EEG) monitoring for 48 h showed nonspecific, moderately severe diffuse encephalopathy. Review of records from the outside facility showed he had received olanzapine, ondansetron, and linezolid. Given his worsening catatonia and the use of serotonergic medications, serotonin syndrome was a leading differential diagnosis. All serotonergic medications were held, and he was started on cyproheptadine; however, there was no clinical improvement.

During a sedation-weaning trial, he had further episodes of stiffening and upper lip tremors. A second long-term EEG for 48 h again showed nonspecific severe diffuse encephalopathy without definitive motor seizures. Given his prolonged condition and failed extubation attempts, he underwent tracheostomy and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube placement three weeks after initial intubation.

While awaiting autoimmune panel results, he was empirically started on a steroid course of 1 g for three days, followed by a prednisone taper. The autoimmune panel from the lumbar puncture eventually reported positive for anti-NMDA receptor antibodies (IgG) (See Table 1). He completed the steroid course and five courses of plasma exchange, but with no noticeable clinical improvement. He then received five days of IVIG followed by Rituximab. With the latter therapy, he slowly began to show neurologic improvement. He was weaned off sedation and was able to move his limbs, although he still had delays with speech and communication, making his long-term prognosis unclear. Discussion

NMDA encephalitis is a rare autoimmune condition that should be considered in patients presenting with worsening encephalopathy. A 2013 cohort study found that women comprise approximately 81% of all cases, with a median age of 21 years.1 The condition is less common in those over 454 and has a higher crude incidence in Black, Hispanic, and Asian populations.5 Approximately half of all cases are associated with teratomas, particularly in the ovaries or testes.6 Our patient, a male above the median age without a tumor, demonstrates the importance of considering this condition regardless of typical demographic or etiological factors.

The disease is mediated by autoantibodies that target NMDA receptors in the central nervous system (CNS).6 While the mechanism is not fully understood, one proposed theory is that teratomas, which can contain brain cells, trigger a breakdown in immunological tolerance. This allows autoantibodies to cross-react with NMDA receptors in the brain after the blood–brain barrier is compromised. 6–8 These autoantibodies are thought to cause neuronal damage by internalizing receptors or through the recruitment of the complement cascade.7,8 Diagnosis is confirmed by detecting NMDA receptor antibodies in serum or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).

As our patient's case illustrates, one of the primary issues hindering diagnosis is that symptoms are often mistaken for other conditions. Prodromal symptoms such as headache and cough can mimic a respiratory illness,9 while subsequent manifestations like paranoia and psychosis are often misdiagnosed as psychiatric illnesses like schizophrenia.9 However, the presence of seizures, catatonia, autonomic instability, and hyperkinesia should raise suspicion for an autoimmune encephalitis3,10 as highlighted in Table 2.

Electrographic abnormalities are another key feature. A unique EEG finding in NMDA encephalitis is Delta Brush Variance (DBV), characterized by generalized delta activity with superimposed bursts of beta frequency.11,12 While highly associated with the condition, its absence does not rule out the diagnosis.13

Standard treatment for NMDA-receptor (NMDAR) encephalitis involves resecting any underlying tumor, if present. Adjunctive therapies include intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) or plasmapheresis to remove antibodies and immunomodulatory agents like rituximab to target autoreactive B-cells. Recovery is often prolonged, spanning several months, with clinical symptoms fluctuating.1 Early tumor removal and immunotherapy are associated with fewer neurological relapses. 14 Cognitive and psychological deficits, such as memory and attention issues, can persist for months to years, necessitating long-term follow-up with psychiatry and neurology.14

The Anti-NMDAR Encephalitis One-Year Functional Status (NEOS) score can help predict a patient's one-year functional outcome.15 The score considers five variables: need for ICU admission, lack of treatment within four weeks of symptom onset, no clinical improvement after four weeks of treatment, an abnormal MRI, and CSF WBC count >20 cells/μL. A lower score indicates a better prognosis. Our patient would have scored 3 (ICU admission, delayed treatment, and elevated CSF WBC), which corresponds to a 70–80% chance of a good functional outcome. Had he been diagnosed sooner, his score would likely have been 2, increasing his chance of a good outcome to over 90%. This underscores that timely intervention is a crucial prognostic factor.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of considering rare CNS autoimmune conditions in patients with worsening encephalopathy, even when they do not present with stereotypical features. Our patient, a male without a tumor, confirms that NMDA receptor autoantibody encephalitis should be in the differential for all encephalopathic patients. Misdiagnosis and delayed treatment can lead to poor clinical outcomes, emphasizing the need for early consideration of this condition.
