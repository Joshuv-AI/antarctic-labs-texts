Exploring the lived experiences of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus-related complications in a healthcare setting in Singapore: a phenomenological study
Authors: P V AshaRani, Kumarasan Roystonn, Madhumitha Ramu, Yeow Wee Brian Tan, Fiona Devi, Muhammad Iskandar Shah, Peizhi Wang, Sum Chee Fang, Subramaniam Tavintharan, Tan Hwee Huan, Lee Eng Sing, Chong Siow Ann, Mythily Subramaniam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe increasing prevalence of diabetes and its associated complications presents a significant public health challenge, particularly in ageing populations. This qualitative study conducted in a healthcare setting in Singapore explored the lived experiences of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) progression and complications, aiming to uncover challenges and enablers of effective self-care and complication management, thereby informing patient-centred policy and healthcare improvements.MethodsA phenomenological approach was employed, involving one-to-one in-depth interviews with 13 participants with T2DM with complications. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling from the outpatient setting of a community-level diabetes care centre. Data were analysed through a reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsSeven themes emerged from the analysis: (1) pathways to initial diagnosis of diabetes, (2) diagnosis of diabetes-related complications (with subthemes of diagnosis in emergency and non-emergency settings), (3) barriers to help-seeking for complications, (4) barriers to diabetes complications management, (5) enablers of complications management, (6) experience of living with diabetes complications and (7) suggestions and recommendations for better care. Subthemes for barriers and enablers encompassed personal, healthcare-related and social/community factors. Findings revealed significant gaps in symptom recognition, treatment adherence and glycaemic control among participants. Despite these challenges, participants demonstrated considerable resilience and adaptability.ConclusionThis study identified complex trajectories in T2DM progression, from the initial diagnosis to complication management, identifying key challenges and enablers. The findings highlighted the necessity for enhanced public education, tailored interventions and holistic care approaches. These insights have the potential to significantly improve the quality of life of individuals living with T2DM complications and alleviate the burden on healthcare systems, providing a foundation for future patient-centred diabetes management strategies.Keywords: Qualitative Research, Community Health Planning, Education

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is one of the most rapidly evolving public health challenges of the 21st century. Recent data indicate that 537 million adults (aged 20–79 years) were living with DM in 2021, a figure projected to increase to 783 million by 2045.1 The economic burden is evident from the 316% increase in healthcare expenditure over the past 15 years, with estimates predicting a further rise to $1 trillion by 2030. Type 2 DM (T2DM) accounts for >90% of all diabetes cases, with cardiovascular complications being the primary cause of mortality within this population.2

DM related complications (henceforth referred to as DM complications) can surface even before the disease is diagnosed. One in two individuals with T2DM remain undiagnosed, often presenting with DM complications at the time of initial diagnosis.1 3 An et al4 conducted a retrospective cohort study among patients with T2DM to investigate the prevalence and incidence of DM complications. Their findings revealed that at the time of diagnosis, 12.3% of patients had chronic kidney disease, 3.3% had cardiovascular disease, 0.3% had retinopathy, 3.8% had neuropathy and 0.1% had undergone lower extremity amputations. The highest incidence was observed for neuropathy (26.9%). The high prevalence of microvascular and macrovascular complications was attributed to hyperglycaemia and damage caused by comorbidities.4 The majority of patients exhibited complications within 3–5 years of diagnosis, highlighting the importance of early detection and management.5

In Singapore, the prevalence of DM among those aged 18–74 years was 8.5% in 2021–2022.6 Among this group, only 38.7% had optimal glycaemic control.6 Tan et al7 examined the trends for T2DM complications from 2013 to 2020 and showed an increase in DM, neuropathy, nephropathy, retinopathy and cardiovascular complications until 2018, after which it plateaued. A recent nationwide survey showed that 31.6% of those with T2DM had complications, with nephropathy, neuropathy and retinopathy being the top three.8 Understanding the lived experiences of patients managing these complications is crucial for developing targeted interventions and improving healthcare delivery to prevent further complications and enhance quality of life.

Singapore has an extensive and efficient healthcare system that includes a network of primary care providers (general practitioners (GP) clinics and polyclinics), public hospitals (acute, psychiatric, community and speciality centres) and long-term care centres catering to its 5.7 million population.9 The accessibility of care is ensured by a network of GPs and polyclinics located island-wide, serving a diverse patient population for both acute and chronic disease management. Polyclinics are equipped with DM care facilities and provide referrals to public hospitals for patients requiring specialist care.10 Outpatient specialist care is provided in the community through integrated care facilities, where diabetologists, podiatrists, ophthalmologists, dietitians and nephrologists are housed under one DM care centre.11 These centres aim to provide coordinated care for patients with varying needs. Additionally, both public and private hospitals offer outpatient and inpatient care, ensuring a seamless and holistic approach to treatment. The Ministry of Health Singapore initiated the ‘War on Diabetes’ campaign in 2016 to ensure early detection, efficient management and reduction of DM complications.12 However, a registry-based study on DM complications by Tan et al 7 showed a worrying upward trend in microvascular complications between 2013 and 2020 with a 15% annual increase in events, especially among young patients.

Given this context, a patient experience map is crucial at this stage to identify potential gaps in care delivery, understand the real-life challenges faced by patients living with T2DM and its complications and inform targeted improvements. This study aimed to understand the trajectory of DM and its progression to complications, challenges patients face and enablers of effective complication management and self-care, captured through the lived experiences of patients seeking treatment for DM complications. By exploring these factors through people with lived experiences, we can gain an in-depth understanding of the challenges and opportunities in T2DM management, ultimately informing more effective and patient-centred approaches to care in Singapore.

Methods

Design

This qualitative phenomenological approach is intended to gain insight into the experiences of those with T2DM with complications. The study was reported based on the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist13 (online supplemental table S1).

Participants

Participants were recruited from a community-based integrated care centre (ICC) that offered DM-related outpatient services under one roof. All participants had a diagnosis of T2DM with at least one of the microvascular or macrovascular complications as identified by the attending diabetologist/endocrinologist. Those who were 21 years and above, Singapore citizens or permanent residents, had the capacity to provide written informed consent and were fluent in either one of the local languages (English, Chinese, Malay or Tamil) were recruited. Those with other diagnostic types of DM, unwilling to be audiotaped or who did not have the mental capacity to provide consent were excluded. A maximum variation sampling was achieved by ensuring representation from different age groups (<60 and >60 years), ethnicities (Chinese, Malay and Indians), sex and socioeconomic status (as assessed by the attending medical social worker from the financial assistance scheme).

Procedure

All study-related procedures were approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board (DSRB Ref: 2022/00339), and written consent was obtained from all the participants. All study documents were translated into local languages (Mandarin, Malay and Tamil) by professional translation firms and subsequently cross-checked by bilingual study team members to ensure accuracy and cultural appropriateness. Participants were selected using purposive sampling. All participants who met the inclusion criteria were screened and referred by the attending clinician. None of the participants withdrew from the study. All participants filled up a short sociodemographic questionnaire that captured their age, sex, ethnicity, education, employment, income, housing type and type of complications. One-to-one in-depth interviews were conducted at a place or mode convenient for the participants (home, clinic rooms or online through videoconferencing). The interviews lasted for 1–1.5 hours. All the interviewers introduced themselves by name, organisation that they belong to and their role within the organisation, before the start of the consent taking. The purpose, duration and process were explained to the participant before the interview. Privacy and confidentiality were ensured throughout the study. No identifiers were collected in the audio recordings, and participants were assigned pseudonyms. All study-related data were stored securely – hard copies under lock and key and electronic files in password-protected files in shared network drives with restricted access. All the interviews were audio-recorded with the written consent of the participants.

Characteristics of the study team and reflexivity

The study team comprised diabetologists and researchers with mental health and biomedical science backgrounds who were involved in the development of the interview guide. None of the attending clinicians was involved in the interview process. All interviews were conducted by AR, MY, WP, KR, FD, IS, BT and MR. Further details and credentials of the interviewers are indicated in online supplemental table S2. None of the study team members had T2DM; however, they had family members with T2DM with complications and were knowledgeable about these experiences. Regardless, steps were taken to prevent introduction of bias through personal experiences. A pre-recruitment training session for all interviewers emphasised the need for bracketing and to remain empathetic but neutral during the entire process. Each interviewer was accompanied by a note taker who monitored the interview process to ensure that no bias was introduced during the process. In addition, the participants included those from low socioeconomic status (SES) who could be sensitive to the interview questions. The study team has years of experience conducting qualitative research in diverse populations, including vulnerable people, which has equipped them with a non-judgmental attitude. Before each interview, the interviewers emphasised that there are no right or wrong answers to any of the questions and that the study team was trying to learn more about DM care from their experience as they had navigated through the process. These steps created a comfortable environment for the participants and built a good rapport. Reflective notes were taken by the interviewers and note takers, which were discussed during the debriefing sessions conducted after every interview for the initial three and thereafter at the 5th, 7th, 10th and 12th interviews. Methodological shortcomings, potential biases, information collected, potential revisions to the guide and follow-up actions were discussed during each meeting.

Additionally, none of the interviewers were employees of the DM clinic where recruitment took place; rather, they were healthcare staff who had direct experience of both positive and negative aspects of the system, which could have influenced their beliefs. Regular debriefing sessions included discussions to ensure that these potential biases did not interfere with data collection and analysis. The study team remained open to any new findings that challenged their preconceptions, and all possible explanations were explored for these findings. The detailed interview guide is given in online supplemental table S3.

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed verbatim and were checked for accuracy. Interviews in other languages were translated to English by a professional translation firm, which was cross-checked by a native speaker. A reflexive thematic analysis was employed.14 Transcripts were distributed among the interviewers who familiarised themselves with the contents and organised it into preliminary codes. The team discussed these codes and combined similar codes through regular discussions (inductive coding). The remaining transcripts were distributed as interviews progressed while updating the coding tree and tracking data saturation. Codes were refined or removed and were further categorised into themes through discussions. The coding framework was initially tested on two transcripts, and following refinements, the final framework was reviewed and approved by the research team. Code saturation was achieved after interviewing 11 participants, with no new codes/themes emerging from the data. Meaning saturation (depth of the data, adequate understanding of the relationship between codes) was attained at 13th interviews.15 A codebook was created with definitions of each code, inclusion, exclusion criteria and examples of codes. AR, KR and MR achieved an inter-rater agreement of 0.7 and coded all the transcripts. A Nvivo V.14 (QSR International) was employed for the analysis.

Pathways to the initial diagnosis of DM

More than half of the participants experienced symptoms characteristic of DM, including excessive thirst, fatigue, frequent urination and lack of concentration, which prompted them to seek medical attention for further investigation. Some participants noted atypical symptoms, such as itching and burning sensations. A lack of DM awareness was evident across this symptomatic group, irrespective of the socio-economic status. Nevertheless, these individuals recognised their symptoms as conditions warranting medical attention. The remaining participants were asymptomatic and received their diagnosis during routine clinical care for other conditions or emergency care for unrelated health issues.

Diagnosis of DM complications

Diagnosis in the emergency setting

Three-quarters of the participants reported emergency admissions for DM complications, such as stroke- or heart-related conditions. Participants described struggling with glycaemic control throughout their DM journey, from the initial diagnosis until they developed complications. This was particularly evident in the narratives of participants from low SES categories. Participants typically disregarded initial symptoms, attributing them to common ailments, until family members or colleagues intervened, or when symptoms became intolerable, necessitating medical attention. Those requiring emergency admission frequently reported a history of treatment non-adherence or disengagement from DM care.

Diagnosis in a non-emergency setting

Complications were diagnosed in non-emergency settings through routine care and specialist referrals. Participants reported adhering to regular screening appointments; however, their narratives revealed a pattern of overlooking early warning signs until symptoms became unbearable. For participants not under the care of ICC, referral to specialists was initiated either by primary care providers or through patient-initiated consultations.

Participants with diabetic foot ulcers often misinterpreted them as normal sores and frequently delayed seeking treatment until infection developed. This reflects a lack of understanding regarding the accelerated progression of such lesions in patients with DM. Paradoxically, despite awareness of the amputation risk associated with untreated minor wounds, participants failed to initiate timely interventions. Episodes of hyperglycaemia were consistently reported across all participant narratives.

Barriers to help-seeking for DM complications

Analysis of participant narratives revealed multiple personal barriers to timely treatment-seeking for DM complications when they were referred to specialist care by primary care due to suspected complications or when they experienced symptoms of complications. Financial constraints were prominent, with many participants reporting an inability to afford hospital expenses despite symptom recognition. Treatment discontinuation was common because of anticipated financial burden, exacerbated by unemployment and overall household economic instability, especially among those with low SES, which was attributed to inadequate family support and competing family obligations. Busy lifestyles and limited symptom knowledge were frequently cited as contributing to treatment delays. Many underestimated the severity of their condition because of an insufficient understanding of untreated complications. Additional barriers included distrust in healthcare providers, preference for alternative therapies like Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), competing priorities and self-reported procrastination. These narratives illuminate the complex interplay of personal, social and economic factors influencing help-seeking behaviours.

Barriers to DM complications management

Personal barriers

After initiating treatment for complications, participants’ narratives revealed multiple interconnected barriers to effective management. Singapore’s diverse food culture emerged as a significant challenge to dietary adherence. While participants possessed knowledge of necessary lifestyle modifications, many struggled with implementation, citing a lack of self-discipline and yielding to food temptations. Those with suboptimal glycaemic control often continued consuming high-sugar foods, attributing this to perceived physical weakness when following dietary restrictions. Paradoxically, stress from high blood glucose readings discouraged regular monitoring.

Physical limitations, particularly among stroke survivors, significantly affected self-care abilities and increased dependence on others for medication management and daily activities. While the diagnosis of complications initially motivated improved self-discipline, participants struggled to sustain these changes. Barriers to medication adherence included fear of injection pain and perceived side effects, whereas transient effects from diagnostic procedures deterred regular follow-ups.

Age-specific challenges emerged prominently. Older participants questioned the value of late-life lifestyle changes, prioritising quality of life over health benefits. They particularly struggled with daily glucose monitoring and complex medication regimens. Language barriers further complicated healthcare interactions and complications management. Many participants reported using alternative approaches, including TCM and herbal remedies.

Financial constraints and time limitations posed significant challenges, especially for working participants who often prioritised family needs over self-care. The absence of supportive social networks emerged as a key barrier, with participants expressing a desire for platforms to share experiences. Misconceptions about DM management, influenced by religious teachings and social media, further complicate adherence to conventional treatments. These narratives highlight how personal, social, cultural and practical barriers intersect to influence management of complications, particularly among older adults and those with limited support systems.

Healthcare and treatment barriers

Participant narratives revealed significant healthcare system barriers to DM management. A key concern was the complex referral process for subsidised specialist care, particularly among general practice patients who needed to navigate multiple providers through polyclinics. Financial constraints emerged prominently in the context of specialist fees and diabetes supplies.

Systemic challenges included prolonged waiting times, inflexible appointment scheduling and lack of personalised care in primary care. The inpatient experience was often described negatively, with participants citing strictly worded medical advice, limited ward interactions and restricted dietary options. Healthcare team interactions were characterised as rushed, with language barriers particularly affecting those from lower socio-economic backgrounds. Participants emphasised the need for more empathetic, culturally sensitive care that acknowledges individual circumstances rather than standardised advice.

Social and community barriers

Analysis revealed how familial, cultural and environmental factors influenced DM management. Family dynamics emerged as a significant theme, with participants describing challenges such as relatives disregarding their dietary restrictions and expressing frustration over recurring medical appointments and financial strain. Those with mobility issues noted increased dependence on family for accessing care, whereas many desired more open communications about their health status, noting that family members often concealed complication severity or imposed overly strict dietary controls.

Social and cultural influences, particularly during gatherings and festive periods, challenged dietary adherence. Participants highlighted the food industry’s influence, expressing concerns about misleading ‘zero sugar’ advertisements and difficulty identifying healthy options in Singapore’s diverse food landscape. While fitness facilities were accessible, participants, especially older adults, found them unsuitable because of overcrowding and youth-oriented environments.

Enablers to DM complications management

Personal enablers

Acceptance and self-determination emerged as key enablers in DM management. Participants who embraced their condition described conscious lifestyle modifications, including regular glucose monitoring, dietary control, increased physical activity and strict self-care routines. Fear of complications, informed by healthcare providers, personal experiences or social networks, served as a significant motivator.

For many, complication onset acted as a ‘wake-up call’, prompting efforts to personalise lifestyle modifications and proactive management. Those who maintained strict regimens cited visible improvements in body shape and encouraging test results as ongoing motivators. Participants emphasised personal responsibility over sole reliance on clinical teams, demonstrating self-reliance in medication, diet, exercise and transportation management. Their narratives reflected a positive outlook that DM, while challenging, could be managed effectively to achieve stable health and potentially reduced medication needs.

Healthcare enablers

Long-term relationships with ICC providers emerged as a crucial enabler, fostering trust and productive DM management discussions. Participants valued the healthcare team’s emotional support, cross-specialty collaboration and comprehensive approach to complications management. They particularly appreciated clinicians’ experience, empathy and practical support, such as providing printed medication lists for complex prescriptions.

The ICC system’s structural advantages are featured prominently in narratives − accessible community locations, multiple specialties under one roof and efficient service delivery, including joint consultations and automated appointment reminders. Participants perceived ICCs as more efficiently managed than other healthcare facilities, highlighting their holistic, integrated care approach and enhanced patient experience. Community outreach programmes from public hospitals were noted as beneficial additions to care.

Social and environmental support

Friends and family emerged as crucial enablers in managing DM complications. Family members, acting as primary caregivers, provided support through financial assistance, appointment and medication management, glucose monitoring, meal preparation and accompanied them to medical consultations. Friends contributed both emotional and practical support, participating in fitness activities and modifying social environments to accommodate dietary restrictions.

Domestic help further enhanced DM management by supporting lifestyle routines and medication adherence. This network of social and environmental support complements healthcare systems, highlighting the importance of holistic DM management that extends beyond clinical care.

Experiences of living with DM complications

Participants demonstrated significant adaptability in managing DM complications, developing strategies for blood glucose control, symptom recognition and healthcare system navigation. Their daily lives reflected a complex balance of health management activities, including multiple specialist appointments, dietary control and various self-care practices.

ICCs emerged as vital support structures, streamlining healthcare experiences and reducing logistical challenges. Participants showed resilience in balancing work commitments with health needs, often supported by family members who helped overcome self-care barriers and communication challenges.

Many participants took proactive approaches to health management, exploring various strategies, including traditional medicines and supplements, informed by social networks and media platforms. Some embraced these alternatives, whereas others preferred strictly following clinical advice. This diversity in management approaches highlighted the personalised nature of living with DM complications and the crucial role of both familial and institutional support systems.

Post-complications, management experiences were shaped by interconnected personal, healthcare and socio-environmental factors. Personal enablers included condition acceptance, fear of progression and self-motivation, whereas barriers encompassed problematic alternative therapy use, communication difficulties (especially among lower socio-economic groups) and challenges with medical information comprehension. Within healthcare contexts, strong therapeutic relationships and personalised care enhanced management, whereas complex referral processes, long waiting times and language barriers with non-native providers created obstacles.

The socio-environmental context significantly influenced management. Support from family, friends and domestic helpers facilitated care, though cultural practices and social obligations often challenged lifestyle modifications. Despite these challenges, participants demonstrated adaptation over time, developing stronger healthcare relationships and maintaining optimistic perspectives toward ongoing management. Suggestions and recommendations for better care

Participants’ recommendations emphasised several key areas for improvement in DM care. They advocated for a holistic approach integrating physical and mental health management, noting the psychological stress of managing multiple conditions and complex treatment regimens. Mental health support and the potential role of spiritual practices were highlighted as crucial components of routine DM care.

Primary care recommendations included providing GPs access to national medical databases and implementing regular DM education sessions, particularly for older adults. Nutritional interventions were suggested, such as extending labelling to hawker foods and implementing warning labels on sugary products. Financial recommendations focused on increased subsidies for treatment and making healthy food options more affordable.

System-level suggestions included expanding ICC facilities, addressing staff shortages and improving healthcare coordination through better appointment scheduling and longer consultation times. Participants called for 24-hour DM emergency clinics in housing estates and more personalised care approaches. Special consideration for older adults’ needs was emphasised, particularly regarding digital platform usage and additional support requirements.

Discussion

This experience map of patients with T2DM complications revealed several key insights. Participants struggled to identify initial DM symptoms despite recognising the need for medical attention. Their glycaemic control was challenged by multiple factors: personal barriers (lack of self-discipline and awareness), inadequate social support, limited social connections and disabilities. Healthcare system barriers included crowded clinics, long waiting times and lack of personalised care. Complications were diagnosed either through emergency care or routine visits, with participants showing limited understanding of complication symptoms. Several enablers emerged: personal factors (condition acceptance and self-motivation), social and environmental support and healthcare system elements. Despite these challenges, participants demonstrated considerable adaptability in managing their daily lives.

Participants demonstrated significant challenges in recognising DM symptoms at diagnosis. A Singapore survey reported that 82.7% of residents could identify DM symptoms,16 whereas our findings suggest poorer symptom recognition when experiencing them personally. This aligns with Ramachandran’s17 observation that DM symptoms are often overlooked because of their late manifestation, with damage potentially occurring before symptoms appear.

Hyperglycaemia significantly influences complication development through cellular pathway activation, leading to endothelial dysfunctions and subsequent microvascular and macrovascular complications.18 19 A study indicated that 35% of patients with T2DM present with complications at diagnosis.20 Our findings align with the results from Malaysia, showing that lifestyle maintenance challenges, psychological and social barriers, treatment difficulties and insufficient self-care understanding contribute to complications.21 Acute hyperglycaemia, including intra-day fluctuations, increases complication risk.22 Our participants’ experiences mirror international findings,23 24 with emergency presentations typically involving severe macrovascular complications (stroke, heart attack and infected ulcers), whereas microvascular complications were usually identified during routine care. These findings underscore the importance of early intervention, consistent glucose management and regular complication screening.

Treatment delays for DM complications stemmed from multiple factors: cost concerns, poor symptom recognition and risk perception, treatment non-adherence, competing priorities and alternative therapy use. These findings align with the results of Ogulana et al25 in their study of treatment delays of diabetic foot ulcers. Despite healthcare education, participants struggled with information comprehension, possibly due to health literacy levels or communication barriers, and often not taking action until complications became severe.

A meta-synthesis by Blasco-Blasco et al26 supports our findings that the lack of symptoms and medication side effects contribute to treatment drop-out and subsequent complications. Our study participants suggested addressing these challenges through comprehensive education programmes tailored to different socio-demographic groups, incorporating alternative therapy discussions and delivering content in preferred languages. These recommendations emphasise the need for ongoing, personalised patient education to maintain treatment engagement and prevent complications.

This study identified multiple barriers to managing DM complications across personal, healthcare-related and social/community domains. Personal barriers included lack of self-discipline, dietary challenges, medication non-adherence and physical limitations, aligning with findings of Brundisini et al27 and Subramaniam et al.28 Bukhsh et al29 similarly noted barriers, including physical limitations and needle phobia, and Tuobenyiere et al30 highlighted communication challenges among those with lower educational attainment.

Healthcare barriers encompassed cost concerns, long waiting times30 31 and language barriers with foreign healthcare professionals.32 Social and community barriers included insufficient support networks, cultural factors and religious gatherings featuring unsuitable food choices.28 Financial constraints consistently affected self-care across studies.27 29 Sina et al33 observed that individuals with complications face increased physical, systemic and psychological barriers, which were reflected in our findings. Although participants expressed satisfaction with DM ICCs, they noted that community fitness centres were less engaging for older adults. Recommendations included age-targeted fitness programmes, round-the-clock community DM care, reduced waiting times to improve treatment satisfaction34 35 and increased DM care manpower.

This study identified several enablers for managing DM complications across personal, healthcare-related and social domains. Personal enablers included condition acceptance, conscious lifestyle changes, fear of complications and body image concerns, aligning with the findings of Adu et al36 on proactive approaches. Body image emerged as a significant motivator, corroborating research by Coffey et al37 and Putri et al,38 as complications affected clothing choices and self-esteem. Healthcare enablers encompassed clinician–patient rapport and collaborative care, supporting the emphasis of Young et al39 emphasis on encouragement, relationship building and clear communication. Peek et al40 suggested that patient education and physician interpersonal training could reduce care team mistrust.

Social support, particularly from family and friends, proved crucial for medication adherence and lifestyle management.29 31 32 41 However, Rosland et al42 noted that while 25% viewed family support as enabling, most considered it a barrier. Singapore’s unique enabler was domestic help availability, alongside community-level DM care centres providing accessible support. These findings suggest that effective DM complication management requires leveraging personal motivation, supportive healthcare systems and social networks.

This study revealed significant resilience and adaptability among individuals with DM complications. Participants did not report feeling burdened or depressed, contrasting with previous research findings. They expressed feeling supported through community-level DM care and long-term relationships with care teams, aligning with the observation of Stuckey et al43 of the positive experiences among patients. This positivity may be attributed to anti-DM campaign awareness,12 consistent family support and ongoing healthcare team assistance. These findings indicate that supportive healthcare systems and communities can positively influence the lived experiences of individuals managing DM complications.

The strengths of the study included the qualitative approach with reflexive thematic analysis involving multiple interviewers to minimise bias, the rigorous qualitative methodology employed through multiple triangulation approaches: investigator triangulation via ethnically and linguistically matched interviewer–participant pairs; methodological triangulation through diverse interview modes, reflective notes, field observations and regular debriefing sessions; and data triangulation through maximum variation sampling. Strong inter-rater reliability in the coding process, achieved through independent coding by multiple researchers, followed by thorough consensus discussions, further enhanced the analytical rigour. The rich, detailed data captured nuanced experiences across multiple care settings in our multi-ethnic urban population, enabling potential transferability to similar contexts. Trustworthiness was strengthened through systematic processes including collaborative development of the interview guide with diabetologists, care team members and researchers, standardised interviewer training, maintenance of a clear audit trail and regular team reflexivity. Study limitations include recruitment being confined to ICC and primary care facilities within a single healthcare cluster. Although primary care manages a substantial proportion of patients with T2DM, many are also seen in private practices, restructured hospitals and specialist clinics, whose patients may have different experiences. Additionally, as this study was conducted in an urban, high-income Asian setting, findings may not be transferable to low-income to middle-income settings. These considerations, along with potential interviewer knowledge gaps affecting data collection depth, should be taken into account when interpreting our results.

Conclusion

This qualitative study informs the lived experiences of individuals with T2DM complications in Singapore, revealing a complex journey from diagnosis to complication management. Our findings highlight significant gaps in initial symptom recognition and two distinct trajectories to complications: treatment drop-out and persistent hyperglycaemia despite routine treatment. Multiple barriers spanning personal, healthcare-related and social/community domains were identified. However, important enablers emerged, including personal motivation, strong clinician–patient relationships and social support networks. Notably, participants expressed satisfaction with ICC and demonstrated resilience and adaptability in managing their complications. These findings have significant implications for clinical practice and public health policy in Singapore, suggesting the need for enhanced DM education, improved treatment adherence strategies and holistic care approaches.
