Anal cancer screening and the role of clinicians providing gynecologic care: A survey of anal cancer screening knowledge and practice patterns at an urban tertiary medical center in the United States
Authors: Colin B. Russell, Nicholas S. Raja, Gabriela Ashenafi, Arshpreet Kaur Mallhi, Natalie A. Saunders, Elizabeth J. Campbell, Hope K. Haefner, Kathryn C. Welch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aims to assess the current knowledge of anal cancer, screening, and practice patterns among clinicians providing gynecologic care.MethodsIn January 2024, 207 clinicians providing care for adults at an urban tertiary medical center (the University of Michigan Hospitals, Ann Arbor, Michigan, United States) received a 14-question electronic survey regarding their knowledge of anal cancer, screening practices, and desire to learn more about anal cancer screening. Descriptive statistics were used to present findings.ResultsThe final analyses included 121 respondents. Over three-quarters of respondents (80.2 %) reported knowing anal cancer risk factors, while only 11.6 % reported knowing anal cancer incidence. The majority of respondents (66.1 %) reported that they ‘Never’ perform anal cancer screening tests in the setting of anal cancer risk factors, and almost half of respondents (43.0 %) felt like they could correctly perform an anal cancer screening test. Nearly all respondents (97.5 %) reported an interest in learning more about anal cancer screening methods.ConclusionClinicians providing gynecologic care can play a unique role in anal cancer screening. Given the limited studies on clinicians' knowledge of anal cancer screening, this study offers a unique perspective of gynecologic providers' knowledge and willingness to integrate anal cancer screening methods into their practice.

Introduction

Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is the most common sexually transmitted infection worldwide (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025). Oncogenic HPV subtypes have been associated with the development of malignancies, including oropharyngeal, penile, vulvar, vaginal, cervical, and anorectal cancers (De Martel et al., 2017). Anal cancer, while still relatively rare, has been steadily increasing in incidence and mortality since the early 1970s (Davis and Orangio, 2018; Stier et al., 2015). There is a higher incidence of anal cancer in women compared to men, and anal cancer disproportionately affects high-risk populations, such as women with a history of vulvar precancer or cancer, people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), men who have sex with men (MSM), and solid organ transplant recipients (Stier et al., 2015; Stier et al., 2024; Clifford et al., 2021; Kang et al., 2018). Nearly all anal cancers are caused by oncogenic HPV subtypes and there is significant concordance of oncogenic HPV subtypes between the cervix and anus (De Martel et al., 2017; Davis and Orangio, 2018; Stier et al., 2015; Rubinov et al., 2015). Anal squamous cell carcinomas, the most common type of anal cancer, are preceded by precancerous lesions, similar to the cervix, and are screened with similar methods such as Papanicolaou cytology (Pap) tests with or without HPV testing (Stewart et al., 2018; Barroso et al., 2022). There are established cervical cancer screening recommendation guidelines from the United States Preventive Services Task Force, the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology (ASCCP), the American Society for Clinical Pathology, the Society of Gynecologic Oncology, and the American Cancer Society (Wang et al., 2022). Despite slight variations among guidelines, these guidelines are widely dispersed and recognized. In fact, much of the current understanding and literature on development of anal dysplasia, HPV infection, and cancer has been extrapolated from cervical dysplasia research (Davis and Orangio, 2018; Natale et al., 2024).

In 2018, the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons published clinical practice guidelines for anal squamous cell cancer (Stewart et al., 2018). These guidelines recommend that patients at increased risk for anal neoplasms should be identified by history, physical exam, and laboratory testing, and screening should be a shared decision with the patient. In a recent groundbreaking trial, treatment of high-grade anal precancerous lesions was found to significantly reduce the overall anal cancer risk in people living with HIV (Palefsky et al., 2022). This study led to the development of the first anal cancer screening guidelines, the International Anal Neoplasia Society (IANS) Consensus 2024 Guidelines (Stier et al., 2024). IANS calls for primary care providers (PCP), gynecologists, and any other specialty that care for at-risk populations to provide initial anal cancer screening (Stier et al., 2024).

There are limited studies on clinicians' knowledge surrounding anal cancer screening. A single-center survey study found that physicians from gastroenterology, general surgery, colorectal surgery, internal medicine, and hematology departments generally could correctly identify basic characteristics of anal cancer and precancer, however their general knowledge about screening was deficient (Rubinov et al., 2015). Another interview survey of HIV clinicians, including PCPs, colorectal surgeons, dermatologists, and nurse practitioners, demonstrated high screening rates; however, follow-up practices varied, and a proportion of clinicians expressed discomfort with performing anal cytology testing (Koskan et al., 2019). Another single-center survey study of PCPs at a large urban health center reported limited time, limited staffing, and patient-factors (medical literacy, mental illness, substance use, poverty) as barriers to screening (Apaydin et al., 2018). Similarly, there are limited data on knowledge of anal cancer screening among clinicians who perform gynecologic care, hereafter referred to as gynecologic clinicians. One qualitative study that interviewed HIV clinicians, including gynecologists, identified a lack of knowledge of screening guidelines and unclear clinician responsibility as barriers to anal cancer screening (Higashi et al., 2022). Importantly, all of these prior surveys were prior to the IANS 2024 Consensus Guidelines.

Given the IANS recommendation for clinicians providing gynecologic care to offer anal cancer screening, this study aims to assess the current knowledge of anal cancer, screening, and practice patterns among gynecologic clinicians, including physicians, certified nurse midwives (CNMs), and nurse practitioners, at a single large tertiary medical center in the United States as part of a qualitative improvement initiative.

Methods

Study population and setting

We surveyed adult gynecologic clinicians involved in adult women's health care in the University of Michigan department of Obstetrics and Gynecology (OBGYN) and Family Medicine, including attending faculty physicians, nurse practitioners, CNMs, and resident physician trainees. A total of 207 clinicians received an anonymous 14-question electronic survey in early January 2024 via department email listservs at a large urban tertiary medical center in the Midwest, United States. This medical center is an over 900-bed academic teaching hospital in an urban setting, comprised of a children's hospital, women's hospital, adult hospital, comprehensive cancer center, dedicated cardiovascular center, and outpatient facilities. Of the 207 clinicians that were initially contacted, 181 responded and 178 agreed to participate, leading to a response rate of 86 %. Of the 178 respondents, 139 reported offering cervical Pap testing. Eighteen responses were excluded due to missing information. An a priori decision was made to exclude those who do not offer cervical Pap testing so as to focus on clinicians providing routine gynecologic care. A total of 121 responses were included in the final analyses (Online Supplementary Fig. S1).

Survey

A survey was designed to categorize respondent knowledge of anal cancer, including screening recommendations and current anal cancer screening practices (i.e. anal cytology testing with or without HPV testing). Specific questions included knowledge about anal cancer incidence, anal cancer risk factors, and interest in learning about anal cancer screening, and institutional referral pathways were elicited with binary responses (Yes/No). Practice patterns were elicited with questions about routine cervical cancer screening, routine anal cancer screening, confidence in correctly performing anal cancer screening, and interest in incorporating anal cancer screening into current practice with Likert-style responses. The Likert scale responses were coded between 1 and 5 (i.e., 1-least favorable response, 5-most favorable response). Demographic information, including respondent gender identity, clinician type (OBGYN faculty, OBGYN specialist, Family Medicine faculty, CNMs, Nurse Practitioner, physician trainee), and duration of practice, were also collected. All answers were stored in aggregate using Qualtrics (Qualtrics, Provo, UT).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed and presented in frequency and percentage. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS for Windows V9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Human subjects research

This study received Exempt status from the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board (IRB#: HUM00225514).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this represents the largest survey to date of clinicians providing gynecologic care with respect to anal cancer screening knowledge and practices. An additional strength is the robust and diverse clinician population, including faculty from multiple specialties, OBGYN generalists and subspecialists, Family Medicine physicians, trainees, nurse practitioners, and CNMs. We identified a high self-reported knowledge about anal cancer risk factors though a low knowledge about anal cancer incidence among respondents. Most respondents did not routinely perform anal cancer screening tests (i.e. anal cytology with or without HPV testing) and less than half believed that they could adequately perform anal cytology with or without HPV testing. Most respondents (78 %) were unaware of the current referral pathway for positive anal cancer screening tests at the institution, which highlights opportunities for further quality improvement projects and improved distribution of clinical algorithms. Nevertheless, there was a high level of interest in learning more about anal cancer screening methods and most respondents would incorporate anal cancer screening into their practice if training session were offered.

In their 2024 screening guidelines, IANS calls for gynecologists and any other specialty that care for at-risk populations to provide initial anal cancer screening (Stier et al., 2024). There is a dearth of literature describing clinicians' knowledge of anal cancer screening. A small single-center survey study from 2015 administered to physicians in gastroenterology, general and colorectal surgery, internal medicine, and hematology departments found similar results to our survey (Rubinov et al., 2015). Other qualitative studies have described limited knowledge of screening guidelines and unclear clinician responsibility as barriers to anal cancer screening (Koskan et al., 2019; Higashi et al., 2022). ‘Clinician roles and responsibility’ was particularly highlighted between the interviews of gynecologists and HIV clinicians, with each specialist citing each other as the responsible party for anal cancer cytology screening (Higashi et al., 2022).

Previous studies and the current study emphasize the importance of targeting clinician education and information dissemination of current guidelines, especially trainees and clinicians in their early career. At the initial time of this study, there were no recommendations for anal cancer screening from any gynecologic society. Since the publication of the IANS screening guidelines in January 2024, the ASCCP has since published a Practice Advisory statement encouraging physicians caring for at risk patient populations to offer annual digital anorectal screening exam, and anal cytology testing and/or high-risk HPV testing if follow-up testing is available (American Society of Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology, 2025).Anal cancer screening recommendation guidelines for New York-based clinicians from New York State Department of Health have been in place since 2007, primarily focused on people living with HIV, and have since expanded to include MSM, cis- and trans-gender women (Hirsch et al., 2022). These screening guidelines propose that gynecologists and advanced practice providers who have experience performing cervical colposcopy can learn anal cancer screening techniques for anorectal colposcopy, given the similarity between the procedures (Hirsch et al., 2022). It is thus important to explore current awareness and screening competency among gynecologists as it may be easier to train them for anal cancer screening than clinicians in other specialties.

With the findings from the ANCHOR study (Palefsky et al., 2022) and the recent anal cancer screening guidelines from IANS (Stier et al., 2024), it is acknowledged that anal cancer screening is in its infancy and there is a need for longitudinal data on screening recommendation impact. As some studies have noted, there are a lack of data regarding cost-benefit ratio to screening, though screening is generally accepted with low-cost techniques targeting high-risk populations (Natale et al., 2024). Screening clinicians should have access to high-resolution anoscopy (HRA) clinics or a clear referral pipeline to ensure appropriate follow-up of abnormal results for their patients (Stier et al., 2024; American Society of Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology, 2025). Other studies note the significant mismatch in the need for HRA services and coming demand with the IANS screening recommendations (American Society of Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology, 2025; Rim et al., 2024; Barnell and Schechter, 2024). Results from our study are promising for the role of the gynecologic clinician in initial screening for anal cancer, as many respondents indicated they were interested in learning more and willing to incorporate screening into their practice.

A limitation of the study is that the questionnaire was administered to clinicians at a single center, thereby constraining generalizability. Furthermore, as questions were created specifically to assess anal cancer screening knowledge among clinicians at this single institution, a validated questionnaire assessing knowledge was not used. Additionally, the bias due to convenience sampling cannot be ignored, as it may have affected the result estimates. Finally, given the timing of the questionnaire, most respondents were probably unaware of the IANS guidelines released in January 2024, which may have affected their depth of knowledge. This study additionally may have been strengthened by an analysis of differences in knowledge and interest in further training among clinician subpopulations, though this was out of the scope of the original study.

Conclusions

The gynecologic clinician can play a unique role in anal cancer screening, given the similarities and overlapping demographics for cervical dysplasia and cancer of the lower genital tract. Future studies may include clinicians of other specialties from multiple medical centers to increase the generalizability of the results. Additionally, future pre-post studies could evaluate anal cancer screening rates and general knowledge of anal cancer screening before and after quality improvement educational interventions and/or anal cancer cytology training sessions. It is essential to recognize that system-level factors, including referral pathways, available HRA services, and trained medical assistants, are critical beyond clinician knowledge for the adoption of new screening processes. Future quality improvement projects may also target these system-level factors.

The incidence and mortality rates of anal cancer have been increasing in recent years. Our hope is that with the adoption of standardized anal screening guidelines and training provisions for clinicians, anal cancer will mirror the history of cervical cancer and decline following widespread screening.
