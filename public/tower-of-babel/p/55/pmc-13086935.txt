Liquid metal universal grippers for gentle, adaptable, multiscale manipulation
Authors: Xuanhan Chen, Mingkui Zhang, Lu Cao, Dinggang Fan, Long Wang, Yue Chen, Hao Lin, Mingyuan Sun, Shiyuan Tong, Yuguo Deng, Bing Xiao, Weihua Li, Bing Hu, Shiwu Zhang, Shi-Yang Tang, Lining Sun, Xiangpeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The amoeba can flow like liquid to change its morphology to effectively capture and excrete various prey. Inspired by the amoeba, we present a liquid metal universal gripper capable of effective grasping and active releasing of targets with various shapes, sizes, and stiffnesses in liquid and air. We unveil a surface tension induced active release mechanism enabling tunable active release of micro-objects. The gripper operates across 14 orders of magnitude in weight (from 10−12 g to 200 g) and achieves a low gripping contact pressure of ~10 Pa for handling delicate items. It can capture and release moving objects within milliseconds without precise alignment. An environment-agnostic surface activity design extends its functionality to a non-electrolyte environment. The gripper offers notable performance metrics over existing robotic grippers in multiscale operation, low contact pressure, and tunable releasing speed, representing a notable solution for living organisms and microscale objects.

Introduction

In the long-life river, living beings evolved an extensive assortment of manipulative organs and behaviors for survival in the competitive natural world. These efficient manipulative organs and behaviors have inspired the creation of versatile robotic grippers. Human-like multi-fingered manipulator works well for rigid and regular-shaped objects but has limitations with fragile, irregular-shaped, small-scale, or high dynamic items1–3. Caging grippers, enclosing objects in containers by pneumatically-actuated soft materials or kirigami structures, can reduce contact pressure and achieve high payload capability4–8. But these methods cannot tightly fix targets and may suffer low manipulation accuracy. Universal grippers utilizing interlocking mechanisms by shape memory polymers, jamming granular materials and phase-changing materials, have demonstrated versatility in grasping objects of various shapes. However, they usually require auxiliary systems (vacuum or thermal devices) or external force assistance, facing limitations when handling dynamic or delicate objects9–13. Grippers that employ adhesion forces are commonly used for microscale or lightweighted objects manipulation. But they encounter significant challenges in releasing14,15. Although some robotic grippers outperform humans in specific tasks, current manipulation technologies continue to face significant challenges. These arise from the vast diversity of target objects in terms of shape, stiffness (ranging from soft to rigid), size (from microscale to macroscale), and operational environments-particularly when dealing with microscale and lightweighted objects or dynamic targets such as living organisms.

The single-celled eukaryote amoeba has a fluid-like body with a very low stiffness of 0.1 kPa16. It can flow like liquids to adaptively change its morphology to capture and excrete various prey17–19, as shown in Supplementary Video 1. Gallium based liquid metals (LM), such as EGaIn and Galinstan, retains liquid phase at room temperature with a high surface tension of up to 624 mN m−1 ( ~ 9 times that of water)20,21. Like an amoeba, the LM droplet demonstrates reconfigurable morphological transitions via regulating its surface tension by electric oxidation and reduction21,22, as shown in Fig. 1a. The FLM is produced by mixing EGaIn with iron microparticles (see Supplementary Fig. 2 and “Production of functional liquid metal” section in Methods), resulting in improving morphological stability after electrochemical oxidation and the capability to modulate stiffness through the application of magnetic fields25. The LiMU gripper is constituted by fixing a gripper cavity on an electromagnetic coil, as illustrated in Fig. 1c. Results

The gripper cavity was fabricated by 3D printing flexible silicone (Fig. 1c). The soft tube on the left is connected to the interior gripper cavity to inject FLM. A hollow interlayer (thickness of 1 mm), termed electrolyte interlayer, is embedded into the cavity wall to store the electrolyte injected from the tube on the right (Fig. 2a). The essence of the LiMU gripper lies in the development of an environment-agnostic surface activity design. We used microchannels (diameter of 800 μm) to connect the electrolyte interlayer and the inner wall of the gripper cavity. We adhered a layer of porous gauze to the inner wall of the gripper cavity, which absorbs the electrolyte from the electrolyte interlayer, maintains a localized electrolyte environment for the FLM droplet and acts as a leakage barrier for the electrolyte. A copper wire is inserted into the left tube and immersed into the FLM. We used a copper foil (thickness of ~40 μm) to cover the bottom of the gripper cavity. The inner edge of the copper foil is embedded between the gauze and the inner wall of the gripper cavity to avoid direct contact with the FLM.Fig. 2Design of the liquid metal universal gripper (LiMU) gripper.a Cross-sectional scheme of the LiMU gripper. b Fabrication process of micropillars.

Inspired by the root reinforcement mechanism of plants37, we designed root-like micropillars (radius of 0.5 mm, height of 2 mm) on the inner wall of the gripper cavity (Fig. 2b). Copper powders are glued to the micropillars and processed with the copper-gallium alloy formation procedure38,39. Due to the firm metallic bond between the copper and gallium, the FLM droplet is firmly restricted in the gripper cavity. Single micropillar can provide 0.34 N equivalent adhesion. For the LiMU gripper equipped with six micropillars, there exists an upper limit on its diameter of ~40 mm, as detailed in ‘Gripper Size Analysis of LiMU Gripper’ in Methods.

The gripper cavity was fixed on the bottom of an electromagnetic coil, which can generate a high magnetic field strength of 4000 Gs. Both the viscosity and yield stress of FLM in the gripper cavity can be regulated under the magnetorheological effect induced by the magnetic field, thus enabling the LiMU gripper to grasp the wrapped payload against gravity25,40. The LiMU gripper can be mounted to a robotic arm by the flange on the top of the electromagnetic coil.

Similar to the capturing behavior of amoeba, the LiMU gripper can conformably wrap around target objects with complex shapes by reducing the high surface tension of FLM to near zero41. The injected FLM initially forms a droplet at the top of the mold chamber and cannot wrap the preset structure features (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Once applying a positive voltage of 5 V, FLM becomes oxidized (reflected by its tarnished color) and fills the whole mold chamber in ~3 s. The distinctive wrapping capability of the LiMU gripper is due to the FLM’s control over electrochemical interfacial tension and its ability to eliminate Marangoni instabilities23,24. When increasing the applied oxidative potential above the oxidation threshold, the surface tension of the FLM can be reduced to a moderate range under the action of electrocapillary, which is insufficient to trigger wrapping behavior. However, when the applied voltage exceeds the oxidation threshold, an oxide layer begins to form on the surface due to electrochemical reactions. In NaOH solution, the oxide dissolution competes with its electrochemical formation, dynamically maintaining the oxide layer at a specific thickness and reducing the surface tension to nearly zero. This allows the FLM to wrap around the target. It is also important to note that excessive high voltage eventually leads to the growth of a thicker oxide layer, which may halt the wrapping capability of the FLM. The wrapping of FLM in the gripper cavity is governed by the balance of the spreading forces (gravity and injection), viscous force and surface tension, as shown in Fig. 3b (see “Wrapping Mechanism by Electrochemistry” in Methods for details). We further evaluate the wrapping performance of a FLM droplet in the mold filled with NaOH solution of different concentrations, as well as in different electrolytes, including seawater (see Fig. 3c and “Wrapping performance of a FLM droplet” in Methods). The FLM functions effectively in various electrolytes, with the fastest wrapping speed observed in NaOH solution.

Figure 3d and Supplementary Video 4 illustrate the detailed wrapping process of the LiMU gripper to capture a glass ball (diameter of 6 mm) in NaOH solution (the gauze in the gripper cavity is removed in electrolyte for a clear observation, the same below). By applying a positive voltage of 5 V to lower the surface tension of FLM, the glass ball is completely wrapped within 3 s. The wrapping time of the LiMU gripper can be accelerated to ~1 s by increasing the applied voltages, electrolyte concentration, and Fe content within the FLM (see Fig. 3e and “Wrapping performance of the LiMU gripper” in Methods). The wrapping time increases with the target objects’ size, implying a maximum wrapping size limit exists (Fig. 3f).

To enable the operation of the LiMU gripper in the air, we use a layer of porous gauze to adhere to the inner wall of the gripper cavity, which absorbs the electrolyte and provides a local liquid environment for the upper part of the FLM droplet for electrochemical control (see Fig. 2b). When the upper part of the FLM droplet is oxidized, it lowers its interfacial tension and wraps around the object. Upon reduction, the Marangoni effect rapidly transports the electrolyte to the exposed surface of the FLM droplet, restoring the surface tension of the entire droplet to enable object release. Such an environment-agnostic surface activity design of FLM to enable its operation outside the liquid environment was demonstrated by the both of simulation and experiment (see Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Video 4, “Simulation for environment-agnostic surface activity design outside the liquid environment” and “Experiment for environment-agnostic surface activity design outside the liquid environment” in Methods).

To demonstrate the FLM’s ability to adapt to various morphologies automatically for universal gripping, we used the LiMU gripper to capture a rubber ring, a conch, and a stone with complex shapes, in both liquid and air (Fig. 3g). Compared to universal grippers based on shape memory polymers, phase-changing metals, and granular materials9–13, the LiMU gripper exhibits several advantages. First, its very low surface tension allows it to seamlessly conform to complex object features, outperforming membrane and polymer materials. Second, the wrapping process is spontaneous, requiring no auxiliary systems (vacuum pumps, thermal devices) and external force assistance, which minimizes stress on the object and exhibits strong adaptability to various targets and environments.

The electromagnetic coil fixed on the top of the gripper cavity can generate a magnetic field to induce the magnetorheological effect of the FLM25. This effect significantly increases the shear yield stress (i.e., stiffness) of FLM within milliseconds, allowing the gripper to securely grasp objects by interlocking with their structural features. The combination of excellent wrapping and stiffness tuning provides substantial gripping capability. Notably, capturing small fish underwater poses a challenge for many existing grippers due to the mucus layer on the fish. In contrast, our LiMU gripper successfully grasps a goldfish fry (body length: ~18 mm) underwater, as shown in Fig. 4a and Supplementary Video 5. The Fe content in FLM and applied magnetic field intensity are also critical for the payload capacity. Higher Fe content and magnetic field intensity increase the shear yield stress of FLM (Fig. 4c), which enhances the payload capacity. The payload reaches saturation ( ~ 200 gf) when the Fe content surpasses 40% and the magnetic field strength exceeds 4000 Gs. Figure 4d and Supplementary Fig. 5b demonstrate the LiMU gripper grasps heavy objects such as a pepper (165.2 g), a broccoli (157.8 g) and an orange (164.3 g) in air (also see Supplementary Video 6).

The payload capacity of the LiMU gripper is strongly dependent on its size, as shown in Fig. 4e. As the gripper size expands from 4 mm to 10 mm (gripper cavity diameter), the payload increases sharply from ~145 g to ~205 g, and eventually saturates at ~210 g when the gripper size exceeds 20 mm. This trend arises because a larger gripper thickens the wrapped FLM layer, enhancing the shear yield stress until it plateaus42,43, consistent with ‘Production of Functional Liquid Metal (FLM)’ and ‘Grasping Mechanism of the LiMU Gripper’ sections in Methods. Interestingly, from the perspective of payload-to-gripper mass ratio, smaller grippers demonstrate a clear advantage in grasping efficiency. For a 4 mm gripper, the payload-to-gripper mass ratio reaches as high as ~1290. As the gripper size increases to 12 mm, this ratio drops sharply to ~35, and when the gripper exceeds 20 mm, the ratio approaches zero. This indicates the potential advantage of the LiMU gripper in small-scale manipulations.

The LiMU gripper, enabled by variable stiffness FLM, demonstrates an ultra-gentle grasping capability for handling fragile objects. We developed a contact pressure measurement system to quantify the pressure exerted by the LiMU gripper (see Supplementary Fig. 5c and “Contact pressure measurement of the LiMU gripper” in Methods). The contact pressure is minimal ( ~ 10 Pa) when subjected to a magnetic field of ~500 Gs (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Video 7). As the magnetic field strength increases, the contact pressure gradually rises, reaching saturation at ~60 Pa when the field exceeds 2500 Gs. Figure 4g and Supplementary Video 8 demonstrate the LiMU gripper successfully transporting a delicate piece of tofu (yield stress: ~3920 Pa) in the air, with no damage occurring throughout the process. Figure 4h (and Supplementary Table 1) shows that the LiMU gripper applies notably low contact pressure, outperforming most reported grippers and remaining well below the yield stress of many soft-bodied organisms and fragile objects. This makes the LiMU gripper an ideal choice for handling delicate and sensitive targets, particularly living organisms.

The wrapping process of the LiMU gripper typically takes a few seconds (Fig. 3d), somewhat limiting its use for manipulating stationary objects. To address this, the LiMU gripper can be adapted for rapid capture of dynamic objects by immersing the target directly into oxidized FLM with low surface tension. The LiMU gripper demonstrates its swift grasping ability by successfully capturing a fast-moving cockroach, one of the fastest insects on land, as shown in Fig. 5a (see also Supplementary Video 9 and “Experiment setup for dynamic grasping” in Methods). The entire grasping process takes only ~200 ms, while the cockroach moves at a speed of ~30 mm/s. Unlike conventional contact grippers, the LiMU gripper does not require precise alignment to capture irregularly shaped, moving objects. It can swiftly capture and firmly grip an object by immersing only part of the target into the FLM. Figure 5b and Supplementary Video 10 demonstrate the grasping and transport of a wriggling earthworm. The earthworm is securely held within the gripper as it twists and is ultimately transferred to a grassy area unharmed.

Industrial robotic manipulators face significant challenges when accurately grasping parts from a disordered and overlapping pile, even with the aid of precision visual servoing44. The LiMU gripper demonstrates strong potential for addressing this bin-picking problem. As shown in Fig. 5c and Supplementary Video 11, a 6-DOF robotic arm equipped with a LiMU gripper as the end effector was employed to transfer the stones of varying shapes and sizes to four pre-programmed locations, operating independently of visual servoing. During grasping, the LiMU gripper was positioned randomly above the stones and then descent to a specific height to contact with the stones. In four attempts, the gripper successfully grasps and precisely places four parts into indentations, all without the need for visual assistance.

Releasing a macroscopic object is straightforward for most grippers due to gravity. However, releasing micro-scale objects through contact becomes challenging because of microscale effects, such as Van der Waals, capillary, electrostatic, and surface tension forces45,46. The standout feature of the LiMU gripper is its ability to restore the FLM’s high surface tension electrochemically, enabling the active release of wrapped objects by leveraging the significant contrast in surface energy between the FLM and the objects. This active release mechanism effectively overcomes challenges associated with microscale effects, making the gripper particularly efficient at releasing lightweight and microscale objects. Figure 6a and Supplementary Video 12 demonstrate the active release of a lightweight goose feather ( ~ 19 mg), which initially adhered to the FLM within the gripper and could not be released naturally. Upon reduction of the FLM, the goose feather is rapidly launched away, separating from the FLM droplet in under 60 ms. This active release behavior is controlled by the surface tension regulation of the FLM. The underlying mechanism is explained in detail using the model shown in Supplementary Fig. 6 (see “Surface tension induced active release mechanism” in Methods). In brief, upon reduction, the restored high surface tension of the deformed FLM propels the feather, similar to how a bow launches an arrow. The surface tension induced active release is especially pronounced when releasing smaller objects. We quantitatively explore this capability by actively releasing human hair (length ~10 mm, diameter ~70 μm, weight ~0.05 mg) in water using the LiMU gripper (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Video 13). At 5.5 ms, the magnetic field used for grasping is removed, and a reductive voltage of 15 V is applied simultaneously for active release. By 11.5 ms, the hair launches and completely detaches from the FLM surface. The hair undergoes a high acceleration, moving from rest to a velocity of 696 mm/s (~8700 body lengths/s) in just 6 ms (Fig. 6c), resulting in a peak acceleration of 411.84 m/s² ( ~ 42 G). This acceleration surpasses that of other reported grippers (Fig. 6d, Supplementary Table 2). Microscale objects, such as microchips (Mini-Led chip 0201, weight ~0.1 mg), microfibers (diameter of 20 μm, weight ~0.58 μg) and microspheres (diameter ~10 μm, weight ~1.21 pg) can also be captured by LiMU gripper and active released (see ‘Grasping microfibers and microspheres’ in Methods, the high-speed microscopic photography in Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Video 14).

The surface tension induces ultrafast active release by the LiMU gripper can be used to construct catapult actuators for high-performance micro jumping robots. It enables the rapid release of energy in a simpler manner, with higher power and faster response, surpassing the current catapult actuators such as light-driven hydrogel actuator, magnetic-driven bistable soft actuator, and combustion actuator47–49. It should also be noted that excessively high release acceleration may induce damage to sensitive objects to be manipulated or affect operational accuracy50,51. As seen in the “Surface Tension-Induced Active Release Mechanism” in Methods, the active release speed can be regulated by the applied reductive voltage. Figure S11 shows the control the active release acceleration and speed of a human hair by regulating the reductive voltage. As the reductive voltage increases from 4 to 15 V, the maximum acceleration and speed of the human hair during the active release process exhibit a linear correlation with the applied reductive voltage, increasing from 29.3 to 398.8 m/s² and 186 ( ~ 2325 body lengths/s) to 696 mm/s (~8700 body lengths/s), respectively. When the applied reductive voltage exceeds 15 V, the maximum acceleration and speed of the human hair show a downward trend due to the intense electrolysis effect. Nevertheless, in the extended applications of the LiMU gripper, an appropriate voltage for active release should be selected according to specific application scenarios to achieve a trade-off between operational efficiency and task requirements, such as safety and accuracy.

Discussion

In summary, inspired by the hunting behavior of amoebas, we present a liquid metal universal gripper that demonstrates effective grasping and active releasing capabilities. We unveil a surface tension induced active release mechanism that leverages the active control of liquid metal’s high surface tension, enabling the world’s fastest release of micro-objects, with accelerations reaching up to 42 G. This allows the LiMU gripper to handle multiscale objects ranging from pico to hundreds of grams. Even though the LiMU gripper has limitations in terms of payload capacity, it could be enhanced by integrating the phase transition mechanism of FLM into the design11,12. Notably, the LiMU gripper achieves the world’s lowest gripping contact pressure of ~10 Pa, making it ideal for handling delicate items such as mollusks. The LiMU gripper can capture and release moving objects within milliseconds without the need for precise alignment. The essence of the LiMU gripper lies in the development of an environment-agnostic surface activity design, which transcends the longstanding challenge that liquid metal electrochemistry operations confined inside electrolyte environments, enhancing the practicability of the LiMU gripper to non-electrolyte environment. Overall, the LiMU gripper offers notable performance metrics over existing robotic grippers in terms of adaptability, multiscale operation, low contact pressure, and fast manipulation speeds, representing a notable approach for solving various unresolved manipulation challenges, particularly for living organisms and microscale objects.

Wrapping performance of a FLM Droplet

We further evaluate the wrapping performance of a FLM droplet in the mold filled with different electrolytes including seawater and air. As shown in Fig. 3c, a FLM droplet was submerged in seawater, sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) solution (0.5 mol/L), or NaOH solution (0.5 mol/L). In addition, the wrapping performance was also examined in the air (a mold covered by a thin layer of 0.5 mm NaOH solution). In general, applying an appropriate oxidative potential is necessary for successful wrapping. When the applied voltage is too small (<1 V), no wrapping behavior was observed. Conversely, significant electrolysis of the solution can be observed when the applied voltage exceeds 5 V. The appropriate oxide voltage windows vary depending on the environment, with 1–5 V for NaOH solution and air, 2–5 V for seawater, and 1–3 V for Na2CO3 solution. In the operating windows, increasing voltage can accelerate the wrapping process in seawater, NaOH solution, and air. In the case of Na₂CO₃ solution, the wrapping time initially decreased and then increased, with the optimal oxide voltage observed at 2 V. The wrapping process solely relies on the force of gravity in NaOH solution, Na2CO3 solution, and air, without the need for additional injection process. However, a minimum injection velocity of 1 mL/min is required for the case of seawater. Thus, the NaOH solution is the optimal choice for wrapping. Even in the air, FLM can quickly fill the mold with the aid of a thin layer of NaOH solution.

Wrapping performance of LiMU gripper

We verified the wrapping performance of FLM in various environments (Fig. 3c). The LiMU gripper was submerged in seawater, sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) solution (0.5 mol/L), or NaOH solution (0.5 mol/L). In addition, the wrapping performance was also examined in the air. Conversely, significant electrolysis of the solution can be observed when the applied voltage exceeds 5 V. The appropriate oxide voltage windows vary depending on the environment, with 1–5 V for NaOH solution and air, 2–5 V for seawater, and 1–3 V for Na2CO3 solution. In the operating windows, increasing voltage can accelerate the wrapping process in seawater, NaOH solution, and air. In the case of Na₂CO₃ solution, the wrapping time initially decreased and then increased, with the optimal oxide voltage observed at 2 V. However, a minimum injection velocity of 1 mL/min is required for the case of seawater. Thus, the NaOH solution is the optimal choice for wrapping. Even in the air, FLM can quickly fill the mold with the aid of a thin layer of NaOH solution.

In NaOH solution, we investigated the wrapping time of the LiMU gripper (inner diameter of the gripper cavity: 10 mm) as it wrapped a 3D-printed PLA hemisphere (diameter of 8 mm) under different applied voltages, as shown in Fig. 3e. The LiMU gripper failed to wrap the hemisphere when the voltage was less than 1 V. The wrapping time decreased to ~1 second as the voltage increased from 1 V to 5 V. A high voltage ( > 5 V) induces significant hydrolysis and generates hydrogen bubbles near the FLM droplet to compromise the wrapping performance.

Fe content is positively correlated to the viscosity of the FLM25, which further affects the wrapping performance of the LiMU gripper. Figure 3e shows that the wrapping time decreases as the Fe content reduces from 40 % to 10 %, due to the lower viscosity of the FLM. Using a constant applied voltage of 5 V, we further examined the time required for wrapping 3D-printed PLA hemispheres of various diameters in NaOH solutions of differing concentrations (Fig. 3f). When increasing the size of the target objects, the space between the object and the gripper cavity is decreased, thus increasing the flow resistance of the FLM and increasing the wrapping time (see “Wrapping Mechanism by Electrochemistry” in Methods for details). The wrapping time can be reduced by increasing the concentration of the NaOH solution.

Experiment for environment-agnostic surface activity design outside liquid environment

In non-electrolyte environments like the air, only the top hemisphere of the FLM droplet in the gripper cavity contacts the electrolyte in the gauze. The bottom hemisphere of the FLM droplet is exposed to air. However, the LiMU gripper is still applicable in non-electrolyte environments with the aid of the surface tension control system, which induced the formation of Marangoni flows that allow the electrolyte to move to the region exposed to air. We illustrated the effect caused by Marangoni flows using a FLM droplet with half of it immersed in NaOH solution and the other exposed to the air, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3a. The induced Marangoni effect enables the electrolyte to wrap the exposed surface of the FLM in a very short time, thus maintaining the electrical activity of the whole FLM droplet (Supplementary Fig. 3b).

A FLM droplet (volume of 130 μL, diameter of 6 mm) was restricted in a rounded groove located in the center of a 3D-printed circular container (diameter of 5 cm, PLA, 3D printed by HORI printer). NaOH solution (0.5 mol/L) was filled in the container with a depth of 0.5 mm. A circular copper wire (diameter of 0.8 mm) was twined around the internal wall of the container, and another copper wire was inserted into the FLM droplet. The two copper wires were connected to the DC power supply (IT6432, ITECH, China) as electrodes. Copper powder (diameter of 300 nm) was sprinkled into the NaOH solution as an indicator. When applying a negative voltage to the FLM droplet, we observed that the electrolyte flows towards the FLM droplet along a specific radial direction, passing the top hemisphere of the droplet, and sequentially flowing back along the circumferential direction. Interestingly, we first observed that two independent vortices were formed symmetrically along the radial direction, as shown in Supplementary Video 4. When moving the electrode to shift the FLM droplet away from the center, the flow direction always keeps along the radial direction towards the center of the circular container.

Experiment setup for payload capacity test of LiMU gripper

We developed a robotic testing system to quantitatively characterize the payload capacity of the LiMU gripper, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 5a. The LiMU gripper was mounted to a 6-DOF robotic arm (AUBO-i5, AUBO Robotics Technology Co., LTD). The inner diameter of the gripper cavity is 26 mm, and the volume of the injected FLM droplet is 2.5 mL. A DC power supply (IT6432, ITECH, China) was used to drive the electromagnetic coil. For comparability, a 3D-printed sphere hook (diameter of 10 mm) was pre-immersed in the FLM with a certain embedding angle and connected to a force sensor (LY−103, Bengbu Ocean Sensing System Engineering Co., LTD) with a cotton rope. During the experiment, the robot arm was controlled to move far away from the force sensor along the axial direction of the force sensor. The real-time force-displacement data was recorded by a computer. The maximum payload can be obtained by analyzing the yield point of the force-displacement curve.

Experiment setup for dynamic grasping

We used the LiMU gripper to catch a cockroach to demonstrate its ability of dynamic grasping. The diameter of the cavity is 20 mm. The volume of the filled FLM droplet is about 1 mL. The adopted electrolyte is water. The oxide voltage is 5 V. The magnetic field strength for grasping is about 2500 Gs. The LiMU gripper was installed at the robotic arm’s end, suspended 10 mm above the tube. The cockroach can crawl freely in a transparent acrylic tube. A camera was placed on the side of the acrylic tube, perpendicular to the LiMU gripper, to record the process and analyze the position of the cockroach. When the cockroach was detected in the trapping area below the LiMU gripper, the robotic arm was activated to move downward to fixed height of 3 mm to grasp the cockroach (see also Supplementary Video 9). The entire grasping process took only ~200 milliseconds, while the cockroach’s movement speed was ~30 mm/s.

Grasping microfibers and microspheres

During grasping, we gently press the oxidized FLM droplet of LiMU gripper onto a paper bed covered with microfibers and microspheres. The grasped micro-objects exhibit uncertain states: some may be immersed in the FLM droplet, while others adhere to the surface via oxides. As described in the wrapping mechanism, the oxidized FLM surface possesses an oxide layer (Ga2O3) with strong adhesion to various materials.

Experiment setup and assessment of zebrafish embryo manipulation

Zebrafish embryo manipulation

A LiMU gripper with a cavity diameter of 4 mm was mounted at the bottom of a 3D-printed hollow sleeve (length: 100 mm, diameter: 8 mm) for handling zebrafish embryos (AB wild-type, diameter: ~1 mm, purchased from Beyotime Biotech. Inc). The voltage for grasping is set to 5 V, and voltage for active release is set to 15 V. Zebrafish embryos were submerged in culture medium (Hanks’ Balanced Salts Solution, purchased from Sigma-Aldrich) and maintained in a recirculating aquaculture system at a constant temperature of 28.5 °C under a 14-h light/10-hour dark (14 L:10D) photoperiod cycle. The experiment employed both the LiMU gripper and a pipette (purchased from Beyotime Biotech. Inc) to grasp and transfer 200 zebrafish embryos from the culture dish to a target dish for further evaluation.

Cell viability and embryo survival

To assess potential cellular oxidative damage of zebrafish embryos immediately after the manipulation, intracellular ROS levels in zebrafish embryos were assayed using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (Solarbio, D6470). Briefly, embryos successfully transferred by either the LiMU gripper or a micropipette were randomly selected (n = 12 per group), treated with the DCFH-DA probe for 30 min, and then imaged under an Olympus BX60 microscope with 504 nm excitation.

The morphology evaluation was performed to assess the zebrafish embryo development of the two groups (n = 12 per group). The embryos were imaged under a microscope (Olympus BX16 Microscope) on days 0, 1, and 4 post-manipulations and the body length of the resulting larvae were measured on day 4.

Behavioral tests were performed to assess embryonic development of the two groups (n = 12 per group) on day 5. The free-swimming trajectories of hatched zebrafish larvae were recorded for 1 h in a controlled incubation chamber maintained at 28.5 °C. Locomotor activity was automatically tracked by the Zebrabox video system, and data were processed using Zebralab 3.11 software (Viewpoint, France).

Vision recording and processing

Human hair release was recorded with a high-speed camera (Memrecam HX-7S, nac Image Technology, Japan; frame rate: 2000 fps). The release of microchips, microfibers, and microspheres was captured by a high-speed camera (UT030/M, WorkPower Technology Co., LTD, China; frame rate: 815 fps) equipped with a microscopic lens (M Plan 20/10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×, Mitutoyo, Japan). The trajectory of the released objects was analyzed using MATLAB with the template-based object tracking algorithm56. The velocity and acceleration of hair movement were obtained by taking the first- and second-order derivatives of the trajectory data, respectively. The remaining experimental videos were obtained with a DSLR camera (Canon 5D, Japan).
