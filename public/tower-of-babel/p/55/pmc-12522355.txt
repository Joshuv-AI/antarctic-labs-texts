Interaction between embryo transfer timing (D3/D4) and duration of progesterone exposure (P3/P4) on live birth rate in hormone replacement therapy cycles of frozen embryo transfer
Authors: Yujiang Wang, Lin Fan, Chuangqi Chen, Haiying Zhu, Fang Wang, Hu Tan, Xiqian Zhang, Fenghua Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn hormone replacement therapy (HRT) cycles of frozen embryo transfer (FET), progesterone-induced endometrial transformation is critical to clinical outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the interactive impact of the extent of progesterone exposure and the timing of embryo transfer on live birth outcomes.MethodsThis study retrospectively reviewed 3,381 infertile individuals from June 2013 to June 2024. Participants were stratified into four categories based on the number of days of progesterone exposure (P) and embryo developmental stage at transfer (D): P3-D3 group, P3-D4 group, P4-D3 group, and P4-D4 group.ResultsAnalysis showed comparable baseline characteristics among all groups, with no significant variation. When progesterone exposure duration was synchronized with embryo developmental day, the P4-D4 group exhibited significantly higher live birth rate (47.58% vs 30.41%), clinical pregnancy rate (58.89% vs 44.33%), and implantation rate (41.17% vs 25.07%) in contrast to the P3-D3 cohort (P < 0.001), along with a significantly lower miscarriage rate (17.2% vs 26.7%, P = 0.025). When progesterone exposure duration was held constant, D4 embryo transfer significantly improved live birth rate over D3 embryo transfer (P3-D4 group vs P3-D3 group: 44.72% vs 30.41%, P = 0.005; P4-D4 group vs P4-D3 group: 47.58% vs 30.80%, P < 0.001). When embryo developmental stage at transfer was fixed, outcomes related to pregnancy showed no variation between the three-day and four-day exposure schedules. progesterone exposure (P > 0.05). To systematically examine and identify the independent clinical factors that significantly influence live birth outcomes in a target population, a logistic regression approach was employed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the underlying relationships. Consistent results were observed.ConclusionFrozen embryo transfer cycles prepared with hormone replacement demonstrate higher live birth rates when embryos are transferred on day 4 rather than day 3, regardless of whether progesterone exposure is 3 or 4 days. When embryo developmental stage is fixed (D3 or D4), varying the duration of progesterone exposure (3 vs 4 days) does not significantly influence clinical or perinatal outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12958-025-01469-x.Keywords: Progesterone, Endometrium, Frozen embryo transfer, Hormone replacement, Live birth rate

Introduction

Since the introduction of frozen embryo transfer (FET) within medically assisted reproduction in 1983, this technique has markedly improved cumulative clinical pregnancy rates and reduced treatment-related complications. It has also facilitated the advancement of associated technologies such as preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) and oocyte cryopreservation [1–3]. With the widespread adoption of freeze-all strategies, FET has become a standard clinical protocol, and optimizing its outcomes has garnered increasing attention [4]. Embryo implantation success is contingent upon both a developmentally competent euploid embryo and a synchronously receptive endometrium [5, 6]. However, asynchronous development between the embryo and endometrium remains an unavoidable challenge in clinical practice.

Embryo–endometrium asynchrony can arise from interindividual differences in embryo development speed, endometrial responsiveness to progesterone, or procedural timing deviations. Such desynchronization disrupts the implantation window and constitutes a major limiting factor for successful pregnancy [7, 8]. Therefore, precise alignment between embryo developmental stage and period of progesterone supplementation is essential for optimizing transfer timing. However, the optimal duration of progesterone exposure prior to embryo transfer in hormone replacement therapy-frozen embryo transfer (HRT-FET) cycles remains unclear [9, 10]. This study focuses on FET cycles under HRT, aiming to systematically analyze the interaction between varying durations of progesterone exposure (3 vs 4 days) and embryo transfer timing (day 3 vs day 4) with respect to their effects on live birth, clinical pregnancy, and implantation outcomes. The ultimate goal is to establish a theoretical basis for determining the optimal embryo transfer window in clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study population

A total of 5,517 cases were retrospectively reviewed involving individuals who received HRT-FET cycles at the Department of Reproductive Medical Center, Guangdong Women and Children Hospital, from June 2013 to June 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) age between 22 and 40 years (inclusive); (ii) endometrial preparation conducted with a standardized HRT protocol; (iii) fewer than three prior embryo transfer attempts; and (iv) transplantation of top-grade cryopreserved cleavage-stage embryos on day 3 or day 4. Exclusion criteria included: (i) age > 40 years; (ii) three or more prior FET cycles; (iii) transfer of non-high-quality embryos in the current cycle; (iv) serum progesterone level > 1.0 ng/mL during the menstrual cycle; (v) presence of hydrosalpinx or uterine structural anomalies (e.g., uterine malformations, submucosal fibroids, intrauterine adhesions); (vi) preliminary use of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists in advance of the FET cycle; and (vii) suboptimal endometrial thickness (< 7 mm) on the scheduled transfer day. After screening based on these stringent criteria, a total of 3,381 HRT-FET cycles were retained for the final dataset (Fig. 1). The research protocol received approval from the institutional ethics board at Guangdong Women and Children Hospital (202201059).

Endometrial preparation protocol

For patients with ovulatory dysfunction, irregular menstrual cycles, or endometrial thickness < 7 mm during the ovulatory phase, endometrial preparation was performed following a standardized HRT regimen. Specifically, from day 3 to 5 of the menstrual cycle, patients were administered oral estradiol valerate tablets (Progynova®, Bayer, Germany) with a daily amount of 4 mg/day. After seven days of administration, transvaginal sonography (TVS) was carried out to measure endometrial thickness, echogenic pattern (types A/B/C), and vascularity, along with simultaneous measurement of serum estradiol (E2). Estradiol dosage and treatment length were tailored according to individual endometrial response, and hormonal profile. Once the endometrial thickness reached ≥ 7 mm, luteal transformation was initiated with progesterone delivered intramuscularly once per day at 40–60 mg. The first day of progesterone injection was defined as day 1 of progesterone exposure (P1). Embryo thawing and transfer were scheduled within the optimal window following the designated duration of progesterone exposure. Luteal support was maintained for at least 14 days post-transfer. Based on the number of days of progesterone exposure and the developmental stage of embryos at transfer, patients were stratified into four groups: 1) P3-D3 group: cleavage-stage embryo transfer on day 3 after three days of progesterone exposure; 2) P3-D4 group: day 4 embryo transfer following the same three-day progesterone regimen; 3) P4-D3 group: day 3 embryo transfer after four days of progesterone administration; 4) P4-D4 group: day 4 embryo transfer after a four-day course of progesterone treatment.

Embryo grading and selection

Embryo quality assessment was performed according to the standards defined by the Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology (SART) [11], which incorporate three key morphological parameters: blastomere number, degree of fragmentation, and blastomere uniformity. The criteria for cryopreservation in this study were: blastomere number > 5, fragmentation < 25%, and blastomere uniformity graded as at least moderate (Grade B) or better (Grade A). On the scheduled transfer day, embryos were thawed and maintained under standard culture conditions. For transfers involving day 3 cleavage-stage embryos, thawing was performed on the day of transfer itself and incubated in a 37 °C, 6% CO2 incubator for 2–4 h prior to transfer. For day 4 (D4) transfer, embryos were thawed one day before transfer and cultured overnight (16–20 h) under the same incubator conditions before being transferred the following day.

Cryopreservation and thawing protocol

Cryopreservation

Embryos were initially placed in a base medium containing HEPES (Quinn’s SAGE, USA) supplemented with 20% v/v human albumin solution (HAS, Vitrolife, Sweden) at room temperature for 1–2 min for equilibration. They were then transferred to Cryo Solution I to initiate intracellular dehydration. Once the embryos re-expanded to approximately 80% of their original volume (estimated within 2–7 min), they were moved to Cryo Solution II, equilibrated for 45–90 s, loaded onto carrier sticks, and rapidly immersed into liquid nitrogen for storage.

Thawing

The rapid-warming protocol was applied. Thawing solutions were prewarmed to 37 °C in an incubator. Carrier sticks were removed and promptly placed into Thawing Solution I for 1 min. Embryos were then transferred to Thawing Solution II for 3 min, followed by immersion in the base medium for 5 min. Finally, embryos were cultured in microdrops within a 37 °C, 6% CO2 incubator overnight, pending embryo transfer.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the live birth rate, defined as the percentage of transfer cycles that led to a live birth at or beyond 24 weeks of gestation, which was relative to the total number of embryo transfer cycles. Secondary outcomes included the implantation rate, calculated as the ratio of embryos successfully implanted to the overall number of embryos transferred; biochemical pregnancy rate, defined as the proportion of cycles with serum β-hCG ≥ 5 IU/L on day 12 following FET; clinical pregnancy rate, defined as the proportion of cycles in which at least one gestational sac was detected via ultrasound at week 5 after FET; ectopic pregnancy rate, defined as the proportion of ectopic pregnancies confirmed by ultrasound or laparoscopy among all clinical pregnancies; and miscarriage rate, defined as the proportion of spontaneous miscarriages occurring before 24 weeks of gestation among all clinical pregnancies. Perinatal outcomes included gestational age at delivery, neonatal sex distribution, congenital malformation rate, incidence of preterm birth (< 37 weeks), birth weight, and frequency of low-birth-weight infants (< 2,500 g).

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All statistical tests were two-sided, with a P-value below 0.05 considered indicative of significance. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the data characteristics. Continuous variables following normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (Mean ± SEM), with group comparisons made via independent samples t-test (for two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA, for multiple groups). Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction if overall significance was detected.

Univariate logistic regression assessed the association of individual factors (Age, number of transplantation cycles, duration of infertility, type of infertility, endometrial thickness, embryos transferred, duration of progesterone exposure, embryo transfer timing, embryo transfer grading) with Live Birth, identifying potentially associated factors. Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression, adjusting for potential confounders, determined factors independently associated with Live Birth. Variables for the multivariate model were selected based on univariate results, clinical/biological relevance, and researcher expertise, irrespective of their univariate significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 3,381 HRT-FET cycles involving either D3 or D4 embryo transfer were incorporated into the conclusive analysis. All cycles were categorized into four groups according to the number of days of P and D: P3-D3 group, P3-D4 group, P4-D3 group, and P4-D4 group. Baseline characteristics were compared among the groups. Age distribution and infertility history did not differ significantly between cohorts, endometrial thickness, body mass index (BMI), or number of embryos transferred (P > 0.05) (Table 1). Analysis indicated no significant group-level distinctions in infertility type or etiology (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Clinical outcomes of synchronized progesterone exposure and embryo development

The P4-D4 group exhibited significantly higher live birth rate, clinical pregnancy rate, and implantation rate compared with the P3-D3 group (47.58% vs 30.41%, P < 0.001; 58.89% vs 44.33%, P < 0.001; 41.17% vs 25.07%, P < 0.001;). Moreover, the miscarriage rate was significantly lower in the P4-D4 group than in the P3-D3 group (17.2% vs 26.7%, P = 0.025). No significant differences were noted between the two groups in ectopic pregnancy rate, biochemical pregnancy rate, or perinatal outcomes (P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Univariate and multivariate analysis of factors associated with live birth

The multivariate regression analysis revealed several significant associations with live birth outcomes. Increasing maternal age was associated with a reduced likelihood of live birth (adjusted OR, 0.934; 95% CI, 0.917–0.951; P < 0.001). Greater endometrial thickness (adjusted OR, 1.066; 95% CI, 1.029–1.105; P < 0.001) and a higher number of embryos transferred (adjusted OR, 1.804; 95% CI, 1.481–2.196; P < 0.001) were both positively associated with live birth. Day 4 embryo transfers significantly increased the odds of live birth compared to Day 3 transfers (adjusted OR, 2.042; 95% CI, 1.726–2.414; P < 0.001). No significant associations were observed for the number of transplantation cycles, duration of infertility, type of infertility, duration of progesterone exposure or embryo transfer grading (all P > 0.05) (Table 5). The factors related to clinical pregnancy rate and implantation rate were similar to live birth rate based on the results of linear regression analysis. However, there were no related factors affecting the abortion rate (Supplementary Tables 1–3).

Discussion

With advancements in cryobiology, embryo cryopreservation has been widely adopted in the field of assisted reproduction, and clinical outcomes of FET have become a major focus of research. Embryo implantation is a highly complex process that relies on precise synchronization between a euploid embryo and a receptive endometrium [12]. Achieving such synchronization requires two key elements: the optimal selection of embryonic developmental stage for transfer and the accurate assessment and regulation of endometrial receptivity [13, 14]. Among current endometrial preparation strategies, HRT-FET is increasingly favored due to its procedural simplicity and controllable timeline; however, its dependence on exogenous luteal support to maintain pregnancy warrants attention [10, 15]. This study focused on the HRT-FET model to explore how embryo developmental stage (D3 vs D4) and duration of progesterone exposure (P3 vs P4) influence live birth outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that synchronizing progesterone exposure and embryonic developmental stage to the fourth day (i.e., the P4-D4 protocol) significantly improves live birth rate, clinical pregnancy rate, and implantation rate compared with the conventional P3-D3 scheme. These results underscore the pivotal role of coordinated timing between endometrial transformation and embryo development in enhancing live birth outcomes in HRT-FET cycles [16].

In clinical settings, day 3 (D3) cleavage-stage embryo transfers are commonly used for both fresh and frozen cycles, while day 4 (D4) transfers are relatively rare [17, 18]. However, our data and existing literature support the clinical value of D4 embryo transfer [19]. Research by Tao and colleagues demonstrated that aneuploidy rates vary dynamically during embryo development, reaching as high as 60% at the 6–10-cell stage, while significantly decreasing at the morula stage. This provides a biological rationale for the superior sustained pregnancy rates observed with D4 embryo transfers [20, 21]. Moreover, outcomes from D4 morula-stage transfers are not inferior to those from D5 blastocyst transfers, likely because D4 embryos are introduced into the uterine environment at a timing more aligned with natural physiology. Additionally, overnight culture following thawing enables further selection of embryos with greater developmental potential [19]. Our previous work also indicated that moderately delayed embryo transfer after progesterone exposure does not compromise pregnancy outcomes [22], lending theoretical support to the feasibility of D4 transfer adopted in this study.

Endometrial receptivity is constrained by a limited window of implantation (WOI), which is precisely regulated by progesterone signaling [23]. Extending the duration of progesterone exposure during HRT cycles has been shown to widen the receptive window, thereby increasing opportunities for embryo–endometrial interaction and potentially improving clinical outcomes [24, 25]. In this study, we observed a trend toward improved live birth outcomes in the P3-D4 group compared with the P3-D3 group. Notably, when both embryo development and progesterone exposure were extended synchronously to day 4 (P4-D4), the most favorable clinical outcomes were achieved. This finding clearly suggests that either delayed embryo transfer (D4) or extended progesterone exposure (P4) alone may yield certain benefits, but optimal outcomes require simultaneous adjustment of both parameters (P4-D4).

Although the WOI is conventionally viewed as a narrow interval, emerging clinical evidence suggests a degree of flexibility [26, 27]. Two prospective studies by van de Vijver and colleagues [28, 29] demonstrated that transferring D4 embryo transfer performed on the third or fifth day of progesterone administration yielded similar implantation, clinical pregnancy, and live birth rates. These data indicate that endometrial receptivity can remain stable within a specific time frame. Our findings further refine this concept by identifying a “plateau phase” in endometrial receptivity. When comparing transfers of embryos at the same developmental stage (D3 or D4), the difference in live birth rate between 3 and 4 days of progesterone exposure was minimal—only 2.49% and 2.14%, respectively. This aligns with findings by Escribá MJ et al. [30], who reported no significant difference in clinical outcomes in donor cycles when D3 embryos were transferred after 3, 4, or even 5 days of progesterone exposure. Collectively, these data strongly support the existence of a stable receptive interval spanning days 3 to 4 (and possibly extending to day 5) after progesterone initiation in HRT-FET cycles.

This large-cohort analysis confirms that synchronizing both progesterone exposure duration and embryo developmental stage is essential in HRT-FET cycles. Compared with the conventional D3 cleavage-stage transfer, delaying both progesterone exposure and embryo transfer by one day (i.e., adopting the P4-D4 protocol) substantially improves key clinical outcomes including live birth rate. Furthermore, our results clearly delineate a “plateau phase” of endometrial receptivity between 3 and 4 days of progesterone exposure, during which clinical outcomes remain relatively stable. This identification, in conjunction with the P4-D4 optimization strategy, offers practical flexibility in clinical operations: under the premise of prioritizing the P4-D4 protocol, satisfactory outcomes can still be expected if, due to logistical or laboratory constraints, embryos of the same developmental stage are transferred on either day 3 or 4 of progesterone exposure. This facilitates better coordination between clinical scheduling and embryology workflows. Nevertheless, these conclusions warrant further validation through rigorously designed multicenter randomized controlled trials and mechanistic studies involving molecular biology.
