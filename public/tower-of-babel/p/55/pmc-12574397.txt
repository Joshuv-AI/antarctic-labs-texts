ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDeep vein thrombosis (DVT) in critically ill patients is often undetected. However, it is unclear whether ultrasound surveillance for early detection of DVT in high-risk medical-surgical intensive care unit (ICU) patients improves patients’ outcomes. The DETECT trial (Diagnosing deep-vein thrombosis early in critically ill patients) evaluates the effect of twice-weekly bilateral lower limb ultrasound compared to usual care on 90-day mortality of critically ill adult patients admitted to medical, surgical and trauma ICUs.Methods and analysisThe DETECT trial is an international, parallel-group, open-label, randomised trial, which will recruit 1800 critically ill adults from over 14 hospitals in Saudi Arabia and Kuwait. Eligible patients will be allocated to twice-weekly bilateral lower limb ultrasound or usual care. The primary outcome is 90-day mortality. Secondary outcomes include lower limb proximal DVT, pulmonary embolism and clinically important bleeding. The first patient was enrolled on 21 March 2023. As of 8 April 2025, 711 patients have been enrolled from 14 centres in Saudi Arabia and Kuwait. The first interim analysis was conducted on 14 May 2025. We expect to complete recruitment by December 2026.Ethics and disseminationInstitutional review boards (IRBs) of each participating institution approved the study. We plan to publish the results in peer-reviewed journals and present the findings at international critical care conferences.Trial registration numberClinicaltrials.gov: NCT05112705, registered on 9-11-2021.

Introduction

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in critically ill patients is often undetected and could lead to pulmonary embolism (PE) and subsequent morbidity and mortality.1 A systematic review of autopsy-confirmed missed diagnoses in intensive care unit (ICU) patients showed that PE is a leading cause of fatal misdiagnoses.2 A case–control study demonstrated that DVT risk stratification coupled with history and physical examination performed poorly in discriminating between cases and controls, suggesting that history and physical examination may be insufficient for detecting lower limb DVT in the ICU.3 A retrospective study of 2939 hospitalised trauma patients who underwent once-weekly surveillance ultrasounds found that 86% of identified DVTs were not clinically suspected.4

Several studies have shown that surveillance using ultrasound detects many DVTs that would otherwise go unnoticed.357 By earlier identification and treatment of silent DVTs, surveillance ultrasound may reduce the risks of PE, morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients. However, the supportive evidence for this premise is limited. Previous studies of surveillance ultrasonography in trauma and neurosurgical patients showed that surveillance ultrasonography increases the rate of DVT diagnosis. However, the effects on PE and mortality were inconsistent. A pre-defined sub-study from the PREVENT trial (the Pneumatic Compression for Preventing Venous Thromboembolism trial)810 compared ICU patients who were enrolled in the trial and had twice-weekly surveillance ultrasonography with those who were eligible non-randomised and thus did not have twice-weekly surveillance ultrasonography. Surveillance was associated with lower 90-day mortality (adjusted Hazard ratio (HR) 0.75; 95% CI 0.57 to 0.98) and earlier diagnosis of both DVT (median 4 days [Interquartile range (IQR) 2, 10] vs 20 days [IQR 16, 22]) and PE (median 4 days [IQR 2.5, 5] vs 7.5 days [IQR 6.1, 28.9]). Furthermore, surveillance was associated with an increase in the diagnosis of DVT (adjusted HR 5.49; 95% CI 2.92 to 13.02) but not PE (adjusted HR 0.56; 95% CI 0.19 to 1.91).11 Despite the interesting findings, the results are limited by observational design.

We hypothesise that surveillance ultrasound in critically ill patients, by early detection and treatment of DVT, reduces mortality by preventing pulmonary embolism. However, while surveillance ultrasound is a non-invasive test, the clinical importance of detecting silent DVTs remains a subject of ongoing debate. The resulting therapy with anticoagulation may expose patients to bleeding complications. In addition, twice-weekly surveillance ultrasonography is associated with additional costs and burden on strained healthcare systems. Therefore, large, rigorous clinical trials should evaluate the efficacy and safety of DVT surveillance in the ICU before adopting this approach. Herein, we present the DETECT (Diagnosing deep-vein thrombosis early in critically ill patients) trial, which aims to determine the effect of twice-weekly DVT surveillance ultrasound, compared with usual care, on 90-day mortality in critically ill adults. We report the DETECT trial protocol following the guidance of the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials.12

Methods and analysis

Study design and settings

The DETECT Trial is an open-label, multinational, randomised-controlled trial (RCT) that will randomise eligible patients to undergo surveillance ultrasound twice weekly or usual care (online supplemental 1). The trial is funded by King Abdullah International Medical Research Centre (KAIMRC), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, and is registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05112705).

Patient and public involvement

We did not have patient or public involvement in the design or conduct of the study.

Eligibility and enrolment

The research coordinator at each participating site will screen all new ICU admissions within the first 48 hours of ICU admission for eligibility. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined in Box 1. We will document the screened and randomised patients. Given the nature of the intervention, we will use a mixed-consent model, in which eligible patients or their substitute decision-maker (SDM) will be approached for written a priori or deferred informed consent.

Randomisation

Eligible participants will be randomised via a password-protected, secure website (RED-CAP) using a concealed, variable-size block, computer-generated randomisation procedure generated by the Data Management Department at KAIMRC, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Randomisation will be stratified by centre and the patient admission category: trauma, surgical or medical. Patients will be allocated to twice-weekly bilateral lower limb ultrasound or usual care (control) in a 1:1 ratio.

Interventions

Intervention group

Participants randomised to the intervention group will undergo twice-weekly proximal lower limb ultrasound starting within three calendar days of ICU admission (day one is the day of ICU admission); the intervention will continue while the patient is in the ICU for up to 28 days. Additional ultrasounds may be requested if deemed indicated by the treating team.

The ultrasound will be performed by certified technologists and interpreted independently by radiologists (online supplemental 2, Appendix A). A standardised protocol will be followed; both proximal lower extremities will be scanned at 1 cm increments. A diagnosis of DVT on ultrasound will require non-compressibility of one or more of these six deep venous segments: trifurcation, popliteal, distal superficial femoral, mid superficial femoral, proximal superficial femoral and common femoral veins. The detailed ultrasound standard operating procedure is outlined in Appendix A.

Usual care group

Participants randomised to the usual care group will not undergo ultrasound surveillance; any lower limb ultrasound will be performed at the discretion of the treating team.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention, we cannot blind the intervention to patients, their caregivers and the technician or the ultrasonographer. However, the PE and bleeding outcomes will be adjudicated by two assessors blinded to the intervention. In addition, the study biostatistician will be blinded to group assignment, and all analyses will be performed before unblinding. A separate unblinded biostatistician will conduct the interim analyses for the Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB).

Co-interventions

The treating team will have complete, independent control of patient management and, as such, pharmacologic or mechanical thromboprophylaxis. The treating teams will be notified of the results of surveillance ultrasonography. The treating team will make decisions regarding anticoagulation, evaluation for PE, the use of antiplatelets (aspirin, ticlopidine, clopidogrel) and the initiation of anticoagulants after randomisation (including warfarin, therapeutic dose of low-molecular-weight heparin, therapeutic intravenous heparin and other anticoagulants). We will document the treatments and complications in the study data collection.

Co-enrolment

Co-enrolment in another RCT is permissible if approved by both studies’ steering committees.

Outcomes

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome for the DETECT Trial is 90-day mortality. For participants discharged from the hospital before day 90, follow-up will be conducted by telephone.

Secondary Outcomes

1. Proximal DVT. We will follow the definition used in the PROTECT and PREVENT trials.10 We define DVT if there is a partially or completely incompressible venous segment. Venous wall thickening is not considered diagnostic of DVT. If a venous segment is not well visualised and never is well visualised on subsequent ultrasounds, the test is considered indeterminate; such events will be recorded but are not considered trial outcomes. Ultrasonographers may scan the distal leg veins at their discretion. DVTs are considered chronic if a prior test reveals evidence of thrombus in the same or contiguous venous segment. We define a thrombus as catheter-related if a catheter had been in situ in the same or a contiguous venous segment within 72 hours of the diagnosis.

We will collect data on the characteristics of DVTs to explore whether ultrasound leads to the diagnosis of less extensive proximal lower-extremity DVT and increases diagnosis of central-venous-catheter- and non-central-venous-catheter-related incident proximal lower-extremity DVT.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation

Prior results showed that twice-weekly surveillance ultrasonography in medical-surgical ICU patients receiving pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis was associated with decreased 90-day mortality based on our previous study (adjusted HR 0.75; 95% CI 0.57 to 0.98).11 Based on an estimated baseline mortality of 30% and an estimated absolute risk reduction of 6% (from 30% to 24%) and using the power of 80% and alpha of 0.05, the total sample required will be 1716 patients. Considering the possibility of a 5% lost-to-follow-up, the total number of patients needed in the trial was 1800.

Statistical analysis plan

Categorical variables will be reported as number and frequency and will be compared using chi-square test. Continuous variables will be reported as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile ranges. Continuous variables will be compared using the Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test.

The primary outcome will be compared in the intention-to-treat and per-protocol cohorts (effectiveness analysis). Results will be reported as relative risk (RR) with 95% CI. A formal statistical analysis plan will be agreed on and placed in the public domain before the study database is locked for the analysis of the primary outcome. Secondary outcomes will also be compared in the intention-to-treat cohort.

Interim analyses

The DSMB will follow stopping rules based on the primary outcome of 90-day mortality for safety and effectiveness. There will be two interim analyses (when reaching 33% and 67% of the sample size). The trial could be stopped for safety (p<0.01) or effectiveness (p<0.001), but not for futility. We will account for alpha spending using the O’Brien Fleming method, and the final p-value will be considered at 0.048.

Study governance

The study Steering Committee members will be responsible for the overall management of the study. The DSMB should provide an independent opinion on the trial’s safety and/or efficacy. A separate document provides a detailed charter of the DSMB.

Safety monitoring and reporting

Unexpected safety concerns will be reported to the Steering Committee and the DSMB. Safety monitoring will follow the principles for serious adverse events in academic critical care research”.30 As such, reporting of adverse events will be restricted to events that are considered related to surveillance ultrasound procedures, classified as possibly, probably or definitely.

Study compliance

Several measures will be taken to ensure protocol compliance. Start-up training sessions and follow-up training sessions will be conducted. We will collect daily data on the frequency and timing of surveillance ultrasounds.

Ethics and dissemination

This study will follow the Good Clinical Practice guidelines and other applicable regulatory requirements. The study was approved by the IRB of the Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs (Ref: IRB/0215/23-dated 16-June-2020), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, and all participating sites (online supplemental 2), Appendix B). We will use a mixed consent model, a priori or deferred. Consent will be obtained before randomisation from the patient or their SDM whenever possible. The local research team could also enrol eligible patients and obtain consent subsequently under a deferred consent model, which involves enrolling the patient where a priori consent is impossible, followed by the request for the patient’s or SDM’s informed consent at a later date. The participant informed consent form in both English and Arabic can be found in the online supplemental material (online supplemental 3). The findings from the primary analysis will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal. Additional manuscripts addressing secondary objectives will also be prepared and submitted to relevant academic journals. Presentations may also be made at professional and scientific conferences to maximise the dissemination and impact of the study results.

Discussion

The DETECT trial is the first RCT that compares surveillance ultrasound with usual care in medical-surgical-trauma critically ill patients. The trial interventions and outcome monitoring will ensure minimal “loss to follow-up” for the primary outcome. We will closely monitor protocol deviations and violations to ensure compliance (online supplemental 2, Appendix C). We monitor safety by documenting key adverse events such as bleeding as an outcome since more detection of DVT will likely lead to more anticoagulation.

The optimal frequency to perform screening is unknown, and different frequencies have been used in the literature. We used the schedule followed in the PROTECT and PREVENT trials.6 10 28 Most other studies used a less intensive frequency, so we thought that the twice-weekly frequency would be a reasonable option to evaluate efficacy and minimise type II error.

Trial status

The first patient was enrolled on 21 March 2023. As of 8 April 2025, a total of 711 patients have been enrolled from 14 centres in Saudi Arabia and Kuwait. The first interim analysis was conducted May 2025. We expect to complete the recruitment of 1800 patients by December 2026.
