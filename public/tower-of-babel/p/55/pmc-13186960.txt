Disorder suppression and tunable localization in ultrathin SrIrO3 films via SrTiO3 capping
Authors: Jinyoung Maeng, Seonha Hwang, Jungchan Choi, Dongwoo Lee, Jonghyun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStrong spin–orbit coupling in 5d transition-metal oxides produces correlated electronic phases that are highly sensitive to disorder and dimensionality. Here, we investigate the thickness-dependent transport of epitaxial SrIrO3 (SIO) thin films, comparing insulator-like (SIO_i) and metal-like (SIO_m) types. Without capping, SIO_i localizes at 7 unit cells (u.c.), while SIO_m localizes at 3 u.c., revealing distinct disorder thresholds. Notably, applying a SrTiO3 (STO) capping layer suppresses localization in both types. The critical thickness for the metal–insulator transition decreases to below 3 u.c. for SIO_i and to 2 u.c. for SIO_m. Structural analysis suggests that the STO capping mitigates intrinsic disorder by inducing lattice relaxation in the ultrathin films. This approach offers a pathway to control electronic phases in correlated oxide heterostructures via interface engineering.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46195-5.Subject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

Materials based on 5d orbitals exhibit significantly stronger spin–orbit coupling (SOC) than their 3d and 4d counterparts. As a result, these systems often exhibit emergent quantum phenomena driven by the interplay of strong SOC, Coulomb repulsion (U), and crystal field effects1,2. Among 5d orbital-based materials, the n = 1 Ruddlesden–Popper (RP) phase compound Sr₂IrO₄ is a Mott insulator exhibiting a weakly canted antiferromagnetic order. Despite the extended nature of 5d orbitals, which leads to a reduced effective Coulomb repulsion U (~ 0.5 eV), the strong SOC (~ 0.4 eV) results in the formation of the Jeff = 1/2 band3,4. This band structure gives rise to a Mott insulating ground state. In the RP series, the n = 2 compound Sr3Ir2O7 exhibits a slightly broader bandwidth due to increased interlayer coupling although it still remains as a Mott insulator. The n = ∞ compound SrIrO3 (SIO) adopts a three-dimensional structure composed of a corner-sharing octahedral network, which significantly enhances the bandwidth and leads to the collapse of the Jeff = 1/2 Mott gap5,6. However, the strong SOC still suppresses the density of states at the Fermi level, and therefore the system lies close to the Mott transition boundary and exhibits a semi-metallic ground state7,8.

In strongly correlated systems, disorder interacts with electron–electron correlations, resulting in diverse transport properties. The renormalization of correlation effects by strong disorder has thus become an intriguing subject of study9,10. From an experimental perspective, thin films offer a versatile platform where key parameters, such as electronic correlations and disorder, can be tuned. This is achieved by varying the film thickness, controlling lattice strain via the substrate, or engineering interlayer coupling in heterostructures. As the film thickness decreases, many metallic correlated systems begin to exhibit emergent phenomena such as topological insulating phases or metal–insulator transitions, which are rarely observed in their bulk counterparts11,12. Among these systems, SIO thin films exhibit a metal–insulator transition (MIT) as the film thickness is reduced13–16. This MIT can arise either from Anderson localization induced by disorder, or from a reduction in the coordination of constituent ions, which leads to a narrower bandwidth and enhanced localization. Such thickness-dependent localization can be controlled by depositing an additional capping layer. For instance, it has been reported that the metal-insulator transition (MIT) in La2/3Sr1/3MnO3 (LSMO) thin films, which typically appears as thickness decreases, is suppressed by an SrTiO3 (STO) capping layer17. Furthermore, numerous studies have documented various instances where the physical properties of thin films are significantly modified by the presence of a capping layer18,19.

Previous studies have reported that SIO thin films can exhibit either an insulator-like or metal-like electronic phase depending on the growth conditions. These two phases showed no noticeable differences in oxygen vacancy concentration or Sr/Ir stoichiometry; rather, their transport properties were affected by subtle differences in lattice structure20. In this work, we investigate the thickness-dependent transport properties of both insulator-like and metal-like SIO films to compare the disorder-driven localization mechanisms. Furthermore, our experimental results reveal that the SrTiO3 capping layer effectively reduces disorder in the SIO thin films. Our findings suggest that at the ultrathin oxide interface, even the mere presence of a capping layer can induce changes in lattice structure and significantly alters the transport behavior, highlighting a new degree of tunability in complex oxide heterostructures.

Results and discussion

We investigated the thickness-dependent electrical-transport properties of SIO thin films, which were classified into two distinct groups: insulator-like (SIO_i) and metal-like (SIO_m) thin films. First, we examined the resistance of SIO_i films with thicknesses ranging from 20 to 3 unit cells (u.c.). Figure 1(a) displays the thickness-dependent sheet resistance measured at 300 K. The sheet resistance remained relatively constant as the thickness decreased from 20 u.c. down to 8 u.c; however, it exhibited a sharp increase at 7 u.c. For films thinner than 7 u.c., the resistance exceeded the measurement limit, indicating a fully insulating state. The temperature dependence of the sheet resistances is shown in Fig. 1(b). Films with thickness ranging from 20 u.c. to 8 u.c. exhibit similar behaviors. In contrast, the 7 u.c. film exhibits a pronounced increase in resistance, showing a monotonic increase as the temperature decreases.

As explained above, reducing SIO thickness triggers a MIT. The underlying mechanisms include Anderson localization induced by disorder, and localization caused by bandwidth narrowing due to reduced coordination of constituent ions. In previous studies, Anderson localization as a dominant MIT mechanism was typically observed below approximately 3 nm (7–8 u.c.)13,14. In contrast, coordination-driven localization is attributed to the emergence of a Mott gap and drives the MIT in films thinner than 3 u.c.15,16 The observed difference in critical thickness for the MIT between SIO_m and SIO_i films suggests that SIO_i possesses a higher degree of disorder, leading to Anderson localization at a thickness of 7 u.c. Conversely, due to the comparatively lower disorder, SIO_m closely reaches the onset of coordination-driven localization at 3 u.c., avoiding the Anderson localization at 7 u.c.17.

To explore the effect of top capping on the transport properties for ultrathin SIO films, 20 u.c. thick STO layers were deposited on the SIO thin films. We first compared the thickness-dependent resistance of the SIO_i samples without and with the STO capping layer (denoted as SIO_i and SIO_i_c, respectively). In contrast to SIO_i, as shown in Fig. 3(a), the sheet resistances at 300 K did not increase with decreasing thickness down to 3 u.c. for the capped SIO_i_c samples. This result is notable because the introduction of the STO capping layer substantially reduced the resistance. Figure 3(b) shows the temperature dependence of sheet resistance for all SIO_i_c samples, and Figs. 3(c)–3(e) present the temperature dependence of the normalized sheet resistance R/R0. The characteristics of these temperature versus R/R0 curves can be classified into 3 groups depending on the SIO mid-layer thickness and consistently on their behaviors: (i) the samples with thicknesses from 20 to 9 u.c. still exhibit insulator-like behavior [Fig. 3(c)], similar to the uncapped SIO_i films; (ii) the samples with thicknesses from 7 to 8 u.c. show metal-like behavior in the high-temperature region accompanied by an upturn in resistance with decreasing temperature [Fig. 3(d)]; and (iii) 3 to 6 u.c. thick films exhibit metal-like behavior throughout the entire measured temperature range [Fig. 3(e)]. Among these, the behavior of the 3 to 6 u.c. is surprising because the strong insulating characteristic of the uncapped SIO films dramatically changed to metallic even with the ultra-thinned thickness reduced to 3 u.c. These observations indicate that STO capping effectively suppresses carrier localization in the high-resistance 7 u.c. films as well as in the insulating films thinner than 7 u.c., which originally showed unmeasurable resistance [see Fig. 3(a) and also the insulating behaviors for all SIO_i films in Fig. 1].

To explain the observed suppression of carrier localization in SIO_i_c, we consider the following possible scenarios. The first scenario is that the STO capping layer provides additional hopping channels via the Ir–O–Ti network, which increases the bandwidth and reduces the effective electron correlation. In the superlattice of SIO and STO, the STO layers alternately separate the SIO layers in a manner analogous to the Ruddlesden–Popper (RP) phase25,26. As the SIO layers are thinned, a progressive gap opening is observed, which eventually drives the MIT. Nevertheless, the superlattice exhibits a smaller gap than the structurally similar RP phase, which can be attributed to the TiO layers providing new Ir–O–Ti bonds that generate additional hopping channels27. Based on this analogy, if the STO capping layer provided sufficient additional hopping channels, the resistance would be reduced. However, if additional hopping channels were the primary origin of the MIT, the sheet resistance in 3–6 u.c. range should be comparable across samples. This is because the STO capping layer has a constant thickness, and the SIO layers in this range do not contribute to the conductance (as discussed in Fig. 1). Contrary to this expectation, as shown in Fig. 3(b), the sheet resistance values generally decrease with increasing thickness in the 3–6 u.c. range. These results indicate that the additional hopping channels provided by the STO capping layer are not the primary driving force of the observed MIT.

The second scenario is that the STO capping layer itself may exhibit finite conductivity. If the STO layer contains oxygen vacancies or undergoes a structural phase transition, it could enable electron transport through the STO. To examine this possibility, we first evaluated the growth conditions of the STO layer; a reference STO film was deposited directly on a STO substrate with the same thickness and growth conditions as the capping layer of SIO_i_c. As a result, we observed unmeasurably high resistance in this test sample. Next, we investigated the potential role of oxygen vacancies. In SIO, oxygen vacancies are known to increase resistance by disrupting electronic pathways, whereas in STO they typically enhance conductivity by introducing mobile carriers28,29. However, annealing the capped films in an oxygen-rich environment produced no observable change in resistance (see Supplementary Information). Therefore, we conclude that oxygen vacancies in the STO capping layer are not the dominant cause of the suppressed MIT, and that the observed electrical-transport characteristics are intrinsic to the SIO thin films.

The third scenario is that the STO capping layer mitigates disorder in the upper region of the SIO layer. Under this scenario, SIO films with thicknesses of 3–6 u.c. would exhibit metal-like behavior over the entire temperature range due to the reduced disorder. However, for slightly thicker films (7–8 u.c.), only a few unit cells near the top interface would become less disordered, while the underlying layers would retain insulator-like characteristics. Consequently, these films would display metal-like behavior at high temperatures but transition to insulator-like behavior at low temperatures. For films thicker than 9 u.c., the bulk insulator-like behavior would dominate, resulting in insulating transport characteristics across all temperatures.

As shown in Figs. 1 and 2, SIO_i and SIO_m, which exhibit different levels of disorder, also display corresponding differences in their lattice structures20. Consequently, if the STO capping layer indeed mitigates disorder, the SIO_i films should exhibit concomitant changes in their structural properties. Analogous phenomena have been reported in SrRuO3 (SRO), where even a few unit cells of an STO capping layer can significantly alter the lattice structure and magnetic properties30. Specifically, the STO capping layer modifies the rotation angles of the RuO₆ octahedra in SRO, inducing a structural phase transition from a tetragonal to an orthorhombic. This transformation modifies the exchange energy and consequently shifts the Curie temperature. Drawing on this analogy, we hypothesize the STO capping layer may similarly drive a phase transition in the SIO films, leading to a reduction in disorder. To verify this, we analyzed the structural evolution using high-resolution X-ray diffraction (HRXRD).

Figure 4(a) presents the out-of-plane (002) θ–2θ XRD scan of a 10-u.c.-thick SIO film, from which the c-axis lattice constant was extracted. Additionally, reciprocal space mapping (RSM) measurements were performed around the STO(103) reflection to determine the a-axis lattice constant. Figures 4(b) and 4(c) show the RSM images for SIO_i and SIO_i_c, respectively. As shown, the peaks corresponding to the SIO and STO capping layers are visible and vertically aligned with the strong substrate peaks. The identical Qx values observed in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c) confirm that both types of SIO films, regardless of the STO capping, maintain identical in-plane lattice constants (a and b axes) due to the substrate-imposed strain. However, a distinct difference was observed in the out-of-plane reflections: the c-axis lattice constant of SIO_i was 0.401 nm, whereas that of SIO_i_c was slightly reduced from 0.401 to 0.399 nm (see Supplementary Information). In previous studies, SIO_i exhibited a c-lattice constant of approximately 0.400 nm, whereas SIO_m showed a slightly smaller value of about 0.399 nm. These subtle differences in the lattice structure between SIO_m and SIO_i were observed not only through XRD but also in low-energy optical conductivity spectra and Raman spectra20. The changes induced by the STO capping layer are consistent with the comparison between SIO_i and SIO_m, where a smaller lattice constant corresponds to a lower degree of disorder. Thus, based on the thickness-dependent electrical characteristics and the variation in the c-axis lattice constant, we conclude that the SIO_i films underwent a lattice structural modification induced by the STO capping layer, which in turn reduced the disorder17. However, notably, unlike SIO_m, which exhibits insulator-like behavior at 3 u.c. [see Fig. 2], SIO_i_c retains metal-like behavior even at the same thickness. This demonstrates that the STO capping layer dramatically suppresses disorder in the SIO layer.

Lastly, we examined the effect of STO capping on the SIO_m samples. Figure 5(a) compares the sheet resistance at 300 K for the uncapped SIO_m and STO-capped SIO_m (SIO_m_c) samples, and Fig. 5(b) shows the temperature dependencies of sheet resistance for SIO_m_c. In both sample sets, sheet resistance generally increases as the film thickness decreases. However, whereas the uncapped SIO_m sample shows a pronounced increase in resistance at a thickness of 3 u.c., the capped SIO_m_c samples exhibit four times lower sheet resistance at the same thickness while it increases at 2 u.c. The R/R₀ data in Figs. 5(c), 5(d), and 5(e) further reveal detailed transport properties of SIO_m_c, which can be classified into three regimes based on thickness: (i) films from 20 to 9 u.c. show metal-like behavior; (ii) films from 8 to 3 u.c. exhibit an upturn in resistance at low temperatures; and (iii) the 2 u.c. film exhibits insulator-like behavior. These results indicate that the critical thickness for localization, which was originally 3 u.c. for uncapped films, was shifted down to 2 u.c. by the STO capping. This behavior is attributed to the STO capping layer reducing intrinsic disorder within the SIO_m layer, thereby suppressing carrier localization. Consequently, both the SIO_i and SIO_m series exhibit a decrease in the critical thickness for localization upon STO capping, clearly demonstrating that the capping layer effectively suppresses disorder within the SIO thin films.

(b) Temperature dependencies of sheet resistance of all STO capped films. Temperature dependencies of normalized R/R0 with the thickness range of (c) 9–20 u.c., (d) 3–8 u.c., and (e) 2 u.c.

Conclusions

In summary, we have systematically analyzed the transport properties of ultrathin SIO films and the impact of STO capping on their electronic states. Our study distinguishes between disorder-driven Anderson localization in SIO_i films (critical thickness ~ 7 u.c.) and bandwidth-controlled localization in SIO_m films (critical thickness ~ 3 u.c.). The introduction of an STO capping layer dramatically modified these transport characteristics, shifting the localization threshold of SIO_i films from 7 u.c. to below 3 u.c. and shifting that of SIO_m films from 3 u.c. to 2 u.c. Through comparative analysis of transport data and structural characterization using HRXRD, we concluded that the STO capping layer induces a structural modification that heals the intrinsic disorder within the SIO lattice. Our findings highlight the critical role of interface engineering in controlling disorder and manipulating the ground states of correlated oxide thin films in the atomic limit.

Methods

Epitaxial SIO thin films were grown on STO substrates using pulsed laser deposition (PLD). Depending on the growth conditions, the SIO films exhibited either insulator-like or metal-like transport behavior20. The oxygen pressure during deposition was fixed at 75 mTorr. The insulator-like SIO was deposited at a growth temperature of 700 °C with a laser fluence of 2.0 J/cm², while the metal-like SIO was deposited at 600 °C with a laser fluence of 1.5 J/cm². The STO capping layer was deposited in situ immediately after the SIO growth, with a thickness of 20 unit cells (u.c.). The growth conditions for the STO capping layer were identical to those used for the underlying SIO film deposition. All film thicknesses were precisely controlled at the unit cell level using RHEED oscillations. The surface of the SrIrO3 target used for laser deposition tends to become Ir-rich due to the intense pulsed laser ablation31,32. To mitigate this effect, the target surface was polished prior to deposition. After deposition, the films were post-annealed for 30 min under the same growth temperature and oxygen pressure conditions to minimize oxygen vacancies. Electrical contacts to the SIO thin films were made using aluminum wire wedge bonding and silver paste. Resistance measurements were carried out using a standard four-probe method. The temperature-dependent resistance was measured from 30 K to 300 K using a closed-cycle cryostat system. High-resolution X-ray diffraction (HRXRD) θ–2θ scans and reciprocal space mapping (RSM) were performed using a RIGAKU SmartLab diffractometer equipped with a Cu-Kα₁ source.
