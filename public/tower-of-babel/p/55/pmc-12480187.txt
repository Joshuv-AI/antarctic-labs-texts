Epidemiology and Course of Clostridioides difficile Infections in Germany: Retrospective Analysis of German Health Claims Data
Authors: Anna Seiffert, Sebastian Noack, Sebastian Wingen-Heimann, Julia Puschmann, Nils-Olaf Hübner, Jonas Krampe, Christoph Lübbert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionClostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is the leading cause of healthcare-associated infectious diarrhea, with recurrence rates of 15–20% after standard treatment and ≥ 30% after a second relapse. In Germany, reliable epidemiological data remain limited.MethodsA retrospective claims data analysis of the period 2017–2022 was performed using the German Analysis Database for Evaluation and Health Services Research (DADB), which covers 4.1 million insured individuals. Incident CDI cases, recurrent cases, and mortality were assessed and stratified by diagnosis setting. A propensity-score-matched control group without CDI adjusted for age, sex, and comorbidities was created. Cox proportional hazards models were used to determine mortality risk factors.ResultsCDI incidence decreased from 119 to 66 per 100,000 (2017–2022). First-recurrence rate declined from 15% to 11% and second-recurrence rate from 20% to 15%. Of all recurrences, 43% were managed in the outpatient setting. In 2022, severe CDI accounted for 38% of extrapolated statutory health insurance (SHI) cases. Metronidazole use in outpatients decreased from 79% to 53% for incident cases, while vancomycin prescriptions increased from 18% to 39%. In 2022, 72% of first CDI recurrences were treated with vancomycin, 20% with metronidazole, and 2% with fidaxomicin. Despite guideline recommendations, only 8% of patients with a second recurrence received fidaxomicin. In 2021, the 30-day mortality rate for secondary inpatient CDI cases was 20%, compared with 8% for primary inpatient cases and 4% for outpatient cases. Corresponding 1-year mortality rates were 44%, 32%, and 16%. In patients with CDI, 1-year mortality was 1.9 to 2.1 times higher than in controls (p < 0.001), with advanced age (≥ 65 years) being the strongest predictor (hazard ratio [HR] 12.21; 95% confidence interval [CI] 10.91–13.67).ConclusionsDespite declining incidence and recurrence rates, CDI remains a major health burden in Germany, especially for older adults. High severity, limited adherence to treatment guidelines, and excess mortality underscore the need for targeted prevention, individualized therapy, and improved guideline implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-025-01223-1.

Introduction

Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is one of the leading causes of healthcare-associated infections worldwide, contributing significantly to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [1–3]. Over the past three decades, CDI has become one of the most common causes of infectious diarrhea in hospitalized patients, with a growing occurrence in outpatient settings [4, 5].

CDI typically arises when antibiotic use disrupts the gut microbiome, creating an environment for toxin-producing C. difficile to proliferate. This anaerobic, spore-forming bacterium can cause a spectrum of symptoms, from mild, self-limiting diarrhea to severe life-threatening conditions such as toxic megacolon, septic shock, and death [6]. Known risk factors for both CDI and recurrence include advanced age (≥ 65 years), hospitalization, multiple comorbidities, proton pump inhibitor use, and impairment of the immune system [7, 8]. Recurrence of the infection remains a significant challenge, with a rate of 15–35% of all CDI cases [9]. CDI treatment is associated with prolonged hospitalization, increased antibiotic use, a greater need for blood transfusions, and a higher likelihood of intensive care unit admission [10]. Targeted antibiotic therapies, such as the use of metronidazole and vancomycin, are associated with higher recurrence rates compared to newer treatments such as fidaxomicin and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) [11, 12]. International and German guidelines have consequently revised their recommendations, removing metronidazole as first-line treatment [13–15].

In Germany, CDI accounts for approximately 10% of healthcare-associated infections. Its incidence peaked in 2013 at over 137 hospitalized cases per 100,000 population before declining to 81 per 100,000 in 2019 [16, 17]. The emergence of hypervirulent strains, such as ribotype 027, contributed to the increased incidence of CDI [18]. However, infection rates have subsequently decreased, likely due to enhanced infection control measures, improved antibiotic stewardship (ABS) interventions, and reduced prevalence of hypervirulent strains [19, 20]. Despite the recent decline, the national CDI incidence in Germany remains higher than in the USA and continues to impose a significant burden, particularly for older adults and immunocompromised individuals [16]. In 2019, the incidence of hospitalized patients with CDI in Germany was 98 per 100,000 population, compared with 58 per 100,000 in the USA. Moreover, CDI is officially classified as an urgent public health threat by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), further underscoring the importance of continued surveillance and prevention efforts in Germany [6, 16]. Ongoing research into risk factors, antibiotic stewardship, and novel therapeutic options remains essential to further reduce CDI incidence and improve patient outcomes. Furthermore, the documentation of CDI-related diarrheal diseases varies despite the legal obligation to report severe cases under the German Infection Protection Act (Infektionsschutzgesetz [IfSG]) [21] and the criteria set by the Robert Koch Institute (RKI) [22].

This paper aims to estimate the current incidence of CDI, including severe cases and recurrence rates, to identify the therapies used in a real-world setting and to investigate risk factors associated with mortality among adult patients with CDI in Germany, based on a retrospective analysis of health claims data.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This noninterventional, longitudinal, retrospective cohort study used anonymized German claims data from the German Analysis Database for Evaluation and Health Services Research (DADB)—managed by Gesundheitsforen Leipzig GmbH—to examine patients with CDI with regard to the incidence and recurrence rates, mortality, and severe CDI progression. The DADB includes data from 15 statutory health insurers (SHI) from 2013 to 2022, representing approximately 4.1 million individuals, and is representative of the German population in terms of age, sex, and morbidity. To interpret the study results for the entire SHI population, data from the DADB were extrapolated to the broader German SHI system. Since the DADB covers approximately 5% of the German SHI population, adjustments for age and sex were applied to enhance the representativeness of the findings. A detailed overview of the extrapolation strategy can be found in the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM; Appendix S1). Study Population and Case Identification

The study period spanned from 1 January 2017 to 31 December 2022. Inclusion criteria encompassed adult patients, defined as individuals aged at least 18 years in the calendar year of the index date, who had an incident CDI case. CDI cases were considered “incident” if there was no CDI diagnosis (ICD-10-GM code A04.7- or U69.4) and treatment (outpatient prescriptions of fidaxomicin, metronidazole, teicoplanin, vancomycin, or bezlotoxumab) in the 60 days prior to the index date. Previous German claims data studies have repeatedly employed corresponding case and index date definitions [17, 23]. Following the approach taken by Lübbert et al. [23], a CDI case was considered recurrent if a new CDI case occurred within 11–60 days after the index event (or a recurrence). In addition to the CDI diagnosis ICD-10-GM code A04.7, the case definition was expanded to include the diagnosis of recurrence ICD-10-GM code U69.4-. CDI events occurring within the first 10 days of follow-up were not classified as recurrences, as standard CDI treatment lasts for 10 days. Subsequently, a CDI case occurring more than 60 days after the index date was not classified as a recurrent event.

Study outcome measures based on these definitions included incidence and recurrence rates stratified by the setting of the CDI diagnosis (outpatient, inpatient with CDI as primary or secondary diagnosis). Incidence rates were calculated as number of cases divided by the annual number of adult and fully observable insurants in the respective year and were reported as the number of incident CDI cases per 100,000 individuals.

Mortality and Associated Risk Factors

For incident patients with CDI, 30-day and 1-year all-cause-mortality rates were calculated. Only patients who were fully observable (i.e., continuously insured) during the 1-year follow-up period were included in the mortality assessment. Deceased patients were not excluded from the analysis. Mortality rates were determined by dividing the number of deceased patients within the specified time frame after the index date by the total number of incident patients with CDI in the respective year. The outcomes were stratified by the year of the index event and the setting in which the incident CDI case occurred.

Control Group

To construct a control group, propensity-score matching was applied. The control group was selected from the population of fully insured adult individuals without a documented CDI diagnosis. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression based on age, sex, year of the incident case, and comorbidities included in the Charlson Comorbidity Index. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was performed. Matching quality was assessed by comparing standardized mean differences before and after matching. The 1-year all-cause mortality rate in the control group was calculated by dividing the number of deceased individuals in the subsequent year by the total number of individuals in the control group.

Risk Analysis and Statistics

To investigate the relationship between various risk factors and 30-day and 1-year mortality, respectively, a Cox proportional hazards model was employed. The dependent variable in this analysis was mortality within 30 days or 1 year from the index date. The following variables were considered as potential risk factors of mortality: advanced age (≥ 65 years), inpatient setting of the CDI case, CDI as the primary inpatient diagnosis, treatment in the ICU, colectomy within 10 days after the index date, and comorbidities such as diabetes and renal failure (primary or secondary inpatient diagnosis or verified outpatient diagnosis with ICD-10-GM code E11.- for diabetes type 2 or N17.-, N18.-, or N19.- for renal failure) in the year preceding index date. The results from the Cox model are reported as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Variables with a statistically significant HR greater than one were classified as mortality risk factors. Analogous analysis was performed for on-set CDI cases. The focus of the analysis was on the impact of drug treatment on the mortality rate.

Statistical significance was determined using a p-value smaller than 0.05. As the analysis was based on health insurance claims data, the issue of missing data did not arise. Only fully coded and reimbursed services are included in the dataset. Thus, all variables used in the analysis were complete by definition. All analyses were performed using Microsoft SQL Server and R, version 4.4.1.

Ethical Considerations

This study used aggregated and anonymized health claims data and therefore did not require approval from institutional review boards or ethical committees or informed consent from patients. The study was conducted in accordance with legal and regulatory requirements and research practices described in the revised German guidelines and recommendations for ensuring Good Epidemiological Practice issued by the German Society for Epidemiology [24].

Results

Table 1 presents the number of identified prevalent and incident patients with CDI and cases in the study population from 2017 to 2022, along with the total adult population at risk. Over the study period, the number of prevalent patients with CDI and cases declined consistently. In 2017, the DADB recorded 1956 patients with CDI and 2479 CDI cases. By 2022, the number had decreased to 1364 patients and 1678 cases, representing a relative reduction of 30% and 32%, respectively. Similar trends were observed in the incident CDI population during the same period. Figure 2 illustrates the CDI prevalence and incidence rates extrapolated to the SHI population and stratified by diagnosis setting. Similarly to the number of patients with CDI, the prevalence and incidence rates per 100,000 individuals declined over time—from 155 and 119 in 2017 to 84 and 66 CDI cases in 2022, respectively. Most incident CDI cases occurred in the inpatient setting and were reported as secondary diagnoses, with the proportion ranging from 64% in 2018 and 58% in 2022. While the share of inpatient cases declined, outpatient CDI cases increased from 16% in 2017 to 22% in 2022. Among prevalent CDI cases, the shift toward outpatient care was more pronounced, with 29% of all prevalent cases identified in the outpatient setting in 2022. Table 2 presents CDI recurrence rates among incident CDI cases in the DADB, stratified by diagnosis setting and year. In 2022, 11% of incident CDI cases experienced a first recurrence with the highest rate observed in cases initially diagnosed as primary inpatients (17%). Among those with first recurrence, 15% progressed to a second recurrence. Third recurrences were observed rarely, with fewer than ten cases reported, proving insufficient for disclosure. 43% of all recurrences were managed in an outpatient setting, irrespective of the initial CDI episode’s diagnosis or treatment modality, indicating a shift in the therapeutic care setting (Fig. 3). During the analysis period, only 10–15% of first recurrent CDI cases were coded using the ICD-10-GM code U69.4 (recurrent infection with Clostridiodes difficile). CDI recurrence rates declined over the study period, particularly in the outpatient sector. In 2017, 14% of outpatient cases experienced a first recurrence. By 2022, this rate had dropped to 6%. A similar reduction in recurrence rates was also observed for primary and secondary inpatient diagnoses, although the most pronounced decline occurred in the outpatient sector. Figure 4 illustrates the changes in antibiotic treatment patterns for outpatient CDI cases between 2017 and 2022. In 2017, metronidazole was the most commonly prescribed treatment, used in 79% of incident CDI cases, 49% of first recurrences, and 23% of second recurrences. By 2022, these rates had decreased notably to 53% for incident CDI cases, 20% for first recurrences, and 17% for second recurrences. Meanwhile, the use of vancomycin increased across all event types, particularly in recurrent CDI cases. For first recurrences, it increased from 43% in 2017 to 72% in 2022 and, for second recurrences, from 54% to 75%, indicating a shift toward vancomycin as the preferred treatment of outpatient CDI cases. Fidaxomicin was rarely used in 2017, with 1% of all treated cases for first recurrences. To further quantify the clinical impact of CDI beyond the severity of individual cases, both 30-day and 1-year all-cause mortality rates were analyzed for patients with new onset CDI between 2017 and 2021, with 2022 serving as the follow-up period for mortality assessment. To contextualize these outcomes and account for potential confounding factors, control group without CDI was established. An overview of the baseline characteristics in both groups before and after matching is provided in the electronic supplementary material (Table S1). Between 2017 and 2021, a slight downward trend in the 30-day all-cause mortality rate among incident patients with CDI in the DADB was observed, reaching its lowest point of 14% in 2021 (Table ESM S2). A similar downward trend was observed in the 1-year all-cause mortality rate, which decreased from 39% in 2017 to 35% in 2021. The 1-year all-cause mortality rate in the control group remained substantially lower and ranged between 17% and 20% during the analysis period (Table ESM S3). Stratification by diagnosis setting revealed that patients with a secondary inpatient CDI diagnosis had the highest mortality rates, followed by those with a primary diagnosis. Outpatient CDI cases exhibited substantially lower mortality rates. In 2021, the 30-day mortality rate was 20% for secondary inpatient CDI diagnoses, 8% for primary inpatient diagnoses, and 4% for outpatient diagnoses (Fig. ESM S2). In addition, 1-year mortality rates followed the same pattern—44%, 32%, and 16%, respectively. Mortality rates in inpatient setting were approximately twice as high as those observed in the matched control group. Figure 5 further illustrates the 1-year mortality rates of study patients and control group individuals, depending on the number of recurrences in DADB in 2017 and 2021. In 2017, 39% of patients with CDI without recurrences died within 1 year, compared with 18% in the control group. By 2021, mortality in this group had slightly decreased to 34%, while the control group remained stable at 18%. Among patients with at least one recurrence, the 1-year mortality rate was 46% in 2017 and slightly increased to 49% in 2021. In the corresponding control group, mortality rates were lower but also showed a minor increase, from 21% in 2017 to 23% in 2021. A more pronounced increase was observed in patients with two or more recurrences. Discussion

The observed incidence rates indicate a consistent decline in CDI over time, aligning with recent claims data studies [17, 25] and providing updated epidemiological insights. The estimated hospital incidence is slightly lower than previously reported [6, 16], likely due to methodological differences; inpatient cases in this study could only be reliably identified upon discharge and subsequent readmission. Furthermore, under the definition of inpatient CDI cases applied in this study, readmissions within 10 days were not classified as new CDI events. It should be noted, however, that the presented data only cover the period up to 2022. According to the most recent data from the German National Reference Center for Nosocomial Infections (NRZ), a renewed 18% increase in hospital-treated CDI cases was recorded in 2024, indicating a potential reversal of the previous downward trend [25].

Our study also examined the burden of recurrent CDI. Approximately 13% of patients experienced at least one recurrence, emphasizing that recurrences remain a significant concern. The risk of a second recurrence (18%) underscores the growing burden of recurrent CDI, particularly in inpatient settings, where recurrence rates were higher. In contrast, recurrence rates were lowest in the outpatient sector, suggesting potential differences in patient characteristics or treatment approaches. These differences may, in part, be explained by the generally lower clinical severity observed in outpatient cases, as reflected by the substantially lower 30-day and 1-year mortality rates in this group. Patients treated in outpatient settings are likely to be younger, have fewer comorbidities, and present with milder disease courses, all of which may contribute to a reduced risk of recurrence compared with those treated in inpatient settings. Notably, third recurrences were relatively rare, though the limited number of cases may have affected reporting. Overall, recurrence rates were considerably lower than in the previous German claims data studies by Lübbert et al. from 2016 [23] and by Tricotel et al. published in 2024 [23]. These notably lower recurrence rates compared with earlier studies might also reflect a shift in the composition of circulating CDI ribotypes, potentially involving a decrease in hypervirulent strains. Although ribotyping data were not available in this study, this hypothesis is supported by clinical observations and warrants further investigation. From a clinical perspective, the observed trend is consistent with the milder disease courses documented, especially in outpatient settings.

Nevertheless, our analysis demonstrated that approximately 43% of all recurrences were treated in the outpatient setting. This finding aligns with the ongoing trend toward outpatient care in the German healthcare system. However, it also highlights the importance of implementing up-to-date clinical guidelines to ensure guideline-adherent treatment of CDI in all care settings. Furthermore, a thorough patient history should be obtained, as the results indicate that only a small proportion of recurrences are correctly identified as such. The limited use of specific recurrence coding suggests that recurrent CDI cases may be undercoded or inconsistently documented in routine data.

During the study period, outpatient CDI treatment patterns shifted substantially, with a notable decline in metronidazole use, particularly for recurrent cases. This decrease between 2017 and 2022 aligns with guideline recommendations discouraging metronidazole use as first-line treatment for CDI [15]. In parallel, vancomycin prescriptions increased, especially for first and second recurrences. Although the use of fidaxomicin increased slightly, its overall use remained comparatively limited. While these trends indicate a tendency toward guideline-concordant outpatient management of CDI, it is important to acknowledge a major limitation of our study: Treatment administered during inpatient stays could not be assessed. Since inpatient cases tend to be more severe, it is likely that fidaxomicin is used more frequently in this setting. Nonetheless, these findings reflect an adaptation of treatment practices in accordance with updated guidelines, favoring more effective therapies for recurrent CDI cases [13, 14].

The incidence of severe CDI cases declined from 2017 to 2022, mirroring the overall incidence observed in other studies [16]. However, the observed rates remain considerably higher than the figures reported by the RKI [26–30] and also account for approximately 38% of all annual CDI cases in the study population. This discrepancy may reflect differences in case definitions, reporting practices, or data sources. It should also be emphasized that severe cases were identified on the basis of claims data, which may contribute to the differences in reported rates. Notably, Heudorf et al. also identified signs of under-reporting in CDI-associated mortality, further highlighting potential limitations in national surveillance and documentation systems [31]. According to the RKI, potential reasons for this discrepancy include the limited acceptance of the reporting criteria, which are perceived by physicians as too complex and associated with additional administrative burden. These findings reinforce the ongoing need for close collaboration between public health authorities and hospital hygiene departments [32]. It should be noted that all deaths within the specified time horizon were included in the analysis without distinguishing the cause of death.

The occurrence of CDI was associated with a 1.9- to 2.1-fold increase in 1-year mortality compared with matched control patients, independent of the care setting. Mortality risk varied on the basis of the number of recurrences, with the highest risk observed in patients experiencing two or more episodes. These findings confirm that recurrent CDI is a strong predictor of adverse long-term outcomes. In contrast, the slight decrease in mortality among patients without recurrences suggests potential improvements in general CDI management. However, the pronounced increase in mortality among patients with multiple recurrences underscores the urgent need for effective prevention and treatment strategies. Given the low case numbers in this subgroup (fewer than ten cases per group), further studies with larger datasets are necessary to confirm these findings.

Our study findings indicate a steady decline in CDI-related mortality over the study period. However, these rates remain higher than those published by Lübbert et al. in 2016, and are in line with the more recent observations by Antunes et al. [33], who also analyzed CDI outcomes within the German healthcare system [23, 33]. In consequence, CDI remains a significant contributor to mortality, particularly among elderly individuals and patients who are hospitalized. The observed age-related mortality is consistent with previous research, reaffirming advanced age as the most critical risk factor for CDI-related death. Mortality was highest among inpatient cases, especially when CDI was documented as a secondary diagnosis. The role of comorbidities in CDI-related mortality was evident, with renal insufficiency identified as a significant risk factor.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the retrospective design and reliance on health insurance claims data may not capture all clinical nuances, which can limit the interpretability of the data. Additionally, the possibility of miscoding cannot be excluded. Transitions from an inpatient event to a subsequent inpatient recurrence can only be reliably identified if the patient was discharged and the recurrence led to a new hospital admission. Moreover, the study does not capture cases of CDI diagnosed in the outpatient setting that were not followed by treatment or hospital admission, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true incidence of CDI. It is also not possible to determine the results of a laboratory tests confirming CDI was performed prior to the assignment of the ICD-10-GM code, as laboratory results are not available in claims data. An additional limitation is the lack of microbiological data, particularly ribotyping information. The notably lower recurrence rates observed in this study compared with earlier literature could potentially be related to a decline in hypervirulent strains. However, in the absence of ribotype-specific data, this hypothesis cannot be verified and should be explored in future research. Furthermore, CDI treatments administered during hospital stays are not visible in the dataset, which may result in incomplete treatment information. The database also does not include information on the cause of death, which could lead to an overestimation of CDI-related mortality or the number of severe cases. Finally, although the database used in this study is representative of the insured population, SHI claims databases cover only approximately 5% of the total population. As a result, while many cases are captured, it is likely that not all cases are represented when compared with national public health database.

Conclusions

This study highlights a steady decline in CDI incidence and recurrence rates in Germany between 2017 and 2022. However, the disease remains a significant clinical and epidemiological burden, particularly among older adults and patients with comorbidities. Beyond the overall trend, the analysis uncovers novel insights into the epidemiological behavior of CDI. The differentiated analysis by care setting (inpatient versus outpatient) and diagnosis status offers valuable insights into the dynamics of CDI and highlights distinct epidemiological patterns. Recurrence rates were lowest in the outpatient setting, indicating possible differences in patient characteristics or treatment approaches. Nonetheless, recurrent CDI remains frequently underdiagnosed and predominantly treated in outpatient settings. This pattern reveals persistent shortcomings in coding and documentation that may hinder timely and appropriate treatment. The substantial proportions severe cases, combined with mortality observed among patients with recurrences, reinforces the urgent monitoring and the consistent application of evidence-based treatment strategies in line with the current guidelines. The observed shift in treatment patterns—marked by reduced metronidazole use and increased vancomycin prescriptions—reflects growing adherence to updated clinical guidelines. However, although the proportion of severe CDI cases declined, mortality remained high among patients with recurrences and secondary inpatient diagnoses. These findings highlight the need for targeted prevention and optimized treatment strategies, especially for high-risk groups. While claims data provide valuable population-level insights, limitations such as potential miscoding and lack of laboratory confirmation should be considered. Nevertheless, these findings reinforce the importance of ongoing surveillance and adherence to best practices to further reduce CDI incidence and improve patient outcomes.
