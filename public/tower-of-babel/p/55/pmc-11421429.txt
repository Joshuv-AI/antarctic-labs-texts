Engineering Microbial Consortia as Living Materials: Advances and Prospectives
Authors: Shuchen Wang, Yuewei Zhan, Xue Jiang, Yong Lai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The field of Engineered Living Materials (ELMs) integrates engineered living organisms into natural biomaterials to achieve diverse objectives. Multiorganism consortia, prevalent in both naturally occurring and synthetic microbial cultures, exhibit complex functionalities and interrelationships, extending the scope of what can be achieved with individual engineered bacterial strains. However, the ELMs comprising microbial consortia are still in the developmental stage. In this Review, we introduce two strategies for designing ELMs constituted of microbial consortia: a top-down strategy, which involves characterizing microbial interactions and mimicking and reconstructing natural ecosystems, and a bottom-up strategy, which entails the rational design of synthetic consortia and their assembly with material substrates to achieve user-defined functions. Next, we summarize technologies from synthetic biology that facilitate the efficient engineering of microbial consortia for performing tasks more complex than those that can be done with single bacterial strains. Finally, we discuss essential challenges and future perspectives for microbial consortia-based ELMs.

Introduction

Living materials exhibit remarkable properties such as self-organization and self-renewal; they also have the ability to sense and respond to environmental signals.1−4 These properties arise from the behavior of living cells, enabled by the genetic information in their genomes, which allows them to survive in, and adapt to, their environment by receiving, processing, and producing functional molecules in response to environmental cues. Instead of inhabiting solitary niches, microorganisms more commonly coexist as communities of species, forming microbial consortia within natural environments and biospheres.5,6 Within these consortia, the microorganisms interact with each other; their interactions promote their survival. In the course of these interactions, properties emerge that surpass the capabilities of individual organisms.5 The array of metabolic capabilities and emergent properties that multiple organisms exhibit in combination makes them more responsive to their environment than individual organisms and confers a greater range of biological adaptation and function.

Given the tempting features of living materials, we have witnessed a rapid development of the field of Engineered Living Materials (ELMs), which combines synthetic biology and materials engineering approaches to create synthetic materials that mimic the functions of living materials in nature.7−11 Initial trials for developing ELMs included the genetic engineering of biopolymers and the construction of living cell-embedded materials, for a wide range of functionalities and applications, such as healthcare, biomanufacturing, and sustainability.12−14 In typical examples of natural living materials, such as bones and wood, complex self-healing and response functions are achieved through the cooperation of specialized cells.1,2 Despite substantial advancements in the field, most of the ELMs described so far are limited to single strains. Yet, the engineering of multistrain consortia that would fully unlock the capacity of living materials remains nascent. The increasing complexity involved in engineering interactions both within consortia and between consortia and the external environment stands as a roadblock to developing ELMs for practical applications.

Two approaches can be employed in the design of microbial consortia-based ELMs: top-down and bottom-up strategies. For both approaches, specialized microbes are confined within materials for desired purposes. The top-down design focuses on studying and reconstructing existing microbial consortia. By analyzing the biological composition and interactions within natural ecosystems, scientists can reconstruct and subsequently manipulate these systems in the laboratory. This paves the way for genetic and material engineering, as well as further optimization of synthetic systems. The bottom-up design overlooks the inherent interactions within natural consortia, spatially combining members that typically do not coexist in natural environments to build synthetic consortia. Taken together, these two strategies provide routes for engineering consortia-based ELMs for various applications from bioremediation to therapeutics.

In this Review, we discuss the emerging topic of living materials grown from multistrain consortia and the engineering of microbial consortia-based ELM systems. First, we review examples and properties of living materials grown from microbial consortia found in nature, highlighting several pioneering studies that engineer these self-assembled systems. We then discuss constructing ELMs through the assembly of synthetic consortia, with trackable and user-defined properties. Further, we review the synthetic biology toolkits for engineering microbial consortia and summarize currently available techniques. Finally, we discuss the challenges and future outlook of ELMs emerging from microbial consortia.

Engineering Natural Microbial Consortia and Living Materials

Multiorganism consortia are commonly seen in nature. Compared with monospecies communities, these systems exhibit a richer array of interactions between the resident microbes and the external environment, facilitated by intricate metabolic and genetic interconnections.15−17 For thousands of years, the traditional food industry has relied on various multiorganism consortia to make flavorful products, reflecting the long history of human engineering and harnessing microbial communities.18,19 Several pioneering initiatives have been undertaken to advance the engineering of these natural consortia. In this section, we discuss the properties of several systems observed in nature and in traditional food industries, while also examining efforts to reconstruct them as living materials.

Characteristics of Living Symbionts Grown from Natural Consortia

Symbiosis refers to the cohabitation and interactions of multiple organisms within a specific ecological niche. The ubiquity of symbiosis in nature underscores the benefits of symbiotic systems over mono-organism ones. These advantages would be compelling if symbiotic systems could be harnessed in the design of ELMs.

Natural symbiosis improves the robustness of the organisms involved. For example, in the multiorganism biofilm formed by the consortium of the yeast Candida albicans (C. albicans) and the bacterium Staphylococcus epidermidis (S. epidermidis), the presence of C. albicans in the biofilm enhances the vancomycin resistance of S. epidermidis, while S. epidermidis produces an extracellular polymer that impedes the penetration of the antifungal drug fluconazole.20 As another example, lichen, a symbiosis of algae and fungi, exhibits resistance to extremely harsh environments.21,22 In fact, lichen is one of the most well-studied symbiotic systems. Comprising obligate mutualistic interactions, these communities can survive in extremely harsh environments. In large-scale lichens, the removal of member species can diminish the overall efficiency and robustness of the community.23 The ability of natural consortia to maintain long-lasting activity and functionality is sought in the field of ELMs.

Natural symbiosis is self-assembled and patterned. It was observed in a study of lichen consortia that the algae and filamentous fungi assemble spontaneously and that this physical interaction could happen without metabolic dependency.24 In the case of planktonic microbial communities, they are able to self-assemble onto complex carbon sources, promoting the productivity of the whole community by niche complementation.25 Members of a community can spontaneously form spatial structures in response to environmental variables. For example, a defined spatial distribution forms in microbial communities participating in nitrogen cycling of wetland sediments.26 These self-assembled and spatially patterned characteristics, if incorporated into ELMs, could eliminate the need for complex embedding or assembling procedures during fabrication, making it possible to deploy ELMs in situ.

Division of labor (DOL) is a common phenomenon observed in natural communities. Through the collaboration of its members, the entire consortia can broaden its functionalities and expand its outputs. For instance, a recent study demonstrated that two anaerobic bacterial species in the human gut microbiota, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Desulfovibrio piger, cross-feed through exchanges of metabolites (i.e., lactate and acetate), thus maintaining the stability of the microbial community and promoting gut health.27 Another example is drawn from the nitrification process in the soil, during which ammonia is initially oxidized to nitrite by ammonia-oxidizing microorganisms and further oxidized to nitrate by nitrite-oxidizing bacteria.28 The collaboration of the two functional groups shortens the length of a metabolic pathway, thus promoting ATP production and the efficient growth of the community. DOL strategies have been developed in metabolic engineering as a potential solution to alleviate metabolic burden; thus, DOL could be harnessed when ELMs are being designed for specific bioremediation or biocatalytic tasks.

Complex interactions are inherent within natural consortia. The study of marine sponges and their symbionts presents an intriguing case. Numerous protein–protein interactions between the sponge and its symbionts may function as a mechanism for the host to distinguish between food and resident bacteria.29,30 Additionally, within the sponge microbial community, the sharing of DNA modification and restriction systems via the transfer of mobile genetic elements protects the whole consortium from invasion by foreign DNA. These examples indicate that the interactions within consortia occur at multiple levels, extending beyond metabolic exchanges or pairwise interactions. Capturing the interactions may inspire the building of novel communication and interference systems within ELMs, enabling sophisticated programming of community behavior.

Engineering Natural Microbial Consortia as ELMs

In the case of lichen, filamentous fungi offer shelter and metabolites while green algae or cyanobacteria provide carbon sources. Several pioneering studies have demonstrated the construction of synthetic lichen as living materials by coculturing algae and fungi. For example, Li et al.31 established a synthetic lichen system based on the coculture of cyanobacteria (Nostoc) and fungi (Aspergilli), resulting in a biomass production three times greater than the monoculture of Nostoc. These synthetic systems have revealed the mechanism of microbial mutualism. The fabrication and molding of a living material based on a consortium of cellulose-producing Acetobacter and algae were also demonstrated.32 While synthetic lichens hold promise for applications in environmental sustainability, their engineering for real-world applications remains largely undeveloped.

The production of fermented foods with a variety of textures and flavors represents a historic process of, usually unknowingly, harnessing microbial consortia for desired applications. Cooperative microbial communities and abiotic substrates contribute to the distinct flavors, textures, and nutritional characteristics of products used in traditional food industries. The fermentation environment of these foods is a complex habitat, in which microbial consortia undergo adaptations to acidity, salinity, and other stressors. Also, these production habitats usually self-assemble. The pioneering study in engineering such self-assembled living materials was reported by Gilbert et al. (Figure 1a, b).33 Taking inspiration from kombucha, which is made from a symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast (SCOBY), they cocultured strains of the model yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae (S. cerevisiae) and the cellulose-producing bacterium Komagataeibacter rhaeticus (K. rhaeticus) (Figure 1a). Utilizing well-established synthetic biology toolkits,34,35 the researchers cocultured and engineered specialized cells to functionalize the bacterial cellulose (BC), so that the cells could perform various tasks (Figure 1b). Engineered enzyme secretion circuits in yeast provide catalytical functions and can alter the physical properties of the BC. The functionality of sensing and responding to external signals was achieved through an inducible yeast biosensor. Remarkably, by developing and optimizing a light-sensing system in S. cerevisiae,33 BC could be optically patterned (Figure 1b). K. rhaeticus produces copious BC, yet genetic tools to manipulate this species are still under development.36

Mycelium, the vegetative component of fungi, has outstanding mechanical properties and is biodegradable and sustainable. Mycelium has attracted considerable interest as a biomaterial that can feasibly be produced at large scale. McBee et al.37 demonstrated the manipulation of natural fungal mycelium as ELMs (Figure 1c, d). Following the workflow of mycelium production, they first developed a biocomposite that can form macroscopic structures. Metagenomic analysis identified the bacterium Pantoea agglomerans (P. agglomerans) as a robust component of this biocomposite material (Figure 1c). The fluorescence engraftment of the biocomposite required the development of genetic tools and the embedding of a P. agglomerans isolate. Notably, through quorum sensing (QS) circuits in P. agglomerans, the biocomposite can sense and respond to communication signals37 (Figure 1d).

As mentioned above, a number of remarkable studies have demonstrated the feasibility of engineering natural consortia as self-assembled ELMs, enabling sensing, responding, and DOL.33,37 There are certainly numerous other abundant resources awaiting exploration and development, each having unique characteristics and potential benefits. For example, engineered lichen with biocatalytic functions can be developed for bioremediation.38 Fermented foods, which harbor benefits for human health, can be engineered to deliver living probiotics for therapeutic applications.39

Assembling Synthetic Microbial Consortia as ELMs

The advancement of synthetic biology has broadened the scope of constructing living systems, extending from single-species to multispecies communities.40−42 Despite the initial insights gained from natural consortia, substantial obstacles, such as the lack of genetic tools for designated hosts and the recreation of the surrounding ecosystem, make the engineering of natural consortia challenging.7 Alternatively, efforts have been made to design ELMs with synthetic consortia for desired purposes, by assembling organisms whose intrinsic properties are known or for which well-established genetic tools are available.

Assembly Strategies: Embedding and Spatial Patterning

In natural ecosystems, microorganisms employ spatial patterning as a common strategy to maintain community equilibrium.19,43,44 To construct ELMs with synthetic microbial consortia, assembly processes are usually required for incorporating genetically engineered microbes into a material scaffold or interface (Figure 2a–d).

(b) 3D printing uses bioink consisting of a polymer, and living cells produce spatial patterns. (c) The proliferation of cellulose-producing bacteria in self-assembled synthetic consortia is followed by enrichment of specialized members. (d) The encapsulation of subpopulations of consortia confines subgroups while enabling the exchange of substances. (e–f) Approaches to designing synthetic consortia: (e) High-throughput characterization of synthetic consortia with droplet microfluidics or microplates. (f) Modeling methods can be used to study synthetic consortia, including ecological models and metabolic models.

Hydrogel encapsulation in bulk is a simple method to implement assembly for various applications (Figure 2a). Li et al.45 developed a photoautotrophic hydrogel for wound healing that embeds the cyanobacterium Synechococcus elongatus (S. elongatus) together with Lactococcus lactis (L. lactis) or Escherichia coli (E. coli). S. elongatus was engineered to express the sucrose symporter sucrose-phosphate synthase from E. coli, thus producing sucrose to feed chemokine-12-secreting L. lactis. With this system, function was extended after encapsulation. Zheng et al.46 encapsulated, within a multilayer hydrogel, an engineered microbial consortium that mimics the gut microbiota. The hydrogel maintains a gradient concentration of compounds for the diverse metabolic needs of the synthetic consortium. This living material was then used to characterize drug response, treatment optimization, and other effects.

Three-dimensional printing is another approach enabling versatile embedding of synthetic microbial consortia into geometric structures (Figure 2b). Connell et al.47 used 3D printing to construct spatially patterned consortia of Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) and demonstrated that S. aureus gains resistance to β-lactam antibiotics with the protection of a P. aeruginosa shell. Schaffner et al.48 3D printed a phenol-degrading strain of Pseudomonas putida and a cellulose-producing strain of Acetobacter xylinum for bioremediation through a bacteria-laden bioink. Gao et al.49 designed a synthetic consortium consisting of S. cerevisiae and Chlorella spp. for 2-phenylethanol (2-PE) production. After optimizing the circuits in S. cerevisiae for aerobic 2-PE production, Chlorella spp. were bioprinted into hydrogels for stable coculture with genetically modified S. cerevisiae. The productivity of the spatially patterned system showed an improvement of 19% compared to the free consortium, which had no patterning.

Self-assembly is an intriguing strategy for fabricating consortia as living materials (Figure 2c). Usually a BC-producing or biofilm-producing bacterium serves as a substrate to autonomously encapsulate specialized engineered strains. After autonomous assembly, a proliferation process is initiated to enrich the encapsulated bacterial strains. Birnbaum et al.50 cocultured BC-producing Gluconacetobacter hansenii with curli-engineered E. coli to form functionalized capsules. Using a similar method, Qiao et al.51 reported the fabrication of a living capsule facilitating the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease; the capsule was assembled by coculturing Gluconacetobacter xylinus and E. coli Nissle. Liu et al.52 employed Myxococcus xanthus to form a biofilm for immobilizing an acetochlor-degrading consortium, which exhibits enhanced degradation capacity.

Confining the subpopulation of a consortium within a material-based niche serves as another assembly scheme (Figure 2d). Taking inspiration from microcolonies of bacterial consortia in the soil, Wang et al.53 established microbial swarmbots that encapsulate each engineered strain within polymeric microcapsules. This encapsulation strategy enables the interchange of small molecules and proteins while confining subpopulations. Communication and DOL between the subpopulations were demonstrated in assembled consortia. On a more macroscopic scale, Jeong and Irudayaraj54 fabricated alginate hydrogel shells to encapsulate subpopulations of communities, realizing intra- and interspecies communication.

Making good use of abiotic materials and biological organisms with known functions could provide a direct route to desired outcomes.12,14,55 For instance, calcium alginate with the addition of chitosan could provide great mechanical strength as well as a defense against chemical penetration. Based on this idea, a biological self-healing system was developed by encapsulating several Bacillus species in calcium alginate microcapsules and mixing the microcapsules with a cement matrix to “heal” concrete cracks.56 The coculturing of electroactive bacteria Geobacter and Shewanella with porous IO-ITO electrode scaffolds could provide an alternative for power generation.57 Additionally, integrating cocultured photosynthetic cyanobacteria and microalgae into a thin-film paper-based biophotovoltaic cell can produce energy.58

Designing Synthetic Microbial Consortia by Screening and Modeling

Given the wide range of functions of ELMs reviewed in the previous section, our knowledge of the interaction between members of synthetic consortia remains surprisingly limited. Because our prior understanding of synthetic communities is not as great as that of natural consortia, screening platforms and modeling tools have been established to identify functional synthetic communities and predict interactions to guide the bottom-up design of ELMs.

Efforts have been made to establish a high-throughput platform to characterize the functions of multiorganism communities, offering insights and foundations for various applications (Figure 2e). Kehe et al.59 reported a platform for screening ∼100,000 multiorganism consortia in a droplet microfluidics system. With droplets grouped and merged, massively parallel synthetic communities were formed and could be tracked by optical assay. With this platform, the authors successfully identified multispecies consortia that could promote the growth of Herbaspirillum frisingense, a plant symbiont. Hsu et al.60 also took advantage of droplet microfluidics to develop the Microbial Interaction Network Inference in microdroplets (MINI-Drop). MINI-Drop combines fluorescent imaging and a computer vision algorithm to precisely study the interactions within the consortia. They used this tool to systematically investigate pairwise and higher order relationships within synthetic consortia exposed to various combinations of antibiotics, illuminating how antibiotics, temperature, and the addition of members shape community interactions. More recently, Jo et al.61 constructed a microplate-based platform for measuring the growth of consortia, providing more accessible strategies. Another study demonstrated how, apart from interactions among consortium members, synthetic communities respond to the increasing complexity of outer environments.62 These studies offer insights into the dynamics of synthetic consortia, which could support the design of ELMs. Also, high-throughput culturing techniques could be incorporated to characterize uncultured or poorly understood microbes and facilitate the bottom-up construction of microbial consortia with alternative possibilities.63−65

On the other hand, computational methods have also been developed to predict the composition and characteristics of synthetic consortia (Figure 2f). These modeling tools, which can offer deep insights into ecological mechanisms and metabolic interactions, could assist in the design of synthetic consortia.66−68 Ecological models, such as the Lotka–Volterra model and the microbe-effector model, are being used to probe the dynamics and patterns observed in ecological systems by taking different species or cells as fundamental units.66,69 The Lotka–Volterra model describes the abundance of consortia members in terms of a set of coupled first-order differential equations,70,71 while the microbe-effector model captures the biochemical and metabolic interactions within the community.72,73

Genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs), which simulate the genome-based metabolic features of an organism, have also been used in designing microbial consortia.74 For example, FLYCOP (FLexible sYnthetic Consortium OPtimization) optimizes the configurations of microbial communities for desired goals based on GEMs.75 Recent advances in the modeling of spatiotemporal metabolic networks incorporate GEMs and spatial modeling of microbial habitats, such as a framework developed by Borer et al. to predict consortia metabolism within porous soil aggregates.76 This combination of approaches provides insights into consortia behavior when organisms are configured in particular spatial and physical-specific settings, paving the way for designing consortia in material frameworks. Bayesian methods, as an alternative and simpler method for metabolic prediction, have been applied to consortia design.77 Clark et al.78 developed a pipeline for data-driven model training and the prediction of consortia growth and butyrate production by combining Bayesian inference and the Lotka–Volterra ecological model. With this model, they designed 25 communities with diverse compositions and monitored butyrate production. Automated synthetic microbial Community Designer (AutoCD), a model based on Approximate Bayesian computation with sequential Monte Carlo sampling, was developed for designing consortia consisting of two and three strains using QS and bacteriocin as tuning tools.79

Synthetic Biology Toolkits for Microbial Consortia Engineering

Chassis Engineering and Essential Genetic Components

Synthetic biology methods involve exchangeable standardized genetic parts, such as promoters, ribosome-binding sites (RBS), protein-coding sequences, and terminators, for the precise control of chassis microbes.80 Faster and cheaper DNA synthesis and sequencing are being used for the large-scale manipulation of these genetic elements. Additional technologies, such as CRISPR/Cas systems,81 TALE nucleases,82 and zinc-finger nucleases,83 have further simplified genome editing. The basic workflow of genetic engineering is described below, along with important functional parts and advanced techniques.

In the design of ELMs, synthetic consortia may include nonmodel organisms. Many nonmodel organisms have not been extensively investigated; thus, there is a shortage of usable gene-editing tools. Plasmids represent one of the most essential tools for implementing biological functions within living cells. However, only plasmids with compatible origins of replication (ORIs) can be recognized and replicated consistently in the host microbe.84 Gilbert et al.85 established a pan-microbial toolkit consisting of two modules, POSSUM (Plasmid Origins and Selection MarkerS for Undomesticated Microbes) and MACKEREL (Modular, NGS-trACKable ExpRession ELement), to establish genetic tractability in nonmodel microbes. The toolkit includes a library of 29 ORIs from both bacterial and fungal kingdoms as well as 426 bacterial promoter-RBS sequences that are trackable by flow cytometry to facilitate genetic accessibility. This ORI library has proved to be applicable to multiple nonmodel bacterial species, including both Gram-negative and Gram-positive ones (Figure 3a).

Sensing and responding are essential capabilities in implementing desirable biological functions in ELMs. Promoters, which determine downstream gene transcription and affect gene expression, are typically classified as either inducible or constitutive.86−88 Inducible promoters provide dynamic regulation of gene expression in response to specific external stimuli, whereas constitutive promoters function independently of environmental signals (Figure 3b). An integrated inducible promoter can be functionalized only when the host cell is stimulated; thus, inducible promoters can be implemented to avoid possible toxicity or burdens to the host cell. Inducible systems have been combined with ELMs for applications for decades. Engineered chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells have been combined with biomaterials to develop a stimuli-responsive drug delivery device.89 ELM-defined microparticles and nanoparticles have been designed to detect pteridine and release fluorescence signals in situ.90 By integrating engineered signal-transducing bacteria with materials like shape-memory or electroactive polymers, soft robotics can be designed to conduct “pick-and-place” operations.91 In addition, Boolean logic gates, such as AND, NAND, NOR, NOT, and ON/OFF switches can be developed by modifying the genetic regulatory array in living cells; these switches are similar to the logic gates in electronic circuits.92,93

Precise Editing and Controlling of Microbial Consortia

As basic genetic editing tools become standardized and genome information becomes more available, microbial communities can be better understood. With these advances, scientists can edit the host genomes and introduce novel genetic elements with greater efficiency and flexibility. Although the majority of microbes in the human microbiota cannot be cultivated in vitro, metagenomics, which involves sequencing DNA directly extracted from natural microbial communities, provides access to the full spectrum of genetic material. Through functional metagenomic mining, we can identify novel gene-editing tools and genetic parts, to acquire greater flexibility in manipulating microbial communities.94−97

The revolutionary CRISPR/Cas9 system and advanced genome engineering technologies based on it (i.e., base editors, prime editor, and transposases) enable precise genome manipulation, thereby facilitating the exploration of complex genetic interactions, the programmable remodeling of microbial consortium, and the enhancement of our understanding of biological systems (Figure 3c).98−100

Transposon insertion sequencing (Tn-Seq) has been used to identify essential microbial pathways and genes that are required in certain culturing conditions (Figure 3d) and is now being applied to determine genetic interactions in microorganisms. An insertion library is made first, consisting of a collection of mutants that have a transposon inserted randomly. Then, after the mutant library is cultured under certain conditions, genes not required for the specific condition will be screened out, and genes that are required will be identified by sequencing. Microfluidic droplet Tn-Seq was created to assist in the discovery of cell–cell interaction mechanisms within a microbiome population.101 CRISPRi-TnSeq was constructed to map the entire interaction network between nonessential genes and essential genes; such mapping could also be used to study the interactions between species.102 Apart from the screening of interaction networks, Tn-seq has been developed for functional toolkits in recent years. Random barcode (RB)-TnSeq introduces random barcodes that facilitate the recording of genes that are required for E. coli to communicate in a microbial consortium.103 An inducible, self-propagating transposon platform was developed that could not only trigger adaptive evolution toward various parallel environmental conditions but also track the lineage insertions as organisms evolve dynamically among evolving populations.104 ProTInSeq is a Tn-seq-derived tool that can be used to characterize bacterial proteomes, a characterization based on the recognition of open reading frames (ORFs) and the expression of selective markers.105 A database of insertion sequence (IS) elements has been constructed with a computational pipeline to characterize the sites of insertions among microbial communities.106 The authors of the study found that the IS elements drive bacterial genome diversification, can be transferred between humans, and frequently insert into accessory genes important for adaptation.

The secretion system is one of the most important mechanisms of cell-to-cell interactions. Signal peptides (SP), which facilitate the secretion of target proteins, can be assembled by molecular cloning in order to release biomolecules that react with other organisms and regulatory factors (Figure 3e).107 Synthetic biology techniques enable the addition of SP-coding sequences at the N-terminal of target proteins so that they are recognized as the ones to be secreted. It was reported that the SP-guided carrier protein could be mutated to achieve a higher level of protein secretion compared to the wild-type protein.108 Moreover, a modular element called Retained Endoplasmic Cleavable Secretion (RELEASE) was found to strengthen the control of protein secretion. For nonmodel microbes, a machine-learning model named SignalP 6.0 has facilitated the prediction of genome-wide SPs, thus enhancing the communication network between species for which we lack engineering toolkits.109 Glass and Riedel-Kruse110 established a genetically encoded cell–cell adhesion platform for multicellular self-assembly. This platform is based on the fusion of an adhesin and an autotransporter intimin N-terminus, which includes a short N-terminal SP that directs protein trafficking to the periplasm. Through the directed cell–cell adhesion system, it will be feasible to construct a desired structure only by manipulating the genetic sequences.

The development of in situ genetic editing tools is vital for advancing our understanding of microbial ecology and applying it to various fields, such as bioremediation, biotechnology, and medicine. Conventional genetic manipulation techniques are often not applicable to microbes that cannot be cultivated in the laboratory. In situ genetic editing tools, on the other hand, allow researchers to modify the genetic material of microbes within their natural environment. Metagenomic Alteration of Gut microbiome by In situ Conjugation (MAGIC), developed by Ronda et al.,111 is a platform that can genetically modify gut microbiota in situ. This platform is based on community-level horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from an engineered donor species armed with a transposase cassette (Figure 3f). The Environmental Transformation sequencing (ET-seq) and DNA-editing all-in-one RNA-guided CRISPR–Cas transposase (DART) were developed for species- and site-specific editing of several nonmodel bacteria.112 ET-seq facilitates the mapping and quantification of specific microbes within a complex microbial community. The mapped microbes carrying an integrated ET-seq tractable transposon will then be applied with the DART system inserting targeted DNA, enabling genetic manipulation within a microbial consortium that is specific both to the organism and to the genetic locus.112 These platforms enable the genetic modification of microbiota within their native environment, addressing the challenge of reconstructing microbial consortia in laboratory settings, and provide us with alternatives to modifying the whole microbiota. Thus, it is possible to manipulate an entire microbial community and maintain its desired functions. It will also become feasible to introduce genetic payloads and modify genes in situ, even in an ELM with a complex spatial structure.

Division of Labor and Microbial Communication

One strategy of DOL is metabolic division of labor (MDOL), meaning the partitioning of a metabolic pathway into several sequential metabolic tasks that are performed by distinct bacterial populations in a collaborative relationship (Figure 3g).113 Synthetic biology tools can be used to construct a microbiota cellular factory that consists of cells with different specializations. For example, an E. coli–E. coli community has been developed for cis, cis-muconic acid (MA) production, in which the stability of the cooperation is weakened by competition for the carbon source.114 By controlling the initial inoculation ratio, the production of a metabolic intermediate was confined so that the final MA productivity was increased. Moreover, in a community consisting of four Pseudomonas stutzeri strains that each degrades various intermediates in a naphthalene degradation pathway, the synthetic microbiota was stabilized by resource allocation among the community members.115 By limiting the growth of the “cheater” strain in the last step as “personal profit”, the community was made more stable.

Nevertheless, engineered communities may be destabilized not only by cheaters but also by the crosstalk of pathways, the competition for intermediates, and the loss of molecules during transportation. Thus, multiple signaling and controlling systems have been developed to maintain the stability and adaptability of systems in which there is a DOL.40 The QS system can be integrated to monitor certain microbial consortia in real time in situ. Feedback-controlling modules, such as an optogenetic controller, could also be introduced to confine the growth of a given species within a microbial community.116 The fitness of the synthetic microbiota can be modified by the expression of toxins and antimicrobial peptides for limiting the growth of certain species.79,117−119 In a three-strain system, each bacterial strain produces its own toxin–antitoxin pair and an antitoxin of the following strain so that each bacterial strain could kill or be killed by one of the other two strains, thus creating a “rock–paper–scissors” relationship (Figure 3h).120 The Lux-AHL QS system controlling self-limiting synchronized lysis can facilitate the balancing of the synthetic consortium (Figure 3h).120 A programmed lysis system (PLS) was developed to lyse E. coli with a regulatory switch, through which the metabolic pathway could be changed from poly(lactate-co-3-hydroxybutyrate) (PLH) synthesis to its release, thereby largely enhancing butyrate production.121 Certain spatial arrangements of different subpopulations were also reported to increase community stability.122 Additionally, metabolic pathway optimization by nutrition relocation is beneficial to maintaining a stable microbial system.115

Synthetic Patterning

Apart from 3D printing technology for building up material structures, synthetic biology provides a suite of tools for generating patterns. There are mainly two types of patterning mechanisms used in synthetic biology: self-organization and induced organization.

Self-Organization

Since the early 2000s, scientists have programmed cells to form patterns based on cell–cell communication and intracellular signal processing. For example, ringlike patterns were formed by using a group of microbes.123−125 Neural-like computing can be integrated into microbial consortia for the recognition of structural patterns.126 Engineered spatial patterns have been generated from simple no-diffusion control to a more complex multidiffusor interacting system that depends on cellular computation.124 For instance, self-organization, as one of the synthetic patterning techniques, was established through building up intercellular signaling circuits so that an out-of-phase striped pattern could be automatically formed (Figure 3i).127 The out-of-phase patterns were regulated by sensing the cell density through QS and toxin–antitoxin pairs.

Induced Organization

Another strategy for patterning involves integrating inducible systems, such as photoreceptor promoters that respond to light. E. coli, which is not naturally responsive to light, was the first microbe to be developed to sense light and produce pigments at exposed regions. By integrating light-sensing modules, E. coli was induced to form “printed” patterns with spatial and temporal reactivity.128 Recently, light-sensing components were integrated into a cellulose-producing bacterium, Komagataeibacter rhaeticus (K. rhaeticus). When light is shined onto growing cultures, the engineered K. rhaeticus enables the conversion of tyrosine into eumelanin, the typical black pigment.129 The resulting patterned and colored biomaterial provides a vegan, biodegradable alternative to leather.129

Directed Evolution of Microbial Consortia

Microbial consortia, as collections of living microorganisms in a defined environment, can evolve or shift their composition when they encounter environmental changes, which allows them to maintain a stable state.130 The directed evolution of microbial consortia is a feasible way to establish a synthetic microbiota with desirable functions and potential (Figure 3j). Multiple strategies have been developed to direct the evolution of microbiota, including “bottleneck,” “species knock-in,” “species knockout,” “migration from pool,” “coalescence,” and “resource shift” strategies with artificial selection for high-performing communities.131 Moreover, it was reported that a combination of more than one of the strategies is even more suitable for large-scale top-down directed evolution.115 Evolutionary microbiota selection is one of the methods for manufacturing ELM microbiota.

Challenges and Future Prospectives

Understanding the Interactions

The implementation of ELMs grown from microbial consortia depends on understanding multiple types of interactions. These interactions occur at several levels: there are interactions among community members, between consortia and nonbiological components, and between the overall ELMs and the external environment. Although lots of technologies and platforms for studying consortia interactions have been reported, as briefly reviewed in the previous section, they are usually limited to simple experimental settings, for example, nutrient-rich or well-defined liquid medium. As living materials come to be used for a wide range of purposes, the growth environment is likely to become more complex and less predictable. Case-specific characterization of interactions may be necessary for gathering considerable information to facilitate mimicking natural consortia or to build synthetic consortia de novo. For instance, Ponomarova et al.132 reveal the symbiotic mechanism between yeast and lactic acid bacteria through a combination of model prediction, metabolic analysis, transcriptome analysis, and genetic engineering. Similarly, the interactions and division of spatiotemporal niches were inspected in kefir, and it was found that metabolic cooperation helps members to colonize this ecosystem.133 In this case, Acetobacter is not an initial colonizer of kefir but grows later with the support of lactate and other metabolites as they are produced by L. lactis. Thus, conversely, failing to recognize a crucial interaction could result in failure to construct consortia within ELMs.134

Biosafety and Biocontainment Concerns

Most ELM development is currently being conducted in laboratory settings; however, biosafety concerns cannot be avoided when ELMs containing genetically engineered microbes are deployed in real-world applications. The unintended spread of transgenes into the environment has been reported.135,136 The spread of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) among the trans-genetic materials is particularly concerning, as it could lead to the acquisition of antibiotic resistance by pathogenic strains, posing a threat to human health.137 Biochemical strategies have been developed to control the proliferation of engineered strains and prevent the unintended spreading of genetic materials; these strategies include auxotrophy, genetic kill switches, and firewalls.88,138−140 Physical compartmentalization, which has particular significance for the field of ELMs, has also been demonstrated. Tang et al.141 encapsulated engineered strains into tough hydrogel shells, achieving robust biocontainment while retaining functionalities. For biocontainment in multiorganism consortia, synthetic dependency can be engineered to facilitate the stable and controllable propagation of consortia. It is also possible to include essential compounds in the abiotic substrate of ELMs to support the growth of engineered auxotrophy consortia. We anticipate that the combination of multilayer biochemical and physical biocontainment approaches will help address biosafety concerns as ELMs progress from the laboratory to real-world applications.

Expanding the Lifespan and Sustainability of Consortia-Based ELMs

Ensuring the functionality of ELMs and their microbial consortia in desired environments poses a number of challenges. The viability of the consortia within the ELMs needs to be maintained for long-term performance. When embedded in abiotic materials, the constituent cells could face nutrient deficiency, desiccation, and other application-specific unfavorable conditions. Previous examples have demonstrated that phototrophic organisms can be incorporated to extend the lifespan of ELMs and even support the growth of other organisms. Biofilm and spore formation are other ways that organisms within ELMs overcome extremely harsh environments; yet, achieving precise control over these processes remains a challenge.

Another potential strategy is engineering metabolic engraftment systems in microbial chassis.142 Through the implementation of polysaccharide utilization loci (PUL), it was possible to stably engraft in the mouse gut a Bacteroides strain encoding this rare gene cluster, which privileged the utilization of a specific nutrient (porphyran).142 This example can serve as a reference in developing ELMs, as PUL can be integrated into microbial chassis, allowing associated nutrients to be provided within the abiotic substrate to enhance viability in complex external environments. Notably, engineering ELMs from consortia and materials from the traditional food industry may make it possible to circumvent this issue, since the nutrients and compounds supporting the growth of corresponding consortia have already been included. For multiorganism consortia, coordinating populations across different time scales is also desirable for various applications. Methods include QS, bacteriocin expression, programmable circuits, and metabolic exchange.79,121 Scaling up and applying these systems to other chassis in various interfaces will expand the scope of accurately controlling the lifespan of ELMs.

Conclusions and Outlook

Sparked by the powerful functionalities and beneficial features of microbial consortia and symbioses in nature, efforts have been made to develop ELMs consisting of multiorganism consortia, which are anticipated to perform better than traditional ELMs. Synthetic biology tools, originally developed for engineering and functionalizing microbial communities, can be applied to program and increase the complexity of ELMs. Several shared and case-specific challenges impede the future application of these systems and remain to be overcome through multidisciplinary efforts. Previous efforts have demonstrated two successful workflows for integrating model strains or developing local members for functionalized living materials.33,37 The top-down and bottom-up approaches can complement each other as these systems are being designed. The top-down design harnesses the robustness, self-assembly properties, and intrinsic interactions of natural symbioses. However, challenges persist in recreating and simplifying the systems for massive production, as well as in choosing appropriate chassis for genetic engineering. The bottom-up approach provides tunable microbial consortia tailored to specific goals, enabling a balance between system functionality and implementation complexity. A combination of both top-down and bottom-up methods may prove to be most effective in designing next-generation ELMs with integrated capabilities, including self-assembly, autonomous deployment, and case-specific functions based on engineered microbial communities.
