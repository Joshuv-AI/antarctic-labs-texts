Research on the flow properties of fracturing fluids through nozzles during the flowback process in oil wells
Authors: Le Tong, Kang Yang, Yulong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptimizing flowback control through nozzle sizing is critical for preserving hydraulic fractures and maximizing post-fracturing production. This study develops a physics-based fluid-dynamics model integrating the energy-conservation and continuity equations with actual wellhead pipeline configurations to predict flowrate and velocity of fracturing fluid through nozzles during early flowback. Validated rigorously against field data from Well GLX, the model demonstrated exceptional accuracy, with a maximum error of only 2.2% between calculated and measured flowrates across multiple operational stages involving different nozzle sizes (e.g., 2 + 2 mm and 2 + 3 mm). Key quantified findings reveal: flowrate is predominantly governed by nozzle size (sensitivity ranking: nozzle size > wellhead pressure > fluid density > local resistance coefficient), while velocity is primarily driven by wellhead pressure. Finite element analysis visualized the abrupt pressure drop and velocity surge at the nozzle throat, consistent with Bernoulli’s principle. Crucially, discrepancies between model predictions and field measurements serve as reliable indicators of nozzle erosion damage or formation permeability issues. This robustly validated model provides a practical tool for real-time nozzle condition diagnosis and flowback optimization in field operations, enhancing reservoir protection and production stability.Keywords: Flowback, Nozzle size, Flowrate, Sensitivity analysis, Finite element analysisSubject terms: Fluid dynamics, Crude oil, Applied mathematics

Introduction

Hydraulic fracturing is a cornerstone technology for enhancing the recovery of oil, gas, and geothermal resources from low-permeability formations, particularly enabling the commercial development of unconventional reservoirs like shale oil and gas1,2. The process involves injecting high-pressure fluid (typically water, sand, and chemical additives) to create fractures in the subsurface rock, significantly increasing reservoir contact and productivity. Following this stimulation treatment, the flowback phase is critical: it entails the controlled recovery of the injected fracturing fluid from the formation back to the surface through the wellbore. The hydraulic fracturing flowback process in oil and gas fields is shown in Fig. 1, which mainly includes the fluid flow in the formation, the pipe flow in the wellbore, and the throttling flow in the oil nozzle. Effective flowback control is paramount to operational success. Inappropriately managed flowback rates, often regulated at the wellhead using nozzles, can lead to detrimental consequences. Rapid or uncontrolled flowback can cause pressure fluctuations sufficient to damage the newly created fracture network or the reservoir matrix itself, dislodge proppant, overwhelm surface equipment, or even lead to sand production and equipment erosion3. Conversely, overly restricted flowback can unnecessarily prolong operations and delay production. Therefore, accurately predicting and controlling the flowrate through the wellhead nozzle, based on wellhead pressure and nozzle size, is essential for protecting reservoir integrity, ensuring equipment safety, and optimizing hydrocarbon production post-fracturing.

The backflow process of hydraulic fracturing.

The wellhead nozzle serves as the primary control element for flowback, acting as a throttling device to manage formation energy depletion and stabilize flowrates3,4. Accurately modeling the fluid flow through this nozzle is fundamental to achieving effective control. While nozzle flow modeling dates back to the 1990 s, early approaches relied heavily on empirical correlations for single-phase (liquid or gas) and two-phase flow, such as the FORTUNATI5Gilbert6and Ashford-Pierce7 models. Although these empirical models are widely used, their calculation process is relatively complex and difficult to meet the requirements for rapid calculation at the oilfield site. Theoretical foundations offer a more robust basis. The Bernoulli equation, derived from fundamental conservation principles (energy and mass), has proven highly effective and broadly applicable for modeling single-phase incompressible flows in various engineering contexts8. For complex multi-phase flows, advanced computational techniques like the Volume of Fluid (VOF), Mixture, or Eulerian multiphase models have been developed and validated9,10. However, applying these complex multiphase models universally can be computationally intensive and may not be necessary or practical for all field scenarios, especially during the initial flowback stages. To address these gaps, this study develops a physics-based model for early flowback, where fluid behavior (low-polymer slickwater, near-Newtonian viscosity) justifies simplifying assumptions. Integrating the Bernoulli equation and continuity principles with actual pipeline configurations, the model predicts flowrate/velocity from wellhead pressure and nozzle size. Objectives include diagnosing nozzle erosion via theory-field deviations and optimizing flowback control to minimize formation damage11–13.

This study focuses specifically on the early flowback stage after hydraulic fracturing, where the returning fluid is predominantly the original fracturing fluid (e.g., slickwater systems with low polymer concentration). Building on the fundamental Bernoulli principle and continuity equation, this research establishes a practical, physics-based mathematical model for simulating the flow of fracturing fluid through wellhead nozzles during the early flowback phase. The model is explicitly tailored to the actual pipeline configurations and operational constraints encountered in oilfield settings. This model, validated against field data and analyzed using sensitivity studies and finite element simulations, addresses a critical gap in providing a fast, accurate, and theoretically sound method for nozzle flow management during the crucial flowback operation.

Mathematical model

Justification of model assumptions

This study focuses specifically on the early flowback stage after hydraulic fracturing, where the returning fluid is predominantly the original fracturing fluid (e.g., slickwater systems with low polymer concentration). Under these conditions, the fluid exhibits near-Newtonian behavior (viscosity largely shear-independent) as confirmed by rheological observations: shear stress remains linearly proportional to shear rate due to low polymer concentrations (< 0.1%) and low shear rates. Additionally, the flowback fluid can be reasonably approximated as incompressible because it has extremely low compressibility, and operational pressure/temperature variations are limited. Field validation has shown that simplified calculation models with an error requirement of less than 5% usually meet the needs of the mining site. This simplification greatly improves numerical efficiency and stability.

The reservoir in this block is primarily composed of shale. Formation water is of the sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) type, with an average pH of about 7.6. The main fracturing interval was approximately 4192.5 m to 4214 m. Despite the reservoir’s poor initial petrophysical properties, the well demonstrated excellent production performance after fracturing. It is considered a representative case study for this block due to its successful post-stimulation results.

Discussion

Flow parameter sensitivity analysis

Based on the calculation results presented in Figs. 11, 12, 13 and 14, the following conclusions can be drawn. Under the same parameters, the nozzle diameter has a weak influence on the velocity and is very sensitive to the flowrate. With the increase of nozzle size, the velocity changes slightly, but the flowrate increases significantly. Under the same parameters, the influence of local resistance coefficient on velocity and flowrate is weak. With the increase of local resistance coefficient, the flow velocity and discharge decrease slightly. Under the same parameters, the velocity and flowrate increase with the increase of wellhead pressure. Under the same parameters, with the increase of the fluid density, the velocity and flowrate decrease slightly. After passing through the nozzle, the order of sensitivity of fluid flowrate to influencing parameters is, nozzle size > wellhead pressure > fluid density > local resistance coefficient. The order of sensitivity of fluid velocity to influencing parameters is, wellhead pressure > fluid density > local resistance coefficient > nozzle size. To control and stabilize the wellhead flowrate during production, it is essential to manage both the wellhead pressure and nozzle size. Major adjustments to these parameters should be made carefully and gradually, as they can significantly affect the flowrate and require close monitoring to maintain stability.

Flow parameter sensitivity analysis

Figure 15 demonstrates that the following conclusions can be drawn. The simulation results demonstrate that increasing the oil nozzle radius from 1 mm to 6 mm induces localized velocity variations at the nozzle while leaving flow velocities in upstream/downstream surface pipelines largely unaffected. This phenomenon arises from two factors. Pipeline Geometry Stability: The cross-sectional area of surface pipelines remains nearly constant along their length, with minimal geometric variations compared to the nozzle. Under steady flowrate and pressure conditions, this stability suppresses significant velocity fluctuations in non-nozzle regions. Nozzle-Specific Dynamics: The nozzle acts as a critical flow-restricting element. As its radius expands, the cross-sectional area increases quadratically (A = πr²), causing a sharp velocity reduction at the nozzle due to the inverse proportionality between velocity and cross-sectional area (q = Av). For instance, at a 1 mm radius, the velocity peaks due to the minimal flow area, whereas at 6 mm, the velocity drops substantially as the fluid encounters reduced resistance. This behavior mirrors the function of a back-pressure valve, where nozzle sizing directly regulates formation flowrate by modulating localized velocity gradients.

Venturi effect in flow control

The pronounced velocity gradient at the nozzle stems from the interplay between continuity (mass conservation) and energy conservation principles. The continuity equation dictates that velocity inversely scales with the cross-sectional area. For a nozzle radius increasing from 1 mm (A = 3.14 mm2) to 6 mm (A = 113.1 mm2), the 36-fold area expansion forces velocity to decrease proportionally to maintain constant volumetric flowrate. This abrupt deceleration generates shear layers and recirculation zones downstream of the nozzle, which were likely resolved in the simulation through turbulence models (e.g., k-ε). These transient flow features, though not explicitly shown in the figure, contribute to localized energy dissipation and minor pressure recovery post-nozzle. The similarity between nozzle behavior and a back-pressure valve lies in their shared ability to impose flow resistance. However, unlike adjustable valves, fixed-geometry nozzles exhibit a deterministic pressure-velocity relationship governed by their radius. For fracturing operations, selecting nozzle radius involves a trade-off. For small radii (1–3 mm), enhance throttling efficiency but risk damage at high. For large radii (4–6 mm), reduce throttling effects but mitigate erosion and vibration. This highlights the need for transient simulations to evaluate nozzle sizing under dynamic wellhead pressures.

Nozzle erosion mechanics

Based on the results presented in Fig. 16, the following conclusions can be drawn. The pressure variation amplitude in surface pipelines is small. In most areas of surface pipelines, due to the relatively constant diameter of the pipeline and the relatively stable fluid flow, the amplitude of pressure changes is relatively small. Unless there are other significant pressure loss factors in the pipeline (such as bends, valves, friction, etc.), pressure changes in the pipeline are usually relatively gentle. The pressure changes significantly at the position of the oil nozzle. When the fluid passes through the nozzle, the flow velocity will undergo significant changes due to changes in the cross-sectional area of the nozzle. Especially when the radius of the nozzle is small, the flow velocity increases more, resulting in a greater decrease in pressure. As the radius of the nozzle increases, although the magnitude of the increase in flow velocity decreases, the pressure change at the nozzle is still the most significant compared to other parts of the pipeline. Due to the increase in flow velocity when the fluid passes through the nozzle, the pressure at the nozzle significantly decreases. However, the impact force of the fluid on the nozzle wall is relatively high. An increase in flow velocity means that the impact force of the fluid on the nozzle wall will also increase, which may result in greater mechanical stress on the nozzle wall. Long term exposure to high-speed fluid impact may cause fatigue in the nozzle material, thereby reducing its strength and durability. When the actual flowrate exceeds the theoretical flowrate, it can be determined that the oil nozzle is damaged and needs to be replaced in a timely manner. The significant pressure reduction at the nozzle (Fig. 16) aligns with the Bernoulli equation. Assuming horizontal flow (h = constant), the velocity increase at the nozzle (vnozzle ≫ vpipe) converts static pressure into kinetic energy. However, high-velocity fluid impingement induces two damage modes. In fracturing fluids containing proppants, particles accelerate through the nozzle, colliding with walls at oblique angles. The actual flowrate exceeding theoretical flowrate indicates nozzle damage implies that erosion enlarges the effective flow area, at the same time, it also indicates that there is a risk of nozzle damage and leakage. Based on the results presented in Fig. 16, the following conclusions can be drawn. The throttling effect of oil nozzles in surface pipelines represents a complex multiphysics phenomenon that warrants comprehensive analysis from fluid dynamics, materials science, and operational safety perspectives.

As demonstrated in Fig. 17, the inverse correlation between wellhead pressure and fluid flow characteristics reveals fundamental principles of pressure-driven flow systems. The gradual pressure reduction from 12 MPa to complete system stasis at 0 MPa creates a dynamic transition state where the interplay between viscous forces, turbulent dissipation, and geometric constraints governs the flow behavior. The nozzle’s abrupt cross-sectional contraction introduces localized flow acceleration followed by intense pressure recovery, creating a Venturi-like effect that dominates the system’s hydraulic characteristics. Computational fluid dynamics simulations would reveal complex flow separation phenomena at the nozzle throat, where boundary layer detachment generates recirculation zones that amplify energy losses. Microstructurally, the nozzle’s geometric discontinuity (5 mm constriction) induces dramatic increases in shear stress and turbulence intensity. The wall shear stress at the throat can exceed pipeline values by orders of magnitude, following the relationship τ = µ(du/dy) where velocity gradients (du/dy) become extreme near the contraction. This explains the accelerated pressure decline at the nozzle compared to gradual pipeline reductions. From a system dynamics perspective, the transient pressure reduction process involves propagating depressurization waves through the pipeline. The wave propagation velocity dependent on fluid bulk modulus (K) and density (ρ), determines the timescale for system-wide pressure equilibration. This transient phase creates non-uniform stress distributions along the pipe wall, particularly dangerous at welded joints or corrosion defects. The shutdown condition (p_in = 0 MPa) introduces additional considerations - while macroscopic flow ceases, residual thermal stresses from fluid contraction and potential water hammer effects during restart procedures pose separate integrity challenges. Operationally, the nonlinear relationship between pressure reduction rate and nozzle erosion warrants attention. High initial pressure stages (12 − 8 MPa) likely produce the most severe erosion rates due to exponential dependence of erosion on flow velocity. This suggests that pressure reduction protocols should employ nonlinear decay profiles rather than linear decreases to minimize cumulative damage. Furthermore, the interaction between fluid properties (viscosity, particulate content) and nozzle metallurgy creates complex wear patterns.

Although this study establishes a validated single-phase nozzle-flow model, recent advances in computational fluid dynamics (CFD) offer increasingly refined insights into complex flow regimes. The present Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) model can be extended to simulate the real field scale model15.

Model limitations and future work

The model presented in this paper demonstrates high accuracy during the early flowback stage, where single-phase, near-incompressible Newtonian flow dominates. However, it has limitations under specific conditions. For example, if the actual fluid pressure drops below its saturation pressure during flow from the wellbore or surface pipeline to the wellhead, gas-liquid separation may occur, leading to two-phase flow through the nozzle. Similarly, the presence of native or extraneous gas in the reservoir can also result in two-phase flow16. Accurately measuring the flowrate of gas-liquid two-phase flow under field conditions is challenging, and the models established in this paper do not apply to these scenarios. The model’s accuracy degrades under the following conditions:

Multiphase flow: When gas breakthrough occurs (e.g., during late-stage flowback), the single-phase assumption fails. As shown in Figs. 7 and 8, the deviations (> 190 days) arise as produced gas alters flow dynamics, reducing measured liquid rates below model predictions.

Nozzle erosion: Proppant-laden flow can erode nozzles, enlarging the effective diameter. Figure 16 indicates that erosion increases the flow area, causing actual flowrates to exceed theoretical predictions – a key diagnostic for nozzle damage17.

Late-stage flowback: Beyond the early phase, fluid properties (compressibility, non-Newtonian behavior) and multiphase effects invalidate model assumptions, leading to nonlinear increases in predictive error.

These limitations are inherent to the model’s single-phase, fixed-geometry simplifications. Future work will extend this framework to multiphase scenarios, integrating the theory into a multi-coupled communication platform that advances energy transition and the environment18.

Conclusions

The nozzle flow model can simulate the flow process of fracturing fluid backflow in oil fields. Combined with the measured data of the oilfield well, the relative error between the simulated data and the measured data is about 2%, and the fitting effect is good, which verifies the reliability and accuracy of the model.

When the inlet pressure of the nozzle is constant, the output flowrate of the nozzle gradually increases with the increase of the inner diameter of the nozzle. In the process of blowout operation, it is necessary to comprehensively consider the actual test demand and the capacity of ground equipment, and select a reasonable nozzle. The backflow flowrate of the surface pipelines is extremely sensitive to the pressure at the wellhead and the size of the nozzle. When there is a significant deviation between the backflow flowrate of fracturing fluid through the nozzle and the measured flowrate, it indicates that the nozzle is damaged and needs to be replaced in a timely manner according to production requirements. The fluid flow patterns in surface pipelines reveal the interrelationships between multiple flow parameters. Continuous monitoring and process adjustment are necessary in oilfield production.
