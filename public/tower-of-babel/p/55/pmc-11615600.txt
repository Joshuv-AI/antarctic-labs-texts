Comprehensive sex-specific and age-dependent analysis of four-dimensional flow cardiovascular magnetic resonance assessed aortic blood flow-related parameters in normal subjects using single-vendor magnetic resonance systems and single-vendor software
Authors: Mitch JFG Ramaekers, Bastiaan JC te Kiefte, Bouke P Adriaans, Joe F Juffermans, Hans C van Assen, Bjorn Winkens, Joachim E Wildberger, Hildo J Lamb, Simon Schalla, Jos JM Westenberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAortic blood flow characterization by four-dimensional (4D) flow cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is increasingly performed in aneurysm research. A limited number of studies have established normal values that can aid the recognition of abnormal flow at an early stage. This study aims to establish additional sex-specific and age-dependent reference values for flow-related parameters in a large cohort of healthy adults.MethodsTwo hundred and twelve volunteers were included, and 191 volunteers completed the full study protocol. All underwent 4D flow CMR of the entire aorta. Quantitative values for velocity, vorticity, helicity, as well as total, circumferential, and axial wall shear stress (WSS) were determined for the aortic root (AoR), ascending aorta (AAo), aortic arch, descending aorta (DAo), suprarenal aorta, and infrarenal aorta. Vorticity and helicity were indexed for segment volume (mL).ResultsThe normal values were estimated per sex and age group, where significant differences between males (M) and females (F) were found only for specific age groups. More specifically, the following variables were significantly different after applying the false discovery rate correction for multiple testing: 1) velocity in the AAo and DAo in the 60–70 years age group (mean ± SD: (M) 47.0 ± 8.2 cm s−1 vs (F) 38.4 ± 6.9 cm s−1, p = 0.001 and, (M) 55.9 ± 9.9 cm s−1 vs (F) 46.5 ± 5.5 cm s−1, p = 0.002), 2) normalized vorticity in AoR in the 50–59 years age group ((M) 27,539 ± 5042 s−1 mL−1 vs (F) 30,849 ± 7285 s−1 mL−1, p = 0.002), 3) axial WSS in the Aao in the 18–29 age group ((M) 1098 ± 203 mPa vs (F) 921 ± 121 mPa, p = 0.002). Good to strong negative correlations with age were seen for almost all variables, in different segments, and for both sexes.ConclusionThis study describes reference values for aortic flow-related parameters acquired by 4D flow MRI. We observed limited differences between males and females. A negative relationship with age was seen for almost all flow-related parameters and segments.Keywords: 4D flow MRI, Aorta, Flow, Aneurysm

Background

Patients with an aortic aneurysm have an increased risk of dissection or rupture [1]. Risk prediction in patients with an aortic aneurysm currently relies on diameters and growth. However, aortic diameter is an insufficient predictor for aortic dissection [2], [3]. In the search for additional predictors, aortic blood flow is of major interest, especially in patients with bicuspid aortic valves [4], [5], [6]. Four-dimensional flow cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (4D flow CMR) is an imaging method that has been used to visualize blood flow and quantify blood flow-related parameters, such as velocity, vorticity, helicity, and wall shear stress (WSS). To identify abnormal flow, normal flow must be defined first. While this has been described qualitatively, quantitative studies with a large and uniform sample size are limited [7], [8]. These normal values should ideally be sex-specific and age-dependent since men have a higher risk of developing an aortic aneurysm as compared to women, and aortic geometry changes with aging [9]. Moreover, it is hypothesized that cardiovascular risk increases in postmenopausal women [10]. In the current study, we aim to describe 4D flow CMR-derived reference values for velocity, vorticity, helicity, and WSS for different age groups and sexes in a large population of healthy volunteers. While this is not the first study presenting normal values [8], [11], [12], [13], it presents additional normal values in a homogenous group using a single vendor.

Methods

Study population

Two hundred and twelve consecutive volunteers with no history of aortic, cardiac disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or contra-indications for CMR examination were prospectively included at two university hospitals (Maastricht University Medical Center and Leiden University Medical Center) between February 2020 and February 2023 (Fig. 1). Subjects were included in five age groups: 18–29 years, 30–39 years, 40–49 years, 50–59 years, and 60–70 years. Subjects with incomplete scanning protocols, insufficient image quality, or relevant unexpected findings were secondary excluded. The study protocol was approved by the local medical ethical committees and all volunteers provided written informed consent.

Flowchart of the inclusion process and sample sizes. The flowchart shows our inclusion process in which 212 patients were included. After image acquisition, 21 volunteers were excluded due to various reasons. A total of 191 healthy volunteers were included in the analysis

MRI acquisition

All volunteers underwent cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging on 3T systems (Philips Ingenia or Elition, Philips, Best, The Netherlands). Scanning protocol included 4D flow CMR of the full aortic volume (aortic valve to aortic bifurcation). 4D flow CMR was acquired using navigator-guided respiratory gating based on diaphragm excursion and retrospective electrocardiogram gating. Velocity encoding (VENC) was set at 150 cm s−1 and adjusted if deemed necessary (based on 2D flow sequences before the acquisition of the 4D flow sequence). CMR analysis

CMR data were processed using CAAS MR Solutions 5.2.1 software (Pie Medical Imaging BV, Maastricht, the Netherlands). Semi-automatic segmentations were performed by three readers (M.R., Bt.K., J.J.; with 4, 3, and 5 years of experience, respectively) in peak systole (defined as the phase with the highest flow rate identified in the ascending aorta [AAo]) [14]. The aorta was divided into six anatomical sections: aortic root (AoR), AAo, aortic arch (AoA), descending aorta (DAo), suprarenal aorta (SRA), and infrarenal aorta (IRA) (Fig. 2). Geometrical information (i.e. maximum diameter, segment volume, and centerline length) was automatically calculated per segment using in-house developed software [15]. Velocity, vorticity, and absolute, positive, and negative helicity were obtained as an average over the segmental volume using in-house developed software. WSS per segment was obtained from CAAS MR Solutions 5.2.1 software and calculated as previously described [16]. For all parameters, the peak value during the cardiac cycle was used for analysis. Vorticity and helicity (positive and negative) were calculated as previously described and indexed for the volume of the segment (s−1 mL−1 and m s−2 mL−1, respectively) [5]. Pulse wave velocity (PWV) was assessed from the 4D flow CMR data. Considering the limited temporal resolution of 4D flow CMR (when compared to, for example, 2D flow CMR), PWV was only assessed for two segments: the thoracic aorta (AoR, AAo, AoA, and DAo) and the abdominal aorta (SRA and IRA).

Workflow for the quantification of flow-related parameters. The three-directional phase contrast images (anterior-posterior [AP], feet-head [FH], and right-left [RL]) are combined, creating a 3D image. The software (CAAS MR solutions) creates a 3D surface model of the wall boundary, which can be manually adjusted with contouring along the centerline, and the velocity and WSS data can be visualized. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage. A 95% confidence interval was used for all flow-related parameters as lower- and upper limit of normal. The correlation of flow-related parameters with age was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Differences between males and females were assessed using an independent-sample T-test. A false discovery rate (FDR) correction (FDR control level set at 5%) for multiple testing was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for each variable separately (Tables 3 and 4) as well as for the Pearson correlation coefficients (Supplementary Table 1).

Sex differences

Distributions for all parameters per sex and age group are presented in Fig. 3. For velocity, significant differences between males and females were found in the AAo and DAo in the 60–70 years age group (47.0 ± 8.2 cm s−1 vs 38.4 ± 6.9 cm s−1, p = 0.001 and 55.9 ± 9.9 cm s−1 vs 46.5 ± 5.5 cm s−1, p = 0.002, for male vs female, respectively). Discussion

In this study, we acquired age-dependent and sex-specific normal values for aortic flow-related parameters, including velocity, normalized vorticity, absolute, positive, and negative normalized helicity, WSS magnitude, as well as axial and circumferential WSS. Our main results are i) normal values only show minimal significant differences between males and females in all age groups and ii) a majority of parameters show a negative correlation with age in almost all segments.

To our knowledge, only a limited number of studies have acquired normal values for quantitative aortic flow parameters [8], [11], [12], [13]. The published studies that present normal values have some limitations, for example, on the study protocol, segments included, sample size, or acquired parameters, as listed below.

Segments included

Most studies, as listed above, only include the thoracic aorta. Only the study by Scott et al. included the total aorta. However, they do not divide the aorta into anatomical segments but only use AAo, AoA, and DAo as segments. They included 100 subjects (50% male), which is half of the sample size as compared to our study. Moreover, they have fewer parameters included, and their acquisition parameters are not standardized (spatial resolution varies between 1.7 mm and 3.1 mm isotropic). Their scan parameters are also not conforming to the consensus statement. The other studies do not include the total aorta (Ebel et al.: AAo, Callaghan et al. (both studies): thoracic aorta).

Sample size

Two studies by Callaghan et al. included more subjects than ours. However, they were retrospectively selected from a cohort of varying ages, and >65% of the participants were male in both studies. Some of their acquisition parameters were not conforming to the consensus statement. For example, they do not use respiratory gating in their scans, and the temporal resolution is suboptimal 50–60 ms. The other studies have smaller sample sizes (Scott et al.: 100 subjects, Ebel et al.: 86 subjects).

Acquired parameters

None of the mentioned studies include flow-related parameters as much as ours. Scott et al. did not include vorticity. Callaghan et al. provided most parameters but were divided into two different papers. Ebel et al. did not include vorticity, axial, and circumferential WSS.

In conclusion, based on the above arguments, we believe that we have provided the most complete and standardized normal values in aortic 4D flow MRI up until now. This study handled inclusion, acquisition, and post-processing in a standardized manner, following the consensus statement.

Normal values in comparison with other studies

The study by Ebel et al. describes normal values for WSS in different sexes and age groups [8]. The mean normal values for WSS observed in that study are lower than the mean values observed in our cohort (900 mPa in male and female patients <30 years vs 1183 mPa [males] and 1013 mPa [females] in the 18–29 years group, respectively). When looking at other studies describing WSS magnitude in controls, we observe that our values are generally higher (up to approximately 30%) [4], [11]. It is known that differences in spatial resolution could lead to numerical differences in WSS [17]. Thus, the differences in WSS between our study and the studies by Scott et al. and Burk et al. could be caused by their lower spatial resolution. However, since Ebel et al. used a 4D flow protocol with a similar spatial resolution of 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm, the results are likely to be caused by a difference in other scanning parameters or quantification methods. WSS quantification is dependent on different input parameters, such as the definition of wall boundary by segmentation (which varies among commercial software providers), and blood viscosity values. First, in our study, we used a semi-automatic segmentation method, initially creating a 3D surface model and then adjusting the contours along the aortic centerline to create wall boundaries. The study by Ebel et al. applied an automatic segmentation method using Bloodline software [18]. Second, our study assumes a blood viscosity of 4 cP, while it is unclear how other software tools incorporate blood viscosity. Since viscosity is part of the formula for WSS, different assumptions or using patient-specific viscosity can lead to numerical differences. In summary, variations in commercial or in-house developed quantification software could lead to numerical differences as is seen between our study and the study by Ebel et al., which should be kept in mind when analyzing WSS. A study by Callaghan et al. also described normal values for WSS in the AoA and DAo in a large cohort [13]. However, they have acquired their data using multi-VENC 4D flow CMR; therefore, direct comparison of exact numbers is difficult. Nevertheless, they did show an increase in WSS in the DAo as compared to the proximal segments, similar to our results.

There are no studies that applied the same quantification for vorticity and helicity as compared to ours. Our results present absolute, positive, and negative values, and have been normalized for the volume of the aortic segment to correct for differences in the volume of the segments.

Scan-to-scan variations and repeatability need to be taken into consideration when quantifying flow-related parameters from 4D flow CMR [19]. Therefore, normal values as presented in this study can only be compared with studies using similar vendors, acquisition protocol, and post-processing methods. Pathological cut-off values still need to be determined by using the required statistical methods. To allow other research groups to assess the influence of different software methods, the datasets used in this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Sex differences

Despite the fact that normal aortic dimensions are different between males and females, with smaller aortic diameters in females as compared to males [20], we did not find large differences in flow parameters between the sexes which is in line with other results [8]. Also, Scott et al. showed only a significant difference between males and females in the youngest group (8% higher velocity volume) [11]. Our data do not directly support the hypothesis that flow differences could potentially explain why aortic aneurysms are more likely to develop in males and why aortic dissections also occur in males more often (65% male) [21], [22]. The lack of statistical significance in the comparisons between males and females in various age groups is potentially a result of the limited sample size after grouping the subjects. Differences that are not statistically significant after correction for multiple testing could turn out to be significant after an increase in sample size. However, the lack of differences could also be caused by our correction for segmental volume (which could be larger in men than in women, if we base it on the diameters), leading to lower corrected values in men.

Influence of age on aortic flow

The current study confirms preliminary results from others in a larger cohort of healthy volunteers and extends the analysis with multiple flow-related parameters in all anatomical segments of the aorta. A negative correlation of flow parameters with age has previously been observed. The study by Schafstedde et al. described age-dependent and sex-specific normal values for a different set of flow-related parameters [23] showing significant changes in parameters with increasing age. Scott et al. also presented velocity, helicity, and WSS magnitude, but in a smaller cohort of healthy volunteers [11]. They observed a negative correlation of most parameters with age, and like our results, observed that the negative correlation between velocity and WSS is highest in the DAo. The study by Burk et al., despite not aiming to describe normal values, also showed lower velocity and WSS in older subjects as compared to younger subjects [4]. A study by Callaghan et al. probably presented the most complete data on changes in flow dynamics in aging [12]. Like our study, Callaghan et al. presented a clear decrease in velocity, helicity, and vorticity in older age. However, their study was limited to the thoracic aorta only.

Clinical implications

We observe limited differences between males and females. A negative relationship with age was found for almost all flow-related parameters and segments. By defining normal values in healthy adult volunteers, results of 4D flow CMR might be easier to interpret in clinical care. Thus, these values could serve as sex-specific and age-dependent reference values. They may help to identify subjects with abnormal aortic flow pattern at an early stage who would benefit from follow-up. Longitudinal studies could assess the predictive value of the parameters discussed in this study on aortic disease.

Limitations

One limitation in quantifying flow-related parameters from 4D flow CMR is the spatio-temporal resolution. It is known that 4D flow CMR underestimates WSS values as compared to computational fluid dynamics because of its limited spatial resolution [24]. Moreover, in 4D flow sequences with lower spatial resolutions, WSS values are slightly underestimated compared to higher resolution scans [17]. Vorticity and helicity values have also been underestimated in 4D flow CMR sequences with lower spatial resolutions [25]. Therefore, the results in this study are only applicable in 4D flow examinations with similar spatial resolutions and similar quantification methods. Of note, most of the reported measures are directly assessed from the source velocity data (flow velocity, vorticity, helicity). Differences between software may be a result of how background phase correction and phase unwrapping are performed by the software. Only WSS and PWV are directly affected by the post-processing algorithm itself.

However, as the acquisition sequence of our study adhered to the most common requirements for 4D flow MRI sequences, as described in the consensus statement [26], [27], the results should be applicable to most other studies.

4D flow CMR is currently relatively time-consuming in acquisition and post-processing, which complicates implementation in clinical care. However, advances in compressed sensing and deep learning have shown that both acquisition and post-processing could be accelerated [28], [29], [30].

Conclusions

This study describes normal values for velocity, normalized vorticity, absolute, positive, and negative normalized helicity, WSS magnitude as well as axial and circumferential WSS for all anatomical aortic segments as acquired by 4D flow CMR.
