Duck plague virus US2 promotes p62-mediated autophagic degradation of RIG-I to suppress antiviral signaling
Authors: Yuanyuan Hao, Meiyuan Xiong, Mingshu Wang, Anchun Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuck plague virus (DPV) is a highly contagious pathogen that causes severe immunosuppression and high mortality in waterfowl, resulting in substantial economic losses to the poultry industry. However, the mechanisms by which DPV evades host innate immune responses remain incompletely understood. In this study, we investigated the role of the DPV tegument protein US2 in regulating host antiviral responses. pUS2 significantly suppressed IFN-β promoter activation induced by poly(I:C) and poly(dA:dT) and reduced the transcription of IFN-β and interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), including OASL and Mx. Further analysis showed that pUS2 specifically inhibited IFN-β promoter activation triggered by the RIG-I/MDA5-MAVS signaling pathway. Co-immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence assays demonstrated that pUS2 directly interacted with RIG-I in the cytoplasm and reduced its protein abundance in a dose-dependent manner. Mechanistically, pUS2 enhanced K48-linked ubiquitination of RIG-I and promoted its degradation through a p62-mediated autophagy pathway. Deletion of the US2 gene moderately reduced viral replication efficiency in DEF cells and enhanced expression of type I interferons and ISGs. In vivo experiments further showed that ducks infected with the US2-deleted virus exhibited reduced pathogenicity, including lower viral loads, milder tissue damage, and increased survival rates compared with those infected with the parental virus. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DPV US2 antagonizes host innate immunity by targeting the RIG-I signaling pathway and promoting RIG-I degradation through autophagy. This study provides new insights into the immune evasion strategies of DPV and advances our understanding of DPV-host interactions.Keywords: Duck plague virus, US2 protein, RIG-I, Autophagy, Immune evasion

Introduction

Duck plague (DP), also known as duck viral enteritis, is an acute and highly contagious disease that affects ducks, geese, and swans (Sarmah et al., 2020). The disease is characterized by rapid transmission, high morbidity and mortality, and widespread geographic distribution, resulting in significant economic losses to the global waterfowl industry (Salguero et al., 2002; Liang et al., 2022). The causative agent, duck plague virus (DPV), belongs to the family Herpesviridae, subfamily Alphaherpesvirinae (Dhama et al., 2017; Pazhanivel et al., 2019).

DPV virions exhibit the typical structural features of herpesviruses, consisting of a core, capsid, tegument, and envelope (Roizman et al., 1981). The viral genome is a double-stranded DNA molecule containing a unique long (UL) region and a unique short (US) region flanked by internal and terminal repeat sequences (Yang et al., 2014). The DPV genome encodes numerous structural and nonstructural proteins that play essential roles in viral replication, assembly, and host interaction (Aasdev et al., 2021). Among these viral proteins, tegument proteins have attracted considerable attention because they participate in nucleocapsid transport, viral protein localization, and regulation of host immune responses during infection (He et al., 2021).

Innate immunity represents the first line of host defense against viral infection. Upon pathogen invasion, host cells recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which initiate signaling cascades leading to the production of type I interferons (Takeuchi and Akira, 2010; Zipfel, 2014). Among these receptors, the RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs) and the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway play central roles in detecting viral nucleic acids in the cytoplasm (Ishii et al., 2006; Thompson et al., 2011; Li et al., 2023; Oh et al., 2025). The RLR family, including retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I), melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5), and laboratory of genetics and physiology 2 (LGP2), recognizes viral RNA produced during infection or replication (Gillich et al., 2023). Activation of these receptors triggers downstream signaling through mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS), leading to the activation of interferon regulatory factors and the production of type I interferons (IFN-I). Although DPV is a DNA virus, accumulating evidence suggests that DNA viruses can also activate RNA-sensing pathways due to the generation of aberrant RNA species during viral transcription and replication (Ablasser et al., 2009). IFN-I subsequently induces the expression of numerous interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), such as oligoadenylate synthetase-like protein (OASL) and myxovirus resistance protein (Mx), which exert important antiviral functions (Schneider et al., 2014; Rong et al., 2018).

To establish successful infection, DPV have evolved multiple strategies to evade host innate immune responses. For example, DPV tegument protein UL41 functions as a viral host shutoff factor that degrades host mRNA and suppresses antiviral responses (He et al., 2022). In addition, the DPV UL24 protein has been shown to induce polyubiquitination of interferon regulatory factor 7 leading to its degradation through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (Ruan et al., 2024). These findings highlight the importance of viral proteins in modulating host antiviral signaling pathways.

The US2 gene is located in the US region of herpesvirus genomes and encodes a tegument protein in many alpha-herpesviruses (Clase et al., 2003; Zhao et al., 2009). In several herpesviruses, including human cytomegalovirus, pseudorabies virus, herpes simplex virus type 1, bovine herpesvirus 1, and equine herpesvirus 2, the US2 protein has been reported to participate in various biological processes, such as regulation of antigen presentation, protein palmitoylation, and modulation of host signaling pathways (van der Wal et al., 2002; Kang and Banfield, 2010; Lu et al., 2017; Kong et al., 2024). However, the role of DPV US2 in regulating innate immune signaling remains largely unknown. In the present study, we investigated the role of pUS2 in host innate immune responses and evaluated its contribution to viral pathogenicity in ducks. Our findings provide new insights into the mechanisms by which DPV interacts with the host immune system.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethics statement

9-day-old Tianfu Meat Duck (Anas platyrhynchos domesticus) embryos and one-day-old Tianfu Meat ducklings were obtained from the Standardized Experimental Animal Center of Sichuan Agricultural University (Sichuan, China), a DPV-free source maintained under strict biosafety management. One-day-old Tianfu Meat ducklings were reared in an isolation facility under biosafety-compliant conditions until 14 days of age prior to the pathogenicity experiments, in order to minimize potential exposure to external pathogens and reduce the possible influence of maternal antibodies. In addition, prior to the experiments, randomly selected ducklings were confirmed to be negative for DPV by PCR detection. All procedures involving animals were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines to minimize animal discomfort. The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Approval No.: 20250664).

Cells, plasmids, and antibodies

Primary duck embryo fibroblast (DEF) cells were prepared from 9-day-old Tianfu Meat Duck (Anas platyrhynchos domesticus) embryos and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco Life Technologies, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% newborn bovine serum (NBS; Gibco, CA, USA) at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. DEF cells were used for plasmid transfection and DPV infection experiments. The Escherichia coli GS1783 strain used for the maintenance and recombination of the DPV infectious clone, as well as E. coli DH5α cells used for plasmid amplification and preservation, were both maintained in our laboratory. Chemically competent DH5α cells were prepared using the calcium chloride method. Electrocompetent GS1783 cells were prepared by repeated washing with ice-cold sterile water. All bacterial strains and prepared competent cells were preserved and stored in laboratory.

Expression plasmids related to the cGAS-STING and RIG-I signaling pathways were preserved in our laboratory. The pEP-Kan-S plasmid was preserved in our laboratory and used as the template for amplification of the kanamycin resistance cassette, which was used as a selectable marker during the construction and modification of the DPV infectious clone.

Rabbit anti-US2 polyclonal antibody was generated in our laboratory by immunizing rabbits with purified recombinant US2 protein. Mouse anti-HA monoclonal antibody (Cat# 66006-2-Ig), mouse anti-Flag monoclonal antibody (Cat# 66008-4-Ig), rabbit anti-Myc polyclonal antibody (Cat# 16286-1-AP), rabbit anti-HA polyclonal antibody (Cat# 51064-2-AP), rabbit anti-Flag monoclonal antibody (Cat# 80801-2-RR), and mouse anti-β-actin monoclonal antibody (Cat# 66009-1-Ig) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). Mouse anti-α-Tubulin monoclonal antibody (Cat# AC012), HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Cat# AS003), and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Cat# AS014) were purchased from ABclonal (Wuhan, China). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were used for western blot analysis.

Anti-Flag mouse monoclonal antibody-conjugated magnetic beads (MBL, Nagoya, Japan; Cat# M185-11R) and anti-HA mouse monoclonal antibody-conjugated magnetic beads (MBL, Nagoya, Japan; Cat# M180-11) were used for co-immunoprecipitation assays.

Alexa Fluor™ 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat# A32723) and Alexa Fluor™ 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat# A32740) were used for immunofluorescence assays.

Preparation of duck embryo fibroblasts

Fertilized duck eggs were surface-disinfected with 75% ethanol and exposed to ultraviolet light for 10 min. Embryos were aseptically removed, and the limbs and internal organs were discarded. The remaining tissues were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), minced into small fragments, and digested with 0.25% trypsin for 6 min. Following digestion, the cell suspension was centrifuged at 4500 r/min, resuspended in DMEM, filtered through sterile gauze, and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% NBS at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ incubator.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

DEF cells were co-transfected with the IFN-β promoter-driven firefly luciferase reporter plasmid and the Renilla luciferase control plasmid using Hieff Trans® Liposomal 2000 Transfection Reagent (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China; Cat# 40802ES03). Cells were stimulated with poly(I:C) (MCE, Shanghai, China; Cat# HY-107202) or poly(dA:dT) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat# P9764) and simultaneously transfected with either the empty vector or the US2 expression plasmid. At 36 h post-transfection, luciferase activities were measured using the TransDetect® Dual-Luc Pro Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China; Cat# FR203-01) with a GloMax® Navigator Microplate Luminometer (Promega, USA). All experiments were performed with three technical replicates per condition and independently repeated at least three times. The data shown represent the mean of independent experiments. Data from the independent experiments were pooled for statistical analysis, and the results are presented as mean ± SD.

RT-qPCR and DPV DNA quantification

Total RNA was collected using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Japan; Cat# 9109) and reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript™ FAST RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara; Cat# RR092A). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara; Cat# RR820A). Gene expression levels were normalized to 18S rRNA, and relative expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. The sequences of primers used in this study are provided in Table 1.

Western blotting (WB) analysis and Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

DEF cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat# P0013B) or IP lysis buffer (Beyotime; Cat# P0013) on ice. Lysates were incubated with 30 μL anti-Flag mouse monoclonal antibody-conjugated magnetic beads (MBL, Cat# M185-11R) or anti-HA mouse monoclonal antibody-conjugated magnetic beads (MBL, Cat# M180-11) at 4°C overnight. After incubation, the beads were washed with PBST and mixed with 5 × loading buffer, and denatured at 100°C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with the indicated primary antibodies, including rabbit anti-Myc polyclonal antibody (1:5000), rabbit anti-HA polyclonal antibody (1:5000), rabbit anti-Flag monoclonal antibody (1:5000) and rabbit anti-US2 polyclonal antibody (1:800) followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an Enhanced ECL Chemiluminescent Substrate Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, Cat# 36222ES76).

Immunofluorescence assay

DEF cells were co-transfected with RIG-I-Flag and US2-3HA expression plasmids and cultured for 36 h. The culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. Transfected cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature, transfected cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with mouse anti-Flag monoclonal antibody (1:2000) and rabbit anti-HA polyclonal antibody (1:2000). After washing five times with TBST, transfected cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with Alexa Fluor™ 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (1:1000) and Alexa Fluor™ 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (1:1000). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, CAT# C1002) for 10 min, and fluorescence images were captured using a BX53F2C fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Cycloheximide chase and inhibitor treatment assays

To evaluate RIG-I protein stability, DEF cells were co-transfected with US2 expression plasmids and treated with cycloheximide (CHX; Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA; Cat# S7418) to inhibit de novo protein synthesis. Cells were subsequently treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Cat# S2619), the autophagy-lysosome inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (BafA1; Cat# S1413), or the apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (Cat# S7023) (all from Selleck Chemicals) for 8 h, with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma, Cat# D8418) serving as the control. Cells were harvested at the indicated time points, and protein levels were analyzed by WB.

Dose-dependent analysis of RIG-I and p62 protein expression

To investigate the effect of DPV US2 protein on RIG-I expression in a dose-dependent manner, DEF cells were seeded in culture plates and transfected with expression plasmids using a standard Hieff Trans® Liposomal 2000 Transfection Reagent (Yeasen). For dose-response analysis, cells were co-transfected with a constant amount of pCAGGS-RIG-I-Flag plasmid (1500 ng) and increasing amounts of pCAGGS-3Flag-US2 plasmid (0, 500, 800, and 1500 ng per well), while the total amount of transfected DNA was adjusted to be equal (3000 ng) in each group by supplementation with empty vector (pCAGGS). At 36 h post-transfection, cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA buffer for western blot analysis to detect RIG-I protein expression levels.

For further analysis of RIG-I and p62 expression, DEF cells were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected the following day. Cells were co-transfected with pCAGGS-RIG-I-Flag (1000 ng per well) and pCAGGS-p62-HA (1000 ng per well), followed by transfection with increasing amounts of pCAGGS-US2-3Myc plasmid (0, 200, 500, and 1000 ng per well). At 36 h post-transfection, total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime), and the expression levels of RIG-I and p62 were analyzed by Western blotting.

Construction of US2-deleted and revertant viruses

A bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC)-based homologous recombination system was used to generate the US2-deleted DPV mutant. Briefly, a targeting fragment containing the upstream and downstream homologous arms of the US2 gene, I-SceI restriction sites (5′-TAGGGATAACAGGGTAAT-3′), and a kanamycin resistance cassette was amplified using pEP-Kan-S as the template. The sequences of primers are provided in Table 1.

The fragment was electroporated into E. coli GS1783 competent cells using a MicroPulser™ Electroporator (Bio-Rad, USA). Homologous recombination replaced the US2 gene with the targeting cassette. A second recombination step induced by l-arabinose was used to remove the kanamycin resistance cassette. Positive clones (pDPV-ΔUS2) were screened by double antibiotic selection and verified by PCR. The US2-revertant virus (pDPV-ΔUS2-Rev) was generated using the same strategy. The recombinant plasmids pDPV-ΔUS2 and pDPV-ΔUS2-Rev were transfected into DEF cells, and virus rescue was confirmed by observing enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) expression under an IX73 inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

In vivo pathogenicity experiments

14-day-old ducks were randomly divided into experimental groups (n = 13 ducks per group) and intramuscularly inoculated with 1 mL 104 TCID50 DPV-ΔUS2, DPV-ΔUS2-Rev, DPV-CHv50, or DMEM as the negative control. The infectious dose of 10⁴ TCID₅₀ was determined based on a preliminary dose-ranging experiment performed prior to the formal animal study. Ducks were monitored daily for clinical signs, survival rate, rectal temperature, and body weight (n = 10). On day 5 post-infection (dpi), three ducks from each group were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Liver, spleen, duodenum, bursa of Fabricius, and thymus were collected and divided into two portions for different analyses (n = 3). One portion of each tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Histopathological changes were examined using a BX53F2C microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The other portion of each tissue was used for viral load determination. Briefly, 0.1 g of tissue was homogenized in 1 mL PBS, subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Viral DNA was extracted from 100 μL of the supernatant using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China, Cat# GDP304), and DPV genome copy numbers were quantified by qPCR.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to statistical comparisons, data distribution normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For in vitro experiments, all assays were performed in three independent biological replicates, each containing three technical replicates. For each independent experiment, the mean value of technical replicates was first calculated and used as a single biological replicate. The final data used for statistical analysis were then obtained by combining the three independent biological replicates, and differences among groups were analyzed accordingly. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. For experiments involving two independent variables (e.g., virus type and time point), two-way ANOVA was applied, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to evaluate differences among groups and interactions between factors. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, and ns indicates no significant difference.

Results

DPV pUS2 suppresses IFN-β production

Poly(I:C) and poly (dA:dT) are potent inducers of IFN-I, stimulating DEF cells to produce significant amounts of IFN-β. Upon poly(I:C) stimulation, the activity of the IFN-β promoter activity increased approximately 40-fold. However, expression of pUS2 reduced this promoter activity. In contrast, the UL45 protein had no effect on the IFN-β promoter activity (Fig. 1A). Similarly, poly (dA:dT) stimulation resulted in a 15-fold increase in IFN-β promoter activity, which was suppressed in the presence of pUS2 (Fig. 1B). These results indicate that pUS2 significantly inhibits IFN-β promoter activity, thereby affecting the initiation of IFN-β mRNA synthesis in host cells.

Further analysis demonstrated that the transcription of IFN-β and its downstream antiviral molecules, Mx and OASL, was upregulated upon poly(I:C) and poly (dA:dT) stimulation. However, pUS2 significantly reduced their transcription levels (Fig. 1C). The expression of pUS2 was confirmed by WB (Fig. 1D). These data collectively suggest that pUS2 effectively suppresses the IFN-β signaling cascade and its antiviral effectors, thereby inhibiting host innate immune response.

pUS2 targets RIG-I, MDA5, and MAVS

To evaluate the impact of pUS2 on the cGAS-STING and RIG-I/MDA5-MAVS pathways, we exogenously overexpressed key molecules such as cGAS, STING, RIG-I, MDA5, MAVS, TBK1, and IRF7 in DEF cells. We then measured the inhibitory effect of pUS2 on IFN-β promoter activity induced by these proteins and assessed their protein expression levels. Our results demonstrated that pUS2 significantly suppressed the IFN-β promoter activity induced by RIG-I, MDA5, and MAVS, while having no significant effect on other proteins in the pathway (Fig. 2A). Additionally, the protein expression of RIG-I, MDA5, and MAVS was reduced by pUS2, confirming its inhibitory effect on the RIG-I/MDA5-mediated signaling pathway.

pUS2 inhibits IFN-β promoter activation induced by the RIG-I/MDA5 signaling pathway. A. IFN-β promoter activity was measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay. Protein expression levels of the indicated signaling molecules were analyzed by Western blotting.

Interaction between pUS2 and RIG-I protein

To further investigate the mechanism by which pUS2 inhibits RIG-I, we examined the relationship between pUS2 and RIG-I protein expression. In dose-dependent experiments, increasing pUS2 expression resulted in a gradual decrease in RIG-I protein abundance (Fig. 3A). IFA and Co-IP revealed that pUS2 directly interacts with RIG-I in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3B, 3C). These findings suggest that pUS2 reduces RIG-I protein levels and impairs its functional activity through direct interaction with RIG-I.

pUS2 induces RIG-I ubiquitination and mediates its autophagic degradation

To explore how pUS2 affects RIG-I function, we examined the ubiquitination of RIG-I. pUS2 significantly enhanced K48-linked ubiquitination of RIG-I (Fig. 4A). Although K48-linked ubiquitination is typically associated with proteasomal degradation, our inhibitor assays suggested that RIG-I degradation was primarily mediated through the autophagy pathway (Fig. 4B). Next, we investigated the relationship between RIG-I and the autophagy receptor p62. As the dosage of pUS2 that inhibited RIG-I expression increased, p62 expression also decreased in sync with RIG-I (Fig. 4C), suggesting that p62 is involved in the autophagic degradation of RIG-I. Co-IP and IFA analyses confirmed the interaction between RIG-I and p62 in the presence of pUS2 (Fig. 4D, 4E). These findings indicate that pUS2 induces autophagic degradation of RIG-I by promoting its ubiquitination and interaction with the autophagy receptor p62.

DPV-ΔUS2 induces higher levels of type I interferon

We generated a US2-deleted and US2-revertant DPV clone, pDPV-ΔUS2 and pDPV-ΔUS2-Rev, using a BAC-based homologous recombination system. Infectious clones pDPV-ΔUS2 and pDPV-ΔUS2-Rev were confirmed by PCR (Fig. 5A) and successfully rescued in DEF cells (Fig. 5B). The third image was captured at the seventh viral passage and showed loss of EGFP fluorescence after removal of the mini-F/EGFP cassette. Recombined virus DPV-ΔUS2 and DPV-ΔUS2-Rev were identified by Western blotting (Fig. 5C). The multi-step growth curve of DPV-ΔUS2 (MOI = 0.01) indicated reduced replication efficiency in DEF cells (Fig. 5D). Compared with DPV-CHv50 and DPV-ΔUS2-Rev, DPV-ΔUS2 exhibited lower TCID₅₀ values at 12-96 hpi, with an approximately 10-fold decrease in viral yield at 72 hpi.

We then evaluated the innate immune response following infection with DPV-ΔUS2. In both DPV-ΔUS2-infected DEF cells and DPV-ΔUS2-infected duck spleens (102 TCID50), levels of IFN-α and IFN-β, as well as the mRNA expression of OASL and Mx, were significantly higher compared to infections with DPV-CHv50 or DPV-ΔUS2-Rev (Fig. 5E and 5F). These results suggest that the deletion of US2 leads to an enhanced innate immune response in the host.

DPV-ΔUS2 reduces pathogenicity in ducklings

We infected 14-day-old ducks with DPV-ΔUS2, DPV-CHv50, or DPV-ΔUS2-Rev at a dose of 104 TCID50 by intramuscular injection and monitored the survival rate, body weight, and rectal temperature for 10 days. Ducks infected with DPV-ΔUS2 exhibited a 100% survival rate, while approximately 50% of ducks infected with DPV-CHv50 and DPV-ΔUS2-Rev succumbed to infection (Fig. 6A). The body weight of DPV-ΔUS2-infected ducks increased at a rate of 75 g per day, while ducks infected with DPV-CHv50 and DPV-ΔUS2-Rev showed poor or no weight gain (Fig. 6B). Additionally, rectal temperature remained normal in DPV-ΔUS2-infected ducks, while a significant proportion of DPV-CHv50 and DPV-ΔUS2-Rev-infected ducks exhibited fever (Fig. 6C). Gross pathological examination at 5 dpi was performed to preliminarily evaluate the macroscopic pathological alterations in organs caused by DPV infection. Ducks infected with DPV-ΔUS2 exhibited mild splenomegaly with slight congestion. In contrast, ducks infected with DPV-CHv50 and DPV-ΔUS2-Rev developed severe lesions in multiple organs. The liver showed hepatomegaly with dark red to black discoloration and marked congestion. The spleen was swollen, with rounded margins and a dark red to black surface. The bursa of Fabricius exhibited mild atrophy with fragile tissue and scattered pinpoint hemorrhages. The thymus displayed rapid and severe atrophy accompanied by internal necrosis, resulting in a firm and brittle appearance. Additionally, the duodenum showed multiple pinpoint hemorrhages; in some individuals, thinning of the intestinal wall was observed, and the intestinal lumen contained viscous watery contents with sloughed mucosal debris. (Fig. 6D). Histopathological analysis revealed that inflammatory changes were present in multiple tissues following DPV infection. In ducks infected with DPV-ΔUS2, moderate inflammatory cell infiltration, predominantly lymphocytes, was observed, particularly in the thymus and the spleens. In contrast, ducks infected with DPV-CHv50 and DPV-ΔUS2-Rev exhibited more severe histopathological alterations, including extensive hemorrhage, necrosis, vacuolation, and pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration, accompanied by disruption of normal tissue architecture. (Fig. 6E). Viral load of DPV analysis revealed that DPV replication was significantly lower in DPV-ΔUS2-infected ducks compared to those infected with DPV-CHv50 and DPV-ΔUS2-Rev. The most significant reduction in viral load was observed in the thymus, spleen, and duodenum, where viral load decreased by approximately two orders of magnitude (Fig. 6F).

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrated that the DPV tegument protein US2 plays a critical role in antagonizing host innate immunity by targeting the RIG-I signaling pathway. Expression of pUS2 markedly suppressed IFN-β promoter activation induced by poly(I:C) and poly(dA:dT), indicating that pUS2 broadly inhibits nucleic acid-triggered interferon responses. Furthermore, transcriptional analysis revealed that pUS2 significantly reduced the expression of IFN-β and the downstream interferon-stimulated genes OASL and Mx, two key antiviral effectors. These findings suggest that pUS2 contributes to the suppression of host innate immune responses during DPV infection.

Innate immune sensing of viral infection is primarily mediated by pattern recognition receptors, among which the RIG-I/MDA5-MAVS and cGAS-STING pathways play central roles in the detection of viral nucleic acids and the induction of type I interferons (Motwani et al., 2019; Sioud, 2025). In this study, we found that pUS2 specifically inhibited IFN-β promoter activation induced by RIG-I, MDA5, and MAVS, suggesting that the RIG-I/MDA5 signaling pathway represents a primary target of US2-mediated immune evasion. Our data further demonstrated that pUS2 directly interacted with RIG-I protein in the cytoplasm. Although RIG-I is primarily recognized as a sensor of RNA viruses, accumulating evidence suggests that it also contributes to innate immune responses against DNA virus infections (Chiu et al., 2009). Consistently, many DNA viruses have evolved strategies to target the RIG-I/MDA5 signaling pathway in order to evade host innate immunity (Chiang et al., 2021). HSV-1 ICP0 targets the K48 ubiquitination of RIG-I for degradation (Parameswaran et al., 2024), while UL11 directly binds RIG-I and MDA5, blocking ligand recognition (Xing et al., 2012). By reducing RIG-I expression and disrupting it signaling function, the virus may attenuate downstream interferon responses, thereby facilitating viral replication. Ubiquitination plays a critical role in regulating RIG-I stability and activity. K63-linked ubiquitination promotes RIG-I activation and antiviral signaling, whereas K48-linked ubiquitination typically targets proteins for degradation (Arimoto et al., 2007; Inn et al., 2011). In the present study, we observed that pUS2 significantly enhanced K48-linked ubiquitination of RIG-I, suggesting that US2 promotes RIG-I degradation. Interestingly, pharmacological inhibition experiments revealed that blocking autophagy with BafA1 partially restored RIG-I protein levels, indicating that RIG-I degradation occurs primarily through the autophagy pathway rather than the classical proteasomal pathway. Although K48-linked ubiquitination is traditionally associated with proteasomal degradation, recent studies have shown that K48-ubiquitinated proteins can also be degraded through autophagy, particularly when protein aggregates accumulate or when proteasome capacity is limited under certain cellular conditions (Danieli and Martens, 2018; Zaffagnini et al., 2018). Therefore, our findings suggest that DPV US2 may exploit the selective autophagy pathway to facilitate RIG-I degradation.

Selective autophagy typically requires cargo receptors that recognize ubiquitinated substrates and recruit them to autophagosomes (Johansen and Lamark, 2011). The adaptor protein p62 (SQSTM1) is one of the best-characterized autophagy receptors and contains a ubiquitin-associated (UBA) domain that recognizes ubiquitinated proteins (Xiao et al., 2025). In this study, we observed that p62 expression decreased concurrently with RIG-I levels in the presence of pUS2. This observation is consistent with the well-established mechanism in which p62 is degraded together with its cargo during autophagic degradation. Moreover, Co-IP and immunofluorescence analyses confirmed the interaction between RIG-I and p62 in the cytoplasm, suggesting that p62 participates in the selective autophagic degradation of RIG-I. The recognition of autophagic substrates by p62 does not strictly depend on K63-linked ubiquitination, as the UBA domain of p62 can also bind to other types of polyubiquitin chains, including K48-linked ubiquitin, thereby facilitating the selective autophagic degradation of ubiquitinated cargos (You et al., 2019). For instance, TBK1 has been shown to undergo SIAH2-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination followed by p62-dependent autophagic degradation (Xie et al., 2026). Likewise, evidence has shown that LRRC25 facilitates the degradation of RIG-I through selective autophagy by interacting with ISG15 and promoting its recruitment to the autophagy receptor p62 (Du et al., 2018). Unfortunately, we did not determine whether the autophagy receptor p62 recognizes K48-ubiquitinated RIG-I or the RIG-I protein itself in the presence of pUS2. This issue deserves further investigation in future studies.

In addition to in vitro mechanistic analyses, our in vivo experiments further demonstrated the biological significance of US2 during DPV infection. Deletion of the US2 gene significantly enhanced the host innate immune response in both DEF cells and infected ducks, as indicated by increased expression of IFN-α, IFN-β, OASL, and Mx. The expression patterns of IFN-β and ISGs differed between in vitro and in vivo systems. This discrepancy may be attributed to the simplified single-cell context in vitro, which lacks intercellular paracrine amplification, whereas in vivo infection involves coordinated immune responses across multiple cell types, leading to enhanced ISG induction (Pestka et al., 2004; Schoggins, 2014). Moreover, ducks infected with the US2-deleted virus exhibited markedly reduced pathogenicity, including lower mortality rates, normal weight gain, and significantly decreased viral loads in multiple tissues. Histopathological analysis further revealed that DPV-ΔUS2 infection induced relatively mild inflammatory changes, primarily characterized by inflammatory cell infiltration, whereas more severe inflammatory damage, including hemorrhage, necrosis, and immune organ atrophy, was observed in ducks infected with the parental and revertant viruses. These results highlight the critical role of pUS2 in DPV virulence and immune evasion. The deletion of the US2 gene attenuated the virulence of DPV in vivo. This reduction in pathogenicity may be due to multiple factors. On one hand, pUS2 may contribute to reduced viral replication efficiency. On the other hand, as an immune evasion factor, US2 deficiency could enhance host innate immune responses, including RIG-I-mediated signaling and type I interferon production, thereby indirectly limiting viral replication and pathogenicity.

Further studies are needed to identify the specific E3 ubiquitin ligases responsible for RIG-I ubiquitination and to determine whether K48-linked ubiquitination is directly recognized by p62. In addition, p62 knockdown experiments would further validate its role in the autophagy-mediated degradation of RIG-I.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the DPV tegument protein US2 suppresses host innate immunity by targeting the RIG-I signaling pathway. pUS2 induces K48-linked ubiquitination of RIG-I and promotes its degradation through a p62-mediated autophagy pathway. Consequently, downstream type I interferon signaling and the expression of IFN-β and ISGs were markedly inhibited in the presence of an intact US2 protein. In contrast, deletion of the US2 gene partially restored antiviral innate immune responses and was associated with reduced viral pathogenicity in ducks (Fig. 7). These findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism by which DPV evades host antiviral defenses and provide new insights into the complex interactions between DPV and the host immune system. Moreover, our study expands the understanding of herpesvirus-mediated immune evasion strategies and provides a potential theoretical basis for the development of novel antiviral targets and rationally attenuated DPV vaccine candidates.
