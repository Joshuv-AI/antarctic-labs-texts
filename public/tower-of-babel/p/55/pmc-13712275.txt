Intelligent nano delivery systems: Classification, stimuli-responsive mechanisms, and therapeutic applications in critical illnesses
Authors: Mengxin Zhao, Baofeng Guo, Quanhao Wang, Mei Yang, Jiaying Yang, Yueyang Liang, Wei Jiang, Ling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Critical illnesses such as sepsis and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) evolve rapidly and are dominated by systemic inflammatory dysregulation, oxidative stress, and abrupt microenvironmental remodeling, which collectively narrow the therapeutic window and amplify treatment-related toxicity. Conventional pharmacotherapy is often limited by non-selective biodistribution, insufficient lesion exposure, and dose-limiting adverse effects. Intelligent nano-delivery systems (INDS) address these barriers by coupling pathological cues (e.g., pH gradients, redox/ROS imbalance, and disease-associated enzymes) to defined carrier-state transitions, thereby enabling lesion-confined activation, controlled bioavailability, and, in selected designs, real-time readouts. In this review, we summarize the design logic of stimulus-responsive and targeting modules, and critically discuss how they translate endogenous signals into payload release or functional actuation in representative critical-illness settings. We further highlight emerging directions, including self-adaptive multi-input gating and biomimetic interfaces, and discuss key translational constraints (manufacturability, safety, and reproducibility) that must be addressed to advance INDS toward critical-care practice.

Introduction

Critical illnesses, including life-threatening conditions such as critical oncology, sepsis, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), are marked by abrupt physiological decline and a substantial risk of progression to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS)1. The clinical management of these disorders remains highly challenging owing to their acute instability, swift deterioration, and underlying drivers such as widespread inflammatory responses and intense oxidative damage. Three primary obstacles continue to limit effective pharmacotherapy in critical care settings: (i) inadequate target specificity, whereby systemically delivered agents are broadly distributed, leading to suboptimal accumulation at sites of pathology and reduced therapeutic impact2; (ii) significant off-target toxicity, as potent drugs (including certain chemotherapeutics and potent anti-inflammatory agents) affect healthy tissues indiscriminately, resulting in substantial adverse effects3; (iii) suboptimal pharmacokinetic profiles, characterized by poor aqueous solubility, rapid elimination, and brief half-lives, which hinder maintenance of effective local concentrations within the required therapeutic timeframe4.

To get around these problems, critical care medicines need to be able to go to the exact right spot, let the drug out slowly, and really cut down on bad side effects. Intelligent nano delivery systems, or intelligent nano-delivery systems (INDS), seem like a good way to handle this. These are super tiny carriers, usually just 1 to 100 nm wide, that are built to do smart work. They look like a solid option because they can target specific areas and turn on exactly when they are needed5. Leveraging distinct physicochemical attributes, these systems enable focused and conditionally activated therapy that directly counters the barriers of conventional treatment.

First, to address poor specificity, INDS can incorporate surface-bound targeting moieties (such as antibodies, aptamers, or peptides) for active recognition or capitalize on passive mechanisms, including the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect prevalent in neoplastic and inflamed tissues, thereby promoting preferential drug enrichment at diseased sites6. Second, to reduce systemic toxicity, INDS can remain relatively inert during circulation yet undergo pathology-triggered activation at the lesion, releasing payloads only in response to endogenous cues such as acidic pH, redox/ROS abnormalities, or disease-associated enzymatic activity7. Finally, to enhance pharmacokinetic control, nanoencapsulation improves drug solubility, shields sensitive molecules from early degradation, extends systemic circulation, and permits tunable release patterns via deliberate modulation of carrier architecture and material composition, thus refining local exposure and widening the effective therapeutic window8.

The present review examines the classification of INDS and their emerging applications in critical illnesses, with particular attention to severe oncology, acute myocardial infarction, acute lung injury, and ischemic stroke. Going beyond a conventional overview, we include a succinct comparative analysis across representative platforms in Section 4, together with discussion of major controversies, current translational maturity, persistent hurdles to clinical implementation, and prospective research directions. Section 2 outlines key stimulus-responsive mechanisms and targeting approaches that constitute the foundational toolkit for rational design, while Section 3 illustrates how these nanotechnological strategies mitigate the three core challenges in representative critical illness contexts.

Classification and targeting strategies of INDS

INDS differ from traditional drug delivery platforms by utilizing internal and external responsive mechanism to achieve site-specific activation of drugs, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy and reducing off-target toxicity9 (Fig. 1). Internal responsive INDS

Internal responsive INDS are engineered to sense and react to intrinsic biological signals within pathological microenvironments, thereby enabling site-specific drug release9 (Fig. 1). These platforms capitalize on distinct biochemical hallmarks of diseased tissues to achieve tailored therapeutic effects11. The following sections will classify these nanocarriers based on the primary stimuli, including pH, enzymes, redox potential, and biomolecules, with a focus on elucidating their design principles and clinical relevance, following established frameworks for endogenous stimuli-responsive drug delivery12.

pH-responsive INDS

Under physiological conditions, the extracellular pH of healthy tissues is tightly maintained at approximately 7.413. However, pathological conditions such as solid tumors (pH 6.5–6.9), inflammatory tissues (pH 5.0–6.0), and intracellular endo/lysosomal compartments (pH 4.5–6.5) are characterized by distinct acidic microenvironments14. pH-responsive nano delivery systems are designed to exploit these gradients as a trigger for controlled drug release. Two primary design strategies are employed in this field: The first strategy involves incorporating protonatable or deprotonatable chemical groups into the nanocarrier matrix. Polymers containing amine or carboxyl moieties (e.g., poly (l-histidine), poly (acrylic acid)) exhibit pH-dependent changes in charge and conformation. In neutral environments, they remain stable and encapsulate their payload. Upon exposure to acidic conditions, protonation or deprotonation induces electrostatic repulsion or shifts in hydrophobicity, leading to swelling, disassembly, or phase transition of the nanostructure and subsequent drug release15. The second strategy employs acid-labile chemical bonds such as hydrazone or acetal bonds, which hydrolyze at pH below 6.5, enabling precise drug release in intracellular compartments like lysosomes. Such systems hold particular value in acute and critical care therapies requiring rapid localized drug delivery. pH-sensitive micellar drug delivery systems can be categorized into the following two types16. Accordingly, for time-critical indications, linkers with fast cleavage under modest acidity are favored, whereas indications requiring sustained exposure should prioritize physiological stability and controlled, thresholded release.

Utilizing protonatable or deprotonatable chemical groups

pH-triggered release via ionizable groups exploits transitions between hydrophobic and hydrophilic states in acidic conditions, driving structural rearrangements such as swelling, chain reconfiguration, surface charge inversion, or solubility changes that promote payload liberation. Principal mechanisms include: (i) Protonation/deprotonation disrupting amphiphilic polymer structures, releasing payloads; (ii) Disassembly of amphiphilic micelle blocks, liberating drugs, and (iii) Reduced hydrophobicity in micelle cores, causing swelling and drug release17. Polymers containing carboxyl groups (such as polyacrylic acid PAA) are commonly used materials in such systems. In a neutral environment, the deprotonated carboxyl group confers hydrophilicity; under acidic conditions, protonation causes it to become hydrophobic, triggering precipitation or phase transition18. Reported acid-responsive materials can be broadly categorized into polymers, biomolecules, and inorganic materials19. By modifying the phospholipid bilayer with pH-sensitive groups, the liposome can release its payload in response to the lower pH commonly found in tumors20. Alternatively, pH-sensitive hydrogels can be utilized; these cross-linked polymer networks that swell and change in response to variations in pH levels 21.

Utilization of pH-sensitive chemical bonds

This design utilizes the cleavage of acid-labile bonds between drugs and polymers to trigger drug release, making it suitable for scenarios requiring rapid and precise release. Common bond types include imine bonds, hydrazone bonds, ester bonds, amide bonds, and oxime bonds. Recent studies have explored metal-coordination bonds and non-covalent bonds as supplementary pH-responsive mechanisms. These bonds remain stable under physiological pH conditions but undergo hydrolysis in acidic environments, thereby accelerating drug release22,23, further expanded the technical means of controllable delivery.

Acid-labile linkers enable pH-triggered release by remaining stable at physiological pH while accelerating cleavage in acidic microenvironments. Redox-responsive INDS

While pH gradients provide a robust trigger for extracellular or endosomal release, the intracellular environment offers other unique chemical discrepancies that can be exploited. Specifically, the redox potential difference between the oxidizing extracellular space and the reducing intracellular cytosol represents another powerful “on-switch” for INDS activation.

Principle

Pathological tissues, particularly solid tumors, sites of inflammation and ischemic regions, exhibit a profoundly different redox potential compared to healthy tissues31. This difference is primarily driven by two components: glutathione (GSH) and reactive oxygen species (ROS). Intracellular compartments contain high concentrations of the reducing agent GSH (5‒10 mol/L), whereas the extracellular environment and blood have much lower levels32. Conversely, pathological sites often feature elevated levels of ROS, such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which are highly reactive oxidizing molecules33. This significant redox gradient between diseased tissues and the normal physiological environment provides a robust trigger for the controlled release of therapeutic agents from INDS.

Classification

Redox-sensitive chemical bonds are integrated into INDS to facilitate triggered drug release in response to elevated glutathione (GSH) or reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels characteristic of pathological microenvironments. Common linkers include disulfide bonds, diselenide bonds, boronate esters, and platinum complexes.

Disulfide Bonds (–S–S–) represent the most widely utilized redox-sensitive linker. They remain stable in the low-GSH environment of the bloodstream but are rapidly cleaved within the cell. The breakdown mechanism is driven by a thiol-disulfide exchange reaction. Upon entering the cell, the nucleophilic thiolate anion of GSH attacks the sulfur atom of the disulfide bond. This cleavage breaks the –S–S– linkage, generating a free thiol group on the carrier and forming a mixed disulfide with GSH. Subsequently, a second GSH molecule may react to form oxidized glutathione (GSSG), rendering the cleavage irreversible. This rupture leads to the disassembly of the nanocarrier and the subsequent release of the encapsulated payload34.

Similar to disulfide bonds, diselenide bonds are also responsive to the redox microenvironment. The mechanism is twofold: they can be reduced to selenols (‒SeH) by GSH or oxidized to seleninic acid (‒SeOOH) by ROS35. Notably, the Se‒Se bond has a lower bond energy (≈172 kJ/mol) compared to the S‒S bond (≈240 kJ/mol). Consequently, diselenide-based systems are often more sensitive than their disulfide counterparts, responding to lower concentrations of both GSH and ROS, which can be advantageous for certain applications36,37.

Platinum-based drugs are pivotal in chemotherapy. In INDS, Pt (II) can form stable coordination bonds with drug or polymer ligands containing sulfur or nitrogen. Upon entering a high-GSH environment, the drug is released via a ligand exchange mechanism, where the thiol group of GSH, having a strong affinity for the platinum center, displaces the therapeutic agent38.

Thiol-modified nanoparticles offer dual responsiveness. They can be oxidized by ROS to form disulfide bonds, leading to cross-linking and structural changes, or they can directly react with certain ROS species. While synthetically simple and biocompatible, their stability as standalone responsive units can be limited39.

Boronate esters are formed in aqueous media by the reaction of boric acid and diol. They are particularly sensitive to ROS, especially hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The release mechanism involves the H2O2-mediated oxidation and subsequent hydrolysis of the boronate ester bond, which cleaves it into a phenol and boric acid, thereby releasing the conjugated cargo. This makes them highly suitable for diseases associated with oxidative stress. Their responsiveness to reducing agents like GSH, however, is weak40.

Redox-sensitive linkers enable triggered release by remaining stable under normal physiological redox conditions while undergoing rapid cleavage in response to GSH or ROS levels characteristics of pathological microenvironments. Biomolecule-mediated INDS

Biomolecule mediated INDS exploit disease associated biomolecular cues, including enzymes or proteins, metabolites, and nucleic acids, to enable site biased activation and improved therapeutic precision47. In this framework, aptamers are best viewed as modular molecular recognition elements that can be embedded across trigger classes rather than as an independent category, because the same aptamer chemistry can be used to sense metabolites (for example ATP) or specific proteins and translate binding into structural reconfiguration of the carrier48,49.

Enzyme or protein-responsive INDS

Enzymes and disease-associated proteins serve as highly specific triggers due to their aberrant expression and activity profiles in pathological environments. In conditions such as tumorigenesis and inflammation, enzymes like matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), cathepsins, and hyaluronidases can be significantly upregulated, enabling INDS designs that incorporate enzyme-cleavable peptide linkers or enzyme-degradable polymer backbones to achieve site-biased activation50. A primary advantage of this approach is catalytic amplification, where a small amount of enzyme can catalyze the cleavage of numerous chemical bonds, converting subtle biological signals into pronounced carrier disassembly and payload release51. Mechanistically, most designs follow a common sequence: local enzyme enrichment enables selective substrate cleavage or affinity binding, which then shifts carrier state and increases local bioavailability.

Beyond hydrolase driven unlocking, oxidoreductase responsive systems use abnormal redox enzyme activity to activate prodrugs or transform carriers in a spatially restricted manner. For example, NQO1 enriched lesions have motivated designs in which enzymatic reduction initiates prodrug activation or carrier conversion, improving selectivity in hypoxic or metabolically reprogrammed niches52,53. Enzyme gated activation is also frequently coupled with diagnostic readouts to form theranostic platforms. In representative designs, intracellular or lesion associated protease activity turns on fluorescence while simultaneously releasing a photosensitizer or drug, enabling low background imaging guided intervention, and similar logic has been extended to external energy modalities such as photothermal therapy where functional outputs are conditioned on enzymatic activation54,55.

Overall, enzyme and protein responsive INDS are most effective when the selected trigger shows robust disease bias, sufficient catalytic activity, and clinically relevant spatial colocalization with the intended target cells.

Metabolite-responsive INDS

Metabolites that accumulate in pathological microenvironments represent another class of endogenous stimuli suitable for triggering intelligent nano delivery systems. Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) stands out in this regard, functioning both as the principal intracellular energy carrier and as an extracellular signaling molecule released in substantial quantities during tumor progression and inflammation56. Cancer cells frequently exhibit elevated intracellular ATP levels resulting from metabolic reprogramming and accelerated proliferation57,58, while extracellular ATP also rises in diseased tissues, generating concentration gradients that can be exploited for selective activation. Systems responsive to ATP therefore aim to harness these differences to achieve localized carrier destabilization and therapeutic release, thereby increasing precision and limiting exposure of healthy tissues59.

Most designs in this category depend on ATP-binding aptamers, which are short single-stranded oligonucleotides selected for their ability to recognize ATP with high affinity and undergo predictable conformational shifts upon ligand engagement. This binding event is then mechanically coupled to structural changes in the nanocarrier that favor payload liberation.

Several implementation strategies have emerged in the literature. In one widely adopted configuration, aptamers hybridize with complementary DNA strands to form duplex structures that cap the pores of mesoporous silica nanoparticles or serve as gatekeepers on other porous carriers. In the absence of ATP, the duplex remains intact, effectively sealing the drug-loaded interior. When ATP concentrations rise, as occurs after cellular internalization, the aptamer preferentially binds the metabolite, displacing the complementary strand and opening the pores to allow diffusion of the encapsulated agent while minimizing leakage during circulation.

Another approach integrates aptamers into cross-linked nanogels or hydrogel networks, where hybridization contributes to network stability. ATP binding disrupts these cross-links through competitive displacement or conformational rearrangement, decreasing cross-link density, inducing swelling or gradual matrix erosion, and thereby facilitating sustained or accelerated drug diffusion in ATP-rich compartments.

Certain platforms employ ATP recognition to expose previously concealed functional elements on the carrier surface. For instance, binding may trigger conformational changes that reveal cell-penetrating sequences, additional targeting ligands, or catalytic domains otherwise shielded during systemic circulation. This unmasking enhances post-internalization processes such as membrane translocation, endosomal escape, or intracellular activation, improving overall bioavailability.

More sophisticated architectures incorporate ATP sensing into multi-input logic frameworks, combining it with other disease-associated cues such as lowered pH, elevated ROS, or specific enzymatic activity. Through AND or gating principles, these integrated systems achieve tighter control over activation, reducing the likelihood of off-target effects in heterogeneous patient populations or variable microenvironmental conditions.

Nucleic acid responsive INDS

Nucleic acids are not only therapeutic cargo but also disease-associated signals (e.g., miRNA/mRNA/DNA fragments) and programmable materials for constructing responsive nanostructures. Nucleic acids are composed of nucleotide units containing pentose, nitrogenous bases, and phosphate groups60, and can adopt diverse conformations (hairpins, multi-way junctions, i-motifs, A-form helices, Z-DNA, triplexes, and G-quadruplexes) that enable high stability, conformational flexibility, and sequence-specific recognition61. These properties support self-assembly, shape memory, and programmable reconfiguration, making nucleic acids suitable for constructing nanomachines and molecular robots62,63.

Mechanistically, nucleic acid-responsive INDS typically operate through hybridization/strand displacement or programmable cleavage (e.g., CRISPR-associated systems), converting nucleic-acid signals into outputs such as fluorescence activation, structural opening, or payload release. CRISPR technology has been adapted into nucleic acid-responsive systems for drug delivery and diagnostics by pairing guide RNA (gRNA) with Cas proteins to selectively recognize and cleave target sequences64. In responsive designs, CRISPR modules detect specific nucleic-acid cues (viral RNA or cancer-related mRNA) and trigger downstream actions, demonstrating the potential of nucleic-acid sensing and programmable actuation in precision nanomedicine.

Collectively, biomolecule mediated triggers extend responsiveness beyond generic physicochemical gradients by exploiting molecular specificity, and they can be combined with the targeting strategies in Section 2.2 to ensure that recognition and carrier transitions occur at the intended lesion and cellular sites.

External responsive INDS

External responsive INDS enable spatiotemporal control of drug release through exogenous physical stimuli, providing unmatched precision in intensive care scenarios (Fig. 1). This section analyzes three mainstream models with recent translational breakthroughs.

While endogenous stimuli-responsive systems exploit disease-specific signals, their efficacy is often compromised by inter-patient heterogeneity in pathological markers, such as variable tumor acidity or fluctuating enzyme expression levels. To overcome these limitations and decouple therapeutic action from the variability of internal biological signals, researchers have developed systems responsive to exogenous physical stimuli. These strategies utilize non-invasive external energy sources, including light, heat, or magnetic fields, to trigger drug release. This approach offers precise spatiotemporal control determined by the clinician, providing unmatched precision for intensive care scenarios.

Photo-responsive INDS

Light-triggered delivery systems leverage the absorption of electromagnetic radiation by photosensitive materials to induce chemical or physical changes that release therapeutic payloads. Mechanisms typically involve photothermal conversion or the generation of reactive oxygen species. Photothermal therapy utilizes agents that convert light energy into heat to disrupt the nanocarrier structure or ablate tumor tissue65,66, while photodynamic therapy relies on photosensitizers to generate cytotoxic singlet oxygen67.

The common mechanism of light-triggered drug delivery involves materials absorbing electromagnetic radiation and converting it into different energy forms68. Depending on the excitation wavelength, options include ultraviolet (UV; 10‒400 nm), visible light (400‒750 nm), or near-infrared (NIR; 750‒900 nm) as the activation source69. However, ultraviolet light has significant limitations: 1) the penetration depth is insufficient to lead to low drug release efficiency of deep lesions; 2) high phototoxicity causes tissue damage, which limits the clinical application value of ultraviolet triggered INDS69. While near-infrared (NIR) light demonstrates superior tissue penetration and reduced cellular damage, only a limited number of compounds can directly respond to NIR—due to insufficient energy to trigger photoreactions such as bond cleavage, isomerization, or cross-linking. To address this challenge, researchers have developed nanomaterials that convert incident NIR light into ultraviolet (UV)/visible light. This localized UV conversion enables remote control of drug release through NIR light 70.

Light sources can also be utilized in other nanomedicine therapies. Photosensitizers undergo photochemical reactions at specific wavelengths, generating reactive oxygen species or thermal energy to destroy target cells, thereby selectively eliminating diseased cells. These treatments are known as photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT). Photochemically endocytosed delivery represents an advanced technique in PDT, providing a strategy for triggering endosomal/lysosomal escape. This approach has been applied to deliver various drugs and biomolecules69.

Thermo-responsive INDS

Thermo-responsive properties can be naturally present in nanocarrier building blocks (e.g., those composed of temperature-sensitive polymers) or can be imparted through chemical modification (such temperature-sensitive nano delivery system are linked to drugs by enzymes or pH-sensitive bonds)71. Temperature fluctuations in the biological microenvironment can effectively trigger the drug release behavior of temperature-sensitive drug-coated nanoparticles. These temperature-sensitive polymers typically combine hydrophobic and hydrophilic functional groups to regulate their response characteristics. They can transition from a homogeneous dissolved state to a heterogeneous biphasic state through a “coil-sphere” conformational change, achieving hydration-to-dehydration conversion. This process requires only minimal temperature changes to initiate72.

There are two main types of temperature sensitive delivery systems: the lowest critical dissolution temperature (LCST) type and the highest critical dissolution temperature (UCST) type73. Polyacrylate alkylamides, particularly poly (N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAAm), are typical representatives of LCST polymers. When temperatures below the LCST, water molecules form hydrogen bonds with the polymer's polar groups, allowing the polymer to dissolve in water. Above 32 °C, water molecules are expelled from the network structure, and isopropyl dehydration causes structural contraction. Temperature-responsive nanoparticles have been developed using polyethylene glycolized polyasparagine derivatives. These amphiphilic copolymer-based nanoparticles can stably encapsulate drugs in water and trigger release through temperature changes. By precisely controlling release kinetics through temperature regulation, they demonstrate significant potential for intelligent drug delivery applications74.

Electromagnetic INDS

Electromagnetic fields hold promise for precise control or remote operation. Drug-controlled targeted delivery can be achieved through electrically activated plasma gold nanoparticles. Nucleic acids, functional proteins, and Cas9 unidirectional RNA-proteins can be delivered to both adherent and suspended cells via nanopore electroporation platforms.

The Chertok team75 developed polyethyleneimine-modified iron oxide nanoparticles for brain tumor drug delivery, utilizing a magnetic targeting and intrathecal-carotid strategy. The polyethyleneimine-coated nanoparticles enhance cellular uptake and drug payload delivery efficiency, while magnetic targeting enables them to penetrate the blood‒brain barrier and accumulate specifically at tumor sites. In vivo studies demonstrated that compared to non-targeted control groups, this approach significantly improved drug accumulation and therapeutic efficacy in brain tumors, highlighting the potential value of this systemic delivery strategy.

Multi-responsive INDS

Multi-responsive INDS are engineered to react to two or more distinct stimuli that coexist in diseased microenvironments (e.g., pH + redox, pH + enzyme, or an endogenous cue coupled with an external trigger). Such designs are particularly advantageous in critical illnesses, characterized by the simultaneous presence of multiple pathological factors. For instance, severe acidosis, oxidative stress (redox imbalance), and aberrant enzymatic activity in sepsis or ischemia‒reperfusion injury. By requiring the concurrence of multiple disease-specific triggers, multi-responsive systems add an extra layer of specificity to drug release, effectively “filtering out” normal tissues and minimizing premature leakage or off-target effects compared to single-responsive platforms76. In infection sites, for example, an acidic pH (5.0‒5.5) and elevated enzyme levels are both observed relative to normal tissue, and only when such combinations of cues are sensed do multi-responsive carriers unleash their therapeutic payload.

One strategy to achieve multi-responsiveness is to integrate distinct cleavable motifs into a single nanocarrier. For instance, researchers have developed polymeric carriers containing both acid-labile linkers (such as hydrazones) and reduction-sensitive disulfide bonds in their structure77,78. In these “pH + redox” systems, mild extracellular or endosomal acidity can hydrolyze the acid-labile linkages to induce partial destabilization of the carrier, priming it for cellular uptake. Once the carrier penetrates into cells, the abundant glutathione (GSH) in the cytosol then cleaves the disulfide crosslinks, causing full disassembly of the nanostructure and prompt drug release79. Such dual-triggered designs have been shown to achieve more complete and site-specific drug release than single-responsive systems, since both conditions must be met before the drug is liberated. A related approach is to create composite nanostructures where an outer layer is degradable by one stimulus and an inner core by another—for example, a nanoparticle with an enzyme-sensitive outer shell and a ROS-responsive hydrogel core that ensures drug release only in tissues rich in the specific enzyme and oxidative stress.

Hierarchical core–shell architectures are a widely used embodiment of multi-responsiveness, enabling stepwise “gated” drug release. In these designs, an outer shell (e.g. made of pH-sensitive or enzyme-degradable polymers) serves as a protective gatekeeper during circulation80,81. This shell remains intact under normal physiological conditions to prevent premature leakage, and it facilitates prolonged circulation or targeting (for instance, by carrying targeting ligands or charge-shielding groups)82,83, Upon accumulation at the diseased site, the outer layer responds to a macro-environmental cue—for example, it quickly dissolves in an acidic pH or is enzymatically cleaved by matrix metalloproteinases abundant in the tissue–thereby shedding the shell and exposing the therapeutic core. The removal of the shell not only triggers initial drug release but also often promotes cellular uptake of the now-exposed nanoparticle core. The inner core is typically stabilized by secondary linkers that respond to intracellular signals (such as disulfide bonds that break in the reductive cytosol, or ROS-cleavable linkers that break upon oxidative burst). Once the carrier is internalized by target cells, the high-glutathione and ROS-rich intracellular environment rapidly cleaves these inner linkages, resulting in a swift collapse of the core structure and a burst of drug release exactly where it is needed84. This multi-tiered release mechanism ensures that each stage–from bloodstream to tissue to cellular compartment–can be individually gated by the appropriate stimulus, markedly enhancing the precision of drug delivery. Notably, biomimetic nanocarriers–which camouflage nanoparticles with natural cell membranes–represent an advanced form of active targeting, complementing traditional ligand-based approaches (as detailed in the text of Section 2.2).

The stimuli-responsive mechanisms described above represent the core “intelligence” of modern nano delivery systems, enabling controlled drug release in response to pathological microenvironments. However, the clinical efficacy of these responsive mechanisms in critical illnesses ultimately depends on a fundamental prerequisite: sufficient nanocarrier accumulation at disease sites before triggering occurs. A pH-responsive nanoparticle cannot release its payload in an acidic tumor microenvironment if it never reaches the tumor; similarly, enzyme-cleavable linkers are ineffective without proximity to overexpressed proteases. This necessitates integration with targeted delivery strategies. The following section examines how passive targeting (exploiting pathophysiological characteristics such as enhanced vascular permeability) and active targeting (through biomimetic membrane coating and ligand modification) can be synergistically combined with stimuli-responsive mechanisms to create intelligent nano delivery systems specifically tailored for critical care applications, where rapid intervention and precise spatiotemporal control are paramount.

Targeting strategies for enhanced delivery

Targeting strategies in INDS aim to increase lesion-level bioavailability while minimizing systemic exposure and off-target toxicity. Practically, targeting can be achieved through engineered ligands (e.g., antibodies/peptides/aptamers/carbohydrates) or biomimetic interfaces. Notably, biomimetic active-targeted INDS can be viewed as a functional extension of ligand-based targeting: rather than presenting a single recognition ligand, these systems display a cell-derived membrane/vesicle “multivalent interface” that naturally carries adhesion and recognition proteins, thereby improving immune evasion and lesion homing in complex in vivo environments85.

Targeting in critical illness is a multi-step process that involves maintaining adequate circulation, enriching at inflamed or injured tissues, and achieving cellular access before any stimulus responsive activation can occur. Ligand engineered targeting primarily improves receptor level recognition, but its effectiveness depends on sufficient in vivo stability and reduced opsonization. Biomimetic interfaces provide an alternative targeting module by presenting a cell derived membrane or vesicle surface that carries multiple endogenous adhesion and recognition proteins, thereby simultaneously attenuating immune clearance and promoting lesion associated interactions86. For this reason, biomimetic nano delivery systems are discussed here as an integral component of active targeting strategies, rather than as a stand-alone category.

Active targeting INDS: biomimetic interfaces and ligand engineering

Biomimetic interface guided targeting (cell membrane/vesicle-derived INDS)

Bionic (biomimetic) active targeting INDS typically comprise a synthetic core and an outer cell membrane or vesicle-derived shell87.

The core loads therapeutic payloads or functional modules (e.g., immunomodulators, photo-responsive agents), whereas the biomimetic coating governs biological interactions after systemic administration, reducing opsonization and clearance, and enabling disease-site adhesion or cellular tropism through endogenous membrane proteins88. A key mechanistic advantage is that biomimetic coatings can simultaneously provide immune evasion (e.g., “don’t-eat-me” signaling such as CD47-mediated effects) and context-dependent homing/adhesion, which is often difficult to achieve with a single synthetic ligand89.

Recent advances extend beyond conventional membrane-coated nanoparticles to extracellular vesicle (EV)/exosome-based and organoid-derived extracellular vesicle (OEV) platforms. Because organoids recapitulate tissue-like microenvironments, OEVs may inherit enhanced biological complexity and functional relevance compared with standard EVs, offering a potentially more precise interface for disease intervention90,91. Nevertheless, biomimetic systems face translational barriers, including source heterogeneity, batch-to-batch variability, and manufacturing complexity, which directly impact reproducibility and biosafety. Accordingly, high-throughput characterization, process optimization, and quality control are increasingly emphasized; AI-assisted pipelines have been explored to accelerate phenotype identification and standardization, although robust validation and regulatory alignment remain necessary92. Ligand engineered targeting INDS

Ligand engineered targeting relies on displaying affinity motifs on the nanocarrier surface to recognize disease associated receptors or microenvironmental components, thereby increasing local bioavailability and reducing off target exposure105,106. The practical performance of ligand targeting depends on receptor density, accessibility in vivo, internalization pathways, and the balance between binding strength and nonspecific adsorption.

Antibodies and antibody derived formats remain the most established ligands because of their high affinity and specificity toward overexpressed antigens in diseased cells. Beyond monoclonal antibodies, recombinant formats and bispecific antibodies can broaden target coverage or introduce immune modulation functions. Antibody guided delivery has also been integrated with drug conjugation and nucleic acid delivery strategies to improve intracellular availability and pharmacological selectivity. However, antibody based ligands are often associated with higher manufacturing cost and potential immunogenicity, which can limit scalability and repeated dosing in some settings107.

Peptides and oligonucleotide aptamers provide complementary advantages in manufacturability, chemical modifiability, and size. Their smaller molecular footprint can facilitate dense surface presentation and reduce steric constraints, and selected peptides can promote tissue penetration or cellular entry in addition to receptor recognition108,109. Major limitations include protease susceptibility and short in vivo half-life, which can be mitigated by sequence engineering and chemical modification, including terminal capping, incorporation of non-natural amino acids, cyclization, and conjugation strategies that improve stability and pharmacokinetics110.

Carbohydrate based ligands and polysaccharide derived materials offer a biocompatible route to receptor associated targeting through lectin like interactions and cell surface receptor recognition111. Chitosan based systems are frequently explored because they combine carrier functionality with favorable biocompatibility and chemical tunability, and can be further integrated with receptor mediated or stimulus responsive modules to refine lesion bias112. In addition, glucose conjugation and other metabolite linked strategies have been explored to exploit disease associated uptake programs113. Representative receptor targeting designs, such as prostate specific membrane antigen directed nanomedicines, illustrate how ligand functionalization can improve selectivity and in vivo therapeutic performance when target expression and accessibility are appropriate114.

While ligand engineering can improve receptor level engagement, its benefit is often contingent on sufficient vascular access and residence time within pathological sites. Therefore, active targeting is frequently layered onto passive accumulation processes that arise from disease associated vascular leakage and impaired clearance, particularly in tumors and in selected inflammatory or ischemic lesions.

Passive targeted INDS

Passive targeted delivery exploits disease associated alterations in vascular permeability and local clearance to bias nanocarrier accumulation at lesion sites, most notably through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect115. This concept was first proposed by Matsumura and Maeda116 in the 1980s to explain the abnormal intratumoral accumulation of macromolecular therapeutics and nanoparticle-based carriers. At the core of the EPR effect is the fact that neovasculature in tumor or inflamed tissues is structurally abnormal: endothelial cells are loosely arranged and lack proper pericyte coverage, resulting in irregular fenestrations ranging from tens to several hundreds of nanometers, which render the vasculature highly permeable to nanoparticles and other macromolecules; Meanwhile, impaired lymphatic drainage within the lesion prevents efficient clearance of nanocarriers that have extravasated into the tumor interstitium, thereby markedly prolonging their local residence time117. Together, this increased vascular permeability and impaired lymphatic drainage constitute the EPR effect, enabling exogenous nanomedicines to passively accumulate within tumor tissues. This phenomenon is regarded as a fundamental theoretical basis for nanodrug delivery systems targeting solid tumors, as it allows higher drug concentrations to be achieved at the tumor site while reducing toxicity to normal tissues115.

Guided by the EPR effect, numerous nanocarrier-based drug delivery systems have been engineered for passive tumor targeting. Liposomes, polymeric micelles, and various nanoparticles can circulate for prolonged periods after intravenous administration and gradually extravasate through the highly permeable tumor vasculature into the tumor interstitium, where they become retained. Theoretically, this passive accumulation mechanism is broadly applicable: similar patterns of vascular leakage and retention can occur not only in solid tumors but also in pathological sites such as infections, chronic inflammatory lesions, and ischemic injuries118. For example, in the inflammatory zone following myocardial infarction, cytokine release can increase local capillary permeability, giving rise to a certain degree of EPR-like effect119. These observations suggest that the EPR effect may serve as a general mechanism for drug delivery not only in cancer but also in other inflamed or ischemic tissues. However, it is important to note that the extent and duration of vascular leakage vary substantially across diseases and tissue types; in non-tumor lesions, EPR-like effects are often transient and relatively modest118. Consequently, passive targeting strategies still face the challenge of insufficient accumulation in pathological sites outside of tumors.

One of the key determinants influencing the manifestation of the EPR effect is the physicochemical properties of the nanocarrier. The first critical factor is particle size: nanoparticles must be sufficiently small to traverse the endothelial fenestrations of tumor vasculature, yet large enough to exceed the glomerular filtration threshold in order to avoid rapid renal clearance120,121. It is generally believed that nanodrug delivery systems with diameters ranging from several tens to roughly just over one hundred nanometers are more likely to achieve optimal EPR-mediated accumulation. Conversely, carriers that are too large (typically several hundred nanometers or more) exhibit limited ability to penetrate the vascular wall and dense tumor stroma, resulting in suboptimal distribution and retention. Nanomedicines within the approximate size range of 10–100 nm generally demonstrate favorable EPR-mediated accumulation121, and some studies have reported that nanoparticles with diameters of 100–200 nm may achieve the highest tumor retention efficiency122. Therefore, in nanomedicine design, particle sizes are typically controlled within a range from several to several hundred nanometers to balance circulatory stability with effective tumor penetration.

The shape of nanoparticles likewise affects their behavior and efficiency during the EPR process. Carriers with different morphologies exhibit distinct characteristics in terms of hemodynamics, vascular retention within tumors, and tissue penetration. For example, high-aspect ratio elongated structures (such as nanorods and nanotubes) display unique orientation and rolling dynamics within blood vessels, and have been observed in certain models to exhibit enhanced retention within tumor vasculature and tissues123,124. Porous or mechanically flexible nanomaterials are also considered advantageous for traversing vascular fenestrations and improving tissue penetration. However, no consensus has been reached regarding the optimal particle shape. Comparative in vivo studies examining size-matched nanostructures of different morphologies (such as gold nanospheres, nanodiscs, nanorods, and nanocages) have shown that spherical particles exhibit the longest blood half-life and the highest overall tumor uptake in murine breast cancer models, whereas nanorods and nanocages penetrate more effectively into deeper regions of the tumor core125,126. These differences indicate that the impact of particle shape on EPR-mediated accumulation depends on both the intrinsic properties of the nanomaterial and the specific disease model. Thus, the optimal morphology must be determined by balancing these factors in accordance with the intended application.

Surface charge and surface functionalization represent another critical set of physicochemical parameters. Positively charged nanoparticles tend to interact more readily with negatively charged cell membranes through electrostatic attraction, thereby enhancing tumor cell uptake; however, their cationic nature also promotes opsonization by plasma proteins and clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, ultimately shortening their circulation time in the bloodstream127. In contrast, “stealth” nanocarriers with neutral or mildly negative surface charge—such as those modified with polyethylene glycol (PEG)—exhibit reduced nonspecific interactions with plasma proteins and immune cells, thereby prolonging their circulation half-life and facilitating passive accumulation121. Thus, the design of nanocarriers frequently utilizes surface engineering strategies, such as PEGylation and the incorporation of colloidal stabilizers. These approaches aim to optimize long-circulation behavior while enhancing tumor retention. Such modifications help maintain particle stability in the bloodstream to maximize the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, while also allowing for the release of therapeutic payloads and the execution of pharmacological actions upon reaching the tumor microenvironment.

In summary, the EPR effect provides an opportunity for passively targeted nanomedicines to accumulate within tumors and certain other pathological sites, offering important guidance for nanodrug design. However, its universality and effectiveness across different pathological conditions remain limited. Inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity of the EPR effect, along with unfavorable aspects of nanoparticle biodistribution and clearance, means that relying solely on EPR is often insufficient to achieve optimal clinical outcomes128. Therefore, in nanomedicine development, it is necessary not only to optimize particle size, shape, and surface properties to enhance passive targeting efficiency, but also to integrate active targeting ligands, tumor-microenvironment-responsive release mechanisms, and adjuvant strategies that improve tumor perfusion and vascular permeability, in order to compensate for the inherent limitations of the EPR effect. Only by fully addressing the heterogeneity and efficiency bottlenecks that the EPR effect presents during clinical translation can the therapeutic potential of passively targeted nanomedicines in cancer and other diseases be more effectively realized.

In summary, Section 2 delineates the core design toolkit of INDS, including stimulus responsive motifs that translate endogenous signals into controlled availability, and targeting strategies that determine where and to which cells these transitions can occur. Importantly, the clinical value of responsiveness depends on effective lesion access, and the value of targeting depends on whether it enables time appropriate, cell relevant intervention. In the following section, we map these design principles onto representative critical illness scenarios and illustrate how cue responsive chemistries, redox buffering, mediator sequestration, and biomimetic or ligand guided targeting are leveraged to address disease specific constraints such as rapid therapeutic onset, immune dysregulation, and organ selective delivery.

INDS in critical oncology

In critical oncology, “intelligence” is most convincing when a nanoplatform does more than accumulate. Practical INDS architectures therefore combine (i) access/retention modules (Section 2.2) and (ii) tumor-signal-gated activation chemistries (Section 2.1) that translate TME cues into defined structural transitions responsible for drug release or functional switching.

Pathophysiology and limitations of conventional treatment strategies

Critical oncology is characterized by extreme heterogeneity, metastatic dissemination, and microenvironmental barriers that jointly reduce effective intratumoral exposure. Abnormal vasculature elevates interstitial fluid pressure and disrupts convective transport, while dense ECM and collagen networks impede diffusion and limit penetration into hypoxic, acidic niches where resistance programs are enriched. Conventional systemic regimens remain dose-limited by off-target toxicity and often fail to eradicate microscopic residual disease. A further translational bottleneck is the lack of actionable readouts that distinguish insufficient intratumoral delivery from inadequate pharmacology, which complicates personalization in heterogeneous tumors129.

Mechanistic rationale: How INDS address oncology barriers

Mechanistically, oncology INDS typically implement a two-layer control strategy aligned with Section 2. First, targeting/anchoring (Section 2.2) increases residence and cellular entry through ligand‒receptor recognition or biomimetic interfaces. Second, TME-responsive gating (Section 2.1) converts tumor signals into carrier transitions that govern release130. Importantly, tumor triggers must be tied to explicit responsive structures: acidosis is commonly translated through acid-labile linkages (often implemented as pH-cleavable dynamic covalent motifs), reductive cytosolic environments through redox-cleavable motifs (commonly disulfide-like linkers), and protease-rich niches through enzyme-cleavable peptide gates. This architecture turns the TME from a barrier into a switch that controls when and where a payload becomes bioavailable131.

Reversing metabolic plasticity and drug resistance

Tumor metastasis and drug resistance are tightly coupled to metabolic plasticity, in which tumor cells alternate between mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis to maintain survival under therapeutic stress. Redox imbalance is a central driver of this switching, because both ROS excess and ROS insufficiency reshape transcriptional programs such as NRF2 dependent antioxidant adaptation and NF kappa B associated inflammatory survival, thereby promoting invasion and treatment resistance145,146. For this reason, a large class of INDS in this domain are designed to modulate ROS level or oxygenation state in a spatially and temporally restricted manner, using either externally applied physical inputs or redox active catalytic centers as the response element147,148.

In an ultrasound triggered catalytic design, the externally applied ultrasound serves as the initiating signal and is read by the piezoelectric ZnO component as the responding structure. Ultrasound stimulation drives charge separation on ZnO and initiates local catalytic ROS generation as the primary state change. Platinum grown on the ZnO surface acts as a catalytic amplifier that increases the efficiency of these surface redox reactions and supports local oxygen related reaction flux. Cholesterol oxidase functions as a biochemical responder to oxygen availability and uses oxygen to oxidize membrane cholesterol, which destabilizes lipid rafts as the key cellular structural transition. This sequence converts a localized physical input into catalytic redox chemistry and then into membrane architecture remodeling, which suppresses pseudopodia formation and limits metastatic motility as the final functional output147.

A respiration chain modulation strategy addresses radioresistance by treating tumor hypoxia and oxygen consumption as actionable biological signals. In a biodegradable assembly integrating atovaquone, IR820, and doxorubicin through noncovalent association, atovaquone acts as the responding bioactive component to mitochondrial electron transport activity and suppresses electron transport chain function, which decreases cellular oxygen consumption and shifts the microenvironment toward improved oxygen availability as the first state change. IR820 functions as the responding photothermal transducer and enables mild hyperthermia under irradiation, which improves perfusion as an additional microenvironment transition that further supports oxygen delivery. Within the same assembly, doxorubicin becomes more bioavailable through carrier loosening and increased exchange of noncovalently held cargo in the remodeled microenvironment, strengthening chemosensitization alongside radiosensitization. The resulting output is coordinated oxygen management with perfusion enhancement and increased treatment sensitivity in regions that would otherwise remain hypoxic and resistant149.

Reprogramming the immune axis for systemic control

Cancer immunotherapy enhances systemic anti-tumor immunity through active or passive regulation but faces challenges in delivering functional immunotherapeutics150, reversing TME151, overcoming low tumor immunogenicity and optimizing immune cell therapy efficacy. INDS address these by enabling targeted delivery, TME modulation and enchanced immune activation, improving outcomes for metastatic cancers. Unlike traditional therapies, NDDS facilitate immune cell access to metastatic sites (e.g., bones, lungs, lymph nodes), optimize antigen presentation, reverse primary tumor immune suppression, harness peripheral immune cells to prevent pre-metastatic niche formation, and suppress postoperative recurrence152.

A recurring target for immune reprogramming is the tumor associated macrophage compartment, because macrophages are abundant in many tumors and frequently polarize toward an immunosuppressive M2 like phenotype under Th2 skewed cytokine signaling1153. In this context, the microenvironment signal is the combination of macrophage abundance and immunosuppressive polarization cues, while the responding elements in INDS are typically macrophage addressing ligands or biomimetic surfaces together with immunostimulatory cargos. The key state transition is a shift in macrophage activation programs toward an M1 like phenotype, which increases antigen presentation capacity and proinflammatory effector signaling in the tumor microenvironment, thereby amplifying downstream T cell mediated tumor control as the functional output154,155.

A protease gated nucleic acid immunotherapy provides a direct and mechanistically clean intelligent response. In this nanorobot system, enrichment of MMP2 in the tumor microenvironment is the signal, and the peptide sequence GPLGVRGS serves as the responding chemical gate because it is selectively cleaved by MMP2. Proteolysis removes the protective constraint and converts the carrier from a shielded transport state into an active delivery state, which enables CpG to become bioavailable within the tumor and reach TLR9 competent cells. The arginine rich domain functions as the responding condensation motif that binds nucleic acids electrostatically and stabilizes CpG during delivery, while local CpG exposure triggers TLR9 signaling and promotes a broader immune activation cascade. This sequence links a tumor enriched protease signal to a specific gate cleavage transition and culminates in enhanced systemic antitumor immunity as the output156.

An exosome based nanovaccine addresses the common limitation that immunogenic cell death induced by localized therapies often fails to generate durable systemic protection against metastasis or recurrence. In this design, tumor derived antigenic content present on glioblastoma exosomes functions as the biological signal source, and the exosome membrane and cargo composition are the responding biomolecular structures that are naturally competent for uptake and processing by antigen presenting cells. The critical transition is efficient internalization by antigen presenting cells in lymph nodes followed by antigen processing and presentation, which drives dendritic cell maturation and primes cytotoxic and memory T cell responses. The output is strengthened cytokine production and durable immune protection at the organism level157.

Targeted immune checkpoint inhibitor delivery is most persuasive when the manuscript specifies the gating logic rather than stating confinement in general terms. In these approaches, tumor acidity, protease enrichment, or immune cell addressing markers serve as signals, while the responding structures are engineered linkers or coatings that either unmask binding domains or release checkpoint inhibitors locally. The relevant transition is activation dependent unshielding or cleavage that increases local checkpoint inhibitor bioavailability in the tumor while limiting systemic exposure, with the output being improved response rates and reduced off target immune related toxicity when compared with untargeted systemic administration158.

Integrated application of diagnosis and treatment

Theranostic INDS integrate imaging probes with therapeutic payloads to enable real-time localization and treatment assessment, addressing the challenges posed by tumor heterogeneity. Stimulus-responsive materials are employed to maintain payload stability during circulation while triggering release specifically within the tumor. Concurrently, the imaging component visualizes biodistribution or microenvironmental changes. This design establishes a cohesive link between drug delivery, site-specific activation, and therapeutic monitoring, thereby significantly reducing off-target systemic toxicity159,160. Furthermore, leveraging the unique properties of nano delivery systems, these controlled-release drug delivery systems can be further integrated with tumor imaging to precisely track drug delivery pathways 161.

Environment responsive metal ion imaging systems exemplify this requirement for mechanism specificity. In many designs, tumor acidity and elevated hydrogen peroxide act as signals and are read by metal containing components as responding structures because their coordination state, valence, or solubility shifts under these conditions. The key transition is conversion into a more imaging active species, such as generation of a paramagnetic ion that increases magnetic resonance signal or formation of a higher attenuation state for computed tomography. The output is contrast enhancement that reflects microenvironment engagement and supports spatial confirmation of where therapeutic activation is expected to occur (Fig. 4A)162. Hybrid magnetic nanoparticle liposome systems illustrate a concentration linked reporting logic. Tumor accumulation serves as the signal, iron based magnetic cores act as the responding structure, and increased local iron concentration produces the transition of shortened relaxation times that strengthens magnetic resonance contrast. Remaining challenges

The key translational challenges include (i) ensuring that trigger thresholds (pH/protease/ROS) used for activation are clinically representative across patient heterogeneity, (ii) disentangling delivery benefit from therapeutic payload effect in robust comparative studies, and (iii) demonstrating scalable manufacturing and reproducible stimulus-response kinetics for regulatory acceptance.

INDS for ALI and ARDS

ALI and ARDS is dominated by abrupt inflammatory escalation and oxidative/proteolytic bursts, making Section 2.1 (ROS/enzyme-responsive motifs) particularly relevant166. In contrast to oncology, feasibility in ARDS depends strongly on whether delivery can occur early enough and whether carriers can access the correct lung regions under marked ventilation perfusion heterogeneity.

Pathophysiology and limitations of routine treatment strategies

ALI and ARDS arise from direct or indirect lung insults and are characterized by diffuse alveolar damage, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema, and severe hypoxemia, often culminating in respiratory failure167,168. Current management is largely supportive, but glucocorticoids and systemic anti-inflammatories show inconsistent benefit and carry systemic risks169, while ventilator-induced lung injury can exacerbate damage170,171. Systemic drugs often fail to reach effective concentrations within flooded or collapsed alveoli, leading to heterogeneous pulmonary exposure despite severe pathology172.

Mechanistic mapping: How INDS address ARDS barriers

ARDS provides endogenous signals that can be converted into actionable delivery logic, most notably oxidative bursts and enzyme enrichment together with inflamed endothelium that can be exploited by targeting and biomimetic interfaces173, aligned with Section 2.1 and Section 2.2. ARDS oriented INDS are therefore most persuasive when they achieve lung enriched exposure, then translate ROS or protease signals into a defined carrier state change that determines local bioavailability, and finally incorporate monitoring elements that allow biodistribution to be linked to inflammatory dynamics rather than being inferred indirectly.

Targeted drug delivery to the lungs

Inhaled INDS improve pulmonary selectivity primarily because the route itself places carriers into the airspace compartment, generating high local concentration while reducing systemic exposure174. When nanoparticles additionally present ligands that recognize diseased lung cells, the biological signal becomes receptor enrichment on the target cell population, and the responding structure is the surface displayed ligand on the carrier. Ligand receptor engagement converts a freely mobile deposited particle into a retained and uptake competent state through strengthened adhesion and receptor mediated internalization. The resulting output is higher intracellular and intratissue drug availability in inflamed regions compared with non binding carriers, which is particularly important in ARDS where vascular barrier disruption and edema can otherwise decouple plasma exposure from alveolar delivery175.

Regulate inflammatory pathways and antioxidant stress

During the acute phase of ARDS, cytokines and chemokines together with infiltrating inflammatory cells injure epithelium and endothelium and disrupt junctional integrity, promoting leakage and cell death176,177. INDS can improve anti-inflammatory efficacy when inflamed lung tissue provides the targeting signal, the responding element is a carrier surface that binds inflamed endothelium or inflammatory cells, and the transition is preferential retention and cellular entry within the inflamed compartment. This transition elevates local bioavailability of glucocorticoids or NF kappa B pathway inhibitors at the lesion, producing the output of suppressed cytokine amplification with less systemic exposure than free drugs.

Oxidative stress is a parallel driver and the mechanistic basis of ROS related intelligence should be stated explicitly because two distinct paradigms exist. In the cleavage triggered paradigm, excessive ROS in inflamed lung lesions serves as the signal and is sensed by ROS labile chemical bonds embedded in the carrier matrix. In a representative dexamethasone formulation using thioketal chemistry, the thioketal bond is the responding structure. Oxidative cleavage fragments the polymer network and shifts the carrier from an intact encapsulating state to a permeable or disassembling state. This transition accelerates dexamethasone release preferentially within ROS high inflammatory foci, producing lesion biased pharmacologic suppression178.

In the catalytic buffering paradigm, ROS functions primarily as the reaction substrate rather than a switch for carrier breakdown. In a representative cerium dioxide nanozyme system, excess ROS is the signal substrate and the responding structure is the redox active Ce3 plus and Ce4 plus centers on the cerium oxide surface together with oxygen vacancy associated active sites. The key transition is sustained redox cycling at the catalytic interface rather than polymer cleavage. The output is continuous ROS scavenging that attenuates oxidative damage and downstream inflammatory amplification even without a release event179.

This distinction matters because cleavage triggered systems are judged by release kinetics and trigger thresholds, whereas catalytic systems are judged by catalytic turnover, resistance to biofluid deactivation, and long-term pulmonary fate.

Encapsulation based antioxidant delivery should be framed with the same logic. The signal is the short half-life and instability of free antioxidants in vivo together with oxidative burden at the lesion, the responding structure is the protective carrier matrix that shields enzymes such as SOD or catalase from degradation, and the transition is prolonged residence and preserved activity in the lung. The output is higher effective antioxidant function in situ compared with free antioxidants that clear rapidly.

Integrated diagnosis and imaging applications

Theranostic INDS integrate imaging capabilities with therapeutic functions to facilitate the real-time assessment of lung delivery and inflammation. This approach addresses a critical gap in ARDS management, which is often limited by a lack of dynamic monitoring tools. Specifically, fluorescent labels can track nanocarrier localization within inflamed alveolar regions, while magnetic components serve as MRI contrast agents that simultaneously deliver therapeutics. This dual functionality enables researchers to correlate biodistribution with inflammation severity, thereby guiding dose optimization in preclinical models180,181.

Remaining challenges

Clinical translation remains constrained by the interplay of access and triggering. Ventilation perfusion heterogeneity limits whether an inhaled formulation can reach poorly ventilated distal units, while intravenous systems face liver and spleen sequestration that reduces effective lung dose. Even when the correct lung region is reached, mucus and edema can prevent carriers from contacting the cellular compartment that provides the intended trigger field. Finally, long term pulmonary safety including fibrosis risk and the fate of inorganic or catalytic components must be established with rigorous evaluation before human use182.

INDS for acute myocardial infarction (AMI)

Pathophysiology and limitations of traditional treatment strategies

AMI is caused by coronary thrombosis and occlusion leading to ischemic necrosis, followed by ROS production, mitochondrial dysfunction, calcium overload, enzyme and cytokine upregulation, and immune cell infiltration that drives cell death and inflammation183,184, has a high mortality rate and risk of complications. Conventional treatment strategies include percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy, oxygen therapy, and drug therapy185, and significantly improve the survival rate of patients. Inadequate control of inflammation and insufficient myocardial repair contribute to fibrosis and long term dysfunction186. Rapid blood flow and myocardial motion wash therapeutics away, leading to minimal myocardial retention and systemic risk such as hypotension for vasoactive agents187. Precision deployment is also difficult because time to treatment varies and patient heterogeneity is large188. The pathophysiology is phase dependent, with acute needs centered on limiting oxidative and inflammatory injury and later needs centered on remodeling and repair.

Mechanistic mapping: How INDS address AMI barriers

AMI provides endogenous trigger landscapes including local acidosis, oxidative bursts, altered redox state, thrombus associated interfaces, and inflamed endothelium. INDS address AMI barriers most credibly when myocardium homing interfaces convert infarct associated signals into increased retention, and when pH or redox responsive modules convert ischemia reperfusion cues into defined carrier transitions that determine release or functional activation in a phase aligned manner.

Targeted myocardial drug delivery

Myocardial specific targeting relies on infarct associated molecular signals such as exposed extracellular matrix components, activated endothelium markers, or thrombus related interfaces189. The responding structures are cardiac homing peptides, antibodies, or biomimetic membranes on the carrier surface. Ligand receptor or ligand substrate engagement converts a rapidly circulating carrier into a retained lesion associated state through stronger adhesion and enhanced endocytic entry, producing the output of higher local bioavailability than non-targeted formulations190.

For stimulus control, ischemic acidosis can serve as the signal when carriers include proton responsive motifs191. The responding structures are protonatable groups or pH labile linkers embedded in the carrier architecture. Protonation or acid triggered bond cleavage shifts the carrier from a stable transport state to a destabilized or permeable state, which increases payload release in the ischemic compartment192. Intracellular reductive capacity can provide a second signal and is sensed by disulfide type linkers or crosslinks. Reductive cleavage shifts the carrier to a disassembled state after cell entry, producing the output of intracellularly amplified release and stronger pharmacologic action in the target cells193.

Regulating inflammatory responses and promoting wound healing

In AMI, excessive inflammatory responses exacerbate myocardial injury and promote adverse ventricular remodeling. Intelligent nano-delivery systems can precisely deliver anti-inflammatory drugs (such as dexamethasone or NF-κB inhibitors) to inflamed myocardial regions, inhibiting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and TNF-α. siRNA-loaded nanocarriers can silence inflammation-related genes, providing molecular-level regulation. After myocardial infarction, cardiac tissue is replaced by scar tissue, which blocks electrical signals in the infarcted area. As ventricular remodeling progresses, ventricular walls thin and ventricular stress increases. Providing mechanical support and reconstructing electrical conduction to myocardial tissues has been proven effective in promoting post-MI cardiac function. However, most adhesive hydrogels exhibit poor mechanical properties that fail to meet the requirements for cardiac patches195,196. Accordingly, He et al.197 developed a Janus hydrogel patch with responsive, reversible, asymmetric adhesion that supports infarct repair while reducing postoperative tissue adhesion and inflammatory invasion. Importantly, its on-demand detachment property enables noninvasive removal with minimal biological agents, addressing the clinical drawback of irreversible adhesives that are difficult to remove once placed.

Antioxidation and myocardial protection

Additionally, myocardial infarction triggers the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which further exacerbate inflammation, necrosis, and fibrosis198. These factors serve as primary drivers of AMI and reperfusion injury, leading to cardiomyocyte apoptosis and pathological progression199. Persistent oxidative damage and inflammatory responses not only induce cardiomyocyte apoptosis and injury but also impede normal myocardial repair and regeneration. Reducing ROS and inflammatory factor levels in tissues can prevent adverse cellular signaling cascades following myocardial infarction, minimize cardiomyocyte apoptosis and necrosis, and inhibit ventricular remodeling200. In a ROS responsive and ultrasound responsive bilayer microneedle system designed for in situ sequential therapy, elevated ROS in the acute phase is the signal and is read by the ROS responsive upper layer as the responding structure. The transition is ROS triggered release of cerium dioxide nanoparticles from the microneedle layer, increasing local catalytic ROS scavenging. The output is reduced oxidative stress and inflammation during the acute stage. Ultrasound then serves as a controllable external signal for the lower layer, where ultrasound responsive micro reactor components act as the responding elements and undergo an activation transition that generates therapeutic mediators reported to support later stage repair. The output is phase adapted myocardial protection and remodeling control in a sequential manner (Fig. 5B)201. A thrombus specific and responsive biomimetic nanomedicine provides an especially clear example of multi signal logic in AMI. Thrombus presence is the signal and is read by the platelet membrane coating that displays integrins and glycoproteins able to interact with damaged endothelium and fibrin. This targeting transition concentrates the carrier at the clot. The acidic ischemic microenvironment then becomes a second signal, read by a pH responsive core built from phenylboronic acid modified branched polyethyleneimine complexes, which transitions by decomposing in acidic conditions and releasing tissue plasminogen activator to reopen the artery. During reperfusion, ROS becomes a third signal and is countered by release of the antioxidant protocatechualdehyde that becomes bioavailable to scavenge ROS, producing the output of combined thrombolysis and protection from reperfusion injury (Fig. 5C)202.

Integrated diagnosis and treatment platform with monitoring applications

An integrated diagnostic treatment platform is most defensible when biomarker sensing is described as a signal response process rather than a generic detection claim. In one stop immunosensing SERS platforms for multiple cardiac biomarkers, biomarker concentration is the signal and is read by antibody recognition elements on plasmonic nanostructures. Formation of the antigen antibody immunocomplex changes the local electromagnetic enhancement environment, which is the state transition that modulates the Raman signal. The output is quantitative, multiplex biomarker readout that supports rapid screening and longitudinal tracking after AMI (Fig. 5D)203.

While targeting efficacy has improved, a critical translational gap remains regarding the timing of intervention. Most INDS are administered post-infarction in animal models, but in clinical settings, the time-to-needle varies drastically. Future systems must demonstrate stability during the hyper-acute phase and efficacy in the sub-acute remodeling phase. Furthermore, the long-term fate of inorganic components (e.g., gold nanorods used for conductivity) in the beating heart requires rigorous nanotoxicological evaluation.

Remaining challenges

The most persistent barriers are rapid washout from the beating heart, phase heterogeneity that makes timing decisive, and the gap between controlled animal dosing windows and variable clinical time to needle. Future evaluations should align observation windows with clinically realistic timelines and establish safety profiles for inorganic or functional components that may persist in mechanically active myocardium204.

INDS for ischemic stroke

Pathophysiology and limitations of conventional treatment strategies

IS is an acute cerebrovascular event caused by vessel occlusion, resulting in cerebral ischemia and hypoxia with high mortality and disability205. Brain injury progresses through a rapidly evolving cascade. Energy failure and oxygen deprivation trigger stress responses that elevate cytokines and ROS and activate microglia and astrocytes. Glial-derived mediators then compromise blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity, permitting leukocyte infiltration (including neutrophils) that amplifies inflammation, neuronal death, BBB disruption, and edema. During the subacute and chronic phase, macrophages can contribute to debris clearance and tissue repair. Overall, inflammation, apoptosis, oxidative stress, and glutamate excitotoxicity jointly drive disease progression206,207.

Current clinical interventions include intravenous thrombolysis such as recombinant tissue plasminogen activator, abbreviated as rt PA, mechanical thrombectomy, and supportive therapies. These approaches are effective for early recanalization but face limitations that motivate intelligent nano delivery systems208. Reperfusion is a double-edged sword because restoring flow can trigger secondary edema, inflammation, and hemorrhagic transformation. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage after rt PA has been reported at about six to eight percent and is associated with increased mortality209,210. Drug delivery is also constrained by the disease itself. Blood brain barrier restriction, hypoperfusion in the ischemic core and penumbra, and rapid systemic clearance lead to insufficient and heterogeneous drug exposure in target brain regions211. Because time is brain, an ideal strategy should produce rapid lesion enriched bioavailability and ideally provide feedback that supports timing sensitive dosing.

Mechanistic mapping: How INDS address IS barriers

IS oriented INDS draw on Section 2 to improve effective delivery under blood‒brain barrier restriction and hypoperfusion through targeting and size and surface engineering, to confine action to diseased tissue by coupling therapy to injury linked signals such as oxidative bursts and pathway activation, and to integrate monitoring readouts that support stratification and timing sensitive intervention212. For stroke, the mechanistic bar for intelligence is not only selectivity but kinetics and safety, because platforms must act within a narrow window while remaining safe under hemorrhagic risk.

Targeted drug delivery to brain tissue

A monitoring enabled lipid nanodroplet design illustrates how release verification can be built into delivery rather than inferred after the fact. Post stroke microthrombi and injured microvasculature provide the localization signal that concentrates nanodroplets at the neurovascular lesion. The responding structure for release reporting is a Förster resonance energy transfer pair integrated into the nanodroplet, in which donor and acceptor remain in close proximity while the cargo is retained within an intact carrier. When the carrier loses integrity and the payload is liberated in situ, molecular separation of the donor and acceptor alters energy transfer efficiency and produces a measurable change in the optical readout. Regulating neural inflammation

For pathway focused neuroinflammation control, the mechanistic claim becomes strongest when an upstream molecular driver is explicitly linked to the material response. Cytosolic nucleic acids released by injured cells act as an inflammatory stimulus signal that feeds microglial cGAS–STING activation. In a combined design, the responding chemical structure is a cerium based motif with DNase like activity that can interact with and cleave phosphodiester backbones, reducing nucleic acid stimuli that would otherwise activate the pathway. In parallel, the responding pharmacologic component is a small molecule STING inhibitor such as C176 that suppresses STING signaling once delivered. The critical transition is removal of nucleic acid triggers together with local pathway inhibition, which reduces microglial inflammatory amplification. The output is stronger neuroprotection than either element alone while minimizing systemic exposure by concentrating action in the inflamed brain compartment (Fig. 6C)215.

Inflammation resolution strategies aim to accelerate the transition from damaging inflammation to repair. Injured neurovascular regions express adhesion and chemotactic cues that serve as a homing signal. Neutrophil membrane derived nanovesicles act as the responding biomolecular interface because their membrane proteins retain inflammatory trafficking and adhesion features that recognize activated endothelium. The transition is enrichment at injured neurovascular sites and local vesicle uptake, which increases local bioavailability of a pro resolving mediator such as Resolvin D2. The output is a shift toward resolution programs with improved neurological outcomes in an IS model (Fig. 6D)216.

Antioxidant and brain tissue protection

Oxidative stress is a major driver of ischemic stroke and reperfusion injury, leading to neuronal apoptosis and blood‒brain barrier damage217,218.

Functional nanoparticles with reactive oxygen species scavenging activity are therefore mechanistically aligned, particularly when they support neurovascular repair during the evolving reperfusion phase. In a combined pharmacologic and nanozyme formulation integrating a small molecule neuroprotectant with ceria, the injury signal is reactive oxygen species enrichment within the ischemic and reperfused tissue. The responding structure is the ceria surface redox centers that buffer reactive oxygen species through catalytic cycling, while the co delivered drug contributes complementary neuroprotection once it becomes locally bioavailable. The relevant transition is sustained antioxidant activity within injured tissue enabled by the nanosystem, which extends functional exposure beyond what is achievable with rapidly cleared free antioxidants. The output is improved neurological deficits together with angiogenesis supportive effects in mouse models219.

Integrated diagnosis and treatment and monitoring applications

A theranostic approach is most credible when the imaging physics is tied to a material state that reports delivery or tissue status. For magnetic nanoparticle carriers, the signal is magnetic resonance interrogation and the responding structure is the magnetic core whose relaxation effects depend on local concentration. The transition is a measurable change in contrast within ischemic tissue where carriers accumulate, which can be paired with neuroprotective payload delivery to relate distribution to effect. For fluorescently labeled systems, the responding structure is the fluorophore that emits upon excitation, and the transition is the spatial redistribution of fluorescence intensity as particles traverse and accumulate in brain regions. The output is the ability to track biodistribution in real time and reduce unnecessary exposure when hemorrhagic risk makes overtreatment hazardous220.

Remaining challenges

Translation is hindered by stroke heterogeneity, time dependent variability in triggers, and safety concerns including hemorrhagic risk. Monitoring enabled stratification will likely be required to match activation profiles to patient specific pathophysiology221. Because pH and reactive oxygen species levels vary markedly across patients and with time from onset, platforms may need to evolve toward diagnostic guided selection rather than one size fits all formulations222.

INDS for sepsis

Pathophysiology and limitations of the routine treatment strategy

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. The pathology involves cytokine storm biology driven by overactivated macrophages and severe oxidative stress, together with endothelial dysfunction, microcirculatory abnormalities, and mitochondrial damage that exacerbate organ failure223. Current treatment relies on antibiotics and supportive measures, but outcomes remain limited by rapid clearance of therapeutics, poor tissue targeting, and the difficulty of neutralizing reactive oxygen species while modulating immunity without systemic immunosuppression224.

Mechanistic mapping: How INDS address sepsis barriers

Sepsis oriented INDS are most persuasive when they implement mediator interception and cell guided immunomodulation. Pathological mediators such as reactive oxygen species and endotoxin act as actionable signals that can be neutralized directly, while immune cell receptor expression and inflammatory microenvironment cues guide delivery to the cellular sources of cytokine amplification225. Redox responsive modules further allow payload availability to increase in tissues with the highest oxidative burden, aligning with Section 2.1 and Section 2.2 226.

Immunomodulation and ROS related organ protection

Sepsis can be viewed as a coupled cascade in which upstream pathogen derived triggers initiate myeloid activation, and downstream oxidative injury amplifies inflammation in return. Circulating endotoxin and other danger signals provoke macrophages and neutrophils to release cytokines and generate reactive oxygen species. The resulting redox imbalance accelerates lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in vulnerable organs, while inflammatory signaling further elevates oxidative stress, forming a self reinforcing injury loop227. A coherent INDS framework therefore benefits from being organized by where the design intervenes in this loop, since different materials read different signals and produce different state changes that ultimately determine therapeutic output.

One intervention point is downstream tissue protection, where the dominant signal is oxidative burden within injured organs. Ceria curcumin hybrid nanoparticles illustrate a coupled redox and immune mechanism. Reactive oxygen species enrichment and lipid peroxidation pressure constitute the injury signal, while ceria surface redox centers are the responding structure that catalytically buffers reactive oxygen species and limits lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. Curcumin serves as a responding pharmacologic component that modulates inflammatory signaling within immune cells once delivered. The transition is reduction of oxidative stress coupled with suppressed inflammatory signaling and a shift in macrophage activation programs toward a reparative phenotype. The output is attenuation of the coupled oxidative immune pathology and protection of organ function such as myocardial preservation in lipopolysaccharide induced sepsis models228.

A second intervention point is upstream trigger removal, which prevents the loop from escalating in the first place. Here, lipopolysaccharide in circulation is the signal, and an endotoxin binding biomolecular interface is the responding structure. In macrophage disguised magnetic nanocomposites, CD14 retained on the macrophage membrane coating binds endotoxin and converts freely circulating lipopolysaccharide into a nanoparticle associated state that is less available to engage TLR4 signaling on host cells. The state change is functional depletion of endotoxin activity from the circulating pool, and the output is reduced cytokine amplification with improved outcomes in endotoxemia and bacterial sepsis models229. This mechanism is conceptually distinct from redox buffering because the therapeutic effect originates from trigger sequestration rather than downstream pathway blockade.

A third intervention point sits at the level of immune effector cells, where the goal is to reshape cytokine output at its cellular source without inducing systemic immunosuppression. Cell directed immunomodulation becomes convincing when receptor biology is specified and the delivery transition is linked to intracellular bioavailability. In an ose modified exosome strategy for sepsis induced acute lung injury, mannose receptor enrichment on macrophage subsets serves as the targeting signal, and surface mannose on the exosomes is the responding structure that drives preferential uptake. The transition is receptor mediated internalization that concentrates the nucleic acid cargo within macrophages, enabling post uptake gene regulation that shifts inflammatory output locally in the lung rather than relying on systemic immunosuppression. The functional output is improved inflammatory control and lung protection in a sepsis induced injury model, with the intelligent element residing in receptor guided cellular partitioning of the therapeutic payload230.

Some platforms are more appropriately framed as immune state modulation rather than selective delivery, and placing them after the receptor guided example helps clarify that not all benefits arise from targeting. For gold nanoparticle treatment in cecal ligation and puncture models, systemic inflammatory stress is the dominant signal and the nanomaterial immune interaction surface is the responding structure. The state change is macrophage phenotype reprogramming with downstream cytokine remodeling, producing organism level protection as the output231. Presenting such cases explicitly as phenotype shifting avoids overstating selectivity and keeps the logic consistent with the mechanisms actually claimed.

Finally, stimulus responsive release provides a unifying material design layer that can reinforce any of the three intervention points by confining where and when a payload becomes bioavailable. In severe lesions, reactive oxygen species enrichment and enzyme upregulation serve as signals, while ROS labile linkers or enzyme cleavable motifs embedded in carriers serve as responding chemical structures. Trigger driven bond cleavage increases permeability or destabilizes the carrier, creating the state change that elevates local payload bioavailability in tissues with the highest oxidative burden. When the payload is a pro resolving mediator such as Resolvin D2, localized release promotes resolution programs and limits collateral damage in susceptible organs such as lung and kidney (Fig. 7D). Diagnostic and monitoring nano platforms

Nano diagnostic platforms typically convert biomarker recognition into an amplified physical signal. Sepsis associated proteins, nucleic acids, pathogenic DNA, or intact bacteria provide the biochemical signal. The responding structures are affinity ligands such as antibodies, aptamers, or complementary oligonucleotides presented on gold, magnetic, fluorescent, or lipid nanoparticles. Binding and complex formation produce a measurable transition, such as altered plasmonic behavior, changed magnetic relaxation, or fluorescence modulation, which yields the output of improved sensitivity and earlier detection compared with non-amplified assays232.

Remaining challenges

A major limitation is timing because sepsis progresses rapidly yet many nanosystems require time to distribute. Future systems must demonstrate activation kinetics under clinically relevant reactive oxygen species and enzyme levels, safe clearance compatible with sepsis associated acute kidney injury risk, and biomarker guided deployment that matches immune phase233.

INDS for AKI

Pathophysiology and limitations of routine treatment

The kidney maintains systemic homeostasis while receiving roughly 20% to 25% of cardiac output, which supports efficient clearance of metabolic wastes. At the same time, intensive solute exchange along renal tubules imposes a high oxygen demand that accounts for about 7% of whole body oxygen consumption, leaving renal tissue continuously exposed to hypoxia susceptibility and diverse circulating insults234,235. Acute kidney injury, abbreviated as AKI, refers to a rapid decline in renal function with impaired waste elimination. Common causes include renal ischemia reperfusion injury, sepsis, and nephrotoxins, and clinical manifestations can involve metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, volume overload, and reduced glomerular filtration rate236. Current care remains largely supportive and includes fluid management, correction of electrolyte disturbances, pharmacologic symptom control such as diuretics or vasoactive agents, and renal replacement therapy such as dialysis237.

These approaches can stabilize patients but often fail to interrupt the injury biology that drives progression and incomplete repair. AKI is typically accompanied by strong inflammatory activation and oxidative stress that promote tubular cell apoptosis and interstitial fibrosis, yet routine therapies rarely deliver effective control of cytokine circuits such as IL-6 and TNF-alpha or of reactive oxygen species within the injured kidney238. Drug delivery also faces a kidney specific contradiction. The glomerular filtration barrier rapidly clears very small entities, while robust retention within inflamed tubular regions often requires larger constructs with cellular anchoring. As a result, many small molecules are cleared before reaching effective local exposure, whereas conventional larger nanoparticles may exhibit limited access to the relevant renal compartments239.

Nanotechnology offers a route to reconcile this contradiction by using size, surface chemistry, and active targeting to convert injury associated signals into preferential renal partitioning and intracellular bioavailability240. A representative biomimetic example is macrophage extracellular vesicles. For example, macrophage extracellular vesicles can achieve specific and effective renal targeting and uptake through the interaction of integrins on their surface with ICAM-1, which is highly expressed in AKI, RTEC, endothelial cells and macrophages241.

Mechanistic mapping: How INDS address AKI barriers

AKI provides a set of endogenous cues that can be translated into delivery decisions. Tubular injury markers and activated endothelium provide anchoring signals for renal accumulation, while hypoxia related oxidative bursts and protease upregulation provide trigger fields for on site activation242. A mechanistically tight AKI INDS section therefore benefits from keeping the same logic throughout. The signal is defined by the injured kidney microenvironment, the responding structure is the ligand or stimulus sensitive chemistry embedded in the carrier, the transition is a change in localization or carrier state, and the output is increased local bioavailability together with suppression of oxidative inflammatory amplification243.

Renal specific targeted delivery

Injury induced tubular markers such as kidney injury molecule 1 provide a molecular signal on damaged renal tubular epithelial cells. Antibodies or peptides displayed on carriers such as liposomes function as the responding structure. Binding increases residence in the injured tubule region and promotes endocytosis, which is the transition that converts vascular exposure into intracellular access. Once internalized, the same carrier can be designed so that intracellular conditions actuate stimulus controlled release modules, yielding the output of higher local drug concentration and reduced systemic exposure compared with untargeted administration244,245. This anchoring to uptake sequence also applies to other receptor mediated entry routes on tubular cells that are exploited by active targeting strategies to address heterogeneous renal exposure246.

Regulate inflammation and antioxidant stress

Ischemia reperfusion injury is a major driver of AKI and initiates reactive oxygen species generation together with increased expression of proteases such as matrix metalloproteinase 2, creating a microenvironment dominated by oxidative stress and inflammatory recruitment (Fig. 7A)247. Proinflammatory cytokines including IL 6 and TNF alpha further aggravate tubular injury and interstitial fibrosis248.

One class aims to confine anti-inflammatory pharmacology to inflamed renal compartments. Here, cytokine rich inflamed tissue is the signal and the responding structure is a renal homing interface that biases accumulation and uptake into injured tubules and associated immune cells. The transition is lesion enriched retention followed by cellular entry, which increases local drug bioavailability and suppresses cytokine amplification while reducing systemic exposure249.

A second class neutralizes inflammatory mediators directly rather than only inhibiting their downstream signaling. Excess proinflammatory factors in the local milieu provide the signal and the responding structure is a binding or scavenging interface on the nanosystem that captures these factors. The transition is sequestration that removes mediators from the bioavailable pool, lowering the effective inflammatory drive. The output is reduced tissue injury and improved histopathology (Fig. 7B)250.

A third class modulates immune cell infiltration and phenotype within the kidney. Chemotactic and adhesion cues in injured tissue serve as signals, and biomimetic coatings or immune cell addressing ligands act as responding structures that bias interaction with recruited leukocytes. The transition is altered trafficking and uptake patterns that reshape the inflammatory cellular landscape. The output is reduced damaging infiltration together with a microenvironment more permissive to repair (Fig. 7C)251,252.

A fourth class focuses on oxidative injury control, where reactive oxygen species is the central signal substrate. Nanozymes and redox active materials serve as responding structures that consume reactive oxygen species through catalytic cycling. The transition is sustained redox buffering within the injured kidney rather than a one time release event. The output is attenuation of oxidative stress driven apoptosis and inflammation and preservation of renal function253.

When the platform also includes ROS labile linkers, oxidative cleavage can additionally shift the carrier toward increased permeability and on site release, which couples catalytic protection with spatially confined payload availability.

Integrated diagnosis and treatment and precision medicine

Theranostic designs for AKI are strongest when the sensing module is written as a signal to readout transition that directly informs deployment, and when the therapeutic module is aligned to the same injury chemistry. A DNA origami platform for sepsis associated AKI illustrates this logic. Elevated miR 21 acts as the biomolecular signal. A complementary sequence in the origami acts as the responding structure, and miR 21 binding initiates strand displacement that separates a fluorophore and quencher pair, restoring fluorescence as the measurable transition for real time detection. A photoacoustic readout changes in parallel through the embedded absorber element, providing a second monitoring transition. Therapeutically, the origami framework contributes reactive oxygen species scavenging capacity that responds to oxidative burden, and combination with an antimicrobial peptide such as LL 37 adds bactericidal activity in the sepsis context. The output is coupled early detection with mechanism aligned therapy that improves survival in sepsis associated AKI models (Fig. 7D)254.

Summary and perspectives

Critical illnesses, characterized by complex and dynamically evolving pathophysiology, present a therapeutic bottleneck that conventional pharmacotherapy struggles to overcome. While intelligent nano delivery systems (INDS) have shown theoretical promise in targeted release and integrated theranostics, a stark contrast remains between their robust efficacy in preclinical models and their limited success in clinical translation. A critical comparative analysis reveals a trade-off in current INDS strategies: Non-biodegradable inorganic carriers (e.g., gold, silica) offer precise tunability and stability but face significant hurdles regarding long-term clearance and potential nephrotoxicity in acute conditions like kidney injury. Conversely, biological carriers (e.g., cell membranes, exosomes) provide superior biocompatibility and immune evasion but are plagued by manufacturing scalability issues, batch-to-batch heterogeneity, and regulatory uncertainties regarding their complex composition.

Furthermore, the field faces unresolved debates regarding the mechanism of accumulation. The validity of the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect in human critical illnesses is increasingly challenged. Unlike uniform tumor models in mice, human pathologies such as sepsis or metastatic cancer exhibit high physiological heterogeneity. The variations in pH levels, enzyme concentrations, and vascular permeability among patients mean that a “one-size-fits-all” passive targeting strategy is destined to fail. This necessitates a paradigm shift from passive accumulation to active, logic-gated engagement, where INDS must navigate the immune system without triggering cytokine storms—a risk particularly heightened when using bacterial-derived components or immunogenic synthetic polymers.

To bridge the “valley of death” between laboratory research and clinical application, the future of INDS must transcend traditional materials science and converge with artificial intelligence (AI). We propose that the next generation of INDS should be defined not just by “smart” materials, but by “intelligent design” powered by machine learning (ML).(i)AI-guided de novo design: Instead of laborious trial-and-error synthesis, generative AI models can be employed to inversely design nanocarriers based on desired pharmacokinetic profiles (e.g., specific biodegradability timelines and clearance pathways). This computational approach can predict and mitigate potential immunotoxicity and off-target effects before physical synthesis begins.(ii)Precision stratification: To tackle patient heterogeneity, AI algorithms can integrate multi-omics data with real-time physiological markers to identify which patient subgroups will respond to specific nanocarriers. This moves INDS towards a truly personalized “precision nanomedicine” approach.(iii)Digital twin modeling: The development of “digital twins” for nanodelivery—virtual simulations of nanoparticle-bio interactions—can help clarify the regulatory landscape. By quantitatively modeling the interaction between biological components (like cell membranes) and the host immune system, we can establish the robust safety guidelines currently missing in the field.

In conclusion, while the potential of INDS is undeniable, their clinical viability hinges on resolving the dichotomy between functionality and biosafety. The future lies in evolving from descriptive “proof-of-concept” studies to robust, scalable, and AI-optimized formulations. By leveraging data-driven design to navigate the complex biological constraints of critical illnesses, INDS can revolutionize therapeutic management, turning unpredictable biological challenges into manageable engineering solutions.
