Global Long-Term Cost Effectiveness of Newer Antidiabetic Drugs for Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: A Systematic Review
Authors: Dan Li, Alesha Smith, Carlo A. Marra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveThe rising global burden of type 2 diabetes mellitus, compounded by the expanding therapeutic landscape, has placed increasing pressure on health systems to generate robust evidence on the long-term value for money of newer antidiabetic drugs (NADs). This systematic review addresses the urgent need for an updated synthesis of evidence on long-term health economic evaluations of NADs for the treatment of type 2 diabetes, including studies on the latest agents such as tirzepatide and finerenone. It aims to synthesise global long-term cost-effectiveness analyses, assess reporting completeness using the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) 2022 framework and outline key implications for clinicians, payers and policymakers seeking to optimise type 2 diabetes management within resource-constrained health systems.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Cochrane, EBSCOhost, Scopus and Web of Science were systematically searched for studies published between 1 January, 2008 and 20 February, 2025. Eligible studies were long-term incremental cost-effectiveness analyses conducted in adults with type 2 diabetes, comparing NADs with other NAD classes or with standard treatment, and reporting outcomes such as incremental cost-effectiveness ratios. A formal risk of bias assessment using tools designed for clinical trials was not undertaken. Instead, reporting quality and transparency were assessed using the CHEERS 2022 checklist, with modelling-related sources of uncertainty and potential bias examined qualitatively. Given the methodological heterogeneity, results were synthesised narratively. Full-text English articles were included. This review was not prospectively registered.ResultsThe search identified 1481 records, of which 142 studies met the inclusion criteria. Overall, 81% of incremental cost-effectiveness ratio-based analyses reported that NADs were cost effective compared with conventional therapies under country-specific willingness-to-pay thresholds. Using Thailand as an example of a developing country, studies generally found NADs not to be cost effective, largely because willingness-to-pay thresholds (USD 4336–5310 per quality-adjusted life-year) are substantially lower than those in higher income settings. Recently introduced agents (e.g. tirzepatide and finerenone) and early-line use were typically cost effective only at higher willingness-to-pay thresholds (USD 100,000–150,000 per quality-adjusted life-year) or following substantial price reductions (≥ 70% for sodium glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors and ≥90% for oral glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists). Most evaluations employed established diabetes models and adopted lifetime horizons from a payer perspective, while reporting quality assessments revealed limited disclosure of stakeholder involvement.ConclusionsThese findings should be interpreted cautiously given the substantial heterogeneity across studies and methodological limitations inherent to long-term economic modelling. Overall, NADs generally provide favourable long-term cost effectiveness, owing to their cardiovascular and renal benefits; however, recently introduced agents and early-line use tend to be cost effective only at higher willingness-to-pay thresholds or after significant price reductions. Future evaluations should integrate real-world evidence and advanced modelling to capture long-term impacts and incorporate context-specific affordability considerations to support equitable and sustainable adoption of newer antidiabetic therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40261-026-01530-6.

Key Points

Introduction

The management of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has undergone a profound transformation in recent years, driven by compelling evidence that newer antidiabetic drugs (NADs) confer benefits well beyond glycaemic control. Contemporary clinical guidelines, including the 2024 American Diabetes Association Standards of Care and joint American Diabetes Association-European Association for the Study of Diabetes consensus reports, now position sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is), glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and other emerging agents as foundational therapies for patients with elevated cardiovascular or renal risk [1–3].

Large cardiovascular and kidney outcome trials have shown that these agents reduce heart failure hospitalisations, slow chronic kidney disease progression, and improve major cardiovascular outcomes [4–6]. More recently, dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and GLP-1RAs such as tirzepatide have demonstrated even greater metabolic and cardiorenal potential [7], while finerenone has further expanded therapeutic options for patients with T2DM and chronic kidney disease as a non-glucose-lowering therapy that provides clinically meaningful renal and cardiovascular protection [8,9].

Yet despite these advances, the rapid expansion of therapeutic options has introduced substantial complexity for health systems worldwide. Newer antidiabetic drugs remain among the highest-priced treatments in T2DM care, posing affordability challenges, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where out-of-pocket payments are substantial [10]. Real-world integration is further complicated by differences in administration routes, treatment adherence and glycemic responsiveness, including reduced SGLT2i efficacy in advanced kidney disease [11,12]. These challenges raise important questions about optimal patient selection and long-term sustainability as payers increasingly shift toward value-based reimbursement and cost-conscious decision making.

Although numerous cost-effectiveness studies evaluating NADs have been published over the past two decades, the evidence base remains fragmented. Many evaluations rely on short-term clinical data, apply heterogeneous methodological assumptions, or omit societal perspectives essential for understanding broader economic implications. Geographic gaps in available analyses also limit global generalisability, particularly in settings where economic evaluations are most needed. Recent methodological developments, including strengthened reporting expectations under the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) 2022, underscore the importance of re-assessing existing evaluations using contemporary standards of transparency and completeness [13].

Against this backdrop, a comprehensive synthesis of long-term cost-effectiveness evidence is needed to clarify the clinical and economic value of NADs, identify gaps in current practice and inform sustainable decision making. This systematic review, therefore, aims to synthesise global long-term cost-effectiveness analyses of NADs, assess reporting completeness using the CHEERS 2022 framework, and outline key implications for clinicians, payers and policymakers seeking to optimise T2DM management within resource-constrained health systems.

Methods

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement and the CHEERS 2022 checklist [14]. Although no prospective protocol registration was undertaken, all methodological components, including eligibility criteria, search strategy, data extraction and plans for evidence synthesis, were specified a priori and implemented consistently throughout the review process. Because this review synthesises model-based economic evaluations rather than clinical outcomes, prospective registration was unlikely to materially affect analytical transparency or the risk of selective reporting.

Search Strategies

A comprehensive literature search was undertaken in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, EBSCOhost, Scopus and Web of Science. The search window was restricted to studies published from 1 January, 2008 onwards. This start date was defined a priori because long-term model-based economic evaluations of NADs became sufficiently established and routinely reported from 2008 [15].

The initial search was conducted on 7 June, 2023. In accordance with PRISMA 2020 recommendations, three updates were subsequently performed on 16 October, 2023, 31 May, 2024 and 20 February, 2025. All records retrieved from the initial and updated searches were pooled and deduplicated prior to screening.

Search strategies combined controlled vocabulary (e.g. Medical Subject Heading [MeSH] in PubMed and Emtree terms in Embase) and free-text keywords related to an economic evaluation (e.g. ‘cost effectiveness’, ‘economic’, ‘cost utility’) and all relevant NAD classes, including GLP-1RAs, SGLT2is, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4is), GIP and GLP-1RAs and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), as well as variations in both drug class names and individual drug names. The reference lists of all included studies were also screened to identify additional eligible articles. Details of the full search strategy are available in Table S1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM).

The literature searches were conducted independently by two reviewers (DL and AS), who developed and executed the search strategy across all databases. A third reviewer (CM) cross-checked the search results to ensure completeness and accuracy.

Eligibility Criteria

Types of Studies

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they evaluated the long-term cost-effectiveness of NADs for T2DM through cost-effectiveness analyses or cost-utility analyses. In this review, ‘long-term’ was defined as a modelled time horizon of at least 10 years, reflecting the chronic progressive nature of T2DM and the long latency of major complications. Studies had to compare one or more NADs as interventions for T2DM and include real-world costs of NADs currently available on the market. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was used as the primary metric to assess cost effectiveness. Studies were included if they explicitly reported ICERs alongside quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) or other utility-adjusted survival outcomes and total costs. Net monetary benefit (NMB) was considered when available.

The following exclusion criteria were applied:Studies focusing on undefined diabetes types, prediabetes, or mixed populations with type 1 diabetes and T2DM;Publications without original economic evaluation data, including reviews, conference abstracts, letters, notes, editorials, comments and study protocols;Studies published in languages other than English; andStudies that reported costs or cost offsets only, without estimating health outcomes or any cost-effectiveness measure (e.g. ICERs or NMB).

Screening Process

Three reviewers (DL, CM and AS) independently screened the titles and abstracts identified in the initial search and subsequently assessed the full text of potentially eligible studies against the predefined eligibility criteria. Screening decisions were compared, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion among the three reviewers. Consensus was reached for all decisions; therefore, no additional adjudication step was required.

Data Collected and the Collection Process

A standardised data extraction form was developed a priori based on selected domains from the CHEERS 2022 checklist and was refined after pilot testing on a small sample of studies to ensure clarity, consistency and shared interpretation of key variables. Two reviewers (DL and AS) independently extracted all data, while a third reviewer (CM) verified completeness and internal consistency. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, adjudication by the third reviewer.

For each included study, information was extracted across three analytic domains:First, study and contextual characteristics captured information on country, study perspective, study design (model or trial based), and the currency type and price year.Second, model structure and analytical assumptions covered model characteristics (e.g. model type, time horizon, discount rate), patient subgroup, and intervention and comparator structures.Third, economic outcomes and reporting items included incremental costs, incremental effectiveness, ICERs, adverse events, NMB when reported, uncertainty analysis, study limitations, source of funding and conflicts of interest.

All cost and ICER values were extracted exactly as reported in the original publications. Costs were typically provided in local currencies to reflect country-specific price levels and health system structures; when studies had already converted costs into a common currency, the converted values were extracted directly. No additional adjustments or currency conversions were applied because willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds, price structures and underlying economic contexts differ substantially across countries, and applying standardised conversions may distort the country-specific validity of the original evaluation.

The main economic outcomes extracted included ICERs, incremental costs and incremental effectiveness, expressed as QALYs or life-years adjusted for health-related quality of life. For comparability, only studies reporting explicit ICER estimates were included in ICER-based comparative analyses. Studies that applied NMB-based decision rules without calculating ICERs were extracted descriptively and incorporated only into the qualitative synthesis, because NMB outcomes are not directly comparable to ICER values within this review framework [16]. Information that was missing or unclear in the original publications was coded as ‘not reported’, with no assumptions or imputations made. All extracted data were organised and managed using Microsoft Excel 2024 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).

Data Synthesis and Analytical Approach

Results of individual studies were summarised in structured tables that presented key study characteristics (e.g. country, intervention, model type, time horizon, perspective, sponsorship and CHEERS 2022 score) and primary outcomes (e.g. ICERs, incremental costs, incremental effectiveness and WTP thresholds). In addition, graphical visualisations such as bar charts, dot plots and kernel density plots were created to illustrate temporal trends in publication, model utilisation and other patterns across studies.

For studies reporting ICERs, each ICER was treated as an individual unit of analysis. This allowed a single study to contribute multiple ICER-based comparisons reflecting different populations, comparators, modelling assumptions or decision thresholds.

Given the substantial heterogeneity in interventions, model structures and outcome reporting, quantitative pooling was not feasible and a meta-analysis was not planned for this review. Accordingly, no sensitivity analyses were conducted. Instead, a structured narrative synthesis approach was undertaken. Studies were compared descriptively across key analytic dimensions, including drug class, modelling framework, study perspective, time horizon and WTP thresholds, to synthesise methodological features and summarise economic outcomes. This approach aligns with PRISMA guidance for reviews in which heterogeneity precludes meaningful quantitative synthesis.

Reporting Quality and Consideration of Risk of Bias

A formal risk of bias assessment was not conducted because this review synthesises model-based economic evaluations for which no widely accepted or validated risk of bias tool currently exists. Traditional tools developed for clinical trials or observational studies (e.g. Cochrane RoB, ROBINS-I) are not applicable to decision-analytic models, which introduce methodological, structural and parameter uncertainty rather than empirical bias in data collection [17–19]. Instead of evaluating risk of bias, this review assessed the completeness and transparency of reporting using the CHEERS 2022 checklist, the recommended reporting standard for health economic evaluations. Potential sources of methodological bias, such as industry sponsorship, modelling assumptions and perspective, were explored narratively in the results and discussion sections. Reporting bias due to missing results was not formally assessed. No formal methods were used to assess certainty of evidence for outcomes.

DL assessed all studies, while CM and AS independently reviewed a random sample of 20 studies. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. CHEERS 2022 items were rated as ‘Yes’, ‘Not reported’ or ‘Not applicable’, with detailed results provided in the ESM. Total CHEERS scores were described using the interquartile range (IQR). Lower and upper bounds were defined using Tukey’s method (Q1 − 1.5 × IQR; Q3 + 1.5 × IQR), and studies were categorised accordingly:Low completeness: scores below the lower bound, indicating substantial reporting gaps.Medium completeness: scores between the lower and upper bounds, reflecting typical adherence to reporting standard.High completeness: scores above the upper bound, representing strong reporting adherence.

Continent Distribution

The geographic distribution of economic evaluations was uneven, with more than half of the included studies conducted in Europe (54%; 76/142), followed by Asia (n = 34), and North America (n = 28), and limited representation from Oceania (Australia; n = 2) and South America (n = 1). Additionally, one study spanned regions across Europe, Asia and North America. At the country level, the UK (n = 26), China (n = 23) and the USA (n = 21) accounted for the largest number of evaluations.

Across studies conducted in Thailand (n = 5), nearly all evaluations concluded that the assessed NADs were not cost effective [20–24]. These studies applied a WTP threshold of 160,000 Thai Baht/QALY (approximately 4336–5310 USD/QALY). In contrast, reported WTP thresholds in other settings ranged from approximately 6080 USD/QALY (e.g. China) to over 150,000 USD/QALY (e.g. USA), with the highest values exceeding 365,000 USD/QALY in Denmark and 546,000 USD/QALY in Qatar.

Economic Models Used

Among the 142 studies analysed, Markov state-transition model dominated (89%), followed by microsimulation approaches (11%), with no discernible geographic clustering in model selection. The most frequently used platform was the Center for Outcomes Research Diabetes (CORE) Model, followed by the Cardiff Diabetes Model and the UK Prospective Diabetes Study Model. A minority of evaluations employed alternative academic or bespoke models, such as the Economic and Health Outcomes Model of T2DM and the Swedish Institute for Health Economics-Diabetes Cohort Model, in addition to several country-specific Markov or microsimulation structures.

To examine whether cost-effectiveness conclusions differed by model choice, we compared ICER-based evaluations using the CORE Diabetes Model with those employing non-CORE academic or bespoke frameworks (e.g. Cardiff, UK Prospective Diabetes Study-based models, and study-specific Markov or microsimulation models). CORE-based evaluations were predominantly industry sponsored (92%; 59/64). Among CORE-based evaluations, 88% (56/64) concluded that NADs were cost effective at the study-specific WTP threshold, compared with 82% (62/76) among non-CORE evaluations. Both categories exhibited a mix of cost-effective and non-cost-effective outcomes, and given the substantial heterogeneity in thresholds, parameter inputs and structural assumptions, these descriptive differences should not be interpreted as evidence of systematic bias attributable to model type. Details stratified by model type and funding source are provided in Table S3 of the ESM, with model categorisation and usage frequency shown in Fig. S1 of the ESM.

Perspective Used

Most analyses were conducted from a payer perspective (89%; 126/142), with societal perspectives remaining uncommon. Only 13 studies (9%) adopted a societal perspective alone, and a further three (2%) incorporated both payer and societal perspectives.

Time Horizon Used

Long-term time horizons (≥ 40 years) dominated the analyses (87%; 124/142), with clear peaks at 40 years, 50 years and lifetime (Fig. 3). Economic Results

Detailed study-level characteristics, individual ICER-based comparisons and corresponding references are provided in Tables S4–S18 of the ESM; the main text focuses on synthesised patterns across studies and settings.

Overall Cost-Effectiveness Patterns

Overall, cost-effectiveness conclusions for NADs varied across studies and decision contexts but favoured NADs in the majority of evaluated comparisons. Economic outcomes were shaped primarily by drug class, comparator choice and local WTP thresholds, with consistent advantages observed for certain classes in high-risk clinical populations and more heterogeneous results in broader treatment settings.

Among the 142 studies included in this review, 140 reported ICERs and were therefore included in ICER-based quantitative syntheses, yielding a total of 332 ICER-based pairwise comparisons. Across all ICER-based evaluations, 87% concluded that NADs under assessment were cost effective relative to the study-specific WTP threshold. When restricting the analysis to comparisons against conventional therapies under country-specific WTP thresholds, 81% of ICER-based evaluations similarly reported NADs to be cost effective. The remaining two studies applied NMB-based decision frameworks without reporting ICERs and were summarised descriptively.

Comparator selection exerted a strong influence on economic outcomes. Newer antidiabetic drugs were cost effective in the vast majority of comparisons against insulin regimens, with all identified GLP-1RAs versus insulin comparisons favouring GLP-1RAs. Comparisons against sulphonylureas (SUs) also frequently favoured NADs, particularly for SGLT2is. In contrast, comparisons against metformin or standard of care (SoC) produced substantially more heterogeneous findings, with cost-effectiveness conclusions varying across drug classes, patient populations and health-system contexts.

Clear differences were observed across drug classes. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors demonstrated the most consistent economic value, being cost effective in 94% of comparisons (73/78), particularly in populations with established cardiovascular disease or chronic kidney disease. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists also showed broad cost effectiveness, with 85% of comparisons (140/165) favouring GLP-1RAs, although greater variability was observed across comparator types and settings. In contrast, DPP-4is exhibited more heterogeneous economic profiles, with cost-effective conclusions in 77% of evaluations (39/51).

Newer agents with a more limited evidence base consistently demonstrated favourable economic profiles within the available evaluations, albeit typically at higher WTP thresholds (USD 100,000–150,000 per QALY). Dual GIP/GLP-1RAs, represented by tirzepatide, were found to be cost effective in all identified comparisons. Finerenone, evaluated as an add-on therapy for patients with T2DM and chronic kidney disease, was also consistently cost effective across available analyses. However, the evidence for these agents was restricted to a small number of recent studies conducted in high-income settings.

In a subset of evaluations, most commonly those conducted under lower WTP thresholds or in resource-constrained settings, cost-effectiveness conclusions were reversed, with non-NAD comparators identified as more cost effective in specific decision contexts. Comparisons with Traditional Therapies (Including First-Line Comparisons)

Comparisons between NADs and insulin consistently favoured NADs across drug classes. At the ICER level, all comparisons of GLP-1RAs with insulin (16/16) supported GLP-1RAs, while comparisons involving SGLT2is (1/1) and fixed-dose combinations (8/8) likewise favoured NADs, albeit based on more limited evidence. Exceptions were observed for DPP-4is, with divergent conclusions attributable to comparator-specific pricing and modelling assumptions rather than a consistent pattern across studies.

Comparisons against SUs also frequently favoured NADs, although results were more heterogeneous than those observed for insulin. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors consistently demonstrated cost-effectiveness relative to SUs (7/7 comparisons), whereas GLP-1RAs and DPP-4is showed greater variability, with cost-effective conclusions in approximately 70% (7/10) to 53% (8/15) of evaluations. Divergent findings were primarily observed in settings with very low drug acquisition costs for SUs, highlighting the strong influence of comparator pricing on cost-effectiveness conclusions.

Importantly, heterogeneity was strongly patterned by treatment context, with clear differences observed between add-on and first-line evaluations. Evaluations assessing NADs as add-on therapies, particularly SGLT2is in patients with established cardiovascular or renal disease, more frequently concluded cost effectiveness, whereas analyses evaluating NADs in first-line or upfront treatment contexts showed less favourable economic profiles under current pricing.

Notably, finerenone, evaluated exclusively as an add-on to SoC in patients with T2DM with kidney disease, was consistently cost effective across all identified analyses, representing a distinct non–glucose-lowering therapeutic context, with all identified analyses conducted in high-income settings.

In a subset of evaluations conducted under lower WTP thresholds or in resource-constrained settings, SoC or metformin remained the more cost-effective option, underscoring the strong influence of local decision thresholds on economic conclusions. Evidence comparing NADs with thiazolidinediones and other less frequently evaluated traditional therapies was sparse and did not materially alter the primary patterns observed for comparisons with insulin, SUs, metformin or SoC. Together, these findings show that, although NADs frequently offer favourable economic value as add-on therapies or in high-risk populations, their cost effectiveness relative to established first-line or background therapy is highly context dependent and varies considerably by drug class and decision threshold.

Head-to-Head Comparisons Between NAD Classes

Among the 142 included studies, 72 (51%) reported direct head-to-head comparisons. Between-class comparisons indicated a generally directional pattern in cost effectiveness across drug classes.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists consistently outperformed DPP-4is, with all comparisons favouring GLP-1RAs (26/26). Similarly, SGLT2is uniformly demonstrated superior cost effectiveness relative to DPP-4is (16/16), indicating a general economic advantage of newer drug classes over earlier incretin-based therapies, particularly in populations with established cardiovascular or renal disease.

Comparisons between GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is yielded mixed results, with GLP-1RAs being cost effective in 60% of evaluations (18/30), highlighting substantial context dependence rather than a universally dominant class. Notably, class-level patterns were less consistent in price-sensitive settings, where alternative GLP-1RAs occasionally ranked more favourably than semaglutide.

Fixed-dose combinations exhibited a modest but context-dependent economic advantage over GLP-1RAs, being favoured in the majority of comparisons (71.4%, 5/7). Dual GIP/GLP-1RAs (tirzepatide) demonstrated a uniform economic advantage over established GLP-1RAs, with all available comparisons favouring tirzepatide (100%, 6/6). However, this evidence remains limited, as all evaluations were conducted in high-income settings.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2is consistently outperformed DPP-4is, whereas comparisons between GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is showed substantial variability, reflecting a strong context dependence rather than a universally dominant class. Emerging evidence suggests a favourable economic profile for tirzepatide relative to established GLP-1RAs, although this conclusion is currently supported by a limited number of evaluations.

At the within-class level, cost-effectiveness outcomes exhibited a general directional pattern across agents. Within the GLP-1RAs, head-to-head comparisons frequently favoured semaglutide, particularly against dulaglutide, liraglutide and lixisenatide across multiple high-income settings. The direction of preference varied across countries, formulations and treatment regimens, with exceptions in price-sensitive settings and in combination-therapy analyses, in which semaglutide did not consistently retain an economic advantage. Formulation-specific evidence further indicated that oral semaglutide did not uniformly replicate the cost effectiveness observed with subcutaneous formulations, underscoring substantial context dependence within this class.

Within the SGLT2is, available head-to-head evidence was more limited but comparatively consistent, most often favouring empagliflozin over alternative agents. Comparisons between canagliflozin and dapagliflozin also tended to favour canagliflozin. Nevertheless, context-specific exceptions were observed, indicating that within-class rankings can shift depending on pricing assumptions and background therapy.

Evidence for fixed-dose combinations was sparse but directionally consistent, with iDegLira generally favoured over iGlarLixi in the reported comparisons. However, the limited volume of available evidence precludes definitive conclusions regarding a stable within-class hierarchy.

Treatment Strategy Comparison

Across intensification strategies and pathway-based evaluations, earlier initiation of SGLT2is was commonly associated with favourable cost effectiveness across multiple countries, perspectives and risk strata. Similar patterns were observed in a population-level use-expansion scenario. In contrast, intensification strategies centred on GLP-1RAs demonstrated substantially greater dependence on local pricing and the WTP context. Although GLP-1RAs-based pathways were consistently associated with gains in QALYs, their cost effectiveness was more variable and was frequently not achieved in settings with lower WTP thresholds.

Pathways incorporating DPP-4is alone generally produced a modest economic value, whereas combination strategies that added SGLT2is to DPP-4is-based regimens consistently yielded more favourable cost-effectiveness outcomes. Evidence directly comparing GLP-1RAs- and DPP-4is-led intensification strategies remained limited, precluding robust quantitative synthesis.

Sulphonylurea-based strategies demonstrated cost effectiveness primarily in budget-constrained settings, where low acquisition costs offset their limited clinical benefits. Insulin-based strategies were generally less cost effective when introduced early in treatment sequences compared with pathways incorporating NADs.

Discussion

Context-Dependent Economic Value

In this systematic review of long-term economic evaluations, NADs were found to provide a more meaningful economic value in a substantial proportion of ICER-based analyses, particularly when evaluated against insulin or SUs and in populations at elevated cardiovascular or renal risk. However, their cost effectiveness was highly context dependent and varied substantially with comparator choice, national WTP thresholds and drug pricing structures. These findings indicate that the economic value of NADs cannot be interpreted independently of the health-system context, underscoring the need for contextualised economic evidence to inform reimbursement and policy decisions.

Pricing and WTP Thresholds

A central finding of this review is that cost-effectiveness conclusions are strongly shaped by drug pricing and national WTP thresholds. Even when NADs demonstrated substantial clinical benefits, high acquisition costs frequently preclude favourable economic evaluations, particularly for early-line or first-line use where long-term gains may not offset upfront expenditures under constrained budgets. Evidence from high-income settings further illustrates this tension. For example, an ADA-endorsed analysis from the USA suggested that prices of SGLT2is and oral GLP-1RAs would need to be reduced by approximately 79% and 90%, respectively, to achieve cost effectiveness as first-line monotherapies [25]. Together, these findings indicate that current pricing structures may limit the economic feasibility of widespread early adoption, even in well-resourced health systems.

Timing Mismatch Between Clinical Benefit and Economic Assessment

Beyond pricing, our findings point to a structural mismatch between the timing of clinical benefit realisation and prevailing economic evaluation frameworks. Many NADs derive much of their value from long-term reductions in cardiovascular and renal complications, benefits that accrue gradually over extended horizons and are well supported by evidence from cardiovascular and renal outcome trials [5,6,8]. In contrast, economic evaluations are often conducted under fixed WTP thresholds and budget cycles that prioritise short- to medium-term affordability [26,27]. This timing mismatch disproportionately affects assessments of early-line use, where high upfront drug costs are incurred well before downstream savings from avoided complications materialise. As a result, therapies that are clinically aligned with contemporary guideline recommendations for early risk-based intervention may appear economically unattractive under conventional decision frameworks. Notably, comparative cost-effectiveness findings between GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is were inconsistent across studies, reflecting differences in target populations, outcome emphasis, pricing structures and modelling assumptions.

Newer Therapies and The Innovation–Affordability Tension

The emergence of recently introduced therapies, such as dual GIP/GLP-1RAs (tirzepatide) and adjunctive organ-protective therapies (finerenone), further accentuates the tension between pharmaceutical innovation and affordability. Across included studies, these agents were generally found to be cost effective only at the upper end of commonly accepted WTP thresholds [28–34]. While this suggests meaningful value in selected high-risk populations, such as individuals with obesity, established cardiovascular disease or chronic kidney disease, it also indicates that broad unselective adoption may not be economically sustainable under current pricing conditions. These findings support the need for targeted use, stratified reimbursement policies and pricing strategies that better align costs with population-level health gains.

Modelling Validity, Transparency and Cross-Study Comparability

Most economic evaluations identified in this review relied on established T2DM simulation models, such as the CORE Diabetes Model, which extrapolate long-term outcomes primarily from clinical trial data. Although these models are widely used and have undergone validation, they do not fully capture the dynamic and heterogeneous progression of T2DM observed in real-world populations. Clinically meaningful effects of NADs, including sustained weight loss, benefits in heart failure, renal protection and metabolic improvements, are often incompletely represented, while rare adverse events and broader cognitive or metabolic outcomes are frequently omitted [35–38]. In addition, the widespread reliance on glycated haemoglobin as a surrogate endpoint introduces further limitations. Although glycated haemoglobin remains a central marker in T2DM management, its relationship with long-term microvascular and macrovascular outcomes remains contested, potentially constraining the validity of long-term economic projections that rely heavily on glycaemic surrogates [39,40]. Beyond these conceptual and clinical limitations of existing T2DM models, a further challenge relates to the transparency and cross-study comparability of economic evaluations. As models increasingly incorporate complex simulation approaches, real-world evidence and multiple treatment pathways, their underlying structural assumptions, input justification and validation processes are not always fully reported. Studies published after 2022 demonstrated markedly improved reporting completeness, reflecting a growing adoption of the CHEERS 2022 standards [24,33,41–43]. These improvements are increasingly critical as health economic models become more complex and influential in policy decision making. Transparent reporting of structural assumptions, input data, risk equations and validation procedures is essential to ensure reproducibility and to enable a meaningful cross-study comparison [44,45].

Given the heavy reliance on proprietary industry-sponsored models, including the CORE Diabetes Model, greater transparency is essential to support independent validation and to reduce the risk of unobserved structural or assumption-driven bias. As models become more complex and increasingly incorporate real-world evidence, transparency becomes more, not less, critical. Clear reporting of structural assumptions, input justification, validation processes and uncertainty characterisation is essential to support reproducibility, independent validation and credible policy use.

Global Access and Equity Considerations

The economic value of NADs varied substantially across countries, reflecting differences in disease burden, health system capacity, resource constraints and WTP thresholds. Although these therapies were often cost effective in high-income settings, such findings rarely translated to low- and middle-income countries, where affordability constraints and Gross Domestic Product-based thresholds are substantially lower. Evidence from Thailand indicates that NADs frequently fail to meet local cost-effectiveness criteria, raising concerns about widening global inequities in access to high-value T2DM care [20–24]. To support equitable adoption, future economic evaluations must be context specific and incorporate regionally relevant epidemiologic data, cost structures, comorbidity patterns and system constraints. A distributional cost-effectiveness analysis offers a promising framework to assess not only efficiency but also the equity implications of treatment adoption within resource-constrained health systems [45–47].

Model Origin and Sponsorship-Related Uncertainty

The modest differences observed between cost-effectiveness results generated by CORE models and those produced using academic or bespoke frameworks should be interpreted cautiously. Across academic and bespoke models, substantial variation in structure, assumptions and risk equations, along with differences in comparators, price inputs and national WTP thresholds further complicate the attribution of outcomes to model choice alone. Although our review did not identify consistent systematic differences between modelling platforms, the predominance of CORE-based studies, many of which are industry sponsored, highlights ongoing challenges related to transparency, reproducibility and independent validation in economic modelling.

Industry sponsorship represents another important source of uncertainty. Among industry-funded evaluations, 90% concluded that the NAD was cost effective, compared with 61% of publicly funded studies. Although this descriptive pattern aligns with long-standing concerns regarding potential sponsorship influence, it should be interpreted cautiously, given the relatively small number of publicly funded evaluations and substantial cross-study differences in modelling assumptions, clinical data, comparators, price structures and jurisdiction-specific thresholds. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of sponsorship-related bias, but rather underscore the need for greater transparency, independent replication and publicly funded economic evaluations to strengthen confidence in long-term cost-effectiveness evidence.

Limitations and Future Research Directions

This review has several limitations. The predominance of industry-funded studies and the widespread use of proprietary models limit transparency and may introduce unobserved structural bias. Substantial heterogeneity in model structures, input data and assumptions precluded a formal meta-analysis and necessitated a narrative synthesis. Reporting costs and ICERs in original currencies and price years preserves contextual validity but limits cross-country comparability. Applying CHEERS 2022 to studies spanning nearly two decades may underestimate reporting completeness in older evaluations conducted under earlier standards, and accordingly, lower completeness scores should therefore be interpreted as reflecting evolving reporting expectations rather than deficiencies in scientific rigor. The absence of a validated risk-of-bias tool for model-based economic evaluations constrains a quantitative assessment of methodological credibility, and structural or assumption-driven biases may persist despite a narrative appraisal.

Taken together, these limitations define important boundaries for interpreting the reported economic outcomes of NADs. Methodological innovation is therefore needed to better characterise structural uncertainty, improve representation of real-world treatment dynamics and enhance transparency. Advances in artificial intelligence and data-driven modelling offer opportunities to support more flexible, individualised projections and scalable scenario analyses, with the potential to strengthen reproducibility and policy relevance. Equity-oriented approaches, including distributional cost-effectiveness analyses and context-specific affordability assessments, will also be important for guiding sustainable and equitable adoption across diverse health systems.

Conclusions

This systematic review indicates that NADs deliver substantial long-term clinical and economic value by reducing cardiovascular and renal complications. However, their cost effectiveness remains highly sensitive to pricing, WTP thresholds and population risk profiles, revealing a persistent misalignment between therapeutic innovation and global affordability, particularly in low- to middle-income countries. For policymakers, these findings underscore the need for context-sensitive pricing and reimbursement strategies, including differential pricing, managed-entry agreements and the explicit incorporation of equity considerations into health technology assessment frameworks. Greater alignment between pricing decisions and local health system constraints is essential to ensure that the demonstrated value of NADs can be realised in routine care. For researchers and funders, the review highlights several critical evidence gaps that must be addressed to support more robust decision making. These include the limited number of studies adopting a societal perspective, the scarcity of real-world evidence on long-term outcomes and resource use, and pronounced regional disparities in the geographic distribution of economic evaluations.
