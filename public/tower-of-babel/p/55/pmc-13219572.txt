A novel, simple, and affordable technique for arthroscopic soft-tissue tenodesis of the long head of the biceps tendon
Authors: Chiara Fossati, Attilio Ioppoli, Rossella Ravaglia, Faizan Vaja, Alessandra Menon, Pietro Simone Randelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe purpose of this study was to describe a novel, simple, and implant-free arthroscopic soft-tissue tenodesis technique of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) to the rotator cuff using an absorbable monofilament suture and to evaluate its clinical and functional outcomes at a minimum 1-year follow-up.MethodsA retrospective case series of 23 patients (mean age 58.0 ± 7.8 years) who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair with concomitant LHBT soft-tissue tenodesis between June 2021 and June 2023 was analyzed. Functional outcomes were assessed using Constant–Murley Score (CMS), American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (SANE), visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, and the Long Head of Biceps (LHB) score. Supination strength was measured with a handheld dynamometer and compared with the contralateral side. Incidence of Popeye deformity and tenderness over the bicipital groove were recorded.ResultsObjective Popeye deformity was observed in 13% of patients, with subjective concern reported by only 4.3%. Supination strength and LHB scores were similar to the contralateral side (means of 104.65 versus 104.64 N; LHB 93.7 versus 94.6 points). The mean CMS and ASES scores were 90.3 ± 12.4 and 89.6 ± 15.9 points, respectively. The SANE score averaged 87.4 ± 20.9, and the VAS for pain was low (1.65 ± 2.59 cm).ConclusionsThis implant-free, arthroscopic LHBT soft-tissue tenodesis technique is technically simple, cost-effective, and yields excellent clinical and functional outcomes with minimal cosmetic concerns. It represents a reliable option for patients undergoing rotator cuff repair with concurrent LHBT pathology.Level of evidenceLevel IV.

Background

Pathologies of the long head of the biceps tendon (LHBT) are a well-recognized source of anterior shoulder pain and are frequently associated with rotator cuff tears [1–8] and are therefore often managed in conjunction with arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, a procedure widely performed with generally favorable clinical outcomes across heterogeneous patient populations [9, 10].

LHBT disorders are commonly caused by tendon degeneration related to persistent inflammation or micro-tearing, anchor-related pathologies such as SLAP (Superior Labrum Anterior to Posterior) lesions, and biceps instability [11, 12].

When conservative treatment fails, tenotomy and tenodesis of the LHBT are the two most commonly performed surgical procedures. Both techniques have demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes, and the superiority of one treatment over the other remains controversial [13–17].

Tenotomy is associated with a quicker return to activity and less postoperative pain but has been shown to result in a higher incidence of cosmetic deformity (Popeye sign), muscle cramping, and potential loss of elbow flexion and supination strength [13–16]. Conversely, tenodesis aims to preserve bicep contour and function but is not free from complications, including residual anterior shoulder pain, tenderness over the bicipital groove, stiffness, fixation failure, and implant-related complications described in anchor-based techniques [18].

For these reasons, LHBT tenodesis is often preferred in younger, active, and high-demand patients [17, 19, 20].

Several tenodesis techniques have been described, differing in surgical approach (open or arthroscopic), fixation method (bony or soft-tissue), fixation site (suprapectoral or subpectoral), and materials used (free sutures, suture anchors, or interference screws) [15, 17, 18]. Overall, these techniques have demonstrated satisfactory outcomes, and the choice of procedure is frequently influenced by surgeon preference.

Among these options, soft-tissue tenodesis to the conjoint tendon, pectoralis major, or rotator cuff has the advantage of avoiding bone tunnels and implant-related complications [15, 17, 18, 21–23].

In particular, arthroscopic soft-tissue LHBT tenodesis at the level of the rotator cuff has already been described in the literature, providing effective stabilization through tendon-to-tendon fixation [22, 23].

However, some of these techniques require additional portals, extensive dissection, or complex suture configurations, potentially increasing surgical time, technical demand, and the risk of neurovascular injury [24–28].

In this context, an implant-free arthroscopic technique that is technically simple and cost-effective may represent an attractive clinical alternative.

The purpose of this study was therefore to describe a novel, simple, and cost-effective technical variation of arthroscopic soft-tissue LHBT tenodesis performed to the rotator cuff without implants using a single absorbable monofilament polydioxanone suture (PDS II; Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) and to evaluate the clinical and functional outcomes at a minimum 1-year follow-up.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of our hospital (approval number CTS-P-2024–033-CF, 27 September 2024) and carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Indications for the soft-tissue biceps tenodesis technique included degenerative or inflammatory LHBT pathologies associated with reparable postero-superior rotator cuff tears, such as LHBT tendinopathy, partial-thickness LHBT tears, SLAP lesions, and biceps instability. The technique was not indicated in cases of complete LHBT rupture, severe tendon degeneration with poor tissue quality, large or massive rotator cuff tears, revision surgeries, or when distal subpectoral biceps tenodesis was deemed necessary.

Clinical and functional outcomes were assessed at a minimum follow-up of 1 year postoperatively. Objective clinical evaluation included assessment of the Popeye sign, tenderness over the bicipital groove (on both the treated and contralateral sides), and forearm supination strength. Supination strength was measured in Newtons (N) using a handheld dynamometer and compared with the contralateral side. In addition, patients were asked whether they subjectively perceived any cosmetic deformity or bulging in the bicep’s region (subjective Popeye sign).

Functional outcomes were evaluated using shoulder-specific scoring systems, including the Constant–Murley Score (CMS) and the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score. General functional status was assessed using the Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (SANE) and the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain. Biceps-specific function was measured using the Long Head of Biceps (LHB) score.

Operative time was recorded for all procedures and defined as skin-to-skin surgical time.

All evaluations were conducted by a single author (I.A.) who was not involved in the surgical procedures.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R statistical software (version 4.0.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and GraphPad Prism version 6.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc). The Shapiro–Wilk normality test was used to evaluate the normal distribution of the sample. Continuous variables were reported as means ± SD when normally distributed and as median [Q1–Q3] when not normally distributed. Categorical variables are expressed in numbers of cases and frequencies; their differences were tested using with the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. The within-group differences from treated to contralateral sides for continuous variables were evaluated with the paired t-test or Wilcoxon matched pairs signed-rank test, according to the characteristics of the data distribution. For all analyses, the significance level was set at a p-value < 0.05.

Surgical technique

The procedure is performed in lateral decubitus position, under general anesthesia with interscalene brachial plexus block (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Patient positioned in the lateral decubitus, with the operative site prepped and draped in a sterile manner

Patient positioned in the lateral decubitus, with the operative site prepped and draped in a sterile manner

Anatomical landmarks are outlined using a sterile marker.

The arm being operated on is connected to a traction device with a weight of 3–4 kg.

Through a standard posterior viewing portal placed in the “soft-spot” (1.5 cm inferior and 1.5 cm medial to the postero-lateral border of the acromion), a 30° arthroscope (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, U.S.) is inserted. The antero-lateral portal is established using an “outside-in” technique, where a spinal needle (18G) is inserted under direct arthroscopic visualization in order to identify the correct position of the portal. A 6.5-mm disposable cannula is placed in the antero-lateral portal. A routine diagnostic arthroscopy is performed to examine the gleno-humeral joint and check integrity and stability of the biceps, using a probe through the anterior portal. Once a LHBT pathology is identified and considered appropriate for a soft-tissue tenodesis, a first spinal needle loaded with a no. 2 polydioxanone suture (PDS), folded on itself to form a loop (Fig. 2), is percutaneously inserted through the anterior third part of the supraspinatus tendon. After an accurate bursectomy, avoiding damaging the PDS suture (Fig. 8), the rotator cuff is repaired. Postoperative rehabilitation

Postoperatively, the arm is maintained in a sling with a pillow at 15° of abduction for 4 weeks and a standard rehabilitation protocol is followed.

Passive range of motion (ROM) exercises and scapular stabilizing exercises were initiated following sling removal at 4 weeks postoperatively.

Active-assisted ROM commenced at 10 weeks, followed by the initiation of active ROM at 14 weeks.

Progressive strengthening exercises were introduced gradually starting at 18 weeks postoperatively.

Return to sports was permitted no earlier than 24 weeks postoperatively, on the basis of the patient’s recovery status.

Results

A total of 36 patients (37 shoulders) were assessed for eligibility: 11 were unreachable by telephone or unable to participate, 2 were excluded owing to exclusion criteria, and 23 were available for a complete clinical examination. Overall, 13 patients (36%) were lost to follow-up. Most nonresponders were unreachable despite repeated contact attempts or declined participation for logistical reasons unrelated to clinical outcome. A review of available baseline data did not suggest clinically relevant differences between patients lost to follow-up and those included in the final analysis.

The Popeye sign was objectively detected by the clinician in 13% of the subjects (3/23), and in all cases it was defined as mild. One patient (4.3%) complained about a Popeye sign that was not objectively present. Supination strength was comparable to the contralateral side (104.65 ± 31.96 N versus 104.64 ± 24.35 N, p > 0.05). The LHB score was also comparable to the contralateral side (median 95.00 [92.50–98.50] versus 100.00 [92.00–100.00] points, p > 0.05). Residual pain was low (median VAS 0.00 [0.00–2.50] cm), and clinical and functional results were excellent (median CMS 95.00 [86.00–100.00] points). The mean operative time was 68.4 ± 15.6 min.

Discussion

The management of LHBT pathology in the context of rotator cuff tears continues to generate debate regarding the optimal surgical strategy. Both tenotomy and tenodesis have demonstrated favorable outcomes, yet tenodesis is often preferred in younger, active patients to preserve bicep contour and function [15–17]. Our findings demonstrate that arthroscopic soft-tissue tenodesis of the biceps to the supraspinatus tendon, using an absorbable monofilament suture, is a safe and effective option, with excellent clinical and functional outcomes at a minimum 1-year follow-up.

One of the key findings of this study is the low incidence of Popeye deformity. Objective Popeye sign was observed in only 13% of cases, all mild, and subjective perception was even lower (4.3%). These results are consistent with Kim et al. [30], who reported Popeye rates of 8% for soft-tissue tenodesis and 5.8% for anchor-based tenodesis, both significantly lower than 20.8% for tenotomy (p = 0.035). Similarly, Meraner et al. [13] observed only 1.9% Popeye deformity after tenodesis, compared with rates as high as 62% reported for tenotomy by Boileau et al. [3].

Residual tenderness over the bicipital groove was noted in 34.7% of patients on the treated side and in 13.6% on the contralateral side. This finding aligns with McCrum et al. [18], who reported persistent anterior shoulder pain in 11.4% of patients regardless of the fixation method, although soft-tissue techniques showed slightly higher rates of new-onset pain (11.9% versus 2.6% for implant-based methods, p < 0.001).

Shoulder function scores indicated a high level of recovery and patient satisfaction. The Constant–Murley Score (median 95.00 [86.00–100.00]) and the ASES score (median 100.00 [84.00–100.00]) are comparable to those reported by Shang et al. [7], whose pooled analysis revealed a small but significant advantage for tenodesis over tenotomy in CMS (SMD = −0.23, p = 0.025). The SANE score (median 100.00 [80.00–100.00]) and the VAS for pain (median 0.00 [0.00–2.50] cm) further confirmed excellent subjective outcomes. Finally, the LHB score (median 95.00 [92.50–98.50]), specific for long head of biceps function, showed excellent values comparable to the contralateral side.

This technique also presents practical advantages, as the absence of implants avoids hardware-related complications [20].

Several implant-free and/or all-suture options have been proposed for LHBT management. However, the term all-suture often refers to fixation with all-suture anchors and therefore does not necessarily indicate a truly implant-free construct. Accordingly, many reported techniques remain anchor-based, including suprapectoral or in-the-groove fixation with all-suture anchors, which still require humeral anchor deployment and permanent fixation material [25–27].

Other implant-free techniques are not purely all-arthroscopic. For example, implant-free subpectoral tenodesis may be performed through open or arthroscopy-assisted approaches and therefore does not represent a strictly arthroscopic comparator [24]. Anchor-sharing constructs integrated into rotator cuff repair similarly streamline workflow but remain anchor-dependent and rely on nonabsorbable fixation [28].

Fully arthroscopic implant-free soft-tissue alternatives include fixation to extra-cuff structures such as the conjoint tendon [21], or loop-based stabilization constructs at the bicipital groove [29].

Compared with previously described all-arthroscopic soft-tissue tenodesis techniques at the rotator cuff level, such as those reported by Lafosse et al. [22] and Franceschi et al. [23], the present technique intentionally avoids suture anchors, accessory portals, and multi-suture configurations. Instead, it relies on two percutaneous spinal-needle passes and a single absorbable monofilament to achieve cuff-level tendon-to-tendon stabilization. This simplified, fully arthroscopic and implant-free construct reduces implant dependence and technical complexity while preserving effective LHBT stabilization.

From a practical perspective, the implant-free nature of the procedure also has economic implications, with reported costs estimated at only 5–10% of implant-based tenodesis [24].

Beyond economic and technical considerations, the biological behavior of tendon-to-tendon fixation using absorbable material deserves consideration.

This technique relies on temporary tendon-to-tendon fixation of the LHBT to the supraspinatus tendon using an absorbable monofilament suture, followed by progressive biological healing after suture absorption. Although permanent mechanical fixation is not provided, the concept of tenodesis does not necessarily rely on permanent fixation materials.

The biological rationale of this approach is based on temporary stabilization followed by fibrous healing. Although direct experimental evidence specifically demonstrating healing of a damaged tendon onto the intact articular surface of a healthy tendon is limited, established biological principles of tendon healing—including fibrovascular ingrowth and collagen remodeling described in experimental and clinical tendon healing models—suggest that temporary stabilization may permit biological integration over time. Accordingly, absorbable sutures provide short-term stabilization, whereas long-term tendon continuity is expected to depend primarily on biological healing rather than permanent fixation [31–33].

From a clinical perspective, the favorable functional outcomes, preservation of supination strength, and low incidence of Popeye deformity are consistent with effective stabilization of the LHBT and support the functional validity of the technique, although they do not constitute direct biological proof of tendon-to-tendon healing.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. The sample size was relatively small, and no control group was included for direct comparison with other tenodesis techniques or tenotomy. The relatively high loss to follow-up represents a potential source of attrition bias. Although a review of the available baseline data did not suggest clinically relevant differences between patients lost to follow-up and those included in the final analysis, it cannot be excluded that nonresponders may have experienced different clinical outcomes.

The absence of postoperative imaging or histological assessment precludes direct confirmation of fibrous healing between the LHBT and the supraspinatus tendon. Therefore, the proposed tendon-to-tendon healing mechanism remains an indirect clinical inference rather than a directly demonstrated biological finding.

These factors prevent definitive conclusions regarding superiority of our procedure compared with other techniques of biceps tenodesis, and highlight the need for future comparative studies to further validate these preliminary findings.

Conclusions

This novel arthroscopic soft-tissue tenodesis technique for the LHBT provides excellent clinical and cosmetic outcomes while preserving forearm supination strength. Its technical simplicity, implant-free approach, and low cost make it a valuable option for selected patients with biceps pathology associated with small- to medium-sized posterosuperior rotator cuff tears.
