Takotsubo Cardiomyopathy With Dynamic LVOT Obstruction Mimicking Inferior STEMI

History of Presentation

A 64-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with epigastric pain radiating to the chin, associated with nausea and intermittent palpitations, especially with ambulation, over several days. She denied tobacco, alcohol, or illicit drug use. On arrival, she was hypotensive with a blood pressure of 80/59 mm Hg, a heart rate of 64 beats/min, a respiratory rate of 24 breaths/min, a temperature of 35.5 °C, and an oxygen saturation of 94%. High-sensitivity troponin-I was elevated at 116.3 ng/L (upper reference ≤14 ng/L). Physical examination showed a well-appearing woman without jugular venous distention, with clear lungs, no murmurs, and no peripheral edema. Given ongoing symptoms and evolving hemodynamics, repeat electrocardiography (ECG) was obtained.

Past Medical History

Past medical history notable for hypertension being treated with diltiazem ER 120 mg and metoprolol succinate 100 mg, though this combination is less commonly used for primary hypertension management.

Differential Diagnosis

The combination of chest discomfort, hypotension, elevated troponin, and evolving ECG findings raised concern for 1) ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI); 2) myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries; 3) Takotsubo syndrome (stress-induced cardiomyopathy); 4) spontaneous coronary artery dissection; and 5) acute myocarditis. Noncardiac causes, albeit less likely given ECG/troponin, were pulmonary embolism and aortic dissection.

Investigations

Initial ECG reportedly showed sinus rhythm without acute ST-T changes (Figure 1A). Repeat ECG demonstrated inferior ST-segment elevation and reciprocal lateral changes, meeting criteria for STEMI activation (Figure 1B). Cardiac biomarkers, including hs-TnI at 116.3 ng/L, were measured. Coronary angiography showed normal left coronary arteries without obstructive disease and a large dominant right coronary artery supplying the lateral wall and extending inferiorly (Figure 2). Hemodynamic assessment showed left ventricular (LV) end-diastolic pressure markedly elevated at 33 mm Hg. Left ventriculography was suggestive of severe regional wall-motion abnormality consistent with Takotsubo physiology showing apical/mid akinesis with basal hyperkinesis (Video 1). LV-aortic pullback assessment demonstrated a Brockenbrough-Braunwald-Morrow phenomenon, with failure of augmentation of aortic systolic pressure after premature ventricular contraction, supporting the presence of dynamic over fixed LV outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) (Figure 3). Echocardiography showed normal LV size, moderately reduced systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction 37%), and hyperdynamic basal segments with the remaining segments akinetic (Video 2). There was systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve with dynamic LVOTO (peak gradient 48 mm Hg) (Figure 4, Supplemental Figure 1, Video 3) and mild, mid-to-late systolic mitral regurgitation (Figure 5, Video 4). Right ventricular size and systolic function were normal, and the right ventricular systolic pressure was 38 mm Hg.

Management

Given ECG findings concerning for inferior STEMI, the catheterization laboratory was activated. The patient received aspirin, a P2Y12 inhibitor, and heparin per acute coronary syndrome protocol. However, coronary angiography revealed no obstructive coronary artery disease. Ongoing shock prompted escalation to vasoactive support (norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine, and phenylephrine), endotracheal intubation for respiratory failure/airway protection, and placement of an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) on 1:1 for mechanical circulatory support. She was promptly transferred to a tertiary cardiovascular intensive care unit for advanced shock management. On arrival, lactate increased to 5.4 mmol/L (from 2.9 mmol/L), and point-of-care ultrasound suggested severe LV dysfunction with apical and midventricular hypokinesis and severe dynamic LVOTO gradient in the setting of basal hyperkinesis. Given worsening shock, escalation of mechanical circulatory device was considered for venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation after identification of significant LVOTO. The shock treatment strategy emphasized avoidance of afterload reduction and optimize preload to reduce outflow tract obstruction. First, the patient underwent fluid resuscitation to optimize preload. Then, as blood pressure started to respond to repeated fluid challenges, norepinephrine was weaned off, then epinephrine to avoid tachycardia, and both were transitioned meticulously to phenylephrine as a pure afterload-supporting agent as the sole pressor. Then, IABP support was weaned down from 1:1 to 1:4 to minimize the afterload reduction with ongoing systemic anticoagulation. The patient's hemodynamic profile improved significantly with these measures and did not need to escalate to venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. Phenylephrine was successfully discontinued, and follow-up ECG demonstrated normal results (Figure 1).

Outcome and Follow-Up

Within 24 hours, vasoactive support was weaned off, and the patient was extubated after passing spontaneous breathing trials. With clinical stabilization and improved perfusion, the IABP was removed. A plan was made to consider cautious initiation of negative inotropy (eg, β-blockade) once blood pressure was adequate and LVOT physiology could be reassessed, along with guideline-directed therapy after recovery from shock.

Discussion

Our case underscores 3 high-yield clinical lessons: 1) serial ECGs save lives; 2) Takotsubo syndrome belongs on the STEMI differential; and 3) shock management must match the hemodynamic phenotype, especially when dynamic LVOTO is present.

Takotsubo syndrome often presents with chest pain, troponin elevation, and ischemic ECG patterns including ST-segment elevation mimicking STEMI.1 Large registries show substantial acute morbidity, including cardiogenic shock, despite the condition's reputation as “reversible.” The 2018 international consensus documents emphasize that Takotsubo is not benign, and that diagnostic certainty frequently requires coronary angiography when STEMI criteria are met.2 In a case series, 18% of patients were found to have LVOTO and were associated with adverse outcomes.3 Notably, Takotsubo syndrome can occur without an obvious trigger, with approximately one-third of cases having no precipitating cause.1

In Takotsubo syndrome, basal hyperkinesis and a relatively small LV cavity can cause SAM and dynamic LVOTO, producing hypotension that is often misattributed to pump failure. This is crucial because usual shock therapies (inotropes, vasodilators, and aggressive diuresis) can worsen obstruction by increasing contractility and lowering preload/afterload.4 Prior reviews and consensus guidance support fluid optimization when appropriate, alpha-agonist vasopressors, and avoidance of inotropes in such cases.5

IABP reduces afterload, which may be counterproductive in LVOTO physiology, and there are reports of IABP-induced or exacerbated obstruction and hemodynamic compromise.6,7 In this patient, our shock team urgently de-escalated augmentation (1:4) to avoid excessive afterload reduction while maintaining stabilization and facilitating transfer. The key clinical principle is to recognize this physiology early and titrate vasoactive support to avoid worsening the gradient, with frequent bedside echocardiographic reassessment. Published data emphasize the same bedside sequence.3,5,7 First, it is recognized that a basal hyperkinesis with a relatively small LV cavity can generate SAM, dynamic LVOTO, or secondary mitral regurgitation. Next, the focus would be on stopping or de-emphasizing therapies that intensify the gradient with the aim of reducing obstruction. This means withdrawal of β-agonist–predominant support as feasible, cautious preload optimization when pulmonary congestion is not the dominant problem and prioritization of alpha-adrenergic vasopressors such as phenylephrine. Short-acting negative inotropy may be considered only after blood pressure stabilizes and the obstructive physiology has been reassessed.

When ST-segment elevation and troponin elevation occur without obstructive coronary disease, myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries becomes a working diagnosis that requires targeted evaluation for Takotsubo syndrome, myocarditis, coronary spasm, embolus, and spontaneous coronary artery dissection. In this case, the ventriculogram and echocardiographic pattern (extensive wall-motion abnormality beyond a single epicardial territory plus SAM/LVOTO) supported Takotsubo syndrome as the unifying diagnosis.

The ECG evolution highlights a practical pearl. Repeat ECGs in ongoing symptoms or evolving shock are mandatory. This case also illustrates how a STEMI-like ECG can still represent a nonocclusive process where the catheterization laboratory activation is appropriate, but postcatheterization management must rapidly pivot to the correct shock phenotype.

Conclusions

Takotsubo syndrome can present with inferior STEMI patterns and cardiogenic shock. Early recognition of dynamic LVOT obstruction is essential because standard shock therapies may worsen hemodynamics. Serial ECGs, emergent angiography when STEMI criteria are met, and echocardiography-guided hemodynamic management are central to favorable outcomes.
