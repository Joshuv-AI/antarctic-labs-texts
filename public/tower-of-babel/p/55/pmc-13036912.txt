Genomic surveillance of H1N1, H3N2 and influenza B Victoria lineage viruses in Qingyang (2020–2023): phylogenetic and molecular characteristics analysis of HA/NA genes
Authors: Guina Zhu, Huaizhe Hou, Wenjun Wang, Zhihui Dou, Yanhong Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHA and NA are important surface proteins of influenza viruses. The purpose of this study was to explore the molecular evolution and amino acid mutations of HA and NA of H1N1, H3N2 and Influenza B Victoria lineage (B/Victoria) viruses in Qingyang from 2020 to 2023.MethodsThrough the China Influenza Surveillance Information System, the positive data of influenza etiology surveillance in Qingyang from 2020 to 2023 were screened. A total of 19 samples of H1N1, 47 samples of H3N2 and 51 samples of B/Victoria were screened. Subsequently, virus nucleic acid extraction, whole genome sequencing, amino acid and nucleotide homology analysis, phylogenetic analysis, and amino acid site mutation analysis were performed.ResultsMiniseq sequencing was performed on 117 influenza strains. Homology analysis showed that H1N1 and H3N2 strains had relatively low homology with vaccine representative strains. Amino acid mutation analysis of HA and NA showed that the H1N1 strains had receptor binding site and antigen site mutations, but no glycosylation site and resistance site mutations. However, H3N2 strains had mutations at receptor binding sites, antigenic sites, glycosylation sites, and resistance sites. The B/Victoria strains had antigenic site mutations, but no drug resistance site mutations. Moreover, only strains from 2021 to 2022 exhibit a glycosylation site mutation.ConclusionGenetic characteristics analysis was conducted for the first time on H1N1, H3N2, and B/Victoria viruses prevalent in Qingyang, which enables us to understand the molecular evolution and amino acid mutations of HA and NA.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12853-2.Keywords: H1N1, H3N2, Influenza B Victoria lineage, Haemagglutinin, Neuraminidase, Phylogenetic, Amino acid mutations

Introduction

Influenza viruses are common pathogens in the human respiratory tract, causing a large number of outpatient visits, hospitalizations and deaths each year [1, 2]. Influenza A and B are the main causes of annual influenza outbreaks in humans [3]. Influenza A and B viruses consist of 8 RNA segments, including PB1, PB2, PA, HA, NP, NA, MP, and NS [4]. Influenza A viruses are divided into multiple subtypes according to HA and NA, the most noteworthy are the H1N1 and H3N2 subtypes that are co-transmitted in the population [3, 5]. Influenza B viruses are divided into Victorian and Yamagata lineages according to the antigen characteristics and HA sequence, and Yamagata causes fewer epidemics than Victorian influenza [6].

HA is a glycoprotein on the surface of influenza viruses that binds to receptors on the surface of host cells and mediates viral infection of host cells [7]. HA has a high degree of variability, and there are significant differences in the structure and antigenicity of HA in different subtypes of influenza viruses [7, 8]. The structure of HA is mainly composed of a spherical head (containing the main antigenic sites) and a relatively conserved stem, which is a potential target for the development of universal influenza vaccines [9]. NA is another important influenza virus surface glycoprotein, which is able to cut the sialic acid on the surface of the host cell, facilitating the release of the virus from the infected cell, thus assisting in the transmission of the virus within the host [10]. NA is also the major target of current neuraminidase inhibitors, a class of anti-influenza drugs (such as oseltamivir and zanamivir) [11]. The gradual accumulation of point mutations in influenza genes, especially those encoding HA and NA (antigenic drift), can lead to selection and emergence of novel variant strains which can cause annual epidemics [9, 12]. Currently, influenza vaccination and antiviral drug therapy are the two main strategies to combat influenza viruses. However, due to the rapid evolution of the HA and NA genes of influenza viruses, the nucleotide and amino acid site mutations continue to increase, which affects the effectiveness of vaccine protection and drug treatment. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct influenza surveillance at the molecular level to understand the gene evolution and amino acid mutation of HA and NA, which will help to discover new influenza and provide a scientific basis for updating vaccines and drugs.

In this study, genetic characteristics analysis was conducted for the first time on H1N1, H3N2, and Influenza B Victoria lineage (B/Victoria) viruses prevalent in Qingyang City from 2020 to 2023 to understand the molecular evolution and amino acid mutations of HA and NA. The purpose of this study was to explore the molecular evolution and amino acid mutations of HA and NA, hoping to help the precise prevention and control of influenza in Qingyang City and provide a theoretical basis for the development of vaccines and drugs.

Materials and methods

Source of samples

Year-round continuous surveillance data from the Chinese Influenza Surveillance Information System, which is a subsystem of the China Disease Prevention and Control Information System, were used to systematically screen the positive results of influenza etiological surveillance in Qingyang City from 2020 to 2023, thus avoiding temporal sampling bias in this study. For each year, samples were selected in strict accordance with the actual epidemiological characteristics of influenza subtypes in Qingyang City to ensure the representativeness of samples for locally circulating influenza strains. Based on the surveillance data, H1N1 was prevalent in 2023, H3N2 was prevalent in 2020 and 2023, and B/Victoria was continuously prevalent during 2020–2023. Accordingly, a total of 117 samples were screened for miniseq sequencing, including 19 H1N1 strains (2023), 47 H3N2 strains (15 from 2020 to 32 from 2023), and 51 B/Victoria strains (5 from 2020, 9 from 2021, 19 from 2022, and 18 from 2023). We acknowledge that the distribution of the selected samples in years and subtypes is uneven. This unevenness is an objective reflection of the natural epidemic characteristics of influenza in Qingyang City. The prevalence and intensity of different influenza subtypes are different among different years. The original throat swab specimens were stored in a -80 °C refrigerator.

Virus nucleic acid extraction

Viral RNA was extracted from throat swab samples using the viral RNA/DNA extraction kit from Xi’an Tianlong Technology Co. according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Influenza A/B virus nucleic acid detection (fluorescent PCR method) kit (commercial kit produced by Jiangsu Shuoshi Biotechnology Co., LTD., China) was used to detect the extracted nucleic acid by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). Subsequently, PCR amplification, purification and qubit quantification were performed.

Whole genome sequencing (WGS)

Influenza virus gene capture using ULSEN Ultra-sensitive influenza virus whole genome capture kit (Beijing Micro Future Technology Co., LTD., China). Qubit dsDNA HS (High Sensitivity) kit (Beijing Micro Future Technology Co., LTD., China) was used for quantification. Library preparation and microarrays were performed using the Illumina kit. Finally, Illumina Miniseq sequencing platform was used for sequencing. The data were spliced and analyzed using the influenza virus genome-wide analysis software V1.0 (MicronCoV Analyzer) of Beijing Microfuture Technology Co., Ltd.

Sequence and phylogenetic analysis

Clustal W in MEGA7.0 software was used for sequence alignment. MegAlign in DNASTAR Lasergene 11 software performed nucleotide and amino acid homology analysis between the sequence measured and the corresponding vaccine representative strains. NCBI online tools (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/FLU/annotation) were used to complete nucleotide to amino acid conversions. MEGA 7.0 software (Maximum Composite Likelihood) was used to calculate nucleotide genetic distances. NetNGlyc 1.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetNGlyc-1.0/) online tool was used to predict glycosylation sites, and the threshold greater than 0.5 was the indication value of glycosylation. MEGA7.0 software and GISAID FluSurver (https://flusurver.bii.a-star.edu.sg/) online tool were used to complete amino acid mutation site analysis and branch query.

The prevalence of influenza viruses has significant seasonal differences between the northern and southern hemispheres. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends influenza vaccine components for the northern and southern hemispheres each year based on data from the Global Influenza Surveillance Network (GISN). Therefore, the selection of vaccine strains recommended by WHO can most authoritatively represent the main epidemic strains that need to be targeted for prevention in the corresponding epidemic season, so that the variation analysis has clear epidemiological and public health significance. In this study, according to the collection time of the samples, the vaccine strains recommended by WHO in the same period were selected as reference. GISAID EpiFlu (https://gisaid.org/) and NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database were used to download the WHO recommended vaccine representative strains A/Wisconsin/588/2019 (H1N1) pdm09 (2022–2023 Northern Hemisphere vaccine representative strain), A/Kansas/14/2017 (H3N2) (2019–2020 Northern Hemisphere vaccine representative strain), A/Darwin/9/2021 (H3N2) (2022–2024 Northern Hemisphere vaccine representative strain), B/Colorado/06/2017 (B/Victoria) (2019–2020 Northern Hemisphere vaccine representative strain), B/Washington/02/2019 (B/Victoria) (2020–2022 Northern Hemisphere vaccine representative strain), B/Austria/1,359,417/2021 (B/Victoria) (2022–2024 Northern Hemisphere vaccine representative strain) and domestic and foreign representative strains of H1N1, H3N2 and B/Victoria viruses and their branches were used as reference strains in this study. In MEGA7.0 software, Neighbor-joining (NJ) method was applied to construct the phylogenetic tree, and the BootStrap value was set to 1000 to evaluate the reliability of the phylogenetic tree.

Results

Basic information of influenza strain sequencing

Miniseq sequencing was performed on 19 H1N1 viruses. The average HA fragment length was 1733 bp, and the average NA fragment length was 1445 bp. The genomic coverage of both HA and NA was greater than 99.0%. The median of HA average sequencing depth was 5377, and the median of NA average sequencing depth was 6798. Miniseq sequencing was performed on 47 H3N2 viruses. The average HA fragment length was 1724 bp, and the average NA fragment length was 1433 bp. The genomic coverage of both HA and NA was greater than 99.0%. The median of HA average sequencing depth was 7359, and the median of NA average sequencing depth was 7412. Miniseq sequencing was performed on 51 B/Victoria viruses. The average HA fragment length was 1762 bp, and the average NA fragment length was 1416 bp. The genomic coverage of both HA and NA was greater than 99.0%. The median of HA average sequencing depth was 7585, and the median of NA average sequencing depth was 7765. Detailed information is shown in Table S1.

Homology analysis

The nucleotide and amino acid homology ranges of the HA gene among 19 H1N1 viruses were 99.1%-100% and 99.5%-100%, respectively (Figure S1A). The nucleotide and amino acid homology ranges of the HA gene of the 19 H1N1 viruses and the vaccine representative strain A/Wisconsin/588/2019 (H1N1) pdm09 were 98.6%-98.8% and 98.4%-98.6%, respectively (Figure S1A). The nucleotide genetic distance range of the HA gene among 19 H1N1 viruses was 0-0.008 (Figure S1B). The nucleotide genetic distance range of the HA gene of the 19 H1N1 viruses and the vaccine representative strain A/Wisconsin/588/2019 (H1N1) pdm09 was 0.012–0.015 (Figure S1B).

Among the 47 H3N2 viruses, the nucleotide and amino acid homology ranges of the HA gene were 95.8%-100% and 94.1%-100%, respectively, and the nucleotide genetic distance range was 0-0.039 (Figure S2A and B). The nucleotide and amino acid homology ranges of the HA gene of the 15 H3N2 viruses in 2020 and the vaccine representative strain A/Kansas/14/2017 (H3N2) were 96.6%-96.8% and 96.6%-97.2%, respectively, and the nucleotide genetic distance range was 0.034–0.035. The nucleotide and amino acid homology ranges of the HA gene of the 32 H3N2 viruses in 2023 and the vaccine representative strain A/Darwin/9/2021 (H3N2) were 97.6%-98.5% and 97.2%-98.1%, respectively, and the nucleotide genetic distance range was 0.015–0.020.

For the 51 B/Victoria viruses, HA gene nucleotide and amino acid homology ranges were 97.5%-100% and 97.9%-100%, respectively, with a nucleotide genetic distance of 0-0.025 (Figure S3A and B). The 5 B/Victoria viruses from 2020 exhibited 98.7%-99.0% nucleotide and 98.8%-99.0% amino acid homology with the vaccine representative strain B/Colorado/06/2017 (B/Victoria), with a nucleotide genetic distance of 0.010–0.013. For the 28 B/Victoria viruses from 2021 to 2022, HA gene had 98.5%-99.1% nucleotide and 98.1%-98.6% amino acid homology with the vaccine representative strain B/Washington/02/2019 (B/Victoria), and the nucleotide genetic distance range was 0.009–0.015. The 18 B/Victoria viruses from 2023 exhibited 98.9%-99.1% nucleotide and 99.3%-100% amino acid homology with the vaccine representative strain B/Austria/1,359,417/2021 (B/Victoria), with a nucleotide genetic distance of 0.009–0.011.

Phylogenetic analysis

The HA gene sequences of 19 H1N1 strains were clustered with A/Qinghai-Chengdong/SWL1691/2023, A/Xinjiang-Kuerle/SWL2833/2023 and A/Tianjin-Hongqiao/SWL1192/2023 in the same cluster, which belonged to the 6B.1 A.5a.2a branch (Fig. 1A). The H1N1 strains and the vaccine representative strain A/Wisconsin/588/2019 (H1N1) pdm09 belonged to the 6B.1 A.5a.2 branch, and formed 2 evolutionary branches respectively. Moreover, the NA gene evolutionary tree of 19 H1N1 strains was similar to that of the HA gene evolutionary tree (Fig. 1B).

HA and NA molecular characteristics of H1N1 strains

Compared with the vaccine strain A/Wisconsin/588/2019 (H1N1) pdm09, the HA gene sequences of 19 H1N1 strains had 10 amino acid site mutations (Table 1). Two H1N1 strains (A/Qingyang/QYBFLG2023003/H1N1 (2023), A/Qingyang/QYBFLG2023004/H1N1 (2023)) experienced an amino acid mutation on the 130 loop (P135S) of the receptor binding site. No mutations were observed in other receptor binding sites. All 19 H1N1 strains had amino acid site mutations in the antigenic determinants Cb (K71Q) and Ca1 (A203T). In addition, there were no mutations in the 7 glycosylation sites of the HA gene of 19 H1N1 strains. Compared with the vaccine representative strain A/Wisconsin/588/2019 (H1N1) pdm09, the NA gene sequences of 19 H1N1 strains in this study had 6 amino acid site mutations (Table 2). However, 9 key amino acid sites related to neuraminidase inhibitor resistance on the NA gene were not mutations.

HA and NA molecular characteristics of H3N2 strains

Compared with the vaccine representative strain A/Kansas/14/2017 (H3N2), the HA gene sequences of 15 H3N2 strains in 2020 had 24 amino acid site mutations (Table 3). Compared with the vaccine representative strain A/Darwin/9/2021 (H3N2), the HA gene sequences of 32 H3N2 strains in 2023 had 27 amino acid site mutations (Table 3). Compared with the vaccine representative strain A/Kansas/14/2017 (H3N2), the 15 H3N2 strains in 2020 had 2 amino acid site mutations at the front (N137K) and bottom (S153F) walls of the receptor binding site. No mutations were observed in other receptor binding sites. Compared with the vaccine representative strain A/Darwin/9/2021 (H3N2), the 32 H3N2 strains in 2023 did not have any receptor binding site mutations. Compared with the vaccine representative strain A/Kansas/14/2017 (H3N2), the 15 H3N2 strains in 2020 had 5 site mutations on antigenic determinants, which were E78G in the E region, N137K in the A region, and K160S, N187K and I195V in the B region. Compared with the vaccine representative strain A/Darwin/9/2021 (H3N2), the 32 H3N2 strains in 2023 had 3 site mutations on antigenic determinants, which were I156K, K160N and V198I in the B region. In addition, compared with the vaccine representative strain A/Kansas/14/2017 (H3N2), all 15 H3N2 strains in 2020 exhibited changes in 3 glycosylation sites. A glycosylation site NGTS was lost due to the mutation of T151K amino acid site, a glycosylation site 174 (NYTY) was added due to the mutation of S175Y site, and the glycosylation site 499 (NGTY) was changed to NETY due to the mutation of G500E site. Compared with the vaccine representative strain A/Darwin/9/2021 (H3N2), the 32 H3N2 strains in 2023 added a glycosylation site 110 (NSSC) due to the mutation of N112S amino acid site, and a reduced threshold (< 0.5) for glycosylation site 61 (NSSI) due to E66K site mutation. Furthermore, 19 H3N2 strains had a reduced threshold (< 0.5) for glycosylation site 142 (NWTG) due to the mutation of N138D site, 2 H3N2 strains had an additional glycosylation site 160 (NSSF) due to the mutation of S160N site, and 1 H3N2 strain was lost a glycosylation site 79 (NCTL) due to the mutation of N79D site, and 1 H3N2 strain changed the glycosylation site 262 (NSTG) to NNTG due to the mutation of S263N.

Discussion

The influenza epidemic situation of Qingyang City in 2020–2023 was checked through the China Influenza Surveillance Information System. It was found that H1N1 virus was prevalent in 2023, H3N2 virus was prevalent in 2020 and 2023, and B/Victoria virus was prevalent in 2020–2023. In this study, 19 H1N1 strains from 2023, 15 H3N2 strains from 2020, 32 H3N2 strains from 2023, 5 B/Victoria strains from 2020, 9 B/Victoria strains from 2021, 19 B/Victoria strains from 2022, and 18 B/Victoria strains from 2023 were selected for sequencing. Miniseq sequencing was performed on 117 influenza strains, and the genome coverage of both HA and NA was greater than 99.0%. Homology analysis showed that H1N1 and H3N2 strains had relatively low homology with vaccine representative strains. For the B/Victoria strains, the strains in 2020 and 2021–2022 had relatively low homology with the vaccine representative strains, while the strains in 2023 had relatively high homology with the vaccine representative strains.

Phylogenetic analysis showed that 19 H1N1 strains formed 2 branches with vaccine representative strain A/Wisconsin/588/2019 (H1N1) pdm09. The receptor binding site of HA protein is distributed in the HA1 region of H1N1 virus, which is composed of 3 domains 130 loop, 190 helix, 220 loop and 3 conserved amino acid residues 98Y, 153 W and 183 H [13]. Moreover, there are 5 antigenic determinants closely related to the HA antigenicity of H1N1 virus, which are distributed in the HA1 region, namely Sa, Sb, Ca1, Ca2 and Cb [14]. Compared with the vaccine representative strain A/Wisconsin/588/2019 (H1N1) pdm09, the HA sequence of 19 H1N1 strains had 10 amino acid site mutations. Among these 10 amino acid mutation sites, 2 sites (K71Q and A203T) are involved in the Cb and Ca1 regions of the antigenic determinant, respectively, and are common mutations. There are also 2 strains of A/Qingyang/QYBFLG2023003/H1N1 (2023) and A/Qingyang/QYBFLG2023004/H1N1 (2023) that have undergone P135S mutation at the receptor binding site 130 loop. The above results suggest that H1N1 is constantly evolving.

Phylogenetic analysis showed that the 15 H3N2 strains in 2020 were not in the same branch as the vaccine representative strain A/Kansas/14/2017 (H3N2), and the 32 H3N2 strains in 2023 were distantly related to the vaccine representative strain A/Darwin/9/2021 (H3N2). These phylogenetic patterns, combined with subsequent sequence variation analyses, provide indirect clues about potential discrepancies between vaccine strains and circulating strains. The receptor binding site of HA protein is distributed in the HA1 region of H3N2 virus, which is composed of the front wall, back wall, left arm, right arm and bottom wall [15, 16]. Moreover, there are 5 antigenic determinants closely related to the HA antigenicity of H3N2 virus, namely A region, B region, C region, D region, and E region [17, 18]. Compared with the vaccine representative strain, the HA sequences of the 15 H3N2 strains in 2020 had 24 amino acid site mutations, including 2 mutation sites (N137K and S153F) involving the front and bottom walls of the receptor binding sites, and 5 mutation sites involving the E (E78G), A (N137K), and B (K160S, N187K and I195V) regions of the antigenic determinant. For influenza virus, if more than 4 amino acid site mutations occur in 2 or more antigenic determinants of HA, or if more than 1 amino acid site mutation occurs in both the antigenic determinant and the receptor binding site, antigenic drift may occur and new variant may emerge, which has epidemiological significance [12, 19–21]. The HA sequences of the H3N2 strains in 2020 had 6 amino acid site mutations in 2 receptor binding sites and 3 antigenic determinants, and the mutation of the receptor binding site N137K overlapped with the antigenic determinant A region. The antigenic determinants close to the receptor binding site play a key role in evading the immunity of neutralizing antibodies [22]. Therefore, it is suggested that the H3N2 strains in 2020 may have undergone antigenic drift, and could be are new variants with epidemiological significance. Compared with the vaccine representative strain, the HA sequences of the 32 H3N2 strains in 2023 had 27 amino acid site mutations, of which 3 mutation sites (I156K, K160N and V198I) occurred in the B region of the antigen determinant, which may be the main reason for the distant relationship with the vaccine representative strain.

Phylogenetic analysis showed the 5 B/Victoria strains in 2020 belonged to the V1a.3 branch and were not in the same branch as the vaccine representative strain B/Colorado/06/2017 (B/Victoria) in the V1a.1 branch. One of the 46 B/Victoria strains in 2021–2023 belonged to the V1a.3a.1 branch, and the remaining 45 strains belonged to the V1a.3a.2 branch. The evolutionary distance between the 27 B/Victoria strains in 2021–2022 and the vaccine representative strain B/Washington/02/2019 (B/Victoria) located in V1a.3 is relatively far, while the 18 B/Victoria strains in 2023 are in the same evolutionary branch as the vaccine representative strain B/Austria/1,359,417/2021 (B/Victoria) located in V1a.3a.2. There are 4 antigenic determinants closely related to the HA antigenicity of B/Victoria strains, namely 120 loop, 150 loop, 160 loop and 190 helix [23]. Compared with the vaccine representative strain, the HA sequences of the 5 B/Victoria strains in 2020 had 7 amino acid site mutations, including 2 sites (G144D and G148R) involving the antigenic determinant 150 loop. Compared with the vaccine representative strain, the HA sequences of the 28 B/Victoria strains in 2021–2022 had 15 amino acid site mutations, among which 27 B/Victoria strains have 5 amino acid site mutations (R148G, G197E, H137Q, A142T and N165K) involving antigenic determinants 120 loop, 150 loop, 160 loop and 190 helix. Compared with the vaccine representative strain, the HA sequences of the 18 B/Victoria strains in 2023 had 5 amino acid site mutations, among which E143G and E196K mutations involving the antigenic determinant 150 loop and 190 helix, respectively. For influenza virus, if more than 4 amino acid site mutations occur in 2 or more antigenic determinants of HA, or if more than 1 amino acid site mutation occurs in both the antigenic determinant and the receptor binding site, antigenic drift may occur and new variant may emerge, which has epidemiological significance [12, 19–21]. Herein, the B/Victoria strains in 2021–2022 involve 5 amino acid site mutations on 4 antigenic determinants, indicating they may be variant strains with potential epidemiological significance. From a sequence perspective, the evolutionary differences and antigenic determinant mutations in 2020–2022 B/Victoria strains may weaken the ability of the corresponding vaccines to protect against these strains, while the 2023 strains’ close evolutionary relationship with the 2023 vaccine strain indicate that the vaccine can provide better protection against them. However, these inferences are preliminary and need confirmation through functional assays and epidemiological data on vaccine effectiveness.

Glycosylation is considered to be involved in the folding of viral proteins, stabilizing the spatial structure of proteins and hindering the recognition of antibodies. Therefore, changes in glycosylation sites, including changes in location and number, may change the antigenicity, pathogenicity and immune escape of viruses [24, 25]. The amino acid mutation of glycosylation sites in the HA sequence of influenza virus may lead to changes in biological characteristics such as virus structure, receptor binding ability, pathogenicity, and antigenicity [26]. In this study, there were no glycosylation site mutations in the HA sequences of 19 H1N1 strains, indicating that the antigenicity of H1N1 viruses in 2020 may not have been significantly affected, and the vaccine strain may still corresponding protective effects. The HA sequences of H3N2 strains in 2020 and 2023 showed multiple glycosylation site mutations, which may have caused antigenic changes and immune evasion of the H3N2 virus in Qingyang City in 2020 and 2023. In addition, there was no glycosylation site mutation in the HA sequence of the B/Victoria strains in 2020 and 2023, and only 1 potential glycosylation site variation occurred in 2021–2022, indicating that the B/Victoria strains in the 3 time periods did not undergo significant antigenic changes. It should be noted, however, that functional experiments were not performed in this study to verify the actual glycosylation status of the viruses and their corresponding biological effects.

NA is an important surface glycoprotein and plays a vital role in viral replication and drug development [27]. It is also the main target of antiviral neuraminidase inhibitors (such as oseltamivir and zanamivir) [28, 29]. The 6 amino acid mutation sites in the NA sequences of 19 H1N1 strains in 2023 did not involve resistance site, which suggests that neuraminidase inhibitors such as oseltamivir and zanamivir may remain effective for the treatment of H1N1 virus in Qingyang City, but this conclusion is based solely on sequence data and lacks phenotypic validation. Compared with the vaccine representative strains, there were 18 amino acid site mutations in the NA sequences of 15 H3N2 strains in 2020 and 12 amino acid site mutations in the NA sequence of 32 H3N2 strains in 2023. However, only the NA sequences of 7 H3N2 strains in 2023 had mutations at the I222V site associated with drug resistance. It has been reported that mutations in the NA 222 position may be related to strong and mild drug resistance, and will reduce the sensitivity to oseltamivir and zanamivir [30–32], so it deserves attention. However, the clinical relevance of this mutation, including the actual level of drug resistance it may induce, remains unconfirmed due to the absence of phenotypic and functional assays in this study. Compared with vaccine representative strains, the NA sequences of 51 B/Victoria strains in the 3 time periods of 2020, 2021–2022, and 2023 all had amino acid site mutation, but no resistance sites were involved. This suggests that neuraminidase inhibitors may still be effective in the treatment of B/Victoria virus infection in Qingyang City, and can be used as the first choice for clinical treatment, while this conclusion requires further validation.

As the first molecular evolutionary analysis of influenza viruses in Qingyang, this study covered two subtypes (influenza A and B) including three viral strains (H1N1, H3N2 and B/Victoria) for HA and NA gene detection and analysis, with a more comprehensive research scope and detailed multi-strain analysis compared with the existing regional influenza surveillance studies [33–36]. In addition, the results of this study may be closely related to the geographical location, population mobility and regional influenza prevention and control measures in Qingyang City. This not only provides molecular evidence for the selection of influenza vaccine strains and clinical rational use of drugs in Qingyang City and the border areas of Gansu, Shaanxi and Ningxia, but also enriches the national influenza virus genetic variation database and lays a solid molecular foundation for influenza prevention and control in Northwest China.

In conclusion, this study revealed the molecular evolution and amino acid mutation of HA and NA genes of H1N1, H3N2 and B/Victoria viruses in Qingyang City. This will contribute to the precise prevention and control of influenza in Qingyang City and provide a theoretical basis for the development of vaccines and drugs.
