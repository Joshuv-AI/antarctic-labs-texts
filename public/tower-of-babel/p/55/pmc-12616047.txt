Middle to long-term outcomes and pathological changes of implanted expanded polytetrafluoroethylene valved conduits
Authors: Yixuan Cai, Yaping Shan, Gang Chen, Yaping Mi, Hui Zhong, Ming Ye, Huifeng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study evaluated the mid to long-term outcomes of expanded polytetrafluoroethylene valved conduits and preliminarily explored the reasons for conduit failure.MethodsThe study included patients who received reconstruction with ePTFE valved conduits between June 2014 and March 2025. Echocardiography was used to evaluate the conduit function. Histopathological staining was applied to demonstrate the details of the failure conduits.Results104 patients had a median age of 90 months (interquartile range, 44.25–144 months) and a median follow-up of 48 months (IQR, 16–54.25 months). 4 early deaths (3.8 %) and 1 late death (1.0 %) occurred. 28 conduits (26.9 %) were detected with dysfunction, with 10 (9.6 %) progressing to failure. Infective endocarditis only occurred in 2 patients (1.9 %). 7 patients (6.7 %) received re-interventions, including 2 percutaneous interventions, 1 replacement after percutaneous intervention, and 4 direct replacements. Pathological staining showed no thrombus formation, but calcification on the valve junction. The valved conduit exhibited insufficient endothelialization, accompanied by the infiltration of inflammatory cells and remarkable neointimal hyperplasia.ConclusionsThe application of the ePTFE valved conduit presented low mortality rate and relatively appreciable conduit function. Conduit stenosis was the main cause of failure, characterized by calcification and neointimal hyperplasia, and might be related to inflammatory response and material damage.

Introduction

Materials for right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) reconstruction (RVOTR) are crucial in the treatment of right heart diseases. In recent years, there is some controversy over the durability of Homograft and bovine jugular vein valved conduit (BJVC) in their application in young patients due to limited size, significant immune rejection reactions [1] and infective endocarditis [2,3]. Therefore, it is necessary to obtain a safe and highly durable material.

Recently, expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) valved conduit has been applied due to good biocompatibility and anti-calcification properties [4,5]. However, the current preparation process was complex and difficult to replicate [6,7]. We developed a simple, hand-sewn tri-leaflet 0.1 mm ePTFE valved conduit and observed good midterm application outcomes [8]. This study evaluated the mid to long-term outcomes of this conduit for RVOTR in pediatric patients and preliminary explored the causes of conduit failure.

Patients and methods

Design and construction of the valved conduit

The 0.1 mm ePTFE film and Gore-tex conduit (W.L. Gore & Associates) were used as construction materials. With the help of self-designed valve suturing positioning molds and valve cutting molds (Supplementary Fig. 1), the production time was in about 30 min. The selection of conduit size was based on the Z score corresponding to standard body weight and age charts [9,10], as well as the diameter of the distal pulmonary artery trunk.

Clinical study design

A flow diagram is included to depict the number of eligible patients, exclusions, and final cohort (Fig. 1). This retrospective study evaluated all patients who received RVOTR with ePTFE valved conduits at Children’s Hospital of Fudan University between June 2014 and March 2025. Patients treated with non-tricuspid ePTFE valve, palliation surgery, and non-biventricular repair were excluded. The primary outcomes were survival and conduit function.Fig. 1A flow chart depicting the number of eligible patients, exclusions, and final cohort. Surgical technique

Surgery was performed through median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass routinely. Combined cardiac malformations were repaired concurrently. During surgery, the ePTFE valve was sewn close to the bifurcation of the left and right pulmonary arteries at the distal end of the conduit to avoid regurgitation caused by potential blood flow filling. The proximal end of the valved conduct was trimmed into a trapezoid and the distal end was cut obliquely to expand the anastomotic site and match the diameter of the RVOT and the pulmonary artery. If the branches or bifurcation of the left and right pulmonary arteries were narrow, bovine pericardium should be used to expand the distal anastomosis.

Cefazolin (50 mg/kg) was administered 30 min before surgery and continued every 12 h for at least 3 days. Heparin was applied (5–15 U/kg/h) intravenously 6 h after surgery and continued for 2 to 3 days. Warfarin was used as an alternative to heparin to keep the international normalized ratio at 1.5 to 2.0 during the subsequent 6 months. Afterwards, it was transitioned to oral anticoagulation with aspirin for 2 years.

Data collection

The clinical data were collected from the patients’ medical records. Infective endocarditis was defined as an infection of the heart valve, endocardium, or artificial heart implants caused by pathogenic microorganisms, which used modified Duke criteria [11]. Active bleeding was defined as hemorrhage with more than 4–5 mL/kg/h postoperatively and lasted for 3–4 h without reduction. Delayed chest closure, low cardiac output syndrome, and the use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), had also been recorded.

Doppler echocardiography was used for evaluating conduct function. Conduit dysfunction was defined as moderate or severe conduit stenosis [12] or regurgitation [13]. Conduit failure was defined when surgical conduit replacement or percutaneous intervention was needed as a result of severe conduit stenosis [12] or regurgitation [13]. Early death was defined as death occurred within 30 days and late death as death occurred beyond 30 days after conduit implantation. Echocardiography, electrocardiogram, and chest X-ray examination were provided at 6 months and 1 year after surgery. Then, annual examination was conducted every 1 year.

Ethics committee

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013) and was approved by the Research Ethics Board of the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University [Approval no. (2024)210]. Exemption of informed consent from patients with the consent of the ethics committee.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR, 25th-75th percentile), and categoric variables as absolute numbers and percentages. The Kaplan-Meier curve was used to analyze the survival rate and conduit function. Cox regression analysis was performed to identify risk factors for death and conduit failure. Variables with P < 0.05 in the multivariate analysis were considered as independent risk factors. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS statistics 25.0 (SPSS, Inc). Images were created with GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc).

Pathological staining analysis

Von kossa and Movat staining was adopted to display the calcification and fibrosis of the valved conduit. CD31, CD68, and CD19 immunohistochemical staining were used to demonstrate the endothelialization, macrophage infiltration, and B lymphocyte infiltration of the valved conduits, respectively. CD206 and α-SMA immunofluorescence staining were used to demonstrate the generation of macrophage-to-myofibroblast transition (MMT) on the valved conduits.

Perioperative results

The conduit size was 14 mm for 5 (4.8 %) patients, 16 mm for 10 (9.6 %), 18 mm for 27 (26.0 %), 20 mm for 24 (23.1 %), 22 mm for 27 (26.0 %), and 24 mm for 11 (10.6 %). The median conduit size was 20 mm (IQR, 18–22 mm) and the median conduit Z score was + 1.32 (IQR, +1.18-+1.61).

After surgery, delayed chest closure was implemented on 23 (22.1 %) patients. 14 (13.5 %) patients experienced active bleeding, 3 (2.9 %) developed low cardiac output syndrome, and 3 (2.9 %) received ECMO treatment. The median ventilation time was 56.1 h (IQR, 22.5–119.4 h) and the median intensive care unit time was 6 days (IQR, 3–9 days).

Mortality

The median follow-up time was 48 months (IQR, 16–54.25 months). 4 Early deaths occurred in patients diagnosed with PA/VSD (n = 2), truncus arteriosus (n = 1) and complex transposition of the great arteries (n = 1). Late death occurred only in one patient with the diagnosis of PA/VSD. The overall 7-year survival rate was 95.1 % (95 % CI, 88.6 %-97.9 %) (Fig. 2A). Conduit function

Two patients developed infective endocarditis and recovered after receiving antifungal treatment. Freedom from contracting infective endocarditis was 97.8 % (95 %CI, 91.6 %-99.5 %) at 7-year follow-up (Fig. 2B).

Twenty-eight patients experienced conduit dysfunction. Freedom from conduit dysfunction was 83.6 % (95 % CI, 72.8 %-90.4 %) and 57.0 % (95 % CI, 38.7 %-71.6 %) and 38.0 % (95 % CI, 18.2 %-57.7 %) at 3, 5 and 7-year follow-up. Ten patients experienced conduit failure due to valved conduit stenosis (n = 8) and stenosis combined with regurgitation (n = 2). Freedom from conduit failure was 97.0 % (95 % CI, 88.4 %-99.2 %), 81.5 % (95 % CI, 62.9 %-91.4 %) and 75.3 % (95 % CI, 52.9 %-88.1 %) at 3, 5 and 7-year follow-up (Fig. 2C).

Seven patients experienced re-interventions. Three patients experienced percutaneous intervention due to PS. Five patients experienced conduit replacement, including one patient previously received percutaneous intervention. Freedom from re-intervention was 100 %, 95.1 % (95 % CI, 85.6 %-98.4 %) and 64.9 % (95 % CI, 33.2 %-84.4 %) at 3, 5 and 7-year follow-up (Fig. 2D).

Four patients experienced moderate and above level valved conduit regurgitation. Freedom from moderate and above level valved conduit regurgitation was 97.1 % (95 % CI, 88.9 %-99.3 %), 97.1 % (95 % CI, 88.9 %-99.3 %) and 83.2 % (95 % CI, 54.8 %-94.5 %) at 3, 5 and 7-year follow-up (Fig. 3A). Twenty-eight patients experienced moderate and above level valved conduit stenosis. Freedom from moderate and above level valved conduit stenosis was 83.6 % (95 % CI, 72.8 %-90.4 %), 57.9 % (95 % CI, 40.0 %-72.3 %) and 38.6 % (95 % CI, 18.6 %-58.4 %) at 3, 5 and 7-year follow-up (Fig. 3B).Fig. 3Kaplan-Meier curve of freedom from moderate and above level valved conduit regurgitation (A) and valved conduit stenosis (B).

Pathological changes of the decayed ePTFE valved conduit

There was no significant thrombus formation. Von Kossa staining showed concentrated calcification deposition at the valve junction and Movat staining showed clear neointimal formation on the conduit surface (Supplementary Fig. 2). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated insufficient endothelialization, the infiltration of macrophages and B lymphocytes, and the transformation process of MMT on the conduit surface (Supplementary Figs. 3–6).

Discussion

We retrospectively evaluated 104 patients who underwent RVOTR using hand-sewn ePTFE valved conduits. A total of 5 patients died, 28 had conduit dysfunction and 10 of them experienced conduit failure. Among these 10 patients, 4 received conduit replacement directly. 3 received percutaneous intervention at first, and 1 of them received subsequent replacement. 3 requested follow-up observation.

As a new material, ePTFE valved conduit has attracted widespread clinical attention. According to the applications of 110 cases by Shinkawa [6], 3 patients (2.7 %) died, including 2 early deaths (1.8 %), 1 late death (0.9 %), with a 5-year survival rate of 95.3 %. Miyazaki [14] reported 37 deaths (4.1 %) out of 902 cases, including 15 early deaths (1.7 %), and 22 late deaths (2.4 %). Li [15] reported 2 deaths (2.0 %) out of 102 cases with a median follow-up of (18.4 ± 10.6) months. Our data showed a 7-year survival rate of 95.1 %, proving the safety of the ePTFE valved conduit implantation surgery.

Speaking of conduit function, we reported an ideal 96.2 % PR grade at mild or trivial but only 73.1 % PS grade below moderate at total, comparing to 95.6 % and 91.1 % reported by Miyazaki [14]. Freedom from percutaneous intervention at 5-year was 96.9 %, better than 83.7 % reported by Shinkawa [6]. Freedom from conduit replacement at 5-year was 96.5 %, close to the 93.9 % [14] and 92.7 % [6] reported in literature. Compared with the data of Li [15], freedom from conduit dysfunction at 1 and 2 years was 96.7 % and 92.6 % at ours, relatively better than the 92.7 % and 78.3 % they reported [15]. As a xenograft, ePTFE conduits was not prone to contract infective endocarditis like BJVC [3,16], with a reported [7,[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]] incidence of only 4.7 %, and even lower as 1.9 % in our unit.

Neither postoperative active bleeding nor delayed sternal closure were independent risk factors for death. Possible reasons were the application of bovine pericardium to improve connection compliance, the reinforce at the anastomotic site, and the local gauze filling and compression made the postoperative active bleeding under control. In addition, delayed sternal closure did avoid direct compression of the sternum on the edematous heart and prevented pericardial tamponade, which was crucial for critical patient’s recovery. Eventually, 3 of 23 patients with delayed sternal closure died, and the remaining 20 successfully closed the chest without any infection [23].

Different from the previous reports that smaller conduit size would affect conduit durability [24], no independent risk factors for conduit failure was discovered. The reason might be the application of conduit with a larger Z-score due to the elder age of patients. The average Z-score in our cohort was + 1.56 ± 0.69, which was larger than the recommended + 1.4 by Yamagishi [7].

In further research, we discovered calcification deposition at the valve junction, and neointimal formation on the conduit surface, which might have close connection with the collision of leaflets and the chronic inflammatory response against structurally damaged ePTFE implants. Similar pathological manifestations and related mechanisms have also confirmed our findings [25,26]. Our research group has begun to use fluid–solid interaction simulation models to guide the optimization of valve trimming, in order to reduce valve collision caused by excessive distortion and overlap of the free edges of the valve leaflets. Although this optimization design scheme could not achieve optimal hemodynamic parameters for all, it could maximize the overall performance improvement (Fig. 4). Limitations

Our study is conclusive, albeit it has some limitations. Firstly, it was a retrospective study with limited patients from a single center. Secondly, the median follow-up time was relatively short, which might not be enough to provide a comprehensive evaluation. Thirdly, the number of death and conduit failure is relatively small, hence, more data from multiple centers and age groups should be accumulated to improve the potential overfitting and to confirm relevant conclusions.

Conclusion

The application of the ePTFE valved conduit presented low mortality rate and relatively appreciable conduit function. The progression of valved conduit stenosis might be the arch-criminal of conduit failure. No independent risk factor for death and conduit failure were discovered. More examinations should be conducted to clarify the structural damage and changes that occur after the implantation into the human body.
