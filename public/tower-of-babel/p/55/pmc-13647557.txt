Genome-wide analysis and expression profiling of the phenylalanine ammonia-lyase gene family in Chrysanthemum morifolium
Authors: Feng Wen, Lisi He, Suqing Zhu, Gongmin Cheng, Jianlu An, Wenying Meng, Hao Meng, Ruikang Ma, Yi Wu, Yunxiang Zhao, Maosheng Gu, Xiaozhu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChrysanthemum morifolium, a perennial herb of the genus Chrysanthemum (Asteraceae), is highly valued for its ornamental, edible and medicinal properties, primarily attributed to its abundant flavonoids. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) is the rate-limiting enzyme initiating the phenylpropanoid pathway, which synthesizes lignin, flavonoids, and phytoalexins, key metabolites for plant growth, development, and stress adaptation. Despite the availability of the chromosome-level C. morifolium genome, the PAL gene family remains insufficiently characterized. Specifically, the expression patterns of CmPAL genes across floral developmental stages, floret types, and various abiotic stresses have not been elucidated.ResultsIn this study, 19 CmPAL genes were identified from C. morifolium. Systematic analyses of their molecular evolutionary characteristics, including gene/protein structures, chromosomal localization, phylogenetic relationships, and genome-wide synteny were performed. CmPAL genes were found to be unevenly distributed across 8 of 27 chromosomes, and phylogenetically clustered members exhibited similar gene/protein structures, reflecting evolutionary conservation and functional importance. Expression profiles revealed that CmPAL genes were preferentially expressed in ray florets and at the color-emerging/blooming stages, indicating their involvement in floral pigmentation. Additionally, several CmPALs were significantly upregulated/downregulated under cold, salt, drought, and heavy metal stresses, suggesting roles in abiotic stress responses.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive characterization and expression profiles of the C. morifolium PAL gene family. These findings provide a valuable genomic resource for functional studies of CmPAL genes, as well as identify candidate genes for molecular breeding to improving flower quality and environmental adaptability of chrysanthemums. Specifically, CmPAL1, 6, 11, and 13 are prioritized as major candidate genes for floral pigmentation, while CmPAL3, 5, 9, 10, and 15 are highlighted for their broad-spectrum stress adaptation roles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09513-7.

Background

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL, EC 4.3.1.5) catalyzes the deamination of L-phenylalanine to trans-cinnamic acid, representing the rate-limiting and first committed step in the phenylpropanoid pathway. This pathway synthesizes a diverse array of phenylpropanoid metabolites, including lignins, flavonoids, and phytoalexins, which are essential for plant growth, development, and stress adaptation [1–3]. Since its initial identification in Hordeum vulgare, PAL genes have been characterized in a wide range of organisms, spanning plants, fungi, and bacteria, with a notable absence in animals [4–8].

Structurally, PAL typically functions as a homotetramer comprising three highly conserved domains: the core domain, the shielding domain, and the 4-methylidene-imidazole-5-one (MIO) catalytic domain that harbors an essential Ala-Ser-Gly triad [9–12]. This structural conservation is indispensable for maintaining efficient phenylpropanoid metabolic flux. For Chrysanthemum, this metabolic flux is particularly critical, as it directly drives the targeted accumulation of flavonoids and anthocyanins responsible for its distinct floral pigmentation and medicinal value, alongside lignin and phytoalexins necessary for its stress adaptation and defense responses [13–15]. Phylogenetic analyses have classified the PAL gene family into three major clades corresponding to monocots, dicots, and gymnosperms [16], reflecting evolutionary divergence across major plant lineages. Species-specific expansion of the PAL gene family is predominantly driven by gene duplication events, leading to lineage-specific variations in gene copy number. For example, C. sativus encodes 11 PAL genes [13], S. lycopersicum has 14 [14], and Juglans regia contains 12 [17]. This divergence reflects adaptive evolution tailored to species-specific ecological pressures and developmental demands, enabling fine-tuned regulation of phenylpropanoid metabolism [18].

PAL genes play a central role in shunting carbon flux from primary metabolism into the phenylpropanoid pathway, governing the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites vital for plant development, environmental adaptation, and defense [19, 20]. These functions are mediated by tissue-specific PAL expression and dynamic transcriptional regulation in response to endogenous and exogenous stimuli [18, 21, 22]. First, PAL activity is involved in plant structural and developmental processes, particularly through its regulation of lignin and flavonoid biosynthesis. In Arabidopsis thaliana, AtPAL1 and AtPAL2 are predominantly expressed in vascular tissues, where they promote lignin deposition, which is critical for xylem integrity, vascular conductance, and mechanical support. Disruption of these two genes in pal1/pal2 double mutants impairs root elongation and branching [23]. Similarly, in J. regia, JrPAL3 and JrPAL7 are highly expressed in floral tissues, facilitating flavonoid accumulation that acts as antioxidants and modulates pollen development [17]. In strawberries, PAL expression is coordinated with fruit ripening, coinciding with anthocyanin accumulation that enhances pigmentation and promotes seed dispersal via animal pollinators [24–26].

PAL genes also contribute significantly to plant tolerance to abiotic stresses by boosting phenylpropanoid-derived metabolite synthesis. Under heat stress, CsPAL9 and CsPAL7 are significantly induced in C. sativus, promoting cell wall lignification to maintain cellular integrity and reduce membrane permeability, thereby enhancing thermal tolerance [13]. Concurrently, increased phenolic content mitigates oxidative damage by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS). A similar mechanism operates under drought stress in Arachis hypogaea, upregulation of AhPAL1 and AhPAL2 result in the accumulation of phenolics, which not only neutralizes ROS but also induces stomatal closure to reduce transpiration and improve water-use efficiency [27]. In cold-stressed Vitis vinifera, elevated expression of VvPAL genes regulates the biosynthesis of stilbenes and related phenolics, conferring frost resistance [28–30]. These transcriptional responses are often mediated by cis-acting elements such as ABRE (abscisic acid-responsive elements) and MBS (MYB-binding sites), enabling PAL genes to integrate hormonal and environmental signaling pathways [13, 31, 32].

Beyond abiotic stress, PAL genes are integral to plant defense against biotic threats, facilitating both constitutive and inducible production of antimicrobial metabolites and structural barriers. Upon root-knot nematode infection, S. lycopersicum upregulates SlPAL5 and SlPAL8, which drive root lignin synthesis to restrict nematode penetration and gall formation [14]. In A. hypogaea, AhPAL1 and AhPAL2 contribute to phytoalexins and phenolic acids biosynthesis, inhibiting fungal proliferation and aflatoxin production [27]. PAL activity also enhances rice leaf resistance by promoting phytoalexins biosynthesis [33, 34]. Furthermore, in flowering plants, PAL genes regulate multiple aspects of floral development, particularly traits influencing pollinator interactions such as pigmentation, volatile production, and structural robustness [35–38]. For instance, PAL activity in immature broccoli anthers correlates positively with fertile pollen grain number at flowering, suggesting a role in microspore maturation [39]. In Phalaenopsis equestris, PePAL1 regulates phenolic volatile synthesis in the labellum (lip petal), serving as olfactory attractants for pollinators [40].

Chrysanthemum morifolium, a perennial herb of the genus Chrysanthemum (Asteraceae), holds significant ornamental, edible, and medicinal value, primarily due to its high anthocyanin content (a class of flavonoids). It possesses a large genome that has experienced whole-genome triplication (WGT) and additional smaller-scale duplication events, processes known to drive the expansion of related gene families in this species. Given the central role of PAL in secondary metabolism, PAL gene copy numbers vary markedly across plant lineages, reflecting lineage-specific evolutionary trajectories linked to gene family expansion [23, 41, 42]. Despite the availability of high-quality genomic resources for this species [43], research on PAL gene family in chrysanthemum remains limited. In this study, we identified 19 PAL genes in C. morifolium, conducted in-depth analyses of their gene structures, characteristics, and evolutionary relationships, and investigated their expression profiles across different floral developmental stages, floret types, and abiotic stress conditions. These findings enhance our understanding of the evolutionary dynamics of the PAL gene family in chrysanthemum, lay a foundation for deciphering the PAL functions in regulating floral development and stress responses, and provide valuable insights for molecular breeding to improve chrysanthemum’s ornamental traits, medicinal value, and stress resilience.

Results

Identification and characterization of the CmPAL gene family

A comprehensive genome-wide search for PAL genes in C. morifolium was conducted using both Hidden Markov Model (HMM) analysis (PF00221) and BlastP analysis, using PAL proteins from A. thaliana and Oryza sativa as queries. This integrated approach yielded 19 non-redundant PAL genes, designated CmPAL1 to CmPAL19 according to their positional order along the genomic scaffolds (Table 1). These genes were unevenly distributed across 8 of the 27 C. morifolium chromosomes, with the 24th chromosome harboring the highest density (5 members) and others containing 1 to 4 genes each (Fig. 1). Notably, structural mapping and phylogenetic integration revealed that these clustered members on chromosome 24 belong to the same evolutionary clade. This indicates that the high gene density in this specific chromosomal region is driven by localized tandem duplication events.

To assess the biochemical baseline of these identified members, the physical and chemical properties of CmPAL proteins were analyzed via the ExPASy ProtParam tool (Table 1). Predicted protein lengths ranged from 691 (CmPAL8) to 778 (CmPAL17) amino acid residues, and the theoretical isoelectric points (pI) varied between 5.56 (CmPAL14) and 6.01 (CmPAL15), consistent with PAL proteins from other plant species. All CmPAL proteins had an instability index below 40, indicating in vivo stability, and an aliphatic index of 89.25 to 94.25, reflecting high thermal stability. Additionally, negative Grand Average of Hydropathicity (GRAVY) values (-0.162 to -0.1) confirmed these proteins were primarily hydrophilic (Table 1). InterProScan verification confirmed that all 19 CmPAL proteins contained the conserved PAL domain (IPR005922), and subcellular localization predictions placed them in the cytoplasm, consistent with PAL proteins from other plants (Table 1).

Intra-species synteny and phylogenetic clustering

To understand the evolutionary expansion of this family within the chrysanthemum genome, synteny analysis of the C. morifolium genome was performed, identifying 85,130 collinear gene pairs, accounting for 61.36% of the total genes. Among these, 7 pairs of CmPAL genes showed homology (Fig. 2). The ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions (Ka/Ks) for these pairs ranged from 0.06 to 0.37, indicating that strong purifying selection has acted to maintain their ancestral functions (Table 2). A Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree of CmPAL proteins divided the 19 members into three main clades: Group I (11 genes), Group II (6 genes), and Group III (2 genes) (Supplementary Fig. S1). This clustering aligned with chromosomal distribution patterns, underscoring evolutionary conservation. ClustalX-based multiple sequence alignment revealed over 80% amino acid identity among CmPAL proteins, further confirming their functional conservation and evolutionary significance in phenylpropanoid metabolism and plant development (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Gene and protein structure analysis of the CmPAL gene family

Analysis of gene and protein structure provided valuable insights into the balance between evolutionary conservation and functional divergence of the CmPAL family. Exon-intron organization and conserved protein motifs were examined to explore their evolutionary history and potential functional specialization. All 19 CmPAL genes contained at least one intron, with exon numbers ranging from 2 to 7 (Fig. 3). Sixteen genes exhibited a simple structure, with two exons separated by one intron. In contrast, CmPAL15 and CmPAL17 contained three exons (two introns), and CmPAL14 presented a unique structure with seven exons and six introns. These structural features correlate closely with phylogenetic clustering: most members of group I (11 genes) and group II (5 of 6 genes) had a single intron, indicating structural conservation within subclades. The complex structure of CmPAL14 may result from intron gain or genomic rearrangement events during the evolutionary process. Similar structural divergence has been reported in PAL genes of other plant species, and these differences are usually linked to functional specialization or the lineage-specific regulatory needs [17, 44].

To investigate the functional conservation of CmPAL proteins, a conserved motif analysis via MEME was conducted, identifying 15 motifs across all members (Fig. 4), indicating a structural conservation. This motif consistency reflects strong selection pressure to preserve key structural and functional domains essential for PAL enzyme activity. Several motifs correspond to critical functional regions, including the conserved Ala–Ser–Gly (ASG) tripeptide within the MIO domain, which is indispensable for phenylalanine deamination [45]. The ubiquity of these motifs across all CmPAL proteins confirms their essential role in catalytic function and structural stability, supporting the notion that CmPAL proteins primarily act as gating enzymes in the phenylpropanoid metabolism pathway, with minimal divergence in their catalytic active site structure [27, 46].

Building upon the primary sequence and motif analyses, we predicted the 3D conformations of all 19 CmPAL members using AlphaFold3 to investigate their higher-order structural conservation (Supplementary Fig. S3). The structural models showed that each CmPAL monomer comprises four functionally distinct domains: an N-terminal extension, an MIO catalytic domain, a core domain, and a C-terminal shielding domain, consistent with the canonical architecture of plant PAL enzymes. To detail the structural conservation and variation underlying their functional divergence, three representative members from distinct phylogenetic clades (CmPAL1, CmPAL14, and CmPAL19) were selected for high-resolution comparative analysis (Fig. 5A). Superimposition of these representative 3D structures revealed a highly conserved overall backbone. Detailed visualization of the active center confirmed that the catalytic MIO motif (comprising the Ala-Ser-Gly triad) is highly conserved, with their spatial conformations overlapping in 3D space, ensuring their deamination activity (Fig. 5B, MIO inset). However, distinct amino acid variations were observed in the peripheral residues constituting the substrate-binding pocket surrounding the MIO core (Fig. 5B, Vars inset). These critical structural variations in the binding pocket likely alter the spatial volume and microenvironment of the active site, providing a direct structural basis for the functional divergence among CmPAL subgroups in specialized phenylpropanoid metabolic pathways.

Comparative genomic analysis of PAL genes across diverse species

To investigate the evolutionary relationships and conservation of PAL genes across diverse plant species, comparative genomics analyses were conducted, including phylogenetic reconstruction and genome-wide synteny assessment. A ML phylogenetic tree was constructed using aligned PAL protein sequences from C. morifolium and six other representative species (A. thaliana, Nicotiana tabacum, V. vinifera, Brassica rapa, O. sativa, and Zea mays) (Fig. 6). The tree divided PAL proteins into four major clades, reflecting the evolutionary divergence between monocots and dicots. PALs from (A) thaliana and (B) rapa clustered together, reflecting their close evolutionary relationship within the family Brassicaceae. Similarly, N. tabacum PALs grouped with the majority of (C) morifolium PALs, suggesting a conserved evolutionary lineage between Solanaceae and Asteraceae. Notably, V. vinifera PALs clustered with CmPAL13 and CmPAL19, implying a lineage-specific association. In contrast, the monocots O. sativa and Z. mays formed a distinct clade, separated from the dicots. This phylogenetic structure highlights the monocot-dicot divergence in PAL genes and their long-term conservation within plant lineages, consistent with their essential role in the phenylpropanoid pathway [17, 44].

To complement this phylogenetic analysis, an inter-species genome-wide synteny analysis was performed between C. morifolium and four other species: A. thaliana (dicot, Brassicaceae), O. sativa (monocot, Poaceae), Helianthus annuus (dicot, Asteraceae), and Chrysanthemum nankingense (dicot, Asteraceae, congeneric with C. morifolium). Results showed C. morifolium shared 33,718 syntenic gene pairs with A. thaliana (20.3%), 9,937 with O. sativa (5.9%), 76,352 with H. annuus (39.0%), and 88,880 with C. nankingense (48.7%), confirming a greater number of collinear genes with dicots than with the monocot O. sativa (Fig. 7). The results of a PAL-specific synteny analysis were similar: 9 homologous PAL gene pairs between C. morifolium and A. thaliana, 5 pairs with O. sativa, 22 pairs with H. annuus, and 11 pairs with C. nankingense. These findings reinforce PAL conservation in dicots, particularly within the family Asteraceae, and support the notion that PAL genes retain key roles in secondary metabolism throughout plant evolution [27, 45, 46].

Promoter architecture and transcriptional regulation of CmPAL genes

To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms driving the functional divergence of CmPALs, cis-acting elements in the 2 kb upstream promoter regions of CmPAL genes were analyzed in conjunction with their expression patterns during flower development and under abiotic stresses. PLANTCARE analysis identified 64 known cis-acting elements, categorized into six types: tissue-specific (6), stress-related (14), hormone-responsive (19), light-responsive (22), MYB-binding and MYC-binding elements (Fig. 8A). Except for CmPAL3 and CmPAL10 lacking tissue-specific elements, and CmPAL9 and CmPAL15 lacking MYC-binding motifs, all other CmPAL genes contained all six element types.

To further investigate the mechanistic link between promoter architecture and transcriptional output, we categorized the CmPAL family into two groups based on the presence of specific MYB-binding sites (MBSI and MRE) associated with flavonoid/anthocyanin regulation. Notably, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S4A, genes enriched in these MYB elements exhibited markedly higher expression levels across all floral developmental stages (BS, CES, EBS, FBS) and floret types (DF, RF) compared to those lacking these elements. This distinct correlation suggests that specific MYB transcription factors directly target these cis-elements to drive floral pigmentation.

To clarify CmPALs roles in stress responses, the number and types of hormone- and stress-related elements were quantified and presented in Fig. 8B. Hormone-responsive elements were widely distributed: ABRE (abscisic acid response) appeared 58 times across the gene family, with CmPAL5 (9), CmPAL11 (10), and CmPAL19 (7) containing the most. Salicylic acid-responsive elements were also frequent. Stress-related elements included MBS (related to drought inducibility), WUN-motif and WRE3 (wound response), LTR (low-temperature responsiveness), ARE (anaerobic induction), and TC-rich repeats (general defense and stress responses). Among them, WRE3, ARE, and the W-box element were most abundant, suggesting broad CmPAL involvement in stress responses. In particular, the prevalence of W-box elements implied regulation by WRKY transcription factors, consistent with previous research results in tea plants and poplar trees, linking WRKY-mediated PAL regulation to stress resistance [47, 48]. A grouped quantitative analysis revealed that the absolute number of ABRE elements did not directly correlate with the stress-induced expression amplitude. Under PEG-simulated drought treatment, no significant difference in relative expression values was observed between the ABRE-enriched group and the ABRE-poor group across various time points (Supplementary Fig. S4B), suggesting a complex combinatorial regulatory mechanism in vivo.

Expression profiling and qRT-PCR validation

To further clarify the functional roles of CmPAL genes, expression profiles across different floral developmental stages and abiotic stresses were analyzed using transcriptome data from C. morifolium cv ‘Chuju’ (Fig. 9). CmPAL genes were expressed in all tested floral tissues, with most showing peak expression at the color-emerging stage, supporting a key role for the CmPAL family in pigment biosynthesis and other flower color-related metabolic processes. Cluster analysis grouped CmPAL into two main expression clusters: Cluster I (CmPAL1, 4, 6, 7, 12, 13, and 19) was highly expressed at bud (BS), color emerging (CES), and early blooming stages (EBS) but downregulated at the full blooming stage (FBS); Cluster II showed the opposite trend, with increased expression at the full blooming stage. While most CmPALs were weakly expressed in ray florets (RF), CmPAL5, 9, 10, 11, and 16 were highly expressed in disc florets (DF), suggesting involvement in disc florets-specific development, consistent with tissue-specific PAL expression in walnut flowers [17].

Under abiotic stresses, CmPAL genes exhibited diverse, condition-specific expression patterns. Cold stress induced rapid upregulation of most CmPALs within 3–6 h, consistent with findings in rubber trees [46], implying their potential involvement in cold tolerance. In contrast, cadmium stress downregulated several CmPALs after 12 h, suggesting phenylpropanoid metabolism is suppressed under heavy metal toxicity. Salt stress induced a biphasic response, no significant changes within the first 3 h, followed by widespread downregulation, indicating a delayed negative regulatory mechanism. PEG-induced drought stress initially repressed CmPAL expression, with subsequent induction of several genes, reflecting dynamic transcriptional reprogramming in response to osmotic stress. Collectively, these transcriptome data demonstrate multifaceted CmPAL roles in developmental and stress-responsive pathways, with time- and tissue-specific expression likely coordinated by a combination of hormone signaling, environmental cues, and transcription factor networks.

To validate the spatiotemporal expression patterns of CmPAL genes and address the analytical challenges posed by the high sequence conservation within the CmPAL family, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted. Because the high sequence similarity among CmPAL paralogs makes individual short qPCR primers susceptible to cross-amplification, primers were specifically redesigned to amplify five distinct phylogenetic sub-groups: CmPAL1/6/12, CmPAL2/7/16, CmPAL3/4/8/9/10/14/15/17/18, CmPAL5/11, and CmPAL13/19. The analysis covered four floral developmental stages (BS, CES, EBS, and FBS), two floret types (RF and DF), and three vegetative organs (root, stem, leaf) (Fig. 10).

The qRT-PCR results were highly consistent with the RNA-seq data, confirming the functional divergence of CmPAL sub-groups across different tissues and developmental stages. Notably, the transcript levels of the CmPAL5/11 and CmPAL2/7/16 were significantly higher in disc florets DF than in RF, which corroborates the transcriptome profiles. In vegetative organs, CmPAL1/6/12 and CmPAL13/19 exhibited predominantly high expression in stems, while CmPAL2/7/16 was highly expressed in both stems and leaves. The large sub-group (CmPAL3-18) displayed the highest expression in roots. This vegetative expression implies their essential roles in phenylpropanoid metabolism linked to structural development and defense, such as lignin biosynthesis in vascular tissues.

During flower development, the CmPAL sub-groups displayed distinct dynamic transcriptional shifts. Most groups, including CmPAL1/6/12, CmPAL2/7/16, and the large sub-group, peaked early at the color-emerging stage (CES) and subsequently downregulated as the capitulum matured. In contrast, the CmPAL5/11 group maintained high expression levels through the early and full blooming stages (EBS and FBS). These spatial and temporal divergences in CmPAL expression provide reliable molecular evidence for the functional diversification of the CmPAL family, emphasizing their specialized roles in regulating phenylpropanoid metabolism during chrysanthemum floral development and vegetative growth.

Regulatory network construction and functional divergence of CmPALs

Synthesizing the promoter architecture and expression data, bipartite regulatory networks were constructed to directly address the functional divergence among the 19 CmPAL genes and identify the candidate members responsible for flower pigmentation versus broad-spectrum stress tolerance. These networks integrated the occurrence of specific cis-elements (MRE/MBSI for MYB binding; W-box for WRKY binding) with the transcriptomic expression profiles (Fig. 11).

In the flower-specific regulatory network (Fig. 11A), genes including CmPAL1, 6, 11, and 13 exhibited dense connectivity to MYB transcription factors and displayed the highest relative expression levels in floral tissues. This strong MYB-mediated transcriptional module identifies them as the major candidate genes driving flower coloration. Conversely, under various environmental stress conditions, the regulatory landscape shifted significantly toward a WRKY-dominant module (Fig. 11B). A distinct subset of genes, notably CmPAL3, 9, 10, and 15, displayed WRKY connectivity and significant stress-induced upregulation, marking them as the primary broad-spectrum stress-tolerance genes. Certain members such as CmPAL5 maintained high connectivity and expression across both networks, suggesting a dual role acting as metabolic hubs.

Based on these distinct transcriptional modules, a working model for the functional divergence of the CmPAL family was established (Fig. 11C). The model illustrates how light and developmental signals preferentially activate the MYB-driven CmPAL subset to channel phenylalanine towards the flavonoid/anthocyanin pathway, whereas stress stimuli recruit the WRKY-driven subset to redirect metabolic flux toward lignin and phytoalexin biosynthesis for defense.

Discussions

The species-specific expansion of the CmPAL gene family in C. morifolium, driven primarily by localized tandem duplications [23, 41], reflects an evolutionary adaptation to enhance secondary metabolism, similar expansion patterns observed in other diverse plant lineages [36, 49]. Despite this extensive duplication, CmPAL paralogs have evolved under strong purifying selection to maintain metabolic stability [50]. They consistently retain the conserved structural architecture and catalytic motifs, most notably the ASG triad essential for MIO cofactor formation, which are widely conserved across plant and microbial taxa to ensure baseline deamination activity [2, 51, 52]. This evolutionary trajectoryis further supported by our phylogenetic and syntenic analyses. The distinct clustering of CmPALs with Asterid lineages (e.g., N. tabacum) and their high collinearity with Asteraceae relatives (H. annuus and C. nankingense) confirm a conserved evolutionary lineage [16, 53, 54]. Such deep taxonomic conservation underscores the fundamental function of PAL genes across angiosperms, while their localized duplications provide the genetic raw material for species-specific metabolic adaptations.

To address the genetic mechanisms driving functional divergence, we integrated structural genomic mapping with promoter architecture analysis. Interestingly, CmPAL loci do not co-localize with major reported anthocyanin QTL regions in small-flowered chrysanthemum [55]. This lack of structural co-localization indicates that the differential accumulation of floral pigments is driven by trans-acting transcriptional regulation rather than structural variations at the PAL loci themselves. Promoter analysis identified multiple cis-acting elements associated with hormone responses and stress adaptation, suggesting that CmPAL expression is modulated by upstream regulatory networks [46, 56, 57]. WRKY and MYB transcription factors are well-documented regulators of PAL genes during pathogen attack and abiotic stress [20, 58], highlighting their conserved role in PAL-dependent defense and metabolic pathways. Indeed, our grouped expression analysis revealed that CmPAL members enriched with MYB-binding motifs (MRE and MBSI) display significantly higher transcript abundance in floral tissues, supporting the model that MYB factors directly recruit these specific promoters to activate anthocyanin biosynthesis. Conversely, the non-significant correlation between the absolute number of ABRE elements and PEG-induced expression highlights the complexity of in vivo transcriptional networks. Gene activation under abiotic stress is rarely dictated by a simple additive effect of a single cis-element type; instead, it is likely governed by combinatorial control involving coupling elements, spatial hindrance of clustered motifs, or the competitive binding of transcriptional repressors. The abundance of these cis-elements in CmPAL promoters thus links transcriptional regulation to functional specialization.

Divergent spatiotemporal expression profiles further illustrate the subfunctionalization of the CmPAL family in coordinating complex developmental processes. Most CmPALs are highly expressed at the color-emerging stage, supporting their involvement in pigment biosynthesis via the flavonoid branch of the phenylpropanoid pathway. This temporal expression pattern is evolutionarily conserved [59, 60], ensuring sufficient metabolic flux for flavonoid-derived pigment synthesis during capitulum maturation. Furthermore, the sub-group qRT-PCR validation reveal that major CmPAL sub-groups (e.g., CmPAL5/11 and CmPAL2/7/16) are significantly upregulated in disc florets compared to ray florets. This spatial divergence corresponds to the distinct metabolic requirements of different floral organ types, echoing the tissue-specific PAL subfunctionalization observed in other species [17, 61, 62]. In vegetative organs, the preferential expression of specific sub-groups (such as CmPAL1/6/12 and CmPAL13/19) in stems and roots implies their participation in vascular tissue metabolism, such as lignin biosynthesis, reflecting a functional specialization within the gene family.

Under abiotic stress conditions, the CmPAL family undergoes dynamic transcriptional reprogramming to mediate metabolic resource reallocation during abiotic stress. Cold and drought (PEG) stresses triggered rapid upregulation of select CmPAL genes, a pattern consistent with the early activation of defense signaling [63–65]. This rapid induction suggests that specific CmPALs contribute to tolerance by enhancing phenylpropanoid flux toward the synthesis of lignin for cell wall reinforcement or antioxidants to alleviate oxidative damage. Importantly, this dynamic reprogramming mechanism is consistent with the latest functional breakthroughs in major cereal crops. For instance, recent studies in maize (Zea mays) demonstrated that ZmPAL5 functions as a key positive regulator of drought resistance, directly modulating osmotic substances and antioxidant enzyme activities under water deficit [66]. Similarly, in rice (Oryza sativa), drought tolerance is significantly enhanced when specific transcription factors (such as OsDof12) directly bind to and activate OsPAL4 and OsPAL6, driving the substantial accumulation of phenolic compounds to efficiently scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) [67]. Our findings in chrysanthemum, particularly the specific recruitment of the WRKY-driven CmPAL subset (e.g., CmPAL3, 5, 9, 10, and 15) under stress conditions, are consistent with these recent discoveries. Conversely, the delayed downregulation observed under prolonged cadmium and salt stresses [68, 69], suggests an energy-conservation strategy. Under severe and sustained toxicity, the plant likely suppresses costly secondary metabolism to prioritize basal survival pathways. This biphasic dynamic illustrates a metabolic feedback loop that fine-tunes PAL activity according to stress duration and intensity, balancing resource allocation between growth and defense.

To conceptualize how chrysanthemum allocates metabolic resources between optimal development and environmental adaptation, we constructed a bipartite MYB/WRKY regulatory network that maps the functional division of CmPALs to distinct, stimulus-specific transcriptional modules (Fig. 11). The network analysis pinpointed CmPAL1, 6, 11, and 13 as the major candidate genes for floral pigmentation. These genes are integrated into the MYB regulatory module via MRE and MBSI cis-elements. In diverse angiosperms, MYB transcription factors are recognized as the key regulators of the flavonoid/anthocyanin pathway [70, 71]. The specific enrichment and activation of this CmPAL subset in floral tissues suggest that they are responsible for catalyzing the initial deamination of phenylalanine to funnel carbon flux specifically toward anthocyanin accumulation, thereby defining the ornamental coloration of the capitulum. Conversely, environmental adversity recruits a distinct subset of CmPAL members. The regulatory network identifies CmPAL3, 5, 9, 10, and 15 as the primary broad-spectrum stress-tolerance genes, governed primarily by the WRKY/W-box regulatory module. WRKY transcription factors are classic, rapid responders in plant defense signaling networks. By activating these specific CmPAL paralogs, the plant redirects phenylpropanoid metabolism away from pigmentation and towards the biosynthesis of lignin and phytoalexins. This metabolic reprogramming is essential for reinforcing cell wall integrity and producing antimicrobial/antioxidant compounds to construct a physical and chemical defensive shield. Collectively, the proposed working model (Fig. 11C) provides a mechanistic framework for the C. morifolium PAL family. It reveals that the CmPAL family achieves functional divergence not merely through random genetic redundancy, but via coordinated transcriptional partitioning. This dual-track regulatory mechanism, MYB-driven pigmentation versus WRKY-driven defense, enables the chrysanthemum to flexibly balance its ornamental development with environmental resilience [72].

While this study provides comprehensive genomic, evolutionary, and transcriptional insights into the functional divergence of the CmPAL family, it primarily relies on bioinformatic and transcriptomic evidence. We acknowledge that in planta functions cannot be confirmed without physiological and biochemical validation. Future studies must focus on the in vivo functional characterization of the priority candidate genes identified here (e.g., CmPAL1, 5, and 15). This will require systematic quantification of targeted flavonoid and anthocyanin metabolites, as well as the determination of in vitro/in vivo PAL enzymatic activities, ROS scavenging capacities, and MDA/proline accumulation using stable transgenic chrysanthemum lines under various stress conditions.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive genome-wide characterization of the PAL gene family in C. morifolium, identifying 19 CmPAL members shaped by localized duplications. While all paralogs retain the conserved ASG catalytic triad essential for baseline phenylalanine deamination, variations in their peripheral substrate-binding pockets provide a structural basis for their metabolic subfunctionalization. Our findings reveal that the functional divergence of the CmPAL family is governed by coordinated transcriptional partitioning rather than simple genetic redundancy. Specifically, a MYB-driven regulatory module preferentially activates a distinct subset of genes (e.g., CmPAL1, 6, 11, and 13) to channel metabolic flux toward floral pigmentation, whereas a separate WRKY-driven module recruits stress-responsive members (e.g., CmPAL3, 5, 9, 10, and 15) to redirect resources toward broad-spectrum defense. These systematic insights significantly enrich the Asteraceae PAL genomic resources and provide candidate genes for future functional validation. This dual-track regulatory model provides a foundation for future in vivo functional characterization and molecular breeding strategies aimed at simultaneously optimizing the ornamental quality, flavonoid accumulation, and environmental resilience of chrysanthemum [65, 73].

Methods

Identification and characterization analysis of CmPALs

To identify potential PAL genes in C. morifolium, the hidden Markov model (HMM) profile of the PAL conserved domain (PF00221) was downloaded from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) and used to search the C. morifolium genome database (NCBI Accession No.: PRJNA796762) via HMMER 3.0 with an E-value cutoff of 1 × 10− 5. Additionally, BLASTP was performed against the C. morifolium genome using PAL protein sequences from A. thaliana and rice as queries, with an E-value threshold of 1 × 10− 20 to ensure homology. Candidate sequences were filtered to remove redundancies, and the presence of the conserved PAL domain (IPR005922) was verified using InterProScan (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) [74]. Physicochemical properties of the identified CmPAL proteins, including molecular weight (Mw), isoelectric point (pI), instability index, aliphatic index, and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), were calculated using ExPASy ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). Subcellular localization of CmPALs was predicted using Plant-mPLoc (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/plant-multi/) with default parameters for plant proteins [75].

Phylogenetic analysis of PAL proteins

Full-length amino acid sequences of CmPAL proteins were aligned using ClustalX 2.1 with default parameters [76]. A Maximum Likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA-X (v10.2.6) based on the JTT + G substitution model, which was identified as the best-fit model, with 1000 bootstrap replicates to assess node reliability [77]. To investigate across-species evolutionary relationships, PAL protein sequences were downloaded from public databases: N. tabacum, V. vinifera, O. sativa, and Z. mays sequences from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), (A) thaliana sequences from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org), and (B) rapa sequences from the Brassicaceae Database (http://brassicadb.cn). All amino acid sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. A combined ML tree including CmPALs these cross-species PAL sequences was constructed using MEGA-X with the same parameters. Multiple sequence alignments of CmPAL proteins were visualized using GeneDoc software to highlight conserved motifs.

Chromosome localization, gene structure, conserved motif, 3d structural modeling, and duplication event analysis of CmPALs

Chromosomal locations of CmPAL genes were determined based on genome annotation files, and their distribution was visualized using TBtools (v2.315) [78]. Gene structures (exon-intron organization) were analyzed by comparing coding sequences (CDS) with genomic DNA sequences and visualized in TBtools. Conserved motifs of CmPAL proteins were identified using MEME Suite (http://meme-suite.org/) with parameters: maximum number of motifs = 15, motif width = 6–50 amino acids [79]. Motif distributions were plotted using TBtools. Three-dimensional (3D) structural models of CmPALs were predicted using AlphaFold3 through its official computational pipeline with default parameters [80]. Subsequently, the predicted 3D structures of representative members were visualized, superimposed, and analyzed for spatial variations in their substrate-binding pockets using the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (version 3.2.0a, Schrödinger, LLC) [81]. Gene duplication events (tandem and segmental duplications) were analyzed using MCScanX implemented in TBtools, with BLASTP hits (E-value < 10− 10) as input. Synteny blocks between C. morifolium and four other species (A. thaliana, O. sativa, H. annuus, C. nankingense) were identified using the genome-wide synteny analysis module in TBtools, and collinear relationships were visualized with Circos plots.

Cis-acting element analysis and regulatory network construction

Promoter sequences (2000 bp upstream of the start codon) of CmPAL genes were retrieved from the C. morifolium genome database. Cis-acting elements were predicted using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/), and results were filtered to retain stress-responsive, hormone-responsive, and light-responsive elements [82]. The distribution of these elements was visualized using TBtools. To perform grouped quantitative expression analysis based on promoter architecture, CmPAL genes were categorized according to the presence or abundance of specific cis-elements (e.g., MYB-binding sites and ABREs). The corresponding boxplots comparing relative expression levels across these groups were generated using Python (version 3.11.2) with the matplotlib library. Furthermore, to elucidate the functional divergence of the CmPAL family, bipartite regulatory networks integrating specific cis-elements (MRE/MBSI and W-box) with transcriptomic expression profiles were constructed and visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10.4) [83].

In silico expression analysis of CmPAL genes

Expression profile data were derived from our RNA-seq datasets, which have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under the accession number PRJNA1470079. The sequenced samples encompassed different floral developmental stages (bud stage, color-emerging stage, early blooming stage, full blooming stage) and two floral types (ray florets and disc florets), and abiotic stress treatments (cold: 4 °C; drought: 15% PEG6000; salt: 200 mM NaCl; heavy metal: 100 µM CdCl2). Expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM), with details provided in Supplementary Table 2. For floral developmental stages and floral types analysis, heatmaps of CmPAL expression patterns were generated using TBtools with row-normalized FPKM values. For abiotic stresses analysis, expression patterns were plotted on a log2 scale based on FPKM value of CmPAL genes.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis of CmPAL genes

C. morifolium cv. ‘Chuju’, which is not a protected plant in China, was formally identified by Dr. Cheng according to Flora of China. The original samples were collected from the core production area in Chuzhou City, Anhui Province, China (32.35o N, 118.21o E). The voucher specimens were deposited in Chuzhou University (accession number: Chuju01). The seedlings were cultivated in a climate-controlled greenhouse at Chuzhou University under standardized conditions (25 °C/22 °C day/night temperature cycle, 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod, 60% relative humidity), with regular watering and fertilization management. Tissues of C. morifolium cv ‘Chuju’ were collected, including four floral stages (BS, CES, EBS, FBS), two floret types (DF, RF), and three vegetative organs (root, stem, leaf). Each sample was harvested with three independent biological replicates, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C until use. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the Hifair® II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (with gDNA digester, Yeasen, Shanghai). qRT-PCR reactions were performed using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, Shanghai) on a LightCycler® 480 Real-Time PCR System (Roche, Switzerland), with gene-specific primers for target CmPAL genes and the stably expressed reference gene Actin. Relative expression levels of CmPAL genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to Actin gene. For each gene sub-group, the sample with the lowest expression level was utilized as the internal calibrator to determine the relative fold change. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test.
