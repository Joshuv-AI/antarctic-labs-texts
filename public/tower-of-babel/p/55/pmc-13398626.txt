Audiological Characteristics in Vestibular Schwannoma Patients
Authors: Shan Zeng, Yi‐Yun Lou, Hui‐Qian Yu, Wei‐Dong Zhao, Wen‐Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveVestibular schwannoma (VS) is a benign tumor that originates from the Schwann cells of the vestibular nerve sheath in the internal auditory canal. This retrospective study collected data from patients with VS who underwent surgical treatment to identify their demographic characteristics and audiometric features.MethodsThis retrospective study included 330 VS patients who underwent surgical treatment at the ENT Hospital of Fudan University between April 2017 and December 2019. The demographic information, audiograms, and tumor details of these patients were collected. Demographic and audiometric characteristics were summarized. Correlation analysis was performed to compare the basic characteristics of patients with tumor size, pure‐tone threshold average (PTA), and tumor grades.ResultsAmong the patients, 38.8% (128/330) exhibited predominant high‐frequency hearing loss on pure‐tone audiometry. Approximately 74.5% (246/330) of patients exhibited moderately severe or worse hearing loss. Of the 211 patients who underwent speech testing, 65.4% (138/211) did not have practical hearing function (American Academy of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery Class C&D) at the time of surgery. Correlation analysis revealed that symptom duration was the most influential variable. No correlation was found between tumor size and PTA or speech discrimination score (SDS). Multivariate ordered regression analysis identified age over 45 years, symptom duration over 12 months, symmetric threshold difference greater than 15 dB, and SDS lower than 75% as high‐risk factors for higher Koos grading.ConclusionConsidering the preservation of postoperative hearing, for patients with lower Koos grades (I + II)—especially for younger patients (age below 45 years) with functional hearing—if PTA increases or if SDS decreases or falls below 75% in their follow‐ups, surgery or radiation intervention may be considered after a thorough evaluation, regardless of tumor growth.

Introduction

Acoustic neuroma, also known as vestibular schwannoma (VS), is a benign tumor that originates from the Schwann cells of the vestibular nerve sheath in the internal auditory canal. It constitutes approximately 6%–10% of intracranial tumors and accounts for 80% of tumors in the cerebellopontine angle region [1, 2]. Due to its growth within the internal auditory canal and the cerebellopontine angle region, which is closely related to the cochlear and facial nerves, although the tumor usually grows slowly, it could compress the accompanying nerves and even the brainstem, causing serious symptoms and even life‐threatening situations [2, 3]. Over the past few decades, with advancements in diagnostic technology, particularly the widespread use of MRI examinations, the early diagnosis rate of VS has improved greatly. Moreover, with the development of intraoperative neural function monitoring techniques, the treatment goal of VS has shifted from focusing on low mortality, complication rates, and preservation of facial nerve function to now encompass hearing preservation and enhancement of patients' quality of life [4, 5].

The existing literature on VS focuses primarily on various aspects such as the characterization of hearing loss, indications for MRI examinations, and outcomes of radiation therapy or surgeries via approaches [6, 7]. Extensive studies have also investigated factors associated with postoperative hearing preservation. The rate of postoperative hearing preservation ranges from 55% to 93%, with a long‐term hearing preservation rate ranging from 36% to 72%, depending on variables including the surgical approach, tumor size, and pre‐surgery PTA, among others [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13].

This article retrospectively collected data from patients with VS who underwent surgical treatment in the ENT Hospital of Fudan University between 2017 and 2019. The primary objective was to identify the characteristics of these patients to provide valuable information and guidance for the early diagnosis of VS, as well as to provide clues to determine the appropriate surgical time during the monitoring process.

Methods

Patients and Data Collection

This was a retrospective study including 330 patients diagnosed with VS who underwent surgery. Patients with neurofibromatosis Type 2 (NF2) and other cerebellopontine angle tumors such as facial neuroma or meningioma were excluded from the study. All patients received a comprehensive preoperative clinical evaluation, which included the following evaluations: PTA, auditory brainstem response (ABR), multiple auditory steady‐state evoked responses (ASSR), vestibular function, facial nerve electroneurography, enhanced MRI, etc. In addition to these evaluations, 211 of them accepted speech discrimination testing.

The following information was collected for each patient: (1) demographic details, including sex, age, and duration of symptoms at the time of operation; (2) presentation of symptoms and therapy history, including information regarding the symptoms associated with hearing impairment and the patient's previous therapies; (3) audiological examination data, which included (a) PTA, (b) speech recognition threshold (SRT) and speech discrimination score (SDS), and (4) details on the available imaging technique (CT or MRI), including the side, site, and size of the lesion (maximum diameter).

Pure‐Tone Audiometry

All patients underwent pure‐tone audiometry at the frequencies 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000, and 8000 Hz. Patients were examined by air and bone conduction, with the lowest thresholds recorded. The PTA was calculated from the means of the hearing thresholds at 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz. In those cases where no tone was heard up to the maximum output of 120 dB, the threshold was set at 120 dB for calculation purposes. The hearing threshold curve of each patient was determined to be one of the nine types according to the following criteria [14, 15, 16]: (1) descending: the mean threshold of 250–500, 100–2000, and 4000–8000 Hz gradually increases, with a difference of > 20 dB HL between 8000 and 250 Hz. (2) Precipitously falling: flat or gradually sloping, then threshold increasing at 25 dB or more per octave. (3) Ascending: the mean threshold of 250–500, 100–2000, and 4000–8000 Hz gradually decreases, with a difference of > 20 dB HL between 8000 and 250 Hz. (4) Flat: a difference of < 20 dB HL between the mean threshold of 250–500, 100–2000, and 4000–8000 Hz. (5) Profound deafness: a loss of hearing of at least 90 dB HL at every frequency from 250 to 8000 Hz. (6) Concave: elevated thresholds throughout the mid‐frequencies (500–4000 Hz) and more than a 20‐dB HL difference between the lowest and highest hearing thresholds. (7) Convex: at least 1 frequency between 500 and 4000 Hz that is 20‐dB HL better than 250 and 8000 Hz. (8) Others: audiogram patterns that do not meet the above classification criteria. (9) Normal hearing. The PTA is classified into 0–6 grades according to the WHO hearing loss grades [17]. Pure‐tone audiometry was conducted by certified audiologists using a clinical audiometer (Madsen Astera, GN Otometrics, Germany) with supra‐aural headphones (HDA 300, Sennheiser Electronic GmbH & Co. KG, Germany). All equipment was calibrated quarterly in accordance with the Chinese national metrological specification JJG 388‐2012 (technically equivalent to IEC 60645‐1:2010).

SRT and SDS Testing

In a quiet environment, the speaker is placed at the same height as the patient's head and 1 m away from the patient's head. The speaker (sound source) faced the patient directly at 0°, the assisted listening ear at 90°, and the unassisted listening ear at 270°. Standard short sentences were played in these three directions using the Angel Language Training Chinese Open Speech Assessment System (V5.04.01). The lowest decibel level at which the patient could recognize 50% of the short sentences was recorded as their SRT. Furthermore, the sound intensity required for the patient to achieve the maximum SDS was also recorded [18, 19].

Tumor Grades

All patients included in this study had available CT or MRI data. Radiologists confirmed the location and size of the tumor, measuring maximum values of the cross‐sectional area in the transverse, sagittal, and coronal planes to determine its size. The largest diameter was used for classification purposes. Tumors were classified according to Koos Grades [20]: Grade I tumors located entirely within the internal auditory canal (Figure 1A); Grade II tumors that extend beyond the internal auditory canal with a maximum diameter less than 2 cm (Figure 1B); Grade III tumors that occupy the cerebellopontine angle without brainstem displacement and a maximum diameter less than 3 cm (Figure 1C); Grade IV tumors compressing the brainstem causing displacement and a size larger than 3 cm (Figure 1D).

For patients with complete SDS data, they were evaluated using the American Academy of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery (AAO‐HNS) classification [21]: Class A for PTA ≤ 30 dB and SDS ≥ 70%; Class B for PTA > 30 dB ≤ 50 dB and SDS ≥ 50%; Class C for PTA > 50 dB and SDS ≥ 50%; Class D for SDS < 50%.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

The study population consisted of 330 patients diagnosed with VS, including 138 men and 192 women. Among them, there were 171 cases on the left side and 159 cases on the right side. The male‐to‐female ratio was 1:1.39, and the left‐to‐right ear ratio was 1:0.93. The median age at the time of surgery was 49 years, and the median duration of symptoms before surgery was 12 months (Table 1). All surgical procedures were performed by Dr. Wei‐Dong Zhao.

Audiometric Characteristics

Before surgery, only 5 patients (1.5%) had normal hearing according to WHO grades, 1 of which had a precipitous fall at 8000 Hz. In total, 74.5% (246/330) had hearing loss at the level of severely moderate or worse (severely moderate: 20.9%; severe: 20.6%; profound: 10.6%; and deafness: 22.4%). Approximately 38.8% (128/330) of the audiograms showed high‐frequency hearing loss (32.1% descending curve, 6.7% precipitously falling). Flat‐type hearing loss was observed in 23.3% (77/330) of the audiograms of the patients, while profound deafness was present in 20.0% (66/330). Koos Grade II accounted for 45.8% (151/330) of all patients, while Grade IV accounted for 27.9% (92/330). When considering the AAO‐HNS classification, 65.4% (138/220) of all patients opted for surgical therapy when they had no functional hearing (Classes C and D).

Correlation Analysis of Tumor Classification

Ordinary one‐way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test, with Koos grade as the grouping factor, identified statistically significant differences in both duration of symptoms and history of sudden deafness among the groups (Table 1). A correlation analysis was performed that compared the basic characteristics of patients with tumor size, PTA, and tumor grades (Table 2). The results revealed a weak statistically significant correlation between age, duration of symptoms, PTA, audiogram types, sudden deafness history, and tumor size. Furthermore, there was a statistically significant correlation between the duration of symptoms, tumor size, audiogram types, and PTA. Similarly, a statistically significant correlation was observed between the duration of symptoms, sudden deafness history, and the tumor grades of the patient.

Logistic Regression Analysis of Tumor Classification

Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify variables that require careful consideration during the subsequent monitoring of VS. To examine factors associated with higher Koos grade, Koos grade was dichotomized (low grades [I–II] vs. high grades [III–IV]) and analyzed using ordinal logistic regression. The results indicated that several factors were related to a higher tumor stage, including age over 45 years, duration of symptoms that exceeded 12 months, a history of sudden hearing loss, and a profound hearing loss audiogram. These findings were consistent in multivariate analyses using ordered logistic regression for Koos grades Ⅰ to Ⅳ. In addition, an elevation of PTA greater than 15 dB was also found to be associated with a higher tumor staging (Table 5).

In conclusion, during the follow‐up of VS, age greater than 45 years, duration of symptoms greater than 12 months, elevation of PTA greater than 15 dB, SDS less than 75%, and history of sudden hearing loss are all considered risk factors for higher grades.

Discussion

VS is a slow‐growing benign tumor that develops on the vestibular cochlear nerve at the cerebellopontine angle. Although benign, even small VSs could cause hearing and balance‐related symptoms due to their location. With the advancement of imaging technology, particularly the widespread use of MRI scans, VSs are increasingly diagnosed at an early stage. Although the literature reported a significant correlation between asymmetrical hearing loss of more than 15 dB at 3000 Hz and the occurrence of VS [22, 23, 24, 25], our retrospective study cannot provide a correlation between the elevation of the 3 kHz threshold and the level of VS since we do not routinely test the 3000 Hz threshold in our clinic. However, whether it is a multifactor ordered regression analysis of the Koos classification system or a conventional multifactor regression analysis of the AAO‐HNS classification, an asymmetrical PTA elevation exceeding 15 dB is considered a risk factor for higher grade VS (both p < 0.01, Tables 5 and 6). Furthermore, our results showed that several other variables were risk factors for VS, including age over 45 years, duration of symptoms longer than 12 months, and history of sudden hearing loss. It is strongly recommended for a further MRI examination when clinicians encounter asymmetric hearing loss patients with a PTA elevation over 15 dB, especially those who also meet these risk factors.

Taking into account the risks associated with intracranial surgery and the proximity of the tumor to the brainstem, many patients with VS historically opt for surveillance rather than immediate surgical intervention. Surgical treatment is usually pursued only when the tumor grows rapidly or when symptoms such as hearing loss or balance problems worsen to a significant extent. Analyzing data from patients with VS who underwent surgery in our hospital between 2017 and 2019, we found that 74.55% of patients had moderate‐to‐severe hearing loss at the time of surgery. Among the patients with available SDS data, 65.40% of them had no functional hearing when they underwent surgery. The precise mechanisms underlying hearing loss in patients with VS remain incompletely understood. Current evidence suggests that auditory deterioration may arise from multiple interrelated pathophysiological factors, including mechanical compression of the cochlear nerve or auditory structures [26, 27], vascular insufficiency due to tumor‐induced compromise of the labyrinthine artery or microcirculation [2, 28, 29, 30], disruption of cochlear homeostasis (e.g., endolymphatic hydrops or ionic imbalance) [31, 32, 33, 34], bioelectrical dysfunction of the cochlear nerve via demyelination or aberrant neural signaling [35, 36, 37], tumor‐directed inflammatory and immune‐mediated responses (e.g., TNF‐α) [34, 38, 39]. Notably, tumors originating from the superior vestibular nerve exhibit a higher postoperative hearing preservation rate compared to those arising from the inferior vestibular nerve. This discrepancy likely stems from the superior nerve's more favorable anatomical relationship with the cochlear nerve and relative ease of surgical separation. Additionally, mid‐to‐high‐frequency hearing loss tends to occur earlier and more frequently in VS patients than low‐frequency hearing loss, which may be explained by the more central location of low‐frequency neural fibers within the cochlear nerve [40]. These findings underscore the critical role of tumor compression and surgical traction in hearing preservation. Furthermore, numerous studies suggested VS‐associated auditory decline is more strongly linked to chronicity of symptoms than to tumor dimensions [41, 42, 43, 44]. Alexander L. Luryi et al. reported that VS more than or equal to 5 mm is associated with continued hearing loss even without tumor growth after follow‐up of 105 VS patients for 3 years [42]. Although this study was limited to surgically treated patients, our observations align with existing evidence, demonstrating a significant association between hearing loss severity and symptom duration. The correlation analysis indicates that tumor size was weakly correlated with the patient's PTA but not correlated with both SDS and AAHONS classification Tables 2 and 3). Therefore, for patients with VS greater than 5 mm, as the disease progresses, the tumor size, pure‐tone audiometry [25], SDS, and tumor grade all undergo changes. The impact of the tumor on hearing is not directly proportional to its size; rather, tumor size is a critical influencing factor for postoperative hearing preservation [12]. Bioelectrical dysfunction of the cochlear nerve and tumor‐directed inflammatory and immune‐mediated responses may play a more important role in VS‐related hearing loss. Thus, the trend of hearing deterioration and symptom duration during watchful waiting must also be factored into surgical timing decisions along with tumor location and growth rate, especially in patients with small VSs and serviceable hearing.

It has been more than 120 years since Dr. House first reported successful preservation of hearing in a case of VS surgery using the middle cranial fossa (MCF) approach [45]. Otologists have been exploring hearing preservation techniques in the surgical management of VS since the 1970s [46]. Recent studies have shed light on various factors that influence hearing preservation following different treatment modalities for VS since the beginning of the 20th century. Postoperative hearing preservation varied. Factors such as tumor size, filling of cerebrospinal fluid in the internal auditory canal, tumor origin, and intraoperative monitoring of ABR all influence postoperative hearing preservation [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Compared to radiation therapy, although the immediate postoperative hearing preservation rate is comparable to that of surgery, long‐term efficacy showed a higher retention rate of functional hearing in patients who have undergone successful surgery [6]. Although this retrospective study focused on preoperative audiological characteristics and tumor classification in patients with VS, without our own follow‐up results on postoperative hearing outcomes, there have been numerous reports on short‐ and long‐term observations of hearing preservation after surgical treatment for patients with VS. Surgical treatment using the MCF or retrosigmoid (RS) approach is a better choice to preserve hearing in cases of VS, and the MCF approach led to better long‐term outcomes of hearing preservation [47]. The retrolabyrinthine approach with preservation of the endolymphatic sac was also reported to be a promising route for hearing preservation and facial nerve functions [48]. In addition to the duration of the symptoms, age also exhibited a significant correlation with the AAHONS class in this study. Oghalai et al. also reported that age was an independent factor affecting postoperative hearing preservation in VS cases [49].

Therefore, for patients with lower Koos grades (I + II), especially younger patients (age below 45) with functional hearing (AAHONS A and B), during their follow‐up, regardless of tumor growth, if there is an upward trend in PTA or if SDS decreases or falls below 75%, surgical or radiation intervention should be fully considered after thorough evaluation, especially for patients with a higher predicted hearing preservation rate.

While our findings provide valuable insights into the audiological characteristics of VS patients before surgical intervention, several important limitations must be acknowledged.

First, this study retrospectively analyzed preoperative clinical data from 330 VS patients who underwent surgical resection at a single tertiary referral center (Eye & ENT Hospital, Fudan University). The single‐center, retrospective design inherently carries risks of selection bias and may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the study cohort exclusively comprised surgically treated patients, excluding those managed conservatively or with radiotherapy. Consequently, our results may not be representative of the broader VS population, particularly patients with smaller tumors or those who are not surgical candidates. Third, while existing literature demonstrates significant variability in postoperative hearing outcomes depending on surgical approach and tumor size, the present study focused solely on preoperative audiological characteristics [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Our aim was to identify potential indicators that might help determine the optimal timing for surgical intervention during patient monitoring. However, the prognostic value of these preoperative profiles for postoperative hearing preservation requires further validation.

Despite these limitations, our study contributes to the growing body of literature on VS‐related hearing loss. The detailed preoperative hearing profiles we describe may assist clinicians in patient counseling and surgical planning. Future prospective, multi‐institutional studies incorporating both surgical and non‐surgical patient data are needed to validate our findings. We strongly advocate for comprehensive longitudinal studies that integrate both pre‐ and postoperative audiometric evaluations to develop more accurate predictive models and optimize treatment strategies for VS patients.

Conclusions

This retrospective study revealed that the duration of hearing‐related symptoms is the most significant factor affecting tumor size, tumor grades, and PTA. There was no significant correlation between tumor size and tumor grades or PTA. Considering the postoperative preservation of practical hearing, for VS with a diameter greater than 5 mm, during follow‐up, regardless of tumor growth, if patients exhibit an increasing trend in PTA or a decreasing trend in SDS, even slipping below 75%, surgery or radiation intervention should be thoroughly evaluated as options.
