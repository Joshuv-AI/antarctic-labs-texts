Breaking the Size Barrier: Endoscopic Submucosal Dissection of a Massive Flat Gastric Neuroendocrine Tumor
Authors: Gennaro Tagliamonte, Mariangela Giunta, Davide Ravizza, Eleonora Pisa, Giuseppe De Roberto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTType I gastric neuroendocrine tumors, arising in chronic atrophic gastritis, are usually small, mucosal, or submucosal elevated lesions. Current guidelines recommend endoscopic resection of lesions ≤20 mm and surgical treatment for those >20 mm. We report an unusual case of a 56-year-old woman with a completely flat (Paris 0-IIb) gastric body neuroendocrine tumor (NET) measuring 140 × 35 mm, detected by virtual chromoendoscopy and successfully resected by endoscopic submucosal dissection without complications. Histology revealed about 40 well-differentiated low-grade NET foci. After 6 months, no recurrence occurred, confirming endoscopic submucosal dissections curative potential in this exceptional giant but flat NET case.

INTRODUCTION

Type I gastric neuroendocrine tumors (gNETs) account for 0.3%–1% of all gastric neoplasms, although their incidence and prevalence have markedly increased over the past decades.1,2 Type I gNETs arise in the setting of chronic atrophic gastritis under continuous hypergastrinemic stimulation. They are typically very indolent neoplasms with a negligible risk of metastasis.3–6

They usually present as multiple, small, mucosal—or more rarely mucosal–submucosal—yellowish lesions with an ectatic vascular pattern. Since most of these lesions are small (below or around 1 cm), confined to the mucosa or superficial submucosa, and without distant spread, the mainstay of treatment is endoscopic resection. Lesions smaller than 5 mm are often removed using cold forceps or cold snare techniques,7,8 while larger lesions approaching 1 cm require more advanced resection methods such as conventional endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR), modified EMR, endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), or full-thickness resection.9,10

Currently, no randomized controlled trials have compared the different endoscopic resection techniques to treat gNETs. In a recent review including 189 type I gNETs, no significant differences were observed between EMR and ESD in terms of en-bloc resection rates (97.4% vs 98.7%) or histologically complete (R0) resection (92.3% vs 96.3%), with similar local recurrence rates for both techniques. This may be related to the small median lesion size (<10 mm) in both groups.11

For larger type I gNETs, although no clear size cutoff for surgical indication is well defined due to the rarity of lymph node metastases, data from a large Taiwanese Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program database reported an increased risk of nodal involvement for lesions >2 cm.12,13 Accordingly, current European Neuroendocrine Tumor Society guidelines recommend considering surgical treatment for lesions >2 cm or of high grade.14

We report a case of a type I gNET with an extremely unusual endoscopic appearance—remarkable both for its large size and completely flat morphology—successfully treated with ESD. This case highlights a very rare presentation of gNETs that may nonetheless remain amenable to curative treatment through advanced endoscopic resection.

CASE REPORT

A 56-year-old woman with chronic atrophic autoimmune gastritis underwent surveillance gastroscopy at our center. Virtual chromoendoscopy revealed a subtle, flat (Paris 0-IIb) erythematous lesion with preserved glandular pattern and prominent, irregular “corkscrew-like” vessels extending from the proximal to the distal gastric body, with a maximal diameter of approximately 100 mm. Although the lesion was completely flat and morphologically inconspicuous, its distinct vascular pattern compared with the surrounding mucosa allowed for precise delineation of its margins (Figure 1).

Endoscopic view showing an irregular, corkscrew-like vascular pattern of the gastric lesion.

In this patient, 2 small 2-mm NETs had been removed during the previous year, whereas the larger lesion had not been recognized at that time. A missed diagnosis is possible, likely related to its completely flat morphology; indeed, upon retrospective review of the images from the first endoscopy, the lesion appears to have already been present. Thus, this case most likely represents 3 synchronous lesions, with the exceptional feature being attributable only to the largest lesion, not only because of its size, but also especially because of its highly unusual flat morphology, which likely contributed to it not being diagnosed during the initial endoscopic examination.

Targeted biopsies obtained from 4 different areas of the lesion all showed low-grade gNET (Ki-67 = 1%). The patient underwent complete oncologic staging. Somatostatin receptor PET/CT with 68Ga-DOTA-peptides and contrast-enhanced CT excluded local and distant metastases. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) demonstrated thickening confined to the mucosal layer.

Given the lesion's large size, the case was discussed at our multidisciplinary neuroendocrine tumor board. Despite its extent, the negative oncological staging and superficial involvement confirmed by EUS supported an endoscopic approach, and ESD was planned.

The tunnel ESD technique was used. The procedure was technically demanding due to several critical steps. The first challenging step was the presence of poorly defined lesion borders; therefore, the margins were carefully marked before initiating the mucosal incision. Another challenging step consisted of maintaining a constant and correct cutting orientation during the mucosal incision of the margins and preserving proper orientation within the submucosal tunnel, given the different anatomical planes along which the lesion extended, as it involved both the proximal and distal gastric body. No traction technique was used. The endoscopist preferred to carefully follow the dissection plane, adapting it to the changes in the anatomical planes involved by the extension of the lesion. Endocut mode was used for the mucosal incision, while Swift Coagulation or Forced Coagulation mode was used during the submucosal dissection. Finally, the last challenging steps included the management of active bleeding occurring during submucosal dissection and the application of prophylactic hemostasis with bipolar forceps, necessitated by the presence of several large-caliber vessels within the submucosal layer (see all phases of the procedure in the attached Video 1).

En-bloc resection was successfully achieved (Figure 2), yielding a 140 × 35-mm specimen (Figure 3).

Resection bed of the lesion after endoscopic submucosal dissection.

Resected specimen after endoscopic submucosal dissection, mounted for margin assessment.

Given the large extent of the lesion, closure of the postresection defect was not considered technically feasible or likely to be effective. Alternatively, the resection bed was carefully inspected to identify and prophylactically coagulate visible vessels, and a hemostatic gel was applied with the aim of reducing the risk of delayed postprocedural bleeding.

The procedure lasted 6 hours and 27 minutes.

Histopathologic examination confirmed a well-differentiated, G1 multifocal NET with negative lateral and vertical margins and no vascular invasion. Remarkably, serial sectioning revealed more than 40 microfoci of neuroendocrine tumors ranging from 0.5 to 5 mm (Figure 4).

Histopathologic section with Chromogranin A immunostain (2.5×), confirming multifocal neuroendocrine proliferation.

Postoperative recovery was uneventful. The patient was discharged 5 days after the procedure, having resumed a soft diet on postoperative day 2 and gradually returning to a regular diet. Follow-up endoscopies at 2 and 6 months revealed a mildly retracted scar (Figure 5) and no clinically relevant symptoms.

Mucosal scar observed at the 6-month endoscopic follow-up with no evidence of residual or recurrent lesion.

The patient has provided informed consent for publication of this case report.

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported description of a gNET presenting as a large, completely flat lesion. Type I gNETs typically present as multiple small mucosal lesions and, less commonly, as submucosal nodules, often with a central yellowish discoloration.15 Mucosal lesions usually appear as sessile, easily recognizable elevations with an ectatic or serpiginous vascular pattern suggestive of neuroendocrine differentiation. Submucosal lesions may be less morphologically evident but generally produce a visible bulging or exhibit a mixed mucosal–submucosal component, facilitating endoscopic detection.

In the present case, the lesion appeared completely flat (Paris 0-IIb), without any evidence of subepithelial elevation. The only abnormal finding was an altered microvascular pattern, in the absence of any microsurface abnormality. Virtual chromoendoscopy proved particularly useful for lesion detection, revealing a fine superficial ectatic vascular pattern homogeneously distributed across the lesion, with a demarcation line from the surrounding mucosa, which lacked similar microvascular features.

Because of the lesion's unusual morphology and large size, the case was extensively discussed first within the endoscopy team and subsequently in a multidisciplinary setting involving surgeons and oncologists within the NET tumor board. The key point of the endoscopic discussion was that, despite the suspected superficial involvement, the lesion appeared macroscopically as a single large lesion, characterized by a homogeneous vascular pattern throughout its entire extension. In addition, biopsies obtained from multiple sites within the lesion consistently confirmed the diagnosis of NET. Considering the lesion size and its atypical endoscopic appearance, we believed that en-bloc resection by ESD was the only approach that could ensure accurate and complete histopathological staging while also providing a potentially curative treatment. An alternative endoscopic strategy would have been piecemeal resection, potentially associated with lower procedural risk and shorter procedure time. However, preoperatively, we did not anticipate the presence of multiple distinct NET foci; therefore, given the patient's young age, excellent general condition, and the availability of advanced ESD expertise at our center, en-bloc resection was considered the most appropriate strategy for both staging and treatment. Surgical resection was also considered during multidisciplinary discussion. In gNETs, lesion size is one of the main prognostic factors associated with lymph node metastasis and correlates with deeper submucosal invasion.5,16 Accordingly, management is largely size-based, and current European Neuroendocrine Tumor Society guidelines recommend surgery for lesions larger than 20 mm, although this recommendation is supported by limited evidence.

In our patient, however, negative cross-sectional imaging together with lesion morphology and EUS findings strongly suggested disease confined to the mucosal layer. Therefore, despite the lesion's large size, the estimated risk of nodal involvement was considered low. Based on these findings, a less invasive approach than surgery was proposed, and endoscopic resection by ESD was performed. Definitive histology confirmed complete confinement to the mucosa, with no lymphovascular invasion.

Overall, ESD achieved curative resection of this giant NET lesion, sparing the patient surgery and its potential complications. This case underscores the importance of individualized treatment strategies—even for lesions larger than 20 mm—and highlights the value of multidisciplinary decision-making that carefully balances patient characteristics, lesion morphology, and the estimated risk of lymph node metastasis when considering advanced endoscopic resection vs surgery.

In our experience of more than 20 years managing patients with type I gastric NETs, this was the first lesion with such an unusual morphology. Given its uniqueness and the absence of similar descriptions in the available literature, it remains unclear whether this presentation represents a distinct morphological subtype of type I gNET. Further cases will be needed to determine whether this represents a distinct morphological subtype; in the meantime, we hope this report increases awareness among endoscopists of this unusual presentation of gastric NET, which may otherwise be overlooked.
