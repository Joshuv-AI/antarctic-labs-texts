Resist to persist: resistance of the HIV reservoir to immune-mediated clearance
Authors: Thuong DT Nguyen, Cristina Peligero-Cruz, Beatriz Mothe
Published: 2025

INTRODUCTION

Widespread implementation of modern antiretroviral therapy (ART) has revolutionized the HIV field, leading to remarkable improvements in the clinical outcomes of people with HIV (PWH). Despite this, ART is ineffective at eliminating the HIV reservoirs, primarily consisting of CD4+ T cells harbouring integrated intact proviruses that can rapidly refuel viraemia once treatment is interrupted (ATI) [1,2]. Such viral reservoirs appear to be established early during acute infection as well as close to ART initiation [3–5]. After ART initiation, HIV proviruses decay [6,7]; however, homeostatic proliferation of some infected cells [8,9] results in a highly stable reservoir that persists for a lifetime in most PWH despite suppressive treatment [10].

To achieve either a functional cure (defined as durable ART-free remission) or a complete eradication of HIV, a range of immunotherapeutic strategies are being developed aimed at eliminating or significantly reducing the viral reservoir while simultaneously enhancing antiviral immune responses to prevent viral rebound following ART interruption (ATI) (reviewed in [11]). Among these, therapeutic vaccines that leverage the antiviral function of HIV-specific CD8+ T cells have shown encouraging advances over the last years. Inspired by studies in elite controllers and the recognized role of CD8+ T cells in mediating partial viral control during acute infection [12], novel therapeutic vaccines have demonstrated improved viral control following ATI in early-treated PWH [13]; yet with insufficient viral suppression capacity to match the clinical efficacy profile of ART [14]. In addition, while potent, these vaccine-induced responses have shown limited impact on the HIV reservoir [13,15▪]. This underscores the fundamental challenge posed by viral latency, which significantly impairs the ability of even potent CD8+ T cells to eliminate infected cells under suppressive ART [16].

While latency serves as the most important means for reservoir-harbouring cells to circumvent immune clearance and has thus fuelled the search for safe latency-reversing agents [17], a growing body of evidence demonstrates that not all HIV proviruses within the reservoir are strictly latent. It has been shown that a substantial fraction of the HIV reservoir expressing viral transcripts could be detected in PWH after decades of durable treatment [18–21], which may translate to protein production [22] and be the main source of rebound viraemia during treatment interruption [23]. In addition, associations between residual viral expression and readily detectable T-cell responses further indicate ongoing recognition of infected cells by CD8+ T cells [24,25]. With these findings, a critical question arises: Why are these ‘active’ reservoir cells not cleared by immune surveillance but instead, persist for years? In light of this, it has been suggested that a subset of these reservoir cells might evolve to become resistant to CD8+ T lymphocyte (CTL)-mediated elimination [21,26,27], thus representing a novel persistence mechanism. In this review, we aim to give an updated overview of this phenomenon and discuss potential therapeutic strategies to overcome reservoir's resistance to immune-mediated clearance for an HIV cure.

CYTOTOXIC T LYMPHOCYTE-RESISTANCE

Resistance to CTL-mediated clearance of the HIV-1 reservoir parallels the immunoediting process observed in some cancer types to evade immunosurveillance. This phenomenon was first experimentally described by Jones [28] using a sequential killing assay model with infected CD4+ T cells and T-cell clones specific for two nonescaped HIV epitopes presented on the target cells. In this model, a small fraction of Gag-expressing CD4+ T cells could still persist after 2 rounds of CTL exposure. Several studies have suggested that resistance to CTL-mediated killing of HIV-infected cells may be driven by several factors intrinsic to both the virus and the host cells that can culminate in reduced antigen presentation, enhanced survival capacity and/or direct antagonism of cytotoxicity.

Decreased antigen presentation

Although not usually strictly referred to as a CTL-resistance mechanism, interference with antigen presentation through downregulation of the histocompatibility class I (MHC-I) molecules is a common immune evasion mechanism employed by many pathogenic viruses including HIV-1. The role of Nef in downregulating HLA-A and HLA-B expression has been well documented across different studies [29–31], as well as Vpu that downregulates HLA-C [32,33], which consequently alters the sensitivity of HIV-infected cells to CTL-mediated clearance. Apart from Nef and Vpu, the host cell protein EZH-2, a histone methyltransferase, has also been recently suggested to downregulate MHC-I molecules and to contribute to CTL-killing survival, as it is found to be highly expressed in Gag-expressing CD4+ T cells surviving from CTL clone exposure [34▪▪].

Enhanced survival capacity

There is substantial evidence from recent single-cell multiomic studies showing that, compared to uninfected cells, HIV reservoir cells downregulate death-related pathways [35], and that HIV-1 proviruses tend to preferentially persist in cells overexpressing pro-survival factors such as the antiapoptotic protein BCL-2 [26,36]. It is well known that several viral accessory proteins such as Nef, Tat and Vpr may contribute to suppressing cell death by counteracting the activity of the pro-apoptotic protein BAD through phosphorylation [37], desensitising infected cells to FAS/TRAIL/TNFα-mediated apoptosis [38], or upregulating the expression of the pro-survival factors BCL-2 [38,39] and SURVIVIN [40]. In addition, proviral integration in genes associated with cell survival and cell growth such as MKL2 and BACH-2 [41] may also have the potential to influence the cell's ability to resist CTL-mediated killing, albeit such possibility remains to be experimentally validated. Alternatively, rather than HIV-1-driven, resistance to CTL cytotoxicity might also be dictated by preexisting factors inherent to host cells regardless of infection, which paradoxically become an advantage for viral persistence. This argument is well supported by the knowledge that different CD4 T-cell subsets naturally possess heterogeneous levels of susceptibility to CTL-mediated killing, with transitional memory CD4+ T cells being more susceptible while naïve, transitionally differentiated, and central memory cells being more resilient [42,43]. Indeed, studies have shown that the majority of the persistent HIV-1 reservoir in PWH under long-term ART reside in central memory CD4+ T cells, together with a smaller fraction in the naïve subset [8], which, interestingly, also express high levels of BCL-2 [44–46]. The role of BCL-2 in conferring a superior survival advantage of HIV-infected cells has been demonstrated across multiple studies [26,47–49]. Similarly, HIV-infected cells have also been found to express high levels of BIRC-5 (SURVIVIN) and its upstream regulator OX40 to act in counteracting cytotoxic pressures [50,51].

Direct antagonism of cytotoxicity

In addition to pro-survival factors, HIV-1 may persist by directly subverting the cytolytic functions of CD8+ T cells. Latent HIV-1 proviruses have also been shown to be enriched in CD4+ T cells exhibiting a cytotoxic phenotype [36,52▪▪]. These cells, as part of their intrinsic self-protection machinery, naturally express elevated levels of endogenous inhibitors of cytolytic effector molecules such as Serpin B9, which counteracts Granzyme B, and Cathepsin, which restricts Perforin [53]. Thus, by residing in these ‘cytotoxic-like’ CD4+ T cells ‘armed’ with anticytotoxicity mechanisms, HIV-1-infected cells may gain the advantage to become intrinsically less susceptible to CD8+ T cell antiviral functions. Taken together, these findings indicate that beyond escaping immune detection or resisting apoptotic signals, HIV-infected cells may exploit direct molecular mechanisms to antagonize CTL effector function at the point of cytolysis, constituting a third and distinct layer of immune evasion.

Additional mechanisms

Further findings from various studies also support the existence of additional CTL-resistance mechanisms in some HIV-1 reservoir cells. One such involves the downregulation of the adhesion molecule ICAM-1 [54▪▪], which leads to unstable engagement with CTLs during cell-cell interactions. Another contributing factor is the presence of a fluid-like cell membrane [54▪▪], which could complicate effective perforin pore formation. Additionally, similar to what has been observed in some cancers, HIV-1 reservoir cells resisting CTL clearance displayed a quiescent metabolic profile with reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [52▪▪], which is desirable in inhibiting ROS-induced apoptosis. Alternatively, increased expression of the poliovirus receptor (PVR/CD155) on infected cells [27] might enable interaction with the exhaustion marker TIGIT on effector cells, thereby dampening immune responses. Lastly, killing efficiency might also be affected by a compromised type I interferon (IFN) signalling essential for amplifying CTL responses, which has been seen to be mediated by the HIV-1 proteins Vif [55,56] and Vpr [55,57].

PROPOSED INTERVENTIONS TO REVERSE CYTOTOXIC T LYMPHOCYTE-RESISTANCE

The hypothesis that CTL resistance may contribute to HIV-1 persistence has prompted ongoing efforts to develop strategies aimed at sensitizing reservoir cells to CTL-mediated clearance. Among these, the EZH-2 inhibitor tazemetostat, an FDA-approved agent for relapsed or refractory follicular lymphoma, has emerged as a promising candidate for repurposing in HIV-1 cure research. Preclinical studies, both in vitro and in a humanized mouse model, have shown its efficacy in restoring MHC-I expression, counteracting Nef activity, and enhancing CTL-mediated elimination of reservoir cells [34▪▪].

Of the therapeutic strategies currently in clinical evaluation, BCL-2 antagonism is the most advanced. Venetoclax, is a BCL-2 inhibitor approved for chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, that has shown encouraging results in vitro by reverting killing resistance, [26,48,49] and in vivo by reducing the reservoir in ART-treated humanized mice [58]. Based on these findings, venetoclax is now under investigation in a dose-escalating clinical trial in ART-suppressed PWH (NCT05668026). However, its potential limited tissue penetrance, particularly within secondary lymphoid organs, may constrain its potential for broad reservoir eradication. As an alternative, baricitinib, an approved JAK/STAT inhibitor that acts upstream of BCL-2, has also been proposed to target CTL-mediated killing resistance. With a different safety profile, baricitinib may confer additional advantages including limiting reservoir seeding and systemic inflammation [59,60]. Preclinical studies have confirmed the potency of baricitinib in reducing the reservoir, both in the periphery [61,62] and the central nervous system, thus validating its permeability to immune sanctuary sites. Three clinical trials testing the effects of baricitinib in ART-treated PWH are currently underway (NCT05452564, NCT05849038 and NCT07028385) that will soon help elucidate its effect on cell survival pathways, reservoir and chronic inflammation.

Similar to BCL-2, resistance to CTL-mediated killing might also be reversed through targeting of the prosurvival protein SURVIVIN. Preliminary data suggested this could be achieved through treatment with sepantronium bromide (YM155) [51], a SURVIVIN inhibitor which is currently under clinical investigation for cancer therapy, or the DDX3 inhibitors RK-3 and FH-1321 [63], leading to an enhanced elimination of HIV-infected primary CD4+ T cells. Likewise, several other cancer-targeted compounds acting on apoptosis-related proteins may also help to overcome CTL resistance. These include the AKT inhibitors perifosine, edelfosine and miltefosine, which have been shown to inhibit HIV-induced AKT activation, resulting in the death of macrophages; the SMAC mimetic birinapant and XIAP antagonists GDC-0152 and embelin that could mediate apoptosis in HIV latently infected CD4+ T cells [64–66].

More recently, in light of emerging evidence linking the quiescent metabolic profiles to CTL-resistance in HIV-1 reservoir cells [52▪▪], deferoxamine (DFO) has been proposed as a novel candidate for adjunctive HIV cure strategies. DFO is an FDA-approved iron chelator available as a generic medication widely used in the treatment of chronic iron overload conditions. Mechanistically, DFO promotes intracellular iron depletion, which disrupts iron-dependent enzymatic processes and enhances mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to increased production of ROS. In vitro studies have demonstrated that DFO-induced ROS [52▪▪] can sensitize HIV-1-infected cells to immune-mediated clearance. Beyond its mechanistic rationale, DFO has a well established clinical safety profile, with decades of use in both paediatric and adult populations, with side effects that are typically dose-dependent and reversible upon discontinuation, which might be mitigated by a short-term use of DFO in the context of HIV cure interventions.

CURRENT KNOWLEDGE GAPS

While the ongoing studies will help us gain a more profound understanding of potential mechanisms underlying resistance to CTL-mediated killing of the HIV-1 reservoir, there are still several knowledge gaps that remain to be addressed. First, it is still unclear how relevant this phenomenon is in-vivo and what proportion of the total reservoir is CTL-resistant. This will be critical to interpret efficacy results of ongoing clinical trials and to understand the contribution that reverting CTL-resistance will play in immune-mediated clearance of the HIV-1 viral reservoir. Second, it is worth noted that most of existing data derive exclusively from studies focused on peripheral blood. Whether similar killing resistance mechanisms also exist in tissues where a greater abundance of the HIV-1 reservoir is located remains a question for future investigation. This is critical as it is also unclear whether the so-called ‘killing-sensitizing’ drugs mentioned above can penetrate well in tissue compartments, which is central to an efficient HIV-1 clearance. Thus, a better understanding of this phenomenon, both in the periphery and tissues, is needed to identify shared markers that can be broadly targeted in both compartments. Finally, we still have an incomplete understanding on when CTL-resistance arises during the course of HIV-1 infection and treatment, and whether the CTL-resistance onset and kinetics are different between PWH treated early versus late after HIV acquisition – the key factors to define study populations, and determine the best opportunity window for combination immunotherapies aimed to sensitize the HIV-1 reservoir and enhance antiviral immunity.

CONCLUSION

Antiretroviral treatment (ART) effectively suppresses HIV replication, but the virus persists in resting memory CD4+ T cells, particularly within immune tissue reservoirs. These reservoirs can restart viral replication if ART is interrupted, resulting in a long-standing barrier to curing HIV. Several advances in therapeutic vaccines have demonstrated its ability to induce strong polyfunctional CTL responses that improve viral control but fail to reduce reservoir levels or prevent viral rebound upon ART interruption. Several recent works suggest that, in addition to viral latency, HIV reservoir may evolve to a CTL-resistant phenotype to evade immune-mediated clearance. Here we review current data on mechanisms involved on CTL-resistance and the strategies being proposed to sensitize the HIV reservoir to immune-mediated clearance. We also highlight remaining knowledge gaps and identify priority areas for research. Addressing these gaps would be essential to construct a more comprehensive understanding of HIV reservoir’ s CTL-resistance that will help advance the HIV-1 cure field. Importantly, these findings may also have broader applications beyond HIV. Targeting CTL-resistance pathways is relevant to any condition where immune evasion enables harmful cells to persist, including cancers and persistent infections. Exploring analogous sensitizing strategies for therapeutic vaccines targeting tumour antigens, offer the potential to transform immune-based therapies in oncology and open entirely new opportunities to tackle chronic viral infections such as EBV or CMV implicated in a multitude of clinically serious conditions.
