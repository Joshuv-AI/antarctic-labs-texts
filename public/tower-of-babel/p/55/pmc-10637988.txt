Innate immunity: Looking beyond T-cells in radiation and immunotherapy combinations
Authors: RA McMahon, C D'Souza, PJ Neeson, S Siva
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiation therapy is an established and effective anti-cancer treatment modality. Extensive pre-clinical experimentation has demonstrated that the pro-inflammatory properties of irradiation may be synergistic with checkpoint immunotherapy. Radiation induces double-stranded DNA breaks (dsDNA). Sensing of the dsDNA activates the cGAS/STING pathway, producing Type 1 interferons essential to recruiting antigen-presenting cells (APCs). Radiation promotes cytotoxic CD8 T-cell recruitment by releasing tumour-associated antigens captured and cross-presented by surveying antigen-presenting cells. Radiation-induced vascular normalisation may further promote T-cell trafficking and drug delivery. Radiation is also immunosuppressive. Recruitment of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and innate cells such as myeloid-derived suppressive cells (m-MDSCs) all counteract the immunostimulatory properties of radiation. Many innate immune cell types operate at the interface of the adaptive immune response. Innate immune cells, such as m-MDSCs, can exert their immunosuppressive effects by expressing immune checkpoints such as PD-L1, further highlighting the potential of combined radiation and checkpoint immunotherapy. Several early-phase clinical studies investigating the combination of radiation and immunotherapy have been disappointing. A greater appreciation of radiotherapy's impact on the innate immune system is essential to optimise radioimmunotherapy combinations. This review will summarise the impact of radiotherapy on crucial cells of the innate immune system and vital immunosuppressive cytokines.Keywords: Radiation, Immunotherapy, Innate, Immune system, Radiotherapy

Introduction

Radiation therapy (RT) is an established and sophisticated treatment modality in cancer medicine. Radiotherapy induces ionising damage to cellular DNA. Over the past two decades, there has been a greater understanding of the immunostimulatory effects of radiation. Antigen-experienced and memory T cells were found to be more radioresistant than naïve T cells [1], [2], [3]. Ionising radiation induces and promotes anti-tumour T cell immunity through several mechanisms reviewed in detail in Demaria et al [4]. Historically, radiation sensitises cancer cells to T cell killing by mediating major histocompatibility complex class one (MHC-I) and Fas up-regulation. RT also increases antigen availability and improves antigen-presenting function by releasing damage associated molecular signals and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as type I interferon [5,6]. Furthermore, radiotherapy has also been shown to increase T-cell infiltration [6], [7], [8], [9] by inducing T-cell recruiting chemokines, namely CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL12 [6,8,10] and promoting vascular normalisation [6,11] (See Fig. 1).

Immunostimulatory effects of irradiation in promoting a CD8+ anti-tumoural T cell response. Localised radiotherapy promotes the release of tumour-associated antigens (TAAs) that are captured and presented by surveying dendritic cells. The dendritic cells drain to regional lymph nodes, priming CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells. CD8+ T cells trafficking to the tumour is promoted by the chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL12.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionised the management of solid organ tumours. Antibodies that disrupt immune regulatory pathways, such as a programmed cell death protein (PD-1/PD-L1), aim to modulate T cell function to improve tumour destruction [12]. Innate immune checkpoints that suppress innate sensing and subsequent clearing of malignant cells are critical components of immune-mediated tumour escape [13]. Despite ICIs promise, not all patients respond to ICIs. ICI effectiveness is limited by hostile tumour microenvironment factors. Solid tumours with poor immune infiltration are considered immunologically cold. By contrast, immunologically "hot" tumours have high infiltration levels of immune effector cells such as CD8+ T cells and are more responsive to immunotherapy interventions [14,15]. Engagement of the adaptive immune system is one aspect of radiotherapy's immunostimulatory properties. Radiotherapy is one therapeutic modality that promotes the transition of immune cold into hot tumours by promoting immune cell infiltration into the tumour microenvironment (TME) [7,14,16]. Therefore, combining radiotherapy with ICI (radioimmunotherapy) has the potential to improve outcomes for patients with advanced solid organ malignancies.

The innate immune response modulates the inflammatory microenvironment during T-cell priming and mediates and activates the adaptive immune response. Many cells are considered exclusively innate, such as natural killer (NK cells), while dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and monocytes also operate at the interface of adaptive immune responses [17]. Tumours employ numerous mechanisms to avoid immune detection. HLA-I downregulation is an important tumoural mechanism of cytotoxic T-cell immune escape [18]. The inability of T cells to effectively kill HLA-I negative tumours highlights the importance of NK cells' unique activation pathway. NK cells can recognise tumours independent of their HLA-I expression and produce immunomodulatory cytokines and chemokines that direct local and distant immune responses [19]. Strategies to improve anti-tumoural NK killing may overcome deficiencies in the adaptive response [18]. Paradoxically, ionising radiation also induces innate immunosuppressive effects. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), Type 2 tumour-associated macrophage (TAM2) and the cytokine TGF-β are all upregulated by radiotherapy. The role of radiotherapy in the adaptive immune response is well established. The impact of radiotherapy on the innate immune response, however, remains underappreciated. Given the extensive literature on radiotherapy's role in the adaptive immune response, this review will explore radiotherapy's impact on the innate immune response. This review will summarise the role of cells of the innate immune system, their regulation, and the impact of irradiation on their function. In the second half of the review, we summarise radiation's impact on STING signaling, the resulting production of the soluble type 1 interferons, and the immunosuppressive cytokine transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β).

Radiotherapy & Natural Killer cells

Natural killer cells (NK cells) uniquely kill virus-infected cells in vitro [19]. NK cells do not require antigen presentation on MHC class 1 molecules for activation and thus can preferentially target MHC-I negative cells that escape T-cell recognition [19]. NK cells serve as an essential early defense against tumours. Low peripheral blood NK activity is associated with increased cancer risk [20], with their activation dependent on the balance between activating and inhibitory signals [21](See Fig. 2). Activation receptors include (1) NKG2D, which recognises the NK activating ligands (MIC-A, MIC-B and ULBPs), (2) natural cytotoxicity receptors (NCRs) NKp30, NKp46 and NKp44, and (3) nectin like binding receptors DNAM-1 [21], [22], [23]. Inhibitory molecules expressed on the target cell surface, such as human leukocyte antigen (HLA) HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C, bind the killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs) family of receptors suppressing NK effector function [24]. NK cells play an essential role in antibody dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). Engagement of the FCγIII receptor, with the FC portion of IgG antibodies bound on other cells, mediates NK cytotoxicity [21]. Activated NK cells directly lyse tumour cells through the release of perforin and granzymes [19]. The cytokines IFNγ and TNFα are also released and mediate the immune response [19]. NK-directed immunotherapy's efficacy in solid organ tumours is limited by NK cells' ability to infiltrate into deep tumour tissue effectively. Low density of tumour infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) has portended a poorer response to chemotherapy in multiple human cancer types [25,26]. Ex-vivo real-time trafficking of NK cells in human breast cancer tumour xenograft models demonstrates only marginal tumour infiltration [14,27]. An abundance of NK cells in the tumour microenvironment (TME) is associated with increased overall survival in patients across multiple cancer types [28]. NK cells and Type 1 DCs also form important intracellular cross talks with NK cells being implicated in DC maturation, recruitment and survival within the tumour [28], [29], [30]. NK cells also perform DC editing, selective killing of immature DCs that ensures mature and immunogenic DCs prime T cells [28,29,31]. Furthermore, NK cells encourage the recruitment of Type 1DCs to the TME through the production of chemokines CCL5, CXCL1 and CXCL2 [28,32]. Activated DCs also produce IL-12, an essential cytokine in promoting NK and T cell anti-tumour activity [28,33]. NK cells also produce FLT3LG, essential for DC survival and differentiation and may be regulated by the immune checkpoints TIM-3, TIGIT, LAG3, CTLA-4 and PD-1 [28,31,34].

Additionally, there are numerous clinical studies underway investigating the safety and efficacy of anti-TIGIT, TIM-3, LAG3 and PVRIG in promoting NK-cell recruitment [35], [36], [37], [38], [39]. NK cells also demonstrate memory like features. Cooper et al [40] demonstrated at murine NK cells that have undergone activation with cytokines IL-12, IL-15 and IL-18 produce significantly more IFN-gamma up to 22 days following re-stimulation. These are termed Cytokine-induced memory like (CIML) NK cells. CIML NK cells are a potential target for immunotherapy. In a melanoma xenograft model, adoptively transferred pre-activated NK Cells in vitro with IL-12/15/18 rejected tumours more efficiently [41]. Similar enhanced interferon gamma and production and cytotoxicity was observed in leukemia cell lines and primary human AML blasts in vitro [42]. Adoptive transfer of pre-activated IL-12/15/18 NK cells mediated graft-versus-leukemia while suppressing acute graft-versus-host disease in a murine model of allogenic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [43]. CISH, which encodes the cytokine-inducible SH2-containing protein (CIS), a negative regulator of IL-15 signaling, is also an emerging target for tumour infiltrating NK cells [44], [45], [46]. NK Cells engineered with chimeric antigen receptors (CAR-NK) targeting tumour antigens are similarly encouraging [47]. To date, radiation is not currently employed in the methodology of CAR-NK therapy, and the synergistic benefits of radiotherapy in combination with checkpoint inhibition in promoting NK cell recruitment is yet to be determined. Despite its infancy, combining radiotherapy and NK-cell-directed immunotherapy remains an exciting potential combination that warrants further investigation.

In-vitro studies have demonstrated that RT upregulates the ligands MICA/B and ULBPs, increasing tumour cell susceptibility to NK-induced lysis [48,49]. Radiotherapy may also promote the recruitment of NK cells through upregulating tumour production of CXCL8 [50]. Gasser et al [51], investigated the mechanistic pathways of DNA damage repair pathway in regulating human NKG2D ligand receptor expression in non-tumour cell lines. Ovarian cells lines from p53-/- mice were transduced with K-ras & c-myc (C1 cell line) or Akt and c-myc (C2 cell line) respectively. NKG2D ligand expression was upregulated by 40Gy of ionising irradiation. Ligand upregulation was prevented by pharmacological inhibition of ATR and ATM or Chk1 [51]. In tumour cell lines, constitutive NKG2D expression was inhibited by short interfering RNA to ATM, suggesting that ligand expression may be due to chronic activation of DNA damage response pathways. Monjazeb et al [52] investigated the role of in vitro NK migration in human tumour cell lines in the setting of escalating dose of irradiation. NK migration increased in a dose dependent manner from 2Gy up to 10Gy. NK migration was abrogated at 20Gy likely due to tumour destruction. Expression of the chemokines, especially CXCL16 and CXCL19, also increased in a dose dependent manner. Neutralizing antibodies to these chemokines produced a reduction in RT induced NK cell migration. In vivo studies of NOD-SCID IL2 receptor gamma null (NSG) mice bearing human tumour xenografts demonstrated that irradiation induced infiltration of transferred human NK cells into the irradiated site [52]. These tumours exhibited enhanced anti-tumour effects [52].

In vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that NK cells preferentially target tumour cells with a cancer stem cell (CSC) phenotype [53]. CSCs are a subpopulation of tumour cells identified in many tumour types, including pancreatic, sarcoma and breast tumours [54], [55], [56]. CSCs within a tumour can repopulate following treatment and can be a mechanism of tumour relapse. Ames et al. [53] investigated the impact of radiation on NK stem-like tumour cell targeting. In-vitro irradiation of 8Gy of fresh primary sarcoma FSA4, demonstrated upregulation of stress markers MICA/B and Fas on unsorted primary tumour cells. Changes in expression of ALDH, CD24 and CD44 following irradiation corresponded to increased frequency of stem-like cells. Ames et al. [53] examined RT and NK combination therapy in subcutaneous PANC-1 xenograft model in NSG mice. Single intra-lesional injection of 2×107 NK cells demonstrated no significant tumour regression of PANC-1 tumours. 8Gy of radiotherapy alone provided only modest tumour regression. Combination RT and NK therapy were statistically superior to all other treatments (p = 0.014), with mice treated with combined RT and NK surviving longer than 120 days. Radiation was shown to enhance NK cytotoxicity towards CSCs.

Makowska et al. [57] in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) cell lines demonstrated that radiotherapy sensitised NPC cells to NK-directed killing. RT (up to 6Gy) upregulated expression of PD-1 ligand (PD-L1) in NPC cells and PD-1 receptor (PD-1) in NK cells. In the context of radiotherapy, blockade of the PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoint further increased NPC cell killing by NK cells through the upregulation of IFNβ. Kim et al. [14] in a triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) murine model irradiated tumours with 12Gy, 17 days following injection with activated NK cells. Seventy-one days after the tumour injection, the in-vivo bioluminescence intensity of the primary tumours from the RT + NK group was 5-fold lower than those in the RT group (P= .048). Combining NK cell therapy and local RT prevented pulmonary metastasis formation. None of the mice in the RT + NK group developed axillary metastasis. In contrast, the proportion of palpable secondary tumours in the RT group was 85 % (6 of 7) and 20 % (1 in 5) in the NK group. Taken together, these studies demonstrate that localised radiotherapy increases the cytotoxic potential of NK cells by increasing tumour cell susceptibility to NK-mediated cytotoxicity and assists NK migration and infiltration into tumours.

Radiotherapy and Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are a heterogeneous population of immunosuppressive cells comprised primarily of neutrophils and inflammatory monocytes [3,58]. This plastic and immature population expand in pathological conditions such as malignancy [58]. MDSCs are identified as either monocytic (M-) MDSCs (CD11b+HLA-DR−/loCD14+CD15−) or polymorphonuclear (PMN-) MDSCs (CD11b+HLA-DR−/loCD14−CD15+) [59], [60], [61]. M-MDSCs are associated with high levels of the transcription factor, signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT)1 and nitrous oxide (NO) synthase [62,63]. Nitrous oxide (NO) inhibits MHC class II expression [62,64]. PMN-MDSCs are associated with STAT3 [62,65], elevated ROS production and NADPH oxidase activity [62,63] that suppresses T cell function [62,65]. Additionally, MDSCs additionally exert immunosuppressive function through numerous mechanisms, as summarised by the review from Jimenez-Cortegana et al., 2022 [58] and illustrated in Fig. 3.

Wu et al [66], in TC-1 tumour-bearing mice, injected E7 antigenic peptide into an irradiated tumour that previously received 14Gy. The study's aim is to prime antigen-specific CD8+ T cells and myeloid cells with antigen, rendering them susceptible to killing by vaccine-induced antigen-specific CD8+ T cells. Combined RT and E7 injection demonstrated complete tumour regression in 7 of the 10 mice and remained tumour free for up to 60 days following completion of radiotherapy. Following irradiation, there was a 3-fold accumulation of CD11b+ myeloid cells in irradiated tissue compared to non-irradiated tumours (P = 0.015). Irradiation significantly enriched CD11b+ myeloid subset CD11b+Gr-1+ MDSCs and CD11b+F4/80+ TAMs in the tumour microenvironment (P < 0.05). When assessing the number of tumour-infiltrating CD11b+ myeloid cells the tumour microenvironment of TC-1 tumour-bearing mice, mice treated with combined radiation and E7 peptide demonstrated a significant reduction in MDSCs recruited compared to monotherapy radiation or E7 peptide (P < 0.01). A similar reduction of CD11b+F4/80+ TAMs was not seen between combined and monotherapy. Peptide-loaded CD11b+ myeloid cell infiltrated were targeted by antigen-specific CD8+ T cells resulting in death of MDSCs. In vivo killing of tumour stromal cells, including CD11b+ myeloid cells by antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, enhanced tumour control during combined radiation and intratumoral peptide vaccination.

MDSCs promote tumour growth by directly affecting tumour cell survival and indirectly on local T cell suppression [67], [68], [69]. Deng et al.[69], investigated the impact of combined radiation and anti-PD-L1 therapy on MDSCs in TUBO mouse models. Ten days following 12Gy of irradiation, CD45+CD11b+ Gr1+ expression as a percentage of CD45+ cells were most significantly reduced by combined RT and anti-PDL1. The degree of reduction of local MDSCs was associated with enhanced tumour growth delay and tumour regression. There was no change in the percentage of MDSCs on day 3 following single or combined therapy indicating that treatment was not influencing recruitment of MDSCs. Stained tumour sections from untreated and treated mice demonstrated elevated levels of activated caspase 3 in Gr1+ cells closely associated with CD8+ T cells suggesting that CD8+ T cells may be involved in controlling MDSC cells by inducing apoptosis. CD8+ T cell depletion restored MDSCs to levels of untreated control mice. Co-culture of activated CD8+ T cells and MDSCs derived from the spleens of tumour bearing mice resulted in an increase (from 11.75 % ± 0.48 % to 44.38 % ± 0.63 %) in annexin V+ (apoptotic) MDSCs compared to MDSCs cultured with resting CD8+ T cells. Neutralisation of TNF reduced the fraction of annexin V+ MDSCs. MDSC PDL1 expression was protective from CD8+ T cell induced cell death.

Fu et al. [70] in TRAMP-C1 prostate cancer models demonstrated that 25Gy of radiation promoted infiltration of MDSCs into tumours as early as 4 hours post-radiotherapy and these MDSCs persisted systemically in tumour, spleen and peripheral blood for at least two weeks. The MDSCs expressed matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9), promoting vasculogenesis and dissemination, especially in necrotic tumour. Similar results were not observed when 8Gy was delivered. Wu et al. [71] investigated the role of local radiotherapy of 15Gy in TRAMP-C1 in-vivo prostate cancer model on MDSC recruitment. Inhibition of IL-6 following irradiation was associated with reduced MDSC recruitment and increased infiltration of CD3+ T cells into tumours. Radiotherapy fractionation also plays a significant role in MDSC sensitivity and recruitment. Lan et al. [72], in lung carcinoma LL/2 and melanoma B16F10 models, investigated immune alterations in response to escalating radiotherapy fraction sizes. Radiotherapy regimes were ablative hypofractionated radiation therapy (AHFRT) and conventionally fractionated radiation therapy (CFRT). Tumours treated with AHFRT received 23Gy in 2 fractions, and CFRT tumours received 36Gy over 9 fractions. In both tumour models, the recruitment of MDSCs into the primary tumours was lower in the AHFRT groups than in CFRT. Given MDSC's express PD-L1, the increase in PD-L1 expression following irradiation was significantly lower for AHFRT than CFRT. AHFRT inhibited the recruitment of MDSCs by targeting the VEGF/VEGFR axis. The importance of radiation dose fractionation in MDSC recruitment was further evaluated by Grapin et al. [73]. In CT26 murine colon cancer tumour models, mice irradiated with 18×2Gy demonstrated significantly higher tumour infiltrated MDSCs and TAM2 than the 3×8Gy and 1×16.4Gy irradiated mice. Pre-clinical evidence suggests that radiation dose fractionation impacts MDSC recruitment and PD-L1 expression. Current studies offer conflicting reports of the optimal dose fractionation, which may be tumour specific.

Clinical evidence for the impact of radiotherapy on tumoural or circulating MDSCs is scarce compared to pre-clinical studies. Clinical studies investigating the role of MDSCs on local and distant tumour control have involved small patient numbers. Navarro-Martin et al. [74] performed immunophenotyping analysis on peripheral blood samples from seven patients with lung cancer unfit for surgery. All patients underwent SBRT, with four receiving 60Gy (7.5Gy x 8) and three 50Gy (12.5Gy x 4). A reduction in granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (G-MDSCs) was observed 72 hours following SBRT and persisted for 6 months (66.1 % at baseline to 62.6 % at six months). Monocytic (Mo-MDSCs) were also reduced (8.2 % at baseline to 6.2 % at six months). A single-arm study of 29 patients with either Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) or head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) assessed the systemic immune effects of combined chemoradiotherapy [75]. Patients received between 34-70Gy of radiation combined with platinum-based chemotherapy. Peripheral blood analysis demonstrated no significant increase in the overall MDSC population following treatment. Eight patients experienced an expansion of MDSCs, while 5 patients experienced a contraction in MDSCs following treatment. Tsai et at [76] performed a retrospective review of 248 patients with head and neck SCC who underwent adjuvant radiotherapy ranging from 60-66Gy. Intra-tumoural IL-6 was correlated with circulating MDSCs assessed by cytometry analysis of 130 peripheral blood samples. IL-6 over-expression was associated with a poorer prognosis and positively linked to PD-L1 staining and levels of circulating MDSCs. Inhibition of IL-6 resulted in downregulation of PD-L1 expression and MDSC recruitment. Overall, the clinical evidence of the interplay between radiotherapy and MDSCs is still in its infancy. The striking pre-clinical evidence suggests that MDSCs counteract the therapeutic efficacy of radiotherapy, making this heterogeneous cell population a promising target for novel combined therapies.

Radiotherapy and Tumour-Associated Macrophage

Tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) are a subset of myeloid cells, categorized as either pro-inflammatory (M1) or immunosuppressive (M2). The M1/M2 concept is derived from in-vitro experimentation [78,79]. To date, TAM phenotypes have not been observed in cancer [80,81] but have utility in describing macrophage phenotypes' effects. M1 macrophages are essential in the host's innate immune response. M1 macrophage tumour killing is mediated through reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) that accompany TNFα, IL-1β, and IL-6 production [82]. Immunosuppressive M2 macrophages are essential in the wound healing response [82]. Furthermore, M2 macrophages promote tumour growth by producing anti-inflammatory cytokines TGF-β, IL-10 and IL13 [82]. Shiao et al [83] investigated the role of TAMs in the effectiveness of radiotherapy in a murine model of mammary carcinogenesis. Tumour-bearing mice received a neutralising antibody to colony-stimulating factor-1 (CSF-1) or a small molecule inhibitor CSF-1 receptor kinase. The study investigated tumour growth following RT in the setting of macrophage deletion. At 14 days following 8Gy of irradiation, a significant increase in TAMs was observed. A similar increase did not occur following 2Gy. When combined with anti-CSF1, tumour control following RT was greatly improved. In a pancreatic tumour model, Seifert et al. [84] demonstrated that 12Gy in a single fraction of RT was associated with increased early infiltration of F4/80+ TAMs. The tumour infiltrating TAMs exhibited M2 reprogramming through reduced MHC II expression but elevated CD204 and CD206 expression. Arg1+ cell infiltration also increased following RT, and M2 TAM differentiation was driven by RT-induced C-CSF expression in tumour cells. M-CSF blockade, combined with RT, demonstrated marked improvement in tumour control. Contrastingly, Klug et al. [85], in a RIP1-Tag5 pancreatic islet carcinogenesis mouse model, demonstrated that 2Gy was sufficient to induce polarisation of M2-like TAMs to M1-like iNOS expressing TAMs [85]. iNOS activity was responsible for subsequent vascular normalisation, T cell recruitment and tumour rejection. Nitrous oxide is a crucial effector of the innate immune system and is characteristic of M1 macrophage [86]. Coates et al. [86] in CBA/Ca, and C57BL/6 mice irradiated bone marrow-derived macrophage expressing F4/80 with 4Gy. Arginase 1 was expressed at 40-fold higher levels in C57BL/6 than in CBA/Ca macrophages. Cultured macrophages did not change their phenotype expression following irradiation in vitro, demonstrating that genetic differences may play an essential role in levels of macrophage phenotypic expression. The dose of radiation appears to impact the recruitment and polarisation of TAMs. Early studies have demonstrated that a larger dose per fraction in the range of 8-12Gy may promote recruitment and polarisation of M2 TAMs. Klug et al. [85] demonstrate that a smaller dose per fraction of 2Gy may promote polarisation of M2 to M1-like iNOS expressing TAMs. The impact of RT on TAM recruitment and polarisation requires further investigation.

Radiotherapy and STING Signaling

A key mechanism in radiation-induced cell damage is the recognition of double-strand DNA (dsDNA) breaks. dsDNA is released into the cell cytoplasm from the nucleus or mitochondria following radiation insult. Sensing of the dsDNA leads to activation of the cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) synthase (cGAS)/stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway [62,[87], [88], [89], [90]]. STING pathway activation leads to Type 1 interferon (IFN) production within local tumour tissue. Type 1 IFNs are essential in the recruitment of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) [62,91,92] (See Fig. 3). The effectiveness of radiotherapy is thus intimately intertwined with cGAS/STING signaling. However, the role of cGAS/STING signaling in MDSC recruitment and activation requires further investigation. Cheng et al. [93], in CT26 tumour-bearing mice, demonstrated inhibition of liver metastasis via intravenous administration of STING agonist cGAMP that regulated epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through the Wnt/-catenin pathway. cGAMP suppressed MDSC production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitric oxide (NO), abrogating MDSC's ability to suppress T cell function. Zhang et al. [94], in a STING overexpressing human nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) cell line, investigated the role of STING in the regulation of MDSCs. STING reduced MDSC recruitment by enhancing SOCS1 expression in tumours and MDSCs. SOCS1 interacts with STAT3 preventing its phosphorylation, inhibiting GM-CSF and IL-6 production, and reducing MDSC induction. Reduced levels of tumoural STING were associated with a poorer prognosis for NPC patients. Furthermore, the β-galactose-binding animal lectin (Gal-9) upregulates myeloid-derived immunosuppression through STING suppression [95]. In NPC cell lines, Gal-9 upregulates essential genes encoding proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines associated with myeloid cell differentiation and expansion. Most notably IL-1β, IL-6 and CXCL1 and CCL2. Overexpression of endogenous Gal-9 downregulated STING expression. Interestingly, Gal-9 also induces apoptosis of effector T cells through binding T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing molecular 3 (Tim-3) [95,96]. By contrast, Liang et al. [59] investigated myeloid populations in irradiated MC38 colon tumours in C57BL/6 mice. Ly6Chi myeloid cells express elevated levels of CCR2. CCR2 facilitates the trafficking of myeloid cells from the bone marrow. Following 20Gy, radiation-induced M-MDSC infiltration was reduced in CCR2-/- and anti-CCR2 antibody-depleted mice. M-MDSCs were associated with reduced local tumour control following irradiation. In STING knockout mice receiving 20Gy of irradiation, recruitment of M-MDSCs was significantly lower than in wild-type mice following irradiation. STING signaling following radiotherapy appears to enhance the immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment through M-MDSC recruitment. However, STING activation by both tumour cells and MDSCs may also help reprogram MDSCs into immunostimulatory cells [62]. (Fig. 4)

The Complex Role of TGF-β

Transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) is a potent cytokine with diverse effects on haematopoietic cells [98]. Elevated levels of TGF-β are expressed by numerous tumour types, including prostate, breast, colon, liver, lung, stomach, and pancreatic cancer, along with melanoma [98], [99], [100]. TGF-β regulates lymphocyte proliferation, differentiation and survival and is an essential component of immune tolerance [98]. TGF-β is a regulator of chemotaxis and activation of leukocytes, including lymphocytes, NK cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, mast cells and granulocytes [98]. TGF-β impacts immune functions through several mechanisms. One mechanism is through TGF-β blockade of interleukin 2 (IL-2). IL-2 is a crucial activator of several immune cell types, including T cells and NK cells [98,101,102]. IL-2 suppression is directly related to inhibiting IL-2 promoter activity through a cis-acting enhancer DNA element [98,103]. Cell cycle regulators such as p15, p21 and p27 are upregulated upon TGF-β introduction, while cell cycle promoting factors such as c-myc, cyclin D2, cyclin dependant kinase 2 and cyclin E are decreased [98,[104], [105], [106], [107], [108], [109], [110]]. TGF-β also impedes T cell effector function through inhibiting effector molecules such as IFN-γ and perforin [98,111,112]. Similarly, TGF-β inhibits exocytosis of granules, a key component of CD8+ T cytolytic function [98,113]. Finally, TGF-β is vital in regulating the deposition of extracellular matrix components [114]. Through this role, TGF-β is a key component of the wound healing process [114,115]. Radiation increases intra-tumoral levels of TGF-β [98] which diminishes CD8+ T cell effector and dendritic cell cross-presenting function [98]. Sources of elevated intra-tumoural TGF-β following irradiation include the tumour cells themselves, tumour-associated stromal cells, MDSCs, M2 TAMs and Tregs [3,[116], [117], [118]]. TGF-β is secreted into the extracellular matrix and bound in its inactive form to the latency-associated peptide (LAP) [114]. Following RT, TGF-β increases the TGF- LAP complex [114] and through activation from radiation-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) [119]. TGF-β inhibition sensitises tumour cells to irradiation through its regulation of DNA damage [120]. Additionally, TGF-β is radio-protective by preventing mitotic catastrophe, lowering intrinsic free radicals and reduced activation of death receptor pathways [6,121]. TGF-β neutralisation during radiotherapy effectively generates CD8+ T cell responses to multiple tumour antigens. CD8+ T cells effectively generated non-irradiated lung metastasis tumour regression [122]. Interestingly, Arina et al. [6] showed that TGF-β is an upstream regulator of intratumoral T cell programming and is necessary for tissue memory T cell development (Trm). Lan et al. [123], utilising bifunctional fusion protein (BA) that simultaneously inhibits TGF-β and PD-L1, demonstrated synergy with radiotherapy in multiple therapy-resistant tumour models. In a radiation-induced lung fibrosis model (RILF), the BA and RT (15Gy) combination increases tumour infiltrating leukocytes and attenuates RT-induced fibrosis. TGF-β upregulation following irradiation is a crucial immunosuppressive mechanism and an exciting target to improve radioimmunotherapy's local and systemic effects.

Discussion

Combining radiotherapy with ICIs is an exciting treatment paradigm for solid organ malignancies with limited treatment options. Radiation promotes the release of tumour-associated antigens which can stimulate an effector T-cell response that improves tumour control. Radiation can also normalise tumour vasculature further promoting immune invasion. Irradiation volumes encompass the tumour and the surrounding tumour environment; thus, the impact of irradiation on the innate immune system is essential in understanding immune-mediated tumour control. Table 1 summarises the impact of radiation on key innate immune cells, NK cells, MDSCs, and TAM presented in this review. Radiation promotes MDSC recruitment and improves NK-directed killing through MICA/B upregulation. RT-induced upregulation of PD-1 in NK cells, and PD-L1 on MDSCs, further highlights the potential rationale for combining ICI to mitigate immune escape. RT also has immunosuppressive effects on the tumour microenvironment. RT upregulation of MHC-I is inhibitory for NK signaling but key in DC and cytotoxic T cell activation. Likewise, RT increases intra-tumoural TGF-β, decreasing effector T cell function and dendritic cross-presentation. Radiotherapy activates the STING pathway. The production of intra-tumoral type 1 interferon recruits APCs. In contrast, STING signaling may play a role in recruiting MDSCs.

Future studies must aim to promote radiotherapy's immunostimulatory effects and curtail its immunosuppressive components. Radiotherapy has a unique ability to transform immunologically cold tumour microenvironments and can ameliorate ICI resistance. The clinical efficacy of combined radioimmunotherapy, especially for advanced tumours, is still in its infancy. The promising pre-clinical results have yet to be translated to the clinic, with early-phase clinical trials disappointing. The optional radiation dose, delivery and sequencing with immunotherapy are yet to be determined. Pre-clinical interrogation of RT's immunosuppressive effects is essential in optimising the treatment approach. Blockade of TGF-β, as seen in Lan et al [123], demonstrated potent local and distant tumour control. Overcoming innate immunosuppression may be crucial in the widespread adoption of combined radioimmunotherapy.

Conclusion

Radiotherapy has both immune-stimulatory and suppressive effects. Immune checkpoint upregulation following radiation is a crucial component of immune escape. Innate immune responses to radiation is a critical part of the immunomodulatory effects of radiation and affords novel targets to improve clinical efficacy. Present efforts to combine radiation with immunotherapy may be one strategy to overcome innate immunosuppression.
