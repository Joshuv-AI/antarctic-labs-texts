Evaluating the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on viral load and CD4 monitoring for people living with HIV on ART in rural Lesotho
Authors: Afom T Andom, Donald Fejfar, Ramohapi Mahloane, Melino Ndayizigiye, Tapiwa Tarumbiswa, Masebeo Koto, Mokenyakenya Matoko, Mohloki Chere, Mathabang Mokoena, Joalane Mokhohlane, Joia S Mukherjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWith the rapid spread of COVID-19, many governments put various measures in place to contain the spread of the pandemic. These measures, such as travel restrictions, curfews, and public gatherings, may affect health service delivery, including monitoring of viral load and CD4 for People Living with HIV (PLHIV) on antiretroviral therapy (ART). Lesotho is a lower-middle-income country with one of the world’s highest prevalence of HIV (22%). This study aims to examine the effect of COVID-19 on viral load and CD4 monitoring of PLHIV on ART in Lesotho.MethodsWe employed routine data from seven Ministry of Health health centers supported by Partners In Health from March 2017 to February 2023. We extracted de-identified data for all patients with HIV on ART who were followed by the health centers, including demographic variables of interest, viral load, and CD4 count. We analyzed the data using the chi-squared tests of independence to compare before and after COVID-19, and we used a logistic regression model predicting a result of “suppressed” and advanced disease as binary outcome variables.ResultsWe observed significantly higher rates of viral load suppression and CD4 counts > 200 with p-values < 0.001 during the COVID-19 pandemic, March 2020, 2021, 2022, and 2023, versus before the pandemic in March 2017, 2018, 2019, and February 2020. Fewer patients seen during the pandemic were classified as having “advanced HIV” based on CD4 count (< 200 cells/ml) test results during COVID (7.0%) than before (8.0%), though it was no significant and likewise, fewer PLHIV seen during the COVID-19 pandemic were “unsuppressed” based on viral load (> 1000 copies/ml) (11.1%) vs. before the pandemic (15.8%) which was significant with p-value < 0.001.ConclusionsMaintaining high viral load suppression in remote and rural communities during the pandemic is possible. A robust primary health system and strong pandemic preparation and response plans are crucial for effective HIV program response. Measures and communication introduced into the viral load and CD4 monitoring during the pandemic outbreak are paramount for sustaining the viral load suppression and ultimately reducing new HIV transmission.Keywords: Antiretroviral therapy, Viral load suppression, Advanced HIV disease, COVID-19, 95 95 95 UNAIDS target, HIV program

Background

Lesotho has the second-highest HIV prevalence in the world with 22.7%[1]. The country had made tremendous strides toward the 95, 95, and 95 UNAIDS targets before the COVID-19 pandemic. The Lesotho Population-based HIV Impact Assessment Survey 2020 showed that 89.6% of people living with HIV know their status, 96.9% who were diagnosed have started on ART therapy, and 90.8% of People Living with HIV (PLHIV) on ART have suppressed viral load [2, 3]. The rapid spread of COVID-19 globally posed a significant threat to health systems in the world, particularly in lower and middle-income countries where the health systems were not well prepared for such a huge pandemic [4, 5].

Lesotho is the first country in sub-Saharan Africa to implement the WHO-recommended test and treat approach for PLHIV in June 2016 [6, 7]. In the test and treat program, a positive rapid test immediately results in initiation of ART without delay, CD4 or viral load thresholds. Following the test and treat approach in Lesotho, antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage significantly increased, resulting in 95% of people who are known to have HIV receiving ART [8]. Long-term quality care of PLHIV depends on the regular monitoring of viral load and CD4 count. The close monitoring using viral load and CD4 can help to assess the efficacy of ART treatment, and help identify poor ART treatment adherence and treatment failures [9, 10].

A key measurement for the success of the HIV program is the percentage of people on ART who have achieved viral load suppression. PLHIV who are virally suppressed are less likely to contract opportunistic infections and have an extremely low probability of transmitting HIV [11]. The importance of adherence to clinic visits for PLHIV is recognized as a means to monitor clinical status and adherence to treatment. Even closer follow-up of viral load and CD4 counts is needed to ensure treatment response in those with advanced HIV disease, poor ART adherence, and a history of treatment failure. In addition, women with HIV who are pregnant and breastfeeding also require frequent monitoring to prevent vertical transmission [12, 13]. However, close monitoring of PLHIV with viral load and CD4 count was challenging during the COVID-19 pandemic, when various lockdown measures to curb the spread of the COVID-19 virus changed the access of HIV patients to the services to which they needed to adhere to ART [14, 15].

The rapid spread of the COVID-19 pandemic strained the already highly burdened health system of Lesotho, which was already struggling to respond to the pandemic [16]. Like many other governments, Lesotho also imposed several restriction measures to contain the COVID-19 pandemic and to protect its citizens [17]. As of April 2024, at least 36,138 COVID-19 cases and 723 deaths have been recorded in Lesotho [18]. During the COVID-19 period, there were four lockdowns in which gatherings and transport were imposed [19]. However, there is yet to be a study in evaluating the impact of COVID-19 disruptions on the viral load and CD4 count monitoring among PLHIV who are on ART toward the 95 95 95 UNAIDS target in Lesotho [14].

This study assesses the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on viral load and CD4 count monitoring for PLHIV who are on ART in the seven hard-to-reach rural Ministry of Health clinics supported by Partners In Health Lesotho (PIH Lesotho). This study may contribute to improving the follow-up of PLHIV in Lesotho and similar settings, and provide evidence for progress toward the 95-95-95 UNAIDS target, the attainment of HIV epidemic control efforts in sub-Saharan Africa.

Methods

Partners In Health Lesotho (PIH Lesotho) supports seven rural Lesotho Ministry of Health clinics in remote areas of Lesotho with an emphasis on strengthening primary healthcare services by integrating HIV, tuberculosis, maternal and child health, mental health, and non-communicable diseases care with the routine provision of primary care and with close follow-up with Village Health Workers (CHWs). These facilities are located in four districts across the country: Mohale’s Hoek, Thaba Tseka, Mokhotlong, and Qacha’s Neck.

We extracted anonymized data of PLHIV enrolled in care at the seven clinics from the electronic medical records (EMR). We extracted data from all PLHIV who were active in the ART programs in the PIH Lesotho-supported rural health facilities and eligible to be monitored for viral load and CD4 per the Lesotho national ART guidelines after six months of starting ART. Extracted data included: patient identifier, gender, age, duration of time since enrollment on ART and regimen, as well as a viral load and CD4 counts for each patient for each year in the program (see Table 1). These variables were compared with the viral load and CD4 count pre- and during the COVID-19 pandemic. Quality checks were performed to ensure that data entry errors and extreme values were not included. Viral load suppression is defined as < 1000 copies/mL of blood [9, 20]and viral load result > 1000 copies/mL is defined as unsuppressed. While a normal CD4 count is defined as 500 to 1500 cells/mL of blood, the Lesotho National AIDS Program sets a target to have patients > 200 cells/mL, classifying patients as not having advanced HIV disease, and patients with a CD4 count of < 200 cells/mL are classified as having advanced HIV disease [9, 21]. The data extracted covers March 2017 through February 2023. The first COVID case in Lesotho was identified in May 2020, and lockdown measures were taken before the first COVID-19 case report in March 2020 [22]. We consider the period before March 2020 a pre-COVID-19 period. The USA Department of Health and Human Services issued a 90-day notice in February 2023 for governors to prepare for unwinding of COVID-19 as a public health emergency [23], and the WHO declared on 5th May, the end of COVID-19 as a public Health emergency [24]. Therefore, we took February 2023 as the timeline for the end of our evaluation time during the pandemic.

We performed two methods of analysis: First, we used standard chi-squared tests of independence to identify differences in the PLHIV who visited the clinics before and after COVID began in Lesotho (which we define by year), reporting p-values with significance defined by < 0.05. Secondly, we fit a logistic regression model predicting a result of “Suppressed” as the binary outcome variable and using COVID, age, gender, and duration in the HIV program. We report beta coefficient estimates with corresponding odds ratios (with 95% confidence intervals or “CIs”). All data cleaning and analysis performed in R V4.3.0 (with use of the functions chisq.test, and glm).

Results

We reported 10,480 patients tested for the HIV program monitoring from March 2017 to February 2023 (see Table 2). Of these, 4979 (47.5%) results were before COVID-19 (i.e., during March 2017 and February 2020), and 5501 (52.5%) were during COVID-19 (i.e., during March 2020, 2021, 2022, and February 2023). 6525 (62.3%) of the participants were female, and 3955 (37.7%) were male. Out of them, 9929 (94.7%) were adults with an age of > 18 years, and 551 (5.3%) were < 18 years old. The number of patients on ART for more than 5 years and less than 5 years was 6292 (60.0%) and 4188 (40.0%), respectively. Overall, the number of participants with non-advanced HIV disease and suppressed viral load was 9694 (92.5%) and 9082 (86.7%), respectively (see Table 2).

Fewer patients were seen with “advanced HIV” based on CD4 count test results during COVID-19, 387(7.0%), than before, 399 (8%), with a p-value of 0.063. Likewise, fewer PLHIV were “unsuppressed” based on viral load testing during COVID, 613 (11.1%), than before, 785 (15.8%), with a p-value of < 0.001. No difference observed in the gender (p = 0.84) or age of patients (p = 0.6) before or during COVID-19 (see Table 3).

The logistic regression model showed a significant relationship between all predictor variables and viral suppression except for gender, as evidenced by p-values < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals for the corresponding odds ratios not containing the neutral value of 1.00 (see Table 4). Odds ratios can be interpreted as the factor by which the odds change for a patient’s test results that year, classifying as < 1000 copies/mL or “Suppressed”. For the year of test, a test during COVID-19 was 1.51 times as likely (95% CI: 1.34, 1.7) to read suppressed compared to before the pandemic. Male patients were less likely to be suppressed, with 0.87 times the odds of suppression as compared to female patients (95% CI: 0.77, 0.98), though this finding was not significant. Patients below 18 years were more likely to be suppressed, with the odds of suppression in children being 0.37 times the odds of adults in the study (95% CI: 0.27, 0.5). The patients enrolled in the ART program for more than 5 years were more likely to be suppressed, with the odds of suppression in patients more than 5 years being 1.3 times the odds of patients less than 5 years on the ART program (1.11, 1.52). Patients receiving ART treatment for HIV in the program were more likely to be suppressed; each other categories had lower odds of suppression. In other words, those who were in the program for maternal to child transmission, who eventually died, were lost to follow-up, or had some other status in the program, all had odds ratios significantly below 1.00.

Discussion

In this study, we found that more of the PLHIV seen during the COVID-19 pandemic had viral load suppression compared to pre-COVID-19. Moreover, the PLHIV seen during the pandemic were less likely to have advanced disease (as evidenced by CD4 count). Patients less than 18 years and patients who enrolled in the ART program more than 5 years were more likely to be suppressed and less likely to develop advanced HIV disease. The study conducted on the interplay between CD4 cell count, viral load suppression, and duration of antiretroviral therapy aligns with our findings, and it showed that as the current CD4 and duration on ART increased and viral load suppression occurred, and ultimately, mortality dropped [25]. Additionally, a study on the duration of HIV-1 Viral Suppression on Cessation of Antiretroviral Therapy showed that as the duration of patients on ART increased, the viral load suppression remained low when patients stopped taking ART, when compared to patients who stayed on ART for a shorter duration [26].

There was no significant difference in age or gender between the group seen before and during the pandemic. The higher percentage of patients with viral load suppression may have been due to the efforts that the Ministry of Health in Lesotho, with the support of PIH Lesotho, implemented to mitigate the impact of COVID-19 on access to HIV programs in Lesotho [27]. Some of these methods include multi-month dispensing of ART for stable patients, unwell ART patients sending a well buddy/relative to collect their ART drug from the facilities. ART stock outs monitoring, community health workers locally known as village health workers (VHWs) training, and strengthening of the community ART groups for stable patients were some of the contributing factors for the continuation of ART monitoring and adherence. Additionally, the community ART group, whereby a member of each group whose laboratory investigation is due goes to the health facilities to collect ART for PLHIV in the group to decongest health facilities and prevent COVID-19 infection. Additionally, PIH Lesotho, in collaboration with the Ministry of Health, has been working to strengthen comprehensive primary health services for nearly two decades in these remote hard to hard-to-reach communities. The VHWs in Lesotho were designed to address the high burden of HIV, TB, and maternal and child health programs, including the establishment of well-coordinated and designed VHWs for each PLHIV, including accompaniment of PLHIV to the health facilities when it is needed, free services including referral payment, and point of care viral load monitoring and CD4 count.

The lower percentage of virally suppressed patients and patients with advanced disease seen during the COVID-19 pandemic may have been due to the sicker patients staying home during that period for fear of falling ill or for lack of transport. The study conducted Pediatric and adolescent HIV viral load coverage and suppression rates in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic in 12 PEPFAR-supported sub-Saharan African countries in 2019 and 2020, including Lesotho, aligns with our findings, and it showed an increase in viral load suppression in children and adolescent PLHIV [28]. A study conducted by Jiang T et al. on the impact of COVID-19 on the UNAIDS target showed, different from our study, that HIV viral load testing decreased from 96 to 85% and CD4 testing decreased from 32 to 22% during the COVID-19 pandemic [14]. In that study, the lower rates of testing were thought to be due to the use of viral load platforms and staff time for COVID-19. The study conducted on the evaluation of virology suppression rates during the COVID-19 pandemic showed that while 81.5% of the clients had suppressed viral load (< 20 copies/ml) in pre-COVID-19, only 74.4% of the clients had suppressed viral load (< 20 copies/ml) during COVID-19 [29]. Additionally, 92.6% of clients had CD4 counts > 200 cell/mm3 pre-COVID-19 pandemic and 78.3% had CD4 counts > 200 cell/mm3 during the COVID-19 pandemic. A similar study conducted at San Francisco also showed the odds of viral load non-suppression increased by 31% during the COVID-19 pandemic when it compare to the pre COVID-19 performance [30].

The impact of COVID-19 on the HIV programs differs from country-to-country as various measures taken to curb the spread, most in-person visits were discouraged during the COVID-19 and patients were only asked to come physically when blood work is needed. The impact of the pandemic on the viral load and CD4 count monitoring also varied among countries. Studies conducted in diverse settings, from Malawi viral load with a very slight increase [31] to the Democratic Republic of Congo, showed that there was no difference in the viral load suppression pre and during COVID-19 [32]. Both studies documented various measures taken in Italy and the Democratic Republic of Congo to keep the viral load suppression stable during and before the COVID-19 pandemic. Our study, however, shows a significant increase in viral load suppression during COVID-19 despite measures taken to control COVID-19 spread.

Maintaining viral load suppression and regular monitoring of PLHIV is critically important for early identification of treatment failure [33] and without routine viral load and CD4 monitoring, treatment failure usually remains undetected and patients remain without switching to the appropriate regimen or patients are switched very late, with disease reaching an advanced stage [34, 35]. Patients with high viral load should get timely enhanced adherence sessions and timely decision on the appropriate regimen for effective ART response and curb new transmission of HIV [36, 37]. Strengthening the primary health system, including active community engagement, building robust laboratory services, timely and uninterrupted supply of drugs and commodities, and establishing effective and responsive village health programs in lower and middle-income countries, is critically important for a resilient health system with improved health services delivery, including good adherence to medication with optimal viral load suppression for PLHIV [38–40]. While our conclusions are not causal, our study shows that these goals are important, especially during times of stress on the health system, such as pandemics like COVID-19, and can even improve outcomes potentially.

A strength of our study is that we tried to assess the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the viral load and CD4 count monitoring in high-burden HIV settings. Our findings showed the resilience of the Lesotho HIV program in very remote and hard-to-reach areas, with significant progress in viral load and CD4 monitoring during the COVID-19 pandemic. This shows long years of community-centered comprehensive primary health system-building efforts. However, the results of this study may be limited by several factors. Firstly, limited valid data were available for viral load testing and other important demographic and program characteristics for this analysis. Secondly, missing data and some data errors (removed in data quality checks during cleaning) are a challenge of the manual data collection and entry at certain steps in the process, and were unavoidable as the data was extracted from an existing system. Thirdly, collapsing data into categorical variables (e.g., numeric test results into “Suppressed” vs. “Unsuppressed”) loses some analytical power, but adds meaning to the conclusions of the analysis. Data aggregation also loses the granularity to distinguish results by facility and some other individual-level characteristics. Fourthly, PIH Lesotho supports seven rural Lesotho Ministry of Health clinics, and the result of the study did not include a larger urban patient population. The results of this study may not be generalizable to the most urban population.

Conclusions

Viral load and CD4 count sustaining during the pandemic is possible in high HIV prevalent and remote settings. Implementation of viral load test scale-up as a golden way of monitoring ART and scale up of point of care monitoring technology is important for preventing new HIV transmission due to high viral load suppression rate. Additionally, investing in strengthening primary health service delivery with adequate and reliable viral test turnaround time is crucial for addressing the HIV infection rate. Regular and scheduled sample collection, transportation, and sample preservation using laboratory information systems that are linked with remote health centers can significantly help patients to get timely and regular monitoring with good adherence to ART therapy and an excellent viral load suppression during the unprecedented time of the COVID-19 pandemic.
