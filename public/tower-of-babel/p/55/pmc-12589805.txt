Rituximab in New‐Onset Generalized Myasthenia Gravis: Long‐Term Follow‐Up of the RINOMAX Clinical Trial
Authors: Jing Wu, Ann Eriksson‐Dufva, Anna Budzianowska, Amalia Feresiadou, William Hansson, Max Albert Hietala, Irene Håkansson, Rune Johansson, Daniel Jons, Ivan Kmezic, Christopher Lindberg, Jonas Lindh, Fredrik Lundin, Ingela Nygren, Anna Rostedt Punga, Rayomand Press, Kristin Samuelsson, Peter Sundström, Oskar Wickberg, Thomas Frisell, Susanna Brauner, Fredrik Piehl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe placebo‐controlled RINOMAX trial (NCT02950155) demonstrated superiority up to 12 months of rituximab over standard‐of‐care in new‐onset generalized myasthenia gravis (MG), but benefit–risk over longer time frames remains unknown.MethodsRINOMAX included 47 participants with a Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG) score ≥ 6. Twenty‐five patients were randomized to a single intravenous infusion of 500 mg rituximab, and 22 to placebo of which 16 received rituximab after the double‐blinded phase (7 ± 2.9 months). Data were extracted from the Swedish MG registry to track hospitalizations, treatments including rescue, and disease activity scores.ResultsCompared to the placebo arm, lower mean time‐weighted QMG scores at 12 months (mean difference [MD]: 2.9, 95% CI: 0.9, 4.9; p = 0.005) and 24 months (MD: 2.6, 95% CI: 0.3, 4.9; p = 0.027) were observed in the RTX arm. The incidence rate of rescue from 48 weeks up to 5 years was numerically higher in the placebo arm than RTX (0.16 vs. 0.09/person‐year; p = 0.121). Compared to delayed RTX, early exposure displayed lower QMG, risk of hospitalization (HR 0.24, 95% CI 0.07, 0.83), and rescue (HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.14, 1.57), but also the six patients never receiving RTX showed lower hospitalization risk (HR 0.08, 95% CI 0.01, 0.96). Corticosteroid doses were low globally throughout. Overall, 12.5% and 18.8% and of patients with early and delayed RTX, respectively, suffered a severe infection.ConclusionDisease activity and treatment burden, including hospitalization and rescue treatments, remained low, indicating a potential benefit of rituximab on the long‐term disease trajectory. Infection risk with B cell depletion, however, remains a concern.Keywords: controlled clinical trials, generalized myasthenia gravis, observational study, randomized, rituximab

Introduction

Myasthenia Gravis (MG) is a rare autoimmune condition causing muscle weakness with an incidence of about 1.5–2 per 100,000 [1]. Autoantibodies to the acetylcholine receptor (AChR+) can be detected in serum in a large majority, whereas smaller proportions of patients carry autoantibodies against muscle‐specific kinase (MuSK+) protein or lipoprotein‐receptor‐related protein 4 (LRP4) instead, or lack detectable autoantibodies altogether (seronegative MG) [2]. MG disease severity varies greatly, but it is well acknowledged that generalized disease, compared with those where symptoms are only ocular, is associated with a substantial risk of MG worsening, including life‐threatening events due to myasthenic crisis [3, 4].

Current treatment guidelines, which mainly rely on empirical experience and consensus agreements, suggest oral corticosteroids, with daily doses of up to 60–100 mg of prednisolone, as first‐line therapy [5]. While often being at least partly effective, steroids are also associated with significant short‐ and long‐term adverse side effects, including metabolic alterations and osteoporosis. In order to increase tolerability, it is therefore common practice to combine a slow steroid tapering with oral steroid‐sparing immunosuppressive drugs such as azathioprine, ciclosporin, methotrexate, mycophenolate, or tacrolimus [5]. Several of these immunosuppressive treatments have undergone randomized clinical trials; however with variable outcomes and with few studies conducted specifically in new‐onset disease [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Also, apart from being associated with a risk of reduced tolerability and side effects [5, 12, 13], a substantial subgroup of patients remains with refractory symptoms [14, 15]. These aspects are highlighted in a recent non‐intervention prospective multicentre trial conducted in new onset MG [16]. Novel treatment options such as biological drugs targeting the complement system and the neonatal Fc receptor, respectively, have shown efficacy in refractory MG, but are associated with high costs and limited access in many countries.

Rituximab is an older anti‐CD20 B cell‐depleting monoclonal approved for B cell lymphoma, rheumatoid arthritis, and vasculitis [17]. Meta‐analyses of mainly smaller observational retrospective studies, as well as a few uncontrolled prospective studies suggest that rituximab may be effective in MG with residual disease activity, in particular MuSK+ MG [18, 19]. However, a placebo‐controlled trial in AChR+ MG failed to show the superiority of rituximab over placebo as a steroid‐sparing agent in this group of patients [20]. In contrast, the RINOMAX trial showed a clear benefit up to 12 months of a single dose of 500 mg rituximab compared to a matched placebo as an add‐on to standard‐of‐care in a cohort of 47 participants with new onset generalized MG [21]. To further inform on the effectiveness and safety of rituximab as a first‐line treatment agent in new‐onset generalized MG, we recorded and analyzed long‐term outcomes for the RINOMAX participants.

Methods

RINOMAX Study Design, and Participants

RINOMAX was a double‐blinded, placebo‐controlled multicenter study in which, from October 2016 to March 2020, a total of 47 participants were recruited across 7 Swedish clinical trial sites [21]. Main inclusion and exclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years with the onset of generalized MG symptoms ≤ 12 months prior to inclusion, and a Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG) score of ≥ 6, with no exposure to immunosuppressive therapies including pulsed high‐dose corticosteroids, oral or monoclonal immune suppressants for any condition ≤ 12 months prior to inclusion, no prior thymectomy or suspected thymoma based on radiology findings, or an MG Federation of America (MGFA) score of I or V at screening. Treatment with prednisolone ≤ 40 mg/day for a maximum of 3 months, and intravenous immunoglobulins or plasma exchange within 12 months of screening were not considered exclusion criteria. The study drug or matching placebo was administered at the baseline visit. During the first 8 weeks, intravenous immunoglobulins and/or plasma exchange were not considered rescue treatment, as was prednisolone ≤ 40 mg/day with tapering to arrive at 10 mg/day by study Week 8. After that, standard‐of‐care medication with prednisolone ≤ 10 mg/day was permitted and non‐corticosteroid oral immunomodulatory drugs were allowed from Week 12. The primary outcome in the core study was the proportion of patients with minimal disease manifestations defined as a QMG score ≤ 4 and a daily dose of prednisolone of ≤ 10 mg/day at Week 16, with no rescue treatments (higher doses of prednisolone, all non‐permitted immune modulatory treatments, intravenous immunoglobulins, and plasma exchange) from Week 9 to 16. See the clinicaltrial.gov entry for complete information: NCT02950155.

For this follow‐up study, we included all 46 randomized participants alive at 48 weeks (one death occurred early after randomization due to an acute myocardial infarction). Written informed consent had been obtained from each participant prior to study inclusion, and approval to extract information from the Swedish MG registry was provided by the Region of Stockholm ethical review board (no. 2016/828‐31, with amendment 2020‐02797). Participants were followed as part of clinical routine practice, with outcomes prospectively entered into the Swedish MG registry (MGreg), with the addition of medical chart reviews to capture safety events. Figure 1 shows the trial flowchart, including both the core trial and the follow‐up. As a majority of participants received rituximab at a later time point, this allowed us to also explore the timing of treatment on long‐term outcomes. We thus categorized the participants into three groups: original RTX allocation (early RTX; n = 25), original placebo with switch to RTX at any time point after randomization but before the end of follow‐up (delayed RTX; n = 16), and original placebo not exposed to RTX (never RTX; n = 6). Analyses were focused on outcomes beyond the first 48 weeks of the RCT.

Outcome Definitions

To assess the overall effect over time considering the fluctuating nature of the disease, we adapted methods from the MGTX follow‐up study [22], using time‐weighted methods to ensure comparability across subjects with different follow‐up durations. Time‐weighted outcomes, including QMG scores, MG activities of daily living (MG‐ADL) scale, and daily prednisolone dose (mg) were based on the area under the curve calculations with the trapezoidal rule divided by the months from randomization to the last visit [22].

Rescue treatment was defined as prednisolone ≥ 40 mg, pulsed methylprednisolone or prednisone, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) cycles, or plasmapheresis. New episodes of exacerbation were defined as the need for rescue treatment ≥ 3 months from a preceding episode.

Information on severe infections, that is, requiring inpatient care, was collected through chart reviews.

To further assess MG disease severity, we adopted the Myasthenia Gravis Status and Treatment Intensity (MGSTI) grade developed by Hehir and colleagues [23], by also including rituximab [24]. Briefly, Grade 0 designated only symptomatic treatment; Grade 1, low‐dose monotherapy; Grade 2, low‐dose therapy with 2 drugs; Grade 3, 2 drugs, with at least 1 of those administered in high doses; and Grade 4, rescue treatment or hospitalisation, in the past year. A grade of ≤ 2 was considered low treatment intensity. Low‐dose therapy was defined as prednisolone, < 20 mg/d; tacrolimus, < 5 mg/d; cyclosporine, < 100 mg/d; azathioprine, < 150 mg/d; cyclophosphamide, < 200 mg/d; tocilizumab, < 162 mg/d; rituximab ≤ 500 mg. High‐dose therapy was defined as the use of immunotherapy treatment at a dose higher than the threshold.

Statistical Analysis

We assessed long‐term outcomes on disease activity, hospitalization, rescue treatment, prednisolone dose, and treatment intensity from 48 weeks after the randomization by initial treatment allocation and rituximab treatment timing, respectively. Time‐weighted outcomes (QMG, ADL, and daily prednisolone dose) were compared with the Student t‐test or linear regression. Time to first rescue treatment and hospitalization from 48 weeks from randomization was assessed using survival curves, hazard ratios (HR), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) with Cox proportional hazard models adjusted for age at onset, sex, and previous rescue treatment during the core study period. Time at risk was calculated from 48 weeks to the first rescue treatment or hospitalization, death, or the last visit. MGSTI grade was calculated on a yearly basis and compared with the Fisher exact test. The incidence rate ratio of rescue treatments was calculated using Poisson regression. Adjustment for multiple comparisons was not performed due to the limited sample size, which may imply a risk of false positive results.

Results

Characteristics of the Study Participants

A total of 47 participants were randomized to receive either RTX (n = 25) or placebo (n = 22) at randomization; 9 had early‐onset MG (EOMG; AChR+ and onset age < 50), 36 were late‐onset MG (LOMG; AChR+ and onset age ≥ 50), and the remaining 2 were classified as seronegative (Figure 1). Since one participant died shortly after randomization, 46 individuals followed for up to 7.6 years (mean ± SD: 4.9 ± 1.6 years) were included here. Sixteen participants of the placebo arm (72.7%) received rituximab open label at some point (delayed RTX), at an average of 7 ± 2.9 months (min‐max: 3.3–13.7 months) from randomization for a first dose. A majority of those starting RTX (75.0%) received more than one treatment cycle, that is, a single infusion of 500 mg rituximab, while 8 participants randomized to the active arm and 2 in delayed RTX only received one dose of RTX. The timing of events at the individual level is illustrated in Figure 1.

Fourteen participants of the early RTX group discontinued further treatment, of which 13 (54.2%) were for stable disease, one (4.2%) due to lack of effect, and none for adverse events. Three participants in each treatment arm (12.5% and 13.6%, respectively, all exposed to rituximab) suffered a severe infection at some point during the follow‐up period, with an average time from the most recent RTX infusion to infection of 9 ± 0.5 months. Eight participants underwent thymectomy, six in the placebo group (four with thymus hyperplasia, one normal/atrophy, and one thymoma; average months from randomization of 10.6 ± 4.6) and two in the RTX group (both hyperplasia; average months from randomization of 12.5 ± 4.7). Five participants died during the follow‐up (mean age 74.9 ± 10.2 years), with causality deemed unlikely to be MG‐ or treatment‐related, except for one unvaccinated male in the early RTX group with additional risk factors who died from respiratory failure with COVID‐19 infection.

Long‐Term Outcome by Initial Treatment Allocation (RTX vs. Placebo)

Time‐weighted mean QMG scores decreased from randomization to 60 months of follow‐up, where mean differences in QMG scores favored RTX over placebo at 12 (2.9, 95% CI: 0.9–4.9) and 24 months (2.6, 95% CI: 0.3–4.9), with a trend in the same direction remaining at later time points (36 months, 2.2, 95% CI: −0.3 to 4.5; 48 months, 1.9, 95% CI: −0.4 to 4.1; 60 months, 1.8, 95% CI: −0.1 to 3.7) (Figure 2 panel A, Table S1). A similar trend favoring RTX over placebo was evident also for mean ADL scores, but diminished faster for prednisolone doses (Figure 2 panels B and C, Table S1). The incidence rate of rescue treatment was 0.09 and 0.16/person‐year in the RTX arm compared to placebo corresponding to an incidence rate of ratio (IRR) 0.54 (95% CI: 0.25–1.18) (Table 1). Similarly, there were non‐significant trends favoring RTX over placebo for hospitalization risk (HR: 0.43; 95% CI: 0.13–1.43) and rescue treatment (HR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.20–2.14), after adjusting for age at onset, sex, and previous rescue treatment given during the core study period (Figure 3 panel A and B).

Long‐Term Outcome by Treatment Timing (Delayed vs. Early vs. Never RTX)

The delayed RTX group received more doses of RTX than the early RTX group, on average 5.9 ± 3.6 doses versus 3.6 ± 2.9 doses (p = 0.029, Table 1), with a median frequency of doses per year of 1.1 (IQR: 0.6–1.7) and 0.7 (IQR: 0.4–1.1) in the delayed RTX and early RTX groups (including the dose given at randomization), respectively. More participants in the delayed compared to the early RTX group received rescue treatments (50.0% vs. 25.0%; p = 0.176), other IST (31.3% vs. 16.7%; p = 0.441), and were hospitalized for MG worsening (56.3% vs. 20.8%, p = 0.021) during follow‐up. Four participants discontinued treatment in the delayed RTX group, of which two (12.5%) reported stable disease state and lack of effect, respectively. Just over a third (37.5%) of the patients in the delayed RTX group had received rescue within 3 months before the first RTX infusion. The 6 patients in the placebo arm who were never exposed to RTX were younger (average age of 53.5 ± 23.3 years), and only 2 ever received another IST (azathioprine; interrupted within 2 years for stable disease, and unknown reason, respectively).

Compared to the delayed RTX group, participants randomized to RTX from the start had significantly lower mean time‐weighted QMG scores during each year of follow‐up until 60 months (all p < 0.05), where the mean ADL score and prednisolone dose at 12 months, but not other time points, favored early vs. delayed RTX (Figure 2 panel D–F, Table S2). Early RTX was associated with a lower risk of hospitalization (HR: 0.24; 95% CI: 0.07–0.83), and with a trend also for less rescue treatment (HR: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.14–1.57), after adjusting for age at onset, sex, and previous rescue treatment given during the core study (Figure 3 panel C and D). Moreover, MGSTI grades were higher in delayed compared to the early RTX group at 24 and 36 months, with a majority of the delayed RTX group being in grade 1 (37.5% and 56.2%) or grade 4 (37.5% and 31.2%) whereas a majority of the early RTX were in grade 0 (29.2% and 41.7%) or grade 1 (37.5% and 29.2%) at 24 months (p = 0.020) and 36 months (p = 0.004), respectively (Figure 4 panel C, Table S4).

Importantly, the never RTX group had an incidence rate of rescue of only 0.06/person‐year compared with 0.09/person‐year and 0.19/person‐year in the early and delayed RTX groups, respectively, as well as a lower hospitalization risk (HR 0.08, 95% CI 0.01, 0.96).

Discussion

In this long‐term follow‐up of participants in the RINOMAX trial, we found that some of the differences between participants randomized to rituximab compared with those that received a placebo also remained beyond the first 48 weeks, although differences tended to diminish over time. Differences were more evident when comparing those receiving RTX from baseline with those who had a delayed treatment initiation, although this comparison should be interpreted with great caution given enrichment for more active disease in the latter group, since the remaining 6 participants (27%) who were never exposed to RTX displayed a mild disease trajectory despite minimal immune therapy exposure. It should also be noted that 9 (37.5%) participants of the early RTX group received only one RTX treatment cycle.

Nevertheless, a general observation is that we recorded a very low frequency of MG worsening, and no instances of MG crisis, although corticosteroid doses were low and other immune suppressive therapies remained infrequent. Thus, mean corticosteroid disease ranged from < 5 mg per day to just above 10 mg in the never RTX and delayed RTX groups, respectively, at the start of follow‐up, and further declining to about 5 mg per day at the end of follow‐up. Further, patients received a low‐dose RTX protocol with more extended dosing intervals than used with autoantibody‐mediated diseases of the central nervous system [25], also associated with a low absolute risk of hypogammaglobulinemia [26].

The RINOMAX core study demonstrated a significantly higher probability of achieving the primary outcome (QMG score ≤ 4, prednisolone ≤ 10 mg/day, and no rescue treatments Week 9–16) in the active arm compared to placebo [21]. The finding that some differences between randomization arms remained also at longer follow‐up is of interest as this strengthens the notion that B cell depletion early after disease onset may be associated with the long‐term disease trajectory. A recent phase 3 trial compared outcomes with inebilizumab, a CD19 targeting B cell‐depleting monoclonal, with placebo in a more heterogeneous MG population with longer disease duration and higher disease scores than the RINOMAX population [27]. Adjusted mean differences at 26 weeks were −1.9 and −2.5 score points lower with active treatment vs. placebo for MG‐ADL and QMG, respectively, while in RINOMAX the active arm displayed −3.2 and −4.4 lower MG‐ADL (Week 16) and QMG (Week 24) scores. Similarly, COVID‐19 infection presents as the most common adverse effect (~10%) in both control and treatment arms during the pandemic. Although statistical methods differed, there were also similarities, such as targeting a low corticosteroid dose and correcting for use of rescue therapy. Together with the data presented here, it can be speculated that B cell‐depleting therapies in MG are more efficacious early after disease onset compared with in states of refractory disease.

It is of interest to note that while close to one‐third of placebo patients met the primary outcome criteria at 16 weeks without the need for rescue treatments, several eventually required more intensive therapy. This suggests that, from a clinical benefit–risk perspective, exposure to more intense immune suppression and associated risks must be weighed against the fact that robust predictive markers of disease severity are still lacking. It should also be acknowledged that costs of rituximab, especially with the low dose protocol used here, are comparatively low compared to total healthcare costs in new‐onset disease [28].

The safety of rituximab is well described in other autoimmune conditions, in particular rheumatoid arthritis [29], but has been less explored in MG. We recorded six events of severe infections, that is, requiring in‐patient care, during follow‐up. We have previously recorded an approximately doubled relative risk of hospital‐treated infections with rituximab compared with platform therapies in multiple sclerosis (MS) [30, 31], but demographic factors that differ between MG and MS populations must also be considered. Thus, a distinct upwards deflection point for the risk of severe infection occurs at about 60 years of age in the general population, with additional augmentation of risk based on disability status in an MS cohort [32]. Severe infections included one death due to COVID‐19 in an unvaccinated individual with additional risk factors (male sex, obesity, cardiovascular disease; 241 days since last dose). Further, a severe case of TBE with remaining sequelae occurred in a younger, unvaccinated woman almost 18 months after a single dose of RTX, when B cell counts had normalized. Although causality in individual cases cannot be determined, it should be noted that infection risk associated with B cell depletion primarily concerns bacterial rather than viral infections, including zoster and Sars‐CoV‐2 [30, 31, 33]. Regardless, risk mitigation strategies including vaccination should be considered, especially for those who are older and/or with comorbidities [34].

This study has several limitations, most importantly that follow‐up after the first 48 weeks was not structured and relied on routine clinical follow‐up. However, the involved centers follow national guidelines for monitoring MG and the structure of the Swedish health care system allowed us to account for all included participants. Further, the Swedish MG registry represents a versatile tool that facilitates aligned prospective follow‐up in clinical routine. Nevertheless, the lack of systematically timed QMG and MG‐ADL assessments over time means that disease activity must be interpreted with caution. We also did not have access to systematically recorded adverse event data, but limited safety reporting to infections necessitating hospitalization and causes of death. The use of time‐weighted mean QMG scores means that there will be inertia in how scores can change later. However, this also provides a reflection of how disease scores evolve during the entire disease course observed since randomization. As mentioned previously for the comparison between early and late RTX groups, the latter group will be enriched for patients with more active disease. However, although less pronounced, differences in MG disease activity also existed between the original treatment allocations, and for the purpose of transparency, it is relevant to show outcomes for this particular group. This also highlights the need for better predictive markers of disease severity that can be used to identify milder forms of MG. Given the sample size, some of the resulting sub‐groups were small and in particular the never RTX group (n = 6), resulting in some estimations with wide confidence intervals, meaning that findings must be cautiously interpreted. Further, the observational nature of the open‐label phase is prone to indication bias. It is therefore important to contrast the results from the intention‐to‐treat and per‐protocol analyses.

In conclusion, we observed a beneficial disease trajectory with few instances of hospital admissions due to MG worsening and the need for rescue treatments despite low immune therapy exposure apart from rituximab. This indicates that early exposure to B cell‐depleting therapies may be associated with better long‐term outcomes. Further studies are needed to shed light on the long‐term benefit–risk balance with RTX in generalized MG, as well as to define predictive markers for disease severity early in the disease course.
