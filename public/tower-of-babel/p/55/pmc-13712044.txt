Porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus hijacks the non-canonical enzymatic function of PHGDH to arrest autophagic flux for viral replication
Authors: Zhangping Yu, Qiongqiong Zhou, Peng Gao, Yongning Zhang, Xinna Ge, Jun Han, Xin Guo, Lei Zhou, Hanchun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTViruses frequently hijack host metabolic enzymes to fuel replication. However, the mechanisms underlying this hijacking and utilization of metabolic enzymes remain poorly understood. In this study, we report a sophisticated mechanism by which porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) exploits a non-canonical enzymatic function of PHGDH (phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase) to modulate macroautophagy/autophagy. We demonstrate that PRRSV infection recruits the transcription factor ZNF143 (zinc finger protein 143) to transcriptionally repress PHGDH expression. Importantly, the antiviral restriction activity of PHGDH is entirely uncoupled from its canonical enzymatic role in serine biosynthesis. Mechanistically, PHGDH depletion triggers the initiation of autophagy via the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-ULK1 (unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1) signaling axis; however, it paradoxically arrests autophagic flux at the autophagosome-lysosome fusion stage. PHGDH is identified as a critical scaffold that facilitates the assembly of the autophagic soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) complex; its downregulation disrupts the interaction between STX17 (syntaxin 17) and SNAP29 (synaptosome associated protein 29), thereby blocking autophagosome-lysosome fusion. This induction of incomplete autophagy creates a favorable cytosolic niche for viral replication. Furthermore, the antiviral effect of PHGDH is also observed in two other swine pathogens, porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) and pseudorabies virus (PRV). Collectively, these findings revealed that viruses weaponized the moonlighting function of a metabolic enzyme to dismantle autophagic flux, highlighting PHGDH as a broad-spectrum antiviral target that bridged metabolism and membrane trafficking.Abbreviation: AMPK: AMP-activated protein kinase; BECN1: beclin 1; CQ: chloroquine; MAP1LC3/LC3: microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3; MOI: multiplicity of infection; PEDV: porcine epidemic diarrhea virus; PHGDH: phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase; PRV: pseudorabies virus; PRRSV: porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus; SGOC: serine-glycine-one-carbon; siRNA: small interfering RNA; SNAP29: synaptosome associated protein 29; SNARE: soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor; SQSTM1/p62: sequestosome 1; SSP: serine synthesis pathway; STX17: syntaxin 17; ULK1: unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1; VAMP8: vesicle associated membrane protein 8; ZNF143: zinc finger protein 143.

Introduction

Metabolic enzymes are indispensable for cell survival and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis, playing critical roles across a wide spectrum of metabolic pathways [1,2]. As obligate intracellular pathogens, viruses strictly rely on host cell metabolism to provide them with the energy and molecular precursors essential for progeny production [3–5]. To maximize progeny virion production, they have evolved multiple strategies to hijack cellular metabolic enzymes; for example, viruses can regulate the expression or alter the activity of metabolic enzymes [6,7]. Uncovering the mechanisms by which viruses hijack cellular metabolic enzymes to enhance replication represents an effective avenue to identify potential targets for antiviral interventions.

The serine-glycine-one-carbon (SGOC) metabolic enzymes function as sophisticated regulatory nodes within the cellular network, forming a pivotal cytosolic-mitochondrial axis that facilitates the conversion of 3-phosphoglycerate into serine, glycine, and folate-bound one-carbon units, thereby sustaining biosynthesis, methylation, and redox homeostasis [8–10]. PHGDH (phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase), the rate-limiting SGOC enzyme of de novo serine synthesis, serves as a gatekeeper for SGOC metabolic flux [11,12]. As an important regulatory node within SGOC networks, PHGDH may be frequently exploited by viruses to optimize cellular environments for viral replication. While research on the interplay between PHGDH and viral infection is still emerging, it has mainly focused on PHGDH’s enzymatic function that modulates host metabolic flux. For instance, Sendai virus/SeV, vesicular stomatitis virus/VSV, and herpes simplex virus 1/HSV-1 suppress PHGDH transcription in mouse primary peritoneal macrophages, thereby impairing serine metabolism and enhancing innate immunity [13]. Similarly, classical swine fever virus/CSFV inhibits serine biosynthesis by promoting the deacetylation and degradation of PHGDH, which in turn dampens antiviral innate immunity [14].

While early studies focused on metabolic products – such as amino acids, nucleotides, and lipids – as raw materials for viral biosynthesis [3], a paradigm shift suggests that metabolic enzymes themselves function as critical regulators beyond their canonical catalysis [2,15,16]. Beyond its canonical metabolic role, PHGDH has been characterized as a critical oncogene with elevated expression across various tumors, highlighting its significance as a multi-functional scaffold or signaling protein in diverse biological contexts [17–23]. By modulating signal transduction and organellar homeostasis, this enzyme might reshape the cellular environment to either restrict or promote viral replication. This suggests that viruses may target metabolic enzymes not to manipulate metabolite pools, but to hijack cellular stress responses or vesicular trafficking pathways – such as autophagic flux – for optimal progeny production. Notably, PHGDH has been implicated in such non-canonical roles in cancer, including epigenetic regulation and cell signaling [23,24]. However, the precise mechanism by which these metabolism-independent functions of PHGDH influence viral replication and host-pathogen interactions remains to be fully elucidated.

Porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) is an enveloped, single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus belonging to the family Arteriviridae, order Nidovirales. Over the past decades, PRRSV has caused devastating reproductive failure in sows and severe respiratory disease in piglets, resulting in substantial economic losses to the global swine industry [25,26]. Notably, PRRSV has been shown to manipulate multiple host cellular pathways, including macroautophagy/autophagy, to create a favorable intracellular environment for its replication [27]. As a pathogen characterized by extreme genetic diversity and immune evasion, both of which have frustrated vaccine development for decades, PRRSV continues to compromise durable cross-protective efficacy through ongoing evolution, highlighting the urgent need for host-directed antiviral strategies [28,29].

Here, using PRRSV as a model, we unexpectedly discover that PHGDH regulates viral proliferation and replication through its non-canonical enzymatic function. By integrating transcriptomics, metabolomics, and chemical genetics, we reveal that PRRSV actively recruits the transcription factor ZNF143 to transcriptionally downregulate PHGDH. Crucially, we demonstrate that the resulting antiviral phenotype is mechanistically uncoupled from PHGDH’s canonical role in serine biosynthesis. Instead, PHGDH functions as an essential structural scaffold for the autophagic machinery: its depletion triggers an AMPK-ULK1-dependent initiation of autophagy but paradoxically arrests the process at the fusion stage by physically disrupting the assembly of the STX17-SNAP29-VAMP8 SNARE complex [30]. This blockade of autophagic flux – termed incomplete autophagy – creates a favorable cytosolic niche for viral replication. Importantly, we observe that PHGDH also exerts a significant inhibitory effect on two other swine pathogens, including a coronavirus (porcine epidemic diarrhea virus, PEDV) and a herpesvirus (pseudorabies virus, PRV).

Results

PRRSV engages in crosstalk with the SGOC metabolic pathways

To investigate the effect of PRRSV on SGOC metabolism, we conducted a metabolic KEGG analysis on PRRSV-infected MARC-145 cells and mock-infected controls. The results revealed that PRRSV infection led to metabolic pathway disorders, including disruptions in the glycine-serine-threonine metabolic pathway (Figure 1(A)). Quantitative analysis of amino acids revealed that the content of serine, an SGOC metabolite, decreased in infected cells (Figure S1). The levels of SGOC metabolites in PRRSV-infected cells were also measured by using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), revealing that PRRSV infection led to a decrease in the secretion levels of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) (Figure 1(B)). Transcriptome sequencing was then used to quantify the transcription of key SGOC-pathway genes in PRRSV-infected MARC-145 cells, covering de novo serine biosynthesis (PHGDH, PSAT1, and PSPH), cytoplasmic one-carbon metabolism (SHMT1, MTHFD1, and MTHFR) and mitochondrial one-carbon module (SHMT2, MTHFD2, MTHFD1L and MTHFD2L). The results showed that PRRSV infection can alter the transcription of genes related to SGOC metabolism (Figure 1(C)). RT-qPCR analysis further revealed that PRRSV infection led to the downregulation of transcription for serine biosynthesis genes (PHGDH, PSPH, and PSAT1), as well as for several genes involved in cytoplasmic and mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism (Figure 1(D,E)). To further delineate the crosstalk between PRRSV and one-carbon metabolism, systematic RNA interference/RNAi was used to screen for key SGOC metabolic enzymes related to PRRSV proliferation. In this primary screen, PHGDH knockdown reproducibly increased PRRSV N (nucleocapsid) and nsp9 (nonstructural protein 9) protein abundance, whereas knockdown of other SGOC enzymes either showed variable effects across independent siRNAs or had no detectable effect (Figure 1(F)). Therefore, we prioritized PHGDH for subsequent investigations. These findings indicated that PRRSV engaged in molecular crosstalk with SGOC metabolic pathways and suppressed serine metabolism. PRRSV infection inhibits the transcription and expression of the serine synthesis gene PHGDH

Both transcriptomic and RT-qPCR data above revealed PRRSV-induced downregulation of PHGDH gene transcription. To further characterize the kinetics of PHGDH transcription and expression during PRRSV infection, MARC-145 and porcine cell line PK-15CD163 (PK-15 cell lines that recombinantly express CD163 receptor) cells were infected with PRRSV at an MOI of 1 and harvested at the indicated times for RT-qPCR and western blot. The results showed that PRRSV infection significantly reduced PHGDH mRNA and protein expression at late time points (Figure 2(A-F)). Meanwhile, the extent of PHGDH protein reduction increased with multiplicity of infection (MOI) (Figure 2(G,H)). Additionally, the immunofluorescence assay also confirmed a reduction in PHGDH fluorescence signal in PRRSV-infected MARC-145 cells (Figure 2(I,J)). ZNF143 inhibits the transcription of PHGDH by binding to its promoter

Transcription factors can modulate promoter activity to influence gene transcription [31]. We hypothesized that certain factors might bind to the PHGDH gene promoter or regulatory regions in the cellular nucleus to downregulate its transcription. To identify candidate regulators, the 5’ flanking region (from −2,000 to −1 bp) encompassing the presumptive full-length promoter of PHGDH from PK-15CD163 cells was cloned into the pGL3-Enhancer vector immediately upstream of the luciferase ORF (luc+), with SV40 enhancer located downstream of luc+. Meanwhile, three plasmids with truncated versions of the putative promoter were also constructed. Promoter activity was evaluated by measuring luciferase activity after transfection into PK-15CD163 cells. The pGL3-Control vector containing both SV40 promoter and enhancer sequences, served as a positive control. Fragments spanning −2,000 to −1, −1,000 to −1, and −500 to −1 bp upstream of the PHGDH promoter all drove luciferase expression (Figure 3(A,B)), and the −1,000 to −1 fragment exhibited the highest promoter activity. To ensure that the region containing the regulatory molecule-binding sites is also included, the activity of these promoter regions was assessed in PRRSV-infected cells. As shown in Figure 3(C), luciferase activity was lower in PRRSV-infected cells than in controls, mirroring the virus-induced reduction in endogenous PHGDH and mapping the response element. To identify candidate transcriptional regulators, biotinylated PHGDH promoter probes were incubated with nuclear extracts from PRRSV- or mock-infected cells, and DNA affinity-isolated products were analyzed by mass spectrometry (Figure S2A-C). Proteins exhibiting differential LFQ intensities between PRRSV- and mock-treated samples, together with their annotated transcriptional functions, were shortlisted for validation (Figure 3(D)). Next, we tested whether these candidates modulated PHGDH transcription. SiRNA knockdown of NRF1 and ZNF143 significantly elevated PHGDH promoter activity (Figure 3(E)), yet only ZNF629 or ZNF143 depletion increased PHGDH protein in PRRSV-infected cells (Figure S2D). As ZNF629 knockdown was inefficient (Figure S2E), NRF1 and ZNF143 were selected for follow-up studies. In mock-infected cells, ZNF143 depletion significantly upregulated PHGDH protein expression, whereas NRF1 knockdown had no effect (Figure 3(F)). Conversely, ZNF143 overexpression markedly suppressed PHGDH mRNA and protein, while NRF1 overexpression did not (Figure 3(G-K)). Transcript analysis revealed PRRSV-specific induction of ZNF143, whereas NRF1 levels remained unchanged (Figure 3(L)). Subcellular fractionation with a nuclear – cytoplasmic extraction kit showed that PRRSV infection increased the nuclear pool of ZNF143 and NRF1 (Figure 3(M)). In addition, we also tested whether any PRRSV structural/non-structural protein affected PHGDH expression. As shown in Figure S2F, overexression of individual viral proteins in MARC-145 cells did not induce significant PHGDH downregulation, indicating that the observed reduction was not directly caused by viral proteins. Collectively, these results suggest that PRRSV infection promotes the upregulation and nuclear translocation of ZNF143, mediating its binding to the PHGDH promoter and thereby inhibiting PHGDH expression.

PHGDH is a host restriction factor that antagonizes PRRSV proliferation

Having identified PHGDH as an anti-PRRSV hit in the siRNA screening above, its role was further validated. MARC-145 cells were transfected with PHGDH-targeting siRNA (siPHGDH) and infected with PRRSV (MOI = 0.5) for 0, 6, 12 or 24 h. Viral mRNA (N and nsp2 genes), N protein, viral titers and immunofluorescence signal of PRRSV all indicated PHGDH knockdown significantly increased PRRSV proliferation, with almost 10 times higher titers at all time points (Figure 4(A-F)). This effect was consistent across varied MOIs and in the porcine-derived cell line PK-15CD163 as well (Figure S3A and S3C). Accordingly, PHGDH overexpression suppressed viral replication in MARC-145 cells (Figure 4(G-L)), and the rescue experiment further supported a restrictive role for PHGDH (Figure S3D). Notably, transient PHGDH knockdown did not significantly reduce MARC-145 cell viability during the experimental window (Figure S3B), indicating that the proviral phenotype was not a secondary consequence of overt cytotoxicity. PHGDH is involved in PRRSV genome replication and release but has no effect on viral attachment, internalization

To clarify which stage of PRRSV proliferation was affected by PHGDH, the effect of PHGDH overexpression or knockdown on the viral attachment, internalization, replication, and release steps during PRRSV infection of MARC-145 cells was analyzed. MARC-145 cells were transfected with PHGDH overexpression plasmid or PHGDH-targeting siRNA at the indicated time points, infected with PRRSV as outlined in Figure 5(A,D,G,J), and then subjected to quantification of the virus by detecting the mRNA of PRRSV N or nsp2 genes with RT-qPCR. The expression of PHGDH was successfully changed as expected (Figure 5(C,F,I,L)). Then RT-qPCR analysis indicated that PHGDH overexpression and knockdown caused no significant change in viral attachment and internalization (Figure 5(B,E)). Consistent with the results above, PHGDH inhibited the replication phase of PRRSV (Figure 5(H)). The role of PHGDH in PRRSV release was assessed by comparing supernatant and intracellular viral mRNA levels. Results showed that the mRNA levels of PRRSV N or nsp2 genes were significantly suppressed when cells overexpressed PHGDH, whereas PHGDH knockdown significantly increased viral mRNA levels (Figure 5(K)). PRRSV, as a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus, undergoes a genomic replication process consisting of the synthesis of both positive- and negative-strand RNAs, as well as the synthesis of genomic RNA (gRNA) and sgmRNAs (sgmRNAs). As shown in Figure S4A-F, reduced PHGDH expression affected all RNA species regardless of the strand type. In conclusion, these results suggested that PHGDH inhibited PRRSV infection by inhibiting viral replication and release but had no effect on the attachment or internalization steps of the viral replication cycle. To further explore whether the SSP enzymatic activity of PHGDH was related to regulating virus replication, we constructed two PHGDH variants (PHGDHR236E and PHGDHV425M mutants), which were previously reported to exhibit nearly undetectable enzymatic activity [32]. Consistent with wild-type PHGDH, both mutants still significantly suppressed PRRSV replication (Figure 6(D-F)). Furthermore, CBR-5884 and NCT-503, the selective small molecule enzyme inhibitors of PHGDH, also had no significant effect on PRRSV proliferation (Figure 6(H-J)). Notably, these inhibitors did not reduce cell viability at the working concentration of 5 μM (Figure 6(G)). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PHGDH-mediated inhibition of PRRSV replication is independent of canonical serine biosynthesis enzyme activity.

As MAP1LC3/LC3 (microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3)-II is commonly used as a marker of autophagosome formation [33], we examined whether PHGDH deficiency induced LC3 puncta by ectopically expressing GFP-LC3 in cells. As shown in Figure 7(D-F), PHGDH knockdown treatment upregulated LC3-II and SQSTM1/p62 levels compared to control cells. To elucidate the specific mechanism by which PHGDH influences cellular autophagy, we first investigated the interaction between PHGDH and LC3. As shown in Figure S6D, co-immunoprecipitation assays revealed that PHGDH failed to co-precipitate LC3, indicating that PHGDH does not regulate autophagy through direct interaction with LC3. Redox imbalance plays a key role in driving autophagy, and ROS and RNS produced by oxidative stress are upstream autophagy modulators [34]. Therefore, we investigated whether downregulation of PHGDH could still activate autophagy when oxidative stress was blocked. Several oxidative stress inhibitors, including N-acetylcysteine (NAC), mitoTEMPO (MT), and GSH, were added but failed to restore the enhanced autophagy mediated by PHGDH knockdown (Figure 7(I-K) and S6E), suggesting that cellular autophagy mediated by PHGDH downregulation was independent of oxidative stress. To understand the mechanism by which PHGDH downregulation activated cellular autophagy, we analyzed the expression of regulatory factors in the autophagy pathway. In PRRSV-infected MARC-145 cells, PHGDH knockdown increased p-AKT levels, but there was no significant effect on downstream p-MTOR levels. In addition, p-AMPK, the positive regulator of autophagy, and its downstream factors p-ULK1 and p-BECN1 (beclin 1, autophagy related), were also increased, but the level of one of the downstream targets p-TSC2 was significantly decreased (Figure 7(L)). Our results were almost consistent with the mechanism of PHGDH regulation of autophagy in cancer cells [22,35]. Moreover, it was directly tested whether the AMPK-ULK1-BECN1 signaling module contributes to the proviral phenotype caused by PHGDH depletion. MARC-145 cells were co-transfected with siPHGDH together with siPRKAA/AMPK, siULK1, or siBECN1, followed by PRRSV infection. RT-qPCR and western blot analyses showed that co-transfection with siPRKAA or siULK1 markedly reversed the PHGDH knockdown-induced increase in PRRSV mRNA levels and viral N protein abundance. In contrast, co-transfection with siBECN1 did not fully abolish the proviral effect of PHGDH depletion, as PHGDH knockdown still promoted PRRSV replication under BECN1-deficient conditions (Figure S6F-N). Collectively, these results suggested that PHGDH downregulation-induced autophagy was regulated by the AMPK-ULK1 signaling axis.

PHGDH depletion triggers incomplete autophagy by disrupting SNARE complex formation

Next, we investigated the type of autophagy mediated by PHGDH knockdown. Previous studies have shown that PHGDH localizes to the inner mitochondrial membrane, promoting the growth of hepatocellular carcinoma by enhancing mitochondrial translation and respiratory metabolism [36]. Therefore, we hypothesized that PHGDH downregulation could activate mitochondrial autophagy. Immunofluorescence assays showed that mitochondrial markers did not colocalize with GFP-LC3 in the PHGDH knockdown cells (Figure S7A). In addition, we treated PHGDH-knockdown and control cells with mitochondrial division inhibitor 1 (Mdivi-1), a specific inhibitor of DNM1L/DRP1 (dynamin 1 like) that potently inhibits mitochondrial division/mitophagy [37]. Since Mdivi-1 treatment failed to reverse the PHGDH knockdown-induced accumulation of LC3-II and SQSTM1/p62 (Figure S7B), and did not attenuate the resultant increase in viral replication (Figure S7C), these data indicated that the proviral phenotype conferred by PHGDH depletion was independent of DRP1-driven mitochondrial fission and, by extension, mitophagy.

Autophagosome accumulation occurs when autophagic flux is induced or autophagosome degradation is blocked [38]. We found that PHGDH knockdown increased both SQSTM1/p62 and LC3-II protein levels (Figure 7D-F), which is a signal of blocked autophagic flux. To investigate the mechanism of autophagosome accumulation induced by PHGDH downregulation, MARC-145 cells were transfected with siPHGDH, followed by chloroquine (CQ; 10 μM) treatment, which induces autophagosome accumulation by impairing autophagosome degradation. As shown in Figure 8A-D, compared with the control group, PHGDH knockdown increased LC3-II levels in the absence of CQ, but LC3-II levels remained unchanged after PHGDH knockdown in the presence of CQ, suggesting that downregulation of PHGDH led to blockage of autophagosome-lysosome fusion, which in turn inhibited autophagosome degradation. Next, we further sought evidence that PHGDH downregulation affects autolysosome formation. The tandem mCherry-EGFP-LC3 fluorescent protein system is commonly used to monitor autophagic flux. GFP signal tends to be quenched in acidic and proteolytic lysosomal cavities, whereas mCherry is more stable. Therefore, red LC3 dots (positive mCherry signal and negative GFP signal) indicate autolysosome formation, and yellow LC3 dots (positive for both mCherry and GFP signals) indicate impaired fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes [39]. As shown in Figure 8(E,F), mCherry and GFP signal intensities were almost identical in the PHGDH knockdown cells. Furthermore, PHGDH knockdown decreased, whereas PHGDH overexpression increased the ratio of cleaved RFP:RFP compared to the control group (Figure S7D). In mammals, the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes is mainly mediated by the autophagic SNARE complex (STX17-SNAP29-VAMP8) [30]. We hypothesized that PHGDH interacts with SNARE complex proteins, which in turn mediates the blockage of autolysosome formation. As shown in Figure 8(G-K), PHGDH interacted with SNAP29 but not STX17 or VAMP8. Next, we investigated the effect of PHGDH on STX17-SNAP29-VAMP8 formation. After transfection of interfering RNA targeting PHGDH into HEK293T cells for 12 h, the cells were co-transfected with plasmids expressing STX17, SNAP29, and VAMP8 for immunoprecipitation analysis. As shown in Figure 8L,M, the level of STX17 co-precipitated with SNAP29 was significantly reduced after knocking down PHGDH, but the interaction between SNAP29 and VAMP8 remained unchanged. These data suggest that the downregulation of PHGDH disrupts the interaction between STX17 and SNAP29, thereby disrupting the assembly of the SNARE complex and impairing autolysosome formation (Figure 8(N)).

PHGDH also inhibits the replication of PEDV and PRV

To determine the generality of PHGDH-mediated antiviral activity, we extended our investigation to porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV), an RNA virus, and pseudorabies virus (PRV), a DNA virus. Consistent with our findings in PRRSV, infection with either PEDV or PRV resulted in the suppression of PHGDH expression (Figure 9(A,E)). Functional assays further revealed that PHGDH knockdown significantly enhanced viral replication, as evidenced by increased levels of PEDV N and PRV gB proteins (Figure 9(B,F)), as well as elevated viral titers (Figure 9(D,H)). Conversely, ectopic expression of PHGDH potently restricted the propagation of both viruses (Figure 9(C,D,G,H)). Collectively, these data indicate that PHGDH abundance was inversely associated with PEDV and PRV replication in exploratory experiments. Moreover, the ability of PHGDH to suppress the replication of PRRSV, PEDV, and PRV further suggests that it has broad-spectrum antiviral activity. Discussion

Emerging evidence suggests that viruses exploit metabolic enzymes not merely for their biosynthetic products, but fundamentally for their structural roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis [14,40–42]. In this study, we unveiled a sophisticated strategy employed by PRRSV to hijack the non-canonical enzymatic function of PHGDH. Mechanistically, PRRSV recruits ZNF143 to transcriptionally repress PHGDH, thereby promoting a cellular state characterized by AMPK- and ULK1-dependent autophagy initiation together with impaired autophagosome-lysosome fusion caused by defective SNARE complex assembly (Figure 10). This dual remodeling of the autophagy pathway favors viral replication. Unlike the classical view where metabolic enzymes are targeted to manipulate metabolite pools, these data reveal another antiviral function of PHGDH, that is entirely distinct from its canonical role in the serine biosynthesis pathway. A critical distinction in these findings is the complete uncoupling of PHGDH’s antiviral potency from its catalytic activity. Neither nutrient supplementation (serine/glycine) nor pharmacological inhibition of PHGDH enzymatic activity could mimic the proviral phenotype caused by PHGDH depletion. Furthermore, enzymatically inactive PHGDH mutants retained their ability to restrict viral replication as effectively as the wild type. Additionally, as indicated in Figure 1(F), while the knockdown of PHGDH significantly enhanced PRRSV proliferation, the depletion of downstream enzymes in the serine biosynthesis pathway, such as PSAT1 and PSPH, did not result in a similar effect, suggesting that the inhibition of PRRSV is not mediated through the direction of this metabolic flux. These lines of evidence robustly confirm that PHGDH operates as a “moonlighting” protein, where its physical presence – rather than its metabolic output – is the limiting factor for PRRSV propagation. This interpretation is in line with the growing concept that metabolic enzymes can exert important biological effects through regulatory functions independent of their catalytic activity [2,15,16].

Autophagy is a complicated catabolic process, in which nonessential organelles or proteins are degraded, and involves autophagosome formation [43]. For complete autophagy, autophagosomes fuse with lysosomes to form autolysosomes that degrade cargo. If autophagosome-lysosome fusion is blocked, reducing degradation efficiency and causing autophagosome accumulation, this condition termed incomplete autophagy [44,45]. It has been reported that PRRSV enhances viral replication by inducing complete autophagy in the early stages of infection and subsequently transitioning to incomplete autophagy in the later stages by preventing the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes [46,47]. In this study, we identified PHGDH as an indispensable structural scaffold that facilitates autophagosome-lysosome fusion. PHGDH depletion generated a dual phenotype: it activated autophagy initiation through the AMPK-ULK1 axis, yet simultaneously disrupted STX17-SNAP29 interaction and thereby impaired SNARE complex-dependent autolysosome formation. Thus, PHGDH depletion appears to reprogram autophagy in a stage-specific manner, promoting autophagosome formation but impairing downstream autolysosome maturation. Such incomplete autophagy may provide a favorable intracellular environment for PRRSV replication by allowing membrane intermediates to accumulate while delaying lysosomal clearance. While the downstream defect in autolysosome formation can be reasonably explained by impaired SNARE complex assembly, how PHGDH loss triggers the upstream activation of AMPK remains less clear. One plausible explanation is that acute loss of the PHGDH scaffold creates localized bioenergetic or metabolite-sensing stress at membrane-trafficking sites, which is then detected by AMPK; alternatively, disruption of PHGDH-containing protein complexes may indirectly trigger an upstream stress response. Additionally, the SNARE data also raise a structural question. Because the catalytic mutants R236E and V425M retained antiviral activity, the SNAP29-binding interface is unlikely to depend strictly on active-site chemistry. We speculate that SNAP29 recognition may involve a surface outside the catalytic pocket, potentially within the C-terminal regulatory region of PHGDH or a composite interface formed by multiple subdomains, a possibility that should be tested in future truncation and structural studies.

The hypothesis that the massive accumulation of autophagic membranes in PHGDH-depleted cells creates a permissive platform for PRRSV replication currently serves as a promising working model. To fully substantiate this model, future research should determine whether these accumulated LC3-positive membranes directly support viral replication complex assembly, genome synthesis, or virion production, which could be examined by colocalization studies of viral dsRNA or nonstructural proteins with LC3-positive puncta. In addition, although exploratory experiments indicated that PEDV and PRV also respond to PHGDH manipulation, further investigations are needed to determine whether these viruses exploit PHGDH through the same mechanism as PRRSV. Another important future direction involves determining whether post-translational modifications of PHGDH or other SGOC enzymes (including acetylation, methylation, succinylation, or crotonylation) regulate their scaffold functions during infection. Such work will help clarify whether virus-induced remodeling of SGOC enzymes converges on catalytic control, scaffold disruption, or both.

In summary, our study establishes PHGDH as a critical host restriction factor that functions independently of its metabolic activity. By depleting or dismantling the PHGDH scaffold, PRRSV induces incomplete autophagy or a blockade of autophagic flux that is favorable for viral replication. These findings not only highlighted the complexity of host-pathogen interactions but also reshaped the therapeutic implications of PHGDH biology. Since the proviral state depends on loss of the PHGDH protein rather than inhibition of its catalytic activity, classical PHGDH enzyme inhibitors such as CBR-5884 or NCT-503 are unlikely to be effective antivirals in this context. More promising host-directed strategies may include blocking ZNF143-mediated transcriptional repression to restore endogenous PHGDH expression, stabilizing the remaining PHGDH protein with small molecules or chaperone-like compounds, designing peptides or protein mimetics that reconstitute the PHGDH scaffold and promote productive STX17-SNAP29 engagement, and restoring autophagosome-lysosome fusion downstream of PHGDH. Together, these results suggest that therapeutic approaches aimed at preserving the non-canonical enzymatic function of PHGDH or reactivating autophagic flux could provide protection against PRRSV and potentially other economically important swine viruses.

Cells and virus

MARC-145 (Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences, SCSP-5510), HEK293T (ATCC, CRL-11268), Vero (Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences, SCSP-520), and PK-15CD163 (stored in our laboratory) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, 12,800–058) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, 16,140,071), penicillin (100 IU/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) (Invitrogen, 15,140,122) at 37°C under a humid 5% CO2 atmosphere. The 8th passage virus of HP-PRRSV strain JXwn06 (GenBank accession number: EF641008) [48], PEDV strain SDLY (GenBank accession number: OL762458.1) and PRV strain HB1201 (GenBank accession number: KU057086.1) were isolated from swine farms in our laboratory. Viral titers were determined as the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) using the Reed-Muench method as previously described [49].

Virus replication assay

To further investigate the role of PHGDH in PRRSV replication, the MARC-145 cells cultured in 12-well plates were transfected with pCMV-Flag-PHGDH plasmids or siPHGDH-1 for the required duration, and then inoculated with PRRSV at an MOI of 0.5. After the specified time point, samples were subjected to freeze-thawing three times. Cells and the cell culture medium were harvested for virus titration, expressed as lgTCID50/ml, according to the Reed-Muench endpoint method. Additionally, mRNA levels of PRRSV nsp2 or N genes were determined by RT-qPCR. The expression of the PRRSV N protein in cells was detected by immunoblotting analysis as previously reported [51].

Luciferase reporter assay

The full-length of the potential promoter of pig PHGDH (2,000 bp, Chr4: 101,478,145–101,480,144, namely −2,000 ~ to − 1 upstream of the start codon, and the first base of start codon ATG is identified as +1), as well as their different truncated fragments, were cloned into the pGL3-Enhancer vector (Promega, E1771). For cell transfection, PK-15CD163 cells cultured in 12-well plates were co-transfected with PHGDH promoter reporter plasmid (PHGDH-luc) and pRL-TK plasmid, which is an internal control for normalization of the transfection efficiency. Cells were infected with PRRSV at 24 h post-transfection, and whole-cell lysates were harvested at 48 h post-infection. Next, firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were evaluated using a dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega, E1910) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data from three independently conducted experiments are shown as relative firefly luciferase activity normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

DNA affinity-isolation assay

DNA affinity-isolation assay was carried out as previously described [52]. Briefly, PK-15CD163 cells were cultured in 100-mm Petri dishes and infected with PRRSV or mock-infected for 24 h. The nuclear protein was extracted by using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, P0028) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Double-stranded DNA probes were amplified from pGL3-Enhancer-PHGDH-1,000–1 plasmid (synthesized by TsingKe Biotechnology) by the 5’-biotin labeled reverse primer, respectively. DNA gel electrophoresis was employed to separate the probe from any primer dimers, and the band was cut out and purified by E.Z.N.A.® Gel Extraction Kit (Omega Bio-tek, D2500) following the manufacturer’s instruction. The biotin-labeled PHGDH gene promoter (fragment 1000–1) was amplified by PCR as shown in Figure S2C. Next, the biotin-labeled PHGDH gene promoter DNA probe was incubated with nuclear proteins. After protein elution, mass spectrometry (MS) analysis was performed to identify the protein components bound to the promoter.

Western blot analyses

The treated cells were rinsed three times with PBS (Solarbio, P1020) and then lysed with cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013). The cell lysate was mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer (Epizyme, LT101), resolved on 12.5% acrylamide SDS-PAGE gels (Epizyme, PG213), and electroblotted onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline (Solarbio, T1083) containing 0.05% Tween 20 (Solarbio, T8220; TBST) for 2 h and incubated with the indicated antibodies at 4°C overnight. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (ZSGB-BIO, ZB-2301) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (ZSGB-BIO, ZB-2305) at room temperature for 1 h. Signals were visualized using a ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (Bio-Rad) and quantified using ImageJ software. Representative densitometric values shown in the manuscript were derived from at least three independent experiments where indicated.

Coimmunoprecipitation assay

For the Co-IP assay, the cell extracts were prepared after transfection or PRRSV infection. Part of the supernatant was used as a control, and the remaining supernatant was incubated with magnetic agarose at 4°C overnight on a rotating shaker. The supernatant was then discarded and washed with lysis buffer. Magnetic agarose was washed with the above lysis buffer 6 times for 5 min each. Subsequently, the immunoprecipitates were eluted with 1× SDS loading buffer and analyzed by western blot.

Cell viability measurement

Cell viability was determined by using the cell counting Kit-8 (Boster, AR1160) according to the manufacturer’s protocols [53].

Immunofluorescence and imaging

Before immunofluorescence, MARC-145 cells were inoculated in a 12-well plate containing a cover glass and were transfected with the specified plasmids or siRNA, or infected with PRRSV. Then, the original medium was discarded after the specified time point and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at room temperature for 15 min. Next, the cells were infiltrated with 1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Solarbio, A8020) in PBS and incubated with the corresponding primary antibody diluted in BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Then, the cells were incubated with the appropriate fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, DNA was stained with DAPI, and the covered slides were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon A1). The images were processed by NIS-Elements Viewer software. The fluorescence intensity profile of images was quantified using ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscopy assay

Transmission electron microscopy assay was conducted as previously described [54]. After being washed three times with pre-cooled PBS, the cells were scraped and centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min. The cell pellets were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight at 4°C, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 h at room temperature, and thoroughly rinsed with distilled H2O prior to staining with 1% aqueous uranyl acetate for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series and embedded in Eponate 12 resin (Ted Pella, 18,010). Ultrathin sections (70–80 nm in thickness) were cut with an ultramicrotome. After staining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, the sections were observed using an HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software). All data were analyzed using two-tailed Student’s t-test, multiple t-tests, or one-way ANOVA. The results were presented as the mean values ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments, and P value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p< 0.05, **p< 0.01, ***p< 0.001).
