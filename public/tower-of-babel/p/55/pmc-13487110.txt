Numerical investigation of influence of nose part shape of a high-speed surface transport on aerodynamic characteristics
Authors: Mykhailo Arseniuk, Unai Fernandez-Gamiz, Dmytro Redchyts, Svitlana Moiseienko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a comprehensive numerical investigation of the influence of the nose geometry of a high-speed surface transport (HSST) on its aerodynamic performance. The study focuses on the role of longitudinal vortex structures generated by non-streamlined nose shapes and their impact on drag, lift, and pitching moment. The unsteady three-dimensional Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (URANS) equations, closed with the k-ω SST turbulence model, are employed to simulate the flow. The numerical methodology is based on the control volume approach with an implicit scheme, solved using the GMRES method with ILU preconditioning. A series of parametric studies is conducted to evaluate the effects of the nose length, width, and height relative to the track structure. The results demonstrate that the formation and evolution of longitudinal vortices significantly influence the aerodynamic characteristics of the vehicle. It is shown that increasing the nose length and height leads to a reduction in aerodynamic drag due to the weakening of transverse flow separation and more stable vortex structures in the wake. Conversely, increasing the nose width intensifies flow separation and enlarges the turbulent wake, resulting in higher drag coefficients. The study also reveals that, regardless of the geometric configuration, the HSST experiences a negative lift force, contributing to improved stability by pressing the vehicle toward the track, and a positive pitching moment. Variations in geometric parameters affect the magnitude of these forces through changes in pressure distribution and vortex intensity. In particular, the location and strength of low-pressure regions under the vehicle play a key role in determining lift and moment characteristics. The findings highlight the potential of controlled vortex generation as an effective approach to aerodynamic optimization. The developed numerical methodology and obtained results can be applied to the design and optimization of high-speed transport systems, including magnetically levitated vehicles.Keywords: high-speed surface transport, aerodynamics, Navier-Stokes equation, control volume method, aerodynamic coefficients, vortex effects

1. Introduction

In the modern world, there are many different means of transportation, such as airplanes, cars, ships, and trains. For intercity travel over medium distances, it is easier and more profitable to use rail transport due to its accessibility and relative cheapness. In addition, among the above-mentioned modes of transportation, the train is the safest. Due to the accelerating pace of modern life, it is becoming increasingly important to speed up travel. In this regard, the development of modern technologies makes it possible to put into operation high-speed trains that can combine the convenience and accessibility of road or classic rail transport with speeds comparable to air travel.

As train speeds increase, aerodynamics becomes one of the key components of overall drag. Accordingly, research and improvement of aerodynamics will increase the efficiency, environmental friendliness, safety and speed of transportation. Modern high-speed trains are typically streamlined to minimize aerodynamic drag. However, overcoming aerodynamic drag still consumes about 40-50% of the energy supplied to the traction motors 1 for a typical high-speed train.

The aerodynamics of high-speed trains began to receive considerable attention more than three decades ago. Researchers all over the world have made attempts to improve train aerodynamics in order to reduce aerodynamic drag and increase the stability of train movement at high speeds and under lateral wind loads. Various methods have been used as research tools, starting with full-scale experiments, wind tunnel simulations on scale models, and numerical simulation based on the Navier-Stokes equations.

An alternative to the wheel-and-rail suspension system is magnetic suspension, which has been used in practice for the past few decades. When using magnetic suspension, there is no physical contact of the train with the track structure and, accordingly, no additional resistance to movement is created, and the increased requirements for the accuracy of the suspension and the principle of operation ensure minimal vibrations during movement.

Several methods are used to study vehicle aerodynamics: experimental (full-scale experiments and wind tunnel simulations on reduced models), analytical (using analytical and engineering techniques derived from full-scale experiments), and numerical (numerical simulation based on the Navier-Stokes equations). Due to their complexity and high cost, field experiments are not popular. The disadvantage of modelling on scale models in a wind tunnel is the difficulty of reproducing natural conditions in the pipe (moving track structure, open space around the train), as well as the need to justify the correctness of the selected similarity criteria. Analytical methods are much cheaper, but much less accurate. Numerical simulation based on the Navier-Stokes equations, unlike analytical methods, allows increasing the accuracy of the results, but requires large computing resources, but, unlike scaled experiments, allows to set the problem as close to natural as possible. Compared to full-scale experiments, numerical simulation allows us to study the flow pattern in all its aspects to the fullest extent possible, but it is advisable to use the results of full-scale and pipe experiments to verify the results of such modelling by integral characteristics.

If in the mid- and late 20th century numerical simulation was just beginning to develop, then due to the rapid development of computer technology in the late 20th and early 21st century, a significant increase in computing power allowed us to move to numerical simulation as the main research method.

Most modern works on numerical simulation of vehicle aerodynamics use simplified approaches to turbulence modelling in order to reduce computational costs. The three main approaches are simulation based on the Reynolds averaged Navier-Stokes equations (RANS), 2 large eddy simulation (LES), 3 and the detached eddy simulation (DES) method. 4

The main focus of research on train aerodynamics was the determination of the aerodynamic drag and the influence of crosswinds.

Baker 5 conducted a study of train flow using full-scale experiments. The data were presented in the form of averaged dimensionless slip velocities along the length of the train for a number of train types. This study was conducted to prepare an experimental database for TSI (Technical Specifications for Interoperability).

Khier 6 performed RANS flow simulations of a simplified model of a German Railway (Deutsche Bahn, DB) InterRegio lead car with yaw angles from 0° to 90°. The k-ε turbulence model was used to close the RANS system of equations, and the numerical solution was obtained by the control volume method. The main goal of the paper was to investigate the possibilities of RANS-based simulation in vehicle aerodynamics and to analyse large flows around trains.

To simulate the flow around a train of a generalized form (a parallelepiped with sharp and rounded edges), Favre 7 used the URANS method (solving unsteady, Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes equations) together with the standard k-ε and k-ω SST turbulence model. The Reynolds number based on the model width was 1.83·108, and the yaw angle was 20°.

Krajnovic 8 performed LES simulations of a simplified train model at a yaw angle of 90° for Re = 3·105) (based on the train height) and on two different grids with 8 and 11.5 million cells, respectively. The numerical solution of the unsteady Navier-Stokes equations, closed using the standard Smagorinsky turbulence model with subgrid functions, is obtained on the basis of the control volume method with a second-order central-difference scheme for space and a second-order Crank-Nicholson scheme for the time step.

Later, Hemida and Krajnovic 9 performed LES flow simulations of a simplified train model to investigate the effect of the train nose shape on the development of the flow structure. Hemida and Krajnovic used the same problem formulation as Krajnovic, 8 except for the van Driest damping function, which they additionally applied in the near-wall region.

Another important area of study in both the aerodynamics of cars and trains is the modeling of the ground effect that occurs when moving near a track structure.10,11

Compared to conventional road vehicles, HSSTs have a much larger length-to-height ratio, typically around 50-100. In addition, HSSTs have a longer disturbed wake length than road vehicles. For example, in Ref. 12, based on the results of studies of the aerodynamics of the ICE3 train in a wind tunnel, on a moving model and on a full-scale experiment, it was found that the disturbance of the train wake was significant up to about 20 train heights behind the tail. As a result, even if a moving track structure is implemented in a wind tunnel, a much longer moving strip will be required to represent the relative motion not only along the train itself, but also in the extended region of the vortex wake.

Previous studies of the air flow under the train floor with different track structure assignments have shown that the track structure can increase the mass flow of air under the vehicle in the longitudinal direction and reduce it in the transverse direction, and this affects the aerodynamic loads on the train floor structure. For example, Krajnovic et al. 13 found that the movement of the track structure reduces the drag of a car with a simplified fastback geometry by 8% and the lift by 16%, while Burgin et al. 14 found an increase in the drag of a blunt body near a moving track structure. In addition, Sardou 15 found a significant dependence of the vortex wake on the movement of the track structure.

Kwon et al. 16 studied the effectiveness of two methods for modeling a moving track surface, a moving belt system and a tangential blowing system, based on the Korean HSST. The results showed that the moving track structure could increase the drag by about 15%, which was explained as a result of increasing both friction forces and pressure drop on the surface. In particular, the changed boundary layer profile under the train increased the friction force on the train bottom, and the pressure force was increased due to the enhancement of the vortex structures in the wake. Xia et al.17,18 used CFD to compare the effects of a stationary and a moving track surface on the flow structure and aerodynamic loading on the Chinese HSST (CRH3). The same dominant wake structure was determined for both cases, with the variant with the moving track structure showing a narrower wake with slower vorticity expansion than the variant with the stationary track structure. In addition, significant changes in pressure in the bottom of the body were found due to the movement of the underbody, which led to a significant deviation in drag and lift compared to the variant with a stationary underbody.

The latest direction in reducing the aerodynamic drag of HSST is the use of vortex effects. An example is the shape of the Maglev train, which is proposed by the Chinese corporation CRRC (Figure 1).19,20

Front and side view of the Chinese corporation CRRC’s Maglev train.

The presence of several depressions on the outer surface of the Maglev train indicates the intention of the designers to influence the formation of continuous vortices in order to reduce the mass of air in the side and tail flows. From a physical point of view, the intensification of vortices in the longitudinal direction can lead to a decrease the aerodynamic drag.

2. The purpose of the study

The aim of this paper is to study the influence of the shape of the nose of a high-speed surface transport on its aerodynamic characteristics due to the intensification of vortices in the longitudinal direction.

3. Statement of the numerical investigation

3.1. Train geometry and computational domain

The calculations were performed for the high-speed surface transport with the general geometric dimensions shown in Figure 2. For numerical simulation, a hexahedral multiblock mesh consisting of 3 million nodes was constructed. In previous works on this geometry, a grid independence study was carried out and this grid size is optimal for calculations. All geometric parameters were referred to the length of the hull L = 28 m. The length of the computational domain was 5.5 L; width ‒ 1.85 L; height ‒ 0.9 L. The minimum mesh spacing was 5·10-7 m. The average thickness of the viscous sublayer along the hull was y+ = 1.5. The calculations were performed at a Reynolds number equal to Re = 2·108.

Exterior view of the geometry of the high-speed surface transport: (a) - left view; (b) - front view.

The parameters of the undisturbed flow were set as initial conditions. The undisturbed speed at the inlet was equal to M ∞ = 0.45 M, since this was the speed considered as cruising for this class of single-car high-speed vehicles developed at the Institute. No-slip conditions were set on the surface of a stationary vehicle. To model the track structure, a flat surface was used, which was located at a distance h=(0.01÷03)·H below the high-speed surface transport at H=0.075·L. The no-slip conditions were set on it with a speed equal to the velocity of the undisturbed flow, which corresponded to the Mach number M ∞ = 0.45, because this paper considers the reverse motion of a train relative to stationary air and the ground.16,21 On the subsonic outer surface the boundary conditions were calculated using the Riemann invariants. 21

The system of equations is written in a divergent form and, when replaced by a discrete analogue, has the properties of conservation of mass, momentum, and energy with appropriate accuracy at each calculation point.

The numerical solution of the system of initial equations was obtained by the control volume method. The block-matrix system of linear algebraic equations of the implicit scheme was solved by the generalized minimal residual method (GMRES) with an incomplete LU decomposition (ILU(k)) of the total matrix of the system as a preconditioner.

The time step was taken to be equal to 2e-04 sec, since it was this step that made it possible to obtain a stable solution using this method and computational grid.

The convergence criterion is the establishment of aerodynamic forces acting on the train with an error of 0.1%, or reaching a stable mode of harmonic oscillations with subsequent averaging of the values.

3.3. Verification of numerical algorithm

The numerical algorithm has been repeatedly verified on model two- and three-dimensional aerodynamic problems and used to calculate the general flow structure near a Maglev-type high-speed vehicle. 23

As an additional verification, a flow calculation was performed around a prolate spheroid (ellipsoid) with a radius ratio a:b=6:1 and an angle of attack α=10° in accordance with experimental data. 24 This test is one of the experimental data selected by NATO RTO for validation of CFD codes.

In this case the incoming flow velocity was 55 m/s, the length of the prolate spheroid was L=2.4m, and the Reynolds number ReL was 7.7·106. For numerical modelling a multi-block structured computational grid was constructed, which consisted of 4·106 cells. The boundaries of the computational domain are located at a distance of 20L. At the inlet and outlet boundaries, the flow parameters were calculated based on Riemann invariants.

4. Results of calculations

Below are the results of three series of computational experiments to determine the effect of the length, width, and height of the nose section on the aerodynamic characteristics of the high-speed surface transport. In the first series of calculations, the length of the nose taper l varied from 0.04 to 0.2 L (Figure 5(a) and (b)), where L is the length of the body of the high-speed surface transport. In the second series, the width of the nasal constriction w varied in the range from 0.2 to 1 W (Figure 5(c) and (d)), where W is the width of the high-speed surface transport. In the third series of computational experiments, the height h of the nose part above the track structure varied in the range from 0.01 to 0.3 H, where H is the height of the HSST (Figure 5(e) and (f)).

Geometry of the bow: (a) and (b) - first series of experiments, l=0.04 L and l=0.2 L, right view; (c) and (d) - second series of experiments, w=0.2 W and w=1 W, top view; (e) and (f) - third series of experiments, h=0.01 H and h=0.3 H, right view.

The geometric shape of the train’s nose was deliberately simplified to generate a vortex system in the longitudinal direction. From a physical point of view, the source of longitudinal vortices is cross-flow separation. This flow structure is absent if the train’s nose has a smooth airplane-like shape. This simplification is a simple CFD experiment that can be applied to real trains if the result is positive.

For all the studied lengths of the bow part, the presence of a system of longitudinal vortices around and behind the high-speed surface transport body is characteristic (Figure 6(a) and (b)). As the length of the nose section decreases, the large longitudinal vortices increase in intensity while maintaining their scale. In this case, small longitudinal vortices in fact disappear, as the dominant structure becomes the cross-flow separation (Figure 6).

With a shorter nose length, the upper longitudinal vortices diverge slightly to the sides and above the body high-speed surface transport, and move to the rear of the body, where they press closer to each other, descend down the stern, while partially being drawn between the large longitudinal vortices and are carried downstream parallel to the path structure. As the length of the nose increases, the intensity of the upper longitudinal vortices also increases and they try to press closer to each other above the roof of the high-speed surface transport body.

At small lengths of the nose part of the HSST, a flow separation is formed along the upper front edges with the flow connection point located near the separation point (Figure 6(c) and (d)). The change in the width of the nose section practically does not affect the scale and location of the upper longitudinal vortices above the roof: originating at the side edges of the nose, they press against each other by the middle of the hull and move to the rear of the hull, going back aft, where they slightly lower and continue to move parallel to the track structure. As the width of the bow decreases, the intensity of the upper longitudinal vortices also decreases (Figures 10(a), (c) and 11), which leads to their faster attenuation immediately behind the stern high-speed surface transport, while with a larger width they attenuate in the wake at a distance of about one length of the high-speed surface transport hull.

The wider width of the nose causes the incoming airflow to sharply bend around the nose from the sides, which leads to greater flow inhibition near the lower lateral edges of the body, as well as to the removal of small lower longitudinal vortices from the body and the formation of a wider turbulent wake (Figure 10(a)–(d)). Also, at a larger width, the sharp flow envelope of the nose leads to greater acceleration and rarefaction directly under the nose of the high-speed surface transport (Figure 12). At a small width, the incoming flow smoothly flows around the nose and enters the bottom with a denser flow with a slight acceleration.

Changing the height of the nose section has virtually no effect on the scale and location of the upper longitudinal vortices above the roof. An increase in the height of the nose section leads to a decrease in the intensity of the upper longitudinal vortices, which leads to their faster attenuation immediately after the stern of the high-speed surface transport (Figure 13(b), (d) and (e)), while at a lower height they attenuate in the wake at a distance of about one length of the high-speed surface transport body (Figure 13(a), (c) and (d)). Reducing the height of the nose part leads to an increase in the turbulence of the upper longitudinal vortices and the vortex wake behind the high-speed surface transport (Figure 14). The increase in the height of the nose section causes a significant part of the incoming flow to flow around it from below and sharply bend it from the sides, flowing under the bottom, which leads to stronger flow inhibition near the lower side edges of the casing and its further expansion away from the casing, forming a wider turbulent wake (Figure 13(a)–(f)).

At a low nose height, the air flow flows more smoothly around the nose from above (Figure 13(a) and (b)). The distribution of the pressure coefficient in the longitudinal section z = 0 along the body of the high-speed surface transport is shown in Figure 15, respectively, for the minimum (a) and maximum (b) heights of the nose section.

Distribution of pressure coefficient along the high-speed surface transport: (a) - h = 0.01 H; (b) - h = 0.3 H.

With an increase in the height of the nose part, the rarefaction region at the inlet under the bottom moves further along the hull from the nose (Figure 15(b)), which affects the pitching moment coefficient mz, which is calculated relative to the HSST nose. The maximum value of the pressure coefficient on the high-speed surface transport body is observed in the nose part (Figure 15). Three minimum values of the pressure coefficient are observed at the bottom under the bow and stern parts of the high-speed surface transport, as well as at the rear upper edge of the hull. Dependence of aerodynamic characteristics on the length of the nose section: (a) - drag coefficient Cx; (b) - lift coefficient Cy; (c) - moment coefficient mZ.

With an increase in the length of the nose section, the drag coefficient Cx decreases from 0.433 to 0.32 (Figure 16(a)). Reducing the length of the nose part leads to an increase in the high-pressure zone in front of the high-speed surface transport, and also increases the area with the maximum pressure coefficient at the nose of the HSST, which leads to an increase in drag. The appearance of flow separation behind the upper edges of the nose of the HSST also increases the drag.

The coefficient of lift Cy takes negative values over the entire studied range of nose section lengths (Figure 16(b)). With an increase in the length of the nose section, the modulus of the lift coefficient decreases from 0.545 to 0.319. Reducing the length of the nose part significantly increases the modulus of the pressure coefficient in the rarefaction zone under the nose part of high-speed surface transport, which leads to an increase in the pressing force.

In the range of nose section lengths from 0.04 to 0.08 L, the pitching moment coefficient mZ decreases from 0.2 to 0.168 (Figure 16(c)). In the interval up to 0.12 L, the coefficient mZ practically does not change, and then, up to a length of 0.2 L, it decreases to 0.13.

With an increase in the width of the nose part, the drag coefficient Cx increases from 0.314 to 0.36 (Figure 17(a)). The increase in the width of the nose part leads to an increase in the area with the maximum pressure coefficient on the nose of the high-speed surface transport, as well as to an increase in the absolute values of the coefficient of friction on the body, which leads to an increase in drag.

Dependence of aerodynamic characteristics on the width of the nose section: (a) - drag coefficient Cx ; (b) - lift coefficient Cy ; (c)−moment coefficient mZ .

The lift coefficient Cy takes negative values over the entire studied range of nose section lengths. As the width of the nose section increases, the modulus of the lift coefficient decreases from 0.33 to 0.24 (Figure 17(b)). As the width of the nose section increases, the pitching moment coefficient mZ decreases from 0.17 to 0.11 (Figure 17(c)).

Reducing the width of the nose section significantly reduces the effect of accelerating the flow entering the bottom of the tubing, reducing the rarefaction below the nose section of the high-speed surface transport. At the same time, with a larger width, the effect of the rarefaction above the nose immediately behind the nose bluntness increases. Increasing the rarefaction above the nose and increasing the nose area has a stronger effect than increasing the rarefaction below the nose, which, with increasing the width of the nose, leads to a decrease in the downforce. At the same time, the change in the nose width has virtually no effect on the pressure distribution and structure of the immediately adjacent vortices near the stern of the high-speed surface transport. As a result of this imbalance of influence on the nose and stern, the mZ coefficient decreases with increasing the width of the nose of the high-speed surface transport.

As the height of the nose section increases, the drag coefficient C x decreases from 0.33 to 0.32 with a slight increase at the maximum height (Figure 18(a)). The coefficient C x takes the maximum value at the minimum height of the nose part, as a consequence of the complicated air flow under the bottom of the high-speed surface transport.

The lift coefficient C y takes negative values in the entire range of nose section heights studied. With increasing height, the modulus of the lift coefficient decreases from 0.35 to 0.26 (Figure 18(b)). Due to the more complete flow of the nose section both from the upper and lower sides, the nose section lift reduces the downward force.

As the height of the bow increases, the pitching moment coefficient m z decreases from 0.17 to 0.14 (Figure 18(c)) due to a change in the pressure pattern near the bow: more pressure is applied to the lower surface towards the bottom, and the rarefaction at the inlet under the bottom shifts a little further aft along the X-axis.

It is shown that the decrease in the drag coefficient C x with increasing length and height of the nose is due to the weakening of the intensity of transverse flow separation, the reduction of the excess pressure zone in the frontal part and the transformation of longitudinal vortices into more stable and extended structures that reduce momentum losses in the wake. In contrast, an increase in the width of the nose leads to an intensification of separation and expansion of the turbulent wake, which directly explains the increase in C x .

Negative values of the lift coefficient C y in the entire range of parameters studied are associated with the formation of a rarefaction zone under the bottom due to the acceleration of the flow and the action of longitudinal vortices that direct the momentum downward. Changes in the modulus C y are explained by the shift and intensity of this rarefaction zone depending on the geometry of the nose. Similarly, the evolution of the pitching moment coefficient m z is interpreted as a consequence of the redistribution of pressure between the bow and stern parts of the hull, in particular the shift of the minimum pressure region along the axis of motion.

5. Conclusions

Unsteady Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes equations were used to calculate the aerodynamic characteristics of a high-speed surface transport. The SST turbulence model was used to model turbulence. Software and methodology for numerical simulation and determination of the main parameters of the unsteady flow of a high-speed surface transport was developed. The numerical solution of the system of initial equations was performed by the control volume method. The distributions of gas-dynamic characteristics of the flow around the body of a high-speed surface transport near the road structure are obtained. The use of vortex aerodynamic effects in the longitudinal direction makes it possible to reduce the drag coefficient by approximately 25%. The influence of the length, width, and height of the nose section on the aerodynamic coefficients of the high-speed surface transport is determined. An increase in length and height reduces drag, and an increase in the width of the nose part of the high-speed surface transport leads to an increase in the drag coefficient. Regardless of the geometric parameters of the nose part, a negative lifting force which presses the body to the track structure, and a positive pitching moment m z act on the body of the high speed vehicle. The developed methodology and calculation results can be applied to the study of coupled problems of dynamics and aerodynamics, optimization of the aerodynamic shape, as well as to the selection of design parameters of a high-speed surface transport on superconducting magnets, suspension system, and track structure.
