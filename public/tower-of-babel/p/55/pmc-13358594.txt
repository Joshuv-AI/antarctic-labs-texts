Effects of 8-week Pilates or yoga intervention on body composition, flexibility, balance, autonomic nervous system function, and mental health in adults with stress
Authors: Jun-Hyeok Song, Jae-Ho Choi, Hyoung Jean Beak, Hun-Young Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
[Purpose]In this study, we aimed to verify the effects of an 8-week Pilates or yoga intervention on body composition, flexibility, balance, autonomic nervous system function, and mental health in adults aged 30-39 years experiencing moderate stress.[Methods]Twenty-three adults with moderate stress corresponding to perceived stress scale scores of 14-26 were enrolled in this study. Study participants were assigned to the Pilates (n = 12) or yoga (n = 11) groups. The Pilates group performed Pilates consisting of 21 movements twice a week for 60 min at an intensity equivalent to a rating of perceived exertion of 12 using the Borg scale (6-20). The yoga group performed yoga comprising 17 movements twice a week for 60 min at the same intensity as Pilates. Body composition, flexibility, balance, autonomic nervous system function, and mental health were assessed before and after the 8-week intervention in both groups.[Results]Neither Pilates nor yoga induced significant changes in body composition, owing to the relatively low total exercise volume. Both groups showed significant improvements in flexibility, balance, autonomic nervous system function, and mental health; similar effects were observed between the two interventions.[Conclusion]Pilates and yoga interventions were associated with improvements in flexibility, balance, autonomic nervous system function, and mental health in adults aged 30-39 years with moderate stress, and similar benefits were observed between the two interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Mental disorders are the second leading cause of illness worldwide [1], and anxiety and depression are the most common mental disorders in the general population [2]. According to World Health Organization (WHO) statistics reported in 2019, 970 million people worldwide currently suffer from mental disorders, with anxiety and depression being the most common disorders [3]. Social Survey by Statistics Korea [4], found that 50.5% of respondents reported experiencing stress in the past two weeks, women reported experiencing more stress than men, with the highest levels of stress among those in their 30 to 49 ages, followed by a gradual decline with age.

Sedentary behavior and lack of physical activity have been reported to contribute to mental disorders such as depression, anxiety, and stress [5]. Previous studies have shown that various types of physical activity are effective in increasing motivation, improving self-esteem, and reducing stress levels, and that exercise can reduce anxiety and depression even in extreme situations such as the COVID-19 pandemic [6-9]. Physical activity increases endorphin and monoamine levels, reduces cortisol, improves mood, and attenuates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis response to stress. Therefore, enhancing physical fitness may be an effective strategy for addressing the impact of unhealthy lifestyles on mental health [10-12]. Denche-Zamorano et al. [13] analyzed the association between physical activity level (PAL) and mental health in 17,641 people using the Goldberg General Health Questionnaire. Higher PAL was associated with better mental health indicators such as successful coping skills, self-esteem, and stress [13]. Thus, the authors argued that a lack of physical activity and a sedentary lifestyle are important factors causing mental health problems, including depression and anxiety. Denche-Zamorano et al. [13] further posited that physical activity and exercise improve mental health.

The most common exercise modalities used to address mental health issues include aerobic, concurrent, and circuit exercises [14-16]. Rao et al. [15] reported that a combination of medication and aerobic exercise, administered three times a week for eight weeks, was more effective than medication alone in reducing depression and anxiety and improving self-esteem and quality of life in adults with depression. Greco et al. [14] divided 42 male workers in the helping profession into control and experimental groups to examine the effect of an 8-week concurrent exercise intervention on mental health. Concurrent exercise reduced burnout and perceived stress in this population. Similarly, Saavedra et al. [16] divided 47 healthy volunteer office workers into control, walking exercise, and circuit exercise groups to evaluate the effects of two types of exercise treatments lasting 30 min twice a week for 12 weeks on health-related parameters. Walking and circuit exercises both effectively improved body composition, health-related physical fitness, and mental health.

Nowadays, Pilates and yoga, among various exercise modalities, have gained increasing popularity among the general public over the past 20 years, and in the annual fitness trend survey conducted by the United States-based Association of Sports Medicine, Pilates and yoga have been listed as Worldwide Fitness Trends since 2008 [17,18]. These exercise modalities have various health benefits, such as weight control, improved posture, flexibility, and improved cardiovascular function [18,19]. Furthermore, they are attractive exercise modalities for both the general population and those with medical conditions because of their low risk of sports-related injuries [18,19]. Previous studies primarily examined the effects of Pilates and yoga on physical health-related parameters in various demographics [20,21]. However, with the growing importance of mental health, various studies have investigated the effects of Pilates and yoga on mental health [22,23]. Fleming et al. [23] estimated the population effect size of a Pilates intervention on mental health outcomes based on a meta-analysis. The authors explained that Pilates treatment can improve mental health by positively affecting depression (11-81% reduction), anxiety symptoms (33-46% reduction), fatigue (22.3% reduction), and quality of life. Bieber et al. [22] divided 83 patients with major depressive disorder receiving antidepressants and psychotherapy into a control group and an experimental group. They subsequently examined the effect of 12-week body-centered yoga (Ashtanga-yoga) on depression; the yoga intervention improved depression. Although both Pilates and yoga are classified as mind-body exercise modalities [17], they differ in their primary physiological emphasis. Pilates focuses on core stabilization and neuromuscular control through precise movement and postural regulation, which may preferentially enhance musculoskeletal function and motor control efficiency [24,25]. In contrast, yoga emphasizes sustained posture and breathing regulation, which are closely related to autonomic nervous system (ANS) modulation and psychological relaxation, potentially exerting stronger effects on mental health and autonomic regulation [26]. These differences suggest that Pilates and yoga may induce differential adaptations despite sharing common mind-body exercise characteristics.

Previous studies have examined the effectiveness of Pilates and/or yoga on mental health. However, research that considers not only the physical but also the mental aspects of Pilates and/or yoga remains lacking. Moreover, comparisons of the effectiveness of Pilates and yoga interventions are lacking. Therefore, we aimed to compare the effects of an 8-week Pilates or yoga intervention on body composition, flexibility, balance, ANS function, and mental health in adults aged 30-39 years experiencing moderate stress. We hypothesized that 8-week Pilates or yoga intervention would improve these parameters, and that there would be differences in effectiveness between the two exercise modalities.

METHODS

Participants

This study included 24 adults aged 30-39 years with moderate stress, as determined by Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) scores of 14-26, without any specific clinical diseases. All participants were randomly and equally divided into the Pilates (PG, n = 12) or yoga (YG, n = 12) groups. They maintained their normal daily lifestyle during the study period and performed the exercise intervention corresponding to each group for 8 weeks, twice a week, for 60 min a day. However, one participant dropped out of the YG (n = 1, withdrawal) during the study period. Consequently, the data from 23 participants were used in the final analysis. The physical characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. No differences in physical characteristics were observed between the two groups before the intervention. All participants received detailed information regarding the purpose and procedures of the study and provided written informed consent before participation. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Konkuk University, Korea (KKUIRB-202411-HR-013), and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Design

The study design, Pilates program, and yoga program are illustrated in Figures 1, 2, and 3, respectively.

The Pilates program consisted of 21 movements performed for 60 min at an intensity corresponding to a rating of perceived exertion (RPE) of 12 on the Borg scale 6-20 [27]. The exercise method for each movement in the Pilates program was as follows: Leg Pull Front, Swimming, Leg Pull, Kneeling Side Kicks, Side Bend, Push-Up, Spine Twist, Jack Knife, Shoulder Bridge, Double Leg Kick, Saw, Scissors, Rolling back, Hundred, Double Leg Stretch, Roll-Over, One Leg Stretch, Spine Stretch, One Leg Kick, Roll-Up, and One Leg Circles. All exercises were performed sequentially from Leg Pull Front to One Leg Circles. Each exercise was performed using predetermined repetitions or sets based on established Pilates practice, with side-specific repetitions applied where applicable. The Pilates program recognizes the stabilization of the trunk, controls the movement of the arms and legs, and creates stable, up-down, and left-right movements of the spine. It also aims to control and smoothly move the entire body through movements such as rolling.

The yoga program corresponded to Surya Namaskar B at an RPE of 12 on the Borg scale 6-20 [28]. It consisted of 17 movements that took approximately 3 min each, and 20 sets were performed for a total of 60 min. The exercise method for each movement in the yoga program was as follows: Utkatasana, Uttanasana, Ardha Uttanasana, Chaturanga Dandasana, Urdhva Mukha Svanasana, Adho Mukha Svanasana, Virabhadrasana I, Chaturanga Dandasana, Urdhva Mukha Svanasana, Adho Mukha Svanasana, Virabhadrasana I, Chaturanga Dandasana, Urdhva Mukha Svanasana, Adho Mukha Svanasana, Ardha Uttanasana, Uttanasana, and Utkatasana. The overall process begins and ends with Utkatasana, where each pose is performed only once before moving on to the next pose. The yoga program is based on the concept of centering, which focuses solely on the inner self and body movements. It utilizes the Ujjayi Pranayama— breathing through the nose without opening the mouth. The goal is to focus on and immerse yourself in the body and mind and integrate them into one.

All participants performed Pilates or yoga for 60 min per session, twice a week for 8 weeks. Changes in body composition, flexibility, balance, ANS function, and mental health before and after the intervention were analyzed in adults aged 30-39 years. All interventions and measurements were performed under the same environmental conditions (22 ± 2°C and 50 ± 5% relative humidity).

Measurements

Body composition parameters (height, weight, BMI, lean body mass, fat mass, and percent body fat) were measured after fasting for more than 8 h and refraining from strenuous physical activity for 48 h before the test. Testing was conducted using a stadiometer (BSM 330; InBody, Seoul, Korea) and a bioelectrical impedance analysis device (Inbody 770; Inbody, Seoul, Korea).

Flexibility was assessed using three methods: sit and reach, trunk flexion, and left and right trunk rotation. Sit and reach (cm) and trunk flexion were measured using a T.K.K. 5111 (Takei, Tokyo, Japan). Left and right trunk rotations were measured using a modified goniometric platform (MGP) with an area of 100 × 70 cm (length × width), comprising a plastic board with a dug-out semicircle with a 23 cm radius from the center of the MGP [29].

Balance was evaluated using the one-leg stand with eyes closed and Y-balance tests. As an indicator of static balance, the one-leg stand with eyes closed test was performed three times on both the left and right sides, and the highest value was used as the measurement value. The Y-balance test was performed twice as an indicator of dynamic balance using a Y-balance test kit (Functional Movement Systems, Inc., Chatham, VA, USA). The length of the limbs of the patients was measured, and a Y-balance score was calculated using the formula [average of normalized reach scores (%) = (anterior + posteromedial + posterolateral) × 100 ÷ 3 × leg length] considering the length of the limbs of the participants.

Heart rate variability (HRV), which quantifies the degree of physiological heart rate fluctuations, was measured in the resting state to analyze changes in ANS activity. After having the participants sit comfortably, a Polar V800 (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland) was worn on the left and right sides below the clavicle and below the 10th rib. After resting for 10 min, measurements were taken for 5 min. ANS activity was evaluated by dividing HRV into time and frequency domains based on the standard values of the European Society of Cardiology and North American Society of Cardiology Electrophysiology. The analyzed data were in the time domain (the square root of the mean of the sum of the squares of differences between adjacent NN intervals, RMSSD; the number of interval differences of successive NN intervals greater than 50 ms, NN50; and the proportion derived by dividing NN50 by the total number of NN intervals, pNN50) and frequency domains (low frequency, LF; high frequency, HF; and LF/HF ratio).

Mental health was assessed using the PSS, State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), and Beck Depression Inventory (BDI). The PSS is a questionnaire that asks participants to subjectively assess the levels of stress in their lives. It is used to measure the level of stress experienced in daily life [30]. The PSS consists of 10 items, and the total score is calculated by summing the scores for each item. A higher total score indicates a higher level of subjective stress perceived by the subject. The evaluation criteria are as follows: 0–13, 14–26, and 27–40 points indicate low, medium, and high stress levels, respectively. The STAI has been used clinically to measure anxiety states in healthy adults without mental disorders [31]. The STAI consists of 20 questions that ask respondents to express their feelings and emotions or assess an individual's inherent emotional anxiety traits. The questions are rated on a 4-point scale. The scale is structured to yield scores ranging from 20 to 80. Scores < 59 indicate mild-to-moderate trait anxiety, whereas those > 59 indicate moderate-to-severe trait anxiety. Similarly, the BDI consists of 21 questions covering the cognitive, emotional, motivational, and physical symptom domains of depression. The total score for each question is added up; scores of 0–10, 11–16, 17–20, 21–30, 31–40, and ≥ 40 indicate normal mental health, mild mood disorder, clinical borderline depressive disorder, moderate depression, severe depression, and extreme depression, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were analyzed by SPSS version 29.0 (SPSS institute, IBM corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The normality of the distribution of all outcome parameters was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test before the parametric tests. To verify the differences in effectiveness between the Pilates and yoga interventions in adults with moderate stress corresponding to PSS 14-26, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures was used to assess the presence of interactions or main effects within the intervention. Partial eta-squared (ηp²) values were calculated as measures of effect size for the ANOVA. When the ANOVA revealed a significant interaction or main effect within the intervention, a Bonferroni post-hoc test and paired t-test were used to identify within-time differences. The level of significance was set a priori at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Table 2 shows pre- and post-intervention body composition data. No significant interactions or main effects of the interventions were observed. In other words, no significant changes were observed in either the PG or YG after the intervention.

The pre- and post-intervention flexibility data for both groups are shown in Table 3. No significant interaction was observed. However, sit and reach (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.738), trunk flexion (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.474), right trunk rotation (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.751), and left trunk rotation (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.737) showed a main effect after intervention. Post-hoc analysis revealed a significant improvement in sit and reach (PG, p < 0.001; YG, p < 0.001), trunk flexion (PG, p = 0.005; YG, p = 0.024), right trunk rotation (PG, p < 0.001; YG, p < 0.001), and left trunk rotation (PG, p < 0.001; YG, p < 0.001) in both groups after an 8-week intervention.

As shown in Table 4, balance parameters showed no significant interaction. However, a right one-leg stand with eyes closed (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.623), left one-leg stand with eyes closed (p = 0.001, ηp² = 0.402), right Y-balance (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.520), and left Y-balance (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.415) showed a main effect after intervention. Post-hoc analysis indicated that the PG showed a significant improvement in the right one-leg stand with eyes closed (p = 0.003), left one-leg stand with eyes closed (p = 0.003), right Y-balance (p = 0.003), and left Y-balance (p = 0.005). Furthermore, the YG showed a significant improvement in the right one-leg stand with eyes closed (p = 0.001), right Y-balance (p = 0.012), and left Y-balance (p = 0.037). However, this group showed a tendency to improve in the left one-leg stand with eyes closed (p = 0.060).

Table 5 shows the pre- and post-intervention ANS functional data. No significant interaction was observed. However, LF (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.573) and LF/HF (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.519) showed a main effect following intervention. Post hoc analysis indicated that both groups showed a significant decrease in LF (PG, p = 0.011; YG, p < 0.001) and LF/HF (PG, p = 0.008; YG, p = 0.006).

The pre- and post-intervention mental health data for both groups are shown in Table 6. No significant interaction was observed. However, PSS (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.846), STAI (p = 0.001, ηp² = 0.561), and BDI (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.507) showed a main effect after intervention. Post hoc analysis indicated that both groups showed a significant decrease in PSS (PG, p = 0.011; YG, p < 0.001), STAI (PG: p = 0.005, YG: p = 0.024), and BDI (PG, p < 0.001; YG, p < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we hypothesized that Pilates or yoga intervention for 8 weeks would improve body composition, flexibility, balance, ANS function, and mental health, and that there would be differences in effectiveness between Pilates and yoga. Consistent with our hypothesis, 8-week Pilates or yoga intervention showed significant improvements in flexibility, balance, ANS function, and mental health in adults with moderate stress. However, no difference in dependent parameters was observed between the two groups, and no significant changes in body composition were observed.

No previous studies have verified the observed differences between the effects of Pilates and yoga intervention on body composition. However, de Souza Cavina et al. [32] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively analyze the effects of Pilates intervention on body composition in various participants, including the elderly, adults, and overweight and obese populations. Pilates did not improve body composition, owing to the relatively low exercise intensity and insufficient exercise volume [32]. Furthermore, Koncz et al. [33] divided 75 middle-aged adults into the CON (n = 29, 48.2 ± 8.91 years of age) and YG (n = 46, 47.4 ± 9.06 years) groups and performed a 12-week complex yoga-based intervention. The authors reported no significant effects on body composition, such as weight and muscle mass; cardiovascular disease risk factors; or balance ability. Contrastingly, Pereira et al. [34] verified the effects of various forms of Pilates intervention on body composition based on systematic analysis and meta-analysis. Various forms of Pilates intervention effectively reduced body weight, BMI, and body fat percentage in middle-aged women [34]. Similarly, Lauche et al. [35] conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 30 randomized trials comprising more than 2,000 participants to investigate the effects of yoga intervention on body composition in various populations (healthy and overweight/obese adults). Yoga did not affect body weight, the percentage of body fat, or waist circumference. However, it reduced WHR in healthy adults and BMI in overweight/obese individuals. Consistent with the findings of de Souza Cavina et al. [32] and Koncz et al. [33], we found no improvement in body composition parameters after Pilates or yoga interventions. This is likely due to the relatively low total exercise volume—which was conducted twice a week for 60 min over a total of 8 weeks—despite the moderate intensity of the exercise program, with an RPE of 12 (Borg scale 6-20). Therefore, we theorized that the Pilates or yoga intervention in the present study failed to induce positive changes in body composition. Future studies should investigate differences in the effectiveness of Pilates or yoga interventions by applying a higher exercise volume.

Regarding physical functions such as flexibility and balance, Suner-Keklik et al. [24] randomly divided 33 healthy adults aged 18-25 into the CON (n = 16) and PG (n = 17) groups to evaluate the effects of a 6-week Pilates intervention on trunk proprioceptive function and core muscle endurance. The results showed positive effects on both parameters, and the authors reported that Pilates intervention contributed to the development of flexibility and balance. Bullo et al. [25] conducted a systematic analysis to summarize and verify the effects of Pilates on health-related physical fitness in the elderly. Pilates intervention was an effective strategy for improving muscle strength, walking and gait performance, balance, and flexibility. Similarly, Luo & Huang [36] divided 40 female college students into the CON group (n = 20) and YG (n = 20) to examine the effects of yoga intervention performed twice a week for 70 min for 16 weeks on flexibility and balance. Flexibility of the waist and legs (sit and reach), flexibility of the groin and hip (split test), and balance (single-limb and Romberg tests) improved [36]. In addition, Lim et al. [37] divided 20 female fencers into PG (n = 10) and YG (n = 10) and administered 50 min of exercise intervention twice a week for eight weeks. Both groups showed significant improvements in dynamic balance and functional movement screening (FMS), a functional index. Furthermore, Lim & Park [38] equally divided 90 volunteers aged 30-49 years into the PG (n = 30), YG (n = 30), and CON group (n = 30) and administered the intervention for 60 min three times a week for 8 weeks. Pilates intervention was more effective than the yoga intervention and controls in improving the FMS and short form of the Rand 36-Item Health Survey. Consistent with the findings of Lim et al. [37], the present study revealed that most balance and flexibility parameters significantly improved with either Pilates or yoga intervention. However, no significant differences were observed between the two interventions. This indicates that both Pilates and yoga are effective in improving flexibility and balance, which are important health-related physical fitness parameters.

The effects of exercise on stress and mental health have been extensively studied [39,40]. Both Pilates and yoga have been effective not only in alleviating mental health issues such as stress, anxiety, and depression but also in improving HRV, a parameter of ANS function [41,42]. Previous studies have evaluated changes in ANS function and mental health following Pilates or yoga intervention. Specifically, Amzajerdi et al. [41] divided 67 female college students into a CON group (n = 35) and PG (n = 32). The participants performed Pilates intervention for 60 min, three times a week for 8 weeks. After an 8-week intervention, the PG showed significant improvements in sleep quality parameters, such as subjective sleep quality and daytime dysfunction, and reduced fatigue compared to the CON group. In addition, Park et al. [40] evaluated the effects of 12 weeks of online or face-to-face Pilates exercises on ANS function in 30 obese middle-aged women using HRV. The results showed significant improvements in the time-domain RMSSD and frequency-domain LF/HF, indicating that Pilates improves ANS function. In contrast, Shobana et al. [26] compared the effectiveness of a long-term yoga intervention by dividing 68 healthy volunteers into the CON group (n = 35) and YG (n = 33). The YG showed decreased sympathetic nervous system (SNS) activity and improved parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) activity. Thus, Shobana et al. [26] argued that yoga is a suitable form of exercise for regular practice, as it reduces stress and prevents lifestyle-related disorders. Furthermore, Kim [42] compared the effects of Pilates or yoga on ANS function and mental health by dividing 20 college students into the PG (n = 10) and YG (n = 10). Both interventions improved ANS balance, PNS activity, and RMSSD. However, yoga reduced SNS activity and ANS balance more effectively. Similarly, Lim & Hyun [17] divided 90 healthy adult volunteers aged 30-49 years into the CON group (n = 30), PG (n = 30), and YG (n = 30) and administered an intervention for 60 min, three times a week, for 8 weeks. Both interventions were more effective than the CON on the Health-Promoting Lifestyle Profile and the Health Self-Rating Scale. The present study examined the effects of Pilates and yoga on ANS function and mental health in adults aged 30-39 years with moderate stress levels. Both interventions significantly improved HRV parameters, such as LF and LF/HF ratio, as well as PSS, STAI, and BDI. Moreover, their effectiveness showed similar positive results. The LF reflects SNS activity; it is significantly and positively correlated with fatigue, depression, anger, and static stress [43,44]. The LF/HF ratio indicates the degree of ANS balance, and a higher LF/HF ratio indicates relatively hyperactive SNS or suppressed PNS activity [45]. Thus, both Pilates and yoga improved ANS functional coordination ability in this study, as measured by HRV. Furthermore, these interventions improved mental health by relieving stress, anxiety, and depression.

Overall, the present study suggests that Pilates and yoga interventions are effective and have similar utilities in improving flexibility, balance, ANS function, and mental health in adults aged 30-39 years with moderate stress. These improvements were observed even within the relatively short 8-week intervention period, indicating the practical relevance of these exercise interventions. However, changes in time-dependent indicators, such as body composition, may require longer intervention durations. Therefore, well-designed future studies based on more diverse protocols and indicators are required to obtain more robust and accurate research outcomes.
