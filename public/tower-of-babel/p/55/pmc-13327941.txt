A cybernetic framework for synthetic biological intelligence in the era of neural tissue engineering
Authors: Étienne P Sellar, Nicolas Rouleau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvances in synthetic biology and tissue engineering have enabled the design and assembly of neural constructs from first principles, renewing interest in cybernetics for understanding control, adaptation, and intelligence. To address shortcomings of artificial intelligence, researchers increasingly draw on the learning, robustness, and energy efficiency of living cognitive systems. Synthetic biological intelligences (SBIs) are beginning to leverage embodied biological computation, while cybernetics provides substrate-agnostic principles to guide minimal cognition research.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Synthetic biological intelligences (SBIs) are engineered systems that leverage biocomputation to display cognitive functions1,2. Whether they are purely organic or hybrid mergences of living and mechanical parts, embodied SBIs can navigate both physical and virtual problem spaces, solving puzzles and achieving goal states by virtue of self-organization and reinforcement learning. The emergence of efforts to design and grow intelligent hybrid systems3,4 follows naturally from the twin histories of computer and cognitive sciences5, which are currently experiencing a reunification after a schism of implementation in the mid-twentieth century. Grounded theoretical frameworks are urgently needed to guide conceptual progress and technological innovation for biocomputing. Revisiting foundational ideas related to control and communication in animals and machines within the modern context may provide novel insights.

On the heels of the second Industrial Revolution, marked by widespread transitions to mechanical automation, behaviorism and electronic analog computing were developed as parallel tools for prediction and control6–8. Brains and machines alike could be programmed to deliver reliable outputs, inspiring the development of new, autonomous technologies including increasingly intelligent weapons of war. Missile guidance systems were ultimately implemented by electronic means9; however, Project ORCON (‘organic control’), led by B.F. Skinner, where pigeons were operantly conditioned to guide missiles10,11, represents one of many would be bio-hybrid technologies that were abandoned in lieu of the more practical, mechanical options of the time. While biological circuits could be programmed like machines12, they were less reliable, expensive to maintain, and contingent on life-supporting conditions. A lack of high-fidelity interfaces between organic controllers and their artificial targets also proved to be a major bottleneck. Despite emerging from a common Zeitgeist, the cognitive and computer sciences gradually diverged as independent fields while more than occasionally borrowing metaphors and nomenclature from their counterpart13,14. While the birth of cybernetics in the 1940s15 provided a unifying theoretical framework without reference to substrate or ontology, bridging concepts of animal and machine intelligence, its application as a roadmap for SBIs could not be fully realized due to longstanding technological constraints.

Our current era, defined by advances in synthetic biology16, tissue engineering17, and microelectronics18, have made it possible to implement organic control as a viable alternative to conventional computing and robotics. As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly powerful and ubiquitously integrated with all technology, it has become more difficult to ignore the inefficiencies of machine learning relative to biological equivalents19. Brains are more efficient learners by several orders of magnitude relative to silicon-based computers, inspiring the current search for neuromorphic and other bio-inspired strategies to solve the runaway energy needs of our AI infrastructure20. However, as we will discuss, key limitations must be addressed to implement SBI-based biocomputing as a competitive computing platform. Several authors have highlighted the relevance of revisiting substrate-independent theoretical frameworks toward a universalization of cognitive science to include diverse forms of intelligence beyond animals with nervous systems4,21. Here, we argue that cybernetics provides such a framework and, in the era of neural tissue engineering, serves as a foundation for the implementation of technologies that leverage hybrid intelligence.

Cybernetics as a framework for minimal cognition research

At the core of the cybernetic framework is a focus on feedback mechanisms, causal structure, and information flow22 (Fig. 1a). These concepts have been applied to understand both animal and machine intelligence23, greatly influencing the fields of cognitive and computer science respectively. Because cybernetics is fundamentally substrate-agnostic, it can be applied to a broad range of systems, from organisms to AI and ecological networks24. Integrating cybernetics with his expertise in neuroanatomy, Valentino Braitenberg described how synthetic minimal agents – or vehicles – containing simple sensory-motor loops could produce actions that were operationally indistinguishable from behavioral displays of aggression, love, fear, curiosity, and goal-directedness in animals24 (Fig. 1b). All the complexities of animal behavior and their inferred mental origins could be explained by minimal feedback loops. This illustrates one of the strengths of cybernetics as a theoretical framework to ground SBI research: it does not discriminate based on ontology or material constraints, offering instead a functionalist lens through which to design systems and interpret their outputs. While it may be worth distinguishing between autopoietic (self-creating, self-organizing) and allopoeitic (engineering, constructing) systems in terms of informational closure, the differences are blurred under a cybernetic framework, which treats intrinsic goals as causally inseparable from the agent-environment feedback loop.

Cybernetic foundations for synthetic biological intelligence and minimal cognitive research.

a Cybernetic loops serve as a basis for synthetic biological intelligence and organic control. b Braitenberg vehicles are minimal systems constructed with basic modules and circuits that can display complex behaviors informed by feedback mechanisms. c Embodied neural constructs in closed-loop feedback systems on microelectrode arrays (MEA) represent minimal, hybrid systems that can display synthetic biological intelligence.

Without invoking the mind as a functional category separate from a system’s sensors, integrators, and motor effectors, Braitenberg demonstrated that intelligent outputs could arise from reciprocal interactions between systems and their environments. Notably, his cybernetic approach to intelligence emphasized the important feature of architecture as embodiment. Organisms and robots alike could be described in terms of their sensors, effectors, the connections between them, and how all of these interacted with an environment as a unified field. Through the lens of cybernetics, architecture is embodiment25; it is a distributed set of cognitive resources that determines everything from ambulation to problem-solving26. Consistent with his view of “living matter” as fundamentally an “image of the world”27, Braitenberg leveraged the cybernetic framework to derive functionalist solutions to longstanding problems in neuroanatomy, such as the evolutionary origin of decussation28 – the tendency for nerve fibers to cross from one side of the body to the other side of the brain. Without a cybernetic framework based on first principles of minimal cognitive systems, explanations of neuroanatomical organization can only be made by vague references to natural selection without specifying the selector. Thus, cybernetics may offer other solutions to problems in cognitive sciences that have exhausted conventional theoretical frameworks grounded in the details of molecular biology.

Braitenberg’s thought experiments in synthetic psychology inspired the design of physical and virtual robots. However, until recently, without biotechnologies with which to systematically control the circuit-level architectures of minimally cognitive living systems, it was not possible to engineer organic “vehicles”. Today, closed-loop feedback platforms and neural tissue engineering offer unprecedented control for in vitro explorations of embodied cognition with SBIs. Despite the current limitations of tissue engineering, brain-machine interfacing, and other technological constraints, the cybernetics framework defines the minimal, essential set of properties that an SBI must include to implement intelligence across diverse embodiments (Fig. 1c). Without a substrate-agnostic framework, SBI research may fail to thrive under the neuro-centric assumptions of cognitive and behavioural sciences. Indeed, feedback loops need not be tethered to assumptions of local connection (e.g., signaling across continuous, physical conduits) or fixed sensory afferents (e.g., non-interoperable receptor-specific sensory bands and data streams) and motor efferents (e.g., rigidly defined, centralized bodies localized in space). Applying a cybernetics framework, looped feedback underlying intelligent response patterns can be implemented in highly diverse ways, drawing on distributed and dynamic sensor-motor couplings across space (e.g., networked embodiments enabled by the internet) and time (e.g., tunable delays, interstimulus intervals, etc.).

Engineering cognitive systems

Neural tissue engineering stands as a radical departure from the three major investigative approaches that have historically defined the science of neurocognition: ablation, stimulation, and neuroimaging. Beginning with ancient (c. 3000 BCE) descriptions of neuropsychological impairments like ataxia and aphasia following brain injuries29,30, which were later reobserved and formalized by Alexander Luria31,32, we now have highly precise technologies that can selectively ablate and “knock out” cognitive functions in animals, thus establishing structure-function relationships. Stereotactic surgery, focused ultrasound, and optogenetic inhibition are among a growing set of techniques that recapitulate brain injuries as a means of inferring neural mechanisms of cognition33. Similar throughlines can be traced from Scribonius Largus’ use of electric rays as peripheral nerve stimulators (c. 47 CE)34,35 to Penfield36 and Gloor’s37,38 cortical mapping of epileptic brains, inspiring the design of technologies like transcranial magnetic stimulation that leverages induced activity to identify neural determinants of mental functions and their behavioural correlates. Most recently, initiated by Hans Berger’s invention of electroencephalography at the turn of the twentieth century39, a capacity to measure neural correlates of cognition continues to captivate the attention of neuroscientists through technologies such as functional magnetic resonance imaging. These deductive approaches are slowly and steadily unraveling the Gordian Knot of human neurocognition; however, technologies imported from the field of biomedical engineering are providing novel alternatives.

In our current era, defined by biology-editing technologies like neural tissue engineering, we have inverted the discovery process, isolating mechanisms by systematic combination of variables in minimal models of the brain40,41. Just as Braitenberg observed “the full [functional] repertoire” of the machines in his thought experiments24, synthetic biology and tissue engineering can be used as tools of invention to overcome the process of delineating complex biological mechanisms. His term for this contrast of approaches was “the law of uphill analysis and downhill invention”, noting that in our overestimation of complexity we often apply tremendous deductive analysis to reach conclusions that might otherwise be achieved by careful induction24. While this bottom-up approach has primarily been used as a tool to understand neurodegenerative diseases and other brain pathologies, an emerging interest in organoid intelligence (OI)42 has motivated some investigators to build research programs around the construction rather than probing of cognitive systems. Of course, as Braitenberg himself admits, the advantage of the “uphill” analytical approach (i.e., the process of deduction), is a guaranteed answer after an exhaustive search. While the “downhill” inventive process does not make similar guarantees by brute force alone, when guided by principled actions (e.g., implementing cybernetic loops, adapting reinforcement learning theory), leaps in progress can be made. Of course, even minimally engineered systems can present as functional “black boxes” with emergent dynamics that are difficult to interpret. However, because tissue engineering involves the systematic inclusion (“knock in”) or exclusion (“knock out”) of minimal elements in the construct design (e.g., cell types, biomaterials, geometries), causal factors can be identified by combining inductive and deductive approaches.

Spheroids, assembloids, and 3D-printed constructs are becoming increasingly popular as platforms to investigate minimal cognitive functions and their neural correlates. Indeed, the recapitulation of neural oscillations, thalamo-cortical circuits, and other candidate determinants of consciousness has been achieved in vitro41. While organoids and spheroids suffer from some notable limitations, including oxygen diffusion issues that limit their dimensions (and cause necrotic cell death with their cores) but can be partially overcome by integrating vasculature, engineered constructs are more flexible41. Indeed, scaffold-based and bioprinted networks can be designed while accommodating for perfusion and other life-support needs by tuning porosity, permeability, conductivity, and other parameters of the materials. However, engineering as an approach to understanding brain function predates the combination of cells and gels to build miniaturized brains-in-a-dish. Building on the seminal works of Steve Potter in the early 2000s – whose hybrid robots and animats, controlled by cultured neural networks on microelectrode arrays (MEA), displayed intelligent behaviour contingent upon closed-loop feedback43–45 several authors have highlighted the remarkable capacities of machine-brain interfaces. Most notably, Kagan et al. 46 successfully trained neurons on a high-density MEA to play video games like Pong (and more recently, DOOM) using closed-loop feedback – a powerful demonstration of the principles that underlie recent technological advances in biocomputing46.

Dimensionality in the design of neural controllers

An engineering approach to the delineation of cognitive mechanisms can only be a downhill, energetically favorable mode of discovery if the inventive process is informed by careful design choice. What kinds of structures are most desirable for neural controllers? 2D monolayer in vitro neural cell cultures have been effective models with which to explore SBI development. Monolayer-implemented cognitive function is rare in nature, with few examples beyond the nerve nets of jellyfish47. Such organic simplicity is reminiscent of Braitenberg’s vehicles and demonstrates the feasibility of monolayer systems as neural controllers, which are easily coupled to neural computer interfaces (NCI) through high-definition MEAs24,48. However, there has been a shift toward more complex, physiologically relevant 3D systems. Mammalian nervous systems, for example, are segmented, layered, and recurrent in three dimensions41. While 2D and 3D cultures provide their own advantages, expanding SBI research into 3D systems enables the development of greater complexity (e.g., network topology, greater degrees of freedom for spatial computing) at the cost of tractability41,49.

Interest and capacity to develop 3D SBIs has increased dramatically as demonstrations of reservoir computing applications have emerged41,50. Kagan50 recently predicted that neural construct design for SBI research will bifurcate to organoid intelligence (OI) and bioengineered intelligence (BI) as distinct strategies50. OI leverages induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived cultures’ adaptability and heterogeneity to produce controllers with metabolic markers, structured histoarchitecture, and neural oscillations similar to those found in the brains of fetal organisms42,50,51. Indeed, these systems are highly translatable but provide us with a kind of black-box cognition that fails to escape the uphill analysis associated with probing animal nervous systems42,51. BI involves building minimally cognitive systems through scaffolds, bioprinted constructs, and hydrogels, thus designing their microcircuitry and resultant cognitive-behavioural profiles as neural controllers (Fig. 1c)50,52. OIs can be thought of as ‘discovered’ controllers, whereas BIs lend themselves well to explorations of organic, Braitenberg-like vehicles50. Both systems, however, present unique challenges for the integration of sensors, NCI compatibility, and media perfusion by virtue of their 3D organization (increased non-surface volume), demanding elaborate engineering workarounds like media perfusion systems and conformal or penetrating electrodes42,46,53,54.

It is important to note that biological reservoirs, whether constructed as BIs or OIs, lack mechanisms for sustained memory, losing state history exponentially over time due to substrate-specific constraints (e.g., ion channel relaxation dynamics, membrane capacitance). Similarly, a consequence of metabolic flux and protein turnover, biological networks exhibit “representational drift”, contributing to instability and poor construct reproducibility55. As a result, tasks that require maintaining highly repeatable, long-term contextual information are beyond the current capabilities of simple reservoir-based SBI systems without the integration of external digital memory and other mechanical resources in hybrid systems. Of course, the human brain is also subject to representational drift, which confers flexibility and promotes probabilistic rather than determined outcomes55. Perhaps, like quantum computing, practical biocomputing with SBIs may be best implemented by repeating operations multiple times to observe statistical convergence. Alternatively, feedback mechanisms like reverberation may minimize drift sufficiently to enable a practical “fuzzy” logic within the SBI55–57.

One of the benefits of the engineering approach is that we don’t need to limit ourselves to forms found in the natural world. Synthetic biology and neural tissue engineering are not limited by morphogenetic blueprints or the caprice of evolution. SBIs can benefit from 2D and 3D dimensionality as hybrid designs, reconciling the computational capacity and translatability of 3D networks with the NCI compatibility of 2D monolayers. Adapting spheroids, organoids, and other 3D constructs to interface with the highest resolution MEA or optical readout technologies, which are maximally resolved at the 2D scale, can provide new possibilities for SBIs and the field of biocomputation46,49,58. Where biological limitations arise, mechanical aids such as external memory can be integrated as compensating modules in a hybrid intelligence architecture. A cybernetics framework for the design of organic control systems could not be realized until recently; however, many engineering hurdles remain along the path to complex, cognitive systems. Today, with the modern toolsets of biomedical engineers, we can begin to explore diverse neural network designs and unique histoarchitectures as organic controllers, which have so far not been observed as results of selection pressures in natural environments, while maximizing cognitive potential in our engineered systems.

Embodying cultured neural networks

Brains evolved as parts of integrated bodies with highly filtered, peripheral access to an external world that cannot be perfectly predicted59. Even the simplest of living systems, including individual cells, rely on continuous feedback59,60. Therefore, when the brain is severed from its environment, highly disordered functions ensue58,61. In vitro, disembodied neural networks exhibit aberrant bursting activity (synchronous barrages of spikes) that is transiently inhibited by random stimulation delivered from an external source, demonstrating that neural tissues require some minimal level of embodiment to maintain their most basic physiological rhythms and capacities to learn58,61. Indeed, Potter’s original experiments with cultured neurons on MEAs demonstrated that voltage applied randomly across the array, which like an external environment cannot be perfectly predicted from internal states, recapitulated the sensory dimension of the causally closed, sensory-motor loop58. Similar results were achieved with closed-loop feedback wherein stimulation was contingent on neural activity58. We view these experiments as evidence of re-embodiment and a fundamental interoperability of potential bodies – physical, virtual, or otherwise – that aligns with the substrate-agnostic cybernetic framework23,58,60. To engineer an embodied SBI, afferent (“sensory”) and efferent (“motor”) regions of the cultured neural network can be predefined at an interface such as an MEA, serving as outputs to and inputs from a continuous loop of feedback46,58. Here, cybernetics can help circumscribe the unconventional “bodies” of SBIs, which in turn provides a substrate-agnostic means of defining sensors, integrators, effectors, and the environments with which they reciprocally interact. Shedding the bias of an understanding of bodies as animal organisms will require a broadening of the definition to include machines and hybrid architectures.

What is a “body” to an SBI? One answer to this question can be derived from experiments with engineered hybrid robots (hybrots)62. For example, the semi-living artist or ‘MEART’ was assembled by combining neurons, a charge-coupled device camera, and a robotic arm with a drawing implement44. Though separated by thousands of miles, the distributed SBI created works of art by virtue of feedback loops44. Other hybrots, combining mobile robots equipped with sensors and cultured neural network controllers learned to avoid obstacles, track objects, and explore open fields with patterns reflective of animal-like behaviour44,58. Similar techniques have been used to organically control avatars and other objects in virtual spaces (e.g., a paddle interacting with a ball)46. Biocomputing approaches may even embody tissues in abstract problem spaces63. In all these cases, spatial proximity of the SBI and its “body” was relatively unimportant44,58,63. Indeed, cognitive resources of the embodied system can be distributed over vast geographical distances by way of virtual connections on the internet44,63. From these engineered hybrid robots, it may be posited that a “body” is simply any interface that exchanges information across an input-output boundary. While it may be convenient to describe the brain or controller as “internal” vis-à-vis its “external” environment, embodiment as a closed loop makes the distinction quite ambiguous. Fundamental questions of self and non-self arise, though these may be reducible to the predictability of information across the boundary59. From one perspective, a highly predictable node within an embodied SBI could be equivalent to “internal” or “self” whereas an unpredictable node would be equivalent to “external” or “non-self” (i.e., the environment)59,64. As “external” nodes become increasingly predictable by the set of all nodes, the organism-environment distinction dissolves.

Programming biological circuits

Embodied cognitive systems evolved over billions of years, adapting and re-adapting to their dynamic environments at timescales that greatly exceed the lifespan of individual organisms59. Embodiments that achieve greater degrees of functional harmony with their environments will sustain closed-loop feedback for longer periods of time. Because learning operates at a finer timescale relative to natural selection, and individual organisms can leverage their history of inputs to optimize outputs (e.g., avoidance, exploration), including the maximization of fitness outcomes, learning is a self-reinforcing trait of the organic world. How do we program SBIs that have been severed from the ecological niches that gave rise to their cellular parts?

Conditioning is the chief coding language of the integrated biocircuit65. Behaviorists of the early twentieth century and their intellectual forbearers identified several operant procedures that demonstrated the programmability of animal behavior12. In essence, when actions are paired with consequences, those actions are shaped and reinforced11,12. The foundations of reinforcement learning originate with Thorndike’s Law of Effect, which states that responses that produce satisfying effects are more likely to occur again while those that are followed by discomfort are less likely to reoccur66. Here, the words “satisfying” and “discomfort” are loaded because they are situated in a study of human psychology12. However, with Skinner’s adoption and formalization of the concept, types of reinforcement came to be more generally described in terms of their consequences without reference to subjective states – a framing that aligns with the cybernetics approach12. Which is to say, the category of reinforcement, which precedes the response, is always retrospectively contingent on its outcome66. Positive and negative reinforcements, whether subjectively pleasant or not, are defined by their influence on the frequency and/or amplitude of the response downstream in time. When training animals or rearing children, these can often be intuited and applied without surprising outcomes (e.g., avoidance of noxious stimuli); however, the identification of specific reinforcements in non-animal systems, such as plants or single-celled organisms, is a wholly empirical project12,66. What may be “desirable” to one organism may be anathema to another. Similarly, intuitions about reinforcement fail to reliably inform the conditioning of neural substrates without bodies or morphogenetically constrained neural pathways. Brains “in-a-dish” can be conditioned – or programmed – by re-establishing the closed loop normally maintained by a body, thus enabling the reciprocal coupling of external stimuli with responses.

How is this coupling achieved? Hebb’s principle, simplified as “cells that fire together wire together”, is one of the most foundational concepts underlying learning in animals67. In the neurobiological context, Hebbian learning arises from activity-dependant synaptic plasticity, in which correlated pre- and postsynaptic firing drives NMDA-mediated calcium influx, triggering long-term potentiation (LTP) or long-term depression (LTD) through AMPA receptor up- or downregulation, respectively67,68. Alternatively, anti-Hebbian learning (“fire together, unwire together”) is the result of the inverse process and largely occurs in inhibitory pathways, where coincident and causal synaptic firing weakens connection, and anti-causal firing strengthens it, producing inverted timing67. These neuroplastic events are initiated through stimulus timing; they can also be termed spike-timing dependant plasticity (STDP) and anti-STDP, respectively49,67. For hybrid systems involving neurons, which must form connections to survive, these biological details are highly relevant. However, cybernetic organisms with non-neural organic controllers (including SBIs) may require alternative potentiation strategies that are nevertheless likely to be established by regularized space-time contiguities (e.g., timed input-output pairings). Uncoupling the connection between conditioning and plasticity from brain-centered mechanisms, the programmability of SBIs can be understood from a functionalist point of view as the restructuring of internal states to optimize the pairing of consequences with expected reinforcements.

Testing cognitive theories with embodied bioengineered brains

SBIs represent tractable platforms to experimentally validate theories that would otherwise be difficult to test in vivo. With systematic control of tissue modules, each conferring functional properties, it is now possible to identify causal elements in complex systems. Let us consider the possibility of testing three prominent theories using SBIs: the Free Energy Principle (FEP), Integrated Information Theory (IIT), and the Criticality Brain Hypothesis (CBH) (see Fig. 2a-c)64,69,70. Because these theories are substrate-independent, a cybernetic framework is particularly useful as it does not rely on the details of biological variables64,69,70. In an era defined by synthetic biology and neural tissue engineering that enables the creation of SBIs, experimental studies of complexity which previously relied on large-scale computer models, or in vivo systems, can now be investigated in controlled, reliable, and minimal in vitro models71–73.

In vitro frameworks for evaluating cognitive theories.

a Cognitive theories can be assessed in vitro, including IIT (integrated information theory) as measured by Φ); b CBH (critical brain hypothesis) by assessing network dynamic information compressibility and fractal dimension; and c FEP (free energy principle) via the reverse-engineering of generative models).

FEP was formally articulated in 2010 by Karl Friston as a system-agnostic theory of brain function74. Under FEP, any self-organizing system that persists over time must minimize a variational free energy bound on surprise64,74. Similar to Bayesian inference, which involves updating beliefs based on new evidence, FEP proposes the generation of internal models, or latent ‘slider positions’ which abstract high-level features of observed data to shape the mappings between latent and sensory neurons75. Active inference is a process theory derived from FEP in the context of systems with perception, action, and complex internal generative models59,76. Here, systems act to minimize the discrepancy between their internal generative model and the sensory states they encounter76. SBIs placed in closed-loop interaction with an artificial body in a virtual environment or problem space can be interpreted as preforming active inference by modifying synaptic strengths and coordinated firing patterns to reduce uncertainty about the consequences of their own activity46,64,76. Any embodied system learning to control a robotic actuator, stabilize a virtual avatar, or solve a toy problem will demonstrate a formation of internal states that anticipate the temporal statistics of stimuli59,74,77. Because the experimenter can directly manipulate perturbations, plasticity rules, sensory contingencies, and feedback, these systems offer an unprecedented platform to test whether biological neural networks spontaneously form prediction-error dynamics, and under what circumstances, when embodied to different degrees52,78. Because the tissue can be engineered by the experimenter, it may be possible to identify necessary and sufficient neural organizations for active inference (i.e., the minimal required conditions)78.

IIT proposes that the internal causal structure of a system – meaning its capacity to generate integrated, irreducible states – informs the level of integrated information, and thus consciousness or existence “for itself”70. Integrated information (Φ) is the metric with which one can quantify this property in a system, thereby inferring the likely presence or absence of consciousness amidst a background of non-integrated “ontological dust”70,79. Similarly, the algorithmic compressibility of induced activity is measured as the perturbational complexity index (PCI), which predicts human conscious states with high accuracy80. Engineered neural systems enable researchers to modulate the complexity and level of embodiment, building up minimal systems where the capacity to generate these states can be controlled46,58,81. In building these systems we can elucidate how Φ is affected by network size, recurrent connectivity, and qualities of embodiment.

Concurrent with IIT and FEP is the CBH which states that when conscious, the brain is in a ‘critical’ state, meaning the propagation of information is balanced between order and chaos, providing the maximal dynamic range of information transmission69,82,83. This theory offers a range of testable metrics as quantifiable measures69. For instance, it hypothesizes that activity in a critical substrate exhibits fractal dimensions and holds information which is maximally compressed, measured through Lempel-Ziv complexity69,83. Cultured networks have displayed these properties in multiple contexts, however, it is yet to be demonstrated how varying degrees and types of embodiment affect these metrics69,84,85. The ability to engineer tissues, such as adding or removing modular elements (e.g., cell types, extracellular matrices, biomaterials, etc.), can inform the directed design of SBIs with variable cognitive potential as measured by efficiency scores on biocomputing tasks42,82.

Considerations of energy efficiency and bandwidth

That the human brain, with its extraordinary functional complexity, is estimated to operate on approximately 20 watts of power86 while maintaining an internal temperature of 37 °C has stimulated significant discussion around the computational efficiency of biocomputing42. The benefits of SBIs are often characterized in terms of energy efficiency and, indeed, when compared to conventional supercomputers, which operate on hundreds of kilowatts to tens of megawatts at temperatures in excess of 80 °C87, the arguments do seem compelling. However, just as the brain is supported by an extended organism that outputs at least 100 watts of power to maintain metabolic flux and support multi-system (including brain) function, the energy efficiency calculus for SBI must include a “life-support tax” which accounts for all the equipment that maintains and micromanages in vitro cultures. Incubators, perfusion systems, stimulators, and other life-support systems are needed to operate SBIs, which collectively output hundreds of watts of power. Thus, it is important not to overstate the case for energy efficiency while acknowledging that, even with clusters of SBIs, biocomputing is likely more efficient than conventional computing. Interestingly, neuromorphic computers, borrowing hardware and algorithm design elements from brains and implemented within conventional inorganic substrates, operate on tens or hundreds of watts of power with individual synaptic events in the order of a picojoule (10-12 J)88. From a metabolic perspective, this is below the range of the energy consumption associated with a single action potential, which is the order of the equivalent 10-10 Joules per second per neuron89. Therefore, neuromorphic hardware may in some cases represent a lower-maintenance alternative to SBIs with similar energetic constraints.

Bandwidth mismatch is another relevant point of consideration in the broader discussion of SBI research – particularly in the context of hybrid systems for biocomputing applications. Biological neural networks, which propagate signals at rates in the order of meters per second are unable to process high-throughput data streams from digital interconnects that operate at near light speeds. This bottleneck will likely limit the use of SBIs for biocomputing to low-frequency domains involving sparse data. However, it must be noted that in vivo neural oscillations, which have been recapitulated in tissue engineered constructs90, and are known to encode the information associated with cognitive functions91, operate within these low-frequency domains ( < 100 Hz). Indeed, neural correlates of consciousness are typically within the range of 40 Hz92 oscillations while rhythms tied to memory consolidation and retrieval operate around 7 Hz93. SBIs and hybrid systems are therefore well-within range of bandwidths conventionally associated with animal neurocognition. Therefore, despite their current limitations as biocomputing tools, modern SBIs represent viable platforms for minimal cognition research.

Diverse intelligence as an extension of cybernetics

The emerging field of diverse intelligence is a modern generalization of the cybernetic approach to cognition. Michael Levin has presented the view that morphogenesis, regeneration, molecular cascades, multicellularity, and the collective actions of superorganisms are simply implementations of intelligence at different scales94,95. Consistent with the cybernetic framework, his concepts of mindedness and embodiment are not dependent on substrate or ontology. Rather, the system’s cognitive potential is best understood as situated within a given problem space. Programmable living agents like Xenobots96 or their partly mechanical hybrid robot counterparts can be understood in terms of the parameters of their sensors, processors, actuators, and feedback without rigid distinctions between living systems and machines97. At a fundamental level, the diverse intelligence and cybernetic approaches view cognitive systems as engaging in recursive problem-solving to navigate problem spaces. Both are informed by a unifying functionalism that can help reveal the existence of embodied cognitive systems in unconventional spaces and timescales. As generalizable frameworks, they can serve as theoretical foundations for engineered approaches to understanding cognition, freeing up the design space for a broader range of intelligent systems.
