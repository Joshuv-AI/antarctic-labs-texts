Arthroscopic primary repair of the anterior cruciate ligament with internal brace ligament augmentation: a viable alternative to reconstruction? A systematic literature review and analysis
Authors: Tamás Mirkó Paukovits, Tamás Bárdos, Mária Bartek, Orsolya Benda, István Berkes, Gábor János Erdélyi, Dániel Farkas, Kitti Kasza, Tamás Németh, András Pavlik, Miklós Iván Tátrai, Marcell Varga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) repair has re-gained significant interest due to its inherent anatomic advantages: retaining the proprioceptive fibers of the ACL may prevent kinesiophobia, while the procedure avoids donor site morbidity. Should revision become necessary, conventional ACL reconstruction remains a viable option. Research regarding this technique has accelerated, with 60.4% of the relevant literature published within the last 8 years.Aim of the studyThe most frequently reported technique is arthroscopic primary repair with internal brace ligament augmentation (IBLA). The objective of this study was to conduct a systematic review and analysis of contemporary papers, define the failure rate of the procedure, assess secondary outcomes, and identify optimal candidates for the procedure.Patients and methodsA systematic search of the PubMed, MEDLINE and Cochrane Library databases was conducted reported in adherence to PRISMA guidelines. The inclusion criteria encompassed English publications (Level of evidence I-IV) from the last 10 years presenting arthroscopic operations with a study population of at least 10 patients. Included studies were required to report failure rates, complication rates and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) or objective knee joint laxity assessments with a mean follow-up exceeding 12 months and retention rates exceeding 80%.ResultsTwenty-four studies, representing 866 patients, were included. The mean age at the surgery was 34.8 years, and 45.0% of the cohort was male. The mean interval from injury to surgery was 41.6 days, with a mean follow-up duration of 31.1 months. A total of 79 failures were identified, corresponding to a mean failure rate of 9.1% CI 95% [7.2;11.0] for all included patients, and 9.8% CI 95% [7.8;11.9] for followed patients (n = 804). The incidence rate of failure was 3.8% CI 95% [2.9;4.6].ConclusionPrimary ACL repair with IBLA appears to be a feasible option for a well-defined patient subset. The results from contemporary studies are promising, demonstrating acceptable complication rate and excellent PROMs at mid-term follow-up, however, generalizability remains limited, consistent with the current level II–IV evidence base.

Background

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is the most frequently injured ligament in the knee and represents one of the most common sports injuries requiring surgery [1]. Reconstruction of the ligament with a graft (ACLR) has been the gold standard operation for recent decades and remains one of the three most performed procedures in orthopedic surgery [1, 2].

Initially, primary ACL repair garnered much attention due to the advantages of this technique,’ conservative and anatomic nature: the procedure avoids donor site morbidity, and the native ACL tissue is preserved. Retaining the proprioceptive fibers of the ACL can be important for functional recovery, since the loss of these fibers may lead to kinesiophobia and a lack of confidence in the knee, which is a common residual complaint of patients following ACLR, despite the restoration of joint laxity [3]. Another advantage of primary repair is its straightforward nature of reoperation: if revision surgery is required following repair, conventional ACLR can be performed without “burning bridges”, yielding better postrevision results than ACLR revisions [4]. Despite many theoretical benefits of primary ACL repair, early series demonstrated poor outcomes. In their seminal paper, Feagin et al. presented 5-year follow-up data on open acute repair of isolated ACL tears in 32 military cadets, reporting 53% re-rupture rate and instability in 94% of patients [5]. Follow-up exceeding 30 years on the same cohort revealed progressive deterioration of the operated joints and reinforced the initial findings of the original paper from 1976 [6, 7].

The primary challenge identified was the poor healing capacity of the torn ligament attributed to compromised blood supply, the hostile environment of the synovial fluid and alterations in cell metabolism [8–10]. Moreover, instability after injury does not allow the torn ligament to heal. Although specific subgroups presented better outcomes after primary suture, particularly proximal ACL tears with excellent tissue quality [11], and despite other papers reporting good results in the repair of proximal tears, the focus was diverted toward ACL reconstruction over the last few decades [6, 12, 13].

However, the pursuit for less invasive techniques and additional reinforcement methods redirected the focus toward the physiological and biomechanical benefits of primary ACL repair [14]. Research has specifically targeted subgroups that demonstrated excellent results in early studies: proximal tears with high tissue quality [15]. A critical finding of the past decade is the ACL’s ability to heal through its fan-like anatomy and vascularization [16]. Emerging evidence suggests that ruptured ACLs may heal during conservative treatment, particularly in low-demand patient groups with increased tibial slopes [17]. By bringing the ruptured ACL ends tightly against the notch wall, healing can be promoted, subsequently stabilizing the joint [18]. Consequently, scientific interest in primary ACL repair has been revitalized [17]. Publications on novel arthroscopic surgical techniques and fixation devices [19], biologic scaffolds [20] and tape augmentation have increased exponentially in recent years, with top sports medicine journals dedication attention to this topic [21]. According to current concepts from the International Society of Arthroscopy, Knee Surgery and Orthopedic Sports Medicine (ISAKOS), anatomical repairs utilizing ligament augmentation techniques are increasingly reported, with 60.4% of the relevant literature published since 2017 [22].

The most frequently reported type of primary ACL repair is arthroscopic primary suture augmented with an internal brace [21, 22], therefore, this review focuses on this specific subgroup. Internal brace technology has been advocated for various knee ligament repairs, utilizing artificial tape for load sharing [23, 24]. In ACL repair, internal bracing is the augmentation of a repair construct with temporary or permanent single high-strength suture tape or multiple high-strength sutures, which can be fixed to intraosseous anchors or to endobuttons via drill tunnels. This reinforces the ligament and acts as a secondary stabilizer to protect against excessive elongation during the early phase of natural healing [25, 26]. A recent biomechanical cadaveric study concluded that for complete proximal ACL avulsions, primary suturing alone failed to restore normal knee stability. The addition of suture tape to augment the repair resulted in knee stability closer to that of the intact ACL [27]. Further cadaveric paper suggested that tensioning of IBLA at 20 degrees of flexion may be considered as the optimal treatment strategy [28]. Similarly, other authors emphasize tensioning in mild flexion to restore physiological anterior tibial translation (ATT) [29]. Both single suture tape and multiple sutures tolerate greater load-to-failure forces than natural ACL loads, however, multiple sutures are associated with reduced displacement after cyclic loading and greater ultimate failure strength [30]. Currently, there is a global consensus that the addition of IBLA to primary repair improves the mechanical environment of the healing construct [17].

Aim of the study

The aim of this study was to perform a systematic review and analysis of contemporary papers, define the failure rate of IBLA as the primary endpoint, assess the procedure’s secondary outcomes, and define the clinical indications and potential candidates for the procedure.

Methods

The current systematic review is presented in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020 updated guideline) statement [31]. A systematic search of the PubMed, MEDLINE and Cochrane Library databases was conducted between November 2024 and August 2025 using the following search strings: (anterior cruciate ligament OR ACL) AND (repair OR suture); (anterior cruciate ligament OR ACL) AND (primary AND repair OR suture); (anterior cruciate ligament OR ACL) AND (augmented AND repair OR suture); (anterior cruciate ligament repair OR ACL repair) AND (internal brace OR suture tape OR IBLA OR internal brace AND ligament augmentation). The studies were evaluated independently by at least two researchers, with subsequent consensus of the researchers on the included papers. The inclusion criteria were English language publications presenting arthroscopic operations from the last 10 years, with a study population of at least 10 patients. The 10-year timeframe was selected to focus exclusively on modern arthroscopic techniques utilizing novel implants and to exclude learning curve effect associated with the rebirth of IBLA procedures. Studies with fewer than 10 patients were excluded to limit low statistical power. The scope of this review included original clinical series with level of evidence I-IV, reporting failure rates, complication rates, subjective PROMs or objective knee joint laxity assessment. To minimize attrition bias, a minimum follow-up of 12 months and a lost-to-follow-up rate lower than 20% were required. Meta-analyses, systematic reviews, technical notes, expert opinions, editorials and letters to the editor were excluded, as were animal and cadaveric studies. The methodological quality and risk of bias (RoB) for the included studies were assessed using the Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies – of Interventions (ROBINS-I V2) tool. Given the heterogeneity of study designs included in this review, the assessment framework was adapted. For non-randomized studies comparing ACL repair with suture tape augmentation versus ACL reconstruction, all seven domains of ROBINS-I were evaluated. For non-comparative studies (case series) lacking a control group, the ROBINS-I tool was modified to assess internal validity. Domain 1 was graded as “not applicable”. This modification prevented these studies from automatically receiving a “critical” risk rating due to the absence of a comparator. Imaging and biomechanical studies were evaluated using the ROBINS-I framework with a focus on selection bias (Domain 2) and measurement bias (Domain 6) acknowledging the frequent lack of clinically relevant control groups in technical feasibility studies. An overall RoB judgment (Low, Moderate, Serious, or Critical) was assigned based on the “worst-of” domain rule, adjusted for the N/A status of Domain 1 in case series.

The extracted information included author, year, journal, level of evidence, number, mean age and sex proportion of included patients; interval from injury to surgery; and mean length of follow-up, including retention rate. The primary outcome was the failure rate, defined as re-rupture of the operated ACL confirmed by imaging or revision surgery for symptomatic instability. The secondary outcomes were subjective PROMs and/or objective clinical instrumental assessments of knee laxity. The number of patients reporting PROM and timing of postoperative PROM data collection was also extracted. In studies where the PROM reporting time point was not specified, the average follow-up time for the study was utilized. The level of evidence was assessed via the adjusted Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine 2011 Levels of Evidence [32]. Overall failure rate was calculated using a crude pooling approach, by summing the number of failures across all studies and dividing by the total number of patients, effectively treating all patients as a single pooled sample; 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for these proportions were derived using the binomial distribution. Additionally, to account for differences in both sample sizes and follow-up periods, the authors calculated the pooled incidence rate by dividing the total number of failures by the cumulative person-time across all studies, with 95% CIs calculated based on the Poisson distribution. Secondary outcome mean PROMs were calculated and weighted by the follow-up time and number of patients. Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel software (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA). Data presented in the reference articles did not allow the authors to calculate standard deviations as they were not reported in many source publications. No automation tools, large language models or artificial intelligence (AI) were used for data collection or in manuscript writing.

Results

Of 2783 publications identified initially, 663 papers were excluded for falling outside the 10-year timeframe, 1349 studies were removed for not presenting arthroscopic primary ACL repair with IBLA in title or abstract and 493 were excluded due to overlapping search results from other combinations of search phrases, this left 278 series for investigation (Figure 1.). The two independent researchers excluded a further 254 articles for failing to meet remaining inclusion criteria. Six level II studies, 11 level III publications and 7 level IV papers were included in the final analysis. The scope of the review comprised nine cohorts, nine case series, three matched analysis, one cross sectional comparative study, one biomechanical study and 1 imaging paper. Due to inter-study heterogeneity regarding study design, age, sex and activity level of included patients, timing of surgery, tissue quality, surgical technique and rehabilitation protocols, no direct comparative analysis was possible. The overall risk of bias across the included studies ranged from moderate to critical. Eight studies received “moderate”, 14 publications “serious” and 2 papers “critical” overall RoB rating (Table 1.). Studies utilizing propensity score matching (PSM), effectively balanced baseline covariates including age, sex, and activity level, resulting in a moderate overall risk of bias [41, 43]. These studies provided the most reliable evidence, suggesting comparable outcomes between repair and reconstruction in selected populations. In contrast, several comparative cohorts without matching, including [33, 36, 39] were rated as serious or critical due to confounding by indication. In these studies, the repair group typically comprised patients with favorable prognostic factors (proximal tears, acute timing), while the reconstruction group included chronic or mid-substance tears, invalidating a direct comparison of effectiveness. Furthermore, studies involving specific subpopulations, such as adolescents [53] or military personnel [40], exhibited serious bias due to significant baseline imbalances between groups. Among the non-comparative studies (where confounding was rated N/A), selection bias (Domain 2) and missing data (Domain 5) were the primary limitations. A high risk of selection bias was identified in studies restricting inclusion to “ideal” candidates [45] or specific populations [51], limiting generalizability. Significant loss to follow-up was noted in some series, representing attrition bias, potentially underestimating failure [45]. Conversely, Dabis et al. [50] and Hopper et al. [3] demonstrated high retention rates (> 90%), resulting in a moderate risk of bias for these domains. Imaging studies such as Van Dyck et al. [47] and biomechanical analyses like Saggar et al. [35] were rated as serious risk due to small sample sizes and the lack of clinical control groups. Wilson et al. recruited patients postoperatively representing example for critical survivorship bias [42]. The 24 included studies represented a total of 866 patients (Table 1). All 24 studies in the current review reported proximal partial or high-grade tears. One study reported complex ligament injuries after knee dislocations [34], while 22 presented primary isolated ACL tears. The mean patient age at operation was 43.8 (range 12.9–44.4) years, and 390 patients (45.0%) were male. The mean time from injury to surgery was 41.6 (range 1.0–90.0) days, and the reported follow-up length was a mean of 31.1 (range 14.8–68.4) months. Failure rate was reported in all included studies, revealing 0–40.9.9% failure of IBLA across the included studies. Total number of failures (n = 79) in the 24 included studies represented a failure rate of 9.1%, CI 95% [7.2;11.0] for all included patients, and a failure rate of 9.8%, CI 95% [7.8;11.9] for all patients (n = 804) available for follow up (92.8% retention rate). The incidence rate of failure was 3.8% CI 95% [2.9;4.6]. Postoperative PROMs were reported in 22 studies representing 735 patients (84.9%). Scores were collected at a mean of 30.7 (range 12.0–60.0) months post-procedure. The patient reported outcome measures and scoring system used across the included studies were highly heterogeneous. Where applicable, the mean postoperative resting VAS score was 0.6 and the mean exercise VAS score was 1.1 (range 1.1–1.3). Other mean scores included: KOOS 88.1 (range 83.1–96.5); IKDC 87.2 (range 73.4–92.0); Lysholm 92.2 (range 86.0–100.0); Marx 7.5 (range 5.6–8.6); Tegner activity scale 5.9 (range 5.6–7.0.6.0). One study reported mean modified Cincinnati score, 92.9. The mean ACL-RSI score was 74.8 (74.5–75.6); the single reported mean EQ-5D-5 L score was 1.0; the mean Forgotten Joint score 88.9 (range 82.0–95.0); the mean SANE score 84.4 (range 77.3–90.6). The mean VR-12 physical score was 52.0 (range 50.3–52.6) and the mean VR-12 mental score was 54.4 (range 53.1–55.0). One study reported mean PROMIS pain interference, PROMIS mobility and PROMIS physical scores of 12.0, 60.0 and 60.0 respectively. The mean WOMAC pain score was 94.8 (range 91.5–98.3); the mean WOMAC function score was 94.3 (range 91.5–97.3); and the mean WOMAC stiffness score was 88.8 (range 85.9–91.5). Ten studies reported instrument-assisted ATT measurements: One presented lower values following the IBLA procedure compared to the ACLR control group [48]. Three papers described similar ATT values between the IBLA group and contralateral healthy knees [33, 36, 50]. Four studies described nonsignificant differences in laxity between IBLA cohort and ACLR [39, 42, 43, 53], while two publications described greater translation in the IBLA cohort than in the ACLR control group [34, 49]. Only one author in this this current review, Fahlbusch et al. performed routine second-look arthroscopy, verifying intact grafts in each patient [34].

Discussion

The early results of primary repair were mixed, which could be partially attributed to the invasive nature of the open technique with relatively invasive surgery, inappropriate patient selection, the inclusion of all rupture patterns and extensive postoperative cast immobilization [5, 6, 55, 56]. Contemporary studies are highly heterogeneous regarding patient demographic data, time from injury to surgery, surgical protocols and rehabilitation.

Timing of intervention.

Several studies have demonstrated the poor healing capacity of the ACL [8–10] suggesting that ACL repair should be restricted to acute procedures [6, 13, 57, 58]. The previously described retraction and/or reabsorption of the ACL in the absence of repair was based on early animal models [56]. Based on the findings, early series suggested a maximum delay of 2 weeks from injury to repair [55, 59, 60]. However, recent literature suggest that proximal tear location, sufficient tissue length, and excellent tissue quality could be more critical determinants for successful outcomes of arthroscopic primary ACL repair than the acuity of surgery [50, 61, 62]. A current international global expert statement recommends ACL repair to be performed after prehabilitation, in the subacute phase, 2 weeks to 3 months post-injury [17].

Potential candidates and tear location.

Careful patient selection for ACL repair is paramount. In pediatric patients, primary repair with IBLA is a minimally invasive, attractive alternative, with reduced disturbance to the growth plates than ACLR does, however, similarly to ACLR in this age group, a high incidence of failure has been noted [17]. Gagliardi et al. reported 40.9% failure rate for IBLA in a series of 22 patients at the mean age of 12.9 years and encouraged surgeons to consider the high rate of early failure in adolescent patients [53]. Ferreira et al. observed significantly higher re-rupture rates (5.3% vs. 0.0%) in patients under 21 years of age following IBLA compared to ACLR, whereas no significant difference was observed in patients over 21 [43]. Schneider et al. published a case series of 86 IBLA patients. The authors concluded that sex, age, BMI, interval from injury to surgery, and concomitant meniscal and collateral injuries had no significant impact on the survival rates [38]. Conversely, Cruz et al. identified age under 17 and above 35 years, elite-level of competition, time from injury to surgery, and active tobacco use as independent risk factors following IBLA [40]. Most studies conclude that proximal (Sherman type I) ruptures with excellent vascularity of the ACL remnant are ideal for augmented primary repair [37, 63–66]. Current evidence does not support direct repair of midsubstance tears due to limited healing capacity [67], consequently, all contemporary studies include proximal ruptures exclusively [68].

Re-rupture rates and secondary outcomes.

The mean failure rate of the current systematic review is in unison with the results of a large previous meta-analysis, reporting 10.4% re-rupture rate at a minimum of 24.0 months follow-up [69]. The longest available follow-up in the relevant literature (mean 68 months) presented 37 consecutive patients undergoing primary repair with IBLA within 3 months of injury. Six patients experienced re-rupture, representing a 17.6% failure rate [3]. Jonkergouw et al. reported a 7.4% re-rupture rate with internal bracing versus 13.8% without at a mean follow-up of 38.4 months, emphasizing the importance of additional augmentation [27]. A recent large series of 86 IBLA patients revealed 90% revision-free survival rate after 48.0 months [38]. Góralczyk et al. reported no failures in a series of 20 patients, concluding that IBLA restored native knee laxity and neuromuscular control better than ACLR in a young and active population [33]. Conversely, a current level II study reported an “unacceptably” high re-rupture rate (23.1%) in high-demand military population at a mean 37.2-month follow-up [40]. Fahlbusch et al. demonstrated the use of IBLA construct in the treatment of complex ligamentous injuries following Schenck type III. and IV. knee dislocations. ACL was treated either with IBLA or conventional ACLR. Both types of procedures were accompanied by simultaneous primary repair of the PCL and augmented repair of the medial and lateral complexes. Although the IBLA group demonstrated greater ATT on stress radiography, both groups presented excellent PROMs [34]. Szwedowski et al. reported significantly lower ATT in 14 IBLA patients compared to 15 ACLR patients at a mean 14.4 months follow-up [48]. Simard et al., comparing IBLA, acute ACLR and delayed ACLR, found no significant differences in instrumented side-to-side laxity or PROMs at two years, concluding that suture tape augmentation offered outcomes comparable to ACLR in every aspect [36]. Similarly, Mattiassich et al. found IBLA outcomes comparable to ACLR [49]. Karlin et al. presented significantly improved patient-reported outcomes measurement information system scores (PROMIS) in 20 IBLA patients compared to 54 ACLR patients [37], and Douoguih et al. reported noninferior re-rupture rates, ATT, KOOS and IKDC scores at 2 years [39]. A recent meta-analysis of comparative found no significant difference in re-rupture rates or PROMs between IBLA and ACLR [70]. According the consensus statement, ACL primary repair can clinical outcomes equivalent to ACLR when indications are appropriate [17].

Rehabilitation.

No exact consensus exists regarding rehabilitation protocols following primary ACL repair with IBLA. In the largest cohort study to date with a minimum 5-year follow-up, patients were permitted full weight bearing, with physiotherapy focusing on early range of motion (ROM), muscle control and functional restoration without external bracing [3]. Wilson et al. found no significant difference in hamstring or quadriceps strength between the IBLA group and the healthy controls, whereas muscle strength and PROMs were lowest in the ACLR group. However, due to study design these results likely represent best-case scenarios for IBLA [42]. A systematic review by Wu et al. noted greater variation in IBLA postoperative rehabilitation protocols, than after ACLR, reporting short, less than 3-week periods of bracing or no bracing, immediate 0–90 degrees of ROM and weight bearing with early strengthening [71]. Zhang et al. suggested initially following ACLR rehabilitation protocols to facilitate RTS data comparison [17]. Despite increasing frequency of rehabilitation recommendations for IBLA patients, high-level evidence regarding optimal rehabilitation remains unavailable [72].

Limitations of the current analysis

This systematic review has several limitations. Crude pooling was used in the current study to calculate the overall failure rate, summing events and sample sizes across studies. This descriptive approach does not account for study-level heterogeneity or employ fixed- or random-effects modeling, so the estimate should be interpreted as an overall summary rather than a precise meta-analytic effect. Standard deviations could not be calculated as they were not reported in many source publications. Available data on IBLA is observational (Levels of evidence II-IV), with short to mid-term follow-up and moderate to critical RoB. The included studies are heterogeneous regarding study design, patient age, sex distribution, activity level, timing of surgery, tissue quality, surgical techniques and rehabilitation protocols making inter-study comparative analysis difficult. Statistical methods and reporting of variables are also inconsistent across the available studies. Results should therefore be interpreted and compared with caution. The term “IBLA” encompasses various techniques, implants and fixations. Furthermore, indications for the procedure are not standardized, primary and secondary outcomes are defined diversely across studies. Little is known regarding the learning curve or the incidence of posttraumatic osteoarthritis after primary ACL repair with IBLA [17].

Conclusion

Primary ACL repair with IBLA may promote healing and appears to be a feasible option with acceptable short- to mid-term outcomes in a well-defined patient group. Results from contemporary studies are encouraging, but with limited generalizability, consistent with the available level II–IV evidence base. Current conclusions should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Careful patient selection and the timing of surgery are imperative to success. Given the necessity of excellent tissue quality for favorable outcomes, younger nonelite athletes (over 21 years), presenting within 3 months of injury with proximal (Type I) partial or complete ruptures may be the most suitable candidates for this procedure. A high proportion of ACL injuries may fit these criteria. Well-designed studies with long-term follow-up are required to establish definitive indications of primary internal brace-augmented ACL repair. Until high-level evidence data proves otherwise, ACLR remains the gold standard procedure.
