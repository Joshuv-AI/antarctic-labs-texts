Quantitative Evaluation of Geographic Atrophy Progression Using Geographic Atrophy Lesion Front Displacement
Authors: Shinichiro Chujo, Jiwon Baek, Akihito Uji, Alberto Quarta, Yu-Chien Chung, Hyunduck Kwak, Ceren Soylu, Rouzbeh Abbasgholizadeh, Mai Alhelaly, Raiyna Rattu, Jianfeng Huang, Giulia Corradetti, Swetha Velaga, Muneeswar Gupta Nittala, Srinivas R. Sadda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the temporal behavior and relative variability of a margin-based metric, lesion front displacement, for quantifying geographic atrophy (GA) progression secondary to age-related macular degeneration and to compare its performance with conventional metrics.MethodsThis retrospective longitudinal analysis included 103 untreated fellow eyes from the MAHALO clinical trial with GA and 18-month follow-up. Fundus autofluorescence images were obtained at baseline and 6, 12, and 18 months. Five progression metrics were calculated: total area change, square root area change, total perimeter change, perimeter-adjusted growth rate (λ′PA), and lesion front displacement. Linearity was assessed using simple linear regression and the Harvey–Collier test. Variability was evaluated using standard deviation, variance ratios, and the Fligner–Killeen test.ResultsLinearity was rejected for area and square root area change but not for lesion front displacement, perimeter change, or λ′PA. Lesion front displacement showed a relatively higher coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.504), whereas area (0.116), square root area (0.104), perimeter (0.001), and λ′PA (0.018) exhibited lower goodness of fit to a linear model. Lesion front displacement demonstrated lower dispersion across follow-up intervals. Variance ratio analysis indicated greater variability for all comparator metrics. Variability was significantly lower for lesion front displacement than for perimeter change and λ′PA at 12 and 18 months (P < 0.05).ConclusionsLesion front displacement demonstrated more consistent linear approximation and lower variability compared with several conventional GA progression metrics. This margin-based approach may serve as a complementary metric for longitudinal assessment of GA progression.

Geographic atrophy (GA), the advanced form of nonneovascular age-related macular degeneration (AMD), is a progressive and irreversible cause of visual impairment worldwide.1,2 Affecting over 5 million individuals globally, its prevalence is expected to rise with population aging.3 Once established, GA enlarges inexorably and often leads to central vision loss following foveal involvement.4 With the recent approval of complement inhibitors and advances in gene therapy, slowing GA progression has become a realistic clinical goal.5–8 In parallel, artificial intelligence (AI), including deep learning, is being investigated for GA progression prediction, with implications for disease understanding and individualized management.9–12 In this evolving therapeutic landscape, accurate and quantitative assessment of GA enlargement is essential for mechanistic insight, treatment evaluation, and visual prognosis prediction.

Traditionally, GA progression has been evaluated using metrics such as total lesion area, square root–transformed area, and lesion perimeter.2,13–18 However, these indices are susceptible to variations in lesion morphology and may be less robust in the presence of asymmetric progression or structural changes.19 The perimeter-adjusted growth rate was introduced to reduce shape dependency,20 but over extended follow-up, events such as lesion merging or the development of new atrophic foci may alter lesion perimeter and increase variability.20

Thus, there is a growing need to establish more accurate and morphology-independent metrics for evaluating GA progression. This is especially critical as changes in the rate of growth may be relied upon by clinicians to determine whether therapeutics are having a beneficial effect on the course of disease for a given, individual patient. Our group has previously proposed a novel method, termed the GA lesion front displacement, which directly measures the directional expansion of lesion margins, and we have reported its initial feasibility.21 We hypothesized that this method, being less susceptible to lesion shape and capable of capturing local progression with high precision, could serve as a reliable and robust metric for long-term GA follow-up. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the clinical utility and reproducibility of the GA lesion front displacement by comparing it with conventional metrics.

Methods

Study Design

The MAHALO study (NCT01229215) was a phase 1b/2 multicenter, randomized, single-masked, sham-controlled trial evaluating the safety, tolerability, and activity of lampalizumab in patients with GA secondary to AMD. Eligible patients had bilateral GA without any history or evidence of macular neovascularization. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of California–Los Angeles and the Ethics Committee (approval number: IRB-15-0083). For this analysis, we included 103 untreated fellow eyes that received neither lampalizumab nor sham injections and had fundus autofluorescence (FAF) images available at all scheduled visits. These eyes provided longitudinal imaging data suitable for evaluating the natural course of GA progression. All analyses were performed at the eye level, with one eye included per participant; therefore, no within-subject (intereye) correlation was present.

Our study aimed to quantitatively evaluate the progression of GA using longitudinal FAF imaging over an 18-month period. FAF images were acquired using a 30° field centered on the fovea with the Spectralis HRA+OCT system (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Images obtained at four time points—baseline (0 months), 6 months (6 M), 12 months (12 M), and 18 months (18 M)—were included for analysis.

At each time point, GA lesions were semi-automatically delineated by a certified grader (MGN) at the Doheny Image Reading Center using the Spectralis Region Finder software. The resulting mask images were subsequently reviewed by a second certified grader (SC), who made any required adjustments to ensure that all lesion boundaries were precisely delineated. All mask images were managed and processed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The foveal center was identified using multimodal imaging. It was primarily defined on the structural optical coherence tomography (OCT) B-scan as the point of maximal foveal depression with absence of inner retinal layers, with FAF and infrared reflectance images used as complementary references to ensure cross-modality consistency.

Quantification of Local Lesion Edge Progression

To quantitatively evaluate local GA lesion progression, we used our previously reported method, which divides GA lesions into 12 clock-hour sectors and calculates the progression distance in each direction.21 A Euclidean distance map (EDM) was first generated from the baseline mask, upon which the follow-up mask was overlaid to extract peripheral distance profiles. Sectoral expansion distances were then measured across 12 clock-hour directions, and the average value was calculated to visualize the GA progression as a heatmap (Fig. 1).

Image Registration

To ensure accurate measurement of boundary displacement/advancement, all follow-up FAF images were rigidly registered to the baseline image using the enhanced correlation coefficient (ECC) algorithm. The ECC algorithm is an iterative, intensity-based optimization method that maximizes the correlation coefficient between images and estimates optimal alignment by iteratively updating transformation parameters, including translation and rotation.22

In this study, registration was performed using the ECC algorithm, while lesion morphology observed in the baseline and follow-up images was used as a reference to verify the validity of alignment. The foveal center was additionally used as an anatomic landmark to confirm consistency across modalities (Figs. 2A, 2B).

(A) Preregistration mask images showing misalignment across time points. Upper panels show FAF images at baseline and 6, 12, and 18 months, illustrating unaligned lesion positions across time points. Lower panels display corresponding binary masks derived from FAF images. As illustrated by the heatmap, the absence of image registration leaves residual misalignment between visits, making accurate assessment of lesion expansion difficult. GA Progression Metrics and Calculation Methods

In this study, we quantified the progression of GA using the following five metrics:

Lesion Area

The area of all GA lesions was measured at each time point, and the slope of area progression was calculated using the following formula: Areaslopemm2/year=Areafollow-up-Areabaselinetfollow-up-tbaseline

This value, representing the annualized increase in GA lesion area, is hereafter referred to as the “area change.”

Square Root of Area

The square root of the GA lesion area was calculated at each time point to reduce the dependence on baseline lesion size, and the slope of square root area progression was calculated using the following formula: Squarerootareaslopemm/year=Areafollow-up-Areabaselinetfollow-up-tbaseline

This value represents the annualized increase in the square root–transformed GA lesion area and is referred to as the “square root area change” throughout the article.

Lesion Perimeter

The perimeter of each GA lesion was measured from the binarized lesion mask at each time point. Lesion boundaries were defined based on the segmented mask, and the perimeter was calculated as the length of the boundary contour using ImageJ-based analysis. Measurements were converted to millimeters using the pixel resolution of the images (approximately 11.5 µm/pixel), based on a 30° field of view and a 768 × 768-pixel image resolution. The slope of perimeter progression was then calculated for each lesion using the following formula: Perimeterslopemm/year=Perimeterfollow-up-Perimeterbaselinetfollow-up-tbaseline

This value, representing the annualized increase in GA lesion perimeter, is hereafter referred to as the “perimeter change.”

Perimeter-Adjusted Area Growth Rate

The perimeter-adjusted area growth rate was calculated based on previously reported methodology, using the following formula20: perimeteradjustedareagrowthratemm/year=Atf-Atbtf-tb·Ptf+Ptb2

As this formula inherently normalizes area change by time interval (Δt), the resulting value can be regarded as a slope over each specified interval (e.g., 0–6M, 0–12M, 0–18M) and was therefore treated as a slope metric in this study. This metric, which adjusts GA area growth by the mean perimeter across the interval, is hereafter referred to as “λ′PA.”

Lesion Front Displacement

The GA lesion front displacement was calculated using a previously established image-processing pipeline, which involved generating an EDM from the baseline lesion mask, overlaying the follow-up lesion on the EDM, and extracting directional expansion distances along 12 clock-hour sectors.21 The average of these sector-wise distances was defined as the GA lesion front displacement. In eyes with multiple GA lesions, each lesion was segmented separately, and its boundary was divided into 12 clock-hour sectors to measure the lesion front displacement. The results from all lesions were averaged to quantify the overall pattern of atrophy expansion. The change in GA lesion front displacement over time was calculated by dividing the difference in mean front between time points by the interval (Δt) as follows: Lesionfrontdisplacementmm/year=GAlesionfrontdisplacementΔt

This value is referred to as the “lesion front displacement” throughout the remainder of the article.

Normalization and Analytical Framework

Each metric was assessed at four time points: baseline, 6 M, 12 M, and 18 M. For all metrics, the rate of progression slope was calculated for three intervals using baseline as the reference point: 0–6 M, 0–12 M, and 0–18 M. Notably, lesion front displacement and λ′PA are defined as interval differences and therefore cannot be expressed as percent change from baseline, in contrast to total area change. To enable comparison across metrics with different units and distributions, we performed median-based normalization. Median normalization helps reduce the influence of outliers and facilitates direct comparison across metrics by placing them on a common scale. This process allowed for robust evaluation of the linear approximation of progression across different metrics. As lesion front displacement and λ′PA represent interval-based slope metrics, conventional trajectory-based comparisons were not suitable. Therefore, temporal behavior was evaluated using regression-based approximation and variability analyses tailored to slope-defined outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

In this study, we use the terms “temporal behavior,” “linear approximation,” and “variability” to describe distinct but related properties of GA progression metrics. Temporal behavior refers broadly to how each metric changes over time across predefined follow-up intervals.

Linear approximation specifically denotes the extent to which longitudinal changes in each metric can be reasonably described by a linear function of time over the observation period. Linear approximation may be useful in progression metrics, as an approximately linear trajectory can facilitate interpretation of growth rates and comparison across time intervals, although it does not imply that the underlying biological process is strictly linear.19 In contrast, variability refers to the consistency of slope estimates across eyes, which is operationally reflected by lower dispersion of slopes.

Although related, linear approximation and variability represent different characteristics: a metric may exhibit low dispersion (stable slopes) yet deviate from linear approximation or, conversely, approximate linearity while showing high intereye variability. Accordingly, linear approximation and relative variability were evaluated separately in the present analysis.

To characterize the temporal behavior of each progression metric, simple linear regression models were applied to pooled longitudinal measurements from all included eyes, with time (months) as the independent variable. The coefficient of determination (R2) was calculated as a descriptive measure of goodness of fit, indicating the extent to which a linear model approximated the observed data. Formal assessment of deviation from linearity was performed using the Harvey–Collier test. Residuals were calculated at each follow-up time point (6, 12, and 18 months), and residual plots, along with scatterplots with fitted regression lines, were visually inspected to assess potential deviation from linearity. The objective of this regression analysis was to compare approximation behavior across metrics rather than to model subject-specific trajectories. Therefore, mixed-effects modeling was not adopted.

To characterize relative variability, the standard deviation (SD) of calculated slopes was compared across all eyes. Differences in variance were evaluated using the Fligner–Killeen test, a nonparametric method suitable for comparing group variances without assuming normality. Because the evaluated progression metrics differ in mathematical structure and transformation properties, scale-normalized indices such as the coefficient of variation were not adopted. Instead, variance ratios were used as a descriptive measure of relative dispersion and were defined as Varianceratio=VarlesionfrontdisplacementslopeVarComparisonmetricslope

A variance ratio less than 1 indicates lower dispersion of GA front slopes relative to the comparator metric. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.10), with a two-sided significance level set at P < 0.05.

Results

GA Area at Baseline and Change During Follow-up

The mean GA area at baseline was 6.88 ± 5.32 mm2. Over the 18-month follow-up period, the mean change in GA area was 2.55 ± 1.80 mm2, confirming measurable progression in this cohort (P < 0.001).

Temporal Behavior and Linear Approximation

Based on formal linearity testing using the Harvey–Collier test, linearity was rejected for area change and square root area change but not for lesion front displacement, perimeter change, or λ′PA (Table 1). Figure 3 presents the median-normalized longitudinal changes of each progression metric. (Figure 3). R2 values are presented as descriptive indices of goodness of fit and were not interpreted as formal tests of linearity. Lesion front displacement exhibited a relatively higher R2 value (0.504), suggesting a comparatively better linear approximation than the other metrics. In contrast, area change (R2 = 0.116), square root area change (R2 = 0.104), and λ′PA (R2 = 0.018) demonstrated lower coefficients of determination. Although linearity was not statistically rejected for perimeter change, the corresponding R2 was very low (0.001), indicating minimal explanatory power of a linear model over time (Table 1). Notably, during the 0- to 18-month period, area change appeared to increase more prominently in the 12- to 18-month interval compared with earlier intervals, consistent with deviation from a simple linear approximation.

Discussion

In this study, we compared conventional metrics used to evaluate GA progression—including area change, square root area change, perimeter change, and λ′PA—versus a novel metric, lesion front displacement. Lesion front displacement showed a relatively higher coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.504) and relatively lower dispersion over long-term follow-up compared with several comparator metrics, suggesting that it may serve as a complementary metric for more stable longitudinal assessment of GA progression.

The strength of the lesion front displacement lies in its ability to minimize the influence of lesion morphology while providing a structurally consistent assessment of disease progression. The expansion of GA lesions fundamentally occurs through the outward growth of their borders, with the earliest observable change being the advancement of the lesion margin itself.2 In contrast, changes in total area or perimeter are considered secondary outcomes that result from this primary process. Indeed, several previous studies have supported the assumption that GA borders progress at a relatively constant rate over time,15,16,23,24 reinforcing the concept that measuring the lesion front displacement provides a more direct structural descriptor of GA progression.

On the other hand, conventional area-based metrics are influenced by baseline lesion size with a tendency to overestimate progression in small lesions and underestimate it in large ones.2,24,25 To address this issue, square root transformation has been introduced; however, this method assumes a circular geometry and may lose accuracy in irregularly shaped or multifocal lesions. Indeed, Domalpally et al.15 reported significantly faster growth rates in noncircular GA lesions, and a meta-analysis by Shen et al.19 showed that multifocal GA lesions exhibited approximately 46% higher square root growth rates compared to unifocal lesions. In addition, the merging of multiple lesions during follow-up may lead to the formation of a larger confluent lesion, which can subsequently accelerate the rate of progression, further contributing to variability in estimated growth patterns and a lack of linearity, particularly in individual cases.

λ′PA has been proposed as a shape-independent metric and has received attention for its potential utility in assessing GA progression.20 However, in the present study, λ′PA showed less consistent temporal behavior compared with lesion front displacement with respect to linear approximation and variability. During the longer follow-up intervals (0–12 M and 0–18 M), lesion front displacement exhibited lower dispersion relative to λ′PA. One possible explanation is that structural features such as multifocal lesion configurations and lesion merging may disproportionately influence perimeter-based measurements, potentially increasing variability in metrics that rely on perimeter normalization.19,20,24 Consistent with this interpretation, perimeter change showed the greatest variation in mean values among the evaluated metrics, and variability differed from that of front change across follow-up intervals. These findings suggest that λ′PA may be more sensitive to lesion morphology and structural heterogeneity over time. In this context, lesion front displacement is defined by local boundary advancement and is therefore less dependent on global changes in lesion morphology, suggesting a limited impact of lesion coalescence. However, as coalescence alters boundary configuration, it may still affect local measurements, for example, by changing the direction of boundary advancement. Ongoing work aims to further characterize this effect.

Differences from previous reports19,20 may partly reflect methodological variations, including differences in imaging modality and study design. In contrast, our study incorporated multimodal imaging, including FAF and OCT, and employed pixel-level rigid registration using the ECC algorithm, which may allow more consistent margin-based assessment.

The lesion front displacement metric proposed in this study is conceptually related to the border expansion rate described by Shen et al.,26 which was introduced as a perimeter-adjusted approach to characterizing GA progression. While both metrics aim to quantify lesion expansion at the advancing margin, differences in study design and methodology should be noted. Shen et al.26 evaluated GA progression using color fundus photography with manual image registration, whereas the present study employed a multimodal imaging approach incorporating FAF and OCT, together with pixel-level alignment based on the ECC algorithm. In addition, our analysis included four longitudinal time points (baseline and 6, 12, and 18 months), allowing assessment of temporal behavior across multiple intervals. These methodological differences may contribute to variations in metric behavior and should be considered when interpreting results across studies.

Lesion front displacement may serve as a complementary and objective outcome measure for evaluating the therapeutic effects of emerging interventions such as complement inhibitors and gene therapies. While area-based metrics have traditionally been used to assess GA progression, previous studies have suggested that these measures may exhibit deviations from linear approximation over long-term follow-up, which can complicate interpretation of progression rates.27,28 Consistent with these observations, area-based metrics showed less consistent temporal behavior in certain extended follow-up intervals; however, differences between lesion front displacement and area-based metrics were not uniformly significant, indicating that the relative performance of each metric may depend on study design, follow-up interval, and lesion characteristics.

Furthermore, although λ′PA has been proposed as a shape-independent metric, it demonstrated greater dispersion and less consistent temporal behavior compared with lesion front displacement, suggesting that its temporal behavior may be more influenced by lesion configuration in certain settings. In contrast, lesion front displacement directly quantifies the outward expansion of lesion margins—an anatomic and pathophysiologic all-relevant feature of GA progression—and may provide a consistent framework for characterizing treatment-associated changes in progression rate.

Moreover, many previous studies utilizing deep learning for GA progression prediction have adopted area-based metrics as outcome variables. Because these metrics inherently reflect nonlinear growth behavior, their use may complicate prediction and interpretation, particularly over long-term follow-up.29–31 In this context, metrics that are structurally grounded and amenable to linear approximation, such as lesion front displacement, may facilitate the development of more interpretable prediction models. GA front may be less influenced by morphological events such as lesion merging or new lesion formation, although further validation is warranted.

This study has several limitations. First, the reading center was masked to visual acuity and other functional parameters from the MAHALO dataset, preventing assessment of the lesion front displacement metric's correlation with visual function. Second, lesion front displacement was calculated using a custom analysis environment based on Euclidean distance maps. For clinical adoption, automated processing and integration into existing workflows will be required. In this study, lesion front displacement was averaged across 12 clock-hour sectors, which may obscure directional or lesion-specific effects. Future refinements may incorporate lesion size–weighted or directionally informed analyses, including consideration of foveal proximity. Incorporating lesion directionality and foveal proximity may enable a more accurate linkage between structural progression and visual function. Studies integrating such spatial factors may further improve the interpretability and clinical relevance of progression metrics such as lesion front displacement. To further address these issues, we are currently conducting ongoing work that integrates lesion front displacement with local photoreceptor density distributions reported by Curcio et al.32–34 to analyze progression toward the fovea and the periphery. In the present study, lesion front displacement was evaluated as an average across all directions to enable comparison with conventional metrics. However, because the method computes boundary displacement for each direction, it allows direction-specific extraction and quantification of foveal and peripheral progression. Such analyses may enable incorporation of both expansion distance and directionality into this metric, providing a framework that accommodates limitations in registration accuracy while remaining clinically applicable.

In addition, this study analyzed fellow eyes from the MAHALO clinical trial cohort. Because clinical trial populations often include patients with bilateral and relatively faster-progressing GA, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings. Validation in independent cohorts will be necessary before extrapolating these results to interventional trial design or formal sample size estimation.

Conclusions

We propose lesion front displacement as a novel metric for characterizing GA progression based on the directional expansion of lesion margins. This metric is structurally grounded and relatively less dependent on lesion morphology and demonstrated more consistent temporal behavior and lower variability compared with conventional area-based metrics. Direct quantification of boundary advancement may provide a metric that improves the consistency and interpretability of progression assessment at both the individual patient level and in clinical trials, particularly in situations where conventional area-based metrics are unstable or difficult to interpret.
