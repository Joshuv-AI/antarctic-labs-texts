Multi-temporal analysis of mountainous terrain changes based on UAV images and point cloud data
Authors: Tsung-Chin Hou, Thanh Bao Nhut Trinh, Tzu-Yi Yang, Jia-Ying Lu, Bao-Tian Chiang, Shun-Hong Zhou, Jiun-Shyan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate multi-temporal terrain change detection in mountainous regions is crucial for disaster monitoring and environmental management; however, it remains challenging due to the complex topography, dense vegetation, and occlusion. This study proposes an integrated, automated framework for terrain change detection that combines 2D semantic segmentation and 3D geometric analysis, utilizing UAV imagery and point cloud data. This method uses the DeepLabV3 neural network to perform multi-class semantic segmentation of orthophotos, combining Fast Point Feature Histograms (FPFH) and Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) algorithms, followed by Iterative Closest Point (ICP) based local refinement. Geometric changes are subsequently quantified using the Multiscale Model-to-Model Cloud Comparison (M3C2) distance metric. When applied to 0.06 km2 of mountainous terrain in the Guanziling area, Tainan City, Taiwan, between January 2024 and January 2025, the framework achieved a mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) of 87.05% in semantic segmentation and a root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 4.2 cm in geometric registration for rigid structures. The model’s capacity to generalize was evaluated by validation against 100 independent, manually annotated tiles, which showed a strong coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.9251) between the predicted and actual change proportions. Comparisons in complex “Medium Change” zones showed that while 2D-only methods identified 9% of changes due to shadows and occlusion, the integrated 3D analysis successfully detected physical displacements averaging 2.12 m. Overall, the proposed 2D–3D fusion framework enables reliable terrain change detection and supports post-disaster assessment, landslide monitoring, and infrastructure management in complex mountainous environments.Keywords: UAV images, Point cloud data, Terrain change detection, Multi-temporal analysis, Deep learningSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Studying terrain changes, especially in mountainous areas, is essential for environmental monitoring and database updating1. As the impact of climate change intensifies, the frequency and intensity of natural disasters have been increasing2. In addition, mountainous areas are prone to various natural disasters due to their steep terrain and complex geological conditions3. Earthquakes can cause severe ground deformation and landslides. Rapid post-disaster assessments can effectively support emergency rescue and loss assessment4,5. Landslides are another common natural disaster, and an increasing number of areas are flooding due to heavy rain3,6. Events such as reservoir filling and impoundment can trigger large landslides and riverbank collapses that damage roads and buildings7.

Monitoring terrain changes in mountainous areas poses significant challenges, especially as the complex terrain can affect the quality and accuracy of image acquisition8. The diverse and irregular terrain of the mountainous regions is prone to partial occlusion, resulting in insufficient image coverage, which in turn makes image matching difficult, and errors are often concentrated at the edges of the study area9. Dense vegetation frequently obscures the ground, resulting in low ground-point density and elevation values in the point cloud, which in turn reduces the reliability of terrain information and can even mask actual terrain deformation10. Shadows on steep surfaces, especially in historical black-and-white aerial photographs, can also cause insufficient contrast and data omissions, which affect photogrammetric reconstruction results11. Therefore, high-resolution data are required to capture small-scale changes, as the quality of the detected changes is susceptible to factors such as point density and distribution12. When the image resolution is low, it is challenging to accurately detect small structures and subtle terrain changes, making it difficult to distinguish them from the surrounding environment13. These challenges underscore the importance of high-resolution point cloud data for accurate 3D change detection.

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have become increasingly important tools for terrain monitoring in complex mountainous terrain3. Compared with traditional surveying methods, UAVs require less space for takeoff and landing and provide high flexibility and stability, resulting in distinct advantages under mountainous conditions9. Through pre-planned flight routes covering the entire survey area, spatial information of the disaster area can be quickly obtained, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional methods regarding equipment deployment locations and line-of-sight constraints14,15. UAVs can capture high-resolution photos while flying at low altitudes. These images can then be used to generate point cloud data with centimeter-level resolution16. For post-disaster rescue, reconstruction, and damage monitoring, they can precisely reconstruct three-dimensional models and provide comprehensive information on ground geometry and surface texture4,17. Compared with conventional surveying techniques such as total stations and terrestrial laser scanning, UAV-based monitoring generally involves lower operational costs, particularly in complex or large-scale terrains. In addition, the rapid data acquisition capability and highly automated processing workflow make UAV photogrammetry a practical and cost-efficient solution for large-area data collection and long-term monitoring applications15,18.

This study uses high-resolution UAV imagery and its derived point cloud data captured over challenging mountainous terrain to perform change detection. Through the use of a deep learning model, DeepLabv3 is employed to perform semantic segmentation on high-resolution images, accurately classifying various land cover types, including buildings, vegetation, and roads, thereby significantly improving the efficiency and accuracy of multi-class semantic segmentation19,20. In addition, UAV photogrammetry technology is used to generate dense point clouds, combined with airborne LiDAR (ALS) data and digital surface model (DSM), as an important method for obtaining building contours and detecting changes in buildings21–23. The above process can be applied to complex mountainous areas, enabling precise, automated, and multi-temporal assessment of building changes through UAV remote sensing technology.

These technologies not only provide efficient and cost-effective data collection but also enable accurate analysis of changes in buildings and landforms. After natural disasters such as landslides or earthquakes in mountainous areas, the use of multi-temporal UAV data and DSM enables effective change detection and assessment of the extent of building damage and terrain variations in the affected regions, allowing for timely rescue operations1. Furthermore, accurate geospatial information can be applied in various domains, such as reconstruction planning, thereby effectively addressing the limitations of traditional surveying techniques23. A UAV-based methodology is developed and evaluated in this study to detect and quantify multi-temporal topographic changes in complex mountainous environments. By combining 3D point cloud analysis with 2D semantic segmentation of high-resolution orthophotos, the proposed workflow aims to improve data alignment and consistency in processing across different acquisition periods. The accuracy of point cloud-based change detection against manually annotated ground truth, the spatial distribution and size of terrain changes, and the influence of data quality and processing parameters on detection outcomes are among the specific goals.

This work presents a comprehensive framework that integrates deep learning-based semantic segmentation with 3D point cloud analysis to address challenges in tracking terrain changes in mountainous locations. The primary contribution is the integration of DeepLabv3 for high-precision multi-class annotation of complex surface features. Robustness of this method is achieved through the complementary use of semantic and geometric information. Even under challenging conditions such as steep terrain, dense vegetation, and shading, the combination of semantic labeling and robust point cloud registration enables stable, interpretable measurement of 3D geometric changes. In addition to its methodological contributions, the proposed workflow offers a valuable tool for post-disaster assessment. Long-term environmental monitoring, rebuilding planning, and disaster response all benefit from well-informed decision-making.

Literature review

The use of UAVs in the architecture, engineering, and construction (AEC) industry has grown from 20.7% in 2015 to 43% in 202017. They were initially deployed in different fields, including military reconnaissance and disaster search and rescue1,24. With advances in science and technology over the past two decades, UAVs have improved image resolution and spatial data-collection capabilities, often surpassing the cost-effectiveness of traditional satellite imagery in some cases, and have revolutionized remote sensing technology15,25. In addition, the flexibility of UAV flight planning and execution allows users to define application-specific spatiotemporal resolutions and to generate high-resolution time series of terrain changes, which is particularly advantageous for monitoring dynamic environments9. UAVs have obvious advantages in acquiring large amounts of spatial data from built environments and complex natural terrain26,27. In the forestry field, UAVs can monitor urban tree cover and identify plant components, enhance vegetation surveys, and support land use or land cover (LULC) monitoring28. UAV-based systems aid in reconstructing past topography, identifying long-term landscape changes, and monitoring intricate natural phenomena such as landslides and ground subsidence caused by mining9,11. UAVs are also critical for urban planning and management, enabling quick and accurate assessments of buildings after disasters, detecting changes in urban form, and conducting detailed as-built surveys17,29.

Point clouds constitute a fundamental data source for 3D scene reconstruction, providing a sampled representation of object surfaces that is free from the perspective distortions inherent in 2D imagery29. LiDAR and photogrammetry are the primary techniques for collecting point cloud data. LiDAR employs laser light to provide high-density 3D point data, whereas photogrammetry uses multi-temporal images, such as those from Structure-from-Motion (SfM), to reconstruct the 3D point cloud1,30. Point cloud data processing first requires removing noise from the raw data, then aligning point cloud data from different phases or sources to the same coordinate system to ensure data consistency, and finally classifying them into ground, buildings, and vegetation11,30,31. The diverse applications of point cloud data are mainly in object detection, where urban objects such as buildings, trees, and roads are accurately extracted, or in the detection of changes in large-scale areas by generating accurate digital terrain models (DTMs) and DSMs from point clouds30,32.

Detecting changes in mountain topography is a challenging process, primarily influenced by factors such as shadow interference, dense vegetation, and terrain undulation. To address these challenges, the research field has developed various detection methods, each with its own advantages and limitations. The first method is image differencing, which is used to detect changes directly by comparing 2D images or DSMs from different time periods1. However, shadows, vegetation interference, and the undulation of steep terrain may all affect30. The second method, point cloud differencing, uses multi-temporal 3D point cloud data for change detection, directly capturing 3D geometric changes and being relatively unaffected by illumination and shading. To address occlusion issues, voxel-based or octree-based space occupancy analysis is commonly employed1. However, there are vertical and horizontal deviations in the point cloud data acquired at different time points23, which may lead to false change detections in the DSM difference map30,32. The third method, object-based change detection, segments the image into objects (e.g., buildings, vegetation) with semantic information. This method can efficiently differentiate geometrically similar vegetation and built structures33 by using the morphological aspects of objects34 and their spectral characteristics28. It also shows improved stability against noise. However, this approach is heavily reliant on the precision of previous object segmentation and classification outputs1. Therefore, poor segmentation can directly affect subsequent change detection results. Even when buildings are surrounded by trees or densely packed objects, misclassification or merging multiple buildings into a single object can still occur32.

Semantic segmentation is a fundamental technique for assigning semantic labels to individual pixels and has become increasingly important in image-based terrain analysis. This technique has a wide range of applications in robust infrastructure planning, accurate updating of geographic databases, and comprehensive land-use analysis, especially for change detection35. The emergence of deep learning has profoundly changed the paradigm of semantic segmentation, and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have demonstrated outstanding performance for this task36. While other models, such as U-Net37 and SegNet38, have also been widely adopted and extended, ongoing research on efficient network design aims to reduce the memory and computational costs in processing large, high-resolution remote sensing images. In existing architectures, DeepLabv3 + achieves high segmentation accuracy by introducing a decoder structure18,39. However, its massive number of parameters (approximately 59 million) results in significant computational and memory requirements, increasing training and inference costs18. To efficiently encode multi-scale contextual information while minimizing computational overhead, this study adopts the DeepLabv3 architecture20. DeepLabv3 offers a more computationally efficient alternative that maintains competitive segmentation performance by omitting the decoder component of DeepLabv3 + . To retain strong feature representation while using fewer parameters, these lighter architectures typically combine multi-scale feature fusion with attention mechanisms. Semantic segmentation plays a fundamental role in terrain change detection, providing a crucial foundation for multi-temporal analysis40,41.

Multi-temporal analysis is crucial for capturing changes in targets in 3D space1. Analyzing data acquired at the exact location at different time points enables the identification of changes in buildings and landforms, as well as the updating of geospatial information for applications such as earthquake damage assessment and monitoring of mountainous terrain24,29. Consequently, multi-temporal analysis plays a fundamental role in disaster assessment and environmental change studies. A wide range of techniques has been developed for multi-temporal analysis, among which time series and trend analysis are commonly employed to monitor long-term dynamic processes, including urban sprawl, forest growth, and surface deformation10,29. Such analyses enable the detection of changes in large metropolitan areas by comparing continuous DSMs16. In construction progress monitoring, building information models (BIMs) are frequently integrated with multi-temporal point cloud data to assess structural changes. In contrast, sudden changes occurring over a short time period, such as earthquake-induced building damage or building demolition associated with new construction activities, are typically categorized as event detection30.

Methods

The overall workflow of this study is presented in Fig. 1. This process first acquires multi-temporal UAV data and preprocesses dense point clouds and orthophotographs. The prepared data was then used for two major tasks. First, semantic segmentation was performed on the orthophotographs. Second, change detection was conducted using point cloud data for the 3D analysis and image data for the 2D analysis. Finally, performance was assessed by combining the outputs of both tasks, enabling us to evaluate the model’s accuracy in capturing landscape changes.

Study area and data acquisition

The study area for this multi-temporal analysis of mountain topographic change is the Guanziling area in Baihe District, Tainan City, Taiwan. This area was chosen because its complex terrain is highly sensitive to natural processes, necessitating detailed, multi-temporal monitoring of topographic change. CoCoTree Technology acquired multi-temporal data in two photogrammetry operations: on January 21, 2024 (hereafter referred to as T0) and on January 13, 2025 (hereafter referred to as T1). The data acquisition dates were selected approximately one year apart. This temporal alignment ensures seasonal consistency, thereby minimizing inter-temporal domain shifts caused by variations in vegetation phenology and solar illumination angles. High-resolution imagery covering approximately 6 hectares was acquired using UAV aerial photogrammetry. The flight operations were planned with a total of eight flight paths to achieve a ground sampling distance (GSD) of 2 cm/pixel (0.02 m/pixel). Each pixel corresponds to a ground area of approximately 0.02 m × 0.02 m. Recent studies have demonstrated that flight altitude and the resulting GSD strongly influence point cloud density, geometric accuracy, and the reliability of multi-temporal change detection, particularly in complex terrain environments, where finer GSD generally improves sensitivity to subtle surface changes while reducing geometric uncertainty. Consistent with these findings, the selected flight altitude and resulting GSD in this study were designed to balance spatial resolution, geometric reliability, and computational efficiency, while ensuring sensitivity to small-scale topographic variations42. The imaging parameters were set to maximize data redundancy and quality: 90% forward overlap between consecutive images and 70% lateral overlap between adjacent flight lines. Each survey phase lasted approximately 61 min. This comprehensive photographic effort captured 831 images per period.

Aerial image processing

This study uses the open-source software WebODM to process two sets of aerial images43,44. The workflow first determines the spatial geometry of the imagery. Using ground control points (GCPs), coordinate transformations are carefully applied to ensure accurate georeferencing and alignment with real-world geographic coordinates. After geolocation, a densification algorithm was applied to generate a dense point cloud model. From this dense point cloud, a Digital Surface Model (DSM) was created using a triangulated mesh. After applying the necessary corrections and refinement to the mesh, the original images were orthorectified to produce orthophotos. These orthophotos have a 2 cm/pixel GSD, enabling accurate measurements of relative distances and planimetric coordinates within the study area.

Voxel grid

The voxel grid method divides the entire 3D space into a grid of fixed-size cubes. Then it uses each voxel as a unit for subsequent analysis or feature extraction, thereby reducing the amount of data. This study sets the voxel size to 0.05 m and fits the sampling density of our point clouds. Based on a density of about 600 points/m2, the average spacing between neighboring points is roughly 0.04 m. Setting the voxel size to 0.05 m, just slightly larger than that spacing, lets us merge nearby redundant points while avoiding overly aggressive smoothing that could wash out subtle terrain variations. This approach enhances registration stability and stabilizes the normal vector and neighborhood statistics required for Multiscale Model-to-Model Cloud Comparison (M3C2), reducing interference from outliers in distance and Level of Detection (LoD) determination. Furthermore, the 0.05 m voxel size is several times larger than the image GSD. This technique provides sufficient spatial resolution to identify terrain variations at the 0.1-m scale while simultaneously reducing computational costs.

Point cloud registration and differencing

The Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm is typically used for registering data from different time phases with a short interval45. It assumes good overlap between the data and a small number of changes. Its goal is to find an optimal rigid transformation (rotation and translation) that minimizes the distance between all points in the two point clouds. However, when the terrain lacks stable feature points or undergoes localized changes that violate the rigid registration assumption, the registration process becomes more uncertain, thereby amplifying the error. To solve this issue, a global feature-based registration strategy was first employed to achieve robust coarse alignment under challenging terrain conditions. This registration was followed by applying the ICP algorithm as a fine-tuning step to locally refine the transformation. Global registration primarily consists of four steps: normal vector estimation, feature descriptor calculation, feature matching, and verification of random sampling consistency.

In this study, normal vectors are first estimated for each point, where the normal direction describes the orientation of the local surface in three-dimensional space. Then, for each point, neighboring points within a fixed radius (using KDTree search)46 are selected to compute the covariance matrix, and the eigenvector corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue is taken as the normal direction to ensure consistency. Fast Point Feature Histograms (FPFH) are also used to statistically summarize these relationships into a multi-dimensional histogram, forming a feature vector that describes the local shape of the point47. After the calculation, the Nearest Neighbor Search is used to find the matching point pairs with the smallest feature distance between the source and target point clouds. Due to uncertainties in feature calculation, the matching results may contain many mismatches. Therefore, Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) is used to select the most reliable matches from the matched points, which often include significant noise and inaccurate data, and the transformation matrix is then calculated to complete the registration48. The registration process is implemented using Python and Open3D49.

After the point cloud registration and M3C2 change detection analysis in this study were facilitated by the open-source software CloudCompare48 . Within this framework, the M3C2 algorithm does not merely compute the Euclidean distance between two point clouds. Instead, it introduces local surface normals to define displacement directions. First, it estimates the local surface normal vectors of the reference point cloud at a defined projection scale. Projection cylinders are then constructed along these normal directions. The average positional difference between the two point clouds within each cylinder is computed, yielding signed distance values. This mechanism enables M3C2 to effectively represent the magnitude and direction of elevation changes when processing complex terrain. Additionally, M3C2 accounts for point cloud surface roughness and registration error by calculating spatially varying confidence intervals for each measurement point, specifically, the LoD95%. By quantifying uncertainties in point measurements and multi-period data registration, M3C2 effectively distinguishes genuine terrain variations from measurement noise. Since the M3C2 calculation relies solely on points present within the projection cylinder, empty voxels are not assigned any values. They are not used to fill space or participate in distance estimation. If certain regions lack sufficient points within their neighborhood due to occlusion or low point density, M3C2 typically renders the core point incapable of obtaining valid measurements, leading to greater uncertainty rather than spurious distance values. This method demonstrates superior robustness compared to traditional interpolated DSM differencing or a point-to-point C2C method50.

Image data preprocessing

The T1 orthophotograph was reprojected to precisely conform to the grid geometry of the T0 orthophotograph using the standard reprojection method. This operation ensured that both images shared the same spatial reference systems, pixel dimensions, and origin points. However, to strictly quantify the co-registration accuracy, a critical factor for pixel-level change detection at 2 cm GSD, the residual misalignment was evaluated using 10 pairs of homologous feature points manually identified within the overlapping area of the T0 and T1 orthophotos. The analysis yielded a Co-registration Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 0.038 m, which corresponds to approximately 1.9 pixels. This alignment accuracy is controlled to within 2 pixels, thereby ensuring spatial consistency of the inputs used for subsequent change detection and reducing potential false detections caused by registration artifacts.

The T0 and T1 images were divided into fixed-size tiles using a sliding window with a stride equal to the window size, resulting in non-overlapping tiles, to prepare high-resolution images for model training. Smaller image patches provide limited overall information, which may hinder reliable identification of complex terrain features. To maintain adequate spatial information, an image patch size of 609 × 609 pixels (covering roughly 12.2 m × 12.2 m at a 0.02 m GSD) was used. Furthermore, given the high spatial resolution of UAV imagery, larger image patches can capture complete geometric structures, thereby reducing feature fragmentation and boundary errors. This configuration balances the computational load with the need to account for the full scale of target features in mountainous environments. All images were extracted sequentially in raster-scan order from left to right and top to bottom using the OpenCV library51. In total, 754 tiles were obtained.

Manual annotations were performed on image patches of 609 × 609 pixels using the LabelMe tool52. As shown in Fig. 2, the labeled categories included buildings, vegetation, roads, artifacts (i.e., manufactured objects other than buildings and roads), rivers, and background (regions that cannot be clearly assigned to a specific category, such as areas with ambiguous boundaries or those that are unrecognizable). Each image tile was annotated individually, and the labeled tiles were subsequently stitched together (as shown in Fig. 3) to ensure continuity in the ground truth dataset. After manual annotation, the JSON annotation files were converted into pixel-level mask images suitable for semantic segmentation training. For the mask images, each annotation category was assigned a specific pixel value during the conversion process: background set to 0, buildings to 1, roads to 2, vegetation to 3, rivers to 4, and artifacts to 5. Each annotation file was processed with the Labelme conversion script, producing a corresponding grayscale image. The generated masks were saved in PNG format. These masks were subsequently used as ground-truth labels for model training. Subsequently, the labeled T0 images were used to train the DeepLabv3 model for multi-class semantic segmentation. To address class imbalance and improve training efficiency, tiles containing only background information were excluded from the training set. As a result, the T0 dataset used for model training ultimately contained 485 information-rich tiles.

DeepLabv3 network architecture

DeepLabv3 is built upon a pure encoder architecture without a dedicated decoder and uses ResNet-101 as the backbone of feature extraction, as illustrated in Fig. 4. By leveraging its residual connections, it is possible to build deeper networks and mitigate vanishing gradients, thereby facilitating the learning of more complex features. Beyond the choice of backbone, DeepLabv3 systematically addresses the problem of degraded feature resolution and multi-scale object recognition caused by excessive downsampling in semantic segmentation. Its architecture is based on a re-evaluation and use of atrous convolutions. The network efficiently increases the receptive field by varying the dilation rate without requiring more parameters or sacrificing spatial resolution. DeepLabv3’s Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (ASPP) module incorporates atrous convolution to enhance multi-scale feature recognition. For accurate pixel-level classification, the network must simultaneously capture contextual information at multiple scales.

DeepLabv3 semantic segmentation model architecture.

Atrous convolution addresses the challenges of processing multi-scale objects and low feature resolution in deep convolutional neural networks (DCNNs) and is a key component of DeepLabv3 for semantic image segmentation. Though DCNNs are good at learning abstract feature representations, continuous pooling operations or convolution strides often significantly reduce the spatial resolution of feature maps. This loss of detailed spatial information hinders dense prediction tasks such as semantic segmentation. Atrous convolution directly alleviates this problem by extracting denser feature maps. It does this by modifying the ImageNet pre-trained network, removing the downsampling operation from the final layer and conceptually upsampling the corresponding filter kernels. By introducing holes (the French word “trous” means “holes”) between the filter weights, this procedure enables explicit control over the resolution at which feature responses are computed in DCNNs without requiring the learning of extra parameters. ASPP introduces notable enhancements that improve the performance of previous iterations. These enhancements include fine-tuning batch normalization parameters and augmentation with image-level features that encode global context. This addition is significant for alleviating issues caused by considerable dilation ratios, where image boundary effects cause convolutions to degenerate into simpler 1 × 1 operations, failing to capture long-range dependencies. The design obtains image-level features by performing global average pooling on the last feature map, feeding the result into a 1 × 1 convolution with 256 filters and batch normalization, and then bilinearly upsampling the features to the desired spatial dimensions. As shown in Fig. 6, the improved ASPP includes one 1 × 1 convolution and three 3 × 3 convolutions with dilation rates of 6, 12, and 18, when the output stride is set to 16. Each convolutional layer uses 256 filters and includes batch normalization. It also incorporates image-level features. All branches’ features are concatenated and passed through another 1 × 1 convolutional layer with 256 filters and batch normalization. A 1 × 1 convolutional layer produces the final logits.

Change detection strategy

The change detection workflow in this study consists of two sequential phases: whole-area inference and rigorous subset validation. For the entire study area, a post-classification comparison strategy was employed, in which the trained DeepLabv3 model was used to generate semantic segmentation maps for the T1 imagery (). These predictions were compared on a pixel-by-pixel basis with the T0 ground truth annotations (), and a pixel was classified as a detected change when the predicted label differed from the corresponding T0 ground truth label (). To address the inter-temporal domain shift and evaluate model performance on unseen temporal data, a high-quality ground-truth validation subset was constructed from T1 imagery, comprising 100 manually annotated tiles. As defined in Eqs. (4–6), the validation subset was constructed using a model-driven stratified sampling framework designed to capture diverse terrain characteristics and change conditions. To avoid potential circularity associated with model-guided sample selection, the ground truth for these validation tiles was established through independent manual re-annotation of the T1 imagery, without reference to the model’s predicted segmentation masks. Consequently, although the model informed the selection of validation tiles to increase the presence of informative cases, the evaluation itself relies strictly on human-verified ground truth labels (). The predicted changes were then evaluated against this reference to assess change detection performance based on actual land-cover changes rather than segmentation errors. The T1 validation subset, therefore, serves as a control mechanism to decouple true terrain changes from segmentation errors. To construct a representative and informative validation subset, a model-driven stratified sampling strategy was employed, inspired by active learning principles. Rather than using purely random sampling, which would result in unchanged background regions dominating, the initial model inference results (compared against the Q0 labels) were used to guide the selection of validation tiles with higher likelihoods of change or prediction uncertainty. Specifically, as illustrated in Fig. 7, 10% of the validation samples were selected based on the rarity score, 50% based on the change score, and 20% based on the stability score, with the remaining 20% randomly sampled from the remaining tiles. This stratified sampling design increases the representation of tiles that exhibit actual land-cover changes or higher prediction uncertainty, allowing the evaluation to focus on cases where segmentation errors and temporal changes are more likely. As a result, the validation set is intentionally enriched with informative cases, and the subsequent evaluation metrics are therefore conditional on this sampling design. These metrics are intended to assess model robustness under change and uncertainty rather than to provide unbiased, area-representative prevalence estimates for the entire study area.

To make sure that tiles containing fewer standard classes are appropriately included in the annotation set, a rarity-based sampling approach was applied as described in Eq. (4). Classes that occur infrequently often take up only a small fraction of an image, and without careful selection, these areas might be missed, which can result in biased model evaluation and weaker performance on minority classes. For each tile, a rarity score was computed to measure the proportion of non-background tiles belonging to these rare classes. Tiles with higher scores were selected first, helping the annotated dataset cover a wide variety of classes, including those that appear only rarely.

Model evaluation

To evaluate the semantic segmentation and change detection performance of the proposed model, four key metrics are used: IoU, accuracy, precision, and recall. Their mathematical definitions are shown in Eqs. (7–10).

In semantic segmentation, Intersection over Union (IoU) is a commonly used evaluation metric that measures the overlap between the predicted segmentation and the ground truth. To evaluate the model’s change-detection effectiveness, various metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and IoU, are used. Accuracy is the proportion of pixels correctly classified by the model out of the total number of pixels. Precision measures the proportion of correctly identified changed pixels among the predicted changed pixels. Recall measures the proportion of pixels that the model was able to determine that had changed, ensuring that all relevant changes were recorded. In change detection tasks, the degree of overlap between detected changes and the ground truth is measured with the IoU metric.

Results

The training hyperparameters employed in this study are summarized in DeepLabV3, with a ResNet-101 backbone and pretrained on the COCO dataset, was employed to accelerate convergence. The standard cross-entropy loss function was employed without additional class weighting or label smoothing. The optimization was performed using the AdamW optimizer with a fixed learning rate of and default weight decay settings; no learning rate scheduler or warm-up phase was applied. The GPU batch size was set to 8. Training lasted for 50 epochs using the 485 filtered tiles from the T0 dataset. Regarding data preprocessing, tiles containing exclusively background pixels were discarded before training to reduce class imbalance and computational cost. For tiles containing partial background regions, those areas were ignored in the loss calculation by setting the index to 255. From the filtered dataset, 80% of the tiles were used for training, and the remaining 20% for validation. No additional data augmentation strategies (e.g., random flipping, rotation, or color jitter) were applied during training. The validation set was used to monitor performance continuously and save the best model checkpoint to mitigate overfitting.

For tiles containing partial background regions, those areas were ignored in the loss calculation by setting the index to 255. From the filtered dataset, 80% of the tiles were used for training, and the remaining 20% for validation. No additional data augmentation strategies (e.g., random flipping, rotation, or color jitter) were applied during training. The validation set was used to monitor performance continuously and save the best model checkpoint to mitigate overfitting.

Discussion

The results presented in the previous section highlight both the robust performance of the proposed model and the inherent difficulties posed by mountainous environments. Based on these quantitative findings, this section discusses the implications of class heterogeneity, data quality, and the complementary nature of the 2D-3D integration.

Segmentation performance and generalization

Compared with other categories, the artifact class had a low IoU (53.30%). This is attributable to the broad definition of the “artifact” category, which encompasses a wide range of manufactured objects while excluding buildings and roads, resulting in greater intra-class variability and increased classification difficulty. As a result, the shape, size, and texture of this category exhibit significant intra-class variability and heterogeneity. The model struggles to learn stable representations because the varied objects lack distinct, consistent features, which negatively affect segmentation performance. Despite its lower segmentation performance, retaining the ‘artifact’ class is methodologically critical. If this category is excluded, blurry artificial pixels are more likely to be misclassified as “buildings” or “roads,” thereby reducing the classification accuracy of these categories. By explicitly representing heterogeneous artificial features, the artifact class helps preserve spectral consistency and the high IoU of the primary structural classes. Furthermore, in the context of change detection, even a coarse segmentation of artifacts can provide an additional cue for distinguishing anthropogenic changes from natural vegetation dynamics.

The error distribution shown in Fig. 11 indicates that the model can attain good overall segmentation quality, reliably capture terrain changes, and seldom exhibit significant differences between model inferences and manual annotations. These results suggest that the model generalizes well, as its performance is evaluated on a temporally distinct T1 dataset. The reported metrics reflect the model’s performance on a stratified subset that emphasizes complex, changing terrain. They were intentionally not re-weighted to approximate the full population distribution, as the goal was to highlight model limitations in critical regions rather than mask errors with predominantly stable background pixels. Consequently, the reported performance should be regarded as a conservative estimate of the model’s generalization capability.

Point cloud registration analysis

Mountainous terrain illustrates the challenges of domain shift, particularly in the context of dynamic vegetation changes. Registration analysis revealed distinct performance characteristics across surface types. For roads and buildings, high accuracy was observed, demonstrating the method’s dependability in mostly static conditions. Vegetation-dominated areas showed larger error spreads (Mean Error = 0.593 m, RMSE = 0.945 m), likely related to their non-rigid nature: branches and leaves can move with the wind, and the irregular, porous, volumetric structure of vegetation causes slight variations in the sampled surface between successive scans. Moderate errors were observed in the river and artifact categories, which may have been caused by transient objects or reflections from the water’s surface during data collection. Despite these environmental variations, the high accuracy achieved on rigid surfaces provides a reliable geometric constraint. By anchoring the analysis on these stable features, genuine topographic changes can be effectively separated from noise caused by non-rigid vegetation dynamics or seasonal effects, ensuring that the detected variations correspond to actual mass movements. Accordingly, the reported change-detection metrics should be interpreted as conditional on the enriched validation design; they primarily reflect robustness to complex transitions and uncertainty rather than unbiased, area-representative estimates of change prevalence across the entire study area.

Integration of 2D and 3D detection

The disparity observed in the Medium Change dataset (Table 5) highlights a clear limitation of 2D-based approaches. Although the M3C2 analysis detected substantial physical displacement, the 2D model identified only a small fraction of the affected areas. This approach highlights the reduced sensitivity of 2D images in areas with shadows and dense vegetation, underscoring the importance of depth-based validation for accurate change detection. To distinguish model misclassifications from genuine landscape changes, two complementary validation mechanisms were applied. First, the model’s reliability in the unseen temporal domain is substantiated by the low error-propagation rate shown in Fig. 11, where the proportion of tiles with noticeable error remains below 5%. Second, instances of apparent disagreement between the model predictions and the reference data, corresponding to outliers in Fig. 12, were examined using 3D point cloud analysis. As detailed in the 3D change detection results (Fig. 14), false positives in 2D detection, often caused by shadows or phenological variations, generally lack corresponding geometric displacement in the 3D data. The integration of 3D geometric constraints, therefore, provides an effective means of reducing semantic misclassification and increasing confidence that the detected changes reflect physical terrain deformation rather than algorithmic artifacts. By integrating the 3D change detection results, the identified 2D changes are confirmed to correspond to physically meaningful structural variations. This 3D geometric validation complements the rigorous T1 sub-area validation based on 100 manually annotated tiles, enabling genuine structural changes to be distinguished from segmentation errors or artifacts induced by spectral or seasonal variations.

Comparison with the Improved RANSAC method and commercial photogrammetry software

The global registration stage in this study employs feature-based matching followed by RANSAC-based robust estimation, a common approach for aligning UAV-derived point clouds. While effective at rejecting outliers, classical RANSAC remains sensitive to high outlier ratios and weakly distinctive features, which are typical in mountainous, vegetation‑dominated environments. Recent studies have proposed improved RANSAC variants that enhance robustness through iterative refinement, adaptive termination, and more effective outlier handling, thereby improving registration stability and efficiency under such challenging conditions53,54. Their reported advantages are directly relevant to the elevated registration uncertainty observed in vegetation-dominated regions in our study, highlighting promising directions for future improvement. Comparative analyses of commercial photogrammetry software (e.g., Agisoft Metashape, Pix4D, DJI Terra)55 examine differences in point cloud density, surface completeness, and elevation accuracy in forested terrain. In contrast to these proprietary pipelines, the proposed framework emphasizes methodological transparency by integrating open-source registration with semantic information, enabling land-cover-aware interpretation of geometric change in multi-temporal terrain analysis.

Conclusions

This study evaluates the performance of a deep learning–based semantic segmentation framework integrated with multi-temporal change detection in complex mountainous terrain. The model achieved strong overall segmentation performance. Except for the “artifact” class (IoU 53.30%), which exhibits significant intra-class heterogeneity, the IoU values for the other categories exceeded 90%, confirming the model’s ability to distinguish diverse terrain features. Although the segmentation accuracy for artifacts was lower, preserving this category remains essential to prevent the misclassification of ambiguous man-made features as buildings or roads, thereby maintaining the integrity of these major structural categories. For change detection, strict validation against independent T1 manual annotations yielded a high coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.9251), indicating that the model’s predicted change proportions are statistically consistent with human ground truth. The integration of M3C2-based 3D point cloud analysis serves as a robust geometric constraint. Vegetation dynamics and occlusions introduce noise into 3D registration (RMSE = 0.945 m). However, combining 2D semantic filtering with the M3C2 algorithm and LoD95% statistics enables the distinction between fundamental topographic changes and spectral artifacts. This integration was particularly beneficial in identifying changes in occluded areas (e.g., Medium Change zones) where 2D models alone were insufficient. Future research should focus on refining the semantic taxonomy to more effectively address the intra-class variation identified within the “artifact” category. Dividing artifacts into specific structural types, such as vehicles, traffic signs, and railings, and vegetation into subcategories, such as trees and low vegetation, would reduce the differences within each class, which would help the model learn more distinct features. Overall, the results suggest that integrating 2D semantic analysis with 3D geometric validation provides a practical basis for automated, large-scale disaster monitoring in complex environments.
