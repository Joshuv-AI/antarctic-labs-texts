Risk factors and predictive model for corneal edema in patients undergoing cataract surgery
Authors: Ming-Wei Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo identify the risk factors associated with developing corneal edema in patients undergoing cataract surgery and construct a predictive model based on the patient’s clinical characteristics.MethodsIn total, 502 patients who underwent cataract surgery at a tertiary hospital between January 2021 and December 2023 were included in this study. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to investigate risk factors for corneal edema. A prediction model was constructed, and its performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The model’s predictive performance was assessed using the area under the ROC curve (AUC).ResultsCorneal edema was observed in 393 patients after cataract surgery. Multivariate analysis revealed that advanced age (odds ratio [OR], 1.027; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.008–1.048; P = 0.007) was associated with an increased risk of corneal edema. Conversely, preoperative visual acuity ranged from 0.01 to 0.10 (OR: 0.476; 95% CI: 0.239–0.951; P = 0.035) and ≥ 0.10 (OR: 0.249; 95% CI: 0.141–0.437; P < 0.001), and intraoperative use of carbachol (OR: 0.378; 95% CI: 0.229–0.623; P < 0.001) were associated with a reduced risk of corneal edema. These combined factors predicted the risk of postoperative corneal edema in patients with cataract with an AUC of 71.5% (95% CI: 65.8%-77.1%; P < 0.001).ConclusionThis study’s findings revealed that a multivariate model based on patient age, preoperative visual acuity, and intraoperative use of carbachol can predict the risk of postoperative corneal edema in patients with cataract, demonstrating a moderate predictive value.Keywords: Corneal edema, Cataract surgery, Risk factors, Prediction model

Background

Cataracts remain the dominant cause of avoidable blindness worldwide, affecting 15.2 million individuals. Notably, 90% of this population resides in low-income countries [1]. Vision loss negatively impacts individuals, families, and communities, exacerbating poverty and diminishing the quality of life [2]. High-quality cataract surgery is a cost-effective procedure that mitigates the adverse effects of blindness and visual impairment. Rapid vision restoration is a crucial objective of cataract surgery, and various intraoperative measures are employed, including using viscoelastic agents to cover the corneal endothelium and corneal protective techniques during nuclear fragmentation [3, 4]. Corneal edema is a common cause of early postoperative visual decline. Typically, corneal edema is reversible; however, recovery times have been reported to vary from 1 week [5] to 4 weeks [6], 60 days [7], 6 months [8], or even up to 1 year [9], which can significantly impair the patient’s quality of life after cataract surgery.

Theoretically, morphological changes in the cornea due to edema, such as alterations in corneal curvature or increased corneal thickness, can directly lead to changes in corneal refractive power. Furthermore, increased water content within the corneal tissue, which modifies the refractive index, is also a significant factor in clinical refractive alterations [10]. Consequently, a thorough examination of the potential risk factors underlying postoperative corneal edema is crucial to enable early intervention strategies tailored for patients with varying risk profiles in clinical practice, thereby enhancing treatment outcomes. Multiple factors have been implicated in the development of postoperative corneal edema, including the initial state of corneal pathology [11], patient age [12], presence of diabetes [13], and inflammatory responses following surgery [14]. Recent efforts to develop predictive models for postoperative corneal edema have shown promise, such as a model based on variable selection with copula entropy, which identified factors like axial length and corneal curvature as predictors in phacoemulsification patients [15]. Nonetheless, there remains a gap in establishing predictive models tailored to corneal edema in patients undergoing cataract surgery based on their detailed clinical characteristics. Given the prevalence of postoperative corneal edema, systematically investigating its risk factors and constructing effective predictive models hold substantial practical significance for enhancing clinical management efficacy and improving patient prognosis. Therefore, this study had two primary objectives. First, we aimed to develop a multi-factorial model to identify high-risk populations for corneal edema after cataract surgery, thereby facilitating the application of targeted preventive strategies. Second, we quantitatively assessed the risk factors associated with corneal edema development in a large consecutive cohort of patients who underwent cataract surgery.

Methods

Participants

We retrospectively examined the clinical records of 502 patients who underwent cataract surgery at a tertiary Class A medical institution between January 2021 and December 2023. Of the 502 included patients, 287 (57.2%) underwent phacoemulsification, and 215 (42.8%) underwent manual small-incision cataract surgery (MSICS). The choice between techniques was determined by preoperative cataract severity (assessed via LOCS III: phacoemulsification for grades 2–3; MSICS for grade 4 + due to harder nuclei) and surgeon judgment regarding optimal visualization and safety, in line with institutional practice. Inclusion was confined to patients whose lens opacities met the grade 2 or higher criteria, according to the Lens Opacities Classification System III for age-related cataracts. We rigorously omitted cases with: age-related macular degeneration or any ocular conditions that could interfere with the study outcomes; a history of previous eye surgeries; existing ocular surface diseases; abnormal corneal endothelial cell quality or cell counts below 2000 cells/mm2; those who had undergone any form of ocular intervention within one month before surgery; patients under medication that could induce drowsiness; those with a history of drug or alcohol dependency; and patients with systemic diseases (including diabetes mellitus, hypertension with severe retinopathy, autoimmune diseases, etc.) that may affect corneal healing or edema formation. During the study, any patient whose test results indicated the possible presence of another disease that could obscure the study’s conclusions was excluded from the study database. The First Affiliated Hospital of Shantou Medical College’s Ethics Review Committee approved the study protocol (No: B-2005-008), and informed consent was obtained from all patients and their legal representatives during the preoperative appointment period.

Preoperative examinations and surgery

All patients underwent a thorough ophthalmic examination, which included best-corrected visual acuity tests, slit-lamp microscopy, pupillary response checks, and Goldmann tonometry. Fundus examination was performed when lens clarity was permitted. Patients with NC6 cataracts, for whom fundus examination was not feasible, underwent B-scan ultrasonography (Echoscan US-4000 Nidek, Japan) to rule out retinal and vitreous issues. The power of the intraocular lenses (IOLs) was measured using the IOL-Master 500 when possible. In NC6 cataract cases, the IOL power was determined using corneal topography and A-scan ultrasound (Echoscan US-4000 Nidek, Japan). Two seasoned ophthalmologists, experts in MSICS and phacoemulsification techniques, meticulously performed all surgeries. To minimize variability, both surgeons adhered to a standardized surgical protocol as per the institutional guidelines for cataract surgery. MSICS procedures employ a U-shaped incision with a scleral flap to minimize surgically induced astigmatism [16]. Patients undergoing phacoemulsification received Tecnis ZCB00 monofocal 1-Piece foldable IOLs (Johnson & Johnson, USA), while those undergoing MSICS were fitted with rigid PMMA ez 0.60 IOLs (Bausch & Lomb, USA) [17]. Phacoemulsification procedures had a mean duration of 12.3 ± 3.5 min, with ultrasonic power set at 18.5 ± 4.2% on average. MSICS procedures lasted 15.6 ± 4.1 min, with no ultrasonic energy applied.

Data collection and outcome definition

Through a literature review, 12 influencing factors were identified: patient age, sex, affected eye, K1 (steep meridian corneal curvature), K2 (flat meridian corneal curvature), axial length, anterior chamber depth, lens thickness, vitreous thickness, preoperative visual acuity, and intraoperative use of carbachol and intraoperative use of ceftazidime. Intraoperative complications, including vitreous loss, were also recorded. Corneal edema was defined as mild-to-moderate corneal clouding or dullness observed via slit-lamp microscopy (by a single ophthalmologist) within 72 h postoperatively, accompanied by a documented increase in corneal thickness compared to preoperative measurements. This definition focused on early postoperative changes, with resolution monitored over 1 week.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of patients with and without corneal edema were described according to the data types: continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. In contrast, categorical variables were defined in terms of frequency and percentage. Continuous variables were compared between groups using either the t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on whether the data followed a normal distribution. For categorical variables, the χ² test was employed. Subsequently, risk factors identified by univariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic regression model, with α set at 0.05, and β at 0.10. The magnitude of the effects was quantified using odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A predictive model was constructed using the identified risk factors from multivariate logistic regression. The model’s performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the ROC curve (AUC) used to assess its predictive accuracy. The significance level was bilateral, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 software.

Baseline characteristics

Table 2 shows the results of the univariate analysis of the risk of corneal edema in patients after cataract surgery. We noted that age (OR: 1.023; 95% CI: 1.006–1.041; P = 0.009) and anterior chamber depth (OR: 1.607; 95% CI: 1.130–2.285; P = 0.008) were associated with an increased risk of corneal edema. Discussion

This study aimed to develop a multifactorial predictive model for corneal edema in patients undergoing cataract surgery, serving as a straightforward tool to identify high-risk factors for corneal edema in this population and to enable preventive strategies targeting modifiable factors. In the Chinese context, our study established a predictive model for corneal edema in patients undergoing cataract surgery and investigated the associated risk factors. A total of 502 patients who underwent cataract surgery were enrolled, 393 of whom experienced corneal edema. Three influencing factors were selected for inclusion in the predictive model: age, preoperative visual acuity, and intraoperative carbachol use. The constructed model demonstrated moderate predictive value for corneal edema, with an AUC of 71.5%.

Several studies have identified risk factors for corneal edema in patients after cataract surgery [18–20]. Gazit et al. identified 100 patients and found that routine post-cataract surgery patching is associated with increased corneal edema and slower visual recovery on the first day after surgery [18]. Claesson et al. included 273 patients with indications for corneal transplantation due to corneal edema after cataract surgery. They found that phacoemulsification and preexisting endothelial disease were associated with an increased risk of persistent corneal edema after cataract surgery [19]. Li et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study of 187,746 patients and found that older age, presence of diabetes, and non-white ethnicity were associated with an elevated risk of corneal edema [20]. However, no domestic studies have investigated the risk factors for corneal edema in patients undergoing cataract surgery, nor are any corresponding predictive models available. Consequently, this study aimed to explore the risk factors for corneal edema in patients undergoing cataract surgery based on their clinical characteristics and to construct a new predictive model.

Our study found that older age was associated with an increased risk of corneal edema. At the same time, preoperative visual acuity and the use of carbachol during surgery could be considered preventive factors for corneal edema in patients after cataract surgery. The potential reasons for this include the following: (1) As age increases, the number and function of corneal endothelial cells gradually decrease and deteriorate, reducing the ability of the cornea to clear water and maintain its average thickness, thereby increasing the risk of postoperative edema. Moreover, older patients often have more complex lens nuclei, necessitating more ultrasound energy and longer ultrasound duration during surgery to emulsify and aspirate the lens. This process may inflict additional damage to the corneal endothelium, leading to edema. Finally, older individuals may exhibit a more vigorous inflammatory response to trauma, and the postoperative inflammatory process can enhance vascular permeability, causing fluid accumulation within the corneal tissue [20]; (2) poor preoperative vision typically suggests advanced cataract development, with a likely increase in lens nucleus hardness. Severe cataracts make surgical procedures more complex and challenging, possibly requiring finer surgical techniques to minimize injury to the cornea and other ocular structures. Furthermore, patients with poor preoperative vision may have a compromised or suboptimal corneal endothelial cell status because the prolonged presence of cataracts may adversely affect the overall eye environment. Lastly, patients with inferior vision may experience a more pronounced inflammatory response post-surgery, potentially because the eye is in a prolonged state of stress, leading to an enhanced sensitivity of the immune system response [21]; and (3) Carbachol helps maintain a stable and moderately sized pupil during surgery, optimizing the surgical view and allowing for more precise manipulation, thereby reducing unnecessary mechanical trauma to the cornea and surrounding tissues and consequently lowering the risk of postoperative edema. Furthermore, carbachol alleviates stress on the eyeball during surgery by decreasing intraocular pressure, safeguarding corneal endothelial cells from damage caused by elevated pressure, which is a crucial factor contributing to corneal edema. Lastly, carbachol may regulate local blood flow, minimizing microcirculatory disturbances in the surgical area, which aids in maintaining the normal metabolic function of the corneal endothelium and facilitating rapid recovery after surgery, thereby diminishing edema formation [22, 23].

A prediction model for corneal edema following cataract surgery was constructed, and three influential factors were identified using multivariate analysis. The AUC of our model was 71.5%, indicating moderate predictive value, and its application in clinical practice is straightforward. Our model, which focuses on age, preoperative visual acuity, and intraoperative carbachol use, complements the findings of Luo et al., who emphasized anatomical factors [15]. The inclusion of intraoperative variables in our model highlights the role of surgical management in mitigating edema risk, offering a more clinically actionable tool for perioperative decision-making. This model enables clinicians to preoperatively identify patients at a high risk of developing corneal edema, facilitating the formulation of personalized treatment plans based on risk levels. Adjustments may include the choice of the surgical approach, preoperative preparations, and postoperative care strategies. Additionally, recognizing factors closely tied to the occurrence of corneal edema allows for targeted preventive measures, such as implementing more meticulous ocular surface protection for patients with specific risk factors (e.g., advanced age, low preoperative vision, or particular medication usage) or modifying perioperative medications.

Notably, the incidence of corneal edema in our cohort (78.29%) is higher than the 5–20% range reported in previous studies [24, 25]. This difference may stem from two key factors: (1) Diagnostic criteria: Our definition of corneal edema included early, transient postoperative changes (e.g., mild corneal clouding and increased thickness detected via slit-lamp microscopy within the immediate postoperative period), whereas many studies focus on persistent or clinically significant edema (e.g., edema lasting > 1 week or impairing vision beyond the early recovery phase) [25]. (2) Selection bias: Our cohort was restricted to patients with lens opacities ≥ grade 2 (LOCS III), indicating more advanced cataracts. Such cases often require longer surgical duration or higher intraoperative manipulation, potentially increasing the risk of transient corneal edema. Additionally, we excluded patients with pre-existing endothelial dysfunction (cell counts < 2000 cells/mm2), but the remaining cohort may still have had subclinical endothelial compromise related to advanced cataract severity, contributing to higher edema rates. These factors collectively may explain the higher incidence observed in our study, emphasizing the need to interpret our findings in the context of our specific diagnostic and inclusion criteria.

Certain limitations of this study warrant mention: (1) given its retrospective cohort design, the findings are inevitably influenced by the representativeness of the sample and the potential for recall bias; (2) patients with cataract in the study presented with varying degrees of severity, which could have significantly affected the risk of corneal edema; (3) the lack of data on preoperative corneal endothelial state (e.g., cell count, morphology), which could not be retrieved due to institutional protocol constraints during the study period. Future studies should incorporate this parameter to better elucidate its role in postoperative corneal edema; (4) key confounding factors such as cataract nuclear hardness (graded via LOCS III), cumulative ultrasound energy, and detailed variations in surgical techniques were not included in the analysis due to retrospective data limitations; (5) the definition of corneal edema relied primarily on subjective slit-lamp assessment by a single clinician, lacking quantitative measures such as central corneal thickness or endothelial cell count. This subjectivity and single-assessor design may introduce selection bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of outcome labeling; (6) surgeon-related variability may have influenced outcomes, as two surgeons performed the procedures. Despite adherence to standardized protocols, differences in technical nuances could not be fully controlled, potentially introducing bias; (7) including both MSICS and phacoemulsification may introduce confounding, as the two techniques differ in invasiveness and intraoperative manipulation. Despite standardized selection criteria based on cataract severity, unmeasured differences could affect edema risk, limiting direct comparability between groups; and (8) the predictive model developed lacks external validation from independent cohorts, and the generalizability and interoperability of the model need to be verified in other centers and clinical settings.

Conclusion

This study established a predictive model for corneal edema after cataract surgery incorporating three influential factors: advanced age, preoperative visual acuity, and intraoperative use of carbachol. The model demonstrated a moderate predictive value. Consequently, clinical management should be adjusted based on model predictions. Further validation of the model’s predictive utility requires large-scale prospective cohort studies conducted at other centers and clinical institutions.
