Dorsal arachnoid web of the thoracic spine associated with an arachnoid cyst and a presyrinx state as an unexplained cause of thoracic myelopathy: illustrative case
Authors: Patrick J Halloran, Nathan K Leclair, Ketan R Bulsara, Bruce S Chozick, Isaac L Moss, Kevin P Becker, Abner S Gershon, Hilary C Onyiuke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDDorsal thoracic arachnoid web is a rare diagnosis and is not commonly seen in neurosurgical practice. Patients can present with symptoms and signs of thoracic myelopathy in the setting of an arachnoid cyst and a presyrinx state.OBSERVATIONSA 57-year-old male with a 10-year history of worsening bilateral leg weakness and chronic back pain re-presented to the neurosurgery clinic after being seen by neurology and orthopedic spine surgery. Initial imaging was concerning for myelomalacia and syringomyelia, and repeat delayed computed tomography myelography findings were consistent with an evolving thoracic arachnoid web, now demonstrating spinal cord compression secondary to arachnoid cyst formation and consistent with the signs of thoracic myelopathy. Intraoperative ultrasound displayed the arachnoid web as the cause of the evolving arachnoid cyst, edematous spinal cord, and a presyrinx-like state. The patient underwent surgical decompression, which restored cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics, resulting in clinical improvement.LESSONSDorsal thoracic arachnoid web is a dynamic condition that can occur in the setting of an arachnoid cyst. There appears to be a relationship between dorsal thoracic arachnoid web formation and the presence of an arachnoid cyst resulting from a ball-valve mechanism leading to the creation of a pressure gradient effect that alters CSF fluid dynamics. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24313 Keywords: arachnoid web, spine, intraoperative ultrasound, presyrinx, arachnoid cystABBREVIATIONS: CSF = cerebrospinal fluid, CT = computed tomography, MRI = magnetic resonance imaging, STIR = short tau inversion recovery, US = ultrasound.

Arachnoid webs result from the disordered formation of the arachnoid mater in the subarachnoid space, which subsequently causes changes in the natural flow of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and can lead to the formation of syringomyelia.1 Syringomyelia can be caused by congenital conditions such as neural tube defects and Chiari malformations, or it can be an acquired condition.2 Acquired causes include infection, malignancy, or trauma; however, it can be idiopathic, particularly when there is no definable history of trauma.1–4 The pathogenesis of acquired syrinx formation is related in part to any alteration or obstruction of normal CSF flow within the spinal canal.2 A “presyrinx” state, first described by Fischbein et al., can occur prior to syringomyelia formation and present with signs of myelopathy that can be reversed with surgical decompression.5 A spinal arachnoid web is extremely rare, with approximately 60 known reported cases.6 It is unclear why formation occurs in the intradural space; however, some proposed etiologies include the “Venturi effect” due to changes in fluid dynamics and pressure in the CSF.7 The Venturi effect describes the relationship between fluid velocity and pressure, stating that when fluid flows through constricted areas of tubular structures, the velocity will increase and pressure will decrease proportionally.8

Here, we discuss the case of a 57-year-old male with a history of progressive lower-extremity weakness, difficulty with ambulation, and an ataxic wide-based gait. His initial imaging studies, which included radiography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and computed tomography (CT) myelography, revealed findings concerning for thoracic syringomyelia of unclear etiology. Repeat CT myelography of the thoracic spine was concerning for a spinal arachnoid web. The intraoperative ultrasound (US) displayed an arachnoid web associated with arachnoid cyst formation, an edematous spinal cord, and a presyrinx state. He underwent surgical decompression, confirming the presence of a spinal arachnoid web.

Illustrative Case

History and Physical Examination

A 57-year-old male with a history of hypertension, low-grade clear cell papillary carcinoma of the left kidney, and left-eye blindness secondary to glaucoma presented with an 8-year history of progressively worsening symptoms, initially attributable to degenerative spine disease, that had not improved with conservative management. The initial noncontrast MRI scan of the thoracic spine showed an unconcerning small thoracic syrinx. The patient was then lost to follow-up for over 1 year, at which point he re-presented to the neurology clinic with a worsening gait. He underwent electroneuromyography, which ruled out the presence of mononeuropathies and polyneuropathies. At his subsequent re-evaluation by neurosurgery, his physical examination was notable for a spastic lower-extremity gait, lower-extremity hyperreflexia, and paraparesis.

Imaging Findings

The patient’s prior CT myelography and MRI were concerning for thoracic syringomyelia of unclear etiology. His progressively worsening symptoms prompted updated MRI and a second CT myelography study of the thoracic spine for further evaluation. MRI findings included T2 hyperintensity within the spinal cord, deformity of the spinal cord, and dorsal indentation of the spinal cord consistent with the typical “scalpel sign” seen in the thoracic arachnoid web. Short tau inversion recovery (STIR) sequences highlighted the edema within the spinal cord. CT myelography showed thoracic cord thinning at T5–7, in addition to a thin linear vertical intramedullary extension of contrast medium from T4 to T6 after intrathecal contrast administration, with an unclear etiology. Overall, the CT myelography findings in combination with the MRI results were concerning for compression of the spinal cord secondary to a dorsal thoracic arachnoid web and cyst (Figs. 1,2A, and 2B). Delayed CT myelography showed that contrast medium had entered the spinal cord as a result of elevated hydrostatic pressure within the adjacent thecal sac (Fig. 2B). Intraoperative US showed a hypoechoic cavity within the subarachnoid space, representing an arachnoid cyst. CSF flow was disrupted due to the arachnoid web via a ball-valve mechanism, in turn causing the cyst to compress the spinal cord as well as edema within the cord, resulting in a presyrinx-like state (Fig. 3).

Surgical Findings

The patient underwent an exploratory decompressive laminectomy at T5–7, during which intraoperative US guidance was used for localization of the lesion and in vivo demonstration of the ball-valve effect of CSF dynamic flow of the arachnoid web into the arachnoid cyst (Video 1). This was followed by intradural lysis of adhesions and fenestration of the arachnoid cyst to reestablish normal CSF flow dynamics (Video 2). An intraoperative photograph was obtained to display widening, swelling, and discoloration of the spinal cord, indicative of a presyrinx state (Fig. 4).

Repeat intraoperative US showed that both the cystic cavity and ball-valve effect were successfully obliterated with normal CSF flow dynamics (Fig. 5). Postoperatively, the patient reported immediate improvement iin lower-extremity strength and gait, and he was subsequently discharged after a 3-day hospital stay. At the 1-month follow-up, he continued to report subjective and objective improvement in lower-extremity strength and gait. The patient’s most recent follow-up was 10 weeks after surgery when he was seen by physical medicine and rehabilitation. His gait was significantly improved, and he required only a cane for minimal stability assistance. He was no longer in need of the wheelchair he had used prior to surgery.

Discussion

Observations

An arachnoid web occurring in the dorsal spine is a rare diagnosis with approximately 60 known reported cases that most often involved the thoracic spine; an occurrence in the cervical spine has been reported only once in the literature.6, 9 The first reported cases of dorsal arachnoid web were published in 1997 by Mallucci et al.10 The mechanism by which these webs form and alter CSF flow dynamics is not well understood, with proposed mechanisms such as the Venturi effect that relate to a pressure gradient that forms within and around the spinal cord.7 Due to the relationship between pressure and velocity described by the Venturi effect, we propose that CSF flows through the arachnoid web at high velocity and low pressure, while the CSF within the arachnoid cyst itself will have relatively high pressure due to the decreased velocity of CSF flow. The high pressure within the arachnoid cyst results in compression of the spinal cord. The observed consequence of this pressure gradient appears to result from the ball-valve mechanism, which has been applied to arachnoid cyst formation and more recently has been shown in rare cases of a thoracic arachnoid web.11, 12 The ball-valve mechanism results from the formation of the arachnoid web and creates the pressure gradient that contributes to the pathophysiology of a presyrinx state and can lead to progressive syringomyelia, resulting in progressive symptoms of myelopathy. The arachnoid web membrane lies perpendicular to the spinal cord, causing intermittent obstruction of the flow of CSF, potentially leading to a presyrinx state. If left untreated, this intermittent obstructive flow of CSF can cause a worsening pressure gradient leading to expansion of the presyrinx state into a definable syrinx cavity.

The pathophysiology of the formation of arachnoid webs is still largely unknown. Although trauma and infection are 2 of the commonly proposed etiologies, many cases of an arachnoid web occur in the absence of these risk factors.1, 3, 4, 13 Our patient did not have a history of major trauma, nor did he have any imaging findings indicating trauma as the cause of his clinical presentation. There was concern for syringomyelia on initial MRI but no obvious target for decompression. Repeat imaging with CT myelography displayed the characteristic scalpel sign, which is well described in the radiographic assessment of a dorsal arachnoid web.13 Had CT myelography been performed earlier, this radiographic sign of the arachnoid web would likely have been seen, and the patient may have benefited earlier from a decompression.

Lessons

For patients presenting with thoracic myelopathy with unexplained imaging findings concerning for syringomyelia, the presence of a dorsal thoracic web should be considered. The best presurgical diagnostic tool is delayed CT myelography in addition to the MRI. Intraoperative US provides the most accurate picture of the pathology of the dorsal thoracic arachnoid web, which we describe in association with a presyrinx state. Patients historically respond well to decompression; thus, an earlier diagnosis and subsequent treatment will lead to improved patient outcomes and quality of life.14, 15
