Happiness and Attention Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder Symptoms as Mediated by Subjective Health Conditions: A Nationwide Cross‐Sectional Internet Survey
Authors: Norio Sugawara, Takahiro Tabuchi, Keita Tokumitsu, Norio Yasui‐Furukori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThere has been limited examination of happiness among individuals with attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), despite growing interest in psychiatric comorbidities associated with this disorder. This study aims to fill this gap by exploring the relationship between ADHD symptoms and happiness on the basis of data from the Japan Society and New Tobacco Internet Survey (JASTIS).MethodA nationwide, cross‐sectional internet survey was conducted in Japan between January and February 2024. The analytic sample comprised 29 268 individuals aged 16 years and above in Japan. Based on questionnaire responses of the 6‐item short version of the Japanese version of the Adult ADHD Self‐Report Scale (ASRS‐J‐6), the respondents were classified as possible ADHD or not. Information on happiness was collected via a single (10‐point Likert scale) measure.ResultsMultivariable ordinal logistic regression analysis and a mediation analysis were performed to examine associations. Possible ADHD was associated with increased odds of feeling less happy. Very high somatic symptoms (41.1% mediation), confusion/difficulty to concentrate (37.2%), and depression (14.8%) were important mediators of the association between ADHD and happiness.ConclusionsAlthough the majority of individuals with possible ADHD were classified as having moderate happiness, more than one in four were classified as having low happiness. To increase the happiness of individuals with ADHD, screening and treating for both physical and psychiatric symptoms might be important.Keywords: ADHD, depression, happiness, health

Introduction

Attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a psychiatric condition that is diagnostically featured by inappropriate levels of inattention, hyperactivity‐impulsiveness, or both that are not suitable for one's age. It had been traditionally regarded as a childhood disorder; however, in more than half of childhood cases, symptoms persist into adulthood despite an age‐dependent decline in ADHD symptoms [1]. An up‐to‐date estimation reported that globally 2.6% of adults have ADHD that began in childhood, while 6.8% of adults show symptoms of ADHD regardless of a childhood onset [2]. ADHD is associated with deficits in several cognitive domains, including arousal, behavioral inhibition, motivation, set shifting, and working memory, which can cause mental confusion [3, 4]. Several studies have indicated that ADHD has a negative impact on developmental or functional outcomes including self‐esteem, academic achievement, physical health, and occupation [5].

Despite growing awareness of psychiatric comorbidities in ADHD [6], the role of positive emotions in ADHD has received relatively little attention [7]. Although positive emotions are generally related to beneficial outcomes in different domains [8, 9], earlier studies have shown that severe ADHD symptoms among children are correlated with difficulties in managing positive emotions, such as happiness or exuberance [10]. This emotional dysregulation could be linked to social challenges, like peer issues [11]. Additionally, higher levels of positive emotions in children with ADHD are associated with internalizing problems [12]. These findings suggest that positive emotional processes in ADHD may be complex and not necessarily reflect subjective well‐being such as happiness. Above mentioned results indicate the need to investigate the role of positive emotions in ADHD.

Happiness is an emotion characterized by high levels of positive feelings, low levels of negative feelings, and overall life satisfaction [13, 14]. There is limited research on the link between ADHD symptoms and happiness. Research from the UK indicates that children with ADHD generally feel significantly less happy about their family life and life overall [15]. In the US, a study reported that high school students in the middle or upper tertile for ADHD symptoms had significantly lower responses of self‐reported happiness [16]. Additionally, young adults previously diagnosed with ADHD report lower levels of happiness in their parenting roles [17]. Research on the general population has demonstrated that adults with severe ADHD symptoms are often less happy. Mood instability, anxiety, and depression are significant factors that mediate the relationship between ADHD and happiness [7]. Identification of mediating factors could lead to the development of effective interventions. Although prior research on the link between ADHD and happiness has been conducted in Western societies, assessments of ADHD and happiness related factors vary due to ethnocultural factors [18, 19, 20]. In Japan, ADHD in adulthood is often underrecognized, with many individuals remaining undiagnosed or misdiagnosed until adulthood [21]. Adults newly diagnosed with ADHD may feel relief yet struggle to accept the diagnosis due to limited knowledge and self‐stigma toward developmental disorders [22]. These prolonged untreated periods and associated psychosocial burdens may contribute to reduced subjective well‐being. Despite these critical issues, the association between ADHD symptoms and happiness remains largely understudied in the Japanese population. Therefore, there is a call for studies in this area within the Japanese cultural and healthcare context.

To our understanding, this is the largest study of the relationship between ADHD symptoms and happiness, and the first study conducted in the general Asian population. The objectives of this study were twofold: first, to assess whether ADHD symptoms are linked to happiness in individuals from the general population; second, to investigate how demographic factors, physical and mental health conditions, and other stressors influence this relationship.

Methods

Design and Participants

This was a cross‐sectional study designed to explore the relationship between symptoms of ADHD and happiness, utilizing data from the JASTIS database (Japan Society and New Tobacco Internet Survey) [23]. The JASTIS online survey has been conducted annually since 2015, with the 2024 survey executed between January 24 and February 27 by Rakuten Insight Inc., in Tokyo, Japan. This agency maintains a panel of approximately 2.2 million members and its data have been employed in various previous studies. The survey aimed to gather data from 32 000 panelists selected to adequately represent a diverse range of demographic and socioeconomic backgrounds. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as the study was designed to achieve broad population coverage rather than hypothesis‐driven sample size determination. Nevertheless, the sample size in the present study was sufficiently large to ensure adequate statistical power. Each demographic category (gender, age, and prefecture) had a preset respondent quota, which, once met, concluded the survey in that category. The preset number was determined from the population distribution in 2019. Details regarding the JASTIS have been previously published [23, 24, 25]. The survey design ensured that each question had to be answered before proceeding to the next, eliminating missing data issues. To achieve a representative sample of 32 000 individuals, aged 16 to 83, from across all 47 prefectures of Japan, 50 850 panelists were invited, with selection through a stratified random sampling method. The response rate was 62.9%.

Out of the 32 000 JASTIS 2024 respondents, individuals were excluded from the analysis if they had too short response time (< 600 s) (n = 358), failed an attention check question (n = 2275), reported using all listed drug categories (n = 107), had all listed health conditions (n = 68), or had more than 15 family members (n = 30), leaving 29 268 participants for analysis (Figure 1). Because some respondents met more than one exclusion criterion, these numbers are not mutually exclusive; therefore, the actual number of excluded respondents was 2732.

Outcomes and Variables

Happiness

Happiness was measured using a single question: “Do you think you are happy?” The participants rated their happiness on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 10 (completely). This method was first implemented in the Japan Gerontological Evaluation Study (JAGES) and has been validated as an effective happiness metric [26]. In this analysis, scores lower than −1 SD from the mean on happiness score were classified as lower happiness (code 2), those within ±1 SD from the mean were classified as moderate happiness (code 1), and those higher than +1 SD from the mean were classified as higher happiness (code 0).

ADHD Symptoms

The presence of ADHD symptoms over the past 6 months was evaluated using the 6‐item short version of the Japanese version of the Adult ADHD Self‐Report Scale (ASRS‐J‐6) [27]. This short form is derived from a longer 18‐item scale designed to measure ADHD symptoms in adults based on DSM‐IV criteria A. Responses were rated on a 5‐point scale from ‘never’ (0) to ‘very often’ (4). The scores from each item were summed to produce a total score ranging from 0 to 24. In this study, a total score of 15 or higher was used to indicate a possible presence of ADHD. The ASRS‐J‐6 has been validated in Japan, and has a specificity of 0.84 and a sensitivity of 0.67 [27].

Covariates

The covariates included in the analysis were age and sex (female/male).

Mediators

We selected several mediators based on prior research and clinical knowledge for our analysis [28, 29, 30]. These mediators included: employment status (employed or not), marital status (married or cohabiting versus other statuses), level of education (college education or higher versus other levels), number of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), somatic symptom burden, history of depression, confusion or difficulty with concentration, and presence of autistic traits.

Childhood trauma was assessed using the ACEs Questionnaire, which evaluates 10 types of childhood adversity [31, 32]. These are divided into personal experiences (verbal abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect) and family dysfunctions (disappearance of a parent through divorce, death, or abandonment, a mother who is a victim of domestic violence, a parent who is an alcoholic, a family member diagnosed with a mental illness, and a family member in jail). Responses to the questionnaire are binary (“yes” or “no”), and the total number of “yes” responses constitutes the ACE score. A higher score reflects an increased occurrence of negative events during the initial 18 years of life. Based on previous findings [31], we categorized the number of Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) into two groups for our study: those experiencing 4 or more ACEs and those experiencing less than 4 [33].

To evaluate somatic symptoms experienced in the week prior to the survey, we used the Japanese version of the SSS‐8 [34, 35], a shortened form of the Patient Health Questionnaire‐15. This scale includes eight items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very much), with a maximum possible score of 32. A score of 16 or above indicates a very high level of somatic symptoms [34].

History of depression was assessed based on a self‐reported question on whether participants had ever experienced depression or were currently experiencing depression.

We measured confusion or difficulty concentrating with a single question: “Are you confused or unable to concentrate?” The participants rated their state on a scale from 0 to 4: never (0), rarely (1), sometimes (2), often (3), and almost daily (4). Based on the frequency of the symptoms described by the response options statement, scores of 3 or 4 were used to identify significant symptoms of confusion or difficulty concentrating.

Autistic traits were assessed using the short form of the Autism‐Spectrum Quotient Japanese version (AQ‐J‐10) [36]. The AQ‐J‐10 is a Japanese version of the internationally used self‐reported questionnaire that quantifies autistic traits, mainly autistic social traits. This 10‐item questionnaire is a condensed version of the 50‐item AQ, which is a sensitive measure of autistic traits in the general population. Each item is rated on a 4‐point Likert scale (from “definitely agree” to “definitely disagree”). Responses indicative of autistic traits (“strongly agree” and “agree”) are scored as 1, allowing a total score between 0 and 10. A score of 7 or above in the Japanese population suggests a possible need for a comprehensive assessment for Autism Spectrum Disorder.

The internal consistency of the ASRS‐J‐6, SSS‐8, and AQ‐J‐10 was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha with unweighted data.

Statistical Analyses

To adjust the characteristics of internet survey respondents to those of the general population, we employed an inverse probability weighting (IPW) method [24]. As a representative sample, we employed the 2019 Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions (CSLC), conducted by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW). We merged data from the JASTIS and CSLC surveys, utilizing it to estimate the likelihood of participating in an internet survey. Stratification by sex and age (14 strata) was implemented, with propensity scores calculated for each group. We employed a multivariable logistic regression model to calculate propensity scores (PSs) separately for each stratum. For this analysis, we used variables that were common to both the JASTIS and CSLC surveys. In most strata, adjustments were made for variables such as area (residence), marital status, education, homeownership (household), self‐rated health, and smoking status. However, for individuals aged 16–19, adjustments for marital and smoking status were excluded because this age group generally has uniform marital status and the Ministry had ceased collecting smoking data from teenagers since 2013. Additionally, for women aged 16–19, we did not adjust for area because regional differences are considered less impactful for this subgroup, and it is noted that young women tend to have lower PC ownership rates irrespective of the size of the city in which they reside [37]. For women aged 70 and above, we excluded education from the adjustment factors because of their historically limited access to higher education.

We used chi‐square tests and Student's t‐test to analyze categorical and continuous variables, respectively, to assess differences in the sample characteristics related to possible ADHD status. Both univariable and multivariable ordinal logistic regression methods were applied to investigate the relationship between possible ADHD and levels of happiness, with the multivariable model controlling for sex and age. Additionally, a mediation analysis was conducted using the Karlson Holm Breen (khb) command in Stata to examine whether certain variables mediated the relationship between possible ADHD status and happiness [38]. This approach allowed us to break down the total effect of a variable into direct and indirect components and to compute the percentage of the main relationship that is explained by these mediators.

Referring to previous studies, variables such as paid employment, marital status, education, ACEs, somatic symptom burden, history of depression, confusion/difficulty to concentrate, and autistic traits were considered as potential mediators. These factors were each included in separate models while adjusting for age and sex. The results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and a p of less than 0.05 was used to denote statistical significance. In sensitivity analyses, we additionally treated the ASRS‐J‐6 total score as a continuous variable instead of using the dichotomized definition of possible ADHD, and repeated the ordinal logistic regression and mediation analyses. Furthermore, to examine the robustness of the findings, we also conducted analyses without applying IPW. All statistical procedures were conducted using Stata version 17.0 (Stata Corp LP, College Station, Texas).

Results

The sample consisted of 29 268 individuals aged ≥ 16 years, and 50.6% of the participants were female. The prevalence of possible ADHD was 3.7%. Among all participants, 10.3%, 76.8%, and 13.0% were classified as having high, moderate, and low levels of happiness, respectively. The overall sample characteristics and stratification by the presence of possible ADHD after IPW are presented in Table 1 and the corresponding unweighted results are shown in Table S1. The prevalence of possible ADHD was significantly higher in participants who were younger, male, had paid work, a higher number of ACEs, a higher score of SSS‐8, a history of depression, confusion/difficulty concentrating, a higher score of AQ‐J‐10, as well as in individuals who were not married/cohabiting. The Cronbach's alpha coefficients were 0.86 for the ASRS‐J‐6, 0.85 for the SSS‐8, and 0.73 for the AQ‐J‐10, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Discussion

Among the participants in this study, 10.3% were classified as having high happiness and 3.7% were classified as having possible ADHD. Individuals with possible ADHD were more likely to exhibit very high somatic symptoms, face confusion/difficulty concentrating, and have a history of depression than those without ADHD. Multivariable analysis revealed that possible ADHD was associated with happiness, and several factors including demographic factors, physical and mental health conditions, and other stressors mediated this relationship. The factors with the higher proportion of mediation, in order, were very high somatic symptoms, confusion/difficulty concentrating, and a history of depression. Results were consistent across analyses with and without IPW.

Individuals with ADHD have more physical illnesses than those without ADHD [39]. A study using a Japanese health insurance claims database also revealed that adults with ADHD, especially those over 40 years old and those with psychiatric comorbidities, are more prone to various physical illnesses such as diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease [39]. Difficulty of maintaining a healthy lifestyle due to ADHD symptoms, along with the impact of psychiatric comorbidities might lead to these multiple physical comorbidities. Our findings, which show that very high physical symptoms contribute to low levels of happiness, indicate that appropriate physical interventions could improve both physical and psychological well‐being. However, a previous study that used mediation analysis to explore the link between ADHD and happiness reported that physical health conditions diagnosed by health professionals in the past year accounted for only 10.9% of the effect [7]. Several studies of the factor structures of psychiatric symptoms have indicated that mental and physical complaints are more likely to merge in Asian populations [40, 41, 42]. In Asia, concepts of mind–body holism are supported by traditional Chinese medicine [41], and Japanese traditional religions such as Shintoism, Buddhism, and Qi also emphasize the unity of mind and body [43]. In our study, the somatic symptoms measured by the SSS‐8 scale could partly indicate a psychological burden.

Individuals with ADHD could experience mental confusion due to various cognitive impairments. These include concentration deficits [44], uncontrolled mind‐wandering [45], reduced post‐error slowing [46], and alterations in the prefrontal cortex and its connections [47]. These cognitive impairments have a negative impact on maintaining focus, resisting distractions, and managing behavior, attention, and emotions and could contribute to lower self‐esteem, lower academic or occupational achievement, and poor physical health [5]. However, to date, a variety of treatments have been effective in enhancing cognitive function in individuals with ADHD. Psychostimulants are known to improve attentional deficits, memory, and vigilance [48]. Additionally, non‐pharmacological interventions, such as aerobic exercise and virtual reality, have been shown to increase attention and processing speed among individuals with ADHD [49, 50]. Detecting and treating ADHD symptoms could increase psychological well‐being in the undiagnosed adult population.

Previous research has demonstrated that depression is associated with low levels of happiness [51]. Depression, which is prevalent in individuals with ADHD [52, 53], could be a significant mediator in the relationship between ADHD symptoms and low happiness [7]. Furthermore, depression has been associated with a greater number of stressful or negative events in individuals with ADHD [54], and the present study also revealed the mediating role of depression between possible ADHD symptoms and low levels of happiness. Although we observed an association between possible ADHD and feeling less happy, it should be emphasized that individuals with possible ADHD had a comparable ratio of high happiness as those without ADHD, with a majority of moderate happiness respondents. Our results are in line with earlier studies on the role of happiness in psychiatric disorders, which have indicated that many people with these disorders report being happy or fairly happy [7, 51].

Our findings suggest that somatic symptoms, cognitive difficulties, and depression are key correlates of lower happiness among individuals with ADHD symptoms. These findings highlight the potential importance of comprehensive assessment targeting both physical and psychiatric symptoms in this population. In particular, somatic symptom burden and concentration difficulties accounted for a substantial proportion of the association, suggesting that these domains may represent clinically relevant targets for evaluation and management.

This study has several limitations related to the measures that were used which should be considered when interpreting the results. First, ADHD was evaluated based on a subjective self‐report, not on an assessment by a clinician according to criteria from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5). Further study using a structured diagnostic interview that is designed to be administered by trained interviewers is warranted. Second, we did not conduct interviews or cognitive assessments for difficulty concentrating and confusion. Third, we employed a single‐item question to assess happiness. This approach might have posed issues since there is evidence suggesting that single‐item measures of subjective well‐being can be less reliable than multi‐item scales [55]. However, other studies have shown that single‐item measures of happiness are both valid and reliable for community samples [56]. Fourth, the data were obtained from an internet survey. Although we adjusted as much as possible to account for possible bias in the collected sample by using an external, nationally representative sample, selection bias was not completely ruled out. Fifth, a cross‐sectional design cannot clarify the direction of causality. However, the aim of our study was not to clarify the causal direction but to investigate how demographic factors, physical and mental health conditions, and other stressors influence the relationship between ADHD and happiness in order to develop effective interventions. Therefore, we do not believe that the cross‐sectional design of this study to have adversely affected the relevance of our results.

In conclusion, our study revealed that ADHD symptoms affect happiness and that several factors mediate this relationship. Although the majority of individuals with possible ADHD were classified as having moderate happiness, more than one in four were classified as having low happiness. Screening and treatment of physical and psychiatric symptoms may be relevant targets for improving happiness in individuals with ADHD, although causal relationships cannot be inferred from this cross‐sectional study.

Please contact the Institutional Review Board of Osaka International Cancer Institute with data requests. Upon request, the board will decide whether to share the data.
