Caregiver’s burden and their relationship to physical activity, exercise self-efficacy, perceived exercise benefits and barriers in caregivers of children with autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Güllü Aydın Yağcıoğlu, Ayşe Kübra Söyler, Kübra Ersoy Temiz, Turan Emre Özdemir, Berkan Torpil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCaregivers of children with autism spectrum disorder (CwASD) often face high levels of stress, emotional exhaustion and physical strain due to the complex and demanding nature of caring for a child with special needs. As caregivers’ well-being may contribute positively to caregiving and thus reduce caregiver burden, there is a need for studies that elucidate the factors associated with caregiver burden. Therefore, this study aims to examine the relationship between caregiving burden and physical activity, exercise self-efficacy, and perceived exercise benefits and barriers in caregivers of CwASD.MethodsThe study population consisted of primary caregivers of a CwASD for at least 1 year. Caregiving burden was assessed using the Zarit Caregiver Burden Scale, physical activity using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short form (IPAQ-SF), exercise self-efficacy using the Exercise Self-Efficacy Scale, and perceived exercise benefit using the Exercise Benefits/Barriers Scale. The relationship between caregiver burden and other parameters was analysed.ResultsThe study included 55 participants. There was no relationship between caregiver burden and IPAQ-SF, exercise self-efficacy scale, and exercise benefit/barriers scale in caregivers of CwASD (p>0.05). In addition, the caregiving burden was associated with the number of children in the family and duration of care (r: 0.35; p<0.05). According to linear regression, the overall model was not statistically significant (p>0.05) and explained 16.7% of the variance in caregiver burden.ConclusionsThe current findings suggest that the burden experienced by caregivers of CwASD may be influenced by factors beyond those related to physical activity. Further research is needed to identify additional determinants of caregiver burden and develop more targeted support approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03468-2.

Background

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by avoidance of eye contact, restricted interests, repetitive sensorimotor behaviours, attention deficit, and impairments in social interaction and communication that begins in early childhood and persists throughout life [1, 2]. Data published in 2022 shows that one in every 100 children worldwide has been diagnosed with ASD. It also shows that prevalence estimates have increased over time and that these rates vary across sociodemographic groups [3]. Because CwASD have significant impairments in their daily activities, parents are the primary caregivers for their children in areas where they struggle [4].

The role of caregiver for CwASD presents challenges beyond the day-to-day responsibilities of typical parenting [5]. The impact on families caring for children with ASD goes beyond emotional, psychological, physical and economic factors. Parents may experience deep and distressing feelings such as shame, guilt or self-blame, which can significantly affect the family’s overall well-being [6]. Caregivers of CwASD often experience high levels of stress, emotional exhaustion and physical strain due to the complex and demanding nature of caring for a child with special needs [7, 8]. As caregivers well-being may contribute positively to caregiving and thus reduce caregiver burden, there is a need for studies that focus on caregivers and elucidate the factors associated with caregiver burden [9, 10].

Physical activity and exercise can be defined as a set of movements that protect against disease and increase resistance, improve health functions of the individual or maintain well-being [11]. Physical activity is important not only to protect and build physical health, but also to support participation with meaningful contributions to mental and spiritual well-being. Previous studies have analysed the relationship between physical activity and exercise and caregiving burden in caregivers of patients with other physical and/or mental disorders [11–14]. Although physical activity has been associated with physical and psychological health outcomes in caregivers, the effect of physical activity or exercise in caregivers of CwASD has been shown in a limited number of studies in the literature [9, 15–17]. In addition, participation in exercise is influenced by factors such as self-efficacy in relation to exercise and the perceived benefits and barriers of exercise. However, it is unclear whether these perceptions are associated with caregiver burden in this population. Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine the relationship between caregiver burden, physical activity level, exercise self-efficacy and perceived benefits and barriers to exercise among caregivers of children with ASD.

Methods

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Gülhane Health Sciences University, (2024/27, 3 Jan 2024). The aim of the study was explained to the caregivers and each caregiver was asked to read and sign the informed consent form.

The study population consisted of caregivers of children with autism who attended the Daily Living Activities Application Unit of the Occupational Therapy Department at the University of Health Sciences’ Gülhane Faculty of Health Sciences for routine treatment or follow-up between February and November 2024. Families or primary caregivers of CwASD who attended the unit were invited to participate in the study. Those who agreed and met the eligibility criteria were included.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) being aged 18 or over (2), being the primary informal caregiver responsible for the care of a child with ASD (3), caring for a child with ASD aged 18 or under, and (4) having provided care for at least one year.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) being aged over 65 years (2), having a chronic physical condition that could affect participation in the assessments and cause disability (3), caring for more than one child with ASD, and (4) being responsible for caring for another individual (e.g. another person with a disability or an older adult).

As no study has analysed the relationship between scales measuring the burden of caregiving and physical activity/exercise among caregivers of children with ASD, the correlation coefficient (r = -0.46) was calculated between physical activity and the burden of caregiving among caregivers of individuals with Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [13].

Therefore, it was determined that the required sample size for a correlation analysis should be 45 to achieve 90% power for an alpha error probability of 0.05, as calculated using G*Power (Ver. 3.1.9.4, Düsseldorf University, Germany) [18, 19]. Taking possible data losses into account, the study was ultimately completed with 55 caregivers.

After obtaining sociodemographic information (age, weight, gender, marital status, educational status, alcohol/smoking, current psychiatric disorder or medication use, etc.) of the caregivers (supplementary file.), caregiving burden was assessed using the Zarit Caregiver Burden Scale, physical activity using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (short form), exercise self-efficacy using the Exercise Self-Efficacy Scale, and perceived exercise benefit using the Exercise Benefits/Barriers Scale. Turkish versions of all the tests used in this study were available in the literature, and these were utilised in the study.

The zarit caregiver burden scale

The Zarit Caregiver Burden Scale (ZCBS) is a widely used instrument for assessing the burden experienced by caregivers of individuals requiring long-term care. It consists of 22 items and the total score ranges from 0 to 88 (low/no burden to severe burden) and is classified as follows: 0–20 low/no burden; 21–40 light/moderate burden; 41–60 moderate/severe burden; 61–88 overload burden [20]. Good internal consistency was found in the Turkish adaptation of ZBI, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.83 [21].

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (Short Form) (IPAQ-SF)

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (Short Form) (IPAQ-SF) questionnaire consists of 4 sections and 7 questions. The questionnaire includes questions about physical activity of 10 min or more in the last 7 days. The MET value is calculated to determine the level of physical activity and one MET is the equivalent of energy expenditure at rest.

METs were calculated by multiplying the number of minutes by the number of activity days and 3.3 (walking), 4 (moderate activity) or 8.0 (vigorous activity). A total MET value of less than 600 MET min/week indicates inactivity, 600–3000 MET min/week indicates minimal activity and > 3000 MET min/week indicates activity [22, 23]. The Turkish validation study showed that the IPAQ-SF had an acceptable test–retest reliability coefficient of 0.69 and good agreement [23].

The exercise self-efficacy scale

This questionnaire was developed to measure self-efficacy for exercise and consists of 18-items. Each item should be scored between 0 (not at all possible) and 100 (definitely possible) with a cut-off point of 10. High scores on the scale are an indication that the participant has a high level of exercise self-efficacy. The Turkish version of the Exercise Self-Efficacy Scale was found to be reliable, with Cronbach’s alphas of 0.978 and 0.977, and also demonstrated test-retest reliability (0.968) [24].

The exercise benefits/barriers scale

The scale consists of two subscales, Exercise Benefits (29 items, 29–116 points) and Exercise Barriers (14 items, 14–56 points), with a total of 43 items. The total score ranges from 43 to 172. A high total score on the Exercise Benefits scale is indicative of a positive perception of the benefits of exercise, whereas a high total score on the Exercise Barriers scale is indicative of a negative perception of the barriers to exercise. A high overall score indicates a positive perception of the benefits of exercise [25]. The Turkish version of the Exercise Benefits/Barriers Scale also demonstrated strong psychometric properties, with validity coefficients of 0.87 for the overall scale, 0.95 for the benefits subscale and 0.80 for the barriers subscale [26].

Statistical analysis

SPSS IBM 26.0 version was used for data analysis. The normality of the distribution of all continuous variables was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test. This test was then used to decide whether parametric or non-parametric methods should be applied in later analyses.

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the socio-demographic characteristics of the caregivers in the study. Data of the caregivers were described as mean and standard deviation (SD) for quantitative variables and median and Q1-Q3 (1st and 3rd quartile) values for non-normally distributed data, and number (n) and percentage (%) for nominal/ordinal data. The relationships between caregiver burden and other parameters were evaluated using the Spearman’s correlation coefficient. The Spearmen’s correlations (rho) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs; derived using Fisher’s z transformation) were classified as follows: weak (0.05 to 0.30), moderate (0.30 to 0.70), and strong (0.70 to 1.00) [27]. A p-value of less than 0.05 was significant.

Results

The study involved 55 people who had been caring for a child with autism for at least 1 year.

Mothers of children with ASD represented 90.9% of those surveyed. Caregivers’ ages ranged from 22 to 58 (39.00 ± 8.94 years). The majority of caregivers were married (n = 49, 89.1%), non-smokers (n = 36, 65.5%), and well-educated (bachelor’s degree [n = 25, 45.5%]). Discussion

Providing long-term care is a very demanding process that affects the physical health as well as the psychological health of the caregiver, therefore the quality of life. Families of CwASD experience high levels of stress and fatigue and limited time for personal activities due to the unique behaviours and communication difficulties caused by autism spectrum disorder [28].

Furthermore, it has been documented that the reduced quality of physical activity among mothers of children with ASD contributes to increased feelings of fatigue [9]. This adds to an already difficult caring burden. Therefore, it is important to determine the factors associated with caregiving burden. The current study examined the relationship between caregiver burden and physical activity, exercise self-efficacy, perceived exercise benefits and barriers among caregivers of CwASD and found that there was no relationship between caregiver burden and other factors mentioned. Furthermore, it was found that the burden on caregivers increased when they provided care for children with CwASD for longer periods, and when they had more children in the family. However, this relationship was not significant in the regression analysis. This may be due to the variables having a non-linear relationship, the effects of other included variables, or the limited sample size.

A previous study indicated that, as parents and children with ASD grow older, the parents tend to become more physically active. This is based on the idea that, as children become more independent, their parents’ responsibilities decrease, enabling them to devote time to activities such as exercise [29]. Another study showed that ASD-caregivers had similar levels of physical activity and perceived health to non-ASD-caregivers [15]. A recent study confirmed the relationship between physical activity and quality of life in families with children with ASD [30]. In addition, a study that included parents of CwASD reported that those with a high physical activity level had a greater caregiving burden [31]. In an another study, low physical activity among parents of CwASD was associated with increased parental stress in defending the child’s needs [32]. A review of studies involving caregivers of individuals with various health conditions revealed that lower levels of physical activity were linked to a greater sense of burden among informal caregivers of individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and dementia [11, 13, 33]. Although 85.4% of parents in this study had low levels of physical activity, this was not related to the burden of care, unlike the studies mentioned above. There are several possible reasons for these results. The parents in our study were mostly highly educated and had a high income. Most of them were married, suggesting that there was at least one other person at home who could help. The wide age range of the participants may also have led to different levels of physical activity, which could have eliminated the relationship with the burden of caregiving.

Similarly, the lack of relationship between caregiver burden and exercise self-efficacy and perceived benefits and barriers to exercise suggests that confidence in one’s ability to exercise may not be a critical factor in alleviating caregiver burden. In the literature, after implementing a weekly 60-minute exercise programme for caregivers of people with dementia for 8 weeks, it was found that the exercise group had higher exercise self-efficacy scores and lower caregiver burden than the non-exercise group [34]. Another study showed that perceived benefits and barriers were related to stages of change in the activity levels of older German caregivers [35]. In contrast to these studies, a previous study showed that there was no relationship between exercise self-efficacy and physical and psychological quality of life in cancer caregivers, similar to our findings [36]. Although the aforementioned studies do not directly reveal the relationship between perceptions of exercise and caregiving burden, they may indicate that exercise may affect caregiving burden as well as physical health for caregivers. In the current study, we attempted to take a different perspective on the issue and sought to determine the relationship between perceptions of exercise (exercise self-efficacy, expected benefits of exercise and barriers to exercise) and caregiver burden among caregivers of CwASD and found that there was no relationship. While this does not change the fact that physical activity is very important for human health and may affect caring responsibilities due to its important contributions to physical and mental health [11, 12, 37], it may be that the overwhelming nature of caring responsibilities overshadows the perceived benefits of exercise. It may be that the overwhelming nature of caregiving responsibilities overshadows the perceived benefits of exercise. Caregivers often prioritise immediate tasks, such as managing behavioural challenges, arranging medical appointments or attending to household responsibilities, over their own health behaviours. Previous studies have noted that competing caregiving demands, time constraints and emotional exhaustion can limit caregivers’ ability to exercise, even when they recognise its benefits [38, 39]. This suggests that caregiving priorities may indeed overshadow the perceived advantages of physical activity. In summary, it is important for caregivers to increase their physical activity levels. However, the small sample size of this study may have been insufficient to establish relationships.

Although no significant associations were found, the descriptive findings provide important context. Compared with previous studies, the caregivers in present study experienced moderate levels of caregiving burden. These findings were consistent with those among mothers of children with cystic fibrosis, but lower than the burden experienced by caregivers of children with cancer, primary ciliary dyskinesia, and other physical disabilities [40–42]. These variations may be explained by differences in the characteristics of the disease, the demands of caregiving, and the amount of social support available. In terms of physical activity, the caregivers in this study were more active than the mothers of children with moderate-to-severe motor disabilities [43]. However, they were still less active than the mothers of children with Duchenne muscular dystrophy [44]. This suggests that the nature and severity of a child’s condition can impact maternal physical activity patterns differently.

Also, although the caregivers of children with ASD in the current study reported positive perceptions of the benefits of exercise, their levels of physical activity and self-efficacy regarding exercise remained relatively low. Similar findings have been reported among caregivers of children with motor disabilities, suggesting that caregiving responsibilities, limited time and competing daily demands may restrict participation in regular physical activity, despite favourable attitudes towards exercise [43]. This discrepancy between favourable attitudes and low self-efficacy, coupled with persistent obstacles such as time constraints and caregiving responsibilities, may be the reason why positive beliefs do not result in physical activity. Overall, these findings suggest that, although caregivers recognise the importance of physical activity, they encounter significant practical and psychological obstacles that hinder their ability to engage in regular exercise. Therefore, interventions that target exercise self-efficacy and modifiable barriers, such as time constraints and caregiving demands, could encourage caregivers to be more physically active.

The association found in the current study between caregiving duration and the number of children in the family is consistent with previous studies of caregivers of individuals with CwASD and other people who require care [45–47]. In the current study suggested that caring for CwASD for longer periods of time leads to greater feelings of strain, possibly through accumulating stress and fatigue. In addition, the presence of more children in the family may contribute to perceived burden by increasing the demands on the caregivers.

This highlights the fact that caregivers need to be supported as they come to terms with the reality of having CwASD.

The limitations of the current study need to be discussed. Firstly, the results of the study represented a small group of primary caregivers of CwASD in Türkiye, so they cannot be generalised to all caregivers of CwASD. A second limitation of the study was the use of a self-report assessment tool to determine physical activity. This may have influenced the results, as participants may have selected the “don’t know/unsure” option to avoid providing a response. We were also unable to determine the severity of autism. Additionally, this study did not assess any latent or unmeasured variables, such as social support, coping strategies, sleep quality, or mental health status. These variables may have influenced the findings.

Finally, there were limited studies on the relationship between caregiving burden and physical activity and exercise in caregivers. This limited the current study’s discussion with others.

Future studies are needed with different assessment instruments (preferably objective measures) and larger sample sizes.

In conclusion, the findings suggest that the burden experienced by caregivers of CwASD is a complex phenomenon that cannot be explained by physical activity-related factors alone. Further studies using objective measurement tools, larger sample sizes and more comprehensive approaches are needed to better understand the factors influencing caregiver burden in this population.
