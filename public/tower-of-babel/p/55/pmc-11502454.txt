Vaginal dinoprostone insert compared with two different oral misoprostol regimens for labor induction in nulliparous and multiparous women
Authors: Damaris Erhardt, Anda‐Petronela Radan, Jérôme Mathis, Daniel Surbek
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLabor induction exhibits considerable variations in protocols and medication regimens. Limited studies compare vaginal dinoprostone inserts with different oral misoprostol dosages, considering parity influence. This study explores the distinctions among 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone inserts and oral misoprostol 25 μg every 2 and every 4 h for labor induction, stratified by parity.Material and MethodsThis retrospective cohort study involved 607 participants across two hospitals. The primary outcome, time from induction to delivery, and secondary outcomes, including mode of delivery and maternal and fetal safety, were assessed.ResultsPatient characteristics revealed differences in indication for labor induction, with the dinoprostone cohort having fewer post‐term and premature rupture of membranes cases but more intrauterine growth restriction/small‐for‐gestational age. Both oral misoprostol regimens showed a shorter time to delivery interval compared to the dinoprostone cohort (median: 1380 min [IQR 1381.0] and 1127.0 min [IQR 1214.0] vs 1631.5 [IQR 1736.2], p < 0.001 and p = 0.014). Only the difference between oral misoprostol q2h and vaginal dinoprostone remained significant for nulliparous but not multiparous women, losing significance over all the population after adjusting for confounding factors. The proportion of women giving birth within 24 h did not significantly differ between misoprostol q2h and dinoprostone after adjusting for confounders. When comparing misoprostol q4h with dinoprostone after confounder adjustment, an increased time to delivery interval for misoprostol q4h was found (p = 0.001). Both oral misoprostol regimens exhibited fewer meconium‐stained liquor (miso q4h: OR 0.44, miso q2h: OR 0.34) and cesarean sections (miso q4h: OR 0.48, miso q2h: OR 0.53) compared to dinoprostone, even after adjustment for confounders.ConclusionsOur study suggests that oral misoprostol 25 μg q4h is less effective than 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone for labor induction if parity and indication for induction are adjusted for, particularly in multiparous women. In terms of side effects, oral misoprostol regimens seem superior to vaginal dinoprostone. Our data support the individualized use of different agents for labor induction according to parity, indication for induction, bishop score, and women's preference.

INTRODUCTION

Labor induction accounts for 26.2% of all deliveries in Switzerland, 9.6% globally, and 6.8%–33% in Europa. 1 , 2 The status of the cervix influences the duration of labor induction and can be stimulated through mechanical agents and prostaglandins. Misoprostol, preferably orally, is the most effective medication for an immature cervix. However, the ideal regimen is uncertain. 3

Different clinical guidelines provide varying recommendations, such as avoiding initial doses exceeding 50 μg and single doses exceeding 100 μg (Working Group of Scientific Medical Specialist Societies (AWMF) guidelines), a dosage range of 20–40 μg administered every 2 h up to a maximum of 12 dosages (International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics [FIGO] guidelines), or doses of 25 μg given every 2 h (Swiss Society of Gynecology and Obstetrics [SGGG] guidelines) 3 , 4 , 5 Since 2022, off‐label use is no longer necessary. A licensed product called Angusta® has been approved for use, prescribed either 25 μg every 2 h or 50 μg every 4 h. 6

Dinoprostone, an E2 prostaglandine, is an alternative for labor induction for an unripe cervix. The standard practice involves vaginal application of 10 mg dinoprostone vaginal inserts and removal after a 24‐h period.

Ideal prostaglandin administration represents a delicate balance. It should be high enough to expedite delivery, thereby reducing the risk of infection after premature rupture of membranes (PROM), 7 increasing maternal satisfaction, 8 and possibly reducing associated costs. 9 Simultaneously, it should be low enough to minimize the rates of uterine hyperstimulation, as typically seen in 200 mcg misoprostol vagina inserts, 10 uterine rupture, adverse neonatal outcomes, 11 and cesarean section. 12

Our study hypothesizes that administering 25 μg of misoprostol for labor induction every 2 h, instead of every 4 h, up to the full dosage of 200 μg, leads to a reduction in the time to delivery. We anticipate that this adjusted regimen will maintain safety levels comparable to vaginal dinoprostone, as suggested by previous research. 13 , 14 , 15 As an exploratory outcome, we aim to compare cohorts with different risk profiles and investigate the impact of distinct patient characteristics and reasons for labor induction on time to delivery.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design

This multicenter retrospective cohort study was conducted in two centers: a regional hospital with around 900 deliveries per year and the university hospital with over 2000 deliveries per year. Because of the retrospective design of the study, informed consent was not obtained.

Cases were identified from a birth register, and patient data were retrieved from electronic databases (KISIM = clinic information system, ipdos = integrated patient dossier Inselspital, Intellispace). No biological material was examined.

Data collection for the first cohort, receiving misoprostol 25 μg every 4 h, occurred from November 2017 to March 2020. The second cohort, receiving misoprostol 25 μg every 2 h, was recruited from March 2020 to December 2021 in the same regional hospital. The third cohort, induced with dinoprostone 10 mg vaginal inserts, was enlisted in the university hospital from May to December 2021. The regional hospital pharmacy follows a standardized process to create 25 μg misoprostol capsules from 200 μg tablets. Eighteen 200 μg tablets are weighed, crushed, and mixed with colored Carmin and Mannitol for even distribution. The appropriate amount of powder for a 25 μg dose is then placed into the capsules. To ensure accuracy, the weight is regularly checked by randomly selecting 20 capsules. No more than two capsules can deviate over 10% from the mean mass, and none can deviate over 20%.

Inclusion criteria

Patients undergoing labor induction with oral misoprostol or the vaginal dinoprostone insert as the first method applied were included, excluding those with a first induction method other than mechanical ones (membrane sweep, cervical priming with rods (Dilapan®) 16 or single or double balloon catheter). Singleton pregnancies with cephalic presentation, Bishop score less than six, gestational age of at least 32 weeks, and no contraindication for vaginal delivery were included. No patients with previous cesarean delivery were included. 17 In the center where oral misoprostol was used for induction, patients with fetuses with suspected intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR)/small‐for‐gestational age (SGA) were excluded in concordance with internal guidelines. The indications for labor induction included postterm pregnancy, PROM/preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), gestational or preexisting diabetes mellitus, preeclampsia or hypertension, other maternal illnesses, social or psychological factors, IUGR or SGA, oligohydramnios or anhydramnios, and other fetal conditions.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was time to delivery, defined as the duration between the first administration of misoprostol and insertion of dinoprostone until delivery, with secondary outcomes, including delivery within 24 h, maternal mortality, placental abruption, uterine rupture, uterine hyperstimulation (>5 contractions/10 min over a period of 30 min), uterine hyperstimulation with fetal heart rate (FHR) alterations, blood loss, mode of delivery, failed inductions, fetal mortality, neonatal resuscitation (including continuous positive airway pressure), admission in the neonatal intensive care unit, low umbilical cord artery gas pH (<7.15) and the presence of meconium‐stained liquor. Maternal characteristics including age, parity, body mass index, use of epidural anesthesia, presence of PROM/PPROM, bishop score before administering the medication under examination, and gestational age, as well as an exploration of the reason for labor induction, were also analyzed. Additionally, time to delivery was compared between the three cohorts when stratified for nulliparous and multiparous women, respectively.

Statistical analyses

Data analysis was performed using Google Sheets and JASP software 18 Descriptive statistics were used for time to delivery, cohort characteristics, and safety outcomes. ANOVA (analysis of variance) with post hoc testing, chi‐square tests, as well as logistic and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted. For the ANOVA and multiple linear regression analyses, time to delivery was transformed into a logarithm to correct for the assumed non‐normal distribution of data.

To determine the sample size required for our study, we conducted a power analysis. Our primary outcome measure is time to delivery between misoprostol administered every four and every 2 h, respectively. We set our desired level of statistical power at 0.80 and an alpha level of 0.05. With an anticipated effect of a reduction in time until delivery of 2 h from a mean time until delivery of 1000 min based on prior research, 19 , 20 our power analysis revealed that a sample size of 177 participants is required. 19 , 21 , 22 For the comparison between oral misoprostol and dinoprostone, an even smaller sample size was expected to be sufficient. 23

The median time to delivery was 1380 min (interquartile range [IQR] 1381.0 min) for misoprostol q4h, 1127.0 min (IQR 1214.0 min) for misoprostol q2h, and 1631.5 min (IQR 1736.2 min) for dinoprostone. Time to delivery was significantly longer in the dinoprostone cohort compared to the misoprostol 2 h cohort (p‐value <0.001) and the misoprostol 4 h cohort (p‐value 0.014). No significant differences were observed between both misoprostol cohorts. In nulliparous women, time to delivery in the dinoprostone cohort was equally significantly longer than in the misoprostol q2h cohort (median misoprostol q2h 1259.0 min, IQR 1223.5 min, dinoprostone 1813.9 min, IQR 1962.0 min, p‐value 0.020), with no significant difference between both misoprostol cohorts as well as between misoprostol q4h and dinoprostone. In multiparous women, no significant difference between the three cohorts was found.

After adjusting for confounders (maternal age, body mass index, parity, gestational age, Bishop Score PROM/PPROM, and epidural anesthesia) in the multiple linear regression analysis, only the difference between misoprostol q4h and dinoprostone remained significant (p‐value 0.001) with a longer time to delivery in the cohort induced with misoprostol q4h, particularly in multiparous women (p‐value 0.036). In nulliparous women, none of the cohorts significantly differed from each other after correcting for confounders (Table 3).

The misoprostol q2h cohort showed a significantly higher (p‐value <0.001) rate of delivery within 24 h (63.1%) compared to the dinoprostone cohort (42.2%). However, after correcting for the same confounders as in the multiple linear regression analysis for time to delivery, the difference was no longer statistically significant.

No maternal deaths or uterine ruptures occurred in any of the cohorts. One case of placental abruption was observed in both the dinoprostone and misoprostol q2h cohorts. One fetal death occurred in the misoprostol q2h cohort, because of severe perinatal asphyxia with refractory circulatory insufficiency. At 8 cm dilatation, severe bradycardia occurred that resolved after hexoprenalin sulfate application, but during the second stage of labor, pathologic tachycardia occurred. After failed vacuum extraction, rescue cesarean delivery was performed. An autopsy confirmed the perinatal asphyxia as the cause of death. The bradycardia occurred 7 h 25 min after the last oral misoprostol administration; these are unlikely causally linked. Rates of meconium (misoprostol q4h p‐value 0.025, OR 0.44; misoprostol q2h p‐value 0.012, OR 0.42), cesarean section (misoprostol q4h p‐value 0.010, OR 0.43; misoprostol q2h p‐value 0.017, OR 0.49), and failed induction (misoprostol q4h p‐value 0.002, OR 0.12; misoprostol q2h p‐value 0.001, OR 0.16) were significantly reduced for both misoprostol cohorts compared to dinoprostone. This, even though adjustment for the same confounders as in the multiple regression analysis for time to delivery and delivery within 24 h was done. Hyperstimulation without FHR changes was lower in the misoprostol q2h cohort (p‐value 0.020, Odds ratio 0.37), compared with dinoprostone (Table 4). Mean postpartum blood loss was significantly lower in the misoprostol 2 h cohort compared to the dinoprostone cohort (p‐tukey 0.004, absolute differences in mean blood loss 98.65 mL). DISCUSSION

Oral misoprostol 25 μg given every 2 and every 4 h both significantly reduced time to delivery compared to 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone inserts. In nulliparous women, only misoprostol given every 2 h resulted in a significantly shorter time to delivery than vaginal dinoprostone. However, after correcting for confounders, a significant increase in time to delivery was found for oral misoprostol 25 μg given every 4 h compared to 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone inserts, particularly in multiparous women. The rate of deliveries within 24 h was also significantly higher in the cohort induced with oral misoprostol 25 μg every 2 h compared to 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone inserts. Again, this difference was not significant anymore when adjusting for confounders. The disappearance of differences after correcting for confounders is likely attributable to variations in cohort characteristics, which were largely significantly different between the cohorts (Table 1). The 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone insert cohort most importantly exhibited a higher proportion of nulliparous women, where additional analyses found distinct outcomes in nulliparous and multiparous women.

The patients induced with 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone inserts were enrolled in a university hospital, and the patients induced with misoprostol in a regional hospital. This resulted in differences in maternal characteristics (Table 1) that are known to influence time to delivery parity 24 maternal age, 25 body mass index, 26 bishop score, 27 gestational age, 28 , 29 epidural anesthesia, 30 PROM/PPROM. 31 Due to the difference in risk profile of the patients enrolled in the university and regional hospital, the reasons for labor induction appeared unevenly distributed among the three cohorts (Table 2). We suspect the indications postterm and PROM/PPROM to have influenced time to delivery and IUGR/SGA, maternal or fetal illnesses, to have influenced safety outcomes. The different time periods of data collection are not suspected to have influenced the outcomes under investigation, as these outcomes are not considered to be time‐sensitive. These discrepancies pose a risk of bias in our analysis, which we have carefully addressed. Our correction for confounders included the two most common indications for labor induction (postterm in the form of gestational age, PROM/PPROM). Additionally, we corrected for maternal characteristics in the multiple linear regression analysis and some of the absolute mean differences we deemed clinically irrelevant (maternal age: 1.7 years, gestational age: 6.1 days, bishop score: 1.53 points).

Due to the multicentric nature of the study, there is additional risk for differences in patient management between the two hospitals, for example, in identifying failed inductions. The differences in application and schedule of the three induction regimens may introduce bias in time to delivery: oral misoprostol 25 μg given every 2 h poses a higher risk of delayed medication administration and possibly prolongation of induction than the other two regimens. Additionally, some inductions were paused, resulting in durations as long as 20 386 min in the misoprostol 25 μg given every 2 h cohort. Initially, we intended to conduct additional analyses to address these issues. However, due to the extensive analyses already performed and the numerous additional analyses required to answer these questions, they were not included in this article.

Given the sample size, we cannot draw conclusions regarding rare events such as maternal and fetal mortality, uterine rupture, or placental abruption. Furthermore, we did not assess the costs of induction or patient satisfaction.

After confounder adjustments, we found 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone inserts to be more efficient than oral misoprostol 25 μg administered every 4 h for labor induction. The shortest time to delivery occurred in nulliparous women induced with oral misoprostol 25 μg given every 2 h, but the difference vanished when accounting for confounders. Safety outcomes favored both oral misoprostol regimens, possibly influenced by a higher‐risk population induced with 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone inserts.

Only a limited number of studies have compared different dosages and schedules of oral misoprostol administration. Pharmacological studies favor a 2‐h interval, 32 whereas some studies would also advocate for a 4‐h interval. 33 In a Cochrane Review examining low‐dose misoprostol regimens for labor induction, no specific regimen was recommended but suggested initial dosages below 50 μg (10 to 25 μg) to minimize the risk of hyperstimulation with FHR changes and cesareans. The review, which considered only one trial for time to delivery as an outcome, reported a not significantly shorter duration for low‐dose oral misoprostol compared to vaginal dinoprostone (mean difference − 3.79 h, 95% CI −5.35 to −2.23 34 ). Additional studies focused on the rate of delivery within 24 h, where after excluding possibly biased studies, vaginal dinoprostone exhibited a higher rate of delivery within 24 h (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.99). Another study by Wang et al. 35 (included in the Cochrane Review) reported a statistically significantly lower rate of deliveries within 12 h (p‐value 0.03, 21.4 vs. 40.1%) and a statistically significantly longer time to delivery for oral misoprostol compared with vaginal dinoprostone (p‐value 0.04, oral misoprostol 21.3 ± 14.5 h, dinoprostone 15.7 ± 9.6 h). It is worth noting that Wang et al. used a titrated solution, starting with doses of 20 μg increasing up to 60 μg, with progressively longer intervals of 1 to 2 h, totaling 220 μg administered over 10.5 h. In a subsequent study in 2021 36 with more participants (411 vs. 5819 participants), deliveries within 12 h remained statistically higher in the vaginal dinoprostone cohort (p‐value 0.007, 25 vs. 28.7%). However, the difference between times to delivery was no longer significant. In the larger study, the misoprostol titration protocol was slightly adapted with a reduction of the maximal dose to 50 μg and applying of the same total dosage of 220 μg in a shorter duration of 6.5 h. Similar to our findings, the Cochrane Review showed a reduced risk of cesareans and hyperstimulation, although with FHR changes, for oral misoprostol compared to vaginal dinoprostone. However, they found little to no difference in the risk for meconium‐stained liquor or blood loss. 12

CONCLUSION

Our study suggests that oral misoprostol 25 μg administered every 4 h results in a longer time to delivery in labor induction compared to 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone inserts. No clear preference is established between 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone inserts and oral misoprostol 25 μg given every 2 h. However, oral misoprostol 25 μg given every 2 or 4 h results in fewer cases of meconium‐stained liquor and cesarean sections compared to 10 mg vaginal dinoprostone inserts. Additionally, administering misoprostol 25 μg every 2 h is associated with fewer cases of uterine hyperstimulation without concomitant FHR changes. More data are necessary to confirm our findings and to further investigate individualized recommendations for the induction regimen depending on the patient's characteristics. Our data did not find a significant difference between both oral misoprostol regimens, and there is a need for more clinical head‐to‐head studies and randomized trials for identifying the optimal regimen for misoprostol administration for labor induction. These findings contribute to the ongoing discussion on labor induction methods, and we hope they will inform clinical practice and improve outcomes for pregnant individuals and their infants.
