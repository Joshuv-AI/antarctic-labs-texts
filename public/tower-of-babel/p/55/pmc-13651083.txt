A physiology-guided ketofol-based anaesthetic approach for emergency burr hole craniotomy in a child with Eisenmenger syndrome: a case report
Authors: Saad Ahmed Naved, Samiah Ijaz, Bushra Maryum Zahid, Muhammad Ashraf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEisenmenger syndrome presents substantial perioperative risk because small perturbations in systemic or pulmonary vascular resistance may precipitate cardiovascular collapse. Evidence guiding anaesthetic management during emergency neurosurgery in paediatric patients remains limited.Case presentationWe report an 11-year-old boy with Eisenmenger syndrome and severe pulmonary hypertension who underwent emergency burr hole craniotomy for drainage of a cerebral abscess. Perioperative management was guided by physiology–driven strategy encompassing invasive haemodynamic monitoring, preserving systemic vascular resistance, limiting pulmonary vascular resistance, and avoiding hypoxia and hypercarbia using vasoactive support and lung-protective ventilation. General anaesthesia was maintained using a self-prepared 1:4 ketamine–propofol (ketofol) infusion titrated to a bispectral index of 45–60, incorporated as one component of this approach. The intraoperative course remained stable without major desaturation or haemodynamic instability. The patient was extubated at the end of surgery, vasoactive support was subsequently weaned, and he was discharged with favourable neurological and clinical outcomes.ConclusionThis case illustrates that ketofol-based general anaesthesia can be incorporated into a physiology-driven perioperative strategy for emergency neurosurgery in selected patients with Eisenmenger syndrome. Introduction

Eisenmenger syndrome is the most advanced form of pulmonary arterial hypertension associated with congenital heart disease and is characterized by irreversible elevation of pulmonary vascular resistance, leading to right-to-left shunting [1, 2]. These patients pose a significant perioperative challenge because of their fragile hemodynamic balance, where even minor reductions in systemic vascular resistance or increases in pulmonary vascular resistance can precipitate life-threatening hypoxemia and cardiovascular collapse [1, 3]. Therefore, anaesthetic management in such cases is highly complex and requires meticulous physiological optimization of the patient.

Neurosurgical procedures under general anaesthesia in patients with Eisenmenger syndrome are particularly challenging, and the literature describing optimal anaesthetic strategies in this setting is limited [4]. Conventional approaches have focused on techniques aimed at preserving systemic vascular resistance while avoiding myocardial depression and pulmonary vasoconstriction [4]. However, there is no consensus on the ideal anaesthetic regimen [5].

The ketamine–propofol combination (ketofol) has been increasingly utilized for procedural sedation and anaesthesia because of its favourable hemodynamic profile, which combines the sympathetic stimulation of ketamine with the titratable and hypnotic effects of propofol [6]. Although ketofol has been reported in patients with congenital heart disease, evidence of its use in Eisenmenger syndrome, particularly in the context of neurosurgical procedures, is limited [7].

This case is notable because it describes a comprehensive physiology-guided perioperative strategy for emergency burr-hole craniotomy in a paediatric patient with Eisenmenger syndrome, within which a ketofol infusion was incorporated as a part of anaesthetic approach, a clinical scenario for which published evidence is extremely limited. Although previous reports have described a variety of anaesthetic approaches in patients with Eisenmenger physiology, practical guidance for emergency paediatric neurosurgery remains lacking. Rather than proposing ketofol as a superior drug regime, this report illustrates how a ketofol infusion can be integrated into a physiology-guided perioperative strategy aimed at preserving the SVR–PVR balance while simultaneously addressing neurosurgical priorities. The clinical value of this case lies in providing a pragmatic framework for decision-making in similarly complex and resource-constrained settings.

Case presentation

An 11-year-old male (weight 24 kg) with a known history of complex congenital heart disease (large ventricular septal defect and atrial septal defect) complicated by Eisenmenger syndrome and severe pulmonary hypertension was admitted with a 5-day history of right-sided weakness and intermittent fever. He had a history of cardiac evaluation in infancy and a previous admission to the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) for septic shock. His long-term medications included bosentan (62.5 mg OD), sildenafil (20 mg BD), and spironolactone (10 mg BD). On admission, the patient appeared ill, dehydrated, and drowsy, with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 12–13/15. Vital signs revealed a heart rate 102/min, blood pressure of 102/62 mmHg, respiratory rate 26/min, and oxygen saturation of approximately 82% on room air. Physical examination revealed grade IV digital clubbing, micrognathia, and right hemiparesis. Cardiovascular examination revealed normal heart sounds, and respiratory examination revealed clear lung fields bilaterally. The combination of cyanotic heart disease and focal neurological deficits raised the suspicion of a brain abscess. Following initial evaluation in the emergency department, the patient was shifted to the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) for further management and diagnostic workup.

CT brain demonstrated a left basal ganglia infarct with evolving abscess formation, later confirmed on MRI. Echocardiography revealed large unrestrictive ASD and VSD with predominantly right-to-left shunting, severe pulmonary hypertension (estimated RVSP ~ 75 mmHg), and mild biventricular dysfunction. Pulmonary-to-systemic vascular resistance ratio was not formally calculated owing to the absence of cardiac catheterisation data; however, the echocardiographic and clinical findings were consistent with established Eisenmenger physiology. Preoperative optimisation included continuation of bosentan and sildenafil, targeted oxygen supplementation, cautious fluid administration, correction of electrolyte abnormalities, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and multidisciplinary planning involving paediatric cardiology, intensive care, neurosurgery, and anaesthesia (Fig. 1).

Given the requirement for definitive imaging due to persistent neurological symptoms, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed, which demonstrated findings consistent with a cerebral abscess (Fig. 2).

Axial T2-weighted magnetic resonance image showing a hyperintense space-occupying lesion with perilesional edema, consistent with a cerebral abscess, a known complication of right-to-left shunting in Eisenmenger syndrome

For the MRI brain, the patient underwent elective endotracheal intubation in the PICU using midazolam (1 mg), ketamine (20 mg), fentanyl (50 µg), and atracurium (10 mg). In view of pre–induction bradycardia (60 bpm), Atropine (300mcg) was administered. Anaesthesia was maintained with ketamine-based sedation during MRI, and hemodynamic stability was preserved with intermittent boluses of phenylephrine. Following imaging, the patient was transferred to the operating room for urgent burr-hole craniotomy and drainage of the brain abscess.

In the operating room, standard monitoring was supplemented with capnography, nasopharyngeal temperature, BIS monitoring, invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring, and ultrasound-guided right internal jugular central venous access. Anaesthesia was maintained using a self-prepared 1:4 ketamine–propofol (ketofol) infusion (ketamine 2 mg/mL; propofol 8 mg/mL), as commercially prepared ketofol is not available in Pakistan. The infusion was titrated at 4–10 mL/h to maintain a BIS of 45–60 over approximately 3 h, delivering total doses of 96–240 mg of propofol and 24–60 mg of ketamine. Neuromuscular blockade was maintained with atracurium, while multimodal analgesia comprised fentanyl, paracetamol, and local lidocaine–epinephrine infiltration. Magnesium sulphate (50 mg/kg) was administered as an adjunct for its potential neuroprotective effects. Haemodynamic management focused on preserving SVR and limiting PVR using judicious fluid administration, low-dose noradrenaline, and milrinone. Lung-protective pressure-controlled ventilation with low tidal volumes (6–8 mL/kg) and PEEP of 3–5 cmH₂O was employed to avoid hypoxia, hypercarbia, and excessive airway pressures. Serial arterial blood gas analyses remained satisfactory throughout the procedure, without significant acidosis or lactic elevation.

Neuronavigation-guided burr hole drainage of the left basal ganglia abscess yielded approximately 10 mL of purulent material for microbiological analysis. The procedure was completed uneventfully with minimal blood loss, and intraoperative physiological stability was maintained throughout the procedure (Fig. 3).

The patient was extubated uneventfully at the end of surgery and transferred to the PACU with stable haemodynamic and oxygen saturation near baseline values. Low-dose noradrenaline and milrinone were continued and successfully weaned within 24 h in the PICU without episodes of desaturation or haemodynamic instability. Cultures grew Streptococcus species, while blood and CSF cultures remained negative. Neurological status improved to a GCS score of 15/15, and follow-up imaging demonstrated reduced cerebral oedema without midline shift or herniation. The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 5 with antimicrobial therapy and continuation of baseline cardiac medications.

Discussion and conclusion

Neurosurgical interventions in patients with Eisenmenger syndrome have rarely been reported [1], and existing evidence is limited to isolated case reports employing a variety of anaesthetic techniques, including awake craniotomy, monitored anaesthesia care, and general anaesthesia with etomidate-based induction [4, 8, 9]. Despite this heterogeneity, meticulous physiological optimisation remains a common principle, with emphasis on maintaining SVR, limiting increases in PVR, and ensuring adequate oxygenation [2]. However, there is no consensus regarding the optimal anaesthetic technique, particularly for paediatric patients undergoing emergency neurosurgical procedures [1].

Our case illustrates the practical challenges of applying these principles during emergency neurosurgery in a child with established Eisenmenger physiology in a resource-limited setting. Beyond preserving cardiopulmonary stability, the anaesthetic strategy also had to accommodate neurosurgical priorities, including adequate brain relaxation, avoidance of further intracranial hypertension, and facilitation of early postoperative neurological assessment using available resources. Ketofol combines the analgesic and sympathomimetic properties of ketamine with the titratable hypnotic effects of propofol which, in procedural sedation and catheterization settings, has been reported to allow lower doses of each agent and reduced haemodynamic perturbation compared with propofol alone [6]; however, this evidence does not extend to major surgery or Eisenmenger syndrome specifically. Although propofol–fentanyl with scalp block is a well-established neurosurgical technique, ketofol was favoured because ketamine may mitigate propofol-induced vasodilation and myocardial depression while providing effective analgesia and an opioid-sparing effect. The patient’s depressed neurological status, the need for airway control and immobility, and the unavailability of remifentanil limited the applicability of alternative techniques in this specific clinical context. BIS monitoring facilitated titration and minimised the risks of both under- and over-sedation. Nevertheless, several limitations of this report must be acknowledged. This is single case and, as such, cannot support generalizable conclusions. The absence of comparator group precludes any assessment of whether alternative techniques would have produced different outcomes. It is equally plausible that other agents employed with equivalent physiological vigilance could have achieved similar results. Favourable intraoperative conditions were achieved through a physiology-guided strategy incorporating ketofol, invasive monitoring, early vasoactive support, lung-protective ventilation, and meticulous avoidance of factors known to increase PVR. Together, these measures facilitated haemodynamic stability and preservation of end-organ perfusion.

This case illustrates that successful perioperative management of Eisenmenger syndrome depends primarily on adherence to physiological targets—preserving SVR, limiting PVR, maintaining adequate oxygenation, and avoiding precipitants of right ventricular decompensation—rather than the choice of a specific anaesthetic agent. The favourable outcome likely reflected the combined effects of invasive monitoring, vasoactive support, lung-protective ventilation, and multidisciplinary care. Ketofol may represent a pragmatic option within such a physiology-guided strategy, particularly when its haemodynamic profile is considered advantageous; however, conclusions regarding its superiority over alternative agents cannot be drawn from a single case.
