Neuroendocrine transformation of prostate adenocarcinoma with liver and brain metastases: case report and literature review
Authors: Luiz Carlos de Araújo Souza, Fernando Victor do Carmo, João Paulo Majella de Godoy Morais, Herbeth Franco Queiroz, Fábio Henrique Mendonça de Oliveira, Pedro Simões Daher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTreatment-emergent neuroendocrine prostate cancer (t-NEPC) is a rare and aggressive variant associated with resistance to androgen deprivation therapy and visceral metastases. We report a case of neuroendocrine transformation of metastatic prostate adenocarcinoma with liver and brain involvement. Diagnosis was confirmed by liver biopsy demonstrating neuroendocrine differentiation and a Ki-67 index of 95%. A literature review identified seven similar cases. Platinum-based chemotherapy was the main treatment, with limited survival. Neuroendocrine transformation should be suspected in patients with rapid visceral progression despite low PSA levels. Metastatic-site biopsy remains essential for accurate diagnosis and treatment selection.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most prevalent malignant neoplasms in men worldwide, with acinar adenocarcinoma being its main histological subtype.1 In advanced stages, the disease presents heterogeneous clinical behavior, especially after systemic therapies. Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) constitutes the mainstay of treatment for metastatic prostate cancer; however, the selective pressure induced by this approach may favor the emergence of more aggressive tumor phenotypes independent of the androgen receptor pathway.2

In this context, neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC) emerges as a rare entity, which can occur de novo or, more frequently, as a treatment-associated transformation, called treatment-emergent neuroendocrine prostate cancer (t-NEPC). This variant is frequently associated with rapid progression, PSA levels that may be disproportionately low relative to tumor burden, and a higher incidence of visceral metastases.2,3

Given its rarity and aggressive clinical behavior, we report a case of advanced prostatic adenocarcinoma that evolved with neuroendocrine transformation and hepatic and cerebral metastases, highlighting its diagnostic aspects and therapeutic implications.

To contextualize the present case, a literature review was conducted using the PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. The search strategy combined the terms “prostate cancer”, “treatment-emergent neuroendocrine prostate cancer”, “small cell prostate cancer”, “liver metastasis”, and “case report”, using the Boolean operators AND OR. Only English-language case reports describing treatment-emergent neuroendocrine transformation of prostate adenocarcinoma with histologically confirmed liver metastases were included. Reviews, de novoneuroendocrine carcinomas, experimental studies, and reports without histological confirmation were excluded. An initial broad search identified 160 articles, followed by a focused search that yielded 14 studies. After title, abstract, and full-text screening, seven reports met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10. One study was excluded because it represented a new neuroendocrine carcinoma, not characterizing transformation of prostatic adenocarcinoma.11

Case presentation

A 61-year-old male patient, previously healthy, presented with initial symptoms of weight loss (5 kg in 3 months), asthenia, and diffuse abdominal pain. In the initial investigation, laboratory tests, serologies, PSA dosage (60.52 ng/mL), contrast-enhanced CT of the chest and abdomen, and pelvic MRI were performed (Fig. 1A and B). Given the suspicion of prostate cancer, he underwent a prostate biopsy, which revealed acinar adenocarcinoma of the prostate, Gleason 7 (4 + 3), ISUP 3. Staging was performed by bone scintigraphy, which showed multiple areas of uptake compatible with bone metastases, in addition to tomography demonstrating iliac lymphadenopathy measuring up to 2.0 cm.

A. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging in a sagittal section showing locally advanced prostate cancer with bladder invasion (red arrow).

B. Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging in an axial section showing locally advanced prostate cancer with bladder invasion (red arrow).

Due to the large tumor volume and the presence of metastatic disease, total bilateral orchiectomy was performed to achieve definitive androgen deprivation. At the time of diagnosis, GnRH antagonists and androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) had not yet been incorporated into our institutional treatment protocol. Therefore, surgical castration followed by docetaxel chemotherapy (75 mg/m2 every 21 days, for a total of six cycles) represented the standard treatment strategy for this patient. During follow-up, adequate androgen suppression was observed, with reduced total testosterone levels (15.9 ng/dL), associated with a significant biochemical response (PSA 0.11 ng/mL).

Despite the initial response, five months after the end of chemotherapy, the patient experienced rapid clinical progression, with the appearance of multiple hypovascular liver lesions, initially absent in previous examinations, and subsequently presenting multiple liver nodules of up to 1.1 cm in the subsequent tomography. Concomitantly, he maintained persistent bone and lymph node disease. It is noteworthy that this visceral progression occurred disproportionately to PSA levels, which remained low (<1 ng/mL), characterizing atypical behavior for classic prostatic adenocarcinoma and raising the hypothesis of neuroendocrine transformation.

A liver biopsy was then performed, which revealed metastatic neuroendocrine carcinoma, with a high proliferative index (Ki-67 of 95%) and expression of neuroendocrine markers, strongly suggestive of transformation of the previously diagnosed prostatic adenocarcinoma. Given this new histological profile, platinum-based chemotherapy was initiated, with cisplatin (75 mg/m2 on day 1) associated with irinotecan (60 mg/m2 on days 1 and 8), in 21-day cycles, totaling 6 cycles. This regimen was selected according to the institutional oncology protocol following multidisciplinary team discussion for high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma. The treatment was partially tolerated, with relevant hematological toxicity, including grade 3 neutropenia, as well as fatigue and asthenia. Treatment response was assessed using serial PSA measurements and conventional computed tomography, demonstrating a partial biochemical and radiographic response, characterized by a PSA nadir of 0.04 ng/mL, regression of the primary prostatic lesion, pelvic lymphadenopathy, and hepatic metastases. Subsequently, brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed disease progression in the central nervous system.

After approximately 9 months, the patient presented with episodes of transient loss of consciousness, initially interpreted as syncope. Investigation with cranial magnetic resonance imaging revealed multiple solid and cystic nodular lesions with contrast enhancement, consistent with metastatic implants in the central nervous system (Fig. 2A and B). There was no confirmation of seizures during the course of the disease. Given the involvement of the central nervous system, palliative cranial radiotherapy (30 Gy in 10 fractions) was indicated.

A. Brain MRI demonstrating multiple enhancing metastatic lesions involving the cerebral hemispheres (red arrow).

B. MRI of the cerebellum demonstrating multiple metastatic lesions with enhancement involving the cerebellar hemispheres (red arrow).

The patient remains in good functional status (ECOG 0), under joint follow-up with urology, clinical oncology and palliative care, with an approach focused on symptomatic control and quality of life, remaining alive 20 months after diagnosis.

Discussion

The neuroendocrine transformation of prostate adenocarcinoma, known as treatment-emergent neuroendocrine prostate cancer (t-NEPC), represents a tumor escape mechanism associated with the selective pressure of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT). This process involves loss of dependence on the androgen receptor pathway and acquisition of a highly proliferative and aggressive phenotype, often associated with genomic alterations involving TP53 and RB1 loss.12 Although t-NEPC has been increasingly recognized after exposure to potent androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs), neuroendocrine transdifferentiation may also occur following conventional androgen deprivation alone. Chronic suppression of androgen signaling itself may promote lineage plasticity and the emergence of androgen-independent neuroendocrine clones, making this transformation biologically plausible even in the absence of ARPI exposure.2,12 From a clinical point of view, t-NEPC is characterized by aggressive clinical behavior and rapid progression, often disproportionate to PSA levels. This behavior has been observed both in the literature and in the present case, in which there was significant visceral progression despite PSA levels that remained disproportionately low and adequate androgen suppression, evidencing tumor independence from the hormonal pathway.3,12

Our literature review identified seven published cases of neuroendocrine transformation of prostate adenocarcinoma with hepatic metastases4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10. Patients were between 60 and 70 years old, with heterogeneous clinical presentations and predominantly high-risk disease at diagnosis (Table 1). Neuroendocrine differentiation was most commonly confirmed through biopsy of metastatic sites, particularly the liver and lymph nodes.

Another striking aspect is the pattern of dissemination. Unlike classic prostate adenocarcinoma, t-NEPC is more frequently associated with visceral metastases. Because our literature review specifically included cases with liver metastases, the observed frequency of hepatic involvement reflects the study selection criteria rather than the natural distribution of metastatic sites. Nevertheless, liver involvement was a prominent feature in both the present case and in the selected reports2,3 (Table 2).

The diagnosis of t-NEPC remains challenging and generally requires biopsy of metastatic sites in scenarios of atypical progression. In the included studies, as well as in the present case, confirmation was performed by immunohistochemistry, with expression of neuroendocrine markers and high proliferative indices. The elevated Ki-67, frequently above 80%, as observed in our patient (95%), reinforces the highly aggressive nature of this entity2,12 (Table 3). In the present case, the loss of expression of prostatic lineage markers (PSA and NKX3.1), associated with strong positivity for neuroendocrine markers and a high proliferative index (Ki-67 of 95%), stands out, findings that suggest a phenotype of complete transdifferentiation, and not just focal neuroendocrine differentiation.

Regarding treatment, there is no established consensus, with platinum-based regimens being widely used, by analogy to small cell carcinoma. In the cases analyzed, these regimens demonstrated partial and short-lived responses, a pattern also observed in the present report, in which there was an initial response followed by progression2,13 (Table 2).

The survival reported in the literature for t-NEPC is limited, ranging from 12 to 20 months in cases with available follow-up (Table 2). Our patient has remained alive for more than 20 months since diagnosis, positioning himself at the upper limit of this interval, despite the presence of extensive visceral disease, highlighting the clinical heterogeneity of this rare entity3,12 (Table 2).

This case stands out due to the association of early visceral progression, loss of expression of prostate markers, and involvement of the central nervous system, configuring an aggressive t-NEPC phenotype. The concomitant presence of liver and brain metastases remains rarely described in the literature, reinforcing the relevance of this report. Molecular genetic testing was not performed in the present case. Therefore, genomic alterations commonly associated with treatment-emergent neuroendocrine prostate cancer could not be assessed.

Conclusions

Neuroendocrine transformation of prostate adenocarcinoma is a rare and highly aggressive entity, characterized by clinical progression disproportionate to PSA levels and a greater propensity for visceral metastases, especially hepatic metastases, differing from the classic pattern of the disease. Suspicion should be considered in patients with rapid progression, even under adequate androgen suppression, and diagnostic confirmation by biopsy of metastatic sites is essential.

Despite the use of platinum-based chemotherapy, the prognosis remains guarded, particularly in the presence of extensive visceral and central nervous system involvement, as observed in the present case.
