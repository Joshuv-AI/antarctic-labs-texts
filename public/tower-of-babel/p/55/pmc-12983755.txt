Barriers, facilitators and unmet needs for achieving a good death and enhancing end-of-life care in the Catalan Health System: a qualitative study
Authors: Vicky Serra-Sutton, Luisa Fernández-Giner, Mónica Caldeiro, Anna García-Altès, Mireia Espallargues
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackground and objectiveA qualitative study was conducted in Catalonia (Spain), incorporating the views and opinions of relatives, healthcare professionals and patients on what they considered a ‘good death’. This study aimed to describe barriers, facilitators and unmet needs related to the achievement of a good death.MethodsWe recruited adult patients with advanced or chronic conditions, relatives and health and social care professionals involved in end-of-life processes of care, management or strategic planning. All participants took part in a qualitative study. The study was informed by phenomenological, hermeneutical and social constructivist perspectives and included 23 in-depth interviews and three focus group discussions with a total of 31 participants. Fieldwork was conducted between February and April 2022. Data were transcribed and analysed using qualitative thematic content and discourse analysis.ResultsSix main themes were identified, comprising 17 subthemes. Facilitators and barriers related to achieving ‘a good death’ were categorised according to whether they occurred before death or during the dying process. Key facilitators include high-quality palliative care, open communication about death and the ability to choose the place of death. Key barriers included bureaucratic delays, inadequate resources, insufficient professional training and lack of respect for patients’ preferences and wishes.ConclusionOur study highlights the need to understand factors that facilitate or hinder the achievement of a good death and the quality of the dying process. Specifically, understanding individual preferences and unmet needs, enhancing communication, increasing awareness, reducing bureaucratic barriers and ensuring adequate resources are essential to support a more dignified end-of-life experience for patients, caregivers and healthcare professionals.Keywords: PALLIATIVE CARE, Quality of Life, HEALTH SERVICES ADMINISTRATION & MANAGEMENT, Quality in health care, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Introduction

Context of the current qualitative study

Over the past two decades, awareness of end-of-life care and patients’ rights in the final stages of life has increased significantly.1 This has led to a growing body of research examining the impact of advanced care planning during this critical phase.1 2 Understanding quality standards for end-of-life care is essential for identifying improvements and addressing unmet needs.

Several studies have identified common and widely shared components that contribute to the perception of a ‘good death’.3 Although this is a highly subjective concept, core elements consistently include pain relief and symptom control, access to high-quality professional care, a sense of fulfilment in life and peace of mind in decision-making in accordance with preferences and wishes of people in their end of life.3,6

Additionally, studies included in a systematic review published by Krikorian et al7 suggest that individual circumstances play a key role in shaping patients’ perceptions of a good death. These circumstances include age, gender, cultural background, socio-economic status, geographical context (rural or urban), quality of life and health status—particularly in relation to disease stage and health interventions at end-of-life (eg, their impacts on physical limitations).

Moreover, there is an increasing recognition of the need to incorporate patient-centred approaches into end-of-life care. However, much of the existing literature has primarily focused on the perspectives of relatives and healthcare professionals. As a result, the insights and lived experiences of patients themselves toward the dying process have often been under-represented.8

In Catalonia, in north-eastern Spain, as in other regions across Europe and in line with recommendations from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), standards of end-of-life care require further development and improvement.9 In fact, improving end-of-life care requires addressing systemic barriers including uneven access to palliative care resources, limited implementation of advance care planning (ACP) and insufficient integration between health and social care services.10 11 As populations age and the complexity of end-of-life needs increases, aligning clinical practice and health policy with patients’ values and preferences becomes an ethical imperative. There is a need to redesign care models to better support the achievement of a good death and receive high-quality care during the dying process in accordance with these preferences and wishes.

In Catalonia, end-of-life care is mainly delivered through PADES (the Catalan acronym for home care programme and support teams), a programme operating across primary care, acute hospital care and intermediate care, with involvement from social services within the health and social care systems. Despite efforts by the PADES programme to improve end-of-life care and the availability of specific clinical decision-making tools aligned with the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines,12 13 there remains scope for further improvement.

In this context, the Death Observatory of Catalonia was created14 with the following objectives: (1) recording and providing data on the quality of end-of-life care; (2) raising public awareness about where, with whom, how and where people prefer to die; (3) compiling data to draw conclusions and propose policies to improve the dying process and (4) promoting breaking social taboos surrounding death. Achieving these challenging objectives requires the systematic measurement of individuals’ preferences. This can be accomplished using both quantitative and qualitative data to strengthen knowledge and understanding of what needs to be considered for health planning.

Purpose and goals

A qualitative study was conducted in 2022 to explore perceptions of a good death in Catalonia as part of the Death Observatory in Catalonia.14 15 This study had several objectives and contents (figure 1). Our initial publication considered what participants agreed on about a ‘good death’ aligned with the definition proposed by the Institute of Medicine.16 Common elements of a good death identified across patients, relatives and health and social professionals included15: comfort, placidity, safety, warmth, harmony, intimacy, respect, peacefulness and fulfilment.

Summary of aims, phases, qualitative techniques and selected and participating patients, relatives and healthcare professionals.

To better understand the factors that enable a good death, we further examined facilitators, barriers and unmet needs. This study was designed to inform decision-making across clinical practice, care delivery, service management, strategic planning and health policy development tools.

This paper presents findings from the second part of our qualitative study. This study aimed to explore key facilitators, barriers and unmet needs within the health system related to achieving a good death. The findings are based on participants’ opinions, views and experiences, including those of patients, relatives and health professionals in Catalonia.

Methods

Qualitative design and research approach

We conducted a qualitative study using multiple data collection techniques and theoretical–methodological approaches. Additionally, the study incorporated a diverse range of participant profiles and analytical strategies to enable an in-depth understanding of how a good death is conceptualised and to identify areas for improvement within the health and social care systems. This approach was also intended to enhance the robustness and validity of the study’s findings. A more detailed description of the methods is provided in the online supplemental materials 1, as referenced throughout the text and in our previous publication.15

Given the anticipated subjectivity and complexity of the topic, we adopted three complementary qualitative theoretical and methodological approaches: (1) first, a phenomenological perspective17 18 was used to understand participants’ subjective understanding of a good death shaped by learning and socialisation processes, as well as perceived barriers and facilitators to achieving it, together with unmet needs within the healthcare system (interviews and discussion focus groups); (2) second, a hermeneutical approach18 19 was employed to interpret underlying and non-explicit meanings in participants’ views, opinions and experiences, considering their social, cultural and historical (life-course) contexts (at the beginning of each interview and discussion focus group using vivid pictures); (3) and third, a socio-constructivist perspective17 18 was applied to explore how collective meanings around death and end-of-life care are generated, how individuals make sense of their reality and how these understandings may evolve through access to information and empowerment (focus group discussion techniques).20

Context and units of study

Patients and relatives were recruited from public and publicly funded private healthcare facilities within the Catalan public health network, as well as from patient, caregiver and civil society associations. Health and social care professionals were selected from public, publicly funded private healthcare facilities and private healthcare facilities. The Catalan Health System provides universal coverage to residents and is administratively organised into nine health regions: Alt Pirineu-Aran; Barcelona (including three regions: Barcelona City, Barcelona Metropolitan North and Barcelona Metropolitan South); Catalunya Central; Girona; Lleida; Penedès; and Terres de l’Ebre.

Ethical considerations

Our study was conducted in accordance with established principles of good research practice. All participants received a participant information sheet and provided written informed consent before participating in the study.

For patients and caregivers (Phase 1), with participants’ permission, basic contact details were shared with the research team for recruitment purposes. Before each interview, participants provided consent for audio recording for analytical purposes. In Phase 2, professionals within the health system and representing patient and civil society organisations were contacted directly by Agency for Health Quality and Assessment of Catalonia (AQuAS) and provided written informed consent before each focus group discussion, including consent for audio recording.

Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout the study. Data were managed in accordance with the data protection and security policies of the Catalan Government and AQuAS, in compliance with the EU General Data Protection Regulation (EU GDPR 2016/679) and the Spanish Data Protection Law (LOPD). All audio recordings and transcripts were stored in secure, access-restricted folders. Transcriptions were fully anonymised to remove any potentially identifying information, thereby protecting participants’ privacy.

Data collection and sampling methods

The study was conducted in two phases. Participants were selected and recruited from December 2021 to March 2022, and data collection took place from February to April 2022. Participant selection, recruitment and fieldwork were coordinated by the AQuAS. Potential participants were identified through patient and civil society associations and healthcare providers collaborating with the steering group, the Consultative Council of Patients of Catalonia and the Catalan Health Institute, as well as through scientific societies and previous literature reviews; further procedural and sampling details are provided in the supplementary material (see online supplemental files 1–3).

Data collection methods (figure 1) included: (1) semistructured in-depth interviews, and (2) focus group discussions. Participants in Phase 1 and Phase 2 were independent and included distinct profiles.

Phase 1: collecting opinions, views and experiences from patients and caregivers.

Phase 1: collecting opinions, views and experiences from patients and caregivers.

We conducted semistructured in-depth interviews with patients and relatives. Interviews were conducted either in participants’ homes or in the healthcare facilities where they were institutionalised for health reasons, including intermediate or long-term care centres. Participants were categorised into four groups based on patients’ health status and stage in the dying process following the theoretical sampling (see online supplemental file 1). Designated reference health professionals within each organisation informed eligible patients and relatives about the study and invited them to participate. Participants who met the inclusion criteria and provided the above-mentioned written informed consent were contacted by the research team to arrange the face-to-face in-depth interviews (see more details in online supplemental file 2).

Phase 2: collecting opinions, views and experiences from multidisciplinary professional profiles working in the healthcare system and patient associations.

Phase 2: collecting opinions, views and experiences from multidisciplinary professional profiles working in the healthcare system and patient associations.

Three virtual focus group discussions were conducted after phase 1 (following the completion of interviews with patients and relatives). Each group included 10–12 participants. Participants were grouped into four categories according to their professional profiles following a theoretical sampling (see online supplemental file 3).

The focus group discussions included healthcare and social care professionals involved in the management of chronic conditions and end-of-life care, professionals engaged in clinical management, strategic planning and professional representatives from patient and civil society organisations.18 19 Participants were drawn from both urban and rural areas and different health regions across Catalonia. Professionals were invited by AQuAS via email to participate. Following informed consent, they were contacted to arrange their participation in the online focus group discussions (see online supplemental file 2).

Some unbalances in the territorial and provider representation between theoretical sampling and recruitment of patients and caregivers in Phase 1 (see online supplemental file 1 and online supplemental file 3) were addressed through collaboration with the steering group of the Death Observatory of Catalonia. They gave support in the recruitment of key informants with a professional profile from those under-represented areas for participation in the focus group discussions (Phase 2; eg, from more rural areas or additional healthcare providers within the regions of the Catalan Health System).

Data collection and technologies

Each interview (Phase 1) lasted between 90 and 120 min and was audiotaped. Interview topics were tailored to the type of informant (patient or relative); please find in online supplemental file 4 the applied interview guidelines. For the focus group discussions (Phase 2), each session lasted between 150 and 180 min and followed predefined guidelines agreed on by the research team (see online supplemental file 5).

The first part of each focus group discussion included selecting a picture of nature to describe what a good death meant for each participant, serving as an icebreaker.20 Then, we presented the main insights and themes from Phase 1 and discussed them in each group to explore facilitators, barriers and unmet needs related to achieving a good death before, near and during the dying process. Finally, participants suggested actions and interventions aimed at improving the quality of end-of-life care.

The focus group discussions were conducted through the AQuAS corporate Zoom platform, and all the sessions were recorded to transcribe them for further qualitative analysis after receiving the participants’ written consent.

Data processing and analysis

All interviews and focus group discussions were audio recorded and transcribed for qualitative analysis.14 15 Data were coded and classified according to main guidelines and emerging themes. A vertical (within-case) reading of each interview was conducted to identify categories, followed by a horizontal (across-case) reading to detect repetitive patterns as well as less frequent but analytically relevant responses.

A qualitative researcher (LF-G) carried out an inductive thematic content and discourse analysis. Categories, subcategories and key findings were reviewed and validated by a second researcher (VS-S), the study coordinator.914,16 The thematic content and discourse analysis were carried out at the level of specific sample segments and globally for each relevant theme.15 21 During the analytical process, participants’ profiles and backgrounds were considered to deepen the interpretation of meanings and social constructions related to the phenomenon under study.

Preliminary findings from Phase 1 were first discussed within the core research team and subsequently with the steering committee. Key insights were then briefly summarised and discussed with participants during Phase 2 focus group discussions. Main findings from the semistructured, in-depth interviews (Phase 1) were triangulated with narratives emerging from the focus group discussions (Phase 2) and the integrated results were further reviewed by the core research team. Subsequently, the findings were presented to and discussed with the steering group to confirm coherence and analytical robustness.

Analysis of participants’ accounts showed that thematic saturation had been achieved, particularly regarding facilitators and barriers to achieving an ideal good death. No new themes or subthemes emerged as data collection and formal thematic and discourse analyses progressed, suggesting sufficient depth and coverage of the topic. Finally, illustrative quotations were selected, accompanied by brief information on participants’ profiles to provide contextual detail.

Patient and public involvement

Patients’ and caregivers’ representatives were actively involved from the beginning of the project (including discussion of the research protocol and interview guidelines) through to the discussion of findings and their dissemination. Their participation was undertaken through the following associations and councils: (1) the Consultative Council of Patients of Catalonia; (2) the Spanish Association Against Cancer (AECC); (3) the Miquel Valls Foundation (Catalan Foundation for Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis); and (4) the Catalan Association for the Right to Die with Dignity.

These associations, together with health providers and other patient and civil society organisations, collaborated in the recruitment of patients and relatives and in dissemination activities. Additional organisations involved included: (5) the Catalan Association of Patients with Advanced Respiratory Disease and Lung Transplantation (AIRE); (6) the Catalan Association for Parkinson’s disease; and (7) the Kreamics Association of Burn Victims of Catalonia, Family Members and Friends.

Preliminary findings were shared with these organisations, as well as with participants involved in the interviews and focus group discussions.

Techniques to enhance trustworthiness and study reflexivity

As mentioned, data triangulation was employed across multiple dimensions, including participants’ profiles in Phases 1 and 2, research techniques and theoretical–methodological approaches. These approaches incorporated both individual and social perspectives to deepen the interpretation of participants’ experiences and viewpoints. Online supplemental file 6 includes more details of steps to enhance trustworthiness, including strategies to reinforce credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability. The criteria to answer the quality checklist Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research are described in online supplemental file 7.

The study was undertaken by a multidisciplinary research team with expertise in sociology, psychology, health economics, preventive medicine, health service evaluation, qualitative methodology and scientific communication. This diversity of disciplinary perspectives informed the study design, development of interview guides, data collection, analysis, interpretation and discussion of findings, thereby strengthening reflexivity throughout the research process. In addition, an external steering group comprising healthcare professionals, management, health planning representatives and patient and civil society organisations was actively involved in all stages of the study. This included sampling design, data collection, interpretation and discussion of results, as well as dissemination activities. This governance structure helped challenge potential assumptions and ensured a plurality of perspectives. The study was conducted within the framework of a knowledge transfer project of the Observatory of Death of Catalonia, coordinated by AQuAS. In online supplemental file 8, more detail of reflexivity and the study context is described.

Preliminary insights were discussed after each interview and focus group discussion by two members of the research team (LF-G and VS-S) and subsequently reviewed with the full core research team and the steering group of the Good Death Study and the Death Observatory of Catalonia. The narratives of participants and verbatims/quotes supporting the main insights of the study are freely available on the website of AQuAS, a public governmental institution dedicated to evaluating the quality of healthcare and development of decision-making tools/knowledge transfer (dataset).22

Facilitators and barriers within the system for achieving a good death

From the perspective of patients, relatives and healthcare professionals, the identified facilitators and barriers are divided into two stages: those arising before death (end-of-life period) and those occurring during the dying process and at the moment of death (figure 2).

End-of-life period (before death).

End-of-life period (before death).

Facilitators identified during the end-of-life period were further subclassified into three main categories: (1) the ability to maintain an acceptable quality of life during the end-of-life period; (2) the ability to close one’s life issues and say goodbye to loved ones; and (3) the ability to retain a sense of control over one’s life and the dying process (see figure 2 and online supplemental file 9 for illustrative quotations).

Related to the first theme, ‘feeling that they can enjoy life in the end-of-life period’, health-related quality of life emerged as a key factor influencing individuals’ sense of well-being and life enjoyment due to pain, impairments and limitations in daily living, alongside other adverse health-related impacts.

I’m pleased that you want to understand what a good death means for people through this study—what helps and what limits it—but this is meaningless unless we first ensure that people have the means to live a good life (before death) […]. (Patient with complex chronic conditions, female, 65–79 years old)

Patients perceived the availability of economic resources, social support and time for them as either facilitators or barriers, emphasising the importance of access to professional care and other resources such as home-based support.

First, they should simplify the bureaucracy because it makes most things difficult… For everything, because there are loads of bureaucracy for everything. […] And once you’re dead… it’s so unfair how much it costs to bury the dead. It’s unfair how expensive it is! (Patient with complex chronic conditions, male, 65–79 years old)

Within the second theme, ‘closing one’s life and being able to say goodbye to loved ones’, several related facilitators or subthemes were identified: being at peace with oneself and with loved ones, being able to manage unresolved personal matters and having the opportunity to say goodbye to loved ones. It is important to point out that, at this stage, support from different professionals such as psychologists, social workers, attorneys and public notaries may be required.

We die as we have lived. This implies that if family and intimate relationships have been previously built with quality, saying goodbye and receiving support in that moment becomes easier. (Clinical psychologist)

Being at peace… It could also mean dying while having everything arranged, having talked to everyone, with many people, having said goodbye, having been able to see the last grandchild who was born. (Patient with complex chronic conditions, female, 65–79 years old)

In life, you need to leave your imprint, leave good friends, good family relationships and all this, and being remembered. (Patient without advanced or complex chronic conditions, female, 65–79 years old)

The third main theme, ‘feeling in control of one’s life and the dying process’, highlights either a facilitator or barrier perspective towards achieving a good death for participants. Key aspects related to this theme included the ability to talk openly about death, having available information about one’s rights and challenges in managing key processes and legal or administrative documentation. In this context, establishing personal ‘red lines’ and patients’ attitudinal profiles at the dying process was considered relevant (see online supplemental file 10). Both aspects are key, as they can interfere with the perception of achieving a good death and with the need to maintain control over one’s life and dying process. In this sense, attitudinal profiles ranged from individuals who preferred not to discuss or plan for death to those who were highly engaged in planning and, in some cases, actively involved in movements advocating for dying with dignity.

The concept of ‘red line’ represents the perceived loss of quality of life or dignity— within the context of a limiting and irreversible health condition—that feels ‘unnecessary, cruel, or humiliating and unbearable’ for the patient. It must be stressed that the patients’ attitudinal profiles imply different ways of facing death and, thus, a more planned or unplanned end of life. This has consequences on the family and professional caregiving environment, who need to adapt to the patient.

Accepting death, the ability to bring closure to one’s life, saying goodbye to the loved ones, drawing on spiritual beliefs and feeling that one leaves a good legacy were described as sources of peace and fulfilment, particularly for those patients who need to have more control of their dying process. By contrast, lengthy approval procedures for euthanasia were perceived as a barrier, as some patients died before their request was granted (figure 2).

Regarding facilitators and barriers identified by patients, relatives and professionals that are activated during the process of dying and near the moment of death, there were three main ones that emerged from participants’ perspectives (figure 2 and online supplemental file 11). Key facilitators related to these vital moments include: (1) receiving continuous care and clinical follow-up to ensure the absence of physical and psychological pain; (2) receiving emotional support; and (3) being able to choose the place of death. Many patients and their relatives did not initially consider dying in an emergency department as an option; consequently, healthcare professionals played an important role in providing guidance and information regarding available alternatives.

I want to be alone, with efficient and effective company of healthcare professionals that will give me something that makes the process go faster, and with as little pain as possible. (Patient with chronic complex conditions, female, 65–79 years old)

I think that if we talk about a good death, we don’t typically picture emergency units, but they are doing an amazing job and are providing essential responses. […] I think it’s a great facilitator that, in case of admission, this admission is fast, and if it needs to be in a Palliative Care Unit, it should be a direct admission instead of transitioning from the emergency unit to the general hospital and from general hospital to extended care facilities. If multiple transitions are unavoidable, they should be as direct and timely as possible. (Clinical manager)

Receiving emotional support was regarded as one of the main facilitators during the dying process and near the moment of death. However, certain attitudes and behaviours, such as keeping the patient alive against their will or conflicts among relatives, were perceived as barriers to achieving a good death (figure 2). Advanced directives and euthanasia were identified as tools to facilitate patients’ wishes and to support understanding of their preferences and values at the end of life. In this context, the right to request euthanasia was perceived as a particularly controversial issue among patients, relatives and healthcare professionals (online supplemental files 11,12).

The ability to choose the place of death was identified as one of the main facilitators during the time near death and the dying process (figure 2; online supplemental files 11,12). An ideal place of death was described as quiet and harmonious. Even if the home place is preferred by many patients because of the privacy, hospitals were often perceived as safer from the medical/clinical point of view. It is also viewed as more practical for the relatives when handling the situation after the death of their loved ones.

Healthcare professionals noted that when death occurred earlier than expected, not having the document of advanced directives could make it more difficult to ensure that patients’ wishes were respected (online supplemental files 12,13). Additionally, funeral parlours are perceived by relatives as impersonal and aseptic places for bereavement. Finally, relatives’ attitudes could act either as facilitators or barriers depending on the extent to which they support the patient’s will in their dying process.

Unmet needs in the health system

Based on narratives from patients, relatives and professionals, we identified several unmet needs that should be addressed to improve end-of-life care within the health system. These unmet needs were closely linked to the barriers described above and hinder the possibility of achieving a good death (figure 2 and online supplemental file 14).

Economic and support resources provided under the Spanish Dependency Act were perceived as insufficient, especially in cases of high dependency. Participants highlighted the need to address both social and healthcare needs, provide additional resources for caregivers and strengthen support to enable patients to remain at home and avoid unnecessary institutionalisation. Caregivers reported receiving limited support, resulting in excessive burden that negatively affected their relationship with the patient, generating conflicts and tension due to the lack of assistance.

There is also a growing need for specific professionals’ training, clear guidelines, a better multidisciplinary approach and improved access to information for patients and healthcare professionals.

Because maybe […] we feel insecure and uncertain, and we need to be honest about this: what are the gaps in a healthcare professional’s training that limit their ability to make decisions in certain situations? We often talk about training and about discussing death in society, but I would add that we, ourselves, also lack the necessary tools for those moments. Why? Because there are so many things behind, we are not aware of, or that we don’t want to talk about. (Geriatrician)

They need to provide the patient with all the information so they can choose with freedom and without anyone being shocked if the person wants a certain treatment or refuses it. (Patient without advanced nor complex chronic conditions, female, 65–79 years old)

In this context, healthcare professionals consider they are used to «saving lives» but indicated insufficient training in palliative and end-of-life care. Moreover, they highlighted a lack of clear guidelines regarding which professionals should be responsible for implementing ACP, related programmes and decision-making tools, as well as how, when and where these should be applied. Participants further emphasised the need for greater integration of psychologists and social care workers in the palliative care teams as these roles are not consistently integrated into chronic care. Many complex emotional and psychosocial needs arise for patients and their families at this stage of life, especially as death gets closer. If they are not resolved, these aspects make it more difficult to achieve a good death from the perspective of participants. Furthermore, there is a lack of training on how to deal with chronic patients with complex needs (online supplemental file 14).

The psychologist supported me, and we talked; I really liked that; I want to be given what I call ANCHORS (referred to things to hold on to). […] We should receive more strategies, like: ‘Take a look at this book, it might be of interest for you’. […] So I started accepting the situation… An acceptance that arrived by searching for these anchors. (Patient with advanced chronic conditions, female, 65–79 years old)

I trust the PADES [palliative care unit] not only because they provide compassionate care to patients, but also because they support family members, which is so important… With every diagnosis, we think so much about the patient, but we also have to consider the person who provides care for the patient. (Patient with complex chronic conditions, male, 65–79 years old).

Discussion

Our qualitative study identifies a range of facilitators and barriers influencing the achievement of a good death in Catalonia. These findings, together with the identified unmet needs, provide a framework for assessing the quality of care during the dying process and end-of-life care more broadly, as perceived by participants. Factors were identified at multiple levels, including those related to patients, relatives and professionals, as well as structural aspects within the healthcare system.

These results contribute to a better understanding of patients’ preferences, quality of care and unmet needs within the Catalan healthcare system. Addressing these barriers and strengthening existing facilitators may lead to improvements in the dying process. Some of these facilitators, barriers and improvements are consistent with findings in other publications.36 8 9 23,25 Thus, these findings may also be relevant to other regions within the Spanish Health System, across Europe and in international contexts, particularly with regard to shared unmet needs in end-of-life care.

One of the strengths of our study is the triangulation of sources of information, techniques, theoretical and methodological perspectives and perspectives from patients, caregivers and professionals that reinforces trustworthiness of its insights and conclusions (online supplemental files 13,14).

Main insights compared with other published studies

When examining facilitators, we found that the ability to choose the place of death is a central facilitator for patients. However, this preference frequently remains unmet within the health system, especially in hospital settings. Healthcare professionals also identified the anticipation of death planning as an important facilitator as it allows for better emotional acceptance and practical preparation. In line with these findings, participants emphasised not only the importance of choosing the place of death, but also the characteristics of that place. In our study, those patients expressing they would prefer to die at home were also worried about the impact that this choice would have on their relatives. The hospital is perceived as a safer and more hygienic environment. For some participants, an ideal alternative would be the introduction of hospice-based models sometimes described as ‘hospices’ or ‘hotels to die’, where patients could personalise their environment, maintain privacy and receive appropriate medical support.

Pedersen et al23 identified factors such as education, employment status, social support and economic resources as influential determinants of place of death. These factors were also related to a higher likelihood of receiving palliative care and engaging in advanced care planning. Information, economic and social support have been shown to be relevant also to keep patients at home in the study published by Sayma.25 Factors such as fear and stigma associated with death, attitudes of doctors, patients and family members, the availability of social networks and resource provision play a key role in preferences regarding place of death, particularly in the possibility of remaining at home. These are key aspects to improve and take into consideration to adapt to patients’ preferences and wishes.

The Spanish Dependency Act was more often perceived as a barrier than as a facilitator, as the associated economic and support resources were viewed as scarce, and bureaucracy as complex and time-consuming— especially for people with complex social and healthcare end-of-life needs. Delays in accessing these resources placed additional strain on caregivers, who often felt trapped in their caregiving role due to a lack of adequate support. To improve the quality of end-of-life care, our study suggests that, besides reducing bureaucracy across all involved processes (from finding resources to applying for euthanasia), implementation efforts should adopt a multidisciplinary approach that integrates health and social care systems. This need for coordinated, cross-sectoral action was consistently emphasised by professionals during the focus group discussions.

Despite the presence of the palliative care teams, which already try to provide this approach, participants identified persistent structural fragmentation and limited coordination between primary care and specialised palliative care services in hospitals and intermediate care facilities. These findings are consistent with the general barriers to end-of-life and palliative care shown in major guidelines and reports, including those published by NICE, OECD and WHO.9 12 13 26 Patients and relatives also agreed that a multidisciplinary approach involving healthcare professionals and social services was needed. Thus, recommendations calling for stronger communication and coordination among professionals, patients and relatives are closely aligned with the findings of the present study.

Specifically, there is a clear need to improve communication between primary care physicians, social services and specialised palliative care teams in hospital settings to create stronger bonds with patients and their family environments. Insufficient access to key information essential for shared decision-making appears to be associated with several factors, including the limited prioritisation of patient autonomy at the dying phase and death, patients’ fears surrounding decision-making, social taboos related to death and inadequate communication processes.23 26 27 This situation can additionally reflect the above-mentioned barrier, the lack of training that professionals receive on this topic and the lack of a clear design outlining which professionals should provide specific information, to whom, when and where.

Out of the quotes, two final relevant aspects emerged as pivotal to achieving a good death: the patients’ attitudinal profiles and their personal ‘red lines’. Understanding a patient’s attitudinal profile helps to identify the level of control they wish to maintain during their end-of-life and dying process, thereby allowing relatives and healthcare professionals to adapt care to the patient’s individual needs. This factor has also been documented as a key factor in the review of Wang in 20236 among patients with cancer. Furthermore, the concept of a personal ‘red line’ helps establish the point where a perceived loss of quality of life or dignity puts the patient in a situation where death is perceived as a liberation from an ‘unnecessary, cruel or humiliating and unbearable’ suffering. These red lines are extremely subjective and dynamic, evolving over time as patients’ circumstances change while approaching the end of life. Understanding them may facilitate communicating about preferences within shared decision-making processes. This perspective aligns closely with the advanced care planning tools and person-centred approaches to end-of-life care, supporting care that is responsive to patients’ values and evolving needs.

Differences in the perception of facilitators, barriers and unmet needs

Although there is broad agreement among patients, family members and healthcare professionals regarding the core elements of a good death—such as the absence of physical and psychological suffering and the importance of emotional support—important differences emerge both in how these goals are pursued and in which unmet needs are prioritised by each group.

Patients tend to focus primarily on symptom control, comfort and maintaining a sense of control over the dying process.7 In contrast, family members often emphasise the need for support in decision-making processes and emotional accompaniment throughout care and into bereavement.23 27 Accordingly, our study suggests that patients are more likely to articulate their personal ‘red lines’ (personal thresholds related to perceived losses in quality of life), whereas some family members experience difficulties in openly addressing these issues. These challenges are particularly evident when divergent views are anticipated or when patients or relatives avoid early and planned discussions about end-of-life and dying preferences.

Patients and family members mainly highlight facilitators related to relational aspects of care, such as clear communication, professional presence and continuity of care, as well as barriers associated with limited access to information, difficulties in accessing professionals and insufficient coordination between services. They also emphasise unmet emotional and social needs and the importance of respecting preferences and decisions at the end of life.

All groups identify shortcomings in training in communication skills and in the availability of appropriate spaces for end-of-life care and dying. Healthcare professionals placed greater emphasis on the lack of specific training in palliative care, acknowledging that the healthcare system remains largely oriented towards ‘curing and saving lives’ rather than accompanying patients through the dying process. Patients and family members experienced this as a barrier, although they did not always explicitly identify it as such.

Finally, professionals tended to analyse the Spanish Dependency Law from a more systemic and critical perspective, noting that it is primarily designed for natural ageing rather than for complex chronic conditions and social needs. In contrast, patients and family members focus on the Act’s shortcomings from a user perspective, highlighting bureaucratic complexity, delays in access and insufficient support.

Limitations and future research

The study has some limitations due to an inherent bias in the inclusion criteria. Participants needed to be open to talk about death and, more specifically, about dying with dignity. In this sense, considerable efforts were made to incorporate patients’ voices and needs in order to gain an in-depth understanding of areas for improvement within the healthcare system from their perspective. The study findings are presented in an integrated manner, allowing for a comprehensive and holistic understanding of perspectives and opinions across participant groups, which represents a key strength of this qualitative research. Nevertheless, future research is needed to assess whether patients and other key stakeholders perceive end-of-life care and healthcare approaches as genuinely patient-centred across different provider settings and healthcare facilities.

Other limitations include some imbalances in the geographical distribution of patients and caregivers, and also their profiles were from higher socio-economic levels than expected. Most participants were older adults, living with family members and were women. Certain groups were under-represented, including patients living in rural inland areas compared with coastal regions, individuals from more socioeconomically disadvantaged and vulnerable backgrounds (as none of the participants lived in poverty), or patients residing in nursing homes. Although data saturation seems to be achieved, particularly regarding facilitators and barriers to reach an ideal good death, we cannot determine how these imbalances may have influenced the study’s main insights.

Including additional subgroups, such as people living alone, middle-aged or younger individuals, and those with even more complex health and socio-economic needs, would likely have provided deeper information into attitudes regarding how, where and with whom people prefer to die. We tried to mitigate limitations related to geographical distribution, health regions, care providers and unrepresented profiles by including professionals and patient representatives, caregivers and civil society associations in the focus group discussions. Future research should aim to replicate this study in different contexts and among more diverse—and potentially markedly different—target populations.

Conclusions and final considerations

This study identifies key facilitators and barriers within the healthcare system that shape the achievement of a good death, as perceived by patients, relatives and healthcare professionals in the Catalan context.

Our study aims to contribute to breaking the persistent taboo surrounding death by promoting open dialogue among patients, relatives and healthcare professionals. It also brings attention to the dying process and to diverse perspectives on the right to request euthanasia.

Patients and families continue to face difficulties accessing clear information about the existing tools, such as advanced directives and shared care planning, as well as about their rights including, if applicable, the right to request euthanasia. These gaps point to key areas for improvement within both health and social care systems.

Based on the reflections of patients, relatives and healthcare professionals, several recommendations have been established to improve end-of-life care and enhance the quality of care during the dying process (figure 3).

Recommendations raised from the participants’ narratives and research team to enhance care at the end of life and, especially, in the dying process and near death.

The most basic ground for improving the quality of care in end-of-life and the dying process is to increase awareness and access to information about death, while emphasising the importance of accepting patients’ wishes and preferences. Achieving this goal requires greater socio-economic and healthcare resources to support patients, caregivers and healthcare professionals during this particularly vulnerable stage of life.
