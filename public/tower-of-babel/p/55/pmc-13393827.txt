Comparative efficacy of various exercise therapies for metabolic syndrome a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Xinyu Shen, Yaqi Xue, Yucheng Dong, Haifeng Li, Yanyan Wang, Niu Liu, Chang Liu, Hongchao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTo identify the most effective exercise interventions for improving metabolic outcomes in individuals with metabolic syndrome, we conducted a network meta-analysis comparing the effects of ten different exercise interventions. A total of 53 studies involving 2,948 participants were included. Combined aerobic and resistance training (AT + RT) ranked highest for improving body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), and fasting blood glucose (FBG). High-volume high-intensity interval training (HV-HIIT) was most effective for reducing body fat percentage (FAT%), whereas low-volume HIIT (LV-HIIT) showed the greatest benefits for total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Traditional Chinese exercise (TCE) ranked highest for increasing high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL) and reducing systolic blood pressure (SBP). Most evidence was rated as low or very low certainty. No single exercise modality was superior across all components of metabolic syndrome. These findings support a phenotype-tailored exercise prescription approach based on patients’ primary metabolic abnormalities.Keywords: metabolic syndrome, network meta-analysis, optimal exercise modality, high-intensity interval training, traditional Chinese exercise

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is not a single disease but rather a collective term for a series of pathological conditions characterized by metabolic disorders, including obesity, abnormal glucose metabolism, and dyslipidemia. Its diagnosis is typically established when three or more of the following five criteria are present: central obesity, hypertension, elevated fasting blood glucose (FBG), elevated triglycerides (TG), and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL).1 The most comprehensive meta-analysis to date, synthesizing 1,129 prevalence estimates from over 28 million adults, reports a global prevalence ranging from 12.5% to 31.4% depending on the diagnostic criteria applied.2 More recent modeling demonstrates that this burden continues to escalate: between 2000 and 2023, prevalence rose from 14.7% to 31.0% among women and from 9.0% to 25.7% among men, translating to an estimated 1.54 billion adults living with MetS by 2023.3 Individuals with MetS face an approximately 1.6-fold risk of cardiovascular morbidity and all-cause mortality, with cohort studies reporting a 64% higher risk of cardiovascular mortality and a 45% higher risk of all-cause mortality compared to those without MetS.4 Beyond its direct cardiometabolic impact, MetS is also associated with increased risks of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, polycystic ovary syndrome, sleep apnea, and certain cancers, imposing a substantial burden on both individual quality of life and public health systems worldwide.5,6,7,8 This escalating burden has necessitated the evolution of standardized diagnostic tools. Earlier frameworks—including those from the World Health Organization (WHO), the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), and the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III)—differed substantially in their mandatory components and thresholds,9 heterogeneity that directly contributed to the wide prevalence variation (12.5–31.4%) observed in global meta-analyses.2 In response, the 2009 Harmonized definition emerged as a consensus framework that streamlines diagnosis by requiring any three of five unified components. Its critical advancement lies in incorporating population-specific waist circumference (WC) cut-offs, formally acknowledging well-established ethnic differences in adiposity-related cardiometabolic risk.9 By enhancing diagnostic accuracy and cross-population comparability, the Harmonized definition provides a methodologically robust foundation for evidence synthesis. This foundation is critical: Inconsistent application of earlier diagnostic criteria across trials would introduce heterogeneity in baseline metabolic risk, violating the transitivity assumption essential for valid indirect comparisons.10 By prioritizing studies that adhere to, or allow alignment with, the 2009 Harmonized definition, we minimized this source of heterogeneity and strengthened the comparability of effect estimates across exercise modalities.

Lifestyle modification, particularly regular exercise, is universally recognized as the cornerstone of management for MetS.11 Although pharmacotherapy supports the management of individual risk components such as antihypertensives for blood pressure, statins for dyslipidemia, and hypoglycemic agents for glucose control, exercise uniquely targets the core pathophysiology of MetS, insulin resistance and visceral adiposity, simultaneously and synergistically.12 Unlike single-target pharmacological agents, exercise confers multi-system metabolic benefits by enhancing skeletal muscle glucose uptake via glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) translocation, mobilizes visceral fat depots, lowers TG while raising HDL, reduces resting blood pressure, and suppresses chronic low-grade inflammation through the modulation of adipokines and cytokines.12,13,14,15 These integrated adaptations occur without the adverse effect profiles associated with polypharmacy and are accessible across diverse healthcare settings with minimal cost. Consequently, a broad consensus across major clinical guidelines and expert statements positions exercise as an indispensable, an essential therapeutic component for MetS.9,16 Building upon this well-established foundation, various forms of exercise interventions have been implemented in individuals with MetS, including aerobic exercise, high-intensity interval training (HIIT), resistance training (RT), and combined modalities, each demonstrating distinct physiological benefits.17,18,19 However, the metabolic adaptations elicited by different exercise modalities are not identical. For instance, while some evidence supports the superior time-efficiency of HIIT,20 other studies emphasize the unique value of RT in increasing muscle mass and improving insulin sensitivity, and combined training may integrate the advantages of both.21

Yet, this very diversity of effective options paradoxically creates a clinical dilemma: The optimal exercise modality for managing MetS remains unclear. This uncertainty is perpetuated by three interconnected limitations of the existing literature. First, previous meta-analyses have predominantly adopted a single-outcome lens, examining effects on isolated endpoints such as fasting glucose or WC; consequently, evidence regarding whether the same exercise modality exerts concordant or divergent effects across distinct metabolic components remains fragmented.22 Second, the evidence base is constrained by narrow intervention scopes—most syntheses compare only two or three modalities (e.g., aerobic versus resistance) and exclude emerging regimens such as low-vs. high-volume HIIT, combined aerobic and resistance protocols, or mind-body exercises, despite their increasing clinical adoption.23,24 Third, the absence of simultaneous multi-intervention, multi-outcome comparisons precludes the generation of a comprehensive, hierarchical ranking that accounts for trade-offs in modality-specific benefits across different cardiometabolic targets.25,26 Together, these gaps leave clinicians without an evidence-based framework to tailor exercise prescriptions to the phenotypic heterogeneity of MetS. Compounding this issue, substantial evidence indicates that exercise interventions exhibit considerable clinical heterogeneity due to differences in their design and delivery.

To address these gaps, this network meta-analysis moves beyond the binary question of “whether exercise works” to the clinically actionable question of “which modality works best for which outcome.” It offers three distinctive contributions. First, it provides the most comprehensive comparison of exercise modalities to date, including traditional, emerging, and mind-body regimens, within a single analytic framework. Second, it concurrently evaluates a broad range of MetS related outcomes, enabling the first integrated assessment of whether a given modality exerts concordant or divergent effects across different cardiometabolic targets. Third, by generating outcome-specific hierarchical rankings, it translates comparative effectiveness evidence into a modality-specific, outcome-stratified framework that equips clinicians with an evidence-based decision aid. Finally, to ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted pre-specified sensitivity analyses exploring key sources such as intervention duration, participant age, and control group type.

Results

Literature selection

A systematic search was conducted across six databases, namely PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, EBSCO, and CNKI, which initially identified 7,715 records. Additionally, 5 records were identified from previous reviews. These records were imported into EndNote X9 for deduplication, which removed 1,130 duplicates and 339 records for other reasons, resulting in 6,246 records for further screening. The titles and abstracts of these records were screened, leading to the exclusion of 6,093 irrelevant records. Consequently, 153 records were sought for full-text retrieval. However, the full text could not be obtained for 21 of these articles. After a detailed full-text assessment, articles were excluded for the following reasons: not meeting participant eligibility criteria (n = 21), non-English or non-Chinese publications (n = 2), ineligible study design (n = 33), irrelevant outcomes (n = 20), and unavailable data (n = 8). For the network meta-analysis, one study was excluded due to its unique exercise modality,27 yielding a final set of 52 studies for comparison, as illustrated in Figure 1.

Characteristics of the included studies

The characteristics of the 53 included studies are summarized in Table 1. A total of 2,948 participants were enrolled in the meta-analysis, with sample sizes ranging from 8 to 182. The mean age of participants varied across studies, ranging from approximately 36 to 73 years. Regarding intervention types, the exercise modalities included low-intensity exercise training (LIET), moderate-intensity exercise training (MIET), moderate-to-vigorous intensity exercise training (MVET), low-volume HIIT (LV-HIIT), high-volume HIIT (HV-HIIT), RT, combined aerobic and RT (AT+RT), HIIT combined with resistance training (HIIT+RT), yoga, and traditional Chinese exercise (TCE). The distribution of participants across exercise categories was as follows: LIET (n = 94), MIET (n = 419), MVET (n = 94), LV-HIIT (n = 142), HV-HIIT (n = 541), RT (n = 120), AT+RT (n = 172), HIIT+RT (n = 53), yoga (n = 194), TCE (n = 143), and other modalities (n = 15). To assess transitivity, we examined the distribution of key effect modifiers across direct comparisons (Table S2). Mean participant ages were generally comparable across comparisons: for example, participants in AT+RT versus Control studies were aged 36.6 to 68.2 years, those in HV-HIIT versus Control studies ranged from 45.5 to 57.4 years, and MIET versus Control studies included individuals aged 35.5 to 72.5 years, showing considerable overlap. Intervention duration also followed a comparable pattern; most direct comparisons, such as HV-HIIT versus MIET, AT + RT versus RT, and LV-HIIT versus MIET, had durations between 8 and 16 weeks, whereas longer interventions (24–80 weeks) were mainly confined to comparisons such as HV-HIIT versus control and yoga versus control. Across all comparisons, control groups consistently involved either daily activities or health education, with no indication of systematic confounding. The consistency of these effect modifiers across the network supports the transitivity assumption underlying our network meta-analysis.

Result of assessment

We assessed the risk of bias of the 53 included randomized controlled trials using the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB 2) for randomized trials. The assessment covered five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. The overall risk of bias was determined based on the judgments across these domains. (Figures S1 and S2) For the randomization process, 15 studies (28.3%) were rated as low risk of bias, 35 (66.0%) raised some concerns, and three (5.7%) were judged as high risk. Regarding deviations from intended interventions, 16 studies (30.2%) were at low risk, 21 (39.6%) at some concerns, and 16 (30.2%) at high risk. In the domain of missing outcome data, 36 studies (67.9%) showed low risk, none (0%) had some concerns, and 17 (32.1%) were at high risk. For the measurement of the outcome, 39 studies (73.6%) were rated low risk, two (3.8%) some concerns, and 12 (22.6%) high risk. In the domain of selection of the reported result, four studies (7.5%) were at low risk, 26 (49.1%) at some concerns, and 23 (43.4%) at high risk. Overall, the risk of bias was low in only one study (1.9%), raised some concerns in 11 studies (20.8%), and was high in 41 studies (77.4%). The most common sources of bias were deviations from intended interventions, a limitation inherent to exercise trials where blinding of participants and personnel is not feasible, and selection of the reported result. The high risk in the latter domain was primarily driven by the absence of pre-registered analysis plans; most studies did not specify in advance how outcome data would be analyzed, and many reported results based on multiple eligible measurements, definitions, or time points within the same outcome domain, thereby increasing the risk of selective reporting.

Network meta-analysis

Body composition

Body mass index (BMI) was assessed as an outcome in 32 studies (total n = 1688), as shown in Figure 2. Node-splitting analysis was performed to evaluate local inconsistency. The results indicated a statistically significant difference between direct and indirect evidence at the node for the Control vs. RT comparison (p = 0.001), suggesting potential local inconsistency for this specific comparison (Table S13). However, the global inconsistency test revealed no evidence of inconsistency across the entire network (χ2 = 21.30, p = 0.502), indicating that the direct and indirect evidence were generally consistent throughout the network. Given this local inconsistency, the findings for the comparison between Control and RT should be interpreted with caution. For all other nodes, no statistically significant differences were observed between direct and indirect evidence (p > 0.05). The consistency model of the NMA demonstrated that, compared with the control group, AT + RT (MD = −1.43, 95% CI: −2.45 to −0.41, low certainty), HV-HIIT (MD = −0.94, 95% CI: −1.54 to −0.35, low certainty), and MIET (MD = −0.82, 95% CI: −1.54 to −0.10, very low certainty) were associated with significant reductions in BMI. No other significant differences were detected among the remaining intervention comparisons (Table S23). The SUCRA rankings suggested that AT + RT had the highest probability of being the most effective intervention for reducing BMI (84.6%), followed by HV-HIIT (65.8%) > LV-HIIT (59.4%) > MIET (58.1%) > HV-HIIT + RT (53.9%) > TCE (53.1%) > RT (52.6%) > MVET (30.7%) > LIET (27.0%) > control (14.7%) (Figure 3).

SUCRA for ten metabolic and cardiovascular outcome indicators

LIET: low-intensity exercise training; MIET: moderate-intensity exercise training; MVET: moderate-to-vigorous intensity exercise training; LV-HIIT: low-volume high-intensity interval training; HV-HIIT: high-volume high-intensity interval training; RT: resistance training; AT + RT: combined aerobic training and resistance training; HIIT + RT: combined HIIT and resistance training; TCE: traditional Chinese exercise. SUCRA values represent the cumulative probability of each intervention being among the best options, ranging from 0 (worst) to 1 (best). Higher values indicate better efficacy for that outcome.

Blood glucose

FBG was assessed as an outcome in multiple studies, as shown in Figure 2. Node-splitting analysis was performed to evaluate local inconsistency. The results revealed statistically significant differences between direct and indirect evidence in several loops, including control vs. RT (p = 0.001), control vs. TCE (p = 0.003), LIET vs. MVET (p = 0.004), LIET vs. TCE (p = 0.003), and MIET vs. MVET (p = 0.009), suggesting potential local inconsistencies for these specific comparisons (Table S22). The global inconsistency test also indicated significant inconsistency across the entire network (χ2 = 80.32, p < 0.001), demonstrating that the direct and indirect evidence were not consistent throughout the network. Therefore, subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate the sources of inconsistency. Subgroup analyses demonstrated that exercise interventions significantly reduced FBG levels across all subgroups, with the exception of small-sample studies. However, moderate to substantial heterogeneity persisted within each subgroup, and none of the subgroup differences reached statistical significance. These findings suggest that age, intervention duration, sample size, exercise frequency, and control group type do not fully explain the observed inconsistency and heterogeneity in the FBG network. Although large studies (n > 50) provided more stable effect estimates, residual heterogeneity remained, suggesting that other factors such as exercise intensity, total exercise volume, adherence, and medication use may have confounding effects on the results. Detailed results of the subgroup analyses are presented in Figures S23–S27. Future large-scale randomized controlled trials with rigorous design and standardized reporting are warranted to further elucidate the effects of different exercise modalities on FBG and to explore the sources of heterogeneity. Therefore, the following results based on the consistency model and the SUCRA rankings should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution; subgroup analyses will be conducted subsequently to investigate the sources of inconsistency. The random-effects consistency model of the NMA demonstrated that, compared with the control group, AT + RT (MD = −0.47, 95% CI: −0.76 to −0.17, low certainty), HV-HIIT + RT (MD = −0.48, 95% CI: −0.94 to −0.02, low certainty), MVET (MD = −0.42, 95% CI: −0.67 to −0.17, very low certainty), LV-HIIT (MD = −0.31, 95% CI: −0.56 to −0.05, very low certainty), MIET (MD = −0.30, 95% CI: −0.52 to −0.09, very low certainty), and HV-HIIT (MD = −0.22, 95% CI: −0.41 to −0.02, very low certainty) were associated with significant reductions in FBG levels. Publication bias assessment

To assess potential publication bias or small-study effects across outcomes in this network meta-analysis, comparison-adjusted funnel plots were constructed for each outcome (Figures S13–S22), accompanied by Egger’s test. Visual inspection of the funnel plots revealed that the data points for all outcomes were generally symmetrically distributed around the pooled effect estimates and predominantly clustered at the top of the plots, indicating a low likelihood of small-study effects. Egger’s regression tests yielded P values >0.05 for all outcomes, none of which reached statistical significance. Both graphical and statistical evaluations suggest that the findings for each outcome are unlikely to be substantially influenced by publication bias, supporting the robustness of the results.

Certainty of evidence

We assessed the certainty of evidence for each comparison and outcome using the CINeMA framework, which evaluates six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. The distribution of confidence ratings across the ten outcomes was as follows: (1) for BMI, 20.0% of comparisons had low confidence, and 80.0% had very low confidence; (2) for WC, 1.8% had moderate confidence, 9.1% had low confidence, and 89.1% had very low confidence; (3) for FAT%, 10.7% had moderate confidence, 42.9% had low confidence, and 46.4% had very low confidence; (4) for TC, 6.7% had low confidence, and 93.3% had very low confidence; (5) for TG, 21.8% had low confidence, and 78.2% had very low confidence; (6) for HDL, 3.6% had low confidence, and 96.4% had very low confidence; (7) for LDL, 14.3% had low confidence, and 85.7% had very low confidence; (8) for SBP, 1.8% had moderate confidence, 10.9% had low confidence, and 87.3% had very low confidence; (9) for DBP, 1.8% had low confidence, and 98.2% had very low confidence; (10) for FBG, 9.1% had low confidence, and 90.9% had very low confidence (Tables S33–S42).

Overall, the certainty of evidence was low or very low for most comparisons across all outcomes. The most frequent reasons for downgrading were major concerns regarding within-study bias, imprecision, and heterogeneity. Only a small number of comparisons reached moderate certainty, including AT + RT versus MIET, AT + RT versus RT, and MIET versus RT for body fat percentage, AT + RT versus control for SBP, and TCE versus control for WC for WC. These findings underscore the need for well-designed, adequately powered randomized controlled trials to improve confidence in estimates of the relative effects of different exercise interventions on cardiometabolic outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the primary findings, we performed pre-specified sensitivity analyses by sequentially excluding studies with intervention durations of <12 weeks, studies with a mean participant age of ≥60 years, and studies in which the control group received structured health education. Overall, the effect estimates for most outcomes remained consistent with the main analysis, supporting the stability of our conclusions. However, the statistical significance of some comparisons changed in specific sensitivity analyses, indicating that certain findings may be influenced by particular study characteristics (Tables S43–S52).

For body composition outcomes, most interventions demonstrated robust effects, though LV-HIIT became significant only after excluding health education controls, while the effect of MIET was attenuated and lost significance across all sensitivity models. For lipid outcomes, greater variability was observed: several non-significant comparisons in the main analysis, particularly for TGs and HDL, became significant after excluding short-duration studies, whereas some significant effects, such as that of RT on LDL, were no longer significant in specific sensitivity analyses. Blood pressure reductions were largely robust, although the effects of MVET, RT, and yoga showed inconsistency across certain exclusion criteria. For FBG, most interventions remained significant, but the effects of AT + RT and HV-HIIT were attenuated after excluding studies with intervention duration <12 weeks.

In summary, while most of our findings were robust, the variability observed in lipid outcomes and for certain interventions in other domains highlights the importance of considering study design and population characteristics when interpreting the results.

Discussion

With the growing promotion of healthy lifestyles, exercise has become a recommended intervention for managing MetS. While numerous studies over the past few years have demonstrated its beneficial effects, and traditional meta-analyses have summarized the overall impact of exercise on patients with MetS, the optimal exercise modality remains unclear due to the complexity of the syndrome. To address this gap, we conducted an NMA. A total of 53 studies involving 2,948 participants met the criteria for quantitative synthesis and were included in the NMA to compare the effects of 10 different exercise modalities on body composition, blood lipids, blood pressure, and glycemic control in patients with MetS. We employed the CINeMA framework to assess the certainty of evidence for the outcomes. The quality of evidence for most indicators ranged from very low to low, with only a few rated as moderate. This limitation, however, is largely unavoidable in exercise intervention trials given the inherent practical challenges of blinding. To ensure the robustness of our meta-analysis findings, we performed sensitivity analyses, as these methodological constraints may result in the under- or overestimation of specific intervention effects.

Regarding body composition, our findings indicated that AT + RT was the most effective intervention for reducing BMI (MD = −1.43, 95% CI: −2.45 to −0.41, low certainty, SUCRA = 84.6%) and WC (MD = −3.60, 95% CI: −6.14 to −1.06, very low certainty, SUCRA = 77.1%). Furthermore, the efficacy of AT + RT remained consistent across sensitivity analyses, which controlled for factors including intervention duration, sample size, frequency, age, and whether the control group received health education. However, given the very low certainty of the evidence concerning WC reduction, its clinical application should be approached with caution. As a form of combined modality, AT + RT integrates the advantages of AT in enhancing energy expenditure and fat oxidation with the benefits of RT in increasing lean body mass and basal metabolic rate.79 Consequently, this synergy exerts a potent effect on improving body weight and abdominal fat distribution. A 6-week intervention involving RT combined with various forms of aerobic exercise demonstrated that combined training had a positive impact on abdominal fat distribution.80 Furthermore, findings from systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that, compared to RT alone, combined training leads to more pronounced reductions in BMI and WC, with these effects being particularly significant in populations undergoing longer intervention durations.81 Our sensitivity analysis (excluding studies with a duration of <12 weeks) yielded similar results, further confirming the robustness of the primary findings.

In terms of FAT%, TG, and TC, HIIT demonstrated significant efficacy. Regarding FAT%, HV-HIIT emerged as the most advantageous modality (MD = −2.62, 95% CI: −3.96 to −1.28, low certainty, SUCRA = 84.3%). The robustness of this result was further supported by sensitivity analysis, underscoring the superiority of HV-HIIT in improving FAT%. Furthermore, LV-HIIT demonstrated superior efficacy in improving TG (MD = −0.25, 95% CI: −0.38 to −0.11, low certainty, SUCRA = 77.5%) and TC (MD = −0.36, 95% CI: −0.61 to −0.11, very low certainty, SUCRA = 82.3%) levels compared to other modalities. Although the primary sensitivity analyses generally supported the robustness of the findings, the effect size exhibited substantial fluctuations upon the exclusion of studies with a duration of <12 weeks. This suggests that current research on LV-HIIT is characterized by relatively short intervention periods, thereby providing a preliminary evidentiary basis for determining the optimal duration in clinical applications. Previous studies have demonstrated that HIIT exerts beneficial effects on body composition by significantly reducing body FAT% and total adiposity.82 In individuals with abdominal obesity, a 12-week HIIT program has been shown to produce significantly greater reductions in body fat compared with MIET.83 Based on total training volume, HIIT can be classified into LV-HIIT and HV-HIIT protocols. HV-HIIT refers to training sessions with an accumulated duration of more than 15 min of high-intensity exercise per session (excluding rest periods), whereas all other HIIT protocols with lower total work volumes are categorized as LV-HIIT.84 A meta-analysis reported that LV-HIIT did not produce significant improvements in overall fat mass or lean body mass among healthy adults without regular training experience.84 In contrast, another meta-analysis focusing on overweight and obese populations found that LV-HIIT effectively reduced total body fat, abdominal fat, and visceral fat in these individuals.85 This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in participant characteristics, as LV-HIIT tends to elicit greater fat-reducing effects in overweight or obese individuals compared with normal-weight populations.

Regarding blood pressure, TCE demonstrated favorable efficacy. Specifically, TCE showed significant improvements in DBP (MD = −13.59, 95% CI: −17.90 to −9.28, low certainty, SUCRA = 99.1%). However, sensitivity analyses revealed substantial fluctuations in the effect size when participants aged over 60 were excluded. This variability is likely closely associated with the target demographic of current TCE research, as most existing studies focus on populations aged 60 and above. Regarding SBP, the NMA indicated that LV-HIIT yielded the most favorable outcomes LV-HIIT (MD = −5.90, 95% CI: −8.20 to −3.59, very low certainty, SUCRA = 89.4%). However, due to the very low certainty of the evidence and the substantial fluctuations in its effect size during sensitivity analysis, LV-HIIT is not recommended as a primary intervention. In contrast, TCE emerged as a robust modality in the sensitivity analysis, consistently demonstrating favorable efficacy. Consequently, TCE is recommended as the preferred exercise intervention for managing blood pressure in patients with MetS. It also demonstrated favorable effects on HDL improvement, with the results of the sensitivity analysis remaining robust. In our study, TCE primarily included Tai Chi, Qigong, and Baduanjin. It is a form of mind-body exercise characterized by low-to-moderate intensity.86 The main features of this type of exercise involve conscious control of body movements and the coordinated integration of movement with guided breathing. Its health benefits have received increasing attention in recent years.87,88 As a low-risk form of physical activity, TCE has been widely applied to evaluate health outcomes in clinical populations.89,90 The mechanisms through which TCE influences lipid metabolism may be related to its integration of controlled breathing and mental regulation during movement. Such practices not only enhance respiratory function but also improve vascular function.91 By regulating breathing, TCE may help balance autonomic nervous system activity and modulate metabolic processes, thereby improving dysregulated lipid metabolism.91 Moreover, as a mind-body exercise, TCE can alleviate anxiety and tension through guided breathing techniques, which may reduce sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone, decrease norepinephrine release at nerve terminals, and enhance parasympathetic activity.

Regarding glycemic control, the results indicated that AT + RT was the most effective intervention (MD = −0.47, 95% CI: −0.76 to −0.17, low certainty, SUCRA = 79.9%). However, given the presence of significant inconsistency, we conducted a head-to-head analysis which confirmed the robustness of the findings. Despite this, factors examined in the sensitivity analysis did not appear to account for the observed heterogeneity. Consequently, based on these observations, the clinical application of the results regarding FBG should be interpreted with caution. AT + RT has been widely recognized for its positive impact on FBG, particularly demonstrating robust intervention effects in individuals with obesity, MetS, and T2DM. Existing research indicates that structured exercise training significantly improves glycemic control, combined aerobic and resistance training exhibit more stable and sustained efficacy in enhancing glucose metabolism.92 From a physiological perspective, AT and RT exert complementary effects on the regulation of glucose metabolism. AT promotes glucose oxidation and utilization by increasing skeletal muscle glucose uptake, enhancing mitochondrial density, and activating insulin signaling pathways. In contrast, RT primarily improves insulin sensitivity by increasing skeletal muscle mass, enhancing muscular glucose storage capacity, and upregulating the expression of GLUT-4.93 The integration of these two modalities creates a synergistic effect that simultaneously enhances skeletal muscle glucose uptake capacity and overall metabolic efficiency, thereby more effectively reducing FBG. RCTs found that, compared with either AT or RT alone, combined training yielded more significant improvements in glycemic control and was more effective at enhancing insulin sensitivity and overall metabolic health.94 Furthermore, network meta-analyses have indicated that among various exercise interventions, combined training demonstrates a distinct advantage in improving glycemic control and addressing metabolic risk factors.95

Overall, our findings revealed that AT + RT had the highest probability of being the most effective intervention for reducing BMI, WC, LDL, and FBG; HV-HIIT had the highest probability of being the most effective for decreasing FAT%; LV-HIIT had the highest probability of being the most effective for reducing TC, TG, and DBP; and TCE had the highest probability of being the most effective for lowering SBP and raising HDL. These findings provide evidence-based guidance for clinicians to precisely prescribe individualized exercise regimens tailored to the differentiated cardiometabolic risk profiles of patients with MetS. Given the inconsistency in the evidence network for FBG and the overall low certainty in the evidence, future high-quality studies are warranted to verify whether this hierarchy of intervention effects can be generalized to different populations.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the comparisons for certain interventions were informed by a limited number of studies. For instance, the comparisons between HV-HIIT + RT and multiple other interventions across various outcomes were represented by only one or two studies, and some estimates relied primarily on indirect evidence with limited supporting data; therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Second, the overall quality of evidence from the studies included was low. The CINeMA assessment indicated that the confidence ratings for most comparisons were low or very low, primarily attributable to risk of bias, imprecision, and heterogeneity. Many studies exhibited methodological shortcomings, particularly regarding the implementation of blinding and selective outcome reporting. Third, our analysis primarily focused on exercise type and did not explore the potential impact of intervention settings, session duration, exercise intensity, or training volume on outcomes, factors that may modulate the effects of exercise on metabolic indicators. Fourth, although funnel plots and Egger’s test did not reveal significant publication bias, the search strategy excluded gray literature and studies not published in English or Chinese, posing a potential risk of publication bias. Future research should prioritize well-designed head-to-head randomized controlled trials with adequate sample sizes and long-term follow-up. Comprehensive reporting of intervention details and outcome data is essential to facilitate in-depth exploration of dose-response relationships and sources of heterogeneity, thereby providing a more precise evidence base for exercise prescription in clinical practice.

Experimental model and study participant details

This network meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-NMA) guidelines.96 The study protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO international database (Registration ID: CRD420251138705) to ensure methodological rigor.

Method details

Search strategy

We performed a systematic literature search across six electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, EBSCO, and CNKI. The searches were conducted on September 5, 2025. Our search strategy was built upon the pre-defined PECOS framework. The search terms encompassed terms extracted from “Medical Subject Headings (MeSH)” or equivalent thesaurus terms in each database, along with comprehensive title/abstract keywords. Our search strategies involved combinations of keywords related to metabolic syndrome (“Metabolic Syndrome”, “MetS”, “Syndrome X”) and exercise interventions (e.g., “sports”, “aerobic exercise”, “resistance training”, “yoga”, “tai chi”). The details of the search strategy are presented in Supplementary material S1. The search was limited to randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Language restrictions were applied, limiting the search to English and Chinese publications. Additionally, we conducted a manual search through the reference lists of pertinent reviews and included articles to identify any potential additional records.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this network meta-analysis were as follows:

Population (P): Adult patients (≥18 years) diagnosed with metabolic syndrome according to the 2009 harmonized definition, which requires the presence of at least three of the following five components:.

The 2009 Harmonized Criteria require the presence of at least three of the following five components:(1) central obesity, defined by population-specific waist circumference cut points (for Europids: > 94 cm in men and > 80 cm in women; for the United States/Canada (NCEP-ATP III): > 102 cm in men and > 88 cm in women; for Asian populations: > 90 cm in men and > 80 cm in women);(2) elevated triglycerides (≥ 1.7 mmol/L or under treatment);(3) reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (< 1.0 mmol/L in men and < 1.3 mmol/L in women or under treatment);(4) elevated blood pressure (≥ 130/85 mmHg or under treatment);(5) elevated fasting blood glucose (≥ 5.6 mmol/L or under treatment).9 Study eligibility was determined based on author-reported diagnoses or baseline data meeting any three of these criteria.

Intervention (I): Structured exercise interventions of any modality.

Comparator (C): Non-exercise control groups.

Outcomes (O): Assessment of at least one metabolic parameter (WC, TG, SBP, DBP, HDL, FBG) and report its pre- and post-intervention values. Furthermore, blood samples for these assays had to be obtained after a minimum 8-hour fast.

Study Design (S): Randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Data availability: Studies must have provided sufficient quantitative data for the calculation of pre-to-post intervention changes.

The exclusion criteria were:

(1) observational studies; (2) animal studies; (3) acute exercise interventions; (4) non-Chinese or non-English publications; (5) studies for which the full text was unavailable or from which valid data could not be extracted; and (6) studies in which exercise was combined with standardized dietary interventions or structured nutritional education programs as a core component of the intervention.

Data extraction

Two authors independently screened the titles and abstracts against the predefined inclusion criteria. The full texts of potentially relevant articles were then retrieved and assessed. The following data were extracted: (1) first author's name; (2) year of publication; (3) mean age of participants and sample size; (4) exercise intervention characteristics (modality, duration, frequency and intensity); (5) post-intervention values of metabolic parameters, including body mass index (BMI), fat percentage (FAT%), total cholesterol (TC), TG, systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP, DBP), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and FBG. For studies with multiple intervention groups, only data from the exercise intervention groups and the non-exercise control groups were included.

Classification of exercise interventions

To ensure consistency and reproducibility in the classification of exercise interventions, all included modalities were standardized according to type, intensity, format, and combination. Interventions were categorized into the following ten types: low-intensity exercise training, moderate-intensity exercise training, moderate-to-vigorous intensity exercise training, low-volume high-intensity interval training, high-volume high-intensity interval training, resistance training, combined aerobic and resistance training, combined high-volume HIIT and resistance training, yoga, and traditional Chinese exercise. Detailed definitions and quantitative criteria for each category are provided in Table S1.

Data harmonization

To ensure consistency across included studies, all laboratory outcomes were converted to International System of Units (SI units) prior to analysis. For studies reporting lipid and glucose concentrations in mass units (mg/dL), conversion to molar units (mmol/L) was performed according to standard clinical chemistry principles using the following formula:

Where M represents the molar mass of the substance. The specific values adopted are as follows: M = 386.66 g/mol for total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol; M = 885.43 g/mol for triglycerides; M = 180.16 g/mol for blood glucose.97,98 Calculations yield the corresponding practical conversion factors of 0.0259, 0.0113, and 0.0555.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias of the included randomized controlled trials was assessed using the Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool recommended by the Cochrane Collaboration. This tool evaluates six aspects of trial methodology: (1) the randomization process; (2) deviations from intended interventions; (3) missing outcome data; (4) measurement of the outcome; (5) selection of the reported result; and (6) the overall risk of bias. For each domain, the risk of bias is judged as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk.” Prior to the formal assessment, all researchers reached a consensus on the evaluation criteria through discussion. Subsequently, two researchers independently assessed the risk of bias for each included study. In cases of disagreement between the two researchers, a third researcher was invited to participate in the discussion until a consensus was reached.

Certainty of the evidence

We assessed the certainty of evidence for each network estimate using the CINeMA(Confidence In Network Meta-Analysis) framework.99,100 CINeMA evaluates six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. Judgments across domains are summarized into confidence ratings of high, moderate, low, or very low. Based on minimally important differences from clinical guidelines and prior meta-analyses, We evaluated imprecision in relation to predefined minimally important differences (MID) for each outcome: WC ≥2 cm reduction; BMI ≥1.0 kg/m2 reduction; FAT% ≥2% reduction; SBP ≥3 mmHg reduction; DBP ≥2 mmHg reduction; FBG ≥0.5 mmol/L reduction; TG ≥0.3 mmol/L reduction; TC ≥0.5 mmol/L reduction; LDL ≥0.3 mmol/L reduction; and HDL ≥0.1 mmol/L increase.3,101,102 For imprecision, we considered whether the 95% confidence intervals crossed these thresholds. For heterogeneity, we examined variability across studies and whether prediction intervals crossed the thresholds. The contribution matrix was used to weight judgments for within-study bias and indirectness.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata software (Version 17.0) and R software (version 4.5.2). Within a frequentist framework, we performed network meta-analyses (NMA) for all outcomes using Stata.103 For all continuous outcomes, we calculated mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals as MD preserves clinical interpretability given consistent measurement scales across studies104; for studies missing standard deviations of change, these were calculated using the Cochrane Handbook method assuming a correlation coefficient of 0.5, and data reported as medians and ranges were converted to means and SDs using established methods.105,106 We employed a random-effects model assuming a common between-study heterogeneity (τ) across all comparisons with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation,107 and for multi-arm trials, we accounted for within-study correlations by constructing a variance-covariance matrix assuming a correlation of 0.5 between comparisons sharing a common control.108,109 Network consistency was assessed globally using the design-by-treatment interaction model and locally using node-splitting (p < 0.05 indicating significant inconsistency),107,109 and treatment ranking probabilities were estimated using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), a higher SUCRA value closer to 100 indicated superiority, while a lower value closer to 0 indicated inferiority.103,110 Comparison-adjusted funnel plots and Egger's test used to assess small-study effects.111 Finally, we conducted pre-specified sensitivity analyses by excluding studies with an intervention duration of <12 weeks, a mean participant age of ≥60 years, and those in which the control group received structured health education, respectively.
