Clinical and modifier gene profiles in reversible infantile respiratory chain deficiency: three Chinese cases report and literature review
Authors: Tongyue Li, Chaolong Xu, Zhimei Liu, Yang Liu, Ying Zou, Zimeng He, Yunxi Zhang, Mingxi Sun, Hong Xue, Hua Li, Fang Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReversible infantile respiratory chain deficiency (RIRCD) is a rare mitochondrial myopathy caused by homoplasmic MT‑TE m.14674T>C/G variants, characterized by severe infantile onset followed by spontaneous recovery. Nuclear modifiers are thought to modulate its incomplete penetrance, but evidence for digenic inheritance remains limited. We aim to analyze the clinical, genetic, and prognostic features of RIRCD, explore the roles of nuclear modifiers and digenic inheritance.Case DescriptionWe report three Chinese RIRCD patients carrying the homoplasmic m.14674T>C variant. All presented with recurrent respiratory infections and feeding difficulties, with gradual developmental recovery after 1 year of age. Notably, we report a case of a homoplasmic m.14674T>C variant (maternal) and a pathogenic EARS2 heterozygous variant (paternal), indicating digenic inheritance of EARS2. Additionally, we report another case of a homoplasmic m.14674T>C variant and a maternally inherited CLCN4 variant associated with language delay and autistic features after recovery from RIRCD, suggesting the presence of additional modifiers or comorbidities. Among 52 published cases and our 3 new cases, 92.16% of cases had onset within 3 months, 85.55% had neuromuscular symptoms, 70.91% required nasogastric feeding, and 43.63% needed mechanical ventilation. Elevated lactate and creatine kinase levels, ragged-red fibers, and mitochondrial ultrastructural abnormalities in muscle biopsies were common pre-recovery. Respiratory chain deficiencies included isolated complex IV and combined I + IV defects. Prognosis was favorable (32.00% full recovery, 58.00% mild residual myopathy). Twenty-four cases (43.64%) had nuclear modifiers, including EARS2 (n=9) and TRMU (n=6).ConclusionsRIRCD is a rare mitochondrial myopathy caused by mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variants with penetrance regulated by nuclear modifiers. Digenic inheritance contributes to phenotypic heterogeneity, supporting precise diagnosis and future therapy.

Highlight box

Key findings

• We firstly report a unique reversible infantile respiratory chain deficiency (RIRCD) case which validates a paternal inherited likely pathogenic EARS2 frameshift variant as a critical nuclear modifier. Combined with the maternal homoplasmic MT-TE m.14674T>C variant, this digenic interaction triggers clinical RIRCD, explaining the incomplete penetrance of the sole mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutation.

• We report for the first time another unique RIRCD case with persistent language stagnation and autistic features attributed to a coexisting CLCN4 variant, greatly broadening the clinical presentation of RIRCD beyond classic reversible myopathy.

• Based on 55 genetically confirmed global RIRCD cases (including the three new Chinese cases), we systematically summarized clinical manifestations, auxiliary features, modifier gene distribution and stratified prognostic outcomes, establishing an updated genetic and phenotypic landscape of RIRCD.

What is known and what is new?

• RIRCD is caused by homoplasmic MT‑TE m.14674T>C/G variants, and incomplete penetrance of this mtDNA variant suggests the contribution of nuclear modifier genes, yet comprehensive evidence for digenic pathogenesis remains limited.

• Our findings underscore the critical role of the EARS2 variant as a nuclear modifier for RIRCD penetrance and highlight the necessity of long‑term follow‑up for affected individuals. Atypical post‑recovery clinical courses may be attributable to additional digenic pathogenic variants.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• For RIRCD, early diagnosis combined with active supportive care is critical for favorable patient outcomes. EARS2 serves as a key nuclear modifier for the penetrance of RIRCD. When clinical features cannot be fully explained by the primary genetic defect, clinicians should consider double pathogenic contributors which account for the observed phenotypic complexity.

Introduction

Reversible infantile respiratory chain deficiency [RIRCD; Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM): 500009] is a rare mitochondrial myopathy characterized by severe feeding difficulties, delayed motor development, and recurrent respiratory infections. Symptom onset typically occurs within the first month of life, followed by spontaneous recovery between 5 and 20 months of age (1,2). It was first described by DiMauro in 1983, initially named as “Benign reversible muscle cytochrome c oxidase (COX) deficiency” by Suzuki in 1989. Its genetic basis was established in 2009 with the discovery of the homoplasmic m.14674T>C variant in the mitochondrial MT-TE gene (1). In 2011, the disease was formally renamed as “RIRCD” (3).

Notably, only ~33% of individuals carrying the homoplasmic m.14674T>C variant develops symptoms, while others remain asymptomatic carriers, suggesting a role for genetic modifiers. In 2020, a pedigree analysis proposed that the EARS2 gene (HGNC 29419) acts as a pathogenic modifier gene, leading to RIRCD onset (4). At present, there are few studies describing cases of disease caused by nuclear modifier genes. Therefore, we report 3 Chinese RIRCD cases diagnosed at our center, analyze modifier gene contributions to pathogenesis, and present a systematic review of the clinical spectrum, outcomes, and genetic landscape. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0512/rc).

Case presentation

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval No. [2022]-E-121-Y). Written informed consent was obtained from legal guardians (parents of probands) prior to genetic testing and muscle biopsy. Children aged ≥8 years additionally provided personal assent. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

Patient 1

A 7-year-9-month-old female (Figure 1, family 1 III-4), the second child born at full term to healthy, non-consanguineous parents, with an unremarkable birth history (birth weight: 3.0 kg). She presented with feeding difficulties and dyspnea at 10 days old and was treated for pneumonia with antibiotics. Readmitted at 1.5 months for bronchopneumonia and malnutrition, she received short-term nasogastric feeding. She exhibited elevated levels of creatine kinase (CK: 307 U/L; reference: 26–192 U/L), alanine transaminase (ALT: 190 U/L; reference: 8–71 U/L), and aspartate transaminase (AST: 171 U/L; reference: 21–80 U/L); serum lactate, ammonia, homocysteine, and urine metabolic screening were normal.

Genogram of the three cases. The hollow circles indicate living unaffected female members; the hollow squares indicate living unaffected male members; the hollow circle with diagonal line indicates deceased female; the solid circle indicates living female patient; the solid squares indicate living male patients; and the arrows indicate proband.

She displayed notable motor development delay: achieving head control at 5 months, rolling over at 8 months, and independent sitting at 11 months. At 11 months, she was referred to our center due to persistent inability to stand independently and nasogastric feeding dependence. Physical examination revealed severe growth restriction (weight 7.5 kg, height 68 cm; both <3rd percentile), generalized hypotonia, reduced muscle strength, and hyporeflexia. Cognitive and language development were age-appropriate. Serum biochemistry markers had normalized, and auxiliary examinations including electromyography (EMG), cardiac, and cranial imaging were unremarkable. Family history revealed healthy parents and siblings, except for one maternal aunt (Figure 1, family 1 II-5) who died at 7 days old from neonatal feeding difficulties. Full-length mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) sequencing and trio-whole-exome sequencing (WES) were performed at 11 months. The mtDNA sequencing identified the homoplasmic MT-TE m.14674T>C variant in the proband’s blood. Initial WES revealed no pathogenic variants. Familial co-segregation analysis confirmed maternally inherited homoplasmy in blood and urine samples across asymptomatic matrilineal relatives. Muscle biopsy of the right biceps demonstrated variations in fiber size, with increased endomysial connective tissue on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Figure 2A, A1), diffuse ragged red fibers (RRFs) without rimmed vacuoles on modified Gomori trichrome (MGT) staining (Figure 2A, A2), and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH)-positive ragged-blue fibers (RBFs) without vascular abnormalities (Figure 2A, A3). Reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide tetrazolium reductase (NADH) staining revealed focal enzymatic hyperactivity, while oil red O (ORO) and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining revealed lipid accumulation and elevated glycogen levels, respectively (Figure 2A, A4–A6). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed myofiber degeneration with sarcomeric disarray, lipid accumulation, and collagen proliferation (Figure 2A, A7–A8). In contrast, muscle biopsy results from her 11-year-old brother were normal (Figure 2B, B1–B8). Respiratory chain enzyme studies were deferred due to the need for clinical stabilization.

Targeted re-analysis of WES data at 7 years old detected a paternally inherited EARS2 frameshift variant, c.427_428insTACCCCTGTTTCTCT (p.Ser143LeufsTer27), classified as likely pathogenic [American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG): strong pathogenic (PS)1 + moderate pathogenic (PM)1 + PM4], while the variant was absent in the mother and brother (Figure 3). No additional pathogenic or modifier variations were found in other genes.

Sanger sequencing charts of patient 1, her parents, and elder brother. The patient and her father (A,C) carried the heterozygous variant of c.427_428 insTACCCCTGTTTCTGCT/p.Ser143Leufs*27 in the EARS2 gene, while her mother and elder brother (B,D) did not carry this variant.

She was diagnosed with RIRCD and received long-term vitamin and coenzyme Q10 supplementation. She achieved independent standing and discontinued nasogastric feeding at 1.2 years, walking at 1.5 years, and started running/jumping at 2 years with mild weakness. At the last follow-up (7 years and 9 months), she measured 125 cm (25th percentile) and weighed 22 kg (10th–25th percentile), attended first grade with strong academic performance and participated in physical education, but was prone to fatigue.

Patient 2

A 1-year-10-month-old male (Figure 1, family 2, II-1) was born at full term by cesarean section (birth weight: 3.6 kg) to healthy, non-consanguineous parents. At 1.5 months, he was diagnosed with severe pneumonia and moderate malnutrition, laboratory evaluation revealed elevated CK (1,217 U/L), AST (243.9 U/L), lactate (8.5 mmol/L; reference: 0.5–2.2 mmol/L). Despite symptomatic treatment and nasogastric feeding, persistent respiratory distress necessitated transfer to our center, where he recovered after short-term mechanical ventilation; however, nasogastric feeding continued. Physical examination at 3 months showed malnutrition (height: 55 cm; weight: 4.15 kg, both <3rd percentile), hypotonia and reduced muscle strength. Serum lactate (2.6 mmol/L), AST (112 U/L), and CK (588 IU/L) had decreased. EMG, echocardiography, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and urine metabolic screening were normal. MtDNA analysis identified maternally inherited homoplasmic MT-TE m.14674T>C variant in blood. The initial WES was negative, and re-analysis revealed no pathogenic variants or modifier genes.

He received coenzyme Q10, vitamin supplementation and rehabilitation therapy. He discontinued nasogastric feeding at 5 months, verbalized “mama/dada” and could roll over at 9 months, achieved independent sitting at 11 months and walking at 14 months. By 9 months, his CK and AST levels normalized, though mild hypotonia persisted. At the last follow-up (1 year and 10 months), he had normal growth (82 cm, 11 kg), muscle strength and tone, and age-appropriate language development, though he fatigues easily and avoids frequent running and jumping.

Patient 3

A 5-year-old male (Figure 1, family 3 II-1) was born at 36 weeks by caesarean section due to severe hydronephrosis to healthy, non-consanguineous parents (birth weight: 3.1 kg). Postnatally, he suffered from multiple neonatal complications including pneumonia, hypoglycemia, jaundice, anemia and feeding difficulties, and required hospitalization. He was re-hospitalized at 2 months for severe pneumonia and respiratory insufficiency, treated with mechanical ventilation, with elevated lactate (9.82 mmol/L), AST (159 U/L), and CK (245 IU/L). At 7 months, he presented with recurrent infection, malnutrition (height: 62 cm; weight: 6.3 kg, both <3rd percentile), global developmental delay (inability to control head) and hypotonia. The AST, ALT, and CK levels normalized. Initial mtDNA analysis and trio WES at 7 months of age identified a maternally inherited homoplasmic MT-TE m.14674T>C variant. He achieved head control at 8 months, and independent walking at 18 months, with normal early language development and eye contact.

At 2 years and 8 months, his language development stagnated, with diminished spontaneous speech and loss of eye contact. Physical examination was normal without dysmorphic features or hypotonia. Cranial MRI/magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) was unremarkable. Cognitive and verbal assessments were both underdeveloped and the M-CHAT screening suggested autism spectrum disorder. Awake electroencephalogram revealed non-epileptiform abnormalities. WES data re-analysis prioritized the CLCN4 c.1172G>A variant, classified as likely pathogenic [ACMG: PM1 + PM5 + supporting pathogenic (PP)2 + PP3] and associated with Raynaud-Claes syndrome (OMIM: 300114, X-linked dominant) (5). This disease coincided with his resolved motor deficits but persistent language stagnation. Currently at age 5 years, he remained nonverbal with minimal peer interaction and restricted interests.

Discussion

A systematic search was conducted on Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science (up to Jun 2026), and case reports with m.14674T>C/G were included. After systematic screening, 11 publications (1-4,6-12) comprise 52 published cases and 3 novel cases presented in this study, totaling 55 genetically confirmed RIRCD patients from 43 families [25 males (42.37%), 29 females (52.72%), and 1 unknown (Figure 4A)]. Geographically, 21 cases (38.98%) originated from Europe, 15 (25.42%) from Asia, 5 (8.47%) from the United States, and 4 (6.78%) from Brazil, with 10 cases (16.95%) of unspecified Caucasian origin (Figure 4B).

Clinically, 92.16% patients (47/51) presented symptoms within the neonatal first three month, with a median age of 4 weeks. Initial presentations included feeding difficulties (n=22), hypotonia (n=12), dyspnea (n=11), developmental delay (n=7), and lethargy or impaired consciousness (n=3). Only one case presented at age 4 with gait instability (10). Disease progression led to skeletal muscle involvement (n=47, 85.55%), manifested as generalized/proximal limb weakness, hypotonia, mild motor developmental delay, and ptosis. Digestive system involvement occurred in 44 patients (80.00%), presenting as feeding difficulties, dysphagia, and vomiting. Twenty-six patients (47.27%) had respiratory involvement, manifested as recurrent respiratory infections, dyspnea and respiratory failure. Mechanical ventilation was required in 24 cases (43.63%) and nasal feeding/gastrostomy in 39 cases (70.91%) (Figure 4C).

Therapeutically, merely 12.73% (7/55) of patients received targeted pharmacological therapy (coenzyme Q10, carnitine, vitamins), while 87.27% (48/55) received supportive care alone. Prognostic data (n=50) revealed mean ages for recovery milestones: symptom resolution at 6.42±2.82 months, head control at 11.1±8.25 months, independent walking at 21.73±8.17 months, and age-equivalent development at 22.31±7.61 months. Outcomes included full recovery in 32.00% (n=16); mild residual myopathy in 58.00% (n=29), manifesting as exercise intolerance, subtle weakness, and ptosis. Two fatal cases due to respiratory failure at 39 days and 3 months post-onset (1,11), and three cases had poor prognosis (appendix available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tp-2026-0512-1.xlsx).

Laboratory tests revealed elevated blood lactate in 85.37% (35/41) cases (ranging 2.27–13.44 mmol/L), elevated cerebrospinal fluid lactate in 87.50% (7/8) cases and elevated CK in 69.44% (25/36) (ranging 203–1,402 U/L). Transient ALT and AST elevations were noted in 9 patients, while 5 showed evidence of myocardial damage (Figure 4D). Cranial MRI abnormalities were extremely rare, detected only in 14.29% cases (2/14) with basal ganglia hyperintensities. Muscle biopsies (median age 3 months) demonstrated RRFs in 91.67% cases (33/36), lipid/glycogen accumulation in 88.89% cases (32/36), and COX-negative fibers in 33.33% cases (12/36), and degenerated mitochondria on TEM in 50.00% cases (18/36). Post-recovery biopsies (median age 5 years) showed minimal residual RRF/fiber size variation in 64.29% cases (9/14), normal histology in 35.71% cases (5/14). Respiratory chain enzymatic testing showed either isolated complex IV deficiency (48.00%, 12/25 cases) or combined complex I/IV deficiency (52.00%, 13/25 cases). REC normalized during post-recovery in all cases (n=5).

Genetically, 96.36% of patients harbored the homoplasmic MT-TE m.14674T>C variant, and two cases carried m.14674T>G. All cases were homoplasmic in blood samples, only 3 cases did not specify variant proportions. Maternal homoplasmic m.14674T>G variand in 97.83% cases (45/46), and only 15.38% (6/39) exhibited childhood recurrent respiratory infections/malnutrition and mild exercise intolerance in adulthood. Nuclear modifier gene screening performed in 61.81% cases (34/55) identified pathogenic heterozygous variants in 43.64% (24/55) patients: EARS2 (n=9, 37.50%), TRMU (n=6, 25.00%), other variant cases are detailed in Figure 4E.

This study presents three single-center cases of RIRCD. Genetic re-analysis revealed a pathogenic heterozygous EARS2 variant as a modifier in patient 1, and a CLCN4 variant contributing to language delay in patient 3. Combined with a systematic review of all 55 previously reported cases, we further characterized the clinical spectrum, modifier genes, and prognostic profiles of RIRCD.

RIRCD is an ultra-rare mitochondrial disorder, most patients present in the neonatal period (~60% within the first month) with feeding intolerance, respiratory distress, hypotonia, and motor delay. Severe manifestations are common: 44% cases required mechanical ventilation and 71% required nasogastric feeding. Most children achieve spontaneous clinical recovery at approximately 6 months of age, with normalized lactate and CK levels, respiratory chain activity, and muscle pathology. However, longitudinal mtDNA copy number and gene expression before and after recovery remain unknown. Compensatory mtDNA copy number increases observed in Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (13) suggests a potential therapeutic target, though underlying regulatory mechanisms require further elucidation.

The incomplete penetrance of the homoplasmic MT-TE m.14674T>C variant strongly suggests that nuclear modifier genes modulate disease expression (4). Our patient 1 harbored the maternal homoplasmic m.14674T>C variant and a paternal pathogenic heterozygous EARS2 variant, while her unaffected brother carried only the maternal mtDNA variant, supporting EARS2 as a modifier, indicating digenic inheritance. Our review identified EARS2 variants in 37.5% cases of 55 RIRCD patients. As a member of the aminoacyl-transfer RNA (tRNA) synthetase (aaRS) family, pathogenic EARS2 heterozygous variants reduces mRNA levels and impair respiratory chain activity (4,14). The aaRS enzymes catalyze tRNA aminoacylation. Heterozygous mutations partially impair enzymes functions, reducing efficiency. When these nuclear variants are combined with mt-tRNA defects (e.g., the MT-TE gene encodes tRNAGlu), protein synthesis is further compromised, exceeding the pathogenic threshold (4,14). Similarly, YARS2 variants can increase the penetrance of LHON caused by mtDNA variants (15), and modulating LARS2/HARS2 expression rescues mt-tRNA mutation-induced dysfunction (16), suggesting the therapeutic potential of aaRS-targeted interventions. Additionally, TRMU variants were detected in 25% of cases as another key modifier. Meng et al. demonstrated that TRMU variants increase disease risk in LHON carriers of m.1555A>G (17). The modifying functional roles of other candidate modifiers (e.g., MTO1, QRSL1, GOT2, GLS, MSS51) (4), which act in glutamyl-tRNA modification and glutamate metabolism remain unvalidated in RIRCD.

Suboptimal nuclear genetic analysis in many published cases has hindered the identification of novel modifier genes (14). Notably, patient 3 exhibited unprecedented language stagnation typical of RIRCD, which was likely attributable to a maternally inherited pathogenic CLCN4 variant associated with neurodevelopmental disorders (5). This case highlights the phenotypic heterogeneity of RIRCD driven by digenic inheritance. The residual low penetrance of the primary mtDNA variant may also stem from undiscovered protective or pathogenic variants, epigenetic modifications, and polygenic interactions, which can be clarified via multiomics and artificial intelligence (AI)-based analytical strategies (18).

This study has limitations, including small sample size and incomplete literature data, which may introduce bias. Key knowledge gaps persist regarding the molecular basis of mtDNA copy number changes, mechanisms underlying reversible muscle pathology, and identification of additional modifier genes in RIRCD.

Conclusions

RIRCD is a rare mitochondrial myopathy caused by homoplasmic MT-TE m.14674T>C/G variants. Early diagnosis and aggressive supportive care are essential to optimize outcomes. EARS2 acts as an important nuclear modifier gene influencing disease penetrance. When clinical features cannot be fully explained by the primary genetic defect, clinicians should consider dual inherited pathogenic contributors, which may yield a double diagnosis and account for the observed phenotypic complexity (14).
