Construction of a predictive nomogram for ICU mortality in patients with cardiac arrest using the MIMIC-IV database and the eICU Collaborative Research Database
Authors: Wei Shi, Xuemei Hu, Hui Li, Qihui Huang, Dian Zhou, Min Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac arrest (CA) is a critical clinical event associated with extremely high mortality and long-term disability. This study aimed to identify risk factors associated with ICU mortality and develop a practical nomogram for risk prediction to support prognostic assessment and risk stratification among patients with CA.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted using two large public critical care databases. The MIMIC-IV database was used as the training cohort for model development, while the eICU-CRD database served as the external validation cohort. Variables were initially screened using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and a predictive nomogram was subsequently established using binary logistic regression analysis. The discriminative performance of the model was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Calibration was assessed using calibration curves and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Clinical applicability and net benefit were further analyzed using decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsA total of 3,029 patients from the MIMIC-IV database and 2,805 patients from the eICU-CRD database were enrolled in this study. After variable selection by LASSO regression, eight predictors were included in the nomogram. The model was compared with conventional ICU scoring systems, including SAPS II, SOFA, and CCI. In the training cohort, the AUROC values were 0.802 for the nomogram, 0.725 for SAPS II, 0.709 for SOFA, and 0.509 for CCI. In the external validation cohort, the corresponding AUROC values were 0.733, 0.596, 0.701, and 0.517, respectively. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test yielded P-values of 0.976 in the training set and 0.905 in the validation set, indicating favorable goodness-of-fit and satisfactory calibration. Moreover, DCA demonstrated that the nomogram provided greater net benefit compared with traditional scoring systems.ConclusionThe established nomogram for predicting ICU mortality in patients with CA demonstrated acceptable predictive performance and calibration. As a quantitative risk stratification tool, it may provide clinicians with useful individualized prognostic information for this vulnerable patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06235-4.Keywords: Cardiac arrest, ICU mortality, Nomogram, MIMIC-IV database, eICU-CRD

Introduction

Cardiac arrest (CA) is one of the most lethal medical emergencies and is defined as the abrupt cessation of effective cardiac mechanical activity, with a consequent loss of detectable systemic circulation [1]. CA imposes a substantial global public health burden; annual incidence of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) alone exceeds 350,000 in the United States [2]. Despite advances in resuscitation strategies, survival remains poor, with survival-to-discharge rates of approximately 10% for OHCA and 25% for in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) [3, 4]. Post-cardiac arrest syndrome (PCAS) involves a complex ischemia-reperfusion cascade affecting multiple organ systems, including hypoxic-ischemic brain injury, myocardial dysfunction, and systemic inflammation [5]. Following return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), successfully resuscitated patients remain vulnerable to post-CA complications during ICU care. Notably, in-hospital mortality remains high among CA patients admitted to the ICU, with approximately 50–60% dying during hospitalization [6, 7]. Early identification of patients at high risk of ICU mortality may support timely risk stratification, prognostic assessment, and clinical decision-making.

Several general severity scoring systems are widely used for risk stratification in critically ill patients. The Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II are commonly employed for mortality prediction in the ICU [8, 9]. However, these general severity scores were originally developed and validated in heterogeneous ICU populations, rather than specifically in patients with CA. Conventional severity scores demonstrate only moderate discrimination in post-CA populations. For example, in patients undergoing targeted temperature management, SAPS II, SOFA, and APACHE II achieved AUROC values of 0.686, 0.641, and 0.715, respectively, for mortality prediction [10]. Similarly, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), which quantifies pre-existing comorbidities, shows limited predictive accuracy in acute critical illness [11]. These findings suggest that disease-specific prognostic models may be needed to improve risk prediction in patients with CA.

Nomograms serve as useful tools for individualized risk prediction in clinical practice. By integrating multiple prognostic variables into an accessible format, nomograms enable estimation of individual patient probabilities in clinical settings [12, 13]. Unlike conventional scoring systems that rely on categorical risk stratification, nomograms provide continuous probability estimates, facilitating individualized prognostic assessment. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression is particularly suited for developing parsimonious predictive models [14]. This method simultaneously performs variable selection and regularization, addressing multicollinearity among candidate predictors while mitigating overfitting. These properties make LASSO regression advantageous for clinical prediction model development, as it helps identify the most predictive variables while preserving interpretability and generalizability [15]. Recent studies have combined LASSO regression with logistic regression to develop nomogram-based models for predicting ICU mortality across diverse critically ill populations [16, 17].

CA confers a high risk of ICU mortality, yet few prediction models have been developed and validated specifically for this endpoint. Existing prognostic models for this population predominantly address in-hospital mortality or neurological outcomes, with limited attention to ICU mortality [18, 19]. Additionally, many rely on variables that are not routinely available during early ICU care or require complex calculations, restricting clinical applicability. To address these limitations, we developed and externally validated a nomogram for predicting ICU mortality in patients with CA based on clinical variables collected during the first 24 h after ICU admission.

Materials and methods

Data sources

This study was designed as a retrospective cohort study, with data derived from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database (version 3.0). The dataset includes patient admission records spanning 2020 to 2022 as an extension of the existing records included in version 2.2; access to MIMIC-IV v3.0 was obtained via PhysioNet (physionet.org). As a well-established, publicly available clinical repository, the MIMIC-IV database stores de-identified medical data linked to more than 40,000 ICU admissions at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts [20]. In addition, the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD, version 2.0) was incorporated as an independent external validation cohort. This multicenter critical care telemedicine database is widely used in intensive care research and contains de-identified clinical records for over 200,000 ICU admissions across multiple medical centers in the United States. The first author, Xuemei Hu, completed the mandatory “Data or Specimens Only Research” training course and obtained formal authorization for public data usage (authorization ID: 13677449). Given that this study was performed using fully anonymized public databases in strict compliance with institutional review board guidelines, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived.

Population selection criteria

Inclusion criteria: Patients were enrolled only if they met the standardized diagnostic criteria for CA, defined as the complete cessation of cardiac activity with three core clinical features: unresponsiveness to external stimuli, absence of normal spontaneous breathing, and lack of discernible circulatory signs [21]. The specific International Classification of Diseases codes used for disease data extraction are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) failure to meet the aforementioned diagnostic criteria for CA; (2) not the first ICU admission or an ICU length of stay of less than 24 h; (3) age under 18 years (pediatric population excluded); (4) pregnancy or postpartum status; (5) diagnosis of neonatal CA; (6) incomplete, missing, or inaccessible medical records or other key clinical data.

Data extraction and management

Relevant clinical variables were extracted from both the MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD databases, covering six core categories: (1) baseline demographic characteristics of patients; (2) medical history and comorbidities; (3) disease severity assessment scores, including the SAPS II, SOFA, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI); (4) mean values of vital signs measured during the first 24 h after ICU admission and the worst values of biochemical indicators recorded during the same period; (5) therapeutic interventions administered following ICU admission; and (6) the primary clinical endpoint, namely, ICU mortality. All clinical variables used for model development were collected during the first 24 h after ICU admission.

For the MIMIC-IV derivation cohort, variables with a missing rate exceeding 20% were excluded prior to further analysis. Data cleaning and missing value imputation for the remaining eligible variables were conducted using R software (version 4.2.2). Missing data were processed via multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) combined with a random forest algorithm, generating a total of five independent imputed datasets. LASSO regression was performed on each imputed dataset separately to screen predictive variables, and consistent results were obtained across all five datasets (eight identical predictors were retained in every model). Accordingly, one dataset was randomly selected from the five imputed versions for subsequent model construction and external validation.

Model performance was evaluated in the MIMIC-IV derivation cohort and externally validated in the eICU-CRD cohort. The predictive performance and potential clinical applicability of the constructed model were further evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Details are provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and visualizations in this study were performed using R software (version 4.2.2), with the specific software packages used for the analyses listed in Supplementary Table S2. For between-group comparisons, categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) and compared using the independent samples t-test; non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All between-group comparative results were reported using P-values. Model performance was evaluated in the MIMIC-IV derivation cohort and externally validated in the eICU-CRD cohort. The predictive performance and potential clinical applicability of the constructed model were further evaluated using ROC curve analysis, calibration curves, and DCA.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 3,029 patients with CA were identified from the MIMIC-IV database, and 2,805 patients with CA were included from the eICU-CRD database, as detailed in Fig. 1. Enrolled patients were stratified into survival and non-survival groups according to the ICU mortality status. The overall ICU mortality rates in the MIMIC-IV cohort and eICU-CRD cohort were 25.95% and 32.69%, respectively.

Flow chart of patient selection

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the included patients, stratified by ICU mortality status, are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Variable correlation analysis and heatmap

The MIMIC-IV dataset was utilized as the training cohort for model construction. Correlation matrix analysis and significance testing of correlations were performed for all candidate variables among the 3,029 patients in the training cohort, and the corresponding correlation heatmap was generated (Supplementary Figure S2). The heatmap revealed potential multicollinearity among the candidate variables, indicating that these raw variables were not suitable for direct incorporation into the logistic regression model.

Predictor variable screening via LASSO regression

Given the potential multicollinearity among candidate variables observed in the correlation heatmap, LASSO regression was conducted to screen the optimal predictors for constructing the prognostic model for patients with CA. LASSO regression was used for variable selection; model stability and optimal parameter tuning were assessed using cross-validated mean squared error (Fig. 2A), while the dynamic variable coefficient trajectories during the screening process were visualized using a coefficient plot (Fig. 2B). The optimal λ value (0.015) was identified according to the one-standard-error criterion, and the corresponding predictor variables were selected for model construction.

LASSO regression screening of predictor variables for the prognostic model. A Selection of the optimal λ in the LASSO model; B LASSO coefficient profiles

Development of the prognostic nomogram

A prognostic prediction model was established using the optimal variables screened by LASSO regression, with a total of eight indicators ultimately incorporated: age, white blood cell count (WBC), arterial oxygen saturation (SpO₂), respiratory rate, temperature, anion gap, bicarbonate, and mechanical ventilation. A corresponding nomogram was constructed to facilitate individualized risk estimation (Fig. 3). Specifically, the individual point score for each variable in a given patient can be derived from the “Points” scale in the nomogram; the sum of these individual scores yields the “Total Points,” and the corresponding “ICU Mortality” value on the nomogram represents the predicted probability of ICU mortality for that patient. To evaluate the goodness of fit and model selection, we calculated the values of the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC), as detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

Nomogram for predicting ICU mortality in patients after cardiac arrest. For each predictor, a vertical line is drawn upward to the “Points” scale to obtain the corresponding point value. The sum of all individual points yields the “Total Points,” which corresponds to the predicted probability of ICU mortality

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

Multivariable logistic regression was applied to the eight candidate predictors, which confirmed age, WBC, SpO₂, respiratory rate, temperature, anion gap, bicarbonate, and mechanical ventilation as independent predictors associated with ICU mortality in patients with CA. To further detect potential residual multicollinearity among the independent variables included in the final model, the variance inflation factor (VIF) was employed as a diagnostic metric. The detailed VIF test results are presented in Supplementary Table S5.

Discrimination, calibration, and evaluation

To ensure reproducibility, the MIMIC-IV database was employed as the training cohort to construct the predictive model, while the eICU database served as the independent validation cohort. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the developed nomogram exhibited good discriminative performance. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) in the training cohort was 0.802 (95% CI: 0.784–0.820), and the AUROC in the validation cohort was 0.733 (95% CI: 0.714–0.753). These values were higher than those of the SAPS II score (AUCs: 0.725 and 0.596, respectively), CCI score (AUCs: 0.509 and 0.517, respectively), and SOFA score (AUCs: 0.709 and 0.701, respectively), suggesting that the nomogram had better discriminative ability for ICU mortality risk stratification.

Receiver operating characteristic curves for the nomogram, SAPS II, SOFA, and CCI. A MIMIC-IV cohort; B eICU-CRD cohort

Calibration analysis revealed good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes in both cohorts, as reflected by the calibration curves (Fig. 5). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test further supported this consistency, yielding non-significant results (MIMIC-IV: P = 0.976; eICU-CRD: P = 0.905), indicating no significant evidence of poor fit. These findings suggest that the nomogram showed satisfactory calibration for individualized risk estimation.

Calibration curves. A MIMIC-IV cohort; B eICU-CRD cohort

DCA was performed to evaluate the potential clinical applicability of the model beyond statistical metrics. As depicted in Fig. 6, when the threshold probability exceeded 0.3 (corresponding to a 30% predicted risk of ICU mortality, a clinically relevant cutoff), the decision curves of both cohorts remained above the “treat all” and “treat none” baselines, indicating a positive net benefit. Within this threshold range, the nomogram provided greater net benefit than the two default strategies, supporting its potential value for risk stratification in patients with CA.

Decision curve analysis of the nomogram, SAPS II, SOFA, and CCI. A MIMIC-IV cohort; B eICU-CRD cohort

Discussion

We developed and externally validated an eight-variable nomogram to predict ICU mortality in post-CA patients. The model achieved AUROCs of 0.802 and 0.733 in the training and external validation cohorts, respectively, showing higher discriminative performance than conventional ICU severity scores (SAPS II, SOFA, and CCI) in both populations. Calibration analysis demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities (Hosmer–Lemeshow test P = 0.976 and P = 0.905, respectively). Decision curve analysis indicated greater net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities compared with traditional scoring systems. These findings suggest that this nomogram may provide useful prognostic information for risk stratification in CA patients admitted to the ICU, based on first-day ICU data.

The nomogram variables reflect two complementary pathophysiological dimensions: baseline physiological reserve and systemic inflammatory response. Age, white blood cell count, and body temperature characterize these domains. Advanced age was independently associated with ICU mortality, likely reflecting reduced cardiovascular compliance, impaired mitochondrial function, and increased comorbidity burden that may reduce tolerance to ischemia-reperfusion injury [22, 23]. Elevated white blood cell counts indicate systemic inflammation following CA, reflecting tissue injury and immune activation [24]. Moderate leukocytosis following ROSC may represent a physiological stress response; however, extreme values, either high or low, may be associated with poor outcomes. Notably, in patients receiving targeted temperature management, white blood cell counts below 12.7 × 103/µL on day 3 post-ROSC predicted favorable neurological outcomes [25]. Temperature abnormalities may reflect central thermoregulatory dysfunction secondary to hypoxic brain injury or infectious complications (e.g., aspiration or ventilator-associated pneumonia) [26]. Temperatures exceeding 38.7 °C have been associated with poor prognosis. However, recent randomized controlled trials demonstrate that therapeutic hypothermia benefits primarily patients with initial non-shockable rhythms, suggesting that temperature management may require individualization based on clinical characteristics [27].

The second set comprises arterial oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, mechanical ventilation status, anion gap, and bicarbonate, which characterize acute respiratory dysfunction and metabolic derangement. These indicators may reflect key pathophysiological processes involved in PCAS. Hypoxemia and respiratory abnormalities may indicate aspiration, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), or neurogenic pulmonary edema [28]. Mechanical ventilation may indicate more severe respiratory compromise and may also reflect impaired respiratory drive related to neurological injury [29]. Anion gap and bicarbonate are markers of tissue hypoperfusion and metabolic acidosis. Anaerobic metabolism during the no-flow phase of CA and persistent tissue hypoperfusion following ROSC may contribute to metabolic acidosis [30]. An anion gap elevation above 20 mmol/L has been reported to be associated with in-hospital mortality (odds ratio 1.559), whereas reduced bicarbonate may indicate impaired buffering capacity and progressive acid-base deterioration [31]. These two variable sets are complementary. The first reflects baseline physiological reserve and systemic inflammatory status; the second captures acute organ dysfunction severity. Respiratory dysfunction and metabolic acidosis may interact with each other during PCAS: hypoxia exacerbates anaerobic metabolism, increasing lactate accumulation and anion gap, while systemic inflammation increases pulmonary capillary permeability, accelerating ARDS progression. Derangements in body temperature, oxygenation, and acid–base balance are closely associated with increased post-CA mortality risk and should be interpreted as markers of disease severity rather than established therapeutic targets. However, the present prediction model identifies associations only. The causal effects and potential therapeutic benefits of correcting these physiological abnormalities remain to be verified in future prospective or interventional studies.

Disease-specific predictive models may provide better performance than general severity scores in specific patient populations. Chen et al. developed a nomogram for ICU mortality in sepsis-associated encephalopathy, achieving AUROCs of 0.832 and 0.825 in the training and validation cohorts, respectively [16]. Similarly, a nomogram for in-hospital mortality in trauma ICU patients showed better predictive performance than traditional scoring systems [17]. Our nomogram achieved AUROCs of 0.802 in the training cohort and 0.733 in the validation cohort, which were broadly comparable to those of previously reported CA mortality prediction models. Ni et al. reported an AUROC of 0.86 using CatBoost for CA outcome prediction [19]; however, that model used data at 72 h following ROSC, whereas our nomogram uses clinical variables collected during the first 24 h after ICU admission, supporting risk stratification based on first-day ICU data. A random forest model for ICU mortality in CA achieved an AUROC of 0.83 [18], similar to the performance observed in our training cohort. Our nomogram showed higher discriminative performance than established critical illness scores, including SAPS II, SOFA, and CCI. This finding is consistent with previous observations that general scoring systems have limited applicability in specific disease settings. Although SAPS II and SOFA facilitate general ICU risk stratification, they were not developed specifically for patients with CA and do not fully capture the distinct pathophysiology of PCAS.

The present study adopted a combined variable extraction strategy: 24-hour mean vital signs and 24-hour peak or worst laboratory values during the first day after ICU admission. This approach was selected after consideration of PCAS pathophysiology and clinical feasibility. Transient single-time-point measurements, such as values obtained within 0–1 h after ICU admission, are vulnerable to confounding by emergent resuscitation, early therapeutic adjustments, and incomplete monitoring data, and may fail to capture progressive early organ injury. Although averaging vital signs may smooth isolated brief physiological extremes, sustained physiological instability, which is clinically relevant after ROSC, can be reflected by 24-hour average metrics, whereas isolated short-lived derangements may resolve with supportive care and have limited prognostic value. For intermittently measured laboratory biomarkers, which often have limited repeated sampling, the worst value may better capture peak ischemia-reperfusion injury and metabolic derangement. Complex trend-based variables were not adopted because they require relatively standardized testing schedules that are difficult to implement consistently across routine ICUs, which may reduce the clinical feasibility of a simple nomogram for risk stratification based on first-day ICU data. Future head-to-head comparative analyses are needed to further determine how different variable definitions, including initial, mean, worst, and trend-based values, influence model performance.

The nomogram incorporates eight variables routinely collected during the first 24 h after ICU admission, supporting risk stratification based on first-day ICU data. This model may help identify patients at higher risk of ICU mortality and provide additional prognostic information for clinical assessment and communication. The visual format permits direct probability estimation without complex calculations, which may facilitate practical use in clinical settings. Decision curve analysis demonstrated positive net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities, particularly when the threshold probability exceeded 0.3. These findings suggest that the nomogram may have potential value in assisting prognostic assessment and risk stratification, rather than directly determining treatment intensity or clinical outcomes. External validation in the eICU-CRD Collaborative Research Database, a multicenter database from the United States, demonstrated the model’s performance beyond the single-center MIMIC-IV cohort. Although the validation AUROC was lower than the training AUROC, 0.733 versus 0.802, this decrease is expected in external validation and suggests acceptable generalizability. The relatively consistent discrimination across cohorts supports the potential applicability of the nomogram in different ICU populations. Although the nomogram provides individualized risk estimates based on selected predictors, these variables should be interpreted primarily as markers of disease severity rather than confirmed therapeutic targets. Given the retrospective observational design of this study, the observed associations do not imply that direct correction of these abnormalities would necessarily improve clinical outcomes. Dynamic physiological and biochemical changes, such as lactate clearance, temperature trends, oxygenation status, and ventilatory parameters over time, may provide additional prognostic information in post-CA patients. Therefore, future models should consider incorporating longitudinal or time-series variables to improve dynamic risk stratification and clinical applicability.

Strengths and limitations

Predictive models may assist clinicians in identifying patients at high risk of adverse outcomes and provide useful information for prognostic assessment and clinical communication. Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the present model was developed and externally validated using retrospective data from public critical care databases. Because this study was based on observational cohorts, it can only identify statistical associations between predictors and ICU mortality and cannot establish causal relationships. In addition, the model has not yet undergone prospective validation in real-world clinical cohorts or interventional settings. Therefore, its actual clinical applicability and guiding value require further confirmation. Second, although a total of 3,029 and 2,805 patients with CA were enrolled from the single-center MIMIC-IV database and the multicenter eICU-CRD database, respectively, both databases primarily reflect US critical care settings, with limited representation of Asian and other ethnic groups. Consequently, generalizability of the model to other regions, ethnicities, and healthcare systems remains uncertain. Finally, this study primarily focused on ICU mortality and did not evaluate long-term outcomes including long-term survival, neurological prognosis, quality of life, or hospital readmission. Thus, the broader clinical value of the model warrants further exploration and extension.

Conclusion

In the present study, we developed and externally validated a predictive nomogram for in-ICU mortality in patients with CA. The nomogram demonstrated acceptable predictive performance and may provide useful prognostic information for risk stratification and clinical assessment.
