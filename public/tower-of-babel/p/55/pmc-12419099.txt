Raining on the WSI interoperability parade – incorrect assertions with respect to DICOM and fur coats in the summertime
Authors: David A Clunie
Published: 2025

I read with interest Peter Gershkovich's (henceforth, “the author's”) thoughtful and well-researched, but fundamentally flawed, oration1 against using DICOM for interoperable whole slide imaging. It unfortunately leaves the reader with the impression that theoretical architectural purity is more important than effectively leveraging a cross-specialty interoperability standard that has served us well for more than thirty years in fields like radiology, and is increasingly being adopted for all enterprise imaging,2 including pathology.3 I completely reject the assertion that by “embedding metadata, annotations, and communication protocols into a unified container, DICOM limits interoperability and exposes architectural vulnerabilities”. On the contrary, DICOM provides a single comprehensive standard for images with tile-level access, metadata, annotations and communication that leverages other off-the-shelf standards when appropriate, as well as achieving commonality with other imaging modalities, while providing the appropriate level of medical and pathology application specificity in a potentially secure manner. No alternative standard, or composition of multiple unrelated standards, is necessary.

It is undeniable that if medical digital imaging were a greenfield, the standard would be defined differently. However, it is not. It is impractical to ignore the success and huge installed base of mature enterprise-wide solutions based on DICOM, which though initially targeted for radiology applications are in many cases capable of supporting any modality, including WSI, with the addition of the appropriate viewer. Ignoring DICOM would be like ignoring JPEG, or power plugs. Whilst the author may state that the field of pathology offers a “clean slate … free from legacy constraints”, nothing could be further from the truth; at least not if one wants to leverage the practical experience, tools and infrastructure that have been under development for more than forty years since the need for standards in this field was recognized.4

I wholeheartedly agree with the author that it is vital to pro-actively address architectural cybersecurity risks. It is not helpful though, to attribute practical significance to deployment features of implementations of the standard that are a reflection of poor local security practice and lack of customer demand, rather than fundamental flaws “inherent to the standard”. Can DICOM be implemented and deployed in an unsecure manner? Certainly, so can any standard. Does it need to be so? Absolutely not, since the standard accommodates security at rest and in transit, which the author acknowledges. As a case in point, just as web sites were once typically visited using unsecure HTTP protocols, nowadays the use of TLS (HTTPS)5 is routine for significant transactions. The same can be done with both DICOM (whether using TLS or VPNs) and DICOMweb (the latter using the same HTTPS protocol as web browsers). Indeed, such measures are not “embedded within the DICOM standard itself” as the author asserts. It is DICOM's avowed goal to make use of other standards and architectures as espoused by experts such as those at NIST. Given the prevalence of insider threats, the principles of Zero Trust,6 which are now recognized by security professionals to be as vital onsite as they are for over-the-Internet transactions, are equally applicable to deployment of DICOM, even on a nominally physically isolated local network. Audit trails, and the review thereof, are also critical in this respect. Unsecured ports on servers should obviously never be left exposed to the outside world, regardless of the protocol, “legacy” or otherwise; this is as applicable to poor deployments of DICOM as to any other standard (such as those used in actual reported, rather than hypothetical, breaches).

It is indeed vital to critically evaluate the pros and cons of combining metadata with pixel data for interchange. As the author describes, this has consequences for data consistency, as well as for de-identification in a research or teaching context. As with any approach, whether using persistent objects or granular microdata, achieving distributed consistency is challenging. This is more a reflection of the inherent complexity of the problem of controlled, safe, authorized updates, rather than the manner of interchange of objects across a well-defined interoperability boundary. More fine-grained solutions like FHIR are hardly a panacea in this respect, and require as much control, if not more. There are robust solutions that the author already cited7 for addressing de-identification concerns; pathology is little different from radiology in this respect.8 The benefits of detachable self-contained sets of data that are fully self-describing has long been demonstrated in the radiology world, especially when there is a need for cross-organizational data sharing for clinical care continuity. Though new AI approaches are often primarily focused on the pixel data alone, and convenient access is important, increasingly metadata also places a critical role, whether for assessing generalizability, or for improving prediction, so it needs to be standardized and harmonized.

It is also important to recognize that DICOM only defines interchange across specific interoperability boundaries. It does not mandate the internal architecture of an implementation, which may use completely different data structures appropriate to the task. For this reason, the addition of DICOM interfaces to libraries that read from and write to other architectures is strongly encouraged. I note in passing that Bio-Formats, which the author mentions, already supports reading and writing DICOM WSI. The author's suggested emphasis on microservices is not necessarily practical for industrialized solutions that combine products from multiple vendors on a large scale. It also needs to be understood that the boundary at which DICOM has been most successful in radiology, between the scanner and the archive, is perhaps not one that is amenable to or benefits from microservices. Nor is that interface enhanced by the use of proprietary formats and protocols rather than a standard like DICOM, regardless of what may happen downstream once the images have been successfully acquired, ingested, archived and distributed, even if some (though not all) of those proprietary formats are nominally TIFF-based.

To claim that DICOM interoperability is “elusive” completely fails to recognize the current state of the art. Modern radiology and cardiology PACS are essentially commoditized today, largely as a consequence of universal adoption of DICOM for image interchange, regardless of its perceived limitations.9 Scanners (acquisition devices) and archives (image storage and management devices), and more recently viewers and analysis tools, are effectively “plug-and-play” for most routine practical clinical purposes. I fully expect that the same will be true of DICOM WSI as the field matures, just as will be the case for data from other specialties with exotic characteristics, as they gradually come on board, such as the neurophysiology waveforms mentioned by the author as a counter-example.10 DICOM Conformance Statements are critical in this respect. They allow for documentation of those specialty-specific features that are supported by a device, while at the same time leveraging the essential underlying commonality that is fundamental to enterprise-wide cross-specialty solutions. These documents are not a reflection of lack of interoperability, quite the converse. They allow customers to make well-informed decisions to mix-and-match appropriate devices. For example, using the Conformance Statements, a decision-maker can match the need to support WSI from a scanner with a viewer from different suppliers, even though they might leverage an archive in between that supports every conceivable DICOM-defined imaging modality.

I agree with the author that it is absolutely necessary to leverage cross-industry standards for interoperability of functionality, where appropriate. To this end DICOM goes out of its way to make use of TCP/IP, JPEG, TLS, HTTP, SNOMED CT and so on, rather than reinventing the wheel, even going so far as to support dual-personality TIFF-DICOM files.11 Since the clinical use of images has medically-specific requirements though, there is a need to combine medical and specialty-specific metadata with image data. This is what DICOM has excelled at, and is a strength, not a weakness. Pathology can be expected to be no different in this respect, just as many other “‘ologies” are beginning to appreciate.12

Allusions to lack of versioning of the standard itself, and the potential impact of breaking changes, reflect a failure to appreciate the extremely controlled process that allows for extension to support new features without diminishing backward compatibility. DICOM maintainers regard this as critical to success. The decision not to use versioned APIs is a conscious one, to prevent incompatibility at the object level between old and new implementations, without prohibiting the addition of new objects and data elements. As an active participant in DICOM's development, most recently for AI and WSI annotations, I understand how important it is to extend the standard with suitable new modular features for specific-use cases. This can be achieved without abandoning the underlying infrastructure for the sake of something pretty, shiny and new (and likely transient, given the history of bleeding-edge IT APIs).

Modularity of images and annotations is indeed vital. It is for this reason that DICOM emphasizes that these be kept separate, and provides mechanisms for encoding and distributing them individually, contrary to what the author asserts. The design for supporting very large numbers of graphical annotations for WSI13 reflects a desire to be compatible with simple data structures like those in GeoJSON, without forcing recipients to handle the significant but unnecessary complexity of extremely flexible structures such as might be present in SVG. Beyond mere vector graphics, the DICOM annotation feature emphasizes the importance of semantic precision, as well as the persistence of identifying and descriptive clinically and scientifically relevant metadata.

In short, while the paper's author is to be commended for drawing attention to important architectural challenges, particularly for future AI applications, there is no need to “throw the baby out with the bathwater”. I suggest that as Gershkovich desires, DICOM is already a “truly robust standard [with] strong governance, precise versioning, backward compatibility, and a well-defined functional scope that is essential for consistent development”. In short, we don't need a “paradigm shift”, we need robust well-established solutions that build on what we already have, in an evolutionary, not revolutionary manner.
