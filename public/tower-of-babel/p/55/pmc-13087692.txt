Serotonergic modulation of excitatory synapse development and plasticity
Authors: Victoria N Chang, Roberto Ogelman, Raul Satoshi Vargas, Won Chan Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExcitatory synapse plasticity at postsynaptic dendritic spines is directly modulated through neurotransmitter signaling. Serotonin (5-HT) is a primary neuromodulator in the brain that activates 14 subtypes of G-protein-coupled receptors, each with distinct expression patterns and downstream signaling mechanisms. Disruptions of serotonergic transmission during development and adulthood induce long-lasting behavioral and neuronal changes, often characterized by profound alterations in the structure and function of dendritic spines, suggesting 5-HT plays a critical role in excitatory synaptic plasticity. This review summarizes research demonstrating the importance of 5-HT receptors in the development and maturation of excitatory postsynaptic synapses. We focus on 5-HT receptor-mediated signaling and how different 5-HT receptors influence the various stages in the lifetime of a dendritic spine from spinogenesis to stabilization, potentiation, and depression. Finally, we highlight recent advances from the past decade in 5-HT research, focusing on how atypical serotonergic signaling alters excitatory synapse plasticity and how serotonergic psychedelics affect dendritic spine structure and function.Keywords: Serotonin, Serotonin receptor, Dendritic spine, Excitatory synapse, PFC

BACKGROUND

Serotonin (5-HT) is a monoamine neurotransmitter that regulates diverse cellular processes and neural circuit activity underlying behavior. Serotonergic (5-HTergic) axons appear as early as embryonic day 14 and, 5-HT levels peak in the cortex within 2 years after birth in humans and the first postnatal week in rodents (Hohmann et al., 1988, Kepser and Homberg, 2015, Lidov and Molliver, 1982, Suri et al., 2015). 5-HTergic neurons originate from the raphe nuclei in the brainstem, which are divided anatomically into the dorsal raphe nucleus and median raphe nucleus, projecting to differing parts of the central nervous system and receiving reciprocal innervation (Lesch and Waider, 2012, Tork, 1990).

Extensive research has unveiled how 5-HT plays a pivotal role in modulating neuronal activity throughout all stages of brain development into adulthood (Daubert and Condron, 2010, Hanswijk et al., 2020). Studies employing genetic, pharmacological, and chemogenetic approaches have demonstrated that 5-HTergic tone serves as a critical regulator of cognitive function and that disruption of 5-HT signaling produces profound and enduring behavioral and cellular consequences (Ansorge et al., 2004, Kepser and Homberg, 2015, Rebello et al., 2014, Suri et al., 2015, Teissier et al., 2017).

Dendritic spines are the primary postsynaptic sites for excitatory synapses that form and mature to support functional circuits through activity-dependent and -independent synaptic mechanisms (Hayashi, 2022, Hensch, 2005, Kasai et al., 2021, Sigler et al., 2017). Interestingly, high levels of 5-HT in early brain development coincide with the critical period of experience-dependent excitatory synapse maturation and circuit formation (Hensch, 2005, Teissier et al., 2017). Consistently, prior studies have implicated 5-HTergic signaling in excitatory synapse development and plasticity, suggesting an essential role for 5-HT in establishing and regulating the integration of dendritic spines into functional neural circuits (Jones et al., 2009, Kim et al., 2022, Kobe et al., 2012, Ogelman et al., 2024, Wirth et al., 2017).

Together, these findings underscore 5-HT as a key developmental signal that orchestrates the formation and refinement of excitatory synapses. In the following sections, we summarize how distinct 5-HT receptor subtypes and their downstream pathways shape synaptic plasticity from development into adulthood. Throughout this review, we frame serotonergic signaling in the context of the dendritic spine life cycle, including formation, stabilization, potentiation, and depression, to illustrate how different 5-HT receptors bias excitatory synapses toward structural and functional strengthening or weakening across development.

SEROTONIN IMBALANCES, CAUSES, AND OUTCOMES

Evidence for the importance of 5-HT on influencing circuit- and synapse-level neuronal plasticity in the brain comes from studies investigating the outcomes of 5-HTergic imbalances (Table 1). 5-HT levels are influenced by various factors such as maternal deprivation or abuse, dietary patterns rich or deficient in tryptophan, and the administration of medications like selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), which prevent reuptake of 5-HT through the serotonin transporter (Courtiol et al., 2018, Hanswijk et al., 2020, Kim et al., 2019, Suri et al., 2015). Imbalances of 5-HT and genetic variations in serotonin transporter or 5-HT receptor (5-HTR) expression have profound effects on brain development and function and are associated with increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), schizophrenia, depression, and anxiety (Brummelte et al., 2017, Caspi et al., 2010, Daws and Gould, 2011, Lesch and Waider, 2012).

Importantly, the neurodevelopmental disorders associated with changes in 5-HT transmission are often characterized by drastic changes in dendritic spine structure and function (Fiala et al., 2002, Penzes et al., 2011). As such, early 5-HTergic imbalances have major impacts on excitatory synapse development (Ogelman et al., 2024, Rebello et al., 2014, Teissier et al., 2017). From profound behavioral changes to synaptic alterations, many studies have highlighted the consequences of disrupting 5-HTergic transmission throughout brain development, supporting the idea that 5-HT acts as a critical regulator of excitatory synaptic plasticity. The following sections will explore the signaling pathways activated by 5-HTRs and how 5-HTergic activity influences dendritic spine formation, stabilization, potentiation, and depression.

SEROTONIN RECEPTORS AND SIGNALING

5-HTRs are divided into 7 classes and 14 subtypes that are unique in their pharmacological profiles, downstream signaling mechanisms, and distribution throughout the brain (Barnes and Sharp, 1999, Nichols and Nichols, 2008). The 5-HT3 receptor functions via ionotropic signaling, whereas all other 5-HTRs primarily act as G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) (Barnes and Sharp, 1999, Masson et al., 2012, Sharp and Barnes, 2020). G-protein-coupled 5-HTRs are 7-transmembrane proteins with an intracellular C-terminus and an extracellular N-terminus (Nichols and Nichols, 2008). The 5-HT1 and 5-HT5 receptor classes are coupled to traditionally inhibitory G-proteins (ie, Gαi/o), whereas the 5-HT2, 5-HT4, 5-HT6, and 5-HT7 receptors canonically activate excitatory downstream G-protein-coupled molecules (ie, Gαq, Gαs) (Leiser et al., 2015, Masson et al., 2012). 5-HTR expression is not restricted to specific brain regions or neuronal subtypes, albeit expression patterns and modulatory effects can vary drastically between brain regions (Leiser et al., 2015, Sharp and Barnes, 2020). Manipulations of 5-HTRs have been shown to modulate dendritic spine development and growth, indicating their critical role in the development and maturation of excitatory synapses (Andolina et al., 2011, Schill et al., 2020, Wirth et al., 2017).

Gαi/o signaling through 5-HT1Rs (5-HT1A-F) and 5-HT5Rs (5-HT5A-B) activates inwardly rectifying K+ (GIRK) channels, leading to hyperpolarization of neurons and inhibiting the opening of voltage-gated calcium channels (Courtney and Ford, 2014, Leiser et al., 2015, Sharp and Barnes, 2020). Stimulation of the Gαi pathway also leads to inhibition of adenylyl cyclase (AC), decreasing cAMP and PKA activity and therefore playing an important role for plasticity at excitatory synapses (Meunier et al., 2017). 5-HT1Rs can also activate molecular pathways such as ERK and Akt signaling that are known to induce cell-wide transcriptional changes of molecules necessary for excitatory synapse plasticity (Cowen, 2007). Together, these downstream effects allow 5-HT to exert an overall inhibitory effect on neuronal and synaptic activity when bound to these 5-HTRs.

5-HT2Rs (5-HT2A-C) are most commonly coupled to Gαq proteins and lead to calcium-induced calcium release from internal ER stores along with activation of PKC and CaMKII (Masson et al., 2012, Wirth et al., 2017). Internal Ca2+ release, PKC activation, and CaMKII activation are converging mechanisms that facilitate the induction of excitatory synapse plasticity and heterosynaptic crosstalk (Barnes and Sharp, 1999, Masson et al., 2012, Wirth et al., 2017). 5-HT2Rs also activate small GTPases and guanine nucleotide exchange factors such as Cdc42, Rac1, and kalirin-7 that allow for actin cytoskeleton rearrangement and structural spine enlargement (Jones et al., 2009, Masson et al., 2012, Mi et al., 2017).

Stimulation of 5-HT4Rs, 5-HT6Rs, and 5-HT7Rs primarily initiates Gαs-dependent cascades that can activate AC-dependent cAMP production, PKA, and voltage-gated Ca2+ channels that are all critical for excitatory synapse plasticity (Barnes and Sharp, 1999, Diering and Huganir, 2018, Masson et al., 2012, Wirth et al., 2017). These 5-HTRs generally depolarize neurons under resting membrane conditions or dampen afterhyperpolarization of action potentials by inhibiting K+ conductance out of cells (Ciranna and Catania, 2014). 5-HT4Rs and 5-HT7Rs can also activate alternative Gα12 or Gα13 pathways that act on small GTPases (RhoA, Cdc42) to alter actin dynamics (Masson et al., 2012).

GPCRs including 5-HTRs can also bind to ß-arrestin proteins (ß-arrestin 1 and 2) (Guseva et al., 2014). In this alternative conformation, 5-HTRs no longer activate G-proteins but rather initiate signaling pathways that often induce larger-scale cellular changes, including ERK activation and altered gene expression (Bockaert et al., 2021, Sharp and Barnes, 2020). 5-HTRs can also induce cell-wide changes through upregulated release of brain-derived neurotrophic factor and subsequent tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) activation that allow for CREB-dependent nuclear changes (Barnes and Sharp, 1999). Along these lines, SSRI administration in the first 3 postnatal weeks in mice rescues synaptic plasticity deficits via transcriptional reprogramming (Kim et al., 2022) and accelerated excitatory synapse maturation in the somatosensory cortex when administered during a critical developmental time window (Naskar et al., 2019). Overall, many of the most widely studied molecules implicated in excitatory synapse plasticity are activated or inhibited by 5-HTRs, providing a clear link between 5-HTergic signaling and changes in excitatory synapse structure and function in the brain.

SEROTONIN-MEDIATED EXCITATORY SYNAPSE PLASTICITY

Dendritic spines are dynamic protrusions along neurons that undergo extensive structural and functional changes, highly dependent on receptor activity and the dynamics of downstream molecules. From initial formation to stabilization and eventual spine strengthening (long-term potentiation, LTP) or weakening (long-term depression, LTD), there are several stages in dendritic spine plasticity that are reliant on differing signaling pathways (Hayashi, 2022, Kasai, 2023, Kasai et al., 2021). Glutamatergic activity-dependent plasticity is the most well-characterized form of excitatory synaptic modification, but accumulating evidence indicates that neuromodulators such as 5-HT can directly regulate excitatory synapses (Bazzari and Parri, 2019, Brzosko et al., 2019, Leung and Wong, 2017, Meunier et al., 2017, Ogelman et al., 2024).

Serotonin-Mediated Dendritic Spine Formation and Stabilization

Dendritic spines initially form as protrusions emerging from the dendritic shaft that extend in search of a presynaptic partner (Reilly et al., 2011, Runge et al., 2020). Synaptogenesis is regulated by many proteins and receptors that are often activated via activity-dependent cascades that rely on presynaptic release of neurotransmitters such as glutamate, GABA, or 5-HT (Harris, 2020, Kwon and Sabatini, 2011, Oh et al., 2016). New spine initiation is complex, primarily involving actin cytoskeleton reorganization (Runge et al., 2020, Yoshihara et al., 2009). The actin cytoskeleton can be rearranged through interactions of many small molecules, including small GTPases, protein complexes, and other kinases, which can be directly modulated by 5-HTR activation (Khanal and Hotulainen, 2021, Yoshihara et al., 2009).

During initial excitatory circuit development, 5-HT7Rs have been shown to aid in the initiation of new protrusions along dendritic shafts via Gα12-dependent Cdc42, Cdk5, and ERK signaling (Kvachnina et al., 2005, Speranza et al., 2013, Speranza et al., 2017). Importantly, 5-HT7Rs are widely expressed in the nervous system during development and adulthood (Costa et al., 2012, Leiser et al., 2015, Sargin et al., 2019). 5-HT4Rs and 5-HT6Rs have also been implicated in the formation of dendritic spines, as agonism of either receptor increases learning-induced spine formation or increases the number of new dendritic protrusions, respectively (Rahman et al., 2017, Restivo et al., 2008).

Newly formed protrusions do not always mature into functional dendritic spines. Many protrusions do not survive longer than a few minutes to several hours (Hatanaka et al., 2015, Hill and Zito, 2013). Protrusions that do survive are considered stabilized, which subsequently allows excitatory synapses to be incorporated into functional neuronal circuits (Hatanaka et al., 2015, Holtmaat et al., 2006). At their very early stages, new spines lack many of the defining features that mature spines display, such as the presence of a postsynaptic density, glutamatergic AMPA receptors, or a classic mushroom shape (Berry and Nedivi, 2017, Busetto et al., 2008, Harris, 2020). These immature spines often present as long, thin protrusions known as filopodia (Berry and Nedivi, 2017, Yoshihara et al., 2009). The environment around these nascent protrusions, such as the levels of 5-HT, will subsequently set the stage for their fate in circuit connectivity and maturation. 5-HT-mediated stabilization of nascent dendritic spines has been shown to depend on 5-HT7R activation. Pharmacological studies revealed that 5-HT7R-mediated AC activity can stabilize nascent protrusions, and dendritic spine elongation can occur through 5-HT7R activation and downstream MMP-9-dependent cleavage of CD44 and Cdc42 activation (Bijata et al., 2017, Ogelman et al., 2024) (Fig. 1).

Further evidence for the importance of 5-HT in both formation and stabilization of dendritic spines comes from studies investigating spine density after alterations in 5-HTergic signaling. 5-HT depletion during the first 2 postnatal weeks reduces synapse density in the visual cortex and synaptic markers in the hippocampus into adulthood (Matsukawa et al., 2003, Mazer et al., 1997). More recently, it has been shown that raising or lowering the levels of 5-HT in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) during the first 2 postnatal weeks of mouse development bidirectionally increases or decreases dendritic spine density, respectively (Ogelman et al., 2024). Together, studies have identified a clear role for 5-HTR signaling in directly influencing the formation and stabilization of dendritic spines.

Serotonin-Mediated Long-Term Potentiation at Excitatory Synapses

Once dendritic spines partner with presynaptic terminals, they can undergo further maturation involving both structural and functional changes. These modifications can be rapid and dynamic, occurring in a matter of milliseconds to minutes, or slower long-term changes occurring over periods of hours to multiple days and persisting over an entire lifetime (Citri and Malenka, 2008, Harris, 2020). A critical mechanism by which spines mature and establish into circuits is through a synaptic mechanism called long-term potentiation (LTP). LTP is the process of spines enlarging (structural LTP; sLTP) and strengthening (functional LTP; fLTP), often leading to stronger connections with presynaptic partners (Citri and Malenka, 2008, Harris, 2020, Hayashi, 2022). 5-HTR activation not only modulates the temporal window for glutamatergic LTP but can itself directly induce postsynaptic LTP at individual spines (Bazzari and Parri, 2019, Leung and Wong, 2017, Ogelman et al., 2024).

Within the 5-HT2R class, 5-HT2ARs are one of the more abundant subtypes in the brain and have been widely implicated in facilitating LTP induction at excitatory synapses. 5-HT2ARs show widespread localization on pyramidal cells, including on the soma, along dendrites, and at postsynaptic dendritic spines (Jones et al., 2009, Leiser et al., 2015, Miner et al., 2003, Santana and Artigas, 2017, Sargin et al., 2019). Excitatory postsynaptic scaffolding proteins such as PSD-95 have been shown to stabilize 5-HT2ARs to dendritic spines and be required for functional downstream signaling (Abbas et al., 2009, Allen et al., 2008, Xia et al., 2003). Interestingly, 5-HT2ARs directly interact with and modulate glutamatergic AMPARs and NMDARs. 5-HT2ARs colocalize with AMPARs and NMDARs on dendritic shafts and spines, and 5-HT2AR signaling enhances NMDAR currents via ERK activation (Chen et al., 2003, Dantsuji et al., 2019, Peddie et al., 2008). Furthermore, both administration of the SSRI fluoxetine (FLX) in vivo and direct 5-HTergic stimulation of individual dendritic spines by 2-photon 5-HT uncaging can increase spine size and AMPAR-mediated currents via 5-HT2AR-dependent PKC activation and external Ca2+ influx (Ogelman et al., 2024, Udoh et al., 2024) (Fig. 1). While less studied in the context of dendritic spines, 5-HT2B and 5-HT2CRs have also been implicated in synaptic plasticity. 5-HT2BRs can colocalize with NMDARs, while 5-HT2CRs show localization to PSD-95, and receptor mutations impair synaptic plasticity (BÉcamel et al., 2002, Benhadda et al., 2021, Liu et al., 2024). 5-HT2CRs similarly engage signaling transduction pathways as 5-HT2ARs to increase NMDAR-mediated currents through activation of the ERK1/2 pathway (Yuen et al., 2008). Interestingly, 5-HT2CRs have also been shown to be important for transforming eligibility traces of visual cortical responses, converting them to LTD and depressing visual responses (He et al., 2015, Hong et al., 2022). Overall, the intricate interplay between 5-HT2Rs and downstream signaling pathways is known to contribute significantly to the regulation of excitatory synapse development and plasticity in the cortex.

Studies have shown that stimulation of postsynaptic 5-HT4Rs, 5-HT6Rs, and 5-HT7Rs can mediate and enhance excitatory synaptic plasticity through several downstream signaling pathways (Costa et al., 2012, Crispino et al., 2020, Volpicelli et al., 2014, Wirth et al., 2017). 5-HT4R antagonism prevents LTP induction during memory formation, while 5-HT4R activation induces functional dendritic spine maturation and structural enlargement via Gα13 RhoA-dependent mechanisms (Schill et al., 2020, Sonnenberg et al., 2021, Teixeira et al., 2018). Similarly, 5-HT6Rs are expressed on dendritic spines, can induce spine enlargement via RhoA signaling, and 5-HT6R knockout mice display immature-like dendritic spine morphology (Chaumont-Dubel et al., 2023, Rahman et al., 2017, Sun et al., 2021). 5-HT7Rs signal through Ca2+-dependent mechanisms to induce LTP and can activate Cdk5 to modulate synaptic plasticity (Costa et al., 2021, Ogelman et al., 2024, Wirth et al., 2017) (Fig. 1). Supporting these studies, 5-HT7R knockout mice display impaired LTP, and activation of 5-HT7Rs has been shown to rescue impaired LTP in an ASD model (Khodaverdi et al., 2021, Roberts et al., 2004). In summary, LTP induction at excitatory synapses through the 5-HT2, 5-HT4, 5-HT6, and 5-HT7 receptor classes is well-documented and helps lay the foundation for dendritic spine maturation and functional circuit formation.

Serotonin-Mediated Long-Term Depression at Excitatory Synapses

The molecular mechanisms and synaptic changes that accompany LTP are not the only way in which synaptic plasticity can occur. In the same way that synapses can be formed and strengthened through the induction of LTP, the opposite can be said, where synapses can also be weakened (functional LTD, fLTD), shrink, and become eliminated (structural LTD, sLTD) (Collingridge et al., 2010, Oh et al., 2013, Wiegert and Oertner, 2013, Zhou et al., 2004). There are many different types of LTD, defined based on induction and downstream mechanisms (Collingridge et al., 2010). The most well-understood forms of LTD are those dependent on the activation of NMDARs or mGluRs resulting in the shrinkage and endocytosis of AMPARs (Collingridge et al., 2010, Diering and Huganir, 2018, LÜscher and Malenka, 2012). However, recent work has shown that LTD can also be induced via activation of other receptors and neurotransmitters, including evidence of 5-HT-mediated LTD (Collingridge et al., 2010).

Of the 5-HTergic receptors that have been shown to mediate synaptic weakening, the 5-HT1AR subtype (other 5-HT1 subtypes are expressed at comparatively lower levels in the cortex) has been most extensively studied. 5-HT1ARs are located both presynaptically, where they function as autoreceptors inhibiting presynaptic neurotransmitter release when there is an abundance of 5-HT in the synaptic cleft, and postsynaptically as hetero-receptors, located at the soma and along dendrites to inhibit postsynaptic activity at a cellular and synaptic level (Costa et al., 2012, Courtney and Ford, 2014, GÜnther et al., 2011, Lanfumey and Hamon, 2000). It has been shown that the downstream signaling of 5-HT1AR activation differs between autoreceptors and hetero-receptors, possibly accounting for region-specific differences in 5-HT1AR signaling (Albert and Vahid-Ansari, 2019, Polter and Li, 2010). Cortical distribution of 5-HT1ARs varies depending on brain region but are generally found in all layers, most often showing highest density in layers 2/3 and 5/6 (Lanfumey and Hamon, 2000, Mengod et al., 2015, Palchaudhuri and FlÜgge, 2005, Santana and Artigas, 2017, Sargin et al., 2019). Activation of these receptors typically produces a reduction in neuronal excitability, firing, and postsynaptic activity (Polter and Li, 2010). As such, 5-HT1ARs have been shown to modulate glutamatergic activity, with their activation leading to decreased AMPAR and NMDAR currents (Cai et al., 2002, Moyano et al., 2004, Schiapparelli et al., 2005, Yuen et al., 2005, Zhong et al., 2008). In the PFC, 5-HT1AR activation has been shown to suppress AMPAR currents and impair memory retention through inhibition of CaMKII (Cai et al., 2002, Schiapparelli et al., 2005), while activation of 5-HT1ARs leads to a reduction of NMDAR-mediated currents through the inhibition of PKA and ERK (Yuen et al., 2005, Zhong et al., 2008). Past work has also shown that induction of LTP at excitatory synapses is significantly inhibited in the presence of 5-HT1AR agonists (Edagawa et al., 1998, Edagawa et al., 1999, Moreau et al., 2013, Pollandt et al., 2003, Sakai and Tanaka, 1993). 5-HT1ARs also play a role in controlling the excitation-inhibition balance in layer 5 pyramidal neurons in the PFC, suggesting a role in directing their plasticity toward LTP or LTD (Meunier et al., 2013, Moreau et al., 2013).

Structurally, activation of 5-HT1ARs in cultured cortical neurons led to an increase in filopodia density at 11 days in vitro and decreased spine density at 15 days in vitro, suggesting that 5-HT1ARs prevent the maturation of cortical synapses (Yoshida et al., 2011) (Fig. 2). Activation of 5-HT1ARs also led to reduced dendritic length and branching (Kozono et al., 2017, Rojas et al., 2017). In contrast, blockade of 5-HT1ARs resulted in decreased spine density of developing granule cells, while their activation prevented 5-HT-depletion-mediated spine loss (Yan et al., 1997). However, less is known about the structural and functional outcomes following 5-HT1AR activation within the developing cortex. Due to the region-specific differences in 5-HT1AR signaling (ie, pre- vs post-synapses and auto- vs hetero-receptors), the overall effects elicited by 5-HT1AR activation on plasticity also differ based on brain region, and it is important to note that 5-HT1ARs are expressed not only on pyramidal neurons but interneurons and astrocytes as well (LladÓ-Pelfort et al., 2012, Patel and Zhou, 2005, Sharp and Barnes, 2020, Wilson et al., 1998). Many studies have clearly shown that 5-HT1AR signaling plays a critical role in synaptic development and may mediate many of the cognitive impairments observed after 5-HTergic imbalances during the critical period for circuit formation (Altieri et al., 2013, Garcia-Garcia et al., 2017, Goodfellow et al., 2009, Heisler et al., 1998, Mehta et al., 2007, Vinkers et al., 2010). Therefore, it is important to continue investigating the role of 5-HT1ARs in orienting plasticity in the cortex, both functionally and structurally.

5-HT5 receptors are also Gαi/o coupled and reduce neuronal activity but are the least- characterized 5-HT receptors. In layer 5 of the PFC, 5-HT5Rs mediate 5-HT-elicited outward currents when there is no 5-HT1AR activity (Goodfellow et al., 2012). Additionally, genetic deletion of 5-HT5ARs results in a large increase in 5-HT1AR-mediated currents, suggesting that the 5-HT1ARs and 5-HT5ARs in the PFC work together to induce 5-HT-mediated inhibition (Goodfellow et al., 2012). Interestingly, activation of 5-HT5ARs by D-lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) has been suggested to induce psychotropic effects, and blockade of these receptors prevents the LSD discriminatory cue (Popik et al., 2019). The pharmacological tools available for the study of the 5-HT5Rs still require more development, but investigation into this receptor family’s role in synaptic development is crucial for a complete idea of 5-HTergic modulation of synaptic plasticity.

Overall, compared to what is known about 5-HT-mediated potentiation of excitatory synapses, considerably less is understood about how serotonergic signaling drives synaptic weakening and inhibition. Much of the current work is inferred from receptor localization, downstream signaling pathways, and effects on glutamatergic receptors. Future studies that examine how inhibitory 5-HT receptor activation directly regulates structural and functional plasticity at excitatory synapses will be essential for completing our understanding of how serotonergic signaling shapes the dendritic spine life cycle.

DEVELOPMENTAL CHANGES IN SEROTONIN RECEPTOR EXPRESSION AND SIGNALING

5-HTergic signaling can have opposing effects on neuronal excitability depending on the developmental maturity of neurons (Brummelte et al., 2017, Rebello et al., 2014). During postnatal mouse brain development, there is a unique switch in 5-HTR expression and function in the PFC. Prior to P15, 5-HT2ARs and 5-HT7Rs work together to depolarize pyramidal neurons, inducing slow inward currents (Andrade, 2011, BÉÏque et al., 2004). After P15, there is a shift toward 5-HT-elicited hyperpolarization, mediated by increased 5-HT1AR expression levels (BÉÏque et al., 2004, Goodfellow et al., 2009). This suggests that the changes in the expression of excitatory and inhibitory 5-HTergic receptors that coincide with changes in electrophysiological properties of PFC pyramidal neurons in response to 5-HTergic stimulation are a result of coordinated regulation of cortical excitatory synapses (Andrade, 2011, BÉÏque et al., 2004). Along these lines, chronic 5-HT2AR activation in the PFC from P14 to 21 induces AMPAR internalization and induces synaptic weakening, whereas prior to P15, 5-HT2AR activation potentiates excitatory synapses (Berthoux et al., 2019, Jones et al., 2009, Ogelman et al., 2024). These changes are attributed to a combination of several factors. First, 5-HT2ARs seemingly lose their ability to couple to downstream G-protein-coupled effectors at later developmental time points (Béïque et al., 2007). More recent evidence suggests that 5-HT2ARs may internalize in adulthood and are only activated via membrane-permeable agonists (Cornea-HÉbert et al., 1999, Vargas et al., 2023). Second, 5-HT7R-dependent depolarization at early developmental stages is eventually absent due to a decrease in overall receptor expression (Lee et al., 2021). 5-HT7R-dependent Gα12 signaling in the hippocampus regulates synaptogenesis and synapse formation in early postnatal neuronal development, but plasticity induction is developmentally constrained (Kobe et al., 2012, Lee et al., 2021). This critical developmental change will be an important consideration in future therapeutics that target the 5-HTergic system as pharmaceutical interventions may have opposing results in immature and adult brains.

ATYPICAL SEROTONERGIC SIGNALING

More recently, studies have progressed with exciting advancements regarding atypical mechanisms of 5-HTergic signaling. While 5-HTRs are traditionally thought to activate specific G-protein-coupled pathways unique to each 5-HTR, it is now evident that almost all 5-HTRs can activate alternative G-proteins, adding to the complexity of 5-HTergic signaling (Bockaert et al., 2021, Jain et al., 2025, Sharp and Barnes, 2020). 5-HT1ARs activate a wide variety of G-protein-coupled pathways depending on the brain region, neuronal subtype, and cellular localization. Pre- and post-synaptically expressed 5-HT1ARs induce different downstream mechanisms, allowing biased antagonists to be developed that can inhibit only one subpopulation and not the other (DepoortÈre et al., 2021). 5-HTRs can also induce differential signaling pathways through interactions with other molecules, such as the heterodimerization of 5-HTRs to mGluRs or 5-HTRs that are constitutively activated in a specific conformation to signal through non-GPCR-coupled pathways (Bockaert et al., 2021, Price et al., 2019). Serotonylation, in which 5-HT covalently binds to the primary amine of glutamine in proteins, is another alternative mechanism of 5-HTergic signaling that can have profound implications for synaptic plasticity (Bockaert et al., 2021, Jiang et al., 2021, Sardar et al., 2025). Serotonylation can influence the activity of many molecules such as small GTPases and can induce long-lasting changes in gene expression via histone modifications (Bockaert et al., 2021). Altogether, it is important to consider alternative pathways for 5-HTergic signaling in the context of excitatory synapse plasticity to fully understand how 5-HT can influence circuit formation and function.

SEROTONERGIC PSYCHEDELICS

Over the past decade, there has been growing interest in the use of 5-HTergic psychedelics for the treatment of neuropsychiatric disorders, including depression, anxiety, and substance use disorders, reflecting their unique ability to engage 5-HT receptor-dependent plasticity mechanisms that produce lasting circuit-level changes (Carhart-Harris et al., 2021, Davis et al., 2021, Johnson et al., 2017). 5-HTergic psychedelics include classical hallucinogens such as psilocybin, lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA), N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), and mescaline. These compounds primarily elicit their psychedelic/hallucinogenic effects in the brain by activation of 5-HT2ARs (Hatzipantelis and Olson, 2024, Jain et al., 2025, Vorobyeva and Kozlova, 2022). Previous studies show that the hallucinogenic effects of psychedelics can be abolished by 5-HT2AR antagonism (Becker et al., 2023, Cao et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2020). However, it remains an active area of investigation whether the hallucinogenic effects of psychedelics via 5-HT2AR activation are necessary for their therapeutic benefits in neuropsychiatric disorders (Cameron et al., 2023, Hatzipantelis and Olson, 2024, Kwan et al., 2022, Roth and Gumpper, 2023).

At the cellular level, 5-HTergic psychedelics have been shown to promote structural and functional neuroplasticity (de la Fuente Revenga et al., 2021, Hatzipantelis and Olson, 2024, Kwan et al., 2022, Ly et al., 2018). Treatment with DMT, 2,5-dimethoxy-4-iodoamphetamine (DOI), and LSD all led to increased spinogenesis, synaptogenesis, and functional plasticity, an effect that was dependent on TrkB, mTOR, and 5-HT2AR signaling (Ly et al., 2018) (Fig. 3). Additionally, a single dose of psilocybin induces both structural and functional potentiation of excitatory synapses in mice, persisting for over 3 months (Hatzipantelis and Olson, 2024, Shao et al., 2021). Consistent with a receptor-dependent mechanism, this persistent potentiation depends on 5-HT2AR activity (Shao et al., 2025). The actions of 5-HTergic psychedelics extend beyond extracellular 5-HT receptor activation. Notably, certain lipophilic agonists such as LSD and psilocybin can permeate the plasma membrane and activate intracellular 5-HT2ARs, thereby initiating alternative intracellular signaling cascades (Bockaert et al., 2021, Cornea-HÉbert et al., 1999, Vargas et al., 2023) (Fig. 3). These intracellular 5-HT2ARs appear to be tethered to the Golgi apparatus and can engage signaling pathways that produce both rapid and long-lasting changes (Vargas et al., 2023). Although recent evidence suggests that 5-HTergic psychedelics may promote synaptic connectivity in a 5-HT2AR-dependent mechanism (Ly et al., 2018, Shao et al., 2025) (Fig. 3), psychedelics have also been shown to increase glutamatergic activity in a 5-HT2AR-independent manner (Ekins et al., 2026, Hesselgrave et al., 2021). Additionally, 5-HTergic psychedelics exhibit complex pharmacological interactions with a variety of other receptors. Many psychedelic compounds have been reported to interact with additional 5-HTR subtypes, dopaminergic receptors, and, in some cases, receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) (Cameron et al., 2023, Jain et al., 2025, Lewis et al., 2023, Moliner et al., 2023, Pomrenze et al., 2025, Ray, 2010, Sharp and Ippolito, 2025). Collectively, these findings highlight the mechanistic complexity by which 5-HTergic psychedelics modulate excitatory synapses through both 5-HT2AR-dependent and -independent pathways. A comprehensive characterization of the receptor-specific signaling mechanisms and downstream effectors engaged by 5-HTergic psychedelics will be critical for understanding how these compounds produce sustained therapeutic effects in neuropsychiatric disorders.

CONCLUSION

In this review, we highlight the critical role of 5-HT receptors in regulating the development and plasticity of excitatory synapses (Table 1). These synapses reside on dendritic protrusions known as dendritic spines, which are dynamic structures that continuously undergo formation, maturation, and elimination. Changes in these synapses are crucial for neural circuit formation and refinement (Hayashi, 2022, Hensch, 2005, Kasai et al., 2021). These synapses can be modulated by several mechanisms, but importantly, proper levels of 5-HT are critical for normal neuronal activity throughout development and into adulthood (Daubert and Condron, 2010, Hanswijk et al., 2020). Here, we highlight how distinct 5-HT receptor subtypes regulate the development and plasticity of excitatory synapses. We further discuss how serotonergic signaling, including that engaged by psychedelics, modulates structural and functional remodeling of dendritic spines (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Overall, 5-HT acts as a critical neuromodulator of excitatory synapses, and continued investigation into its receptor-specific mechanisms will be essential for understanding how serotonergic signaling shapes synaptic architecture and function. 5-HTR activation can have wide-ranging effects on plasticity at excitatory synapses, supporting a model in which subtype specificity allows for bidirectional control of inducing LTP versus LTD (BÉÏque et al., 2004, Elliott et al., 2018, Higa et al., 2022, Higa et al., 2024, Li et al., 2018, Yuen et al., 2008). It is important that more work be done to investigate the bidirectional role that 5-HT can play through its excitatory and inhibitory GPCRs, as well as how they work in tandem with each other in different brain regions.

Many questions remain as to the role of 5-HT in modulating synaptic plasticity. 5-HT receptor signaling ranges broadly due to the different receptors and their functions. However, 5-HTR activation and the 5-HT system as a whole is not only influenced by endogenous 5-HT but also other co-released neuromodulators capable of directly and indirectly affecting 5-HT signaling and system. The 5-HT system closely interacts with other neuromodulator systems, including dopamine (DA) and endocannabinoids (EC) (Peters et al., 2021). As a result, these systems can act upon the 5-HT system and modulate its activity and vice versa, potentially leading to changes in excitatory synaptic plasticity (Bortolozzi et al., 2005, Galindo-Charles et al., 2024, Haj-Dahmane and Shen, 2011, Hong et al., 2022, Pehek et al., 2006). Additionally, the effects elicited by 5-HT can be similarly induced through activation of 5-HTRs by exogenous drugs that share a similar structure to 5-HT, such as 5-HTergic psychedelics, or other ligands such as cannabidiol (CBD).

Whether the effects of 5-HT at excitatory synapses is 5-HT-specific or can be similarly induced via other neuromodulators is still being assessed. Beyond the interactions between systems, these other neuromodulators engage similar signaling cascades and downstream mechanisms as 5-HT. Many of the intracellular pathways engaged by 5-HT to drive synaptogenesis are shared with other neuromodulatory GPCR signaling systems. Key proteins and signaling molecules involved in synaptic strengthening, such as CaMKII, PKA, and PKC, are all engaged by canonical GPCR signaling that allows for modulation of synapses independently of 5-HT but possibly through similar mechanisms (Nadim and Bucher, 2014). Previous work has characterized other neuromodulators as being capable of excitatory synaptic modulation through similar downstream mechanisms to 5-HT (Nadim and Bucher, 2014). Norepinephrine (NE) and acetylcholine (ACh) have been shown to modulate LTD in the visual cortex (Bear and Singer, 1986, Kirkwood et al., 1999). Cholinergic activity at nicotinic and muscarinic receptors is important for proper dendritic spine formation and can enhance synaptic plasticity, respectively (Giessel and Sabatini, 2010, Lozada et al., 2012). Dopamine can induce sLTP during a critical window following reward-reinforced behaviors (Yagishita et al., 2014). Overall, whether some of the effects by 5-HT within this review are 5-HT-specific or can be induced similarly through different neuromodulators is still being assessed, but it is important to note that they converge on similar intracellular signaling mechanisms, inducing similar effects on plasticity, and may work in tandem with each other (Kirkwood, 2000). Additionally, there are many factors that should be considered as to how 5-HT induces different forms of plasticity, including changes in 5-HT availability or receptors throughout life, genetic differences, and behavioral or environmental factors. Understanding how 5-HT can mechanistically modulate plasticity and the crosstalk with other neuromodulators will be beneficial for developing future targeted therapies. Additionally, with the increased availability of 5-HTergic psychedelics, elucidating how they harness 5-HT-mediated mechanisms of excitatory synaptic plasticity will be critical for linking fundamental 5-HT biology to their emerging therapeutic applications in neuropsychiatric disease.
