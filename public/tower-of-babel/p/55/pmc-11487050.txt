Supervised machine learning of outbred mouse genotypes to predict hepatic immunological tolerance of individuals
Authors: Miwa Morita-Nakagawa, Kohji Okamura, Kazuhiko Nakabayashi, Yukiko Inanaga, Seiichi Shimizu, Wen-Zhi Guo, Masayuki Fujino, Xiao-Kang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is essential to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying liver transplant tolerance and rejection. In cases of mouse liver transplantation between inbred strains, immunological rejection of the allograft is reduced with spontaneous apoptosis without immunosuppressive drugs, which differs from the actual clinical result. This may be because inbred strains are genetically homogeneous and less heterogeneous than others. We exploited outbred CD1 mice, which show highly heterogeneous genotypes among individuals, to search for biomarkers related to immune responses and to construct a model for predicting the outcome of liver allografting. Of the 36 mice examined, 18 died within 3 weeks after transplantation, while the others survived for more than 6 weeks. Whole-exome sequencing of the 36 donors revealed more than 9 million variants relative to the C57BL/6 J reference. We selected 6517 single-nucleotide and indel variants and performed machine learning to determine whether or not we could predict the prognosis of each genotype. Models were built by both deep learning with a one-dimensional convolutional neural network and linear classification and evaluated by leave-one-out cross-validation. Given that one short-lived mouse died early in an accident, the models perfectly predicted the outcome of all individuals, suggesting the importance of genotype collection. In addition, linear classification models provided a list of loci potentially responsible for these responses. The present methods as well as results is likely to be applicable to liver transplantation in humans.Keywords: Transplantation tolerance, Supervised machine learning, Liver transplantation, Outbred mouseSubject terms: Transplant immunology, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The liver has the immune privilege to induce tolerance in organ transplantation1. Indeed, in the clinical setting, the outcomes of liver transplantation are better than those of other organs, with 20–30% of recipients eventually able to be weaned off of immunosuppressive drugs2. Similar phenomena have been shown in some animal models as well, such as outbred pigs1 and rats3,4, where liver allografts are spontaneously accepted without any treatment. In particular, mouse liver allografts are accepted across full major histocompatibility complex (MHC) mismatch combinations among inbred mouse strains5. To date, in bred model has been used to understand the process of allograft rejection and acceptance, and its mechanism has been shown about the mechanism5–12. In mouse liver transplantation, an allograft is infiltrated with lymphocytes early post-transplant. These cells are gradually reduced by apoptosis, until the graft is ultimately accepted; conversely, heart grafts are rejected, with less apoptosis occurring11,13. It has been suggested that the behavior of the grafted liver determines its outcome.

Inbred mice have been preferentially used in the biomedical field, including in studies of transplantation immunology. They are genetically homogeneous and show little heterogeneity within pure inbred strains. Using these animals is expected to reduce experimental variability and increase study reproducibility. However, inbred mice reportedly have a lower phenotypic variability than others14,15. It is well known that humans are an outbred species with considerable heterozygosity and genetic diversity. These factors may contribute to our diverse immune responses. It has also been reported that the immune responses of inbred and outbred mice differ as well16,17. Notably, some studies have suggested that outbred mice might be appropriate for preclinical research15.

Outbred mice are widely used in toxicology and cancer research18–20. However, they have not been used in genetic studies because of their heterogeneous backgrounds. Recent genome-wide association studies have been conducted to analyze the genetic diversity of outbred mice21–23.

In the present study, we performed liver transplantation using outbred mice (CD1) as donors and recipients. In contrast to inbred mice, half of the recipients lost allografts due to rejection within three weeks (rejection group), and the other half survived for more than six weeks (tolerance group). We analyzed the genotype differences in donors between the rejection and tolerance groups using whole-exome sequencing. Although a large number of variants were identified in the closed colony, it seemed difficult to identify specific variants that indicated to which group each individual belonged.

A complex combination of many genotypes may result in a poor prognosis. By applying supervised machine learning algorithms, namely, deep learning and linear classification, we successfully established excellent models that could predict the immune response to transplantation.

Results

Acceptance of outbred liver grafts

As shown in Fig. 1A, in outbred mice (CD1), 18/36 (50%) liver grafts died within 18 days, while the remaining 18 (50%) survived for more than 48 days (MST; median survival time = 33.0 days). In contrast, all grafts of inbred mice (B10 and C3H) were accepted for more than 80 days (p < 0.05).

Histological analyses of rejected outbred liver grafts

Outbred grafts that died within 18 days showed severe rejection associated with massive diffused lymphocyte infiltration extending into the hepatic parenchyma, while lymphocyte infiltration was limited around the portal vein in inbred grafts (Fig. 1B). According to histological analyses with the Banff criteria grading system24, higher inflammation scores were observed on grafts harvested from outbred mice than inbred mice combination (Fig. 1C).

Whole-exome sequencing and sifting genetic variants

We performed whole-exome sequencing for all 36 donor mice. Liver DNA samples were enriched and sequenced using the Illumina platform. We obtained at least 6 Gb of read data for each CD1 sample, resulting in a total of 1179 million read pairs consisting of 929 Gb. Using GATK HaplotypeCaller, 9,474,702 possible variants were identified relative to the C57BL/6 J reference sequence. By limiting the search to variants whose total depth was ≥ 720 reads, 181,075 single-nucleotide and 33,417 indel variants remained. Variants on autosomes, namely chromosomes 1–19, were exclusively considered for further analyses, with chromosomes X and Y and mitochondrial DNA eliminated. Variants on unlocalised and unplaced clone contigs were also eliminated.

As described in the Materials and Methods section, scores were assigned to each called genotype not only to prepare features for machine learning but also to sift useful variants, in order to discriminate between the two groups. Referring to the χ2 test, we first selected 4294 variants and performed further analyses. In the subsequent PCA and in the process of hyperparameter optimization, we noticed that much better or ideal models could be obtained by treating early dead CD1-137 as a tolerance sample. Because the mouse lived only six days after transplantation, it was labeled as a rejection type. Although we do not have evidence, it is possible to ascribe early death to any accident other than immunological rejection. When CD1-137 was forcefully labeled as a tolerance type, 6517 variants were obtained using a χ2 test under the same conditions. We adopted a p-value threshold of less than 0.1, also recognizing the issue of multiplicity. However, our primary goal here was to retain a certain number of explanatory variables for the following machine learning. We thus considered these 6517 variants to be meaningful and practically useful for discerning the two groups (Supplementary Table 2).

Unsupervised machine learning

For all 36 donor samples, a feature vector consisting of 6517 genotypes, viz. explanatory variables, was prepared as described in the Materials and Methods section. To visualize all data, we first performed a PCA. The rejection and tolerance groups were well-separated by the first and second principal components (Fig. 2). The CD1-137 sample, which was originally classified into the rejection group, was placed in the middle of the counterpart group. It was closely positioned to and overlapped with CD1-014. Using the same design matrix, we performed hierarchical clustering and drew a heatmap with a dendrogram (Fig. 3). Except for CD1-021 and CD1-043, most samples formed well-separated clusters. For CD1-137, clustering also suggested that the sample intrinsically belonged to the tolerance group rather than the rejection group. On the horizontal axis, genotypes were arranged according to chromosomal numbers and genomic coordinates. The existence of blocks in the heatmap indicated that a large number of genotypes were associated with chromosomal regions in closed-colony mice. It is highly likely that we could expect much better performance in supervised machine learning by employing the 6517 genotype sets to infer the immune response of individual mice.

Supervised machine learning

Subsequently, we attempt to build a classifier based on deep learning. Because the variants are arranged along chromosomal positions, neighboring variants should be associated statistically or form haplotypes. We therefore designed convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with a one-dimensional convolutional layer. The CNNs were trained with 6517 variants to predict binary immune responses, namely tolerance and rejection. Because the number of samples was not large, we repeatedly performed leave-one-out cross-validation to optimize the neural networks. We noticed that our final model could predict the immune response perfectly if one sample (CD1-137) was eliminated from the training dataset. The rejected individual was the sole individual located in the cluster of the tolerance group in the PCA (Fig. 2). The network consisted of a one-dimensional convolution layer, one max pooling layer, four fully connected layers, one dropout layer, and an output layer with a sigmoid function (Fig. 4A). By treating CD1-137 as a tolerance sample, inference accuracy was determined by leave-one-out cross-validation, in which 35 out of the 36 samples were used for training and the remaining sample was subjected to the test of the model (Fig. 4B). Cross-validation was repeated with 36 samples, and all 36 trained CNN models perfectly predicted the immune response of the remaining test samples (Fig. 4C).

Preparation of 36 possible combinations of training data and test data and creation of 36 different models and running tests on 1 test case that was not used for training. (C). Learning curves of loss and accuracy changes over the epoch. Values were calculated as the means among the 36 leave-one-out cross-validation models.

In addition to the CNN-based model, we tried to build a linear classifier so that we could identify important features that contribute significantly to the prognosis. The Jubatus machine learning framework was employed because it worked satisfactorily in our previous study25. Using the settings described in the Materials and Methods section, the same result was obtained; namely, the models predicted the prognosis perfectly when CD1-137 was treated as a tolerance sample. Validation was also performed in a leave-one-out cross-validation method, in which 36 models were built by leaving out 1 sample, and each model inferred the correct immune reaction of the leaving one. All data were exhaustively employed as both training and test data. Compared with deep learning, linear classification has the advantage of deriving significant features for classification. We were able to identify variants that strongly contributed to the classification (Supplementary Table 3). In this framework, the classification scores were calculated for each group, and a test sample was classified into one of the two groups by comparing the two scores. Large weight values were multiplied by the reference allele code, 1.0, or 0.5 and contributed to an increase in the score. Reference allele code 0.0 thus did not have any effect on the classification. Variants sorted by weight values were tabulated for both the tolerance and rejection groups (Supplementary Table 3).

Prediction of the effects of variation on the gene function

The top 1000 of 6517 sequence variants of the gene function in the tolerance and rejection groups were predicted using SnpEff 4.3. The impact of these effects was categorized into high, moderate, low, and modifier groups. The numbers of variants in both tolerance and rejection groups were similar. This shows that the missense_variant was the most common variant in the moderate-impact group, whereas the synonymous_variant was the most common variant in the low-impact group. The intron_variant was the most common variant in the low-impact group, whereas the upstream_gene_variant was the most common variant in the modifier group (Supplementary Table 4).

Functional enrichment analyses

Pathway and process enrichment analyses using the top 1000 of 6517 variants by Metascape showed that the rejection group was mainly enriched in the negative regulation of immune system processes, negative regulation of leukocyte activation, supramolecular fiber organization, negative regulation of innate immune response, antigen processing and presentation of peptide antigen via MHC class II, regulation of JUN kinase activity, and T cell receptor signaling pathway. In contrast, the tolerance group was enriched in Type II interferon signaling, membrane assembly, ion channel transport, ectoderm development, MET activation of PTK2 signaling, and receptor-mediated mitophagy (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

In the present study, we established a deep learning model along with a linear classification model. Both of them predicted the prognosis of mouse liver allotransplantation with high accuracy within the cohort. To achieve these results, we used outbred CD1 mice for liver transplantation to investigate the heterogeneous background related to the outcome. Half of the outbred liver grafts were rejected 18 days after transplantation and were associated with severe inflammation (Fig. 1). These samples constituted an exceptionally ideal dataset for demonstrating our concept. To generalize it and enable broader application, however, it is necessary to collect more variable samples, including those that can be used to evaluate the presence or absence of overfitting.

Generally, in liver transplantation using inbred mice, lymphocyte infiltration of allografts is gradually reduced due to apoptosis, allowing the grafts to be accepted11. However, this phenomenon may be caused by genetic homogeneity and the relatively little heterogeneity in pure inbred strains, in contrast to the situation in humans, which are an outbred species. There is no doubt that genetic background plays a critical role in graft acceptance.

As expected, some outbred mice in the present study were unable to overcome the rejection status, and the liver grafts were lost. This phenomenon corresponds to liver transplantation in clinical settings2. This suggests that the outbred model may be more appropriate for investigating transplantation tolerance than inbred models, and the outcome of rejection/acceptance may be determined by the genetic background.

In liver transplantation, methods to induce immunological tolerance after grafting have not yet been established, and the mechanisms underlying the induction of immune tolerance have not yet been elucidated. Previous studies have suggested that donor organs influence allograft acceptance after liver transplantation2,13,26. Therefore, in the present study, we used an outbred mouse liver allograft model and focused on analyzing the graft and recipient genotypes.

To elucidate graft genome-related graft outcomes, whole-exome sequencing was performed to identify the genotype. Simple allele coding of 0.0. 0.5, and 1.0 worked satisfactorily. Because the allele coding of C57BL/6 is 0, the linear classification scores for rejection and tolerance are both 0. Many variants showed linkage. As genomic sequencing was limited to protein-coding regions, variants that directly contributed to the phenotype were not identified in the present analysis. Such important variants may be located in intronic or intergenic regions and are chromosomally associated with variants in coding regions; the variants that we identified may have been of the latter type. A hierarchical cluster analysis after whole-exome sequencing showed a trend toward classification into immune tolerance induction and rejection groups (Fig. 3). Furthermore, a PCA confirmed that both groups were able to be properly classified (Fig. 2). With regard to sample CD1-137, it was highly likely that the animal died of causes other than immunological rejection.

We initially attempted to identify several biomarkers that might be related to immunological responses and clearly discriminate between the tolerance and rejection groups. Our previous studies identified several possible biomarkers related to immune rejection and tolerance in liver transplantation in rodents and/or human liver allotransplantaion27–31. A recent study performed whole-exome sequencing to find candidate variants for the prediction of kidney transplantation efficacy, and nine variants were found in six genes that might be involved in renal allograft rejection32. However, these biomarkers were not found in the compiled list of variants. Only a limited number of variants were associated with immune-related genes (Supplementary. Figure 1). Therefore, whole-genome sequencing, rather than exome sequencing, is deemed necessary to elucidate the molecular mechanisms involved in the induction of immune tolerance after liver transplantation. Patterns or combinations of variants may be more appropriate than biomarkers for predicting complex immune mechanisms, such as the induction of immune tolerance after liver transplantation, as shown in this study (Fig. 4).

The variety of genotypes seen in outbred CD1 mice may make it an excellent model for the human population, and the variants found in this study may be able to be applied to humans. However, variants found on sex chromosomes were not considered in this study, so sex chromosome analyses might be necessary if sex differences in the induction of immune tolerance after liver transplantation are to be considered.

In the present study, we established machine learning models that predicts the prognosis of mouse liver allotransplantation. The results showed the value of using outbred CD1 mice in genetics and the usefulness of whole-exome sequencing analyses with supervised machine learning as a powerful tool. The variants found in this study might be able to be applied to humans as well, and further studies analyzing the genotype of liver allografts in patients may be able to elucidate useful variants to establish a model that predicts the fate after liver transplantation. The prognosis after liver transplantation in clinical practice may be able to be predicted by whole-exome sequencing or a microarray analysis to confirm haplotypes.

Methods

Mice

Male CD1 outbred mice (Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA, USA) as well as C57BL/10 (B10) and C3H/HeJ mice (C3H, Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) at 5 to 7 weeks old were used for this study.

Orthotopic liver transplantation

CD1 mice were used as both donors and recipients, while B10 and C3H mice were used as donors and recipients, respectively. Orthotopic liver transplantation was performed as described previously9,11. In brief, after perfusion with cold lactic solution through the portal vein, the donor liver was harvested, and cuffs were attached to the intrahepatic inferior vena cava and portal vein in the same solution. Subsequently, the liver graft was orthotopically transplanted into the recipient mouse using a cuff technique. The hepatic artery was not reconstructed, and immunosuppressive drugs were not administered to the recipients. All surgeries were performed under isoflurane anesthesia, and efforts were made to minimize suffering. The graft survival was determined based on the recipient survival.

Histological studies

Liver graft tissues fixed in 10% phosphate-buffered formalin were embedded in paraffin, and 5-μm-thick sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The Banff criteria have been used to define the pathological grades of hepatic grafts using the rejection activity index (RAI)24. The overall rejection score is the sum of these three scores and indicates the severity of rejection as follows: 0–2, none; 3, borderline; 4–5, mild; 6–7, moderate; and 8–9, severe.

DNA library construction

Genomic DNA from each donor mouse was extracted from liver tissue. Approximately 2 µg of DNA was sheared into several hundred base pairs, with a peak size of 300 bp, using Covalis S220. DNA fragments were then end-repaired, phosphorylated, size-selected, dA-tailed, and ligated to indexed paired-end adaptors using the Agilent SureSelectXT Mouse All Exon Kit V2, which covers 49.6 Mb of mouse protein-coding sequences.

Whole-exome sequencing and mapping

The library was sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 sequencer using the HiSeq SBS Kit v4 or the HiSeq Rapid SBS Kit v2. An image analysis and base calling were performed with a real-time analysis software program, yielding 125- or 100-bp paired-end reads, respectively. The library adapter sequences at the 3’ ends were removed with cutadapt 1.7.133, and low-quality bases (q-score < 16) at both ends were trimmed. Pairs containing reads shorter than 72 nt were eliminated, resulting in 1136 million read pairs consisting of 278 Gb. The trimmed paired-end reads were mapped to the mouse reference sequence GRCm38-based UCSC mm10, which was derived from the C57BL/6 J inbred strain, using the MEM algorithm of BWA 0.7.1734. SAM and BAM files were handled using SAMtools 1.6. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) duplicates were removed using the Picard Tools 2.17.11. Multisample calling following local realignment was performed using HaplotypeCaller of GATK 3.8–1. For local realignment, mouse indel sites were obtained from the dbSNP build 150.

Sifting variants and allele coding for machine learning

Variants other than mm10, chr1–chr19, were eliminated, meaning that variants of sex chromosomes, mitochondrial DNA, and unlocalized or unplaced contigs were not considered. Variants whose total read depth was < 720, which was the sum of all 36-sample reads, assuming 20 reads per sample, were eliminated. Variants that were not called in more than 6 out of 36 individuals were eliminated. Variants that were common to more than 32 individuals were also eliminated. Genotypes of homozygotes of the reference allele, heterozygotes of the reference and alternative alleles, and consisting of non-reference alleles were simply scored or coded 0.0, 0.5, and 1.0, respectively. The numbers of tolerant (long survival) and rejected (short survival) donor mice were initially 18 and 18, respectively. By performing a χ2 test, 4294 variants were obtained under the condition that p-values were < 0.1. When CD1-137 was treated as a tolerance case, the corresponding χ2 test gave us 6517 variants under the same conditions. The SciPy 1.7.0 library was employed to perform the χ2 tests with Yates’ correction. SnpEff 4.3 (https://pcingola.github.io/SnpEff/) was used for gene annotation of each variant.

Combined sequence ontology

To count the number of variants, overlapping variant locations on the same gene ontological features were combined into less specific locations (Supplementary Table 1).

Design matrix and unsupervised machine learning

By consolidating the feature vectors, the design matrix consisted of 36 rows (samples) and 6517 columns (variants or features). The values were 0.0, 0.5, or 1.0. Hierarchical clustering was performed using Euclidean distance and Ward linkage methods. Clustering and drawing of the heatmap and dendrogram were processed using the SciPy library 1.7.0. A principal component analysis was performed using the Scikit-learn library 0.24.2.

Deep learning

The optimized deep neural network consists of one-dimensional convolutional, max-pooling, and affine layers with dropout35. The hidden layers contained four fully connected layers with node numbers of 3271, 312, 29, and 7. A rectified linear unit (ReLU) was employed as the activation function for each. The last output, either tolerant or intolerant, was obtained using a sigmoid function, implemented using a Python script with TensorFlow 1.12.0 (https://www.tensorflow.org/) and Keras 2.2.4 (https://keras.io/) on Ubuntu 16.04.3, running on a Hitachi SR24000/DL1. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) was employed as an optimizer with a 10−2 learning rate and 10−6 decay. Training was performed for 500 epochs with a batch size of 64.

Linear classification and getting average weight values

Linear classification was performed using Jubatus 1.1.1, (https://jubat.us/) installed on CentOS 7.6, running on an HPE ProLiant DL360. The value of the regularization weight was 0.6, and the number of epochs was 200. Two-class classifier models were built using a Bourne shell and Python script. The classification model consisted of the weight or coefficient values of all features for each of the two classes, i.e. rejection and tolerance. In addition to leave-one-out cross-validation, all 36 samples were used to build the final model, from which the weight values for gene analyses were obtained. All scripts and source programs are available at https://github.com/glires/mmexome.

Functional analyses using Metascape

Genetic data from the top 1000 of 6517 variants in the rejection and tolerance groups were used for GO and KEGG signal pathway enrichment analyses36–38 using the Metascape software program (https://metascape.org) with default parameters.

Statistical analyses

The graft survival time was compared between the groups using the Kaplan–Meier survival curve and log-rank test. Parametric data were analyzed using Student’s t-test (two-tailed). P values were considered statistically significant at < 0.05.

Guidelines

All mice were maintained in a specific-pathogen-free animal facility, used under the National Institutes of Health guidelines, and approved by the Institutional Animal Care Use Committee (IACUC) of the Cleveland Clinic.
