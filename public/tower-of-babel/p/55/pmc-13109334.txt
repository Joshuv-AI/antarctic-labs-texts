Four enzymes from button bush enable de novo biosynthesis of spirooxindole alkaloids in yeast
Authors: Alyssa Dawn Seveck, Jacob Owen Perley, Di Gao, Jiazhang Lian, Yang Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Oxindoles are a structurally unique subclass of monoterpenoid indole alkaloids (MIAs) with significant medicinal values such as neuroprotective and anticancer properties and are predominantly found in the Rubiaceae plant family. Recent research has only just begun to uncover the biosynthetic pathways of this complex MIA subclass. Here, we report the discovery, characterization, and functional reconstitution of a four-enzyme pathway from Cephalanthus occidentalis (button bush) that enables the complete de novo biosynthesis of heteroyohimbine-type oxindole alkaloids in engineered yeast. Through transcriptomic mining and biochemical validation, we identified and characterized an ajmalicine synthase (CoAJS), a heteroyohimbine/yohimbine/corynanthe C3-oxidase (CoHYC3O), a C3-reductase (CoHYC3R), and an oxindole synthase (CoOIS) capable of converting strictosidine aglycone into 3-epi-ajmalicine and subsequently into the oxindole alkaloids mitraphylline and isomitraphylline. Saturation kinetics of CoOIS revealed comparable catalytic efficiencies for 3-epi-ajmalicine and its C20 epimer akuammigine, indicating substrate tolerance within the heteroyohimbine subclass. Yeast-based reconstruction of the pathway successfully yielded both mitraphylline epimers and the spirooxindole uncarine F. This work elucidates the enzymatic logic behind oxindole formation and expands the toolkit for MIA scaffold diversification and synthetic biology.

With over 3000 reported members, monoterpenoid indole alkaloids (MIAs) are renowned for their structural complexity and potent medicinal properties [1]. Clinically important MIAs include chemotherapeutics vinblastine isolated from Catharanthus roseus (Madagascar periwinkle) and camptothecin from Camptotheca acuminata (happy tree). Frequent oxidative skeletal rearrangements give rise to a wide range of MIA scaffolds, including a distinct subclass featuring spirooxindole structures, which are well known for their bioactivity on the central nervous system (CNS) [2,3].

One example is gelsemine, a potent CNS toxin from Gelsemium elegans (heartbreak grass) and Gelsemium sempervirens (Carolina jasmine), which targets glycine receptor ion channels [4]. Conversely, oxindole alkaloids such as corynoxeine, rhynchophylline, and isorhynchophylline from Uncaria rhynchophylla (cat's claw) in the Rubiaceae family show neuroprotective and analgesic effects, along with therapeutic potential for Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, depression, and cerebral ischemia [5,6]. Another oxindole, mitraphylline from Mitragyna speciosa (kratom), has demonstrated anticancer activity [7]. Their therapeutic potential warrants investigation into their biosynthetic pathways to enable biochemical production.

Previously, Nguyen et al. characterized a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase from M. speciosa, designated Ms3eCIS (3-epi-corynoxeine/isocorynoxeine synthase), which catalyzes oxidative rearrangement of corynanthe-type substrates such as hirsuteine and hirsutine into oxindole derivatives like 3-epi-corynoxeine and rhynchophylline (Fig. 1) [8]. Almost all MIAs originate from strictosidine, produced via a Pictet-Spengler condensation catalyzed by strictosidine synthase (STR) from tryptamine and secologanin. While the condensation theoretically gives two diastereoers strictosidine (3S) and vindoside (3R), STR strictly yields a 3S stereochemistry at C3 [1]. Deglycosylation of strictosidine produces strictosidine aglycone, a reactive dialdehyde intermediate that spontaneously cyclizes into a range of iminium diastereomers, which are reduced by cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD)-like reductases into stable alkaloid products belonging to the yohimbine, heteroyohimbine, and corynanthe subclasses, all of which retain the original 3S configuration [[9], [10], [11], [12]] (Fig. 1).

Recently, we reported a conserved enzyme pair: C3-oxidase (HYC3O) and C3-reductase (HYC3R) across Apocynaceae, Rubiaceae, and Gelsemiaceae families, which catalyze the consecutive oxidation and reduction steps that invert the stereochemistry from 3S to 3R (Fig. 1) [13]. These genes are found in tight genomic clusters in C. roseus and Rauvolfia tetraphylla, suggesting coordinated evolution and regulation. This discovery, alongside recent studies from other laboratories [14,15], opens new avenues to understand the biosynthesis of diverse oxindoles across plant species.

In this study, we characterize the phytochemistry and the heteroyohimbine oxindole biosynthesis in Cephalanthus occidentalis (button bush), a North American Rubiaceae species. We report the first identification and isolation of ajmalicine, 3-epi-ajmalicine, mitraphylline, and isomitraphylline from this plant. With a sequenced leaf transcriptome, we identify an ajmalicine synthase (CoAJS), HYC3O/HYC3R oxidoreductase pair, and an oxindole synthase (OIS) from C. occidentalis, the latter of which shares 75 ​% amino acid identity with Ms3eCIS. Notably, we demonstrate the broad substrate and product spectra of both CoOIS and Ms3eCIS, showing that these enzymes can form several oxindoles from heteroyohimbine (3-epi-ajmalicine/akuammigine) and yohimbine-type (rauwolscine) skeletons, contributing to the enzymatic toolkit to derive MIA oxindole-based pharmaceuticals. With these new enzymes, we demonstrate the de novo biosynthesis of oxindoles in yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

Material and methods

Chemicals

Mitraphylline and ajmalicine standards were obtained from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Strictosidine was enzymatically synthesized by incubating secologanin and tryptamine with strictosidine synthase (STR), following established protocols [11]. The 3-dehydro-ajmalicine standard was generated by enzymatic oxidation of ajmalicine using HYC3O, as previously described [13]. Tetrahydroalstonine was purified from the leaves of Luculia pinceana from our earlier study [13]. All remaining alkaloid standards used in this study were isolated and structurally validated by 1D/2D NMR spectroscopy.

Alkaloid purification

Cephalanthus occidentalis and Hamelia patens plants used for alkaloid purification were cultivated in a glasshouse under a 16/8 ​h light/dark photoperiod. For total alkaloid extraction, 200–300 ​g of fresh leaves from either species were simply submerged in ethyl acetate overnight. The ethyl acetate extract was concentrated under vacuum, then extracted with 1 ​M HCl. The aqueous phase containing alkaloids was collected, basified to pH 8 with 5 ​M NaOH, and extracted again with ethyl acetate to isolate the total alkaloid fraction. The total alkaloid was evaporated to dryness and reconstituted in methanol for preparative thin-layer chromatography (TLC, Silica Gel 60 F254, Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA). For C. occidentalis, TLC development with ethyl acetate:methanol (9:1, v/v) yielded two major bands: mitraphylline (Rf ​= ​0.90) and 3-epi-ajmalicine (Rf ​= ​0.38). These were further purified on TLC using toluene:ethyl acetate:methanol (15:4:1, v/v/v), affording pure mitraphylline (Rf ​= ​0.40; 5.6 ​mg) and 3-epi-ajmalicine (Rf ​= ​0.12; 3.4 ​mg). For H. patens, TLC development with ethyl acetate:methanol (9:1, v/v) yielded mixed alkaloids including speciophylline and pteropodine (Rf ​= ​0.76), and uncarine F and isopteropodine (Rf ​= ​0.44). These were further resolved using chloroform:acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) as the developing solvent. Final yields of pure compounds were as follows: speciophylline (Rf ​= ​0.21; 1.3 ​mg), pteropodine (Rf ​= ​0.69; 0.9 ​mg), uncarine F (Rf ​= ​0.48; 0.7 ​mg), and isopteropodine (Rf ​= ​0.54; 1.9 ​mg).

LC-MS/MS and NMR

The samples were analyzed by Ultivo Triple Quadrupole LC-MS/MS system from Agilent (Santa Clara, CA, USA), equipped with an Avantor® ACE® UltraCore C18 2.5 Super C18 column (50 ​× ​3 ​mm, particle size 2.5 ​μm), a photodiode array detector, and a mass spectrometer. The mobile phases were: Solvent A: Methanol:Acetonitrile:1 ​M Ammonium Acetate:Water (29:71:2:398) and Solvent B: Methanol:Acetonitrile:1 ​M Ammonium Acetate:Water (130:320:0.25:49.7). A linear gradient was applied as follows: 0–5 ​min, 80 ​% A/20 ​% B; 5–5.8 ​min, 1 ​% A/99 ​% B; 5.8–8 ​min, 80 ​% A/20 ​% B. The flow rate was maintained at 0.6 ​mL/min. The photodiode array detector scanned from 200 to 500 ​nm. The mass spectrometer operated in positive ion mode with a capillary voltage of 4 ​kV, gas temperature of 300 ​°C, and a gas flow rate of 10 ​L/min. Scans were performed from m/z 100 to 1000 with a 500 ​ms scan time and a fragmentor voltage of 135 ​V. Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) was used with precursor/product ion pairs identified from authentic standards. NMR spectra were recorded on an Agilent 400 ​MR and a Bruker Avance III HD 400 ​MHz NMR spectrometer in CDCl3.

Bioinformatics and phylogenetics

Total RNA from Cephalanthus occidentalis leaves was extracted using the Monarch® Total RNA Miniprep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA sequencing was performed by the Atlantic Cancer Research Institute (Moncton, NB, Canada) using the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Raw reads were assembled de novo using the Trinity pipeline with default parameters. BLAST searches and RNA-seq expression analyses were conducted using CLC Genomics Workbench version 20.0.4 (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Phylogenetic analyses were performed using MEGA11. The evolutionary history was inferred using the Maximum Likelihood method based on the JTT matrix-based model. The tree with the highest log likelihood is presented. Bootstrap values (100 replicates) are shown at the branches, indicating the percentage of trees in which the associated taxa clustered together. Initial heuristic searches were generated automatically using the Neighbor-Join and BioNJ algorithms applied to a pairwise distance matrix estimated with the JTT model. The topology with the best log likelihood was selected for final tree construction.

Cloning

Full-length CoAJS, CoHYC3R, CoOIS, Ms3eCIS, CrHYC3R were amplified from plant cDNA using primers 1–10, and cloned in pESC vectors by restriction digestion or Gibson assembly. CrHYC3R and CoHYC3R were cloned in pESC-His (BamHI/SalI). CoOIS was cloned in pESC-Ura (BamHI/SalI). CoAJS was cloned in pESC-Ura (NotI/SpeI) alone or together with CoOIS. Ms3eCIS was cloned in pESC-Leu (ApaI/SalI). CoHYC3O and CrHYC3O were synthesized (Twist Bioscience, South San Francisco, CA, USA) and subcloned in pESC-Leu (BamHI/SalI). The constructs were mobilized separately or in combination to Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain AJM-dHYS previously engineered for strictosidine aglycone de novo biosynthesis [16,17] using a standard Lithium acetate-based transformation. To test the substrate spectra of CoOIS and Ms3eCIS, the constructs were mobilized to Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain BY4741 (MATα his3Δ1 leu2Δ0 met15Δ0 ura3Δ0 YPL154ckanMX4). All cDNA sequences including CoAJS (OQ129431), CoHYC3O (PP911575), and CoOIS (PQ801650) are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The primers are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Yeast cultivation and biotransformation

A single yeast colony was inoculated into 1 ​mL synthetic complete (SC) medium supplemented with 2 ​% glucose, with appropriate amino acids dropped out. The yeasts were grown overnight at 30 ​°C at 250 ​rpm. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed with sterile water, and re-inoculated into 1 ​mL SC medium containing 2 ​% galactose to induce gene expression. Inducing cultures were incubated overnight under the same conditions. For de novo alkaloid biosynthesis analysis, 0.1 ​mL of yeast culture was mixed with an equal volume of methanol, centrifuged, and the supernatant was filtered prior to analysis by LC-MS/MS. For substrate feeding assays to assess CoOIS and Ms3eCIS substrate specificity, induced yeast cells were harvested and resuspended in 100 ​μL of 20 ​mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.5 supplemented with 1 ​μg alkaloid substrate. Cultures were incubated overnight at 30 ​°C at 250 ​rpm. After incubation, samples were mixed with an equal volume of methanol, centrifuged, filtered, and analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

Yeast microsome isolation

Overnight cultures (2 ​mL) of yeast expressing CoOIS or Ms3eCIS were used to inoculate 200 ​mL SC media with 2 ​% glucose. Cultures were grown at 30 ​°C at 250 ​rpm for 24 ​h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed with sterile water, and re-inoculated into 200 ​mL SC medium containing 2 ​% galactose, and incubated overnight under the same conditions. Yeast cells were collected by centrifugation and lysed in TES buffer (10 ​mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 1 ​mM EDTA; 0.6 ​M sorbitol) using glass beads and a Beadbug® homogenizer (Benchmark Scientific, Sayreville, NJ, USA). The lysis was conducted at 30 ​Hz for 3 ​min, repeated four times at 4 ​°C. The crude lysate was centrifuged at 8000 ​g for 10 ​min at 4 ​°C. The resulting supernatant was centrifuged at 100,000 ​g for 1 ​h at 4 ​°C to pellet the microsomes containing cytochrome P450 monooxygenases. The microsomal fraction was resuspended in TEG buffer (10 ​mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5; 1 ​mM EDTA; 10 ​% [v/v] glycerol) and stored at −80 ​°C.

Enzyme kinetics

Saturation kinetics of CoOIS were performed using yeast microsomal proteins in 100 ​μL reactions. For 3-epi-ajmalicine, each reaction contained 200 ​μg microsomal protein, 1 ​mM NADPH, and varying concentrations of substrate (0.179–70 ​μM) in 20 ​mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5. For akuammigine, each reaction contained 50 ​μg microsomal protein, 1 ​mM NADPH, and substrate concentrations ranging from 0.179 to 140 ​μM in the same buffer. Reactions were incubated at 30 ​°C for 30 ​min and quenched by 150 ​μL methanol. After centrifugation, the supernatants were analyzed by LC-MS/MS. Kinetic parameters (KM, Vmax) were determined by non-linear regression fitting using Michaelis-Menten equation in GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.0).

Results

C. occidentalis accumulates ajmalicine 3-epimers and mitraphylline 7-epimers

Using an acid-base extraction method, total leaf alkaloids were analyzed by liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), which revealed five major MIAs in C. occidentalis leaves (Fig. 2). We identified strictosidine, ajmalicine, and mitraphylline with authentic standards. De novo production of 3-epi-ajmalicine in yeast via a three-enzyme pathway

We sequenced total RNA from C. occidentalis leaves and identified all key MIA biosynthetic genes through comparative analysis with characterized genes from other species. (Supplementary Table 5). To functionally characterize the new genes, we expressed the C. occidentalis ajmalicine synthase (CoAJS) [9], CoHYC3O, and CoHYC3R [13] in a previously engineered yeast strain capable of de novo strictosidine aglycone production [16,17]. Expression of CoAJS alone resulted in the biosynthesis of ajmalicine (major product), mayumbine, and tetrahydroalstonine, mirroring the product profile of Catharanthus roseus heteroyohimbine synthase (CrHYS), despite a relatively low amino acid identity of 63.5 ​% between the two enzymes (Fig. 3) [12]. Co-expression of CoHYC3O or MsHYC3O from M. speciosa led to the accumulation of 3-dehydro-ajmalicine and a putative 3-dehydro-mayumbine, which eluted after the 3-dehydro-ajmalicine standard. We next expressed both Ms3eCIS and C. occidentalis candidate in yeast to study their activity. When yeast expressing either CYP were fed with 3-epi-ajmalicine, both mitraphylline and isomitraphylline accumulated in the broth (Fig. 5). Based on the results, we named the new CYP as C. occidentalis oxindole synthase (CoOIS). Interestingly, both CYPs also produced a third product, 3-dehydro-ajmalicine from 3-epi-ajmalicine substrate, showing identical retention time and MS/MS fragmentation patterns with an authentic standard. However, epimerization of mitraphylline standards in aqueous solution complicated definitive product assignments.

When the yeasts were fed with akuammigine (3-epi-tetraphydroalstonine), the 20-epimer of 3-epi-ajmalicine, we observed the production of four oxindole diastereomers differing at stereochemistry at C3 and C7 (Fig. 5). The identity of each compound was identified by comparing to authentic standards that we purified from the plant Hamelia patens and structurally resolved by 1D/2D NMR (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, Figs. 13–36). Notably, the two major diastereomers pteropodine and uncarine F from both CYPs contain 7R stereochemistry. Considering the higher production of mitraphylline (7R) from 3-epi-ajmalicine, we suspect that the direct oxindole products from both substrates are 7R spirooxindoles.

We further evaluated the substrate specificities of CoOIS and Ms3eCIS using a panel of 3S and 3R alkaloids spanning the heteroyohimbine, yohimbine, and corynanthe scaffolds. These included the 3S-configured alkaloids ajmalicine, tetrahydroalstonine, yohimbine, rauwolscine, alloyohimbine, geissoschizine methyl ether, corynantheidine, and mitragynine, as well as 3R-configured alkaloids such as 3-epi-yohimbine, 3-epi-rauwolscine, and speciophylline. CoOIS exhibited no detectable oxidative activity with these additional substrates, indicating high substrate specificity for 3R-heteroyohimbines. Ms3eCIS additionally oxidized 3-epi-rauwolscine into an oxindole, as evidenced by the characteristic 250 ​nm UV absorption peak, in addition to its previously known activity on hirsuteine (Supplementary Fig. 37). These results expand the known substrate scope of Ms3eCIS to include 3R-configured alkaloids across all three primary MIA structural classes, yohimbine, heteroyohimbine, and corynanthe, highlighting its broad catalytic promiscuity relative to the highly selective CoOIS.

To expand structural diversity, we next targeted the biosynthesis of oxindoles derived from akuammigine. Co-expression of HYC3O and HYC3R from C. roseus enabled the de novo production of akuammigine in yeast. Discussion

Oxindole alkaloids represent a structurally distinct and pharmacologically important subclass of MIAs. Although rare within the Apocynaceae family, oxindoles are prominently accumulated in the Rubiaceae and Gelsemiaceae families, with notable examples including rhynchophylline and gelsemine [18].

In contrast to conserved strictosidine reductases, HYC3Os, and HYC3Os from Apocynaceae, Rubiaceae, and Gelsemiaceae families [13], our transcriptomic survey across several well-characterized MIA-producing Apocynaceae species, Catharanthus roseus, Rauvolfia serpentina, Vinca minor, and Tabernaemontana elegans, did not reveal any close homologs of known oxindole synthases such as OcOIS or Ms3eCIS, suggesting that these CYPs have evolved specifically within the Rubiaceae lineage. Interestingly, even within Gelsemium sempervirens, a known producer of oxindoles, we could not identify homologs of either OcOIS or Ms3eCIS, implying that oxindole biosynthesis in this species may proceed via a distinct oxidase with low sequence similarity.

Biochemical assays demonstrated that CoOIS efficiently converts 3-epi-ajmalicine and akuammigine, both belonging to the heteroyohimbine subclass, into their corresponding oxindoles. However, CoOIS showed no activity toward yohimbine or corynanthe-type substrates, indicating higher degree of substrate specificity. This enzymatic profile is consistent with the in planta accumulation of mitraphylline and isomitraphylline, both heteroyohimbine-derived oxindoles, in C. occidentalis.

In contrast, Ms3eCIS, originally characterized for its activity on corynanthe-type substrates, was shown here to possess much broader substrate scope. It not only acts on its known corynanthe type substrates but also converts heteroyohimbines such as 3-epi-ajmalicine and akuammigine, as well as 3-epi-rauwolscine with a yohimbine skeleton. This broad activity range supports the observed accumulation of mitraphylline epimers and structurally diverse corynanthe oxindoles (e.g., corynoxeine) in M. speciosa.

Using a combination of C. occidentalis AJS, HYC3O, HYC3R, and OIS, we achieved the de novo biosynthesis of mitraphylline 7-epimers in yeast. In a complementary approach, substituting the oxidoreductases with C. roseus HYC3O and HYC3R (the latter also functioning as tetrahydroalstonine synthase) enabled biosynthesis of uncarine F, an akuammigine-derived oxindole. While these oxindole alkaloids were successfully synthesized de novo in yeast, their titers remained extremely low, pointing to current limitations in the pathway.

A major bottleneck in the de novo biosynthesis of oxindole alkaloids in yeast lies in the activity of HYC3O, a flavin-dependent oxidase that requires secretory pathway-associated post-translational modifications, such as glycosylation, for proper folding and enzymatic function. Consequently, the accumulation of alkaloids dependent on HYC3O activity, namely 3-dehydro and oxindole products, was approximately two to three orders of magnitude lower than the accumulation of 3S alkaloids derived from direct reduction of strictosidine aglycone.

In previous work, we showed that replacing the native signal peptide of another flavin-dependent enzyme, C. roseus O-acetylstemmadenine oxidase (ASO), with the yeast carboxypeptidase Y (CPY) signal peptide significantly improved its expression and activity in yeast [19]. Similar strategies, including signal peptide engineering or expression in alternate compartments, may be applied to enhance HYC3O activity. Additionally, we observed extracellular accumulation of 3-dehydro intermediates in the yeast fermentation broth. These charged compounds are unlikely to re-enter cells by passive diffusion, which may disrupt pathway flux. Enhancing the activity or expression level of HYC3R, or identifying variants with improved catalytic efficiency, may help redirect flux toward downstream oxindole products.

In summary, our study provides new insights into the biosynthetic origins and diversification of oxindole alkaloids, revealing two homologous but functionally distinct CYP oxidases with complementary substrate preferences. A closely related homolog has also recently been characterized in Uncaria rhynchophylla, another oxindole-rich Rubiaceae species [14]. Together, these enzymes expand the biocatalytic toolkit for structural diversification of MIAs and lay the foundation for engineering microbial platforms for sustainable oxindole production.
