Perioperative and short-term outcomes of laparoscopic radical prostatectomy in elderly patients
Authors: Qi Zhang, Guobin Weng, Xuping Yao, Gang Wang, Jianjun Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo precisely assess whether laparoscopic radical prostatectomy is safe and efficient in elderly individuals suffering from localized prostate cancer in the perioperative and short-term postoperative periods.MethodsA comprehensive retrospective analysis was undertaken of the clinical records pertaining to patients who underwent laparoscopic radical prostatectomy at our institution, spanning from January 2022 to November 2024. These patients were subsequently categorized into an elderly cohort (≥80 years old, n=54) and a non-elderly cohort (<80 years old, n=67) on the basis of age. Aside from that, the surgical safety and efficacy were compared between these two groups.ResultsThe patients were stratified into two distinct groups: the elderly cohort and the non-elderly cohort. Notably, no statistically discernible differences emerged between thesese two groups with regard to the duration of the surgical procedure [(136.9 ± 47.8) min vs. (140.5 ± 40.4) min], intraoperative blood loss [(11.9 ± 8.1) g/L vs. (12.4 ± 6.3) g/L], postoperative renal function changes [(5.6 ± 14.9) μmol/L vs. (2.3 ± 9.1) μmol/L], in dwelling catheter time [(10.0 ± 2.8) days vs. (10.4 ± 2.5) days], hospital stay [(14.8 ± 4.5) days vs. (14.5 ± 3.9) days], and hospitalization costs [(25,638.3 ± 5,389.5) yuan vs. (24,589.8 ± 4,162.5) yuan] (all P > 0.05). Nonetheless, the recovery time of intestinal function [1 (1, 2) days vs. 1 (1, 1) days] demonstrated a conspicuous difference (P < 0.05). At 6 weeks postoperatively, the tPSA levels were [(0.019 ± 0.018) ng/mL vs. (0.017 ± 0.017) ng/mL] in the two groups, with no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05).Postoperative intensive care unit transfers (all for 1 day) were 21 and 8 cases in the two groups, separately, while infection-related fever occurred in 13 and 7 cases, severally, both showing statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). No significant disparity in urinary control was detected between these two groups at the 3-month follow-up post-catheter removal (P > 0.05).ConclusionFor elderly individuals suffering from localized prostate cancer, laparoscopic radical prostatectomy has demonstrated favorable safety and efficacy in the perioperative and short-term postoperative periods, and is deemed a feasible surgical option for patients with favorable overall physical health.

Introduction

As one of the most ubiquitous cancers, prostate cancer (PCA) stands as a complex disease affecting millions of men worldwide [1]. Over the past 30 years, the incidence rate of PCA has been continuously increasing with the economic development and the intensification of population aging in our country [2]. For localized PCA, radical prostatectomy (RP) is the standard treatment modality. Laparoscopic radical prostatectomy (LRP) demonstrates significant clinical benefits, characterized by its minimally invasive nature, accelerated postoperative recovery, and decreased intraoperative blood loss. These attributes collectively support its role as a safe and efficacious surgical approach for prostate cancer management [3]. For elderly PCA patients, considering cardiopulmonary insufficiency and comorbid conditions, there has been a conservative and cautious attitude towards surgical treatment in the past. In recent years, the physical condition of elderly patients has conspicuously improved with the improvement of living standards, making surgery perhaps a better option for treating PCA. This study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of patients who underwent LRP in our hospital from January 2022 to November 2024, so as to probe into the safety and efficacy of surgery in elderly patients in the perioperative and short-term postoperative periods.

Methods

General information

This study included 121 patients who all underwent LRP. Grounded in surgical age, they were divided into an elderly group (≥ 80 years old, n = 54), and a non-elderly group (< 80 years old, n = 67). The average ages were (81.2 ± 1.6) years and (68.4 ± 6.0) years, severally. Inclusion criteria for this study: Individuals enduring localized prostate acinar adenocarcinoma confirmed by prostate biopsy pathology. Exclusion criteria: (1) Preoperative imaging suggesting locally advanced or metastatic tumor; (2) Contraindications to surgery such as coagulation disorders or severe systemic diseases; (3) History of previous pelvic surgery or radiotherapy; (4) Concurrent presence of other pelvic masses. Upon conducting a comparative analysis between these elderly group and the non-elderly group, no statistically significant disparities emerged in prostate volume [(41.3 ± 22.0) ml vs. (35.1 ± 14.6) ml], preoperative total prostate-specific antigen (tPSA) [(17.1 ± 11.0) ng/ml vs. (14.0 ± 13.4) ng/ml], body mass index (BMI) [(22.8 ± 3.3) kg/m² vs. (23.8 ± 3.4) kg/m²], the time interval between prostate biopsy and surgery [(19.9 ± 9.1) days vs. (21.7 ± 12.4) days], and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [0 (0, 1) vs. 0 (0, 0)] (P > 0.05). As demonstrated in Table 1, there was no statistically significant difference in the preoperative American Society of Anesthesiology (ASA) classification between these two groups of patients (P > 0.05). The clinical staging of patients in both the elderly and non-elderly groups was ≤ cT2c. The preoperative Gleason scores were ≤ 6 in 14 and 17 cases, 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 in 24 and 32 cases, and ≥ 8 in 16 and 18 cases in the two groups, separately, and no statistically significant differences were observed between these two groups (P > 0.05), as demonstrated in Table 2.

Surgical method

The patient was positioned supine with the head lowered and feet elevated at a 15-degree angle, legs slightly apart, and an F16 double-lumen catheter was inserted. A vertical skin incision, approximately 3 cm in length, was made below the umbilicus, and the subcutaneous tissue and anterior sheath of the rectus abdominis muscle were cut through. Subsequently, the rectus abdominis muscle was separated, and the extraperitoneal space was dissected along the superficial surface of the posterior sheath of the rectus abdominis muscle using fingers. A balloon dilator was inserted and inflated with 1000 ml of air to expand the extraperitoneal space further. Under finger guidance, 5 mm and 12 mm Trocars were inserted at the left and right lateral edges of the rectus abdominis muscles to serve as operative channels. A 10 mm Trocar was inserted at the upper end of the incision, which was then sutured, and the Trocar was secured with silk sutures. CO2 was insufflated to maintain a pneumoperitoneum pressure of 14 mmHg, and an observation lens was inserted. Under direct vision, a 5 mm Trocar was inserted 2 cm medial to the right anterior superior iliac spine as an auxiliary channel. The fat and loose tissue on the surface of the prostate and pelvic fascia were separated, and the puboprostatic ligament was exposed. The pelvic fascia on both sides was opened, the levator ani muscle was pushed aside, and the puboprostatic ligament was severed. The dorsal venous complex of the penis was suture-ligated with 2 − 0 absorbable sutures. The bladder neck position was confirmed by traction on the catheter, and the bladder neck was routinely severed. The bilateral vas deferens was identified and severed, and the bilateral seminal vesicles were freed using a combination of blunt and sharp dissection. Denonvillier’s fascia was incised, and the posterior aspect of the prostate was bluntly dissected to the apex. The lateral ligaments and vascular pedicles of the prostate were clamped with Hem-o-lok clips and then severed. After the prostate was fully freed, the suture-ligated dorsal venous complex of the penis was severed, and the urethra was exposed using a combination of blunt and sharp dissection. After retaining an adequate length of the urethra, it was severed at the apex of the prostate, and the prostate and seminal vesicles were completely excised. The wound was thoroughly hemostasized, and the urethra and bladder neck were sutured with 3 − 0 monofilament Vicryl absorbable sutures. Halfway through the suturing, a new F18 double-lumen catheter was inserted through the external urethral orifice into the bladder. High-risk patients underwent bilateral extended pelvic lymph node dissection, including the external iliac artery/vein, the medial aspect of the internal iliac artery, and the obturator lymph nodes. The incision was reopened, the specimen was removed through the incision, and a pelvic negative pressure drainage tube was left in place. All incisions were routinely sutured.

Observation indicators

The operation time was recorded. Intraoperative blood loss was calculated as the difference between preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin levels. Postoperative renal function changes were determined by comparing preoperative and postoperative serum creatinine levels. Transfers to the intensive care unit (ICU) postoperatively, length of ICU stay, indwelling catheter duration, total hospital stay, and hospitalization costs were documented. The recovery time of intestinal function, defined as the interval from surgery to resumption of oral intake, was noted. Postoperative pathological examination results were analyzed. Occurrences of postoperative complications, including infection-related fever (> 38 °C), lower extremity venous thrombosis, and urethral stricture, were systematically tracked. The tPSA levels at 6 weeks postoperatively were compared between the two study groups. Urinary control recovery was assessed at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months after catheter removal. Postoperative urinary incontinence severity was categorized as follows: ① ≤1 pad/day was defined as normal; ② 2–3 pads/day indicated mild abnormality; ③ >3 pads/day constituted severe abnormality. And quality of life was assessed with patient reported urinary incontinence severity. All measurements and classifications were consistently applied across both cohorts to ensure comparative validity.

Statistical methods

The SPSS 22.0 statistical software was used for analysis. In addition, the mean ± standard deviation (x ® ± s) was used for measurement indicators that followed a normal distribution. Aside from that, inter-group comparisons were conducted using the t-test. For those that did not follow a normal distribution, the median (first quartile, third quartile) (M (Q1, Q3)) was used, and inter-group comparisons were performed using the U-test. Count indicators were expressed as frequencies and analyzed using the chi-square test. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

All 121 patients successfully completed the surgery without the need for blood transfusion during or after the operation. Apart from that, there were no instances of rectal injury. The postoperative gross pathology in both the elderly and non-elderly groups was acinar adenocarcinoma of the prostate. The Gleason scores were ≤ 6 in 4 and 3 cases, 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 in 32 and 45 cases, and ≥ 8 in 18 and 19 cases, separately. The pathological stages pT2a, pT2b, pT2c, and pT3a were observed in 4 and 2, 3 and 6, 36 and 49, and 11 and 10 cases, severally. Positive surgical margins were found in 6 and 7 cases, and lymph node positivity was observed in 2 and 3 cases. There were no statistically significant differences in the above indicators between these two groups (P > 0.05), as demonstrated in Table 3. For individuals enduring high-risk PCA postoperatively, adjuvant endocrine therapy was initiated 6 weeks after surgery, lasting 12–18 months. individuals suffering from positive surgical margins received immediate adjuvant endocrine therapy, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy rooted in patient compliance after urinary control recovery. Patients suffering from positive lymph nodes underwent immediate adjuvant endocrine therapy. Excluding those with positive margins and lymph nodes, the tPSA levels at 6 weeks postoperatively were [(0.019 ± 0.018) ng/ml vs. (0.017 ± 0.017) ng/ml] in the elderly and non-elderly groups, separately, with no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05), and all tPSA levels were < 0.1 ng/ml at 3 months follow-up, indicating no biochemical recurrence. There were no statistically significant differences between these two groups with regard to operation time [(136.9 ± 47.8) min vs. (140.5 ± 40.4) min], intraoperative blood loss [(11.9 ± 8.1) g/l vs. (12.4 ± 6.3) g/l], postoperative renal function changes [(5.6 ± 14.9) umol/l vs. (2.3 ± 9.1) umol/l], indwelling catheter time [(10.0 ± 2.8) days vs. (10.4 ± 2.5) days], hospital stay [(14.8 ± 4.5) days vs. (14.5 ± 3.9) days], and hospitalization costs [(25638.3 ± 5389.5) yuan vs. (24589.8 ± 4162.5) yuan] (P > 0.05). However, there was a statistically significant difference in the recovery time of intestinal function [1 (1, 2) days vs. 1 (1, 1) days] (P < 0.05), as illustrated in Table 4.

Discussion

The incidence of PCA in China has been demonstrating an upward trend on a yearly basis [4]. Localized PCA is often missed attributable to the deficiency of conspicuous clinical symptoms [5]. As a specific glycoprotein produced by prostate cells, Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is characterized by high sensitivity and specificity in diagnosing PCA. As already evidently demonstrated by studies, when tPSA was higher than 10 ng/ml, the sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing PCA are 61.7% and 69.7%, separately [6]. In recent years, the detection rate of localized PCA in China has gradually increased thanks to the widespread use of PSA screening and prostate biopsies. For individuals suffering from localized PCA, early detection and treatment are associated with low to intermediate recurrence risk, with a 10-year survival rate exceeding 90% [1]. Radical radiotherapy is one treatment option for localized PCA [7]. Nevertheless, this modality is frequently accompanied by deleterious sequelae, particularly radiation-induced cystitis and proctitis, coupled with an elevated risk of secondary malignancies, which collectively engender significant patient apprehension and treatment reluctance. For elderly patients concerned about the risks of radiotherapy and surgery, endocrine therapy alone also serves as a viable option. Nonetheless, studies suggest that for localized PCA, endocrine therapy alone does not improve overall survival (OS) [8]. On top of that, endocrine therapy can give rise to a great many complications such as hot flashes, irritability, and gynecomastia, negatively impacting patients’ quality of life. The long-term cost of maintaining endocrine therapy is also relatively high, particularly for patients in remote areas, where regular hospital visits are time-consuming and labor-intensive, making it a less favorable option from an economic perspective. In addition, long-term endocrine therapy can give rise to disease progression, thereby bringing about castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), which further deteriorates patients’ quality of life [9]. RP is a curative treatment for localized PCA, including open radical prostatectomy (ORP), LRP, and robot-assisted laparoscopic radical prostatectomy (RALP). Since Schuessler et al. [10]pioneered the application of LRP, and through subsequent surgical refinements by Guillonneau and Vallancien [11], this technique has achieved universal adoption and progressively evolved into the standard-of-care intervention.

As age increases, the majority of patients experience a corresponding augment in comorbid conditions, a reduction in overall health, and an heightened surgical risk. In the past, palliative treatments were often adopted for elderly patients on account of immature surgical techniques and outdated equipment [12]. In recent years, RP has evolved with increasing sophistication, incorporating advanced surgical instrumentation into clinical workflows. Central to surgical candidacy evaluation is the patient’s projected survival horizon, with a decade serving as a pivotal determinant for therapeutic decision-making. For healthy 80-year-old individuals, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines indicate a life expectancy of 12.5 years [13]. For this reason, surgical treatment is feasible for elderly PCA patients in favorable health. As already revealed by studies, the 10-year OS rate is 62% for patients aged 80–81 undergoing RP. By contrast, the 10-year OS rate can further increase to 67.9% [14] if the CCI score is 0. Thompson et al. [15] conducted long-term follow-ups on PCA patients over 80 years old who underwent RP and arrived at a conclusion that their OS was not strikingly different from that of patients aged 60–69 and 70–79 who underwent RP, with no notable perioperative complications observed. Wang Keyi et al. [16] analyzed data from 40 PCA patients over 80 years old who underwent RP and concluded that RP is safe and feasible for healthy PCA patients over 80 years old, but they also recommended a comprehensive consideration of the benefits and risks of surgery. Shahait et al. [17] suggested that for well-selected octogenarian men, surgery is a feasible option for localized prostate cancer, while emphasizing the necessity of thorough preoperative patient counseling.

Charlson et al. [18]recommended a CCI score, which is a tool employed to assess the burden of chronic diseases and comorbidities in patients, evaluate their overall health status, as well as predict survival rates and prognosis. Among the elderly group, 38 cases had a CCI score of 0, and 16 cases had a CCI score of 1 (including 11 cases of diabetes without complications, 3 cases of chronic lung disease, and 2 cases of connective tissue disease), all of whom were classified as having low or no comorbidity burden, indicating a higher predicted survival rate. Furthermore, the ASA classification is a grading standard utilized to assess a patient’s physical condition and surgical risk, evaluating perioperative safety [19]. Among the elderly group, 47 cases were classified as ASA I-II, and 7 cases as ASA III. While certain patients presented with comorbid systemic pathologies, their conditions remained within physiological compensatory reserves, and comprehensive preoperative evaluations failed to demonstrate prohibitive operative risks. This study followed up patients for 3 months postoperatively, which was intended to concentrate on evaluating perioperative and short-term postoperative safety and efficacy. We put forth a standpoint that no patients in the elderly group required blood transfusions during or after surgery. More importantly, there were no cases of rectal injury during surgery. Additionally, no significant differences were observed between these elderly and non-elderly groups with respect to operative time, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative renal function changes, catheter indwelling time, hospital stay, hospitalization costs, and positive surgical margins. With regard to tumor control, excluding individuals suffering from positive margins or lymph nodes, the tPSA levels in the elderly group were lower than 0.1 ng/mL at 6 weeks and 3 months postoperatively, suggesting no biochemical recurrence and satisfactory short-term surgical outcome. When it comes to short-term postoperative complications, there were no significant differences between these two groups in the occurrence of lower extremity venous thrombosis or urethral stricture. The proportion of postoperative fever was higher in the elderly group, which may be associated with comparatively lower immunity in elderly patients. Research indicated that elderly patients exhibit diminished immune competence and reduced physiological resilience, predisposing them to higher risks of postoperative pyrexia and delayed convalescence [20]. Intraoperative temperature maintenance and complete evacuation of blood clots from the surgical site, supplemented by scheduled postoperative wound care, are effective measures to minimize febrile morbidity. Febrile conditions necessitate immediate intervention, especially in elderly patients. In our study, all patients’ fever resolved by adopting symptomatic treatment and regular intravenous administration of sensitive antibiotics for 4–7 days, and did not significantly delay postoperative recovery, extend the hospital stay, or raise hospitalization costs. Postoperative patients transferred to the ICU were all admitted on the day of surgery and transferred back to the general ward the next day in this study. Considering that elderly patients often have vascular sclerosis, which reduces vascular elasticity, their postoperative blood pressure fluctuations are relatively more pronounced, making them more susceptible to cardiovascular complications. And elderly patients experience reduced respiratory reserve and impaired gas exchange, making them more prone to postoperative hypoxemia [21]. For patients with significant intraoperative blood pressure fluctuations and/or excessive carbon dioxide accumulation, we transfer them to the ICU for enhanced monitoring and care. Therefore, the proportion of elderly patients transferred to the ICU postoperatively was relatively higher. Additionally, preoperative psychological reactions such as fear and anxiety are common and, if severe, can affect surgical outcomes and postoperative recovery [22]. As a consequence, it holds tremendously paramount for us to provide appropriate psychological care prior to surgery. Elderly patients are inclined to express more remarkable fear of surgery, especially those with higher ASA and CCI scores. For this patient cohort, articulating the option of postoperative ICU admission for a 24-hour period to receive enhanced surveillance and intensive care may effectively mitigate psychological distress. This also explains why the proportion of postoperative ICU transfers was higher in the elderly group compared to the non-elderly group in this study. Finally, no significant cardiovascular complications or hypoxemia occurred in patients transferred to the ICU. The contraction of intestinal smooth muscle is primarily regulated by the myenteric plexus. Studies have suggested that the density and total amount of myenteric nerves decrease in the elderly population, which potentially brings about weakened intestinal motility and conditions such as constipation [23, 24]. To go it further, postoperative gastrointestinal recovery was protracted, with median time to oral intake resumption at 1 day postoperatively, though extending to 3 days in select instances. During fasting intervals, supportive interventions like fluid resuscitation and gastric prophylaxis were implemented, with no substantial discomfort reported. Urinary incontinence not only stands as a common complication after LRP, but also exert remarkable influence on patients’ quality of life postoperatively. Among the elderly group, 7.4% and 38.9% of patients had normal and mild urinary incontinence, severally, one week after catheter removal. One month later, these proportions were 14.8% and 51.9%, separately, suggesting less desirable urinary control in comparison with the non-elderly group. Three months postoperatively, the proportions of individuals enduring normal and mild urinary incontinence were 40.7% and 44.4%, severally, illustrating no dramatic discrepancy in contrast to the non-elderly group. Notwithstanding the fact that urinary control was less favorable in the elderly group in the short term, it steadily ameliorated with regular pelvic floor muscle training and medications such as duloxetine, and quality of life showed sustained improvement over time also. On the whole, LRP is safe and effective for elderly patients in the perioperative and short-term postoperative periods. Rooted in our experience, the following criteria are recommended for patient selection: ① The patient should be in favorable physical condition, with a CCI score ≤ 1 and an ASA score of I-III; ② Despite comprehensive preoperative disclosure of all potential surgical risks and complications, the patient have a keen desire for surgery; ③ The surgeon should be considerably experienced in performing the procedure.

Undoubtedly, this study has certain limitations. First, it is a single-center study with constrained cases. Second, our study was limited to a 3-month postoperative follow-up period, thus the long-term oncologic data remain unknown. Third, we recognized that while urinary incontinence severity serves as an important indicator, postoperative quality of life encompasses multiple dimensions. Our current assessment, limited to this single parameter, may not fully capture patients’ life quality. Finally, octogenarian men demonstrating low sexual activity rates in our country, erectile function was not included in this study. But prior to surgery, all patients received detailed information about possible erectile function impairment. In light of these limitations, longer-term postoperative follow-up and more comprehensive quality of life assessments are required to further elucidate the long-term safety and efficient of LRP.

Conclusion

Altogether, for elderly individuals suffering from localized prostate cancer, LRP has demonstrated favorable safety and efficacy in the perioperative and short-term postoperative periods, and is deemed a feasible surgical option for patients with favorable overall physical health.
