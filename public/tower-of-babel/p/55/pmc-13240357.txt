Impact of Allostatic Load on Overall Survival in Patients With Endometrial Cancer
Authors: Nikita Sinha, Vanessa Miller, Alexandra Diggs, Emily Broaddus, Victoria Bae‐Jump, Olivia D. Lara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionEndometrial cancer (EC) mortality is rising, with increasing rates of advanced disease and high‐risk histologic subtypes. Allostatic load (AL), a biomarker of cumulative physiologic stress, has been linked to cancer‐associated outcomes. We evaluated the association between baseline AL and overall survival among patients with EC.MethodsPatients diagnosed with EC at a single academic center were retrospectively identified. Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory values were used to calculate the primary exposure of interest, AL, based on ten physiologic biomarkers. A cohort‐defined threshold classified patients as having high versus low AL. Data were analyzed using unadjusted and adjusted models and Kaplan–Meier curves.ResultsA total of 2174 patients (mean age = 63.1 years, SD = 11.2) with EC were assessed for allostatic load at baseline, 69.2% met criteria for high AL (n = 1505). The median follow up time was 49.5 months (IQR = 25.7, 76.4). High AL was associated with worse overall survival (unadjusted HR: 1.70 (95% CI, 1.28, 2.24), adjusted HR 1.58; 95% CI, 1.19, 2.11). Patients with high AL had a 52% higher rate of death compared to the low AL group. High AL was more prevalent among Black patients, those who were not married, and those without private insurance (all p < 0.001).ConclusionsElevated allostatic load was associated with worse overall survival in this cohort of patients with EC, consistent with the hypothesis that physiological stress may impact survival outcomes. These findings are hypothesis‐generating and suggest that physiologic stress and social disadvantage may contribute to survival differences warranting further study.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is the most common gynecologic malignancy in the United States, and incidence is rising most rapidly among historically marginalized and minoritized populations [1]. Compared to the broader population, historically marginalized and minoritized groups are more likely to be diagnosed with advanced‐stage disease, with aggressive histologic and molecular subtypes, and subsequently die within 5 years after diagnosis [2]. The disparity in 5‐year survival between marginalized groups and the broader population (63% vs. 84%, respectively) is among the most pronounced of any cancer type, reflecting complex interactions between biological, clinical, and social determinants of health [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

Understanding and subsequently addressing disparities in EC diagnosis and treatment requires consideration of the role of structural inequities and chronic stress [6, 12, 13, 14]. Exposure to discrimination and chronic social adversity results in detrimental and observable biological effects. The biological effects of exposure to social adversity and discrimination occur through chronic activation of stress‐response systems [15]. Allostatic load (AL) is a composite index designed to capture biomarkers of the cumulative physiologic burden imposed by exposure to psychosocial and environmental stressors [16]. AL is derived from biomarkers spanning cardiovascular, metabolic, and immune systems and has been linked to increased risk of hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cognitive decline, and all‐cause mortality [16, 17, 18]. Importantly, AL disproportionately affects individuals facing sustained social and economic disadvantage, who have consistently higher AL scores throughout the life course [17].

A growing body of literature has linked AL to cancer outcomes. A recent review summarized associations between elevated AL and increased cancer incidence, progression, and mortality across multiple tumor types [19]. Research in gynecologic oncology has begun to establish a relationship between AL and cancer mortality outcomes. In ovarian cancer, elevated AL predicted more than double the risk of mortality, while in cervical cancer higher AL was linked to increased recurrence risk [20, 21]. Although AL has been studied in multiple tumor types, the specific impact on endometrial cancer outcomes remains underexplored. One challenge of existing literature is small sample sizes with limited periods of follow‐up.

In this study, we sought to address this need for a large study sample of people with EC followed for multiple years and examine the relationship between allostatic load and clinical outcomes in a racially diverse cohort of patients with EC. Specifically, our objectives were to (1) examine differences in sociodemographic, clinical, and cancer characteristics between patients with high vs. low AL, and (2) quantify the association between AL and overall survival.

Materials and Methods

Cohort

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients diagnosed with endometrial cancer at a single‐academic center within a large healthcare delivery system in North Carolina utilizing electronic medical records. Patients diagnosed between 2014 and 2023 who met the following inclusion criteria were identified: female, age ≥ 18 years, and diagnosed with endometrial cancer defined by ICD‐9 codes and histological findings from surgery. Final follow up of the cohort was conducted in 2024. This study was approved by the UNC Institutional Review Board (IRB #23–3268).

Allostatic Load Measurement

Allostatic load (AL) score was the exposure of interest. We calculated a measure of allostatic load for each patient following the methodology previously reported by Chyu and Upchurch [22]. We identified values from 10 biomarkers extracted from the electronic health record within 6 months (before or after) the date of diagnosis: systolic blood pressure (mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (mmHg), pulse rate (beats/min), body mass index (kg/m2), alkaline phosphatase, blood glucose, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, white blood cell count, and serum albumin (g/dL). AL was calculated using available biomarker data. Patients with incomplete biomarker data were included if sufficient variables were present to compute the composite score. In sensitivity analysis, we allowed up to three missing biomarkers to assess the stability of AL classification. Biomarkers exceeding the 75th percentile were categorized as high risk, except for serum albumin, where values below the 25th percentile were deemed high risk. Based on the distribution of each biomarker, patients received 1 point for a biomarker that was in the worst quartile for that specific biomarker (Table S1). Allostatic load scores ranged from 0 to 10. Based on the distribution of AL scores, we created a binary variable with a median cut‐point defining low AL as 0 or 1 biomarker in the high‐risk category, while those with two or more biomarkers indicating high risk were classified as high AL. AL was calculated using a quartile‐based approach consistent with prior studies in cancer populations, with high AL defined as a score greater than the cohort median [23]. The outcome of interest was overall survival. After histological confirmation, participants were followed until date of death, loss to follow‐up, or the end of the observational period. For participants presumed to be alive, follow‐up was calculated as the date of cancer diagnosis to the date of loss to follow‐up or the end of the study. Dates of death not available in the electronic health record were researched manually using vital statistics records. Patients who stopped receiving care at the healthcare system, moved or left the area or transferred to other medical systems were still considered to be part of the cohort until their date of death or the end of the study if there was no record of death for that person based on name and date of birth in vital statistics records.

Clinical Variables

Baseline clinical and cancer characteristics were extracted from the medical record retrospectively. Sociodemographic information included age (continuous), self‐reported race (White, Black, Asian, American Indian/Alaskan Native, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, those who preferred not to answer, and those who reported their race as unknown or otherwise classified), self‐reported ethnicity (non‐Hispanic or Latino/a, Hispanic, Latino/a or Spanish origin, and prefer not to answer or unknown), marital status (single, married/living as married, widowed, separated, or divorced), insurance (private, Medicaid, Medicare, or self‐pay), smoking history (never, current or former smoker) and alcohol use (never, current or former). Clinical characteristics included FIGO stage and histology (carcinosarcoma, clear cell, undifferentiated/dedifferentiated, endometrioid, serous, mixed cell, or not otherwise specified (NOS)). Comorbid conditions were abstracted from the electronic medical record for each patient to calculate the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Variables collected included history of myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, connective tissue disease, peptic ulcer disease, mild liver disease, diabetes mellitus (with and without complications), hemiplegia, moderate or severe renal disease, any malignancy (including lymphoma and leukemia, but excluding the index cancer), metastatic solid tumor, and moderate or severe liver disease, as well as the presence of AIDS/HIV.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analysis involving univariate and bivariable statistics was performed. Categorical variables were summarized by N and percent while continuous variables were assessed for normality and analyzed using means or medians as appropriate. Bivariable statistics comparing participants by allostatic load category were performed to identify sociodemographic and clinical characteristics that were more common among participants with high allostatic load. To address the outcome of interest, overall survival, we used Cox proportional hazard regression models. Cox proportional hazards models were evaluated by examining any violations to the proportional hazards (PH) assumption using the formal statistical test examining Schoenfeld residuals for each covariate and the correlation with time. Models with a non‐random pattern of residuals were subject to re‐assessment of variables violating the model assumptions and a priori identified approaches including stratification and utilization of time‐dependent coefficients. We also used graphical methods including examination of the Kaplan–Meier curves to identify any overlap indicating a violation of the PH assumption and log minus log plots. Unadjusted and adjusted models were performed with models being nested for comparison. Confounders of the association between allostatic load and overall survival were identified a priori based on subject matter expertise and published literature. Using a directed acyclic graph the minimally sufficient set based on the causal relationships was: age, alcohol use, FIGO stage at diagnosis, histologic type, insurance status, marital status, smoking status (Figure S1). The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score was not included due to collinearity with allostatic load given overlap between biomarkers and chronic health conditions captured by the CCI.

Among the cohort, patients with missing clinical data or incomplete biomarker information necessary to compute allostatic load were excluded (overall missing data 25%). Because there was extensive missing data for albumin (n = 586) and alkaline phosphatase values (n = 596) with 583 patients missing both values, we concluded that dropping these observations could create bias.

Additional Analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed with different levels of missingness in the AL variable. We determined that the proportion of people with high AL did not change when we allowed up to 3 missing values based on chi square tests comparing the proportions of low AL and high AL according to the variable classification described above. Using this approach, 1844 people (75.3% of the sample) had complete data while 20 patients were missing one biomarker, 569 were missing two biomarkers, and 15 were missing 3 biomarkers.

Effect Modification

The potential for effect modification by race, histology type, and the interaction between race and AL category were each explored. We calculated separate hazard ratios for models restricted to only patients of Black race and calculated hazard ratios using the Cox proportional hazards model restricted to only patients identified as White using the race variable. Due to smaller sample sizes for other racial categories, we were only able to restrict the sample to race defined as Black or White. We restricted the sample to include only endometrioid cases and compared the results with the whole sample to identify potential interaction with cancer type and the outcome of survival. Finally, we created a variable that represented the interaction of race and AL. This variable had four categories: White race with low AL, White race with high AL, Black race with low AL, and Black race with high AL.

Results

Description of Study Population

3227 people with endometrial cancer diagnosed between 2014 and 2023 were identified meeting study criteria. 430 were excluded due to missing diagnostic data and 322 were excluded due to missing allostatic load data, 274 were excluded due to missing covariate data, resulting in a total of 2174 patients (Figure S2). The mean (SD) age was 63.1 (11.2) years at the time of diagnosis (Table 1). The sample was 69.3% White (n = 1506), 22.6% Black (n = 492) and 176 (8.2%) patients that were classified as neither White nor Black (Table 1). This group included Asian, American Indian/Alaskan Native, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander and patients who reported their race as unknown, multiple, or otherwise classified. Hispanic individuals were 4.4% (n = 95) of the cohort. The mean BMI was 35.0 (SD = 9.4). Overall, most patients were married or living as married (n = 1101, 50.6%), 60.7% had public insurance, and 54.8% (n = 1189) had a Charlson co‐morbidity index (CCI) between 1 and 3 with a mean CCI of 1.5 (SD = 1.6). Most patients were diagnosed at FIGO stage 1 (n = 1690, 77.7%). The most common diagnosis was endometrioid histology (n = 1727, 72.0%) and 86.7% (n = 1357) were stage I (Table 2). The median length of follow up was 49.5 months (IQR = 25.7, 76.4).

High AL was identified in 1505 (69.2%) of patients. Compared with patients in the low AL group, patients in the high AL group had a higher BMI (30.8 vs. 36.8, p < 0.0001) (Table 1). High AL was associated with Black race. Three quarters of the patients in the low AL group identified as White compared to 66.7% of people identified as White in the high AL group, 14.9% of people in the low AL group were Black and 26.0% in the high allostatic load group were Black (p < 0.001). Patients in the low AL group were more likely to be married (married 56.1% vs. 48.2% p < 0.001) and privately insured (low AL: 40.4% vs. high AL: 32.0%, p < 0.001). More people in the high AL group had comorbidities per the Charlson Comorbidity index (1–3 comorbidities low AL: 46.6% vs. high AL: 58.4% and 4 or more comorbidities low AL: 3.7% vs. high AL: 14.6%, p < 0.001). High allostatic load was unequally distributed in with histology, with more patients with carcinosarcoma, clear cell, mixed, and undifferentiated/dedifferentiated histology having high AL (p = 0.023) while those who were diagnosed with serous histology were more likely to have low allostatic load. The distribution was the same across endometrioid histology (Table 2). Allostatic load was not different by FIGO stage.

Allostatic Load and Overall Survival

Over the course of follow‐up, we observed a total of 301 deaths (13.8% of the cohort). Sixty‐two or 9.3% of deaths occurred among the low AL group while 239 (15.9%) occurred in people in the high AL group. This difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001) even though there was no difference in the median follow‐up time among the two groups. The unadjusted hazard of overall survival among people who had high AL was 1.70 times the hazard of people in the low AL group (95% CI 1.28, 2.24). Adjustment for confounders reduced the magnitude of the estimate, but it remained statistically significant after adjusting for sociodemographic and clinical factors (aHR: HR 1.58, CI 1.19, 2.11) with patients in the high AL group experiencing worse overall survival during the follow‐up period compared to patients in the low allostatic load group (Figure 1, Table 3).

Discussion

Our findings suggest that higher allostatic load (AL), a physiologic indicator of chronic stress, is significantly associated with worse overall survival in patients with endometrial cancer (EC). Notably, White patients with high allostatic load demonstrated worse overall survival compared with those with low allostatic load, while Black patients with low allostatic load had worse overall survival compared to White patients with low allostatic load, and finally, Black patients with high allostatic load had the worst overall survival. High AL clustered with markers of social and economic disadvantage, including public insurance and non‐partnered status [24, 25, 26]. These findings build on a growing body of evidence that links the burden of chronic stress to worse health outcomes and provide novel insight into how these mechanisms may contribute to disparities in gynecologic cancer outcomes.

Prior studies have consistently demonstrated that elevated AL is associated with a higher risk of adverse health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and premature death [18, 27]. In longitudinal cohort studies, individuals with high AL have exhibited significantly greater risk of all‐cause mortality even after adjusting for conventional risk factors such as smoking, physical activity, and baseline comorbidities [28, 29]. These associations are believed to reflect the cumulative toll of chronic physiologic dysregulation across systems that regulate inflammation, metabolism, and cardiovascular health [16]. However, despite biologic plausibility, limited research has evaluated the role of AL in cancer‐specific settings.

Recent studies have identified a significant association between AL and adverse outcomes in gynecologic cancer patients. In patients with epithelial ovarian cancer, high AL was independently linked to more than double the risk of mortality, even after adjusting for clinical and demographic characteristics [20]. Similarly, in cervical cancer, a retrospective study demonstrated that higher allostatic load was associated with increased recurrence risk [21]. Sociodemographic factors such as older age, Black race, widowed status, and substance use were correlated with elevated AL, underscoring the integral role of social determinants in physiological stress and cancer prognosis. However, studies investigating AL and endometrial cancer outcomes are sparse, and existing evidence suggests that chronic stress and its biological effects may adversely impact cancer disease progression [30]. Our study is the first to examine the prognostic implications of AL in a large cohort of patients with EC and suggests that AL may represent a potential intermediate biomarker linking social disadvantage to cancer survival. Furthermore, these findings also suggest that AL could be used as a valuable prognostic indicator to help identify potential targets for supportive interventions in gynecologic oncology that could improve cancer outcomes.

These findings are consistent with prior population‐level analyses, including studies using nationally representative datasets such as NHANES, which have demonstrated that higher allostatic load is associated with worse overall survival across diverse cancer populations and that racial disparities persist even after accounting for AL [31]. Similarly, disease‐specific cohort studies, including large analyses in breast cancer populations, have shown that elevated AL is independently associated with worse overall survival after adjustment for sociodemographic, clinical, and treatment‐related factors [23]. Together, these data support the robustness of allostatic load as a marker of physiologic stress associated with adverse cancer outcomes.

Importantly, our findings extend this literature by focusing specifically on endometrial cancer, a disease with rising incidence and well‐documented disparities. Prior population‐based studies are limited by heterogeneous cancer populations and less granular clinical data, whereas our study incorporates detailed clinical, pathologic, and treatment‐level variables within a single disease context. This disease‐specific approach provides more nuanced insight into the role of allostatic load in endometrial cancer outcomes and highlights that, while AL is associated with worse overall survival, it does not fully account for persistent racial disparities, suggesting that additional structural and healthcare‐related factors contribute independently to observed inequities.

Social and economic marginalization are well‐established drivers of elevated AL. Black patients, for example, have consistently higher AL scores than White patients across all age groups, a pattern attributed to the chronic activation of stress‐response systems in the context of economic inequality, residential segregation, and interpersonal discrimination [17, 32]. These stressors are not only more frequent but also more prolonged among individuals with fewer socioeconomic resources, fewer social supports, and greater exposure to neighborhood disadvantage [27]. Consistent with prior work, we found that higher AL was more common among individuals with public insurance and those who were unmarried or unpartnered, emphasizing the role of both economic and social isolation in physiologic stress burden.

Our results also shed light on persistent survival disparities in EC outcomes. Black patients had significantly higher AL scores and worse overall survival even after accounting for tumor characteristics and comorbidities. These findings underscore that while physiologic stress may contribute to disparities, additional variables such as structural, social, and health‐care‐related factors including differences in access to care, treatment delivery, and systemic inequities play an independent and critical role in shaping outcomes. Allostatic load may represent a promising tool to identify patients at higher risk of poor outcomes, independent of tumor characteristics. However, its integration into clinical risk stratification requires prospective validation. To address this gap, we are prospectively collecting allostatic load and related biospecimens through LCCC 2410 (PROMOTE): Impact of the microbiome and immunotherapy on the treatment and progression of Endometrial Cancer. Additionally, measurement of AL at diagnosis and during preventative care may help inform risk assessment, guide supportive interventions, and provide a framework to evaluate the impact of social determinants in clinical research. Future work is needed to determine if modifying AL can impact outcomes in cancer patients.

A key strength of this study is the use of a large, sociodemographically diverse patient cohort drawn from a single academic center, allowing for exploration of racial and sociodemographic differences in AL and overall survival. We employed a validated AL scoring method based on ten physiologic biomarkers that span cardiovascular, metabolic, renal, and inflammatory systems. Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective design at a single institution may limit generalizability to other populations and introduces the potential for residual confounders, including the possibility of reverse causation, whereby more advanced or biologically aggressive disease may contribute to higher AL rather than AL influence outcomes. Second, the AL biomarkers were captured as close to the time of surgery as possible, with a cutoff point of 6 months, which led to missing data and restricted our ability to assess AL at the time of diagnosis, which may not fully capture the dynamic nature of physiologic stress. Because AL was measured within 6 months of diagnosis, it may reflect not only cumulative chronic stress exposure but also acute physiologic changes related to underlying malignancy or its initial management, potentially limiting its interpretation as a purely pre‐existing stress biomarker. Additionally, certain biomarkers included in AL measurement, such as albumin and alkaline phosphatase, may be influenced by underlying malignancy or disease severity; therefore, their contribution should be interpreted with caution. The construction of AL relied on a composite index derived from clinical biomarkers using cohort‐based thresholds; while consistent with prior literature, there is no standardized method for AL calculation, and alternative approaches may yield different estimates. We also were unable to account for reproductive factors such as menopausal status and parity, which may influence allostatic load through hormonal and metabolic pathways. This may introduce residual confounders and limit interpretation of our findings. Finally, the use of overall survival as the primary endpoint represents a limitation, as it may be driven by non‐cancer comorbidities in this predominantly early‐stage population. While AL serves as a useful summary indicator, it does not isolate specific mechanisms or account for non‐biologic contributors to stress exposure.

Conclusion

In summary, high allostatic load is significantly associated with worse overall survival among patients with endometrial cancer. Elevated AL disproportionately affects individuals facing socioeconomic disadvantage, suggesting that chronic stress may be a key pathway through which social determinants of health contribute to cancer disparities. Our findings suggest a potential role for upstream interventions to target the root cause of chronic stress, including economic insecurity and systemic inequities. Further studies are needed to investigate the role of AL as a marker of risk and a target for future strategies aimed at improving equity in cancer outcomes.
