Lower ghrelin in depression might be connected to the absence of psychotic-like experiences
Authors: Michał Lis, Maciej Majdowski, Tymoteusz Miłuch, Tomasz Zawodny, Olivia Wasilewska, Adriana Kwiatkowska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Major depressive disorder (MDD) prevalence increases every year, with approximately 30% of patients remaining treatment-resistant. Ghrelin, a stomach-derived appetite-stimulating hormone, has been implicated in mood disorders, stress response, sleep-cycle regulation, reward behaviour, and neuroplasticity. However, findings in humans remain unconclusive, with reports of higher, unchanged, or lowered ghrelin concentration in patients with MDD. Psychotic-like experiences (PLEs), which are more common in the course of depression, might contribute to reported inconsistencies. This study aims to assess serum total ghrelin concentrations in individuals with depressive symptoms and investigate whether the presence of PLEs modulates the relationship. A cross-sectional study was conducted among 56 individuals screened for depressive symptoms (with patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)) and presence of PLEs in the past year (with prodromal questionnaire-16 (PQ-16)). Serum total ghrelin levels were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The relationship between PHQ-9 scores and total ghrelin was analysed using linear regression and analysis of covariates (ANCOVA) when controlled for sex, age, and body mass index. Initially, depressive symptoms predicted lower ghrelin levels (P = 0.044). However, the model lost its significance when adjusted for covariates (P = 0.100). Pairwise comparisons revealed lower total ghrelin among patients with MDD but without PLEs when compared to controls (P = 0.037). No significant differences were observed within patients with a history of PLEs. This study provides preliminary evidence that PLEs modulate ghrelin secretion in patients with MDD symptoms. Further studies with larger samples are needed to clarify ghrelin’s role in MDD pathophysiology.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a growing challenge for world healthcare systems, as its prevalence increases every year. The World health Organization predicts that MDD will have become the leading cause of disease burden by 2030, surpassing cardiovascular diseases and cancer. The pathophysiology of MDD is complex, and it sums up to genetic and environmental factors. Disturbances in serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine signalling in various brain regions (including limbic system, prefrontal cortex, and ventral tegmental area (VTA)) lead to clinical depression. Yet, despite advances in understanding neuroscience, even 30% of patients remain treatment-resistant (1), highlighting the need to explore novel neuroendocrine pathways.

Ghrelin is a stomach-derived, appetite-stimulating hormone. It binds to growth hormone secretagogue receptors (GHSR) not only in the hypothalamus (where it stimulates food intake) but also in the hippocampus, amygdala, and VTA. Ghrelin is associated with sleep-cycle regulation, reward behaviour, and memory (2), the functional areas whose activity is disturbed in the course of depressive disorder. Ghrelin has been connected not only to MDD but also to other psychiatric conditions, such as addictive disorder, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia (3).

Preclinical studies on animals revealed that ghrelin plays a role in stress mediation. GHSR-knockout mice show exaggerated corticosterone release and impaired stress coping. Therefore, it was suggested that ghrelin prevents excessive stress reaction by suppressing cortisol release (4, 5). Moreover, ghrelin reduced inflammation by decreasing levels of tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-a), interleukin 1-beta (IL-1b), and IL-6 (6). Ghrelin enhanced serotonin and norepinephrine transmission (3) and increased synaptic plasticity (7) among tested animals. There are also reports of ghrelin showing antidepressant activity (6, 8).

Human data on ghrelin are limited. Genetic variants of GHSR in a Japanese cohort were associated with depression. The methylation profile of GHSR did also differ among depressed and non-depressed individuals (9, 10). Notably, ghrelin concentration was higher among individuals after an acute suicide attempt (11). Circulating ghrelin has been reported to be higher, lower, or unchanged in MDD patients when compared to controls (12, 13, 14). Both antidepressants and electroconvulsive therapy lowered ghrelin concentration among MDD patients (15, 16). These observations give rationale for further exploration of the relationship between ghrelin and mood disorders.

Psychotic-like experiences (PLEs), even if mainly associated with psychotic disorders, are more frequent among patients with MDD and anxiety disorder (17). Ghrelin binds to dopaminergic neurons in the VTA, a part of the mesolimbic tract, which dysfunction leads to the development of schizophrenia (18). Moreover, patients with schizophrenia exhibited higher levels of ghrelin (3). Therefore, we hypothesised that PLEs might influence ghrelin levels in the MDD population, and that the PLEs history might have been a reason for conflicting results.

To address these gaps, and given the heterogeneity of previous findings, we investigated serum ghrelin concentrations in a Polish sample of adults screened for depressive symptoms. Furthermore, we explored the influence of PLEs on ghrelin levels, since ghrelin dysregulation has been observed in patients with schizophrenia but never examined in the context of depression.

Material and methods

The study protocol was approved by the National Institute of Geriatrics, Rheumatology and Rehabilitation Bioethical Committee (KBT-9/1/2024). The research included participants who were in outpatient care of Czerniakowski Hospital in Warsaw, Poland. Written informed consent was obtained from all individuals before data collection.

Population

Only patients screened for depression and PLEs were included in the study. Other inclusion criteria were as follows: i) age greater than or equal to 18 years old, ii) the availability of frozen serum for further analysis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: i) infection in the past 14 days, ii) chronic autoimmune diseases (e.g. rheumatoid arthritis), iii) decompensated endocrine diseases (e.g. uncontrolled diabetes mellitus or severe hypothyroidism), iv) cancer, v) neurologic syndromes (e.g. epilepsy or dementia), vi) gastrointestinal syndromes (e.g. inflammatory bowel disease), vii) use of psychotropic drugs such as antidepressants, antipsychotics, or mood stabilisers in the past 12 months, viii) psychotherapy in the past 12 months, ix) substance abuse, and x) chronic psychiatric conditions (e.g. bipolar disorder or psychotic disorders).

Laboratory parameters (red blood cell count, white blood cell count, platelet count, haemoglobin, C-reactive protein, thyrotropin, morning cortisol, and 25-OH vitamin D3) and medical history were reviewed to confirm eligibility.

Psychometric tools

We used the Polish adaptation of the patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) to screen patients for depressive symptoms. The tool is a self-report measure which was validated for use in the Polish population (19). A cut-off score of 12 points was used to identify subjects with clinically significant symptoms of MDD in the 18–59 age groups and of 6 points in individuals aged 60 or older. These cut-off points were found to represent satisfactory sensitivity and specificity in screening for depressive disorder in the mentioned age groups (20, 21).

We used the Polish adaptation of the 16-item version of the prodromal questionnaire (PQ-16) to determine if patients experienced PLEs over the past year. The PQ-16 is typically used for prodromal schizophrenia screening. The PQ-16 consists of three subscales regarding perceptual abnormalities (nine items), unusual thought content (five items), and negative symptoms (two items) (22). A score can be calculated by summing true answers to stated psychosis-like experiences (items) or by summing the total distress score caused by these experiences. For this study, PLE positivity was defined as endorsing ≥6 items and a distress score ≥9 in the past 12 months (23).

Laboratory assay

Samples of patients’ blood were collected after an overnight fasting period between 08:00 h and 09:00 h. Immediately after, serum samples were prepared in a pre-cooled centrifuge and then stored at −80°C for further investigation.

Total ghrelin was measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (catalogue number: orb561916) (Biorbyt, UK), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay kit had a detection level of 1.875 pg/mL. Each plasma sample was analysed on two different plates, and the mean of two measurements was used for statistical analysis. The intra-assay coefficient of variance (CV) was 1.10% for the first plate and 0.97% for the second one. The inter-assay CV was 0.61%.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed in SPSS (IBM, USA; version 29). The primary analysis assessed the influence of depression symptom severity on total ghrelin level. Second, we explored if PLEs occurrence mediated the effect of MDD symptoms on ghrelin secretion. Assumptions for parametric tests were evaluated before analysis. Variables were checked for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual analysis of Q–Q plots, while the homogeneity of variances was verified with Levene’s test. Group-by-covariate interactions were checked to ensure homogeneity of regression slopes.

Descriptive statistics were showed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables and as median (minimum – maximum) if the normality assumption had not been met. A significance level of two-tailed α = 0.05 was adopted for all tests.

Initially, a linear regression analysis was conducted with total ghrelin as the dependent variable and PHQ-9 score as the independent variable. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to control the model for sex, age, and BMI.

Participants were then divided into four subgroups based on positive screening for MDD (PHQ-9: >12 (18–59 years old) or >6 (60 and older)) and occurrence of PLEs during the past year (PQ-16: ≥6 items and ≥9 points on the distress scale). To compare total ghrelin concentrations across these subgroups, ANCOVA was performed with ghrelin as the dependent variable and sex, age, and BMI as covariates. To assess pairwise group differences, estimated marginal means were compared using Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Results

Out of 132 patients assessed for eligibility, a total of 56 participants met the inclusion criteria and were included in the study. Females constituted 82.1% (n = 46). The participants had a mean age of 47.41 ± 13.39 years and a mean BMI of 29.64 ± 6.27 kg/m2. The mean total ghrelin concentration was 47.6 ± 23.12 pg/mL. The median PHQ-9 score was 8 points (range 0–25). Twenty-two participants (39.3%) had clinically significant MDD symptoms (PHQ-9 >12). Data regarding patients are showed in Table 1.

Discussion

The connection between appetite-regulating hormones and psychiatric disorders has gained significant attention in recent years. There are a limited number of studies on human subjects regarding ghrelin concentration in MDD. Moreover, the results are contrary: some studies report elevated levels of ghrelin within individuals with MDD (13, 16, 24, 25), while others find no difference (12, 26, 27), or observe decreased levels of the hormone (14). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first investigation of ghrelin concentration in a Polish MDD cohort.

In line with Barim et al. (14), our primary analysis showed a trend towards lower serum ghrelin in participants with depressive symptoms compared to non-depressed controls. However, the effect lost its significance when adjusted for sex, age, and BMI.

To address the heterogeneity of previous research, we decided to explore PLEs as a potential modulator of ghrelin secretion in patients with MDD. While ghrelin dysregulation has been documented in schizophrenia (3), its role in depression complicated by subclinical psychotic features has not been studied.

We classified PLEs using PQ-16, by requiring both ≥6 endorsed items and a ≥9 distress score (22, 23). PQ-16 was developed to screen for prodromal signs of schizophrenia mainly among adolescents and young adults. We decided to use PQ-16 in our study because: i) different PQ variations have been used in studies involving older adults up to 78 years old (28), ii) it was validated in non-help-seeking populations (23), iii) the Polish-language version of PQ-16 has been validated and used in previous research (29, 30, 31). However, the tool’s validity specifically for detecting PLEs in MDD populations remains uncertain, and the results should be interpreted carefully.

Our subgroup analysis revealed that individuals with MDD symptoms and no PLEs exhibited significantly lower ghrelin than controls without PLEs, whereas no differences emerged among those reporting PLEs. This observation implies that PLEs could account for inconsistent results across previous studies. The finding is consistent with previous reports of higher ghrelin secretion among patients with schizophrenia (32). However, a recently published meta-analysis on clozapine treatment and ghrelin levels among patients with schizophrenia reported no difference (33). We found no studies regarding ghrelin among drug-naïve patients with psychosis.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The sample was predominantly female (82.1%), potentially limiting the extent to which the findings can be generalised across genders. Depressive symptoms were measured using the self-reported PHQ-9 scale. Diagnosis of MDD was not confirmed by a psychiatrist or structured clinical interview. Therefore, the results reflect the relationship between ghrelin and the presence of depressive symptoms rather than MDD itself. However, as Okasha et al. observed, the ghrelin concentration correlated with the depression severity scale (Hamilton depression rating scale) among patients diagnosed with MDD (13), which adds validity to these results. In addition, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits any causal interpretation, and the small sample size may reduce the generalisability of our conclusions. Because of these limitations, of the study, the results should be interpreted with caution.

Our preliminary findings suggest that lower ghrelin levels in depressive symptomatology may primarily concern individuals without PLEs. Further research with larger samples, structured clinical assessments, and validated measures of PLEs is necessary to clarify ghrelin’s role in the pathophysiology of depression.
