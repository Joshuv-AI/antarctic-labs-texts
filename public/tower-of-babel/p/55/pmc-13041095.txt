A prospective study of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), stress, and birth size in a cohort of U.S. Black women
Authors: Samantha Schildroth, Birgit Claus Henn, Sharonda M. Lovett, Amelia K. Wesselink, Yael I. Nillni, Wendy Heiger-Bernays, Quaker E. Harmon, Anissa I. Vines, Donna D. Baird, Lauren A. Wise
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPer- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) and psychosocial stress can dysregulate hormonal processes underlying fetal growth. Less is known about the effect of co-exposure to PFAS and stress on birth size.ObjectiveWe investigated associations of a preconception mixture of PFAS with birth size and assessed effect modification by perceived stress.MethodsWe used prospective data from 321 participants from the Study of Environment, Lifestyle, and Fibroids, a cohort of 23-35-year-old women from Detroit, MI who identified as Black/African American. We quantified plasma concentrations of six PFAS in baseline blood samples using online solid-phase extraction-liquid chromatography-isotope dilution tandem mass spectrometry. We assessed experience of perceived stress at baseline using the Perceived Stress Scale-4. Participants reported intervening pregnancies at follow-up visits (at ~ 20, 40, and 60 months). We calculated sex-standardized birthweight-for-gestational-age (BW-for-GA) z-scores from reported birthweight and gestational age at delivery. We employed Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression to estimate associations (βs, with 95% credible intervals [CrI]) of the PFAS mixture with BW-for-GA z-scores and assess effect modification by stress scores, adjusting for confounders.ResultsThe 90th percentile of the mixture (vs. 50th percentile) was weakly associated with higher BW-for-GA z-scores (β = 0.37, 95% CrI=-0.20, 0.95), driven by perfluorohexanesulfonic acid (PFHxS) and stress scores. PFHxS was weakly associated with higher BW-for-GA z-scores, and this association was stronger at higher levels of perceived stress.ConclusionA mixture of PFAS and stress scores was weakly associated with higher BW-for-GA z-scores in a cohort of Black women, with evidence of a PFHxS-stress score interaction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12940-026-01279-7.

Background

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a class of synthetic chemicals, many of which are endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) used widely in consumer products and industrial applications [1, 2]. Although the use of some PFAS has been banned or phased-out [3, 4], PFAS are not readily degraded in the environment and can bioaccumulate [5], resulting in continued human exposure [6]. The majority (~ 95%) of American adults have detectable PFAS in their blood [7]. These exposures may increase the risk of adverse reproductive and perinatal outcomes given their effects on hormonal systems [8].

Adverse birth outcomes are strong predictors of infant morbidity and mortality [9, 10] and affect up to 10% of pregnancies [11, 12]. Fetal growth restriction (FGR) reflects impaired fetal growth and is often operationalized in epidemiologic studies using birthweight [13] (e.g., birthweight-for-gestational-age z-scores or small-for-gestational-age [SGA], < 10th percentile for gestational age) [14–16]. Low birthweight infants are more likely to develop issues with neurodevelopment, childhood growth, and cardiovascular health [17, 18], as well as diabetes, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and obesity later in life [19–22]. On the other hand, high birthweight (e.g., large-for-gestational-age [LGA], > 90th percentile for gestational age) [15] infants are also at risk for developing obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular problems [23].

PFAS have been shown to negatively impact several organ systems, highlighting the potential for multiple underlying mechanisms of action. PFAS can alter concentrations of hormones mediating healthy pregnancy, such as estradiol, testosterone, progesterone, and thyroid hormones, thereby affecting fetal growth [8, 24–31]. PFAS may also impair fetal metabolic homeostasis and placental development through activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling [32–36]. The epidemiologic literature for birthweight is inconsistent, with studies reporting positive, inverse, or null associations between maternal PFAS exposure and birthweight [37–60]. Inconsistencies across studies may relate to differences in study populations, PFAS chemicals and concentrations, or timing of exposure assessment (i.e., pregnancy vs. preconception). Because pregnancy-related factors (e.g., hemodynamics) may be strong confounders of PFAS-birth outcome associations [61, 62], additional studies with PFAS exposure assessed in the preconception period can help to clarify the direction of association.

Because of their extensive use and resistance to environmental degradation [1, 2, 5], humans are exposed to PFAS as mixtures, rather than individual compounds. PFAS may further share toxicological mechanisms [63], suggesting exposure to PFAS mixtures could result in additive or interactive effects. Yet, less attention has been given to PFAS mixtures in relation to birth outcomes. Although the direction of effect varied across studies, previous studies that considered PFAS mixtures have reported joint associations of the mixture with birth outcomes, with most reporting inverse associations for birthweight [37–40, 48, 49, 54, 56, 58, 64]. Most of these analyses did not consider potential PFAS interactions, and among those that did, meaningful interactions were not reported [38, 39, 54, 56, 64]. Several of these studies were further limited by their exposure assessments (e.g., measuring PFAS during pregnancy) [38, 39, 54, 56], highlighting the need for additional research on PFAS mixtures and birth outcomes.

Emerging data further indicates that co-exposure to chemical and non-chemical stressors – i.e., psychosocial stress – can have cumulative effects on birth outcomes. Psychosocial stress has been associated with fetal growth and pregnancy complications [65], possibly due to its effect on dysregulating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis [66–70]. Some PFAS can similarly dysregulate the HPA axis, a mechanism which has been hypothesized to contribute to adverse birth outcomes [71, 72]. In humans, maternal PFAS exposure has been associated with higher corticotropin-releasing hormone, and this association was stronger among women with greater psychosocial stress [71]. Two additional studies by Eick and colleagues investigated co-exposure to PFAS and psychosocial stressors in relation to birth outcomes and reported cumulative associations between PFAS and stress/stress-related factors - namely perceived stress, depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and experience of discrimination - with lower birthweight [38, 39]. These findings suggest that psychosocial stress may contribute to PFAS perinatal toxicity. Yet, psychosocial stress remains largely unexplored in studies of PFAS and birth outcomes.

In addition, few epidemiological studies of PFAS exposure and birth outcomes have been conducted among Black women. This is a major research gap because, in the U.S., Black infants are ~ 2 times more likely to be low birthweight and have a 2-fold increased risk of mortality before one year of age [73]. Black infants are also more likely to be born LGA compared with White infants [74, 75]. Moreover, Black women may experience greater levels of stress compared with White women due to disproportionate exposure to adverse sociopolitical and environmental factors [76], potentially contributing to PFAS-birthweight associations and health inequities in adverse birth outcomes. Characterizing cumulative associations of PFAS and psychosocial stress on birthweight among Black mother/infant pairs is therefore a salient research objective that may serve to inform public health and clinical interventions aimed at improving maternal and infant health.

In this study, we evaluated associations of preconception plasma concentrations of a PFAS mixture with birth size from a prospective cohort study of Black women. We further assessed effect modification of PFAS-birth size associations by experience of perceived stress. We hypothesized that the PFAS mixture would be cumulatively associated with lower birth size, and that associations would be stronger among participants experiencing greater levels of stress.

Methods

Study population

We used data from the Study of Environment, Lifestyle, and Fibroids (SELF), a prospective cohort study of reproductive-aged Black women from Detroit, MI, U.S., designed to identify determinants of uterine fibroids. Details on participant recruitment and study protocols have been described previously [77]. In brief, 1,693 participants were recruited between 2010 and 2012 and were eligible for enrollment if they identified as Black or African American, were aged 23–35 years, had an intact uterus, and had no prior diagnosis of fibroids, cancer, or an autoimmune disease requiring medication use. Participants completed extensive questionnaires and provided blood samples at baseline and then completed three follow-up visits (at ~ 20, 40, and 60 months). All study protocols were approved by Institutional Review Boards at Boston University Medical Campus, Henry Ford Health System (HFH), and the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS).

PFAS serum biomarker measurement

We quantified PFAS concentrations in plasma samples provided at baseline, as described previously [78]. Plasma was separated from non-fasting whole blood at HFH by centrifuging the blood for 10 min at 4 °C and 1300 g. HFH shipped plasma samples to the biorepository at the NIEHS, where samples were aliquoted and stored in straws at −80 °C. In 2019, we transferred the plasma from straws to propylene cryovials and shipped the frozen plasma overnight on dry ice in two batches to the CDC for analysis. We used online solid-phase extraction-liquid chromatography-isotope dilution tandem mass spectrometry [79] to quantify plasma concentrations of nine PFAS: perfluorohexanesulfonic acid (PFHxS), linear perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (n-PFOS), branched isomers of perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (Sm-PFOS), linear perfluorooctanoic acid (n-PFOA), branched isomers of perfluorooctanoic acid (Sb-PFOA), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA), perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUnDA), and 2-(N-methyl-perfluorooctane sulfonamido) acetic acid (MeFOSAA). We calculated total PFOS (PFOS) and total PFOA (PFOA) by summing concentrations of linear and branched isomers of PFOS and PFOA, respectively. The limit of detection (LOD) for all PFAS was 0.1 ng/mL. We included PFAS with a detection frequency > 60% in the final analysis (Table 1): PFHxS, PFOS, PFOA, PFNA, PFDA, and MeFOSAA. Birth outcomes assessment

Pregnancy data were collected during telephone interviews that were conducted in conjunction with each study visit. Participants who reported having a pregnancy since the previous study visit were asked to report the (1) outcome of the pregnancy (singleton live birth, multiple live birth, stillbirth, miscarriage, tubal/ectopic pregnancy, or induced abortion), (2) length of pregnancy (in weeks), and (3) sex and birthweight of the infant (in pounds/ounces, which we converted to grams). We used reported pregnancy length and birthweight to calculate sex-standardized birthweight-for-gestational-age (BW-for-GA) z-scores (primary outcome) using previously developed methods based on the 2017 U.S. natality data [81]. A total of 796 pregnancies from 538 participants were reported in the cohort through 60 months of follow-up (Fig. 1). We restricted the current analysis to singleton live births resulting from the first pregnancy reported by participants during study follow-up to reduce the potential for exposure misclassification from pregnancy-related PFAS excretion. We further excluded one participant missing birthweight data, resulting in a total sample of 321 pregnancies. Among this analytic sample, 103 (32.1%), 108 (33.6%), and 110 (34.3%) pregnancies were reported at the first (20-month), second (40-month), and third (60-month) follow-up visits, respectively. The average time between PFAS measurement (baseline) and start of the index pregnancy was 1.8 years (range: 0–4.8.8 years). We did not consider binary birth size variables (i.e., SGA, LGA) as outcome variables in the current analysis due to our small sample size.

Assessment of perceived stress

Participants reported their level of perceived stress at baseline and at each follow-up visit on the Perceived Stress Scale-4 (PSS-4) [82], which asks participants to rate their stress levels in the previous 30 days using four questions: have you felt (1) that you were unable to control the important things in life, (2) confident about your ability to handle your personal problems, (3) that things were going your way, and (4) difficulties were piling up so high that you could not overcome them. Response options were never, almost never, sometimes, fairly often, or very often (scored as 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively). We reverse coded responses to questions 2) and 3) and summed scores for all four questions to reflect total perceived stress levels (range: 0–16), where higher scores (which we refer to as “stress scores”) reflected higher stress levels. The PSS-4 has high short-term (2-month) reliability in Black populations [82, 83]. Our primary analyses used baseline stress scores in statistical models.

Covariates

We ascertained sociodemographic, reproductive, and anthropometric information on baseline questionnaires, interviews, and clinic measurements, including age (years), educational attainment (high school degree/GED, some college/Associate’s degree/technical training, or Bachelor’s/Master’s/Doctorate), annual household income (<$20,000, $20,000-$50,000, or >$50,000 per year), body mass index (BMI; kg/m2), smoking status (never smoker or former/current smoker), alcohol intake (low [< 10 drinks/year], moderate [> low, but lower than heavy], or heavy [≥ 6 drinks per day when drinking alcohol or ≥ 4 drinks at a single sitting twice per month]), and parity.

Statistical analysis

Multiple imputation

There were few missing data in the analytic sample (< 1% for covariates and < 10% for PFAS, Table S1), with the exception of stress scores measured at the 20-month (14%) and 40-month (18%) follow-up visits due to skipped study visits. We generated five multiply imputed datasets using Monte Carlo Markov Chain methods using all available biomarker, outcome, and covariate data, assuming the data were missing at random [84].

Descriptive statistics and data transformations

We calculated summary statistics (means, standard deviations [SDs], percentages [%], medians, 25th —75th percentiles) for all variables and Spearman correlation coefficients for the PFAS biomarkers. We calculated an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to assess the correlation between stress scores reported at baseline and each follow-up study visit. The PFAS plasma concentrations were right-skewed; because mixtures methods can be sensitive to outlier values, we naturally log-transformed PFAS concentrations prior to statistical modeling. We further z-standardized (centered and scaled to the mean) PFAS concentrations and stress scores.

Confounder selection

We selected confounders for inclusion in statistical models using a directed acyclic graph informed by the literature [38, 47, 85]. We adjusted all statistical models for baseline maternal characteristics, including age, educational attainment, annual household income, BMI, smoking status, alcohol intake, and parity. We did not include infant sex as a covariate in statistical models because the BW-for-GA z-scores were sex-standardized.

Generalized Additive Models (GAMs)

We first used fully adjusted GAMs to visually inspect the shape of associations between PFAS concentrations and BW-for-GA z-scores, and between continuous covariates and BW-for-GA z-scores. There was evidence of an inverted U-shaped association between BMI and BW-for-GA z-scores; we therefore tertiled BMI.

Bayesian kernel machine regression

We used Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) as our primary statistical method for assessing associations of the PFAS mixture, and effect modification by stress, with birth size. In addition to the PFAS, we included the baseline stress scores as an exposure variable in the kernel function to assess potential effect modification of the PFAS mixture by perceived stress, an approach that we have used previously [78], and hereafter use “the mixture” to refer to the PFAS-stress mixture. BKMR is a non-parametric model that uses a flexible kernel function to model the mixture, allowing for nonlinear associations of the mixture components, pairwise interactions, higher-order interactions, and estimation of joint effects [86]. We fit adjusted BKMR models for all five imputed datasets using the non-informative prior specifications, 50,000 iterations, and a 50% burn-in. We further selected every 5th iteration to limit autocorrelation and used the component-wise variable selection option, from which we estimated posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) for all exposure variables. We pooled estimates across models from the five imputed datasets using Rubin’s rule [87, 88] and examined trace plots to assess model convergence.

We estimated the following summary measures for the mixture (PFAS and stress scores) from BKMR models: (1) exposure response profiles for each exposure variable, when all other exposure variables were held at their medians; (2) overall associations of the mixture when all exposure variables were held at varying percentiles (10th – 90th), compared to the 50th percentile; (3) associations of each exposure variable, for an increase from the 25th to 75th percentiles, at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of all other exposure variables; and (4) exposure response profiles for each exposure variable at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of a second exposure variable, holding all other exposure variables at their medians. We estimated beta (β) coefficients and 95% credible intervals (CrIs) for all summary measures.

Because there was evidence of multiple pairwise interactions between the exposure variables, we used Bayesian semiparametric regression and sparsity inducing priors, an approach we have used previously in SELF [89], with the NLinteraction package in R to rank the relative importance of the pairwise interactions [90]. NLinteraction flexibly models associations using natural cubic splines and estimates PIPs for the pairwise interactions between exposure variables. We fit NLinteraction models for one randomly selected imputed dataset with 200,000 MCMC iterations, a 50% burn-in, and two degrees of freedom for the splines, selected using Watanabe-Akaike information criteria [90].

Multivariable linear regression

To supplement the interpretation of our primary findings from BKMR and NLinteraction, we fit multivariable linear regression (MLR) models to estimate associations between PFAS plasma concentrations and BW-for-GA z-scores. MLR models were adjusted for selected confounders and co-adjusted for all PFAS. We further fit MLR models with pairwise interaction terms between each pair of exposure variables and considered interaction terms with p < 0.20 significant. We fit MLR models for all five imputed datasets and pooled estimates using Rubin’s rule [87]. We estimated β coefficients, reflecting mean differences in BW-for-GA z-scores per 1-SD increase in PFAS concentrations, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We also fit MLR models stratified by stress scores < 6 (low stress) versus ≥ 6 (high stress) [91].

Secondary analyses

In secondary analyses, we investigated potential sex-specific effects by fitting MLR models stratified by infant sex (female vs. male). We did not fit sex-stratified BKMR models given our limited sample size.

Sensitivity analyses

We fit separate BKMR models (1) adjusting for total duration of prior breastfeeding (months) at baseline; (2) using stress scores reported closest to the index pregnancy (e.g., stress scores reported at the first follow-up visit for pregnancies reported at the second follow-up visit); (3) with 100,000 iterations, and (4) changing the BKMR prior specifications (a) from a uniform to a gamma prior distribution, and (b) to allow for varying degrees of smoothness of the kernel function. Finally, we fit MLR models that were (1) not co-adjusted for all PFAS to assess potential co-exposure confounding; (2) restricted to pregnancies that were reported at the first follow-up visit (n = 103) to assess potential exposure misclassification from longer time lengths between PFAS exposure assessment and pregnancies reported at the 40-month and 60-month follow-up visits; and (3) restricted to nulliparous participants at baseline (n = 101) to assess potential exposure misclassification resulting from PFAS elimination routes related to pregnancy (blood loss at birth, placental transfer, breastfeeding) [92–95].

Results

Study population

At baseline, mean age of the analytic cohort was 28 years (SD = 3.3 years) and mean BMI was 33.9 kg/m2 (SD = 9.5 kg/m2; Table 2). Most participants had attained some level of college education or technical training (76%), 53% had household incomes ≥$20,000, 73% were never smokers, 81% were low-to-moderate consumers of alcohol, and 69% were parous. Mean stress scores were highest at baseline (6.2; SD = 3.1) and were moderately correlated across follow-up visits (ICC = 0.50). 51% of infants born to SELF participants during the incident period were female. Mean gestational length was 38.7 weeks (SD = 2.4 weeks).

PFAS plasma concentrations were weakly (r = 0.12 for PFDA-MeFOSAA) to strongly (r = 0.74 for PFOA-PFNA) correlated with one another (Figure S1). Median concentrations were highest for PFOS (4.0 ng/mL; 25th, 75th %=2.4, 6.2 ng/mL) and PFOA (1.2 ng/mL; 25th, 75th %=0.9, 1.8 ng/mL; Table 1).

PFAS and BW-for-GA

Higher percentiles of the mixture (PFAS and stress scores) were weakly and imprecisely associated with BW-for-GA z-scores: the 90th percentile of the mixture, compared with the 50th percentile, was associated with 0.37 higher BW-for-GA z-scores (95% CrI=−0.20, 0.95; Figure S2). This association was driven primarily by PFHxS and stress scores (PIPs provided in Table S2). PFHxS, for an increase from its 25th to 75th percentiles, was weakly and imprecisely associated with higher BW-for-GA z-scores in BKMR models (when all exposure variables were held at their 50th percentile: β = 0.21, 95% CrI=−0.12, 0.54; Fig. 2) and in MLR models (per 1-SD increase: β = 0.18, 95% CI=−0.05, 0.41; Table S3). Similarly, stress scores were weakly associated with higher BW-for-GA z-scores in the BKMR model (when all exposure variables were held at their 50th percentile: β = 0.15, 95% CrI=−0.05, 0.35; Fig. 2). The remaining PFAS were not strongly associated with BW-for-GA z-scores.

We observed several potential pairwise interactions between the exposure variables in BKMR models (Fig. 3 A), notably, PFHxS-stress scores, PFHxS-MeFOSAA, and PFOA-MeFOSAA. PIPs from the NLinteraction model suggested overall weak pairwise interactions, with the strongest pairwise interaction between PFHxS and stress scores (Fig. 3B and Table S4). Interaction terms in MLR models suggested potential pairwise interactions between PFHxS-MeFOSAA (p = 0.12), PFOS-PFDA (p = 0.10), PFOA-PFDA (p = 0.14), and PFHxS-stress scores (p = 0.14; Table S5). The pairwise interactions identified across models were generally inconsistent, with the exception of the PFHxS-stress scores interaction, which was identified in all three models (BKMR, NLinteraction, and MLR).

The PFHxS-stress score pairwise interaction identified in all models indicated that the weak positive association of PFHxS with BW-for-GA z-scores was stronger among participants with higher stress scores. This effect modification by stress was further evidenced in MLR models stratified by stress scores (< 6 vs. ≥6), where PFHxS was associated with 0.32 higher (95% CI = 0.00, 0.64) BW-for-GA z-scores among participants with high stress (≥ 6), whereas little association was observed (β=−0.14, 95% CI=−0.48, 0.19) among participants with low stress (< 6) (Fig. 4 and Table S6).

Secondary analyses

There was no evidence of sex-specific associations when we fit MLR models stratified by infant sex (female vs. male, Table S7).

Sensitivity analyses

Associations were generally similar in BKMR models in which we used stress scores reported at follow-up visits closest to conception (rather than baseline stress scores for all participants), though the overall mixture association was weaker and the PFHxS-stress score pairwise interaction observed in the primary BKMR model was no longer evident (Figure S3). Results were similar when we adjusted for prior duration of breastfeeding at baseline (Figure S4) and changed the smoothness of the kernel function from its default (r.b = 100) to r.b = 50 (Figure S5).

Associations were similar to our main findings when we increased the smoothness of the kernel to r.b = 1000 (Figure S6) or used a gamma prior distribution (Figure S7) in BKMR models, though the PFHxS-stress score interaction that we observed in our main findings was no longer evident. Our findings, including the PFHxS-stress interaction, were also consistent when we increased the number of iterations to 100,000 in BKMR models (Figure S8).

The PFHxS association was null in MLR models not co-adjusted for all PFAS (β = 0.05, 95% CI= −0.09, 0.20; Table S3), suggesting evidence of co-exposure confounding. Conversely, the association for PFHxS was stronger in MLR models when we restricted to nulliparous participants (β = 0.28, 95% CI=−0.10, 0.66; Table S8).

We further fit sensitivity analyses restricted to births reported at the first follow-up visit to assess the potential for exposure misclassification. In these models, the association for PFHxS was generally stronger and PFDA (β=−0.27, 95% CI=−0.59, 0.04) and MeFOSAA (β = 0.25, 95% CI=−0.03, 0.54; Table S8) were also associated with BW-for-GA z-scores, though these findings had wide confidence intervals given the small sample size. These sensitivity analyses suggest evidence of exposure misclassification in our primary models.

Discussion

In this prospective study of U.S. Black women, a mixture of PFAS and stress scores was weakly associated with higher BW-for-GA z-scores. This overall association of the mixture was driven primarily by PFHxS and stress scores. We observed a weak but consistent interaction between PFHxS-stress scores across statistical approaches, such that PFHxS was more strongly associated with higher BW-for-GA z-scores at higher levels of perceived stress. Although contrary to our hypothesis that the PFAS would be associated with lower birth size, our findings nonetheless suggest that preconception PFAS exposure may affect birth size, and that these associations could be stronger among women with higher levels of stress.

Multiple studies have investigated associations of maternal PFAS exposure and birth size, though with inconsistencies in the direction and magnitude of effects. Most previous studies reported inverse associations between maternal PFAS exposure in pregnancy, including PFHxS, PFOS, PFOA, PFNA, and PFDA, and infant birth size [37–53]. Alternatively, we found a positive association between the mixture and PFHxS with BW-for-GA z-scores. Conflicting findings may reflect differences in study populations, PFAS concentrations, or timing of exposure assessment (i.e., preconception vs. pregnancy). Importantly, assessing PFAS exposure during pregnancy may introduce confounding by maternal hemodynamics,61, 62 leading to a potential downward bias. For example, Verner and colleagues demonstrated that inverse associations of prenatal PFAS concentrations with birthweight were strongly confounded by maternal glomerular filtration rate, and that the strength of confounding was greater with increasing gestational age at sampling [61]. This may explain why previous studies reported inverse associations of pregnancy PFAS concentrations and birth size, whereas we found a positive association for PFHxS. To the best of our knowledge, only one previous study in U.S. women evaluated preconception PFAS serum concentrations and birth size, but in contrast to our study, reported inverse associations between PFHxS (as well as PFOS and PFOA) and birthweight [40]. Median maternal PFHxS concentrations in this earlier study were similar (0.8 ng/mL) to our study (0.5 ng/mL). Interestingly, paternal PFHxS concentrations measured in the preconception period (median = 1.7 ng/mL) were associated with higher birthweight [40].

Other studies reported null or positive associations between maternal PFAS exposure (assessed in pregnancy or at birth) and birth outcomes, including birth size [54–60]. Notably, a study in U.S. Black women from Atlanta reported positive associations between serum PFOA concentrations in the third trimester with birthweight z-scores even after adjusting for maternal glomerular filtration rate, though associations for other PFAS (including PFHxS) were null [60]. Similarly, positive associations between prenatal PFAS exposure (PFHxS, PFNA, PFDA, or perfluoroundecanoic acid [PFUnDA],) and measures of fetal growth - weight-for-length z-scores, ponderal index, BW-for-GA, and LGA infants - have been reported in U.S. and Chinese cohorts [49, 54, 56, 58]. Toxicological data support the hypothesis that maternal PFAS exposure may affect infant growth and development through multiple mechanisms. In vitro and in vivo studies suggest that PFAS exposure increases oxidative stress [96–98]. Contrary to the current findings, increased oxidative stress has been consistently associated with reduced birthweight and gestational length in epidemiologic studies [99–101], and may relate to activation of apoptotic pathways, cellular cytotoxicity, or effects on adipogenesis [96, 102, 103]. PFOS and PFOA, the best studied of the PFAS, have further been associated with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) and other nuclear receptor signaling activation [32–36]. PPAR-dependent pathways play critical roles in metabolic homeostasis and fetal development, including regulation of embryo implantation, placental development, fetal fatty acid metabolism, and fetal adipogenesis and myogenesis [104–106]. PPAR-ϒ aids in the development of the placenta, but activation by agonists has also been shown to alter placental vasculature and fatty acid uptake [96, 106]. These data are consistent with studies demonstrating the ability of PFAS to dysregulate placental fatty acid homeostasis, oxidative stress, and angiogenesis and vasculature [96], [107–109], suggesting that PFAS-induced perturbations to placental function may be important mechanisms underlying associations with birthweight and gestational length. Interestingly, PPAR-ϒ and PPAR-δ activation may increase fetal adiposity [33], where PPAR knockout mice pups showed reduced levels of adipose tissue, accompanied by functional changes in placental vascularization [104, 105]. Taken together, these pathways might help explain our current finding for PFHxS and previously reported associations between maternal PFAS exposure and higher birthweight or LGA births [49, 54, 56, 58, 60].

In vitro and in vivo studies further demonstrate that PFAS exposure can alter concentrations of hormones that support healthy pregnancies, although with inconsistencies. For example, studies have reported both positive and inverse associations between PFAS exposure and estradiol, testosterone, thyroid hormone, and progesterone concentrations in humans, animal models, and cell cultures [8, 24–31]. Directions of association in these studies often varied by PFAS chemical. Perturbations to hormonal function in pregnancy may affect fetal growth; indeed, multiple epidemiologic studies have reported that birthweight was positively associated with higher maternal estradiol, lower thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), lower free thyroxine (FT4), and higher triiodothyronine (T3) concentrations [110–113]. Maternal TSH concentrations were also found to mediate positive associations between maternal PFAS exposure and birth size in Chinese women [56]. Some studies reported that PFHxS concentrations were associated with lower T4 and higher estradiol concentrations in humans and animal models [8], [27], [114, 115], supporting the positive association for PFHxS observed in the current study.

We observed potential additive and interactive associations between the PFAS included in the mixture (PFHxS-MeFOSAA, PFOA-MeFOSAA, PFNA-MeFOSAA, PFOS-PFDA, and PFOA-PFDA) in relation to BW-for-GA z-scores, though the interactions between the PFAS were weak and not consistently identified by all three statistical approaches (BKMR, NLinteraction, and MLR). These interactions should therefore be interpreted with caution and evaluated in future studies with larger sample sizes. Like individual PFAS, previous findings for PFAS mixtures and birth outcomes were inconsistent in terms of the magnitude and direction of effect. For example, several studies have reported that PFAS mixtures, driven by various individual PFAS (e.g., PFHxS, PFOS, PFOA, PDFA, MeFOSAA, 2-(N-ethyl-perfluorooctane sulfonamido) acetic acid), were cumulatively associated with lower birthweight or BW-for-GA z-scores [37–40, 48]. Other studies reported positive cumulative associations between PFAS mixtures (driven by PFHxS, PFOS, PFDA, and PFNA, among others) or a mixture of PFAS and metals (e.g., manganese) and ponderal index, head circumference z-scores, birthweight, Apgar scores, BW-for-GA, and gestational length.[49, 56, 58, 64] Of the studies that utilized statistical approaches allowing for investigation of chemical interactions (e.g., BKMR) [37–39], [56], [64], none reported meaningful pairwise or higher-order interactions between any of the PFAS included in the mixture. Although there is limited epidemiologic evidence supporting the pairwise PFAS interactions observed in the current study, individual PFAS can have similar mechanisms of reproductive toxicity (e.g., estrogen interference) [96], suggesting exposure to mixtures could result in additive and/or interactive effects. Indeed, in vitro studies indicate that PFAS mixtures may have non-additive (e.g., synergistic) effects on sex hormone and receptor dysregulation, including altered estradiol secretion [116–118]. These data support our current findings and suggest that exposure to PFAS mixtures may have cumulative perinatal toxicity, with important implications for public health and policy interventions, for example, reducing PFAS exposure in drinking water [119].

We found evidence of a weak interaction between PFAS and psychosocial stress, such that positive associations of PFHxS with higher BW-for-GA z-scores were generally stronger among participants with higher levels of perceived stress. The pairwise interaction between PFHxS and perceived stress was identified in all three statistical approaches utilized in this study. These findings add to the growing body of evidence implicating non-chemical stressors as effect modifiers of chemical toxicity. Importantly, two previous studies reported effect modification of a PFAS mixture by non-chemical stressors, including perceived stress, depressive symptoms, gendered racial stress, and anxiety symptoms, in relation to birth outcomes [38, 39]. Among participants in the ECHO.CA.IL cohort, a mixture of PFAS, perceived stress, and depressive symptoms was jointly associated with lower birthweight z-scores, and this association was driven primarily by PFDA and depressive symptoms. Other PFAS, including PFHxS (median = 0.38 ng/mL), contributed to the mixture in the positive direction [39].

Another study by Eick et al. found that a mixture that included PFAS, perceived stress, depressive symptoms, gendered racial stress, and anxiety symptoms was jointly associated with lower BW-for-GA z-scores in a cohort of Black women, and that this association was driven primarily by PFOA, gendered racial stress, anxiety symptoms, and depressive symptoms. The PFAS mixture was further associated with lower BW-for-GA z-scores among participants with gendered racial stress above the median but reduced gestational age among participants with perceived stress scores below the median,38 suggesting the direction of effect modification by psychosocial stressors may depend on the specific stressor being considered. This finding (reduced gestational age at lower levels of perceived stress) is generally consistent with our findings, where PFHxS was associated with higher BW-for-GA z-scores at higher levels of perceived stress. Moreover, Eick et al. found evidence of a pairwise interaction between PFHxS and anxiety symptoms, similar to our study, though they found that PFHxS was associated with lower BW-for-GA z-scores at higher levels of anxiety symptoms [38]. PFHxS concentrations were more than double those of the current study (1.2 ng/mL vs. 0.5 ng/mL), which may explain opposing directions of association observed between our studies, and suggests that the direction of effect for PFHxS in relation to birth size could be non-linear and dose-dependent.

Overall, studies indicate that co-exposure to some PFAS and experience of stress may have cumulative effects on infant growth. Toxicological data support this hypothesis. Several PFAS can dysregulate the HPA axis (e.g., altering cortisol or corticotropin-releasing hormone levels) and have been independently associated with stress, depressive symptoms, and markers of oxidative stress and inflammation in women [120–125]. Experience of stress, anxiety, and depression can similarly dysregulate the HPA axis [66–68, 70]. Higher cortisol levels in pregnancy in turn can influence fetal growth [126–130]. As such, maternal stress during pregnancy has been previously associated (both positively and inversely) with birthweight [129–133]. An additional study by Eick et al. found that serum levels of PFOA and PFNA assessed in pregnancy were associated with higher concentrations of corticotropin-releasing hormone, and that these associations were stronger among participants with experience of depressive symptoms, stressful life events, food insecurity, and financial strain [71]. These findings further support the hypothesis that PFAS perinatal toxicity is influenced by psychosocial stress, highlighting the importance of considering non-chemical stressors of PFAS-birth outcome associations.

Investigating interactions between PFAS exposure and psychosocial stress is particularly important in populations of Black participants because Black women tend to experience greater perceived stress, depressive symptom severity, and anxiety symptom severity compared with White women [76, 134–137]. This may relate, in part, to greater experience with racism and discrimination [138–141]. Black women also experience higher rates of adverse birth outcomes [73–75, 142–144]. Cumulative effects of PFAS and psychosocial stressors on perinatal health may therefore contribute to these inequitable birth outcomes among Black women. Identifying psychosocial stressors, like experience of stress, as important modifiers of PFAS-birth outcomes associations also offers the opportunity to tailor public health interventions to minimize the effects of PFAS exposure. For example, our study and those by Eick et al. [38, 39, 71] suggest that reducing experience of stress, discrimination, depressive symptoms, anxiety, food insecurity, and financial strain in the preconception or prenatal periods could limit the potential effects of PFAS on infant growth and development in vulnerable populations. However, it should be noted that the PFHxS-stress score interaction that we observed in our primary findings was null in sensitivity analyses where we changed the smoothness of the BKMR kernel function or utilized stress scores temporally closest to conception (rather than measured at baseline). Additional studies with larger sample sizes and preconception data are therefore needed to confirm these findings. But studies to date could nonetheless be used to inform policies and programs to improve and promote maternal and infant health, especially among Black mothers.

Our focus on Black women from an established cohort was a major strength of this analysis and adds to a growing literature investigating environmental determinants of inequitable birth outcomes in the U.S. This study also represents one of the first to consider non-chemical stressors in relation to PFAS perinatal toxicity. We utilized prospective data and quantitatively assessed PFAS exposure. We employed structured questionnaires, including a validated assessment of perceived stress [82], and adjusted for multiple important confounding variables. Our focus on a PFAS mixture was also notable as fewer studies have considered exposure to multiple PFAS, particularly among Black women. We employed multiple state-of-the-art statistical approaches, which allowed us to assess additive, interactive, and non-linear effects.

Our study had important limitations. Because SELF was designed to investigate uterine fibroids, only a subset of participants reported pregnancies within the cohort, which limited our sample size and reduced statistical precision. However, our sample size was similar to sample sizes of other studies of PFAS mixtures and birth outcomes [37, 38, 40, 58, 145]. Participants in SELF also self-reported pregnancy outcomes, including birth size and gestational age, which may have introduced non-differential outcome misclassification. Self-reported pregnancy outcomes have been shown to have high reliability compared with birth records [146], suggesting any misclassification would likely be minimal and towards the null [147]. However, it should be noted that utilizing birthweight as a proxy for fetal growth has important limitations, notably, low sensitivity and specificity for determining growth-related infant morbidity and mortality [13]. While we consider our exposure measurement in the preconception period as a strength of this analysis, limiting potential confounding by hemodynamics in pregnancy [61, 62], the timing of collection of plasma for PFAS measurement may have introduced some degree of non-differential exposure misclassification. Importantly, the period between exposure assessment (at baseline) and pregnancy varied across participants. When we restricted our analysis to pregnancies reported at the first follow-up visit, our findings were markedly stronger for several PFAS, suggesting our primary findings were indeed biased towards the null by exposure misclassification. This could explain why some PFAS were not strongly associated with birth size in our primary analyses. We also multiply imputed missing data assuming that the data were missing at random, including missing stress scores at follow-up study visits. However, we acknowledge that experience of stress can affect study participation [148]. Stress scores may therefore not have been missing at random, although baseline sociodemographic characteristics for these participants were similar to the full sample (data not shown). Imputing these data (14% and 18% of participants at the first and second follow-up study visits, respectively, see Table S1) may have led to misclassification of the stress stress scores; this could explain, at least in part, null findings in sensitivity analyses where we used imputed stress scores (i.e., analyses utilizing scores reported closest to conception).

In conclusion, we found weak associations between a mixture of PFAS and stress scores, driven by PFHxS and stress scores, with higher BW-for-GA z-scores in a cohort of Black women. There was some evidence of weak interactions between the PFAS and PFHxS-stress scores, suggesting that chemical and non-chemical stressors may have cumulative effects on birth size, with implications for infant growth and development. These findings support the need for public health interventions focused on reducing both chemical exposure and psychosocial stressors and promoting social support for pregnant individuals.
