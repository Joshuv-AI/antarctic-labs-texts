Prospective clinical evaluation of cell-free DNA next generation sequencing in patients with suspected metastatic lung cancer
Authors: Christi M J Steendam, Ruud W J Meijers, Peggy Atmodimedjo, Lisette van Dijk, Cor van der Leest, Keetie C J van Loenhout, Susan C van ’t Westeinde, Daphne W Dumoulin, Wessel E J J Hanselaar, Marthe S Paats, Evert de Jonge, Ron H N van Schaik, Jan H von der Thüsen, Joachim G J V Aerts, Anne-Marie C Dingemans, Hendrikus Jan Dubbink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMolecular analysis of tumor material is standard in metastatic non-squamous non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), but it is not always feasible, limiting targeted treatment options. We investigated the clinical value of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) next-generation sequencing (NGS) in patients with clinically and radiologically suspected metastatic NSCLC. Within the comprehensive cancer network Southwest (The Netherlands), Erasmus MC launched the Lung Cancer Diagnosis (LCD) – cfDNA project. For patients with suspected metastatic lung cancer where tissue analysis was not feasible, thoracic oncologists submitted plasma samples for cfDNA NGS. Results were discussed by the Thoracic Oncology Molecular Tumor board. Between January 1st, 2019, and January 1st, 2023, 108 plasma samples were submitted and analyzed. In 6 patients (5.6%), an activating EGFR mutation was identified as a potential target for treatment. Additional actionable genomic alterations (AGA) include a BRAF p.V600E mutation (n = 1) and KRAS p.G12C mutations (n = 6). In 42 patients, other non-AGA (KRAS other than p.G12C, NRAS, BRAF p.G466V, PIK3CA, TP53) were detected that did not affect the choice of systemic therapy. In a real-world clinical practice setting, our study showed that NGS can detect AGAs in plasma when tumor tissue analysis is not feasible in suspected metastatic NSCLC. Introduction

Patients diagnosed with locally advanced or metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) benefit in terms of improved overall survival (OS) when an actionable genomic alteration (AGA) is present that can be treated with a targeted agent1. For instance, in patients with activating Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) mutations, treated with osimertinib in first line, median OS improved to 38.6 months, the 5-year OS rate for patients with Anaplastic Lymphoma Kinase (ALK) rearrangement treated with alectinib in first line is 62.5%2,3. and the 5-year PFS for lorlatinib is 60%4.

As expanding options for targeted therapy become available based on the molecular profile of the tumor, the genetic characterization of NSCLC has gained considerable importance in recent years. In the diagnostic process of locally advanced or metastatic non-squamous NSCLC, molecular analysis is therefore standard-of-care5. Unfortunately, this is not always feasible, as in daily practice it can be difficult to obtain tumor tissue, or there may be insufficient tissue available for molecular analysis at the time of diagnosis6–8.

In the past years, plasma cell-free DNA (cfDNA) analysis has been shown to identify tumor mutations with high concordance with mutation analyses derived from tumor tissue9. Although shedding of cell-free circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) can be limited in cases with low tumor load, or solely intrathoracic or cerebral disease location, most patients with an EGFR mutation and metastatic disease have the driver mutation detectable in plasma9,10.

To improve the use of molecular testing in patients for whom adequate molecular analysis of tumor tissue is not possible, the Comprehensive Cancer Network Southwest in the Netherlands launched a project for prospective plasma cfDNA analysis to identify additional patients who might benefit from targeted treatment11.

Results

Between January 1st, 2019, and January 1st, 2023, plasma samples from 108 patients were received and analyzed as part of the prospective Lung Cancer Diagnosis (LCD)-ctDNA project. Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. Referring physicians reported the reason for plasma analysis as shown in Table 1. In 84 patients (77.8%) there was no lung cancer tissue diagnosis available at the time of plasma analysis (Supplementary Table 2). In 15 patients (13.9%) there was no tissue available because the lesion, mainly cerebral, lytic skeletal, and/or abdominal lesions, was unreachable for biopsy. Biopsy refusal occurred in 29 patients (26.8%), mainly because these patients had poor (respiratory) condition and a bronchoscopic approach was necessary. Detected aberrations in plasma

In total, in 55 of the 108 samples (50,9%) a genetic aberration was detected, as shown in Fig. 1. Table 2 summarizes all the detected AGA in patients of our cohort. In 6 patients (5.6%) an activating EGFR mutation was detected. Five patients showed an EGFR exon 19 deletion of which four patients started on EGFR-TKI based on the plasma cfDNA analysis. One patient had rapid clinical deterioration and therefore received best supportive care only and passed away shortly after diagnosis. In the plasma of the sixth patient with an EGFR mutation, an exon 20 insertion was detected, for which no regular targeted treatment was available, and the patient declined participation in a clinical trial. In one patient, a BRAF p.V600E mutation was detected in plasma and in a later stage also confirmed in tissue.

In 6 patients (5.6%), a KRAS p.G12C mutation was detected (Fig. 1). In addition, in 42 samples (38.8%) other aberrations were detected in KRAS (other than p.G12C), BRAF (p.G466V), NRAS (p.G13V), TP53 and PIK3CA. (Supplementary Table 1). In one patient, a variant of unknown significance (VUS) in EGFR (p.A864V) was detected at a very low frequency. It is uncertain whether this exon 21 variant is activating, and interpretation with respect to NSCLC is unknown. Multiple mutations were detected in plasma in 22 patients (20.3%) and in 53 cases (49%) no mutations were identified.

All aberrations detected in plasma are shown in Fig. 1 (AGA) and supplementary Table 1 (non-AGA) and results of tissue in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Correlation between plasma results and subsequent tumor tissue results

The Pathological National Automated Archive (PALGA) and record search revealed that tumor tissue was obtained at a later stage in the disease course for 24 out of 108 patients. Additionally, one patient had tissue results available from a previous diagnosis of bladder cancer. In 15 of the 24 patients, the previous pathological analysis (PA) was insufficient for establishing a histological diagnosis and/or performing molecular diagnostics. For five patients, the lesion was previously inaccessible, and for three patients, either the patient or the physician had declined a biopsy.

The aberrations detected in plasma were confirmed in tissue in 4 cases (Fig. 2). In 11 patients, no mutations were detected in the liquid biopsy, but mutations were identified in an additional tissue biopsy (Fig. 2). In 4 of these 11 patients, an AGA was detected in tissue. Two patients (number 90 and 99 of supplementary Table 3) had a splice site mutation in exon 14 of MET, resulting in exon 14 skipping for which both patients received MET targeted therapy (crizotinib and tepotinib). Two patients (number 36 of supplementary Tables 1 and 102 of supplementary Table 3) had an ALK rearrangement based on ISH and positive IHC and were successfully treated with ALK kinase inhibitor alectinib (supplementary Fig. 1). In the other 7 patients, there was no AGA detected in the additional tissue biopsy.

In 6 patients tissue pathology resulted in a different diagnosis than NSCLC (namely mesothelioma in case 13, metastasized pancreatic cancer in case 23, metastasized ovarian cancer in case 32, small cell lung cancer (SCLC) in case 64 and Aspergillosis in case 69 and metastasized papillary thyroid carcinoma in case 108, Supplementary Table 3). For one additional patient (case 92), a probable diagnosis of metastasized renal cell carcinoma was suspected after reevaluation of the PET-CT scan.

One patient had an EGFR (p.A864V) VUS in blood, which was not detected in tissue. Additionally, the histological examination revealed it to be mesothelioma, in which the EGFR variant was not detected. The case with pancreatic cancer showed a KRAS p.G12V mutation in plasma (molecular analysis in tissue was not possible). The patient with metastasized ovarian cancer had a TP53 p.G244V mutation in plasma, while in tissue a different TP53 mutation (p.R181C) and a KRAS p.G12D mutation were detected. No mutations were detected in the plasma of the remaining 4 patients.

In two patients, no mutations were detected in either liquid or tissue biopsy (Table 3). One of them turned out to have no malignancy but aspergillosis (case 69). The other patient had only a low frequency of tumor cells present in the biopsy, so mutational analysis was done using the same Oncomine™ Lung cfDNA Assay V1 panel. This panel is a much smaller gene panel than the regular Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) panel for tissue biopsies, so genomic alterations are possibly missed.

Discussion

In a real-world clinical practice setting, our study showed the ability to use NGS to detect targets for treatment in plasma in a cohort of patients with suspected metastatic lung cancer that would otherwise have no access to molecular analysis. This is important for a substantial number of patients. For instance, in the Netherlands, only 86% of newly diagnosed patients with advanced NSCLC were tested for EGFR in 201912, even though EGFR mutations are detected in around 13.5% of patients who undergo molecular testing13. Although there are several reasons why EGFR testing was not performed, our population, for whom molecular diagnostics were not feasible, is part of this. Another challenge in this era is the widespread use of cytology like small needle aspirates, which has the risk of delivering small amounts of tumour material which is insufficient for additional molecular diagnostics. In these cases, cfDNA analysis can assist to identify AGA.

The availability of targeted therapy in current practice is evident for the patients with a common EGFR mutation, and to a lesser extent for the patient with EGFR exon 20 insertion (treatment in trial possible at the time of the liquid biopsy, new therapies are under development)14. At the time of detection there was no available targeted therapy for KRAS p.G12C. However, sotorasib is nowadays an EMA and FDA approved specific KRAS G12C inhibitor15, and other KRAS inhibitors are under development and tested in clinical trials16. In one patient, an activating BRAF V600E mutation (BRAF-TKI) was detected. As this mutation is not specific for NSCLC, additional tissue biopsy to demonstrate that the BRAF V600E mutation originates from NSCLC was advised by the Molecular Tumor Board (MTB).

Notably, for 24 patients (22.2%), an additional biopsy was performed later in the course of the disease, allowing molecular diagnostics for 21 of these patients. Based on histopathological diagnosis, for 17 patients (70.8%) there was agreement with the previous diagnosis based on radiological and clinical findings. However, for 6 patients (25%), a different diagnosis was made based on this additional tissue biopsy. The number of non-lung cancers in our cohort may be significantly higher, underscoring that treatment based on plasma cfDNA results alone should be carefully weighted, especially when mutations other than in EGFR are detected. Obtaining a tissue biopsy remains important for achieving an accurate histological diagnosis and an appropriate treatment plan. For example, two splice donor site mutations resulting in MET exon 14 skipping were detected in the tissue of two patients and not in plasma, probably due to low amounts of ctDNA. Other factors that may explain the differences in molecular findings between liquid and tissue biopsy include tumor heterogeneity, low tumor shedding, timing of blood collection, analytical sensitivity, and differences between the NGS tests used. Nevertheless, plasma can be of added value in a subset of patients, as demonstrated by the detection of possible targets for treatment in 13 of the 108 patients (12%) in our study. This was also observed in another study, which analyzed results of 323 patients with tissue confirmed metastatic NSCLC who had received routine plasma testing in the workup17. In the 84 (77.7%) patients with only plasma investigation at diagnosis, a therapeutically targetable mutation was detected in 13 (12%) patients. Another study showed a high response rate of 88% in patients with initiated targeted therapy based on cfDNA only at diagnosis, in a population of histologically confirmed stage IV NSCLC18.

Also, the question remains how to interpret the presence of aberrations in non-targetable genes, or unexpected findings. TP53 and KRAS mutations in plasma can sometimes, though rare, be explained by clonal hematopoiesis or could reflect another primary malignancy19. This is illustrated by the patient in our study where the diagnosis turned out to be pulmonary metastasized ovarian carcinoma (patient 32 Table S1), and not a primary pulmonary malignancy, after comparison of the molecular profile of the earlier resected ovarian cancer. Therefore, the discussion of plasma cfDNA results in an MTB in a referral center remains of utmost importance and should consider the medical history to advise the clinical physician in allied centers. Parallel assessment of radiological imaging could possibly further optimize the generated advice in the future.

A limitation of our study is the narrow scope of the NGS panel, which includes only 11 relevant genes. The Oncomine V1 panel detects mutations at key hotspots but does not cover all currently known AGAs in lung cancer, particularly gene fusions and amplifications. This is evident from two cases with ALK rearrangement in tissue analysis (case 36 and 102 Table S2), which cannot be detected in plasma with this panel. ctDNA analysis using a larger, more comprehensive panel with the ability to detect rearrangements, fusions, and amplifications will identify more patients with actionable molecular alterations in the absence of tissue material but was not available at the start of our LCD project. Currently, these panels are available, including the Guardant 360 assay and the TruSight Oncology 500 ctDNA panel, which showed frequencies and distributions of molecular aberrations as predicted in populations with NSCLC, and allow detection of amplifications in plasma ctDNA20,21. In addition, broader panels for ctDNA analysis might be able to trace the tissue origin of the detected alterations, if a high mutational tumor load is present in the plasma, based on mutation signatures22.

However, as not all parts of the world have access to the most advanced panels of genetic investigation in plasma, it is important to realize that even a panel with a limited extent is able to identify patients with possible benefit from targeted therapies in a real-life setting. Finally, it is important to emphasize that discussing such liquid biopsy results during an MTB is crucial for the accurate interpretation of the data. During an MTB, attended by both pulmonologists, pathologists, and clinical scientists in molecular pathology, the liquid biopsy results can be explained and evaluated in the context of the patient’s clinical data. Based on this, the best possible advice can be provided to the treating physician23.

In conclusion, our LCD-project demonstrates that in a prospective unselected clinical setting of patients in whom molecular analysis on tissue cannot be performed in case of radiologically and clinically suspected metastatic lung cancer, that despite the limited extent of the NGS panel, analysis of cfDNA in plasma provides an opportunity to detect driver mutations for subsequent targeted therapy. Nevertheless, plasma diagnostics cannot replace histological diagnosis yet as illustrated by patients with a different diagnosis based on a biopsy at a later stage. Next to that, a negative cfDNA result does not necessarily indicate the absence of actionable driver mutations in the tumor. Lastly, the MTB is important for the interpretation of the results and has a crucial advisory role in further treatment based on findings in cfDNA.

Materials and methods

In 2019, Erasmus Medical Centre Cancer Institute launched the project LCD–cfDNA. Thoracic oncologists from 18 regional hospitals in the Comprehensive Cancer Network Southwest (The Netherlands) were given the opportunity to send plasma samples for NGS analysis. Patients were eligible if an adequate molecular analysis of tumor tissue was not possible and if confirmed or suspected metastatic lung cancer was present based on radiological and clinical findings. Our procedures regarding blood collection, cfDNA isolation, and plasma NGS with Oncomine Lung cfDNA Assay v1 have been previously described9.

Results of plasma analysis were discussed in the Thoracic Oncology - MTB and reported to the referring physician. Medical ethical committee approval and informed consent was not required as molecular testing of plasma is considered standard-of-care in absence of possibility of molecular analysis of tissue17. Only descriptive and anonymized data were used in line with the EU General Data Protection Regulation. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

After the liquid biopsy analysis, results from patients were registered, and we explored whether any molecular analysis on tumor tissue later in the disease course had been performed to correlate these results with the previously obtained plasma results. This was achieved by consulting the Laboratory Management System (LMS) connected to the PALGA, and by retrospectively reviewing the available general pathology results in the patient records in June 2024.
