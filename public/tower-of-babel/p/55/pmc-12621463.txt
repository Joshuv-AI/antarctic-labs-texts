Pseudomonas aeruginosa-targeted mesoporous gold nanorod nanogenerator: NIR laser-triggered alkyl radical generation for enhanced bacterial wound treatment
Authors: Wenkang Liu, Tao Wu, Wenxuan Ye, Mingyang He, Jianfu Zhao, Wei Xue, Siming Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Currently, Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) infections pose a significant threat to public health, owing to the high morbidity, mortality and antibiotic resistance. Alkyl radical (AR)-based photodynamic therapy exhibits great potential in antibacterial applications due to its oxygen-independent advantage. Nevertheless, targeted delivery systems capable of precisely controlling AR release at bacterial infectious foci are still lacking. In the present work, a mesoporous gold nanorod (mGNR)-based multifunctional nanogenerator with P. aeruginosa-targeting and near infrared (NIR) laser-controlled AR release properties was fabricated for enhanced bacterial wound treatment. Herein, mGNR with mild-photothermal therapeutic (MPTT) effect was firstly synthesized and used as a carrier for effective loading of an AR initiator 2, 2′-azobis[2-(2-imidazolin-2-yl)propane]-dihydrochloride (AIPH). Then, cysteamine and polymer of HOOC-PEG5000-Mal were added in sequence to functionalize the nanosystem, obtaining nanogenerator of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal. Due to the specific maleimide (Mal) and Type IV Pilus (T4P) reaction, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal displayed high binding efficiency on P. aeruginosa. Upon NIR laser irradiation, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal triggered AIPH cleavage to generate substantial amount of AR, which enabled high-efficient inhibition on P. aeruginosa and biofilms via the synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial effect. Moreover, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal exhibited excellent antibacterial and wound healing effect on P. aeruginosa infectious rats’ wounds. In-depth mechanism study revealed that the synergistic MPTT/AR therapeutic effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal induced P. aeruginosa death by breaking bacterial membrane, triggering ROS boost, GSH depletion and DNA damage, as well as promoted wound healing by repolarizing M2-type macrophages and upregulating CD 31 and α-SMA expressions.

Introduction

Chronic wound infections, particularly those caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), represent a critical challenge in modern healthcare. World Health Organization (WHO) has classified P. aeruginosa as a "Priority 1: Critical" pathogen due to its high morbidity, mortality, and resistance to first- and second-line antibiotics [1]. P. aeruginosa infections are perennially associated with elevated morbidity and mortality rates, accounting for 10 %–11 % of all hospital-acquired infections worldwide [2]. Current therapeutic strategies, such as systemic antibiotics (e.g., ceftazidime, colistin) and topical antiseptics, often fail due to biofilm-mediated tolerance (up to 1000-fold decrease in susceptibility) and rising resistance [3,4]. Moreover, recurrent infections occur in approximately 80 % of cases due to incomplete biofilm eradication, highlighting the limitations of conventional therapies [5]. Therefore, developing novel antibacterial strategies is urgently needed for the highly effective treatment of P. aeruginosa-associated wound infections.

Up to now, many approaches including antimicrobial peptides, phage therapy, and nanotechnology-based delivery systems have been developed for antibacterial applications, with focus on overcoming biofilm resistance and pathogen adaptability [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Among those, photodynamic therapy (PDT) by utilizing laser of specific wavelength to activate photosensitizers to generate cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) has shown efficacy in eradicating superficial bacterial infections [14,15]. Nevertheless, obvious drawback remains as conventional PDT is strongly oxygen-dependent for ROS generation [[16], [17], [18]], the therapeutic outcome of which is significantly weakened in hypoxic microenvironments such as that of the chronic wounds. Recently, alkyl radical (AR), a type of free radicals that can be generated independently of oxygen levels, has been attracted much attention in many biomedical applications. In a recent work of Dong's group, AIPH was incorporated into the poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA)/polyoxometalate (POM) hydrogel for bacterial wound infection treatment. Upon 1064 nm irradiation, alkyl radical generated from the hydrogel could effectively eradicate drug-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) on mice's infected wounds [19]. However, the majority of the current AR-related studies predominantly focus on anticancer applications, and research exploring the use of AR in antibacterial applications remains limited. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, targeted delivery systems enabling precise AR-controlled release at bacterial infection sites has not yet developed.

Targeted delivery has been considered as an effective way to treat bacterial infections. Generally, nanosystems are functionalized with antibodies, aptamers, or bacteria-specific molecules to precisely recognize bacterial surface markers, thereby enhancing their delivery efficiency and minimizing off-target toxicity to host cells [[20], [21], [22]]. Type IV Pilus (T4P) is a type of polymeric protein nanofiber that specifically exists in several human pathogens including P. aeruginosa, which plays important roles in bacterial adhesion, motility, and biofilm formation [23]. Recent studies indicated that maleimide can specifically target and bind with T4P to reduce movement of P. aeruginosa [24,25]. In a recent work, Xu et al. developed a tobramycin-mediated self-assembling DNA nanotube modified with maleimide to target the T4P of P. aeruginosa. It showed that the targeting capability of the DNA nanotube significantly enhanced the local concentration of tobramycin, leading to a pronounced therapeutic effect on pulmonary inflammation caused by P. aeruginosa infection [26]. This suggested that maleimide mediated T4P-targeting approach is a promising strategy for the targeted delivery of AR to P. aeruginosa-associated infections.

In the present work, mesoporous gold nanorod (mGNR) with excellent photothermal effect in the near infrared (NIR) region was rationally designed and fabricated for AIPH loading, obtaining nanogenerator of mGNR@AIPH. Subsequently, mGNR@AIPH was functionalized with cysteamine and conjugated with bifunctional PEG polymers with terminal carboxyl and maleimide groups (HOOC-PEG5000-Mal) via the amido bond, obtaining multifunctional nanogenerator of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal. Because maleimide can specifically recognize and bind with T4P of P. aeruginosa, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal displayed high P. aeruginosa-targeting ability. Upon NIR laser irradiation, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal exhibited mild photothermal therapeutic (MPTT) effect, which subsequently triggered the cleavage of the loaded AIPH by generating alkyl radicals, enabling synergistic mild photothermal and alkyl radical (MPTT/AR) antibacterial applications. In vitro antibacterial evaluations displayed that the synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was capable of significantly inhibiting the activity of P. aeruginosa, as well as eradicating P. aeruginosa biofilms. In vivo antibacterial evaluations indicated that mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal could effectively eliminate the infected P. aeruginosa in rats’ wounds and promoted wound healing due to the synergistic MPTT/AR therapeutic effect. Furthermore, the in-depth synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial mechanism of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, as well as the in vivo wound healing mechanism, were investigated and elucidated.

Experimental sections

Materials

Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), ammonium hydroxide solution (25–28 %), tetrakis(hydroxymethyl)phosphonium chloride (THPC), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) and potassium carbonate (K2CO3) were purchased from MACKLIN Industrial Co. (shanghai, China). 3-Aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES), cysteamine, chloroauric acid (HAuCl4) and 2,2'-Azobis[2-(2-imidazolin-2-yl)propane] Dihydrochloride (AIPH,96 %) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Ltd (Shanghai, China). HOOC-PEG5000-Maleimide (HOOC-PEG5000-Mal) was obtained from Tanshtech Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). All materials were used without further purification unless declared.

Bacterial strains of Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, ATCC29213), Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC25922) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa, ATCC 9027) were obtained from Guangdong Microbial Culture Collection Center (Guangzhou, China). Lysogeny broth (LB) and LB agar were purchased from Coolaber Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (DCFH-DA) and terminal-deoxynucleotidyl transferase mediated nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay kit were obtained from Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (China). The LIVE/DEAD Baclight bacterial viability assay kit (L7012), Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium were bought from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc (MA, USA). Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from KeyGEN Biotechnology (Jiangsu, China). Glutathione Fluorescent Probe ThiolTrace Violet 500 was obtained from AAT Bioquest (California, USA).

Synthesis of the mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal nanoreactor

Synthesis of mesoporous silica nanorod (mSiNR)

mSiNR was synthesized according to our previously reported method [27]. Initially, 0.05 g of CTAB was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water under ultrasonication. Subsequently, 1.5 mL of ammonium hydroxide solution and 0.3 mL of TEOS were added to the solution under stirring and refluxed at 70 °C for 5 h. The resulting mixture was then extracted with a mixed hydrochloric acid/anhydrous ethanol solution (V/V, 1:1), followed by centrifugation (12 000 rpm, 10 min) and washing three times with anhydrous ethanol. The precipitate was redispersed in a mixed solution (10 mL) containing deionized water and ethanol (V/V, 1:1) and sonicated for 10 min. Next, 360 μL of APTES was added, and the solution was refluxed at 80 °C overnight. The resultant mSiNR was obtained by centrifugation (12 000 rpm, 10 min) and washing three times with deionized water. Finally, the product was redispersed in 3 mL of deionized water for further use.

Synthesis of mesoporous gold nanorod (mGNR)

To prepare mGNR, gold seeds (Au seeds) were first synthesized. Briefly, 1 mL of THPC and 1.5 mL of NaOH solution (0.2 M) were added to 45 mL of deionized water under magnetic stirring, and the mixture was stirred for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 2 mL of HAuCl4 solution (25 mM) was rapidly added to obtain the Au seeds solution. Next, 10 mL of the Au seeds solution was mixed with 1.5 mL of mSiNR solution and 0.2 mL of NaCl solution (0.2 M) and stirred for 3 h. The mixture was then centrifuged (12 000 rpm, 10 min), and the precipitate was washed three times with deionized water and redispersed in 2 mL of deionized water (designated as mSiNR@GNPs). Finally, 200 μL of mSiNR@GNPs and 1 mL of formaldehyde were added to a 5 mL mixed solution containing HAuCl4 (0.02 M) and K2CO3 solution (0.25 mg/mL) and stirred for 30 min. The resulting suspensions were centrifuged (5000 rpm, 10 min) and washed three times with deionized water. Finally, samples were lyophilized and stored at −20 °C for further use.

Synthesis of mGNR@AIPH

10 mg of mGNR and 20 mg of AIPH were dispersed in 10 mL deionized water and stirred in the dark for 24 h at room temperature. The mGNR@AIPH nanoparticles were obtained by centrifugation (5000 rpm, 10 min) and washed three times with deionized water. The supernatant after each washing step was collected and subjected to UV–Vis measurement to detect the UV–Vis absorbance value at 350 nm (UV350). The weight of AIPH in the supernatant was calculated based on the UV350-AIPH concentration standard curve. Then, the AIPH loading efficiency was calculated using the following formula:AIPHLoadingEfficiency(%)=Weight(added)−Weight(supernatant)Weight(added)×100%Where Weight (added) was the original addition amount of AIPH added, Weight (supernatant) was the AIPH in the supernatant that was unloaded.

Synthesis of the mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal

To obtain mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, 20 mg of mGNR@AIPH and 200 μL of cysteamine were dispersed in 10 mL of deionized water and stirred in the dark for 12 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm and washed three times with deionized water to yield mGNR@AIPH-NH2. Next, 10 mg of HOOC-(PEG)5000-Mal was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water and activated with EDC and NHS for 4 h. Then, 5 mg of mGNR@AIPH-NH2 was added, and the reaction was carried out in the dark for 12 h at room temperature. The resultant mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was collected by centrifugation (5000 rpm, 10 min) and washed three times with deionized water. Finally, samples were lyophilized and stored at −20 °C for further use.

Characterization

The morphology and size of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL TEM-1210) at an operating voltage of 120 kV. UV–Vis spectra were recorded on a UV-2450/2250 (Shimazu) spectrophotometer with a wavelength range from 200 to 900 nm. The hydrodynamic diameter and the Zeta potential of samples were measured on a Zetasizer Nano ZS apparatus (Malvern). FT-IR spectra of samples were recorded on a Nexus 670 FT-IR spectrophotometer (Nicolet). Confocal images were recorded on a laser confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM 880).

Photothermal effect evaluation

The photothermal effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal under 808 nm laser irradiation was evaluated using an NIR thermal camera (model 220s, Fotric). The experiments were conducted at different power densities (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 W/cm2) and different concentrations (0, 80, 160, and 320 μg/mL). The real-time temperature of the samples was recorded every 30 s using a thermocouple probe.

For photothermal stability evaluation, a dispersion of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal (160 μg/mL) was irradiated with an 808 nm laser at a power density of 1 W/cm2 for 5 min. The laser was then turned off to allow the dispersion to cool to room temperature. This heating and cooling cycle was repeated five times, with the temperature recorded at 30 s intervals. Finally, time-temperature curves were plotted to assess the photothermal stability of the mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal nanocomposites.

NIR-controlled AIPH release property evaluation

Detection of alkyl radical generation

The alkyl radical generation was detected using the probe of 2,2'-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS). In the presence of alkyl radicals, ABTS in solution is converted to green ABTS·+, which exhibits a strong absorption peak in the range of 500–900 nm. Specifically, 0.2 mL of ABTS solution (2 mg/mL) was mixed with 0.2 mL of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal and incubated at 37 °C and 45 °C for 1, 2, and 4 h, respectively. After centrifugation (5000 rpm), the supernatant was collected and the UV–Vis absorption spectra was recorded using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Notably, the absorption peak of ABTS·+ at 742 nm is linearly related to the concentration of alkyl radicals.

Electron pin resonance (ESR) assay

ESR measurement was conducted to investigate alkyl radical generation. Briefly, a mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal sample containing 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) in a 96-well plate was irradiated using an 808 nm laser with the power density 1 W/cm2 for 5 min, following by ESR measurement. Deionized water and mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal without laser irradiation were used as control groups.

Continuous NIR laser irradiation

The mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal dispersion was subjected to continuous irradiation using an 808 nm laser at a power density of 1 W/cm2, with a total irradiation time of 60 min. At each 5 min interval, 200 μL of the mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal dispersion was took out and centrifuged (5000 rpm), following by mixing with an equal volume of ABTS solution. Finally, the UV–Vis absorbance value of the mixture at 742 nm was recorded.

Intermittent NIR laser irradiation

The mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal dispersion was subjected to intermittent irradiation (5 min irradiation, and 5 min stop) using an 808 nm laser at a power density of 1 W/cm2. At each 5 min interval, 200 μL of the mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal dispersion was took out and centrifuged (5000 rpm), following by mixing with an equal volume of ABTS solution. Finally, the UV–Vis absorbance value of the mixture at 742 nm was recorded.

AR release percentage calculation

AR release percentage was calculated using the following formula:ARreleasepercentage(%)=UV742/sample−UV742/beginUV742/all−UV742/begin×100%Where UV742/begin is the UV–Vis absorbance value of the supernatant (mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal without NIR irradiation)-ABTS mixed solution at 742 nm. UV742/all is UV–Vis absorbance value of the supernatant (mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal with 60 min continuous NIR irradiation)-ABTS mixed solution at 742 nm. After 60 min continuous NIR laser irradiation, the UV742 value did not increase anymore, which we thought 100 % AR was released. UV742/sample is the UV–Vis absorbance value of the supernatant (mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal with X min continuous or intermittent NIR irradiation)-ABTS mixed solution at 742 nm. UV742/begin = 0.4178, UV742/all = 2.8473.

In vitro antibacterial effect evaluation

Bacterial culture

Gram-negative P. aeruginosa and E. coli, as well as Gram-positive S. aureus, were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium for 12 h at 37 °C until the optical density (OD) at 600 nm (OD600) reached 1.0, indicating bacterial growth in the logarithmic phase. The bacterial suspensions were then centrifuged, and the pellets were washed twice with 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to remove residual culture medium. Finally, the bacterial pellets were resuspended in PBS to a concentration of 1 × 108 colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL).

Synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial effect evaluation

In a 96-well plate, 100 μL of each sample (I: PBS, II: mGNR@PEG-Mal, III: mGNR@AIPH and IV: mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal) was mixed with 100 μL of P. aeruginosa suspensions (1 × 108 CFU/mL) to achieve a final material concentration of 160 μg/mL. For groups subjected to NIR laser irradiation, the material-bacterial suspensions were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2) for 5 min and then incubated at 37 °C in a shaker for 4 h. Subsequently, the bacterial suspensions were diluted with PBS, and 100 μL of the diluted suspensions were plated onto LB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Finally, the number of bacterial colonies was counted using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) to evaluate the synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal.

Targeting efficiency evaluation

The targeting efficiency of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal to the T4P pili of P. aeruginosa was evaluated using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Briefly, RB-PEG5000-Mal was incubated with 100 μL of P. aeruginosa suspensions for 1, 2, and 4 h, respectively. The bacterial suspensions were then centrifuged and washed three times with PBS. Finally, the material-bacteria complexes were imaged using a CLSM 880 microscope. Gram-positive S. aureus was used as a control.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) measurement was also conducted to text the binding efficiency of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal. Briefly, 100 μL of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was mixed with 100 μL of P. aeruginosa suspensions in a 96-well plate to achieve a final concentration of 160 μg/mL and incubated for 5 h. Subsequently, the material-bacteria suspension was transferred onto a copper grid and observed using a TEM microscope.

In vitro antibiofilm effect evaluation

Bacterial biofilm creation

To create bacterial biofilms, 100 μL of P. aeruginosa suspensions were mixed with an equal volume of LB broth medium and cultured in a 96-well plate at 37 °C for 48 h. The LB broth medium was gently replaced with fresh medium every 12 h.

Crystal violet staining

To investigate the synergistic MPTT/AR anti-biofilm activity of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, different treating groups were designed: Control, Control + NIR, mGNR@PEG-Mal, mGNR@AIPH, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, mGNR@PEG-Mal + NIR, mGNR@AIPH + NIR, and mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR. Briefly, 200 μL of sample-PBS solution (final concentration 160 μg/mL) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. After treatment, the biofilms were washed twice with PBS, and 200 μL of methanol was added and incubated for 15 min to fix the bacterial morphology. Subsequently, 200 μL of 0.1 % crystal violet dye was added and stained for 30 min. The biofilm-material suspensions were washed three times with PBS and imaged with a digital camera. Finally, 100 μL of ethanol was added and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min to dissolve the crystal violet. The OD570 value of the crystal violet-stained solution was measured using a microplate reader.

Three-dimensional SYTO-9/PI dual-fluorescence staining

After the above different treatments (I: Control, II: mGNR@PEG-Mal, III: mGNR@AIPH, IV: mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, V: mGNR@PEG-Mal + NIR, VI: mGNR@AIPH + NIR and VII: mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR), the biofilms were gently washed twice with PBS to remove residual materials. Subsequently, 3 μL of SYTO-9/PI fluorescent dye was added and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Finally, the bacterial biofilms were washed three times with PBS to remove free dye and imaged using a LSM 880 confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Bacteria membrane damage evaluation

SYTO-9/PI dual-fluorescence staining

The experiments were divided into seven groups with different treatments, I: Control, II: mGNR@PEG-Mal, III: mGNR@AIPH, IV: mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, V: mGNR@PEG-Mal + NIR, VI: mGNR@AIPH + NIR and VII: mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR. After each treatment, the bacteria-material complexes were washed three times with PBS, and then 3 μL of fluorescent dye was added and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, the bacterial suspensions were centrifuged, washed with PBS, and imaged using a CLSM 880 confocal microscope.

SEM measurements

Changes in bacterial membranes after interaction with materials under different treatments were further investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Briefly, after each treatment, the bacteria-material complexes were centrifuged, washed, and redispersed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. The glutaraldehyde was then removed by centrifugation, and the samples were gradually dehydrated using 30 %, 50 %, 70 %, 80 %, 90 %, and 100 % ethanol for 10 min each. The samples were then transferred onto a silicon wafer, coated with gold by sputtering, and imaged using a scanning electron microscope.

Intracellular ROS detection

Changes in endogenous ROS levels in bacteria after interaction with materials were detected using DCFH-DA as a probe. Briefly, after each treatment, the culture medium was replaced with PBS containing 10 μM DCFH-DA and incubated in the dark for 30 min. The bacterial suspensions were then centrifuged, washed, and imaged using a confocal microscope.

Intracellular DNA leakage evaluation

Disruption of bacterial cell membrane integrity leads to the leakage of intracellular DNA, which can be quantified by measuring the optical density (OD) at 260 nm (OD260) to evaluate the antibacterial effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal. Briefly, after each treatment, the bacterial suspensions were centrifuged, and the supernatants were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The OD260 value of each sample was then recorded using a UV-2450/2250 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu).

Intracellular GSH depletion evaluation

The intracellular GSH levels in P. aeruginosa were evaluated according to our previously reported method. Briefly, after each treatment, the culture medium was replaced with 200 μL of PBS containing 10 μM ThiolTrace Violet 500 (AAT Bioquest) and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. The bacterial suspensions were then washed and imaged using a confocal microscope.

DNA damage detection

The DNA damage effect of each material was investigated using a TUNEL staining kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, after each treatment, the bacterial suspensions were centrifuged, redispersed in 200 μL of PBS containing 10 μM TUNEL dye, and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 1 h. The TUNEL-stained bacteria were then washed three times and imaged using a confocal microscope.

CCK-8 assay

The mouse embryonic fibroblast cell line (NIH/3T3) was seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and incubated in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with RPMI 1640 medium containing the materials (mGNR@PEG-Mal, mGNR@AIPH, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal) at final concentrations of 0.04, 0.08, 0.16, 0.32, and 0.64 mg/mL. Following this, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Subsequently, 100 μL of RPMI 1640 medium containing 10 % CCK-8 was added to each well. The absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated according to the following formula:Cellviability(%)=(As−Ab)(Ac−Ab)×100%Where As is the absorbance value of cells with different treatments (Sample), Ab is the absorbance value of blank well (Blank), Ac is the absorbance value of cells without treatment (Control).

In vivo antibacterial and wound healing efficiency evaluation

Animals

Female Sprague-Dawley rats (average body weight 175 g) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All rats were housed in the SPF Laboratory Animal Center of Jinan University and maintained in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Jinan University (IACUC 20211123-06). After sterilization, a circular wound with a diameter of approximately 1.5 cm was created on the back of each rat using surgical scissors. Subsequently, 200 μL of P. aeruginosa suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was evenly applied to the wound. After 2 days, infection was confirmed using the bacterial plate count method, and the rats were randomly divided into seven groups (five rats per group) with different treatments, I: Control, II: mGNR@PEG-Mal, III: mGNR@AIPH, IV: mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, V: mGNR@PEG-Mal + NIR, VI: mGNR@AIPH + NIR and VII: mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR.

In vivo photothermal effect evaluation

Rats were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium before treatment. A 200 μL suspension of the material (160 μg/mL) was carefully dropped to the wounds and irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2) for 5 min. The temperature changes and thermal images of each rat were recorded using a near-infrared thermal imaging camera (Fotric-220s, China).

In vivo synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial effect evaluation

On day 0, 2, 4, 7, and 10, 50 μL of exudate was carefully collected from the wounds and diluted with normal saline. Subsequently, 100 μL of the diluted bacterial suspension was plated onto LB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Bacterial colonies on each agar plate were counted using ImageJ software and imaged with a digital camera to evaluate the synergistic antibacterial capability of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal.

Wound healing effect evaluation

Digital images of each rat's wound were recorded on day 0, 2, 4, 7, and 10 after treatment. The relative wound area was calculated using ImageJ software to evaluate the wound-healing process.

Histological analysis

On day 10, rats were euthanized, and wound tissue and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were collected and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde at 4 °C. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome staining were performed to assess the healing efficiency of each material. Histological sections of major organs were stained with H&E to evaluate the in vivo biocompatibility of each material. To evaluate the antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing effects of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, immunofluorescence staining of IL-6, α-SMA, CD31, CD86, and CD206, as well as immunohistochemistry staining of iNOS and TNF-α, were performed. Related images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica) and a digital slice scanning system (Easyscan, Motic).

Body weight evaluation

Following the treatments described above, the body weight of each rat was recorded on day 0, 2, 4, 7, and 10 to evaluate the safety and antibacterial effects of each treatment.

Blood biochemical index assay

On day 10, blood was collected from each rat group, and routine blood parameters were analyzed using an automatic blood cell analyzer (BC2800vet, Mindray). Parameters included neutrophils (NEUT), white blood cells (WBC), lymphocytes (LY), platelet distribution width (PDW), hematocrit (HCT), hemoglobin (HGB), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), monocytes (Mon), red blood cells (RBC), and mean platelet volume (MPV). Blood from healthy rats was used as a negative control.

Statistical analysis

All data in this work are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Significance levels were defined as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal nanogenerator

In the present work, a multifunctional nanogenerator mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal with specific P. aeruginosa targeting and NIR laser-controlled AR-releasing properties was synthesized for high-efficient treatment of P. aeruginosa-associated wound infection (Scheme 1). Firstly, mesoporous gold nanorods (mGNR) were fabricated using a modified seed-mediated synthetic method (Fig. 1A), which served as the carrier for effective AIPH loading, resulting in the preparation of mGNR@AIPH. As followed, cysteamine was functionalized on the surface of mGNR@AIPH and further conjugated with polyethylene glycol (PEG) polymer with terminal carboxyl and maleimide groups (HOOC-PEG5000-Mal) via the formation of amido bond, obtaining P. aeruginosa-targeting nanogenerator of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal (Scheme 1A). In the UV–Vis spectrum of mGNR (Fig. 1B), a broad absorbance peak at wavelength range of 600–900 nm was observed, suggesting that mGNR displayed strong localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) effect in the NIR region. The LSPR effect endowed the excellent photothermal effect to mGNR upon NIR laser irradiation. Similar LSPR peak at 600–900 nm was observed for mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal. In addition, a small peak at 364 nm, characteristic peak of AIPH, was also observed in the UV–Vis spectra of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal (Fig. 1B), suggesting successful loading of AIPH. Based on the AIPH standard curve (Fig. S1), the AIPH loading efficiency in the nanogenerator was determined to be 27.68 %. Zeta potential, hydrodynamic diameter (Dh) and FTIR measurements were performed to confirm the preparation of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal. Zeta potential measurement showed that AIPH loading caused increase of the Zeta potential from – 9 mV to – 5.43 mV because of the positive charge of AIPH (Fig. 1C). After HOOC-PEG5000-Mal functionalization, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal displayed Zeta potential of – 4.96 mV (Fig. 1C). Similarly, Dh of the nanogenerator increased from 388 nm to 396 nm after AIPH loading and finally increased to 453 nm after HOOC-PEG5000-Mal functionalization (Fig. 1D). In the FTIR spectrum of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, typical band of AIPH at 1362 cm−1 (bending vibration of -CH3) 1595 cm−1 (bending vibration of N-H) were observed (Fig. 1E), indicating the presence of AIPH [28]. Next, the photothermal effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal upon 808 nm laser irradiation was evaluated using infrared thermal imaging. Thermal images showed that temperature of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal increased rapidly in 5 min upon 808 nm laser irradiation (Fig. 1F), implying the excellent photothermal effect of the mGNR-based nanogenerator in the NIR region. Moreover, the photothermal effect of the nanogenerator was both laser power density and concentration-dependent (Fig. 1G). In particular, temperature of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal increased to 46.2 °C upon 5 min 1 W/cm2 laser irradiation at the material concentration of 160 μg/mL (Fig. 1H), suggesting the potential use of the nanogenerator in mild photothermal therapy (MPTT). In addition, we showed that mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal exhibited stable photothermal conversion efficiency of 35.8 %, as the photothermal effect of the nanogenerator did not compromise after 4 cycles of “heating-cooling” process (Fig. 1I and Fig. S2). Subsequently, the NIR laser-triggered alkyl radicals release property of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was investigated using 2, 2'-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) as the probe. In the presence of alkyl radicals, ABTS can be oxidized to from ABTS·+ radical, displaying green color and characteristic UV–Vis absorption peaks at the wavelength ranging from 500 nm to 900 nm (Fig. 1J). We showed that AIPH is a type of heat-responsive molecule that is able to release AR effectively when the temperature is higher than 37 °C (Fig. S3). Due to the mild photothermal effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, AR-controlled release could be envisaged upon NIR laser irradiation. As it was shown, the color of the solution turned to green and gradually darkened along with the irradiation time, and the absorbance value of the characteristic peaks at 500 nm–900 nm also increased with time (Fig. 1J and Fig. S4), demonstrating the NIR laser-controlled AR release property of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal. To further prove it, electron spin resonance (ESR) measurement was also conducted using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) as the trapping reagent. It clearly showed that typical DMPO/alkoxyl adduct peaks could only be detected in mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR treated group (Fig. 1K). Interestingly, we demonstrated that precise AR-controlled release could be realized by utilizing intermittent 808 nm laser irradiation (Fig. 1L), where the total AR release ratio reached at 82.4 % in 60 min intermittent irradiation.

The in vitro P. aeruginosa-targeting and antibacterial effect of the mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal nanogenerator

Encouraged by the excellent photothermal effect and NIR laser-controlled AR generation properties of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, the synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial efficiency of the nanogenerator against P. aeruginosa was investigated using the plate count method. It displayed that mGNR@PEG-Mal and mGNR@AIPH slightly inhibited the growth of P. aeruginosa, while the bacterial viability can be obviously inhibited by the mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal treatment (Fig. 2A and D). This is because mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal can effectively target and bind with P. aeruginosa, and also the nanogenerator can spontaneously release low levels of AR in 37 °C incubation condition, which together caused some extent inhibitory effect on P. aeruginosa. Obviously, mGNR@PEG-Mal and mGNR@AIPH reduced the colony number of P. aeruginosa upon NIR laser irradiation (Fig. 2A and D) due to the MPTT and AR antibacterial effect, respectively. Remarkably, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal treatment almost caused complete elimination of P. aeruginosa colony by exerting the synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial effect. To confirm the specific killing efficiency of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal against P. aeruginosa, the antibacterial effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal against E. coli and S. aureus were also evaluated. As it was shown, mGNR@PEG-Mal showed negligible antibacterial effect on both E. coli and S. aureus, and no differences in antibacterial efficiency between mGNR@AIPH and mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal treated groups were found (Fig. 2B, C, 2E and 2F). Upon NIR laser irradiation, AR generation was boosted in the mGNR@AIPH + NIR (VI) treated group, which significantly eradicated the preformed P. aeruginosa biofilms. Mild photothermal effect of the mGNR@PEG-Mal + NIR (V) treatment also caused obvious decrease in the P. aeruginosa biofilm activity. Remarkably, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR (VII) treatment displayed the most significant biofilm eradication effect by eradicating 95 % bacterial biofilm through the synergistic MPTT/AR antibiofilm effect (Fig. S8). Moreover, a concentration-dependent antibiofilm activity was found for mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal. The synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial mechanism of the mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal nanogenerator

Next, the synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial mechanism of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was investigated. Previous work indicated that photothermal effect of nanomaterials can cause bacterial membrane damage because of the elevated heat [30]. Also, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are able to break bacterial membrane via the strong lipid peroxidation effect [10,31]. On this scenario, the bacterial membrane damage effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was examined. Firstly, SYTO-9/PI dual fluorescent staining assay was performed because the red fluorescent PI can only stain bacteria with damaged membrane. CLSM images showed that some red fluorescence could be observed in mGNR@PEG-Mal + NIR (V) treated bacteria, indicating the moderate bacterial membrane-damage ability of the MPTT effect (Fig. 4A). Plenty red fluorescence could be visualized in the mGNR@AIPH + NIR (VI) treated bacteria, suggesting the strong membrane-breaking effect of AR (Fig. 4A). Notably, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR (VII) treatment resulted in the highest red/green fluorescent ratio (Fig. 4A and F), demonstrating the most severe membrane-damage effect of the combinational MPTT and AR treatments. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) measurement was also conducted to further confirm the membrane-damage effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal. In the control group, regular rod-like P. aeruginosa with smooth membrane could be clearly seen. However, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR (VII) treatment led to severe deformation of P. aeruginosa, which displayed shrunken shape, rough and broken surface (Fig. 4B). Bacterial damage was also confirmed by measuring the optical density at 260 nm (OD260) of the supernatant collected from the bacteria-materials complexes, reflecting the released intracellular components like DNA, RNA and proteins. It showed that mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR (VII) treatment resulted in the highest OD260 value among all the treated groups (Fig. S9), further conforming its strong bacterial membrane damage effect. Bacterial membrane damage can facilitate the penetration of nanomaterials into bacteria to strengthen the oxidative stress effect, resulting the intracellular ROS elevation [32]. Then, intracellular ROS generation level was detected using 2′, 7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) as the detecting probe. Obviously, MPTT (V) or AR (III, IV) treatment alone obviously increased the intracellular ROS levels, while combinational MPTT and AR treatment (VI and VII) triggered the highest intracellular ROS generation (Fig. 4C and G). To further identity the elevated ROS types, dihydroethidium (DHE), SOSG and BBoxiProbe®O26 were used for detecting intracellular O2ꞏ-, 1O2 and ꞏOH, respectively. Notably, the synergistic MPTT/AR effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR (VII) significantly enhanced the generation levels of intracellular O2ꞏ-, 1O2 and ꞏOH (Fig. S10). It is well known that intracellular ROS is regulated by the antioxidant GSH, ROS boost can cause the disbalance of the redox reactions by depleting the intracellular GSH level [33,34]. Then, intracellular GSH was detected using the ThiolTrace Violet 500 fluorescent probe. CLSM images showed that high levels of GSH could be visualized in bacteria of control group, while the intracellular GSH content decreased as the intracellular ROS level increased (Fig. 4D and H). Remarkably, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR (VII) treatment caused the complete depletion of intracellular GSH (Fig. 4D and H). Finally, the DNA damage effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was evaluated by performing the terminal-deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay. Taken together, a potential mechanism regarding to the synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was proposed (Scheme 1B). In vivo synergistic MPTT/AR therapeutic effect of the mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal nanogenerator on wound infection

Inspired by the excellent in vitro antibacterial and antibiofilm outcomes, the in vivo synergistic MPTT/alkyl radical therapeutic effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was investigated using the full-thickness skin wound model with P. aeruginosa infection (Fig. 5A). Firstly, the in vivo mild photothermal effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was evaluated using the NIR thermal imaging assay. As it was shown, slight increase in temperature was observed for the control group (PBS + NIR), while wound temperature in the mGNR@AIPH + NIR treated group increased to 42 °C after 5 min irradiation. Due to the P. aeruginosa-targeting ability of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal, temperature of wound with mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR treatment increased to 46 °C after 5 min irradiation (Fig. 5B and C), demonstrating the in vivo mild photothermal effect of the nanogenerator. Then, the synergistic MPTT/AR therapeutic effect on rats' wound infection was investigated by monitoring changes in bacterial colony number and wound area. In control group (I), rats' wound gradually closed due to the rat's inherent self-healing ability (Fig. 5D). However, there was still about 40 % wound unhealed on Day 10 (Fig. 5F), mainly because that severe bacterial infection hindered the normal wound healing process. Remarkably, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal + NIR (VII) treatment almost led to the complete wound healing on Day 10, thanks to the synergistic MPTT/AR therapeutic effect (Fig. 5D and F). Agar plate confirmed that the bacterial colony number in rats' wounds was obviously reduced by the individual MPTT (IV) or AR (V) treatment, while the synergistic MPTT/AR treatments (VI and VII) resulted in the least number of remanent P. aeruginosa (Fig. 5G and I and Figure S12). Biocompatibility evaluation

To ensure safe biomedical applications, both the in vitro and in vivo biocompatibility of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal were investigated. The in vitro cell compatibility of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal on the mouse embryo fibroblasts NIH/3T3 was examined using the CCK-8 assay. It showed that mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was high biocompatible to NIH/3T3, cell viability still maintained higher than 90 % even at material concentration of 640 μg/mL (Fig. S16). The in vivo biocompatibility of the nanogenerator was evaluated by performing the H&E-stained histological assay and blood biochemical index assay. H&E-stained histological images indicated that no apparent histologic lesions were found for rats' main organs of heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidneys after treatments, suggesting the excellent histocompatibility of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal (Fig. S17). Blood biochemical index detection results displayed that all the routine blood index maintained in the normal values as those of the healthy rats after treatments, indicating the excellent blood compatibility mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal (Fig. S18). Moreover, a slightly gradual increase in rats’ weight was observed during the whole treating period, further implying the non-toxic effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal am its related treatments (Fig. S19). In all, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal is a biocompatible material showing promising use for safe and high-efficient wound infection treatment.

Conclusions

In the present work, a multifunctional nanogenerator of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was rationally designed and fabricated. Due to the surface functionalization of maleimide groups, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal displayed highly specific targeting ability to P. aeruginosa by binding with the bacterial T4P. Under NIR laser irradiation, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal displayed synergistic MPTT/AR antibacterial effect, which effectively inhibited P. aeruginosa, as well as eradicated P. aeruginosa biofilms. Moreover, mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was demonstrated to be effective in treating P. aeruginosa wound infections due to its excellent antibacterial, anti-inflammation and wound healing activities. As most recent studies have primarily focused on the anticancer applications of AR, our work extends its application to the treatment of bacterial infections. Moreover, our work provides a promising way for target delivery of AR to P. aeruginosa-associated infections. Since T4P is also presented in many other types of Gram-negative bacteria like Caulobacter crescentus, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Vibrio cholerae [38], in addition to P. aeruginosa, suggesting that mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal can be used for the precise treatment of various bacterial infections. Last but not least, our work investigated and elucidated the antibacterial infection mechanism of the synergistic MPTT/AR effect. It revealed that the synergistic MPTT/AR therapeutic effect of mGNR@AIPH@PEG-Mal was capable of reversing the severe inflammatory environment of the wound to the anti-inflammatory environment by repolarizing M2-type macrophages repolarization, which subsequently facilitated angiogenesis by upregulating CD 31 and α-SMA expressions and finally promoted wound healing.
