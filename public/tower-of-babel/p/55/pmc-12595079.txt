A data repository of feedyard particulate matter emissions (PM10) in West Texas, US
Authors: Guillermo S Marcillo, Brent Auvermann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA network for continuously monitoring episodic air particulate matter (PM) concentration from smaller-scale abatement and emissions mechanism experiments was implemented at a research cattle feedyard, operated by Texas A&M AgriLife Research, in Amarillo, TX. The data was generated through Tapered Element Oscillating Microbalance (TEOM) monitors– a recognized technology for the direct measurement of particulate matter concentrations. The data files span 10-years of size-resolved particulate matter concentration data (PM10), both upwind and downwind, collected in the period 2011–2019, and complemented by relevant ancillary information, such as on-station meteorological data (including 10- and 2-meter wind speed, wind direction, air temperature, relative humidity, and precipitation), feedyard operational parameters, and regional air quality indicators. The processed and outlier-free datasets are available at hourly and daily scales at a central repository in Zenodo. Researchers, educators, and policymakers can reuse these datasets to implement mechanistic and machine learning models of airborne dust dispersion and discuss air pollution mitigation strategies in the livestock industry.Keywords: Air quality, Air dispersion modeling, Tapered element oscillating microbalance, Time Series Analysis

Background

Particulate matter (PM10) -inhalable aerodynamical particles of 10 um or smaller – emitted from beef cattle feedyards can exert significant impacts on regional air quality and affect nearby communities and the environment [1,2]. A mix of turbulent and stability cycles define the diurnal-nocturnal periodicity of PM10 concentrations, with direct implications for air quality around feedyard operations and their surroundings. In the Texas High-Plains, a region characterized by intense wind activity and high incoming solar radiation, atmospheric turbulence throughout the day promotes the vertical mixing of air, diluting ground-level concentrations, and dispersing PM10 into cleaner air aloft [[3], [4], [5]]. Conversely, reduced thermal convection around sunset leads to more stable conditions, confining elevated PM10 concentrations to a shallow boundary layer. The presence of drier manure and increased dust-generating animal activity often ends in the well-documented “evening dust peak,” which can pose significant challenges for air quality management.

Effective strategies for monitoring, collecting, and analysing PM10 concentration data are crucial for the cattle industry, a vigorous sector in the United States' agroeconomic ecosystem. Further, high quality resolution PM10 data is needed to advance the understanding of the physical drivers of dust emissions and their periodicity. The data repository presented herein addresses these needs by capturing representative information of a typical feedyard operation in the Texas High Plains, an area characterized by abrupt meteorological changes. Individual research campaigns in this area have previously collected these data for specific goals, such as transmissometry, antimicrobial resistance, and LiDAR evaluations. In this paper we compiled, processed, and curated these datasets with the goal of supporting future empirical and mechanistic characterizations of fugitive dust dynamics. Ultimately, these applications can inform researchers, policymakers, and cattle operators to discuss enhanced targeted strategies for mitigation.

Data Description

The repository consists of high-quality PM10 concentration and meteorological time series data. The timestamps across the files follow a consistent machine-readable format, e.g., YYYY-MM-DD for daily data, or YYYY-MM-DD HH:MM:SS for higher resolutions. The column names for downwind PM10 concentrations vary by year within the dataset. Thus, each yearly file will contain one or more columns for downwind PM10, typically named in a similar fashion, with an ending integer depending on the number of active downwind stations for that specific year, for example: “Mass Conc Downwind 1”, “Mass Conc Downwind 2”, etc. This is due to changes in the number and configuration of downwind monitoring stations at feedyard during the study period.

The time-series data exhibit consistent temporal properties (Fig. 1, Table 2). High temporal correlation, i.e., r-lag close to 1, was somewhat prevalent among the meteorological and upwind concentration data across all the years in the database. This indicates persistence of temporal dependence in these environmental variables, where the value at a given time point is positively correlated with its immediate predecessor. The strength of this persistence varies, being very strong for temperature and relative humidity, moderate for wind speeds and PM10, and weaker for wind direction and rainfall, reflecting the prevalent atmospheric dynamics in the Texas High Plains [4,6,7]. The meteorological and upwind concentration data also displayed constant statistical properties over time (tauADF: p < 0.00). It can be noted, however, that downwind PM10 concentration was less stationary in some years (e.g., 2013, 2014, 2018). Despite some interannual variation, the datasets consist of stationary series overall, making them well suited for long term forecasting analyses. Finally, the concentration and meteorological variables undergone homogenization and outlier correction, and removal/interpolation of missing values and duplicates. The data was resampled to the median point-estimate calculated at hourly or daily scales, which are included as the default metric in the database files.

Data inspection

On-site meteorological conditions. Predominant wind direction from the south or south-to-southwest was evident across years, suggesting a relatively consistent airflow pattern in the area (Fig. 2). Within each year, however, there was significant variability in wind direction and wind speed. The North and Northwesterly wind conditions appeared more turbulent during the winter period (December, January, February) compared to other times of the year. Inter-annual wind variation was evidenced as well. The year 2011, for example, is widely acknowledged for record-breaking seasonal warmth conditions, and for displaying stronger winds from the west-southwest. These meteorological variations could be attributed to larger-scale climate patterns or year-specific weather anomalies. Thus, predominant southerly winds, combined with observed inter-annual seasonal variations are typical of the Texas High-Plains and need to be accounted for in future modeling applications of these datasets.

Wind speed and wind direction distributions for the research feedyard aggregated by year of analysis (2011–2019 period).

Median Net PM10 concentration patterns for this dataset follow expected conditions in the area (Fig. 3). A gradual increase linked to diurnal wind speed variation is evidenced during the morning hours (from 6:00 to 10:00), which contributes to greater dust mobilization from the feedyard throughout the day. The peak in net PM10 concentration occurs during the afternoon hours (between 12:00 and 18:00) correlating with highly unstable periods of wind speed intensity (Fig. 1), further supporting the link between wind and dust emissions. Net PM10 concentrations begin to decline in the evening (from 18:00 onwards). This decrease corresponds to the typical drop in wind speeds observed during the late afternoon and evening, leading to reduced dust suspension [4,6,7]. The lowest net PM10 concentrations are observed overnight (from 0:00 to 6:00), reflecting the calmest wind conditions during this period. The database shows the significant role of wind patterns characterizing the Southern High-Plains on daily variation of PM10 concentration. Users of the database can pursue additional exploration of the link between fugitive dust concentration and atmospheric destabilizing variables such as air temperature, relative humidity, and precipitation. The sensitivity of dust concentration to wind quality parameters underscores the need for robust quality control procedures in air quality monitoring and modeling efforts.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Timestamps were the times at the end of the period when averages of PM10 and meteorological variables were calculated, and the records written. For example, a 1-minute record, e.g. 2020–1–6 01:54:00, represents a variable recorded between 2020 and 1–6 01:53:00 and 2020–1–6 01:54:00. Unless noted otherwise, time was recorded in Central Standard Time (CST), and it did not change during summertime or daylight-saving time.

PM10 mass concentrations were recorded at 5-min intervals at two sampling locations in the research feedyard using tapered element oscillating microbalance (TEOM) monitors (Series 1400a; Thermo Fisher Scientific, East Greenbush, NY). The PM10 size-selective inlet was positioned 2.0 m above the ground. Operational tasks for TEOM maintenance, such as filter heating, leak checks, inlet cleaning, pump checking, sampling flow rate audits, software calibration, and in-line filter replacement below a loading threshold, were implemented following manufacturer guidelines and procedures from similar PM studies in Kansas [8] and other areas [3,9]. Our upwind/downwind monitoring approach placed a single, upwind monitoring station on the S side of the project feedyard-C, in an area that received little to no vehicle traffic on the feedyard’s unpaved roads. The six downwind monitors were equally spaced at 440 ft. intervals along an E-W line within a narrow, 9-acre strip of native rangeland immediately north of the feedyard’s northern fenceline. Our monitoring network was setup to control for the typical strong prevailing winds in the Texas Panhandle during the summer dust season, blowing from SW to SSE, with a secondary lobe out of the N—NNE (typically a winter phenomenon).

Air temperature, recorded in degrees Celsius [ °C], and relative humidity in percentage [ %], were measured at 1.5 m above ground by a Vaisala HMP-45 joined sensor, housed in a naturally aspirated radiation shield. For each minute, air temperature represents the overall measure, while relative humidity was calculated by averaging vapor pressure and saturated vapor pressure over that period. Horizontal wind speed and wind direction at 10 m were measured by an RM Young 3D Ultrasonic Anemometer Model 81,000 V, recording sensor output at 10 Hz (i.e., 10 readings per second). The reported wind speed, in meters per second [m/s], is the mean wind velocity over the previous 1-minute, and wind direction, in degrees from North, is the unit vector mean over the same period. Wind speed at 2 m was measured using a Gill WindSonic 2D Sonic Anemometer, with sensor output recorded at 1 Hz, providing the mean wind velocity in meters per second [m/s] over the preceding 1-minute. Precipitation, in mm of rain, was measured by a Hydrological Services Model TB3 Tipping Bucket Rain Gauge, mounted at 1.8 m above ground, with a resolution of 0.254 mm [0.01 in.].

Data Processing. Three main steps were followed for the synthesis of mesoscale weather data and continuous, upwind, and downwind, PM10 concentration data collected at the research feedyard since 2010. First, flat disc text files (.txt) recovered from the TEOM field instrumentation and weather mesoscale field stations were catalogued to ensure consistent timestamps and measurement frequencies across periods of measurement. Next, time-series of PM10 concentration and meteorological variables were homogenized and outlier-corrected through standardization. For the time series Xt:tɛT, the 7-day rolling average and standard deviation for a given observation (xt) were calculated over the preceding 7-day window: (X¯t−i,σt−i):0≤i<7. Outlier correction involved bounding observations within 3 standard deviations (Eq. (1)). Any observation (xt) outside this range was assigned the respective boundary limit.

Further, missing values and duplicates were filled through polynomial interpolation, i.e., finding the missing point between the linear path that connects the two nearest valid known points (xi,xj) [10,11]. Next, the resulting time series were resampled, i.e., aggregated, to the median point-estimate (Mt) calculated at hourly or daily scales (Fig. 3). Hourly and daily median observations are included as the default metric of concentration and meteorological measurements in the files available in the database (Fig. 1). A modular ad-hoc Python processing library is available upon request, and includes the original scripts used in this paper. Users of the database can use these scripts and modify calculate other statistics of interest (e.g., mean, n-quartile moments, truncated median, etc.) to perform data aggregation at finer or coarser scales.

Finally, for purposes of quality checking and verification, a factorial experiment (A x B) was designed to investigate how daily net PM10 concentrations are affected by wind parameter selectivity. This approach was simple yet pragmatic enough considering that the time series involved are stationary and constantly homogeneous throughout. While autocorrelation is often a concern in time series data (Durbin-Watson, DW[p] < 0.00), the main goal here was to detect the sensitivity of PM10 sample selection in response to varying atmospheric conditions. Therefore, no further advanced techniques were pursued (e.g., mixed-models, ARIMA, Transfer Function Noise Modeling). As such, wind direction, calculated as the daily average of 24-hour lagged-differences, varied within a tolerance parameter (A), capturing instantaneous direction changes that indicate the pass of strong frontal passages. Wind direction deviation from the North, aggregated hourly and averaged over 24-hours, varied within a tolerance parameter (B), reinforcing the possibility of strong perturbations in the data due to high wind fluctuation. Additional details about the experiment are included as supplementary material for the interested reader. Statistical significance was not observed for the two-way (A x B) interaction, for which a sensitivity analysis of PM10 was conducted to detect changes in PM10 estimation across a range of wind variation from the North (i.e., parameter B). This analysis found that maximizing the number of daily observations that lead to accurately estimating the median PM10 occurred within 30°-departures from the 180-North (Fig. 4). Accordingly, the data displays median net PM10 estimates within a robust sample size and under a range of wind conditions representative of this geographical area.

Median 24-hour Net PM10 concentration estimation as a function of wind selection criteria (2011–2019 period). The median net concentration was calculated at every 10-degree deviation from the North (e.g., 40° indicates a range of 180 ± 40°). Error bars calculated within 3 standard deviations from the median point estimates.

Wind direction selectivity significantly influences the estimation of PM10 net concentrations [4,12]. As the wind selection criterion is relaxed, there is a wide range of wind conditions represented in the sample (Fig. 4). This is evident in the range 10 to 30 degrees from the north. However, as the criterion is further relaxed, encompassing days with strong wind frontal passages (i.e., deviations greater than 30 degrees), the uncertainty decreases but so do the PM10 concentration estimates. Statistically, this brings important implications for feedyard fugitive emission studies. Often, data selectivity is restricted to empirical assessments or “expert” opinion. Here it is shown that moderate increases in wind parameter selectivity may lead to larger samples and potentially more robust statistical estimates of PM10 net concentrations. However, relaxing deliberately wind quality criteria for data inclusion must consider the potential for bias introduction. The observed decrease in PM10 estimates might be underestimating the true impact of the feedyard on downwind PM10 concentrations. This is because winds from other directions might transport PM10 away from the monitoring stations, leading to lower measured values. Increasing the number of days for analysis is useful for visualizing median and variance of hourly net PM10 concentrations, aiding in understanding the temporal dynamics of fugitive emissions, and uncovering potential links to feedyard operations or meteorological factors to be explored through mechanistic principles.

Limitations

•The datasets may contain missing cases, as indicated by the presence of “.” in the data, especially for particulate matter deposition measured downwind. However, different monitoring stations were available at different times to compensate for this loss of data.
