Low-grade inflammation score (INFLA- score) associated with metabolic syndrome and its components in shift workers
Authors: Suxin Jiang, Jueqi Wang, Xue Guan, Hengqing An, Ning Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the relationship between the low-grade inflammation score (INFLA-score) and metabolic syndrome (MetS) in shift workers working in the oilfield operations in Karamay City, Xinjiang, and to provide data support for the effective prevention of MetS through the reduction of systemic inflammatory responses.MethodsINFLA-score was calculated based on C-reactive protein, leukocyte count, platelet count, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) in 1,758 shift workers working at the oilfield base in Karamay City, Xinjiang, China, in the year 2023, as the study population. A logistic regression analysis model combined with restricted cubic spline was used to analyze the relationship between INFLA-score and MetS and its components in shift workers.ResultsA total of 1758 shift workers were included, of whom 785 were diagnosed with MetS. Multifactorial logistic regression analysis showed that those with higher INFLA score had a higher likelihood of developing MetS (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.07–1.10). In the Q1-Q4 INFLA quartile group, the risk of developing MetS was 3.58 times higher in the Q4 group than in the Q1 group. After adjusting for all confounders, INFLA-score was found to be positively associated with elevated blood glucose, blood pressure, waist circumference, triglyceride levels, and lower HDL (p < 0.001). Restricted cubic spline results showed a dose-response relationship between INFLA-score and MetS and its components. In subgroup analyses and interaction tests, women had a higher risk of MetS than men (men: OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.05–1.09, p < 0.001; women: OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.09–1.16, p < 0.001), and the interaction showed that there was an effect of the association between sex and INFLA score on the occurrence of MetS (interaction p < 0.05).ConclusionsElevated INFLA-score was associated with MetS among shift workers, and the INFLA-score was a simple, cost-effective test for MetS. Therefore, MetS can be combated by controlling the levels of inflammatory factors in shift workers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01850-1.

Introduction

Metabolic Syndrome (MetS), a cluster of metabolic disorders characterized by obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia, has reached a global prevalence of 25%, making it a major challenge to public health. There is growing evidence that MetS may be strongly associated with an increased risk of coronary heart disease (CHD), cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1], type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2 DM), stroke and all-cause mortality [2]. How to recognize and intervene in MetS at an early stage has become the focus of current research in the medical field.

The pathogenesis of MetS is complex and is affected by a combination of several factors, including insulin resistance [3], obesity, chronic inflammatory response, genetic factors, and environmental factors [4]. Of these, chronic low-grade inflammation is a key driver [5]: Obesity, especially neutrophilic obesity, leads to adipocyte hyperplasia, hypertrophy, and the secretion of numerous pro-inflammatory or inflammatory factors, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) [6], and so on. These inflammatory factors disrupt insulin signaling in peripheral tissues through autocrine and paracrine pathways, leading to metabolic disorders and insulin resistance (IR) [7], which promotes the development of MetS. Therefore, control of inflammation may be a key intervention to mitigate metabolic disorders. However, studies on the relationship between inflammation and metabolic disorders remain limited. In order to explore this potential association in depth on all fronts, there is an urgent need for an objective evaluation index that can accurately reflect the inflammatory status of the MetS population.

The low-grade inflammation score (INFLA-score) is a composite index based on C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood count (WBC), platelet count, and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) that shows promise in assessing inflammatory response [8]. The INFLA-score delivers a thorough evaluation of inflammatory markers by combining multiple metrics, resulting in a more precise measurement of the body’s overall inflammatory response compared to individual markers. Multiple studies have shown the effectiveness of the INFLA-score across different populations. For instance, Crotti et al. found that each one-unit increase in the INFLA-score correlated with a 1% rise in total mortality risk and that a higher INFLA-score elevated the mortality risk in obese older adults [9]. Similarly, a NHANES based study found a significant positive correlation between high INFLA-score groups and diabetes compared to low INFLA-score groups [10]. In a cohort study of middle-aged and older adults, higher INFLA-score were found to be associated with a higher risk of cardiometabolic disease and earlier onset, and for INFLA-score ≥ 9, the risk increased by 5.9% for each additional point [11]. These studies have shown that INFLA-score are strongly associated with risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension and cardiovascular disease. Despite these advances, there has been little research on the relationship between INFLA-score and MetS. Therefore, our study recruited and screened this easy-to-calculate and readily available indicator of low-grade chronic inflammation to assess its role in MetS.

The relationship between occupational factors and MetS has received increasing attention in recent years, with shift work becoming the norm in many industries around the world as job opportunities increase and industries flourish. Shift work as an occupationally harmful factor that disrupts circadian rhythms and promotes metabolic dysfunction. Recent studies have shown that circadian disruption is strongly associated with obesity [12], diabetes [13], CVD [14], and metabolic syndrome [15]. Xinjiang is located in the northwestern border of China and has rich oil reserves. Oil workers, as a unique occupational group, are required to work in shifts, and some previous study by this group found that the risk of hypertension among oil workers in Xinjiang was significantly increased [16, 17]. In addition, a cross-sectional cohort study from the CONSTANCES cohort also showed that male shift night workers exhibited an excess risk of obesity/cardioid obesity and hypertriglyceridemia five years after exposure [18]. Both obesity and hyperlipidemia, metabolic disorders, are all key features of MetS. However, INFLA-score is relatively new in the field of shift workers, with many studies still in the exploratory phase, and its association with the risk of developing MetS has not been established. Therefore, investigating the association between chronic inflammation and MetS in shift workers is crucial for the effective prevention and management of MetS in occupational populations.

In summary, the objective of this study is to explore the relationship between INFLA-score and metabolic syndrome and its components among shift workers, to identify people at high risk of MetS through INFLA-score, and to detect patients with MetS at an early stage.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study was a cross-sectional survey that collected data on medical examinations of shift workers in the Karamay oil field operations in Xinjiang for 2023, enrolling 1849 shift workers > 20 years of age and collecting their general conditions. After excluding self-reported receiving treatment for arterial hypertension, obesity, type 2 diabetes, individuals with incomplete data for exclusion of metabolic syndrome, individuals with incomplete data for inflammatory markers (CRP, leukocytes, lymphocytes, platelets, neutrophils) and outliers (< Q1-3 IQR or > Q3 + 3 IQR) [19], the final number of study participants included was 1758 (Fig. 1).

Definition of metabolic syndrome

We defined Mets using the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III, 2010) criteria [20], and Mets was defined as the presence of three or more of the following components: (1) abdominal obesity (waist circumference ≥ 80 cm in women and ≥ 90 cm in men in Asian populations); (2) hypertension (SBP ≥ 130 mmHg or DBP ≥ 85 mmHg or current use of antihypertensive medications); (3) impaired fasting glucose: (FPG ≥ 5.6 mmol/L); (4) hypertriglyceridemia (≥ 1.7 mmol/L or history of triglyceride-lowering medication history); and (5) low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (< 1.03 mmol/L in men and < 1.29 mmol/L in women). Fasting blood samples were collected in the morning after a 9-hour fast, and the physician recorded blood pressure three times to determine the mean value.

Definition of INFLA-score

A complete blood count was measured using an automated hematology analyzer (Coulter DxH 800 analyzer). The INFLA-score consists of four components: CRP, WBC, platelet count, and NLR, which is considered to be a composite indicator of low-grade inflammation in the body. To calculate the INFLA-score, all components in the highest decile (7 to 10) were assigned a value of + 1 to + 4, while biomarker levels in the lowest decile (1 to 4) were assigned a value of − 4 to − 1. INFLA-score ranged from − 16 to + 16, with higher scores indicating higher levels of low-grade chronic inflammation (6).

Covariate

The baseline survey provided self-reported data on various parameters. Based on previous studies, we included covariates for adverse cardiometabolic health risk factors, including age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, and family history of diabetes. Smoking status was categorized as individuals who were current smokers and nonsmokers, and alcohol consumption was defined as individuals who were current drinkers and nondrinkers.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and processed using SPSS (IBM Corp. Released 2017. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0. Armonk, NY) [21] and R software (version 4.4.1) [22], with a significance level of p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Measures that obeyed normal distribution were described as mean and standard error (SE), and those that did not were expressed as [M(P25,P75)], and comparisons between the two groups were made using t-tests or nonparametric tests. Count data were expressed as proportions and data were compared using the χ² test. INFLA-score was grouped by quartiles, and multifactorial logistic regression was used to examine the association of INFLA-score with MetS and its components in 3 different models. No covariate adjustments were made in Model 1; adjustments were made for gender and age in Model 2; and adjustments were made for age, gender, smoking, and drinking status in Model 3. The association between INFLA-score and MetS was analyzed in subgroups using stratified factors: including sex (male/female), age (< 41 years, 41–49, or > 49 years), smoking status (current smoker, current nonsmoker), and drinking status (current drinker, current non-drinker). An interaction term was also introduced to test the heterogeneity of associations between subgroups. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was applied to analyze the nonlinear relationship between INFLA-score and Mets and its components. In the sensitivity analysis, we considered four other metabolic syndrome criteria with different definitions to assess the stability of the results.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

Of the 1758 participants, 1207 were male and 551 were female. A total of 700 individuals were identified as smokers, while 725 reported alcohol consumption. Additionally, 785 were diagnosed with Mets. In both non-MetS and MetS groups, statistically significant differences were found comparing different age, gender, BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), FPG, TC, TG, HDL, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking, drinking status, and INFLA-score, all with p < 0.001 (Table 1).

Subgroup analysis

In all subgroups, the OR of the INFLA-score was significantly greater than 1 (OR > 1, p < 0.001), indicating that the INFLA-score was positively associated with the risk of developing MetS (Fig. 2). The results showed that the risk of MetS was higher in women than in men (Female: OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.09–1.16, p < 0.001; Male: OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.05–1.09, p < 0.001), and that those with advanced age had a higher risk of developing MetS. (< 41 years: OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.03–1.11, p < 0.01; >49 years: OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.06–1.13, p < 0.001).

The interaction showed no significant difference in the correlation of age, smoking, and drinking status with INFLA-score and MetS (all interaction p > 0.05). In contrast, gender influenced the positive correlation between INFLA-score and MetS (interaction p < 0.05).

Discussion

With rapid social and economic development, along with changes in lifestyle, chronic metabolic diseases, particularly MetS, are emerging as the leading threat to human health. A growing number of epidemiologic studies have shown that MetS is strongly associated with systemic inflammation and immune response [27]. In our study, general demographic data and immune-inflammatory markers were collected from oilfield shift workers to investigate the relationship between INFLA scores and MetS. The analysis revealed a positive correlation between INFLA scores and the risk of developing MetS. This relationship remained significant after adjusting for potential confounding variables, with consistent findings across both continuous and categorical analyses. These results lay the groundwork for further clinical and basic research into the underlying mechanisms of this association.

Chronic low-grade inflammation triggers insulin resistance, which is thought to be a key mechanism linking all components of the MetS [28]. Elevated levels of free fatty acids and glucose can stimulate the production of inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, arachidonic acid derivatives, and leukotrienes [29]. These mediators promote neutrophil migration to inflamed tissues and activate inflammatory pathways [30]. In metabolic disease, neutrophils exhibit enhanced survival capabilities and undergo pathological accumulation in inflamed tissues, resulting in sustained cytokine secretion that exacerbates insulin signaling impairment [30, 31]. Satoshi et al. have shown that CD 8+ T cells activate macrophages in adipose tissue [32], leading to changes in the immune microenvironment and ultimately to a shift from an anti-inflammatory to a pro-inflammatory state in adipose tissue [33]. CRP binds to Fc receptors, and the interaction between the two drives the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby enhancing the inflammatory response [34]. In the context of MetS and T2 DM, platelets are usually in a highly activated state, where they contribute to the development of inflammation by releasing small chemokines and cytokines [35], as well as by promoting the adhesion of immune cells and by participating in the process of neutrophil extracellular trap formation [36].

Given the complexity of the inflammatory pathways described above, it is difficult to comprehensively assess the systemic inflammatory load with traditional single biomarkers (e.g., CRP). The INFLA-score introduced in this study, by integrating four circulating biomarkers, may provide a multidimensional and comprehensive indicator for quantifying metabolic inflammation. In recent years, more and more studies have demonstrated that this score is closely related to diabetes, obesity [10], and cardiometabolic diseases [11]. For example, Liu et al. [37] used the INFLA-score as a novel inflammatory marker to assess the risk of cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs) in obese individuals. Their study demonstrated a significant positive correlation between the INFLA-score and the risk of CMD, emphasizing the score’s ability to capture complex immune inflammation associated with insulin resistance and dyslipidemia. Considering that CMD shares a common metabolic pathway with MetS [38], we further explored the relationship between INFLA-score and MetS.

The main finding of our study was a significant positive association between INFLA-score and abdominal obesity and dyslipidemia and hypertension in the shift population, and consistent results were observed in both continuous and categorical analyses. Animal studies have shown that macrophages in mouse adipose tissue undergo significant changes in a high-fat diet-induced obese mouse model, where macrophages that were originally in an M2-polarized state, a state in which they protect adipocytes from inflammatory attack, are transformed into an M1 pro-inflammatory state [39]. These macrophages, with altered phenotypes, released significant levels of pro-inflammatory adipokines, further intensifying systemic inflammation. High triglyceride levels have been found to be associated with a higher systemic inflammatory state and increased particle concentrations in proteomic studies [40]. HDL has the potential to exert anti-inflammatory effects by regulating cholesterol transport and activating T lymphocytes [41], and its reduced levels lead to lipid accumulation in the blood, which in turn induces a severe inflammatory response [42]. These findings are consistent with our observations. Additionally, a study on the UK Biobank suggests that poor sleep and shift work are associated with elevated blood pressure and inflammation [43], and we generally agree that oxidative stress and vascular endothelial dysfunction are the main causes of elevated blood pressure in states of systemic inflammation [44]. Importantly, this hypertension may trigger a cardio-renal vicious cycle: pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β) directly impair renal autoregulation [45], promoting glomerular hypertension and albuminuria-early hallmarks of Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome [46]. By quantifying this type of inflammation, the INFLA-score can help identify shift workers at risk for metabolic and subclinical renal deterioration. Of note, there was no significant correlation between INFLA scores at quartiles and fasting glucose. Although some studies have shown that hyperglycemia exacerbates the inflammatory response [47], we hypothesized that changes in poor lifestyle habits due to a pre-diagnosis of hyperglycemia may have an impact on the currently observed results.

Subgroup analyses and interactions tests showed a significant difference in gender between INFLA-score and MetS, with women being at higher risk of developing MetS. A meta-analysis revealed that reduced levels of sex hormone binding globulin, in tandem with elevated levels of estradiol, significantly augment the likelihood of diabetes development among women [48]. There is substantial evidence that hypertension and obesity are also closely related to changes in sex hormone levels [49, 50]. It has been demonstrated that estrogen exacerbates the inflammatory response in individuals [51], which provides a plausible explanation for the stronger correlation observed between the INFLA - score and metabolic syndrome (MetS) in the female population. Our findings also indicated a more robust positive correlation between INFLA score and MetS in non-smokers. This is because in smoking groups, the immune system tends to be in a disturbed state with overactive levels of inflammation [52]. This abnormality somewhat interferes with the results of subgroup analyses, obscuring the true impact of elevated INFLA levels on the incidence of MetS. In addition, the results of the subgroup analyses revealed an age difference in the relationship between INFLA and MetS. A report has indicated that in the presence of external infections, the extent of immune activation differs among age groups. Specifically, advancing age is generally associated with a diminished immune response. This phenomenon is likely attributable to a decline in the functional capacity of immune cells and a reduction in the number of pattern recognition receptors within the aging population [53].

This study investigates the relationship between INFLA-score and MetS in a sample of shift workers, filling a gap in the field for this occupational population. Compared to considering a single or dual inflammatory cell type, the INFLA score reflects four circulating biomarkers, providing a more effective approach to interpreting the complex inflammatory mechanisms associated with MetS. We analyzed the association between INFLA and each component of MetS, which reflects our rigor. In addition, we adjusted the INFLA-score to account for continuous and categorical variables, improving the reliability of our results. By routinely testing cell counts in the blood of shift workers, INFLA-score support individualized prevention strategies [37]: individuals with high scores receive active lifestyle interventions and monitoring, while individuals with low scores undergo health education outreach.

However, there are some limitations to this study. Firstly, we prioritize the exclusion of participants with incomplete data, which may introduce selection bias. Secondly, as a cross-sectional study, our research cannot establish a causal or temporal relationship between INFLA-score and MetS, necessitating further investigation through cohort studies. Thirdly, the study did not consider preexisting comorbidities (e.g., autoimmune diseases, chronic infections, cardiovascular disease, or nonalcoholic fatty liver disease) that may independently affect systemic inflammatory or metabolic parameters, and these conditions may exaggerate or mask the observed relationship between INFLA-score and MetS. Fourthly, although we adjusted for a number of potential confounders, unmeasured confounders, such as dietary patterns, physical activity levels, and shift work hours, may still have an impact on the results. Fifthly, smoking and drinking were recorded as dichotomous variables rather than multichotomous (e.g., pack-years of smoking; frequency/intensity of drinking), which limited dose-response analyses. Sixthly, occupational factors such as detailed absenteeism/attendance were not assessed due to work constraints, potentially missing work-related stress effects. Future studies should incorporate these variables to enhance the robustness of the findings. Finally, the study population was shift workers operating in oil fields in Karamay, Xinjiang, which may limit the generalization of the findings to other shift populations or regions. Therefore, we should be cautious in drawing conclusions.

Despite some limitations and shortcomings, our study remains clinically relevant. As a novel biomarker of inflammation, the INFLA-score assesses immune and inflammatory responses in a more comprehensive manner [54], and may serve as an important basis for the diagnosis of MetS. Especially in modern society, where MetS tends to affect a large portion of the occupational population, the INFLA-score, which includes four simple and effective hematological indicators, can be used as a direct and effective indicator for primary care physicians to evaluate MetS. In the future, we will follow up with shift workers to explore the potential of the INFLA-score as an independent predictor of MetS. By routinely testing shift workers’ blood cell counts, our goal is to provide guidance, prevention, and protection for shift workers before the onset of metabolic syndrome.

Conclusion

In summary, Our findings suggest a strong association between higher INFLA-score and the presence of MetS, particularly in relation to abdominal obesity, hypertension and dyslipidemia. Our data suggest that the INFLA-score shows promise as a straightforward and cost-effective method to identify metabolic syndrome in shift workers. However, further validation of our findings requires larger cohort studies.
