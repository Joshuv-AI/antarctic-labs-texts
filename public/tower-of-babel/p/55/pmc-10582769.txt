Anti-InlA single-domain antibodies that inhibit the cell invasion of Listeria monocytogenes
Authors: Taichi Yamazaki, Satoru Nagatoishi, Tsukushi Yamawaki, Takashi Nozawa, Ryo Matsunaga, Makoto Nakakido, Jose MM Caaveiro, Ichiro Nakagawa, Kouhei Tsumoto
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractListeriosis, caused by infection with Listeria monocytogenes, is a severe disease with a high mortality rate. The L. monocytogenes virulence factor, internalin family protein InlA, which binds to the host receptor E-cadherin, is necessary to invade host cells. Here, we isolated two single-domain antibodies (VHHs) that bind to InlA with picomolar affinities from an alpaca immune library using the phage display method. These InlA-specific VHHs inhibited the binding of InlA to the extracellular domains of E-cadherin in vitro as shown by biophysical interaction analysis. Furthermore, we determined that the VHHs inhibited the invasion of L. monocytogenes into host cells in culture. High-resolution X-ray structure analyses of the complexes of VHHs with InlA revealed that the VHHs bind to the same binding site as E-cadherin against InlA. We conclude that these VHHs have the potential for use as drugs to treat listeriosis.Keywords: Listeria monocytogenes, InlA, listeriosis, single-domain VHH antibody, surface plasmon resonance (SPR), isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), protein-protein interaction

Listeriosis is a severe foodborne disease caused by the gram-positive bacterium Listeria monocytogenes that has a high mortality rate of 20 to 30% (1). L. monocytogenes can reproduce in various environments, including soil and food, and resist low temperatures and high salt concentrations (2). L. monocytogenes can be transmitted by ingestion of contaminated food. L. monocytogenes can cross the intestinal, blood–brain, and fetoplacental barriers (3). When the bacteria cross the blood–brain barrier, it causes infection of the central nervous system and potentially results in fatal meningitis (4). T cell suppression makes pregnant women susceptible to L. monocytogenes infection (5), and infection can result in the arrest of fetal development (6). Although infection and invasion mechanisms of L. monocytogenes have been well studied (7, 8), no treatment has been developed to prevent infection by this bacterium.

Two proteins, InlA and InlB, which are members of the internalin family of virulence factors, function in the first step in invading L. monocytogenes into host cells (9, 10). The binding of InlA and InlB to host cell receptors activates a signaling cascade that triggers receptor-mediated endocytosis and bacterial internalization. Both InlA and InlB contain leucine-rich repeats (LRRs). Downstream of the LRRs, both proteins have Ig-like domains, which contain an LPxTG motif necessary for binding to the host cell (11). InlA binds to E-cadherin, a receptor expressed on epithelial cells, which allows the bacterium to invade the intestine (11). E-cadherin belongs to the family of classical cadherins of the superfamily of cadherin proteins responsible for Ca2+-dependent cell adhesion (12). E-cadherin has five extracellular cadherin (EC) domains, numbered consecutively from the N terminus. The cocrystal structure of the EC1 of E-cadherin (1–105) in a complex with InlA (36–496) has been reported (13). Single-domain antibodies (VHHs) that inhibit the interaction between InlB and its host cell receptor c-Met have recently been reported; these antibodies inhibit L. monocytogenes cell invasion (14, 15) Since it is known that synergistic action of InlA and InlB is required for L. monocytogenes to cross the placental barrier (16), a cocktail therapy of anti-InlA and anti-InlB antibodies could be effective. In addition, VHH antibodies, at about 15 kDa, are about 10-fold smaller in size than conventional immunoglobulin G antibodies.

In this study, we isolated and purified two VHHs, vhh10, and vhh24 from an alpaca immune library utilizing a phage display method. The two VHHs bound to InlA with pM affinity and inhibited the binding of InlA to the two N terminal-most extracellular domains, EC12, of E-cadherin in vitro. Furthermore, vhh10 and vhh24 inhibited L. monocytogenes invasion into host cells in culture. High-resolution X-ray structural analysis of the complexes of InlA with anti-InlA VHH antibodies and with EC12 revealed that these VHHs competitively inhibit the interaction of InlA with E-cadherin.

Results

Selection of anti-InlA VHH antibodies and the interaction analysis

To obtain anti-InlA VHH antibodies, an alpaca was immunized with recombinant InlA (36–496). After immunization and confirmation of an increase in antibody titer against the antigen, RNA was extracted from lymphocytes to construct a VHH phage display library. Subsequently, panning was performed against InlA, and the enriched VHH clones were singly cloned and evaluated by phage ELISA. We obtained 28 clones that bound with high affinity (Fig. 1A). Analyses of complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) revealed the high convergence into two clones, vhh10 and vhh24 (Table 1).

These two VHHs were expressed and purified as recombinant proteins using an Escherichia coli expression system. To analyze the interaction of these VHHs with InlA, we used surface plasmon resonance (SPR). vhh10 and vhh24 were shown to bind InlA with KD values of 696 pM and 194 pM, respectively (Fig. 1, B and C and Table 2). The interactions were also characterized using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). Accurate binding free energy ΔG and binding entropy ΔS values could not be calculated since the binding affinity is on the order of pM revealed by SPR analysis, resulting in a large c value, above 10,000. For vhh10, the binding stoichiometry was 1:1 (mol:mol), and the binding enthalpy ΔH was −8.7 kcal mol−1; for vhh24, the binding stoichiometry was also 1:1 (mol:mol), and the ΔH was −10.6 kcal mol−1. Thus, binding of both vhh10 and vhh24 was enthalpy driven (Fig. 1, E and F and Table 2). In the binding analysis using anti-Lysozyme antibody D3-L11 (17) as a negative control, no binding signals were observed for both SPR and ITC (Fig. 1, D and G). Thermal stability analyses of the VHHs were performed using differential scanning fluorometry. Midpoints of the melting transitions (Tm values) were above 60 °C for both (Table 2 and Fig. S1).

VHHs inhibit the InlA-EC12 interaction

To ascertain whether the two VHH antibodies inhibited binding to E-cadherin EC12, we performed ITC analysis of the interaction between InlA and EC12 in the presence or absence of VHHs. In the absence of VHHs, an exothermic reaction with 1:1 stoichiometry was observed (Fig. 2A). On the other hand, in the presence of vhh10 or of vhh24, this exothermic reaction was not observed (Fig. 2, B and C). This result indicates that EC12 cannot bind to InlA when InlA is complexed with a VHH.

Anti-InlA VHHs inhibit bacterial invasion

To test whether anti-InlA VHHs inhibit L. monocytogenes invasion into host cells, we used Caco-2 cells derived from human colon and express E-cadherin. The vhh10 or vhh24 antibodies were added at the specified concentration in the culture medium, and then the bacteria were infected simultaneously (Fig. 3, A and B). As a control VHH, we used the anti-lysozyme VHH antibody D3-L11 at 10 μg ml−1. L. monocytogenes was added to the samples, and the number of bacteria attached to the cell surface after 1 h, and the number of bacteria that had invaded the cells was determined to further 2 h. The adhesion of the bacteria to Caco-2 cells did not change with or without the antibody (Fig. 3A). The vhh10 and vhh24 significantly inhibited the invasion of L. monocytogenes into Caco-2 cells. Dose-dependent inhibition of the bacterial invasion into the cells was observed with vhh10 or vhh24, whereas no change was observed with the control antibody. In the no-antibody condition and the control antibody condition, only about 1% of the bacteria invaded the cells, whereas the addition of 100 ng ml−1 of VHH suppressed the bacterial invasion to 0.04% for vhh10 and 0.06% for vhh24. Furthermore, adding VHH 12.5 ng ml−1 suppressed the bacterial invasion to 0.34% for vhh10 and 0.34% for vhh24 (Fig. 3B).

We also performed another experiment in which the bacterial cells were pretreated with the antibodies before infection. The bacteria were treated with antibodies at concentrations of 10 μg ml−1 or 1 μg ml−1 for 30 min before infection. The bacteria were then washed with PBS; we analyzed the bacterial adhesion and invasion. Even in this case, adding vhh10 or vhh24 did not change the adhesion of the bacteria to the cells (Fig. 3C). On the other hand, the bacterial invasion was suppressed to 0.27% for vhh10 and 0.16% for vhh24. The effect of the antibody was attenuated when the bacteria were pretreated (Fig. 3D).

Cocrystal structures of VHH in complex with InlA

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which VHH antibodies inhibit E-cadherin binding, we crystallized the complexes of InlA with EC12, or with vhh10, or with vhh24. The structures were determined at 1.91 Å, 1.53 Å, and 2.35 Å resolutions, respectively (Figs. 4 and 5; Table 3). The structure of InlA in complex with EC12 showed that the EC1 domain is bound to an indentation on the concave surface of the LRR domain of InlA (Fig. 4, A and B). This structure was superimposed on the previously determined structure of InlA in complex with the EC1 domain (Protein Data Bank [PDB] ID: 2omx, 2omy) resulting in an RMSD value of 0.38 Å (Fig. S2). Our InlA-EC12 complex was very similar to the previously reported InlA-EC1 complex. Representative noncovalent bonds at the interaction interface between InlA and EC12 are shown in Figure 4C. We further compared the buried surface area (BSA) and shape complementarity (Sc) values at the binding interface of the InlA-EC12 complex with those of previous InlA-EC1 (Table S2). BSA values were calculated using PISA (Proteins, Interfaces, Structures and Assemblies) server (18). EC12 exhibited lower BSA value (1165.5 Å2) than EC1 (1425.6 and 1364.3 Å2). This is due to the fact that the number of hydrogen bonds and salt bridges at the interaction interface is lower in EC12 than in EC1, and the binding interface in EC12 is slightly further away than in EC1 (Fig. S2A). Sc values were calculated using Rosetta. The Sc calculation in Rosetta was based on the Lawrence and Colman method (19). The Sc values of EC12 were compared to EC1 and found to be comparable. Therefore, EC12, like EC1, also binds to InlA with comparable structural complementarity.

The crystal structures of InlA in complex with vhh10 or with vhh24 show that the anti-InlA VHHs recognize an LRR region in InlA that partially overlaps with the binding surface to EC12 (Fig. 5, A–D). When the structures of the complexes of InlA with vhh10 or with vhh24 were superimposed on the structure of InlA in complex with EC12, it was apparent that the binding sites of the VHHs partially overlap with the interaction interface of EC1 (Fig. 5E). From these structures it was also found that all CDRs (H1, H2, and H3) of the VHHs interact with InlA (Fig. 6). Calculation of the BSAs for each CDR revealed that H3 showed the greatest contact fraction (vhh10: 33.2%, vhh24: 49.5%; Table S1). These data suggest that H3 makes a considerable contribution to the binding affinity of these VHHs. The surface structure of InlA by electrostatic potential is shown in Fig. S3. Although the depressions in the LRR region are positively charge-rich, vhh10 and vhh24 specifically used the LRR region, which has no surface charge bias, as a binding site. The total BSA and Sc values of these VHHs were calculated and compared to EC12 (Table S2). The results showed that the BSA values of our VHHs (1063.3 Å2 and 1037.8 Å2) were close to those of EC12 (1165.5 Å2). On the other hand, Sc values of our VHHs (0.74 and 0.71) was higher than that of EC12 (0.66).

A closer look at the side chains of each H3 showed that hydrogen bonds were formed between InlA and VHHs: In vhh10, Asp100 forms a hydrogen bond with Ser389 of InlA (2.59 Å) and Thr103 forms a hydrogen bond with Trp387 of InlA (2.80 Å) (Figs. 6A and S4A). In vhh24, Arg97 and Arg99 form hydrogen bonds with Tyr369 (3.34 Å) and Glu326 (3.51 Å) of InlA, respectively. Multiple hydrogen bonds were also observed between InlA and the main chain residues of H3 of vhh24 (Tyr103, Ser104, and Ser105) (Figs. 6B and S4B).

Discussion

L. monocytogenes InlA is a virulence factor that binds to the host receptor E-cadherin, an interaction necessary for the bacterium's invasion of host cells. By immunization of an alpaca, we obtained VHH antibodies to InlA. Analyses of the sequences of the CDRs of high-affinity binders revealed convergence into two clones, vhh10 and vhh24. These two VHHs had high affinities for InlA with dissociation constants on the order of pM, due to the low dissociation rates (koff) values. Both also inhibit the binding of InlA to the extracellular domain of E-cadherin in vitro. Furthermore, vhh10 and vhh24 inhibited L. monocytogenes invasion into host cells in culture.

VHH antibodies with neutralizing activity against InlB, another L. monocytogenes protein required for fusion with host cells have been reported. Two of these antibodies, R303 and R330, have dissociation constants of 150 pM and 130 pM, respectively, and koff values on the order of 10−3 (14, 20). Our anti-InlA VHH antibodies thus have more stable complex against its antigen than those of the anti-InlB antibodies, due to lower koff values of anti-InlA VHH antibodies (10−4). Furthermore, the interactions between InlA and vhh10 and vhh24 were enthalpy driven as shown by ITC analysis. This suggests that our anti-InlA VHHs interact specifically with their target. In the high-resolution structures of the InlA-VHH complexes, multiple hydrogen bonds were observed. When BSAs were compared, the area was higher for vhh24 than for vhh10, and a higher percentage of the BSA was located in CDR-H3 for vhh24 than for vhh10. Further, vhh24 forms more hydrogen bonds with InlA residues than does vhh10. These structural features likely underlie the higher affinity of vhh24 for InlA.

From a structural comparison between the reported VHH R303-InlB complex (PDB ID: 6DBG) and our VHHs-InlA complexes, the epitopes of both InlA and InlB are located on the β-strand side of the LRR region in InlA/B and relatively flat surfaces. Both antibodies act with CDR-H3 as the main antigen-binding site. Examination of the structures of the CDR-H3 regions of both anti-InlA VHHs reveals a kinked conformation (21). In CDR-H3 regions that form the kinked-type conformation, the H3 loop is usually long (21). The R303 anti-InlB VHH antibody has a helical-type CDR-H3 conformation with a relatively long H3 chain length (21). Thus, the features of the long loop and kinked conformation of CDR-H3 may be important for the specific molecular recognition of VHH to the InlA/B antigen. Furthermore, the BSA of the antigen-binding interface of our anti-InlA antibody (vhh10: 1063.6 Å2, vhh24: 1037.8 Å2) was larger than that of R303 anti-InlB VHH (792.9 Å2) as shown in Table S2. Furthermore, in terms of Sc values, our VHHs (0.74 and 0.71) were higher than R303 anti-InlB VHH (0.68) as shown in Table S2. Compared to previously reported scores of shape complementarity for VHHs, our VHHs have a relatively high Sc value (17, 22). These larger BSA and Sc values might be related to the koff value of the anti-InlA VHH antibody being lower than that of the anti-InlB VHH antibody.

Both vhh10 and vhh24 inhibited invasion of L. monocytogenes into Caco-2 cells. Compared to the anti-lysozyme VHH antibody D3-L11, which was used as the control in this study, the invasion inhibition activity of vhh10 and vhh24 decreased significantly. The inhibitory activity concentration was less than 100 ng ml−1, which means lower concentration and very high inhibitory activity of the bacterial invasion than the reported InlB VHH antibody (14, 20). It is possible that our antibodies showed higher inhibitory activity than InlB antibodies due to their higher BSA values and more stable binding to the antigen. In our bacterial invasion assay using Caco-2 cells, in the absence of antibodies, about 1% of bacterial cells were detected inside host cells. We observed inhibition of invasion by the anti-InlA VHHs, and also observed inhibition of invasion by the control antibody. The control VHH antibody was used at 1000-fold higher concentration than the dissociation constant obtained from SPR analysis. Therefore, more significant differences in inhibitory activity may be obtained by lowering the concentration of the added control antibody to an appropriate value. The inhibition of the invasion was thought to be reduced because the bacteria were always exposed to the antibody when the antibody was present in the medium. Still, when pretreated, the bacteria grew during infection and became bacteria to which the antibody did not bind, thus reducing the apparent inhibition of the invasion rate. The inhibitory activity concentration of vhh24 tended to be lower than that of vhh10 in the pretreated invasion assay, possibly due to the lower koff value and dissociation constant of vhh24 than that of vhh10. Thus, to the best of our knowledge, we have succeeded in obtaining the VHH that exhibits highest invasion inhibition activity against L. monocytogenes (23, 24, 25).

The single-domain antibodies that target InlB, which recognizes c-Met on host cells, also inhibit L. monocytogenes invasion (14). The anti-InlB VHHs bind to InlB in an interaction mode that blocks binding to c-Met. InlA and InlB are coexpressed on the surface of L. monocytogenes. Therefore, a cocktail therapy of anti-InlA and anti-InlB single-domain antibodies or bispecific antibodies have potential for use in treatment of listeriosis.

Conclusion

In this study, we obtained and characterized two VHH antibodies that inhibit the binding of InlA to E-cadherin. Structural analyses of the InlA-VHH complexes showed that the binding sites of the VHHs partially overlap with that of E-cadherin. As our anti-InlA VHH antibodies vhh10 and vhh24 inhibit L. monocytogenes from entering host cells, these antibodies warrant further study for their potential in the treatment of listeriosis.

Experimental procedures

Expression and purification of recombinant InlA

The DNA sequence encoding InlA (36–496) was cloned into the pET28b vector (Novagen) for expression of the protein with a six-histidine (His) tag and a SUMO tag at the N-terminal side. InlA was expressed using the BL21 (DE3) expression system (Merck). To induce protein expression, the cells were grown at 37 °C in LB medium until the absorbance at 600 nm was approximately 0.55. After cooling to 4 °C, 1 M IPTG was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM. Cells were then incubated at 20 °C for about 16 h. E. coli pellets collected by centrifugation were suspended in 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, and the cells were disrupted by sonication. After centrifugation at 40,000g for 30 min, the supernatant was subjected to immobilized metal-ion affinity chromatography (IMAC) using Ni-NTA agarose (Qiagen, cat. #30230). Elution of recombinant InlA was performed in 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 200 mM imidazole. Ulp1 protease was added to remove the His-SUMO tag. IMAC was performed to remove His-SUMO tag and the Ulp1 protease, and the flow-through fraction was dialyzed against 10 mM Hepes, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM CaCl2. Final purification was performed by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) at 4 °C using a HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva, cat. #28989336,) equilibrated with 10 mM Hepes, 150 mM NaCl, and 3 mM CaCl2.

Expression and purification of recombinant E-cadherin EC12

The DNA sequence encoding E-cadherin EC12 (1–213) was cloned into the pET28b vector for expression of the protein with an N-terminal His-SUMO tag. EC12 was expressed using the Rosetta (DE3) expression system (Merck). To induce protein expression, the cells were grown at 37 °C in LB medium until the absorbance at 600 nm was approximately 0.55. After cooling to 4 °C, 1 M IPTG was added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM. Cells were then incubated at 20 °C for about 16 h. E. coli pellets collected by centrifugation were suspended in 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, and the cells were disrupted by sonication. After centrifugation at 40,000g for 30 min, the supernatant was subjected to IMAC using Ni-NTA Agarose (Qiagen, cat #30230). Elution of recombinant EC12 was performed in 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 200 mM imidazole. Ulp1 protease was added to remove the His-SUMO tag, and IMAC was performed to remove His-SUMO tag and Ulp1 protease. The flow-through fraction was dialyzed against 10 mM Hepes, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM CaCl2. Final purification was performed by SEC at 4 °C using a HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 75 pg column (Cytiva, cat. #28989334) equilibrated with 10 mM Hepes, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM CaCl2.

Library construction and selection

An alpaca was immunized with purified recombinant InlA once a week for a month. After confirmation of antibody titer elevation by ELISA using serum, mononuclear cells were isolated from the serum collected from the immunized alpaca using Leucosep (Greiner Bio-One, cat. # 227290) and Lymphoprep (Cosmobio, cat. # 18001). Library construction and antibody selection from the obtained peripheral blood B cells were performed. Total RNA was obtained using TRIzol, followed by complementary DNA synthesis by reverse transcription using Superscript III. Antibody genes were amplified by PCR and incorporated into a phagemid vector, pLUCK (26). The library DNA was electroporated into E. coli TG-1 (Lucigen) followed by infection with VCS M13 helper phage (Agilent Technologies). Phages were precipitated from the bacterial supernatant with 4% PEG and 0.5 M NaCl and resuspended in PBS. VHH antibodies were selected by two rounds of biopanning using immunotube followed by the binding analysis by phage ELISA. The clones of antibodies showing strong signals in the ELISA were sequenced.

Expression and purification of recombinant VHH

All VHH constructs were expressed using the same method. The DNA sequence encoding VHH for expression with AAA and a His tag at the C terminus was cloned into the pRA2 vector (27). VHHs were expressed using the BL21 (DE3) expression system (Merck). To induce protein expression, the cells were grown at 28 °C in LB medium until the absorbance at 600 nm was approximately 0.55. After cooling to 4 °C, 1 M IPTG was to a final concentration 0.5 mM, followed by incubation at 20 °C for about 16 h. E. coli pellets collected by centrifugation were suspended in 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, and the cells were disrupted by sonication. After centrifugation at 40,000g for 30 min, the supernatant was subjected to IMAC using Ni-NTA Agarose (Qiagen, cat. #30230,). Elution of recombinant VHH was performed in 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, and 200 mM imidazole. Ulp1 protease was added to remove the His tag, and IMAC was performed to remove the tag and the Ulp1 protease. The flow-through fraction was dialyzed against 10 mM Hepes, 150 mM NaCl, and 3 mM CaCl2. Final purification was performed by SEC at 4 °C using a HiLoad 26/600 Superdex 75 pg column (Cytiva, cat. #28989334) equilibrated with 10 mM Hepes, 150 mM NaCl, and 3 mM CaCl2.

Surface plasmon resonance

Kinetics of the interaction of VHH with InlA were analyzed using SPR on a Biacore T200 instrument (Cytiva). His-SUMO tag fused InlA was immobilized at 630 to 670 RU on a CM5 Biacore sensor chip (Cytiva) that had been activated with N-hydroxysuccinimide/N-ethyl-N′-[3-(dimethylamino)propyl]carbodiimide hydrochloride. The activated surface of the sensor chip was subsequently blocked by treatment with 1 M ethanolamine hydrochloride (pH 8.5). To measure the interaction of each VHH with InlA, a range of concentrations of each antibody (0.625–40 nM) were injected at a flow rate of 30 μl min−1 with a 120 s association time and a 180 s dissociation time. The running buffer was 10 mM Hepes-NaOH, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM CaCl2, and 0.005% Tween 20. At the end of each cycle, the chip was regenerated by treatment with glycine–HCl, pH 2.0. Data analysis was carried out using the BIAevaluation software (Cytiva). A global fitting analysis assuming a 1:1 Langmuir binding model was used to determine association rate constants (kon) and dissociation rate constants (koff) for the interactions. The dissociation constant (KD) of the interaction was calculated as koff kon−1. Each experiment was done three times independently.

Isothermal titration calorimetry

Thermodynamic parameters and the binding stoichiometry of the interactions between InlA and EC12 and between InlA and VHHs were evaluated using an iTC200 microcalorimeter (Malvern Panalytical). Buffer conditions were 10 mM Hepes, 150 mM NaCl, and 3 mM CaCl2. In the case of InlA-EC12 interaction, the ITC cell was filled with 15 μM EC12, and the ITC syringe was filled with 150 μM InlA. In the case of InlA-VHH interactions, the ITC cell was filled with 10 μM VHH, and the ITC syringe was filled with 100 μM InlA. Parameters were calculated by fitting titration curves using ORIGIN 7.0 software (https://www.malvernpanalytical.com/en/support/product-support). An inhibition assay was performed under the following conditions: the ITC cell was filled with 15 μM EC12, and the ITC syringe was filled with 150 μM InlA and 150 μM VHH. Each experiment was done three times independently.

Differential scanning fluorimetry

Differential scanning fluorimetry was performed on a CFX Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Rad). Each VHH antibody (0.4 mg ml−1) was dialyzed in the buffer of 10 mM Hepes, 150 mM NaCl, and 3 mM CaCl2. One thousandth of the protein sample volume of SYPRO Orange Protein Gel Stain (5000 × concentrated in dimethyl sulfoxide; Invitrogen) was added, and the proteins containing 5 × SYPRO Orange were loaded onto Hard-Shell 96-well PCR Plates (Bio-Rad) for measurement. The denaturation of each protein was observed via fluorescence emitted from SYPRO Orange upon absorption to the exposed hydrophobic surface. Each experiment was done three times independently.

Cell-based inhibition assay

L. monocytogenes were grown in brain heart infusion medium (Difco) statically to an absorbance of approximately 0.64 at 600 nm. To evaluate adherence, Caco-2 cells (1 × 105 cells per well in 24 well culture plate) and VHH were added in Caco-2 cell medium to various concentrations (12.5 ng ml−1–100 ng ml−1), and L. monocytogenes were added simultaneously at 2 × 105 colony-forming units per well. For pretreating with VHH antibodies, L. monocytogenes (2 × 105 colony-forming units) were incubated with VHH antibodies (10 μg ml−1 or 1 μg ml−1) for 30 min. Then pretreated bacterial cells were added to the cell culture as above. After incubation for 1 h, cells were washed with PBS and disrupted with ultrapure water. The collected cell suspension was diluted and spread on a plate, and the number of bacteria attached to the cell surfaces was determined by counting colonies the next day. Results are expressed as a percentage of the total bacterial cells initially added. For evaluation of internalization, cells were plated as above. After incubation for 1 h, cells were washed with PBS, and the culture medium was replaced with a gentamicin-containing medium with or without antibodies. The control antibody was D3-L11, an anti-lysozyme VHH at a final concentration of 10 μg ml−1. After incubation for 2 h to remove the attached bacteria, cells were lysed, the collected cell suspension was diluted and spread on a plate, and the number of bacteria attached to the cell surfaces was determined by counting colonies the next day. Results are expressed as a percentage of the total bacterial cells initially added. All values are expressed as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett's test was used to evaluate differences compared to control.

Crystallization of InlA-EC12, InlA-vhh10, and InlA-vhh24 complexes

Purified InlA, EC12, vhh10, and vhh24 were each dialyzed against 10 mM Hepes, 30 mM NaCl, and 3 mM CaCl2. After dialysis, each protein was concentrated to approximately 0.75 μM using an Amicon Ultra-15 10K (Merck Millipore, cat. #UFC901024), mixed as appropriate to obtain the desired protein complex, and subjected to SEC after which the proteins were concentrated to approximately 10 mg ml−1. Crystallization was performed on an Oryx8 apparatus (Douglas Instruments) by mixing 0.5 μl of protein solution with solutions contained in commercial screening kits (Hampton Research). Crystals of InlA-EC12 were obtained in a solution containing 0.2 M ammonium acetate, 0.1 M Tris, pH 8.5, and 45% v/v (+/−)-2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol. Crystals of the complex of InlA with vhh10 were obtained in a solution consisting of 0.085 M Hepes, pH 7.0, 8.5% 2-propanol, 16% PEG4000, and 15% glycerol. Lastly, crystals of InlA-vhh24 were obtained in a solution containing 0.2 M ammonium citrate tribasic, pH 7.0, 14% PEG 3350. In all cases crystals were grown at 20 °C. Crystals of the complex of InlA with EC12 or of the complex of InlA with vhh10 were directly transferred to liquid nitrogen and stored until data collection, whereas crystals of InlA-vhh24 were briefly incubated in mother liquor supplemented with 20% glycerol prior to flash-freezing in liquid nitrogen.

Data collection and processing

Diffraction data from single crystals of InlA in complex with EC12 and of InlA in complex with vhh10 were collected at beamline BL5A of the Photon Factory under cryogenic conditions (100 K). Data from InlA in complex with vhh24 were collected at beamline AR-NW12A of the same facility. Diffraction images were processed with the program MOSFLM and merged and scaled with the program AIMLESS (28) (PDB Id 8H62) or SCALE (28) (PDB Id 8H63, 8H64) of the CCP4 suite (29). The structure of InlA in complex with EC12 was determined by the method of molecular replacement using the coordinates of InlA (13) and P-cadherin EC12 (30) with the program PHASER (31). For the complex of InlA with vhh10, the coordinates of a homolog vhh were employed (17) using the same procedure. In the case of the complex of InlA with vhh24, the coordinates of vhh10 were used as initial model. The models were refined with the program REFMAC5 (32) and built manually with COOT (33). The difference between Rfactor and Rfree factors in each structure was in all cases less than 4 %, thus not showing evidence of overfitting. Validation was carried out with PROCHECK (34). Data collection and structure refinement statistics are given in Table 3.
