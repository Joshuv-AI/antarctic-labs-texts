Altmetric Activity and Scientific Integrity: A Retraction Analysis of PubMed-Indexed Social Media-Related Publications
Authors: Maidan Mukhamediyarov, Olena Zimba, Marlen Yessirkepov, Burhan Fatih Kocyigit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial media is an essential tool for the dissemination of scientific information. The growing number of publications related to social media raises concerns about the proliferation of misleading information in the scientific literature. Retractions act as a crucial corrective mechanism in scholarly publishing; however, their relationship with social media visibility has not been thoroughly examined.MethodsAs part of our descriptive research, 55 retracted social media-related publications indexed in the PubMed database were analyzed. Such characteristics as the number of authors, the time from the date of publication to the retraction, the indexing of publications in the Web of Science, Scopus, and Directory of Open Access Journals databases, the country of the authors of correspondence, the reasons for the retraction, and Altmetric Attention Score (AAS) were analyzed. The data on citations and mentions in various social media platforms were analyzed.ResultsThe highest number of retracted publications was published in 2023 (n = 39), and the median time from publication date to retraction amounted to 541 (28–912) days. China and India were the leading countries for corresponding authorship and international collaborations. The leading causes of retractions were Investigation by Journal/Publisher (n = 45), Unreliable Results and/or Conclusions (n = 41), Concerns/Issues about Referencing/Attributions (n = 37), Concerns/Issues with Peer Review (n = 35), and Concerns/Issues about Results and/or Conclusions (n = 35). The median AAS of retracted publications was 1.5 (1–1,627). A positive correlation was found between the number of citations and AAS (rho = 0.562, P < 0.001).ConclusionRetracted social media-related papers can gain substantial digital circulation before formal retraction, highlighting vulnerabilities in the current scientific interaction ecosystem. These findings urge more post-publication supervision, timely retraction notices, and international collaboration to enhance research integrity, especially in the rapid, decentralized digital distribution age.

INTRODUCTION

Over the past two decades, social media platforms like X (previously Twitter), Facebook, Reddit, and YouTube have transformed into crucial instruments for transmitting, discussing, and critiquing scientific information. Researchers utilize these platforms to promote their work, participate in real-time scientific discussions, monitor up-to-date issues, and communicate with a broader audience beyond typical academic settings.12 The rapidity and broad nature of these platforms facilitate the quick dissemination of scientific information,3 both accurate and misleading, heightening the necessity of preserving the integrity of shared knowledge.4

As the quantity and pace of scientific publication grow,5 so do concerns about scientific misconduct, poor peer review, and untrustworthy data. Retractions have evolved into a crucial process for ensuring the integrity of the academic record. Not only do retractions correct the literature, but they also provide essential signals to the research community regarding questionable practices or data.67

Retraction reasons can be multifaceted, encompassing honest errors, fraud, Fake Peer Review, Paper Mills, and improper citation patterns. The heightened visibility of articles on social media may result in erroneous studies attracting attention prior to their formal retraction, disseminating misleading information.89 Recognizing how retractions are transmitted, classified, and discussed is critical for maintaining scientific communication, particularly in the digital age.10

Altmetric Attention Scores (AAS) represent a data-driven approach to quantifying the online attention that scholarly articles receive. Derived from various online platforms—including social media, news outlets, blogs, Wikipedia, and Mendeley—Altmetric data provides insights into how scientific publications are consumed, shared, and discussed by academic and public audiences.1112 These metrics help researchers monitor the public impact of their work, including how misinformation or erroneous findings may have swayed public discourse before formal retraction.

This study analyzes the characteristics, distribution, and altmetric footprint of retracted publications related to social media that are indexed in PubMed. By examining the reasons for retraction, the statuses of journal indexing, collaboration patterns, and AAS, we aim to gain a deeper understanding of the intersection between scientific communication, digital dissemination, and research integrity. Our findings provide a clear snapshot of how unreliable or compromised research can spread through social media.

METHODS

Data were obtained using the PubMed Database (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) for this descriptive study. Retracted publications were searched on PubMed using the keywords “retracted publication”[pt] and “social media” and 55 publications were listed (date: April 13, 2025). The main reasons for preferring PubMed database in this study are that it offers practical and systematic access to social media-related studies and the desire to focus on biomedical literature. PubMed provides reliable and reproducible data by standardized tagging of retracted publications in this field.13

Data extraction

The following data were extracted for the study: Publication title, (Digital Object Identifier) DOI number, number of authors, publication date, retraction date, AAS, source (journal, conference, book), publication category, corresponding country, collaborating country, and retraction reasons. The duration between the publication date and retraction date was calculated. The sources’ indexing status in Web of Science (WoS), Scopus, and Directory of Open Access Journals (DOAJ) was reviewed and recorded. The open access status of the sources was searched and noted.

Citation counts were obtained from the WoS Database.

Scimago Journal & Country Rank (SJR) data were exported via the platform (https://www.scimagojr.com/). SJR is one of the publicly available and free platforms. Journal data, the subject area, and publications category were available using SJR.14

Altmetric data

The AAS provides a numerical representation of the online attention that a scholarly publication receives across various digital platforms. In contrast to traditional citation-based indicators, the AAS reflects immediate and publicly observable interactions, emphasizing both the frequency and context in which a study is referenced online. The data were collected utilizing the Altmetric tool. The score is calculated through an automated algorithm that assigns different levels of significance to various digital mentions. Each category of online engagement is weighted according to Altmetric’s established scoring system. Ultimately, the score signifies the article’s visibility, reach, and degree of interaction within the online community. In addition to the overall AAS, detailed data contributing to the score were recorded for further analysis.1516

AAS and citation data for all publications were retrieved on the same day, May 14, 2025.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to analyze the obtained data. Number (n), percentage (%), and median (minimum–maximum) values were reported. The data was visualized using Microsoft Excel. IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used, and correlations were evaluated with the Spearman rho test.

A binary logistic regression analysis was performed to determine parameters associated with obtaining citations in the WoS. The dependent variable was operationalized as citation count, categorized at the 10-citation level (0 = < 10 citations, 1 = ≥ 10 citations). The independent variables were the AAS as a continuous covariate, China-based collaboration (0 = no, 1 = yes), and journal category (0 = non-medicine, 1 = medicine). The analysis was conducted using the Enter method.

Two more factors, open-access status and article type, were initially contemplated but were omitted due to a significant distributional imbalance. Over 90% of the publications were classified as research articles, and 87% were published in completely open-access journals, leading to inadequate variance for efficient logistic modeling. Their inclusion also resulted in convergence problems during model estimation in SPSS. Additionally, the Mendeley reader count variable was investigated as a potential predictor. Nevertheless, its inclusion resulted in estimation instability, which was likely caused by outliers and its moderate correlation with AAS. Mendeley data and AAS subcomponents were excluded from the final model to prevent issues related to model overfitting and multicollinearity.

The final logistic regression analysis was conducted on papers with complete WoS citation count and AAS data. Articles with absent values for any predictor were eliminated using listwise deletion.

A P value of less than 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Ethics statement

The study did not involve humans or animals. Ethical approval was not required, as the analysis was conducted on publicly available platforms.

DISCUSSION

This study analyzed the features, retraction reasons, indexing status, patterns of international collaboration, and online visibility of 55 retracted PubMed-indexed papers concerning social media. The analysis indicated a considerable increase in retractions following 2020, peaking in 2023. The majority of the retracted publications were published in respected WoS-indexed journals, and the most frequent reasons for retraction were Investigation by the Journal/Publisher (n = 45), Unreliable Results and/or Conclusions (n = 41), and Concerns/Issues with Referencing/Attributions (n = 37). A certain number of these papers had a high AAS, indicating significant digital circulation before their retraction.

The median retraction time of 541 days highlights a notable lag between publication and formal retraction, which permits incorrect or misleading findings to spread unchecked for long durations.17 This interval emphasizes the need for improved post-publication review systems and digital monitoring to identify red flags swiftly. The significant rise in retractions post-2020 can be partially ascribed to the pandemic-induced surge of rapid publications and increased scrutiny of scientific integrity in digital communication.18

An evident geographic trend was identified in the examination of retracted publications, with China and India ranking as the predominant countries for corresponding authorship and international collaboration. China had 21 corresponding author roles and 23 total collaborations, whereas India had 8 corresponding authorships and 19 collaborative contributions. This geographic clustering indicates a possible concentration of publication activity—and consequently, retractions—in countries with increasing scientific output.19

The dominance of China and India in the dataset likely indicates underlying structural pressures within their academic systems. Academic progress is strongly linked to the volume of publications, citation metrics, and institutional performance indicators in high-output settings. Incentive structures that enhance productivity may simultaneously foster questionable research practices.2021

Another factor contributing to the observed trend might be the differences in standards for research integrity training and editorial oversight across various areas. Countries experiencing rapid growth in their scientific workforce may encounter challenges in scaling up robust quality control and mentoring mechanisms at an equivalent pace. Even well-intentioned researchers may succumb to systemic weaknesses in such contexts, including inadequate supervision, institutional pressure, or a limited understanding of ethical standards.22

The retraction reasons uncover a complex array of compromised scientific integrity factors. The predominant noted causes—investigations by journals or publishers (n = 45), unreliable results and/or conclusions (n = 41), referencing or attribution issues (n = 37), concerns regarding peer review (n = 35), and data-related issues (n = 34)—collectively highlight enduring vulnerabilities throughout critical phases of the research and publication process.

The considerable number of retractions from investigations by journals or publishers underscores the critical role that editorial bodies play in maintaining scientific integrity through post-publication oversight. Such investigations may stem from internal audits, whistleblower alerts, or algorithmic screening tools, and their frequency indicates a troubling gap in the efficacy of pre-publication peer review.2324 This trend implies that numerous flawed studies escape initial examination and are only uncovered after concerns arise post-publication, strengthening advocates’ calls for more effective post-publication review systems, increased editorial transparency, and improved institutional backing for investigative efforts.25 The findings indicate serious data management issues— from methodological inaccuracies, breaches of ethics, or outright falsification—that persistently undermine confidence in scientific results. Furthermore, the widespread existence of citation manipulation, encompassing irrelevant or coerced references, exposes fundamental issues associated with metric-driven academic cultures.26

An examination of the subject areas of sources indicated a predominance of health-related and technology-oriented fields, with Medicine notably leading (n = 49), followed by Mathematics (n = 25), Computer Science (n = 22), Neuroscience (n = 21), and Environmental Science (n = 9). The high rate in medicine may reflect both its extensive research production and its swift transmission via public channels, enhancing visibility and post-publication scrutiny. The inclusion of computational disciplines emphasizes challenges related to reproducibility, data complexity, and the demand for innovation in rapidly evolving fields.27

To assess the academic impact of retracted publications, citation data from 54 articles were examined within the WoS database. The findings showed a median citation count of 4, ranging from 0 to 179, highlighting considerable variability in scholarly influence.

In addition to traditional citations, the extended dissemination of these publications within the digital environment was evaluated through the AAS. Data were accessible for 36 articles, exhibiting a median score of 1.5 and a range from 1 to 1,627. This suggests that, although most retracted articles received limited online attention, a select few attracted considerable engagement.

The correlation analysis identified connections between traditional citation metrics and altmetric indicators. Statistically significant positive correlations were found between WoS citation counts and AAS and mentions in news outlets and on X (formerly Twitter). This interaction between digital attention and scholarly citations underscores the growing intersection of public discourse and scientific communication in today’s digital age.15 Despite correlations, logistic regression analysis failed to detect any statistically significant variables related to obtaining ≥ 10 WoS citations. These findings highlight the complexities of citation dynamics and show that altmetrics, while beneficial, may not be standalone predictors of high academic impact in multivariate models.

Stronger correlations were noted between WoS citation counts, Mendeley data, and Dimensions citations, suggesting that Mendeley readership may be a reliable early indicator of academic engagement and prospective citation potential. This corresponds with previous research indicating that Mendeley engagement signifies enduring academic relevance, presumably because it is predominantly utilized by scholars, researchers, and medical professionals.28

This analysis is descriptive and restricted to papers indexed in PubMed, potentially omitting pertinent retracted studies from other databases. Dependence on Altmetric scores may inadequately reflect non-English digital activity or discourse in forums not indexed by Altmetric. These analyses offer a snapshot.

In conclusion, this study presents a nuanced portrait of the retracted social media-related publications circulation within the scientific ecosystem and beyond. Despite their origins in journals with established indexing status, many articles were marred by fundamental issues such as fraudulent peer review and unreliable results. Some were able to achieve significant digital reach before their formal withdrawal. These findings underscore the necessity for vigilant post-publication oversight, enhanced international collaboration on research ethics, and improved transparency in scientific communication, particularly in the social media era, where misinformation can disseminate rapidly and persistently.
