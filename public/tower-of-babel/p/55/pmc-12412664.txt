Robotic micromanipulation for patterned and complex organoid biofabrication
Authors: Mingsi Tong, Gang Huang, Songlin Zhuang, Xinghu Yu, Delin Hu, Weiyang Lin, Yang Wu, Yuchen Chen, Shijia Cheng, Meng Jiang, Li Zhang, Huijun Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Organoids have emerged as powerful models for recapitulating tissue physiology and pathology in biomedical research. However, the need for consistent and complex manufacturing of organoids remains a challenge. The absence of standardization and quality control of cells dispersed within extracellular matrices impedes the widespread application of organoids. Here, we introduce a micromanipulation platform that effectively constrains cell distribution to enable patterned and complex organoid biofabrication. This system integrates multidimensional cell sense technology to provide real-time feedback, enabling adaptive fluid dynamics control that robustly compensates for seeding-induced nonlinear fluidic perturbations. Through this closed-loop control, the micromanipulation platform effectively restrains the spatial distribution of multiple cell types, promoting consistent self-assembly. Using this platform, we have successfully achieved programmable organoid manufacturing as well as the construction of assembloids composed of multiple cell types that better mimic mature organ microenvironments. This supports advancements in organoid-based biomechanism discovery, drug screening, and other organoid-related investigations.

INTRODUCTION

Organoids are self-organized three-dimensional (3D) cell spheroids derived from the adult or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) (1). Organoids not only show high similarity to the histological features of real organs in the body but also retain some key characteristics of their representative organs (2); thus, they are highly promising in vitro models for tissue development, drug discovery, and personalized medicine research (3). However, before organoids can be effectively applied in these areas, the challenge of homogeneous and reproducible organoid production needs to be addressed (4). Specifically, organoids that grow in the extracellular matrix (ECM) hydrogel can exhibit random cell distribution and microenvironmental inconsistencies, resulting in variations in their shape, size, and spatial distribution (Fig. 1A). Addressing this demands unprecedented precision in microscale manipulation and real-time feedback mechanisms that traditional approaches simply cannot provide.

Among the various explored solutions, such as bioprinting (5, 6), droplet embedding (7), and multiphysics field manipulation approaches (8), the use of microwell arrays has emerged as a competitive method that facilitates the high-throughput production of neatly arranged organoids (9, 10). By using micro/nanofabrication techniques (11) or other surface-patterning technologies (12) to create arrays with varying shapes (13, 14), microwells enable the division of cell suspensions into multiple parts (15) through physical boundaries (10). However, manual seeding with pipettes inherently lacks the necessary active control and sensing. This often leads to unpredictable cell displacement and irregular self-assembly (16). Furthermore, the burgeoning field of assembloids (17), which requires assembling multiple distinct cell types within a single microwell (18) (only a few hundred micrometers in size) imposes higher demands on the operational precision (19). Consequently, there is a pressing need for an advanced microrobotic system capable of precise spatiotemporal control of cellular organization (20).

Through systematic analysis of experimental batches spanning different organoids, we identified that the current challenges predominantly lie in the inability to achieve high-resolution robotic operations within the microwell array. This limitation further impedes programmable organoid manufacturing—specifically, the precise control of heterogeneous organoid patterns both inter- and intrawells. Beyond the low cell utilization efficiency caused by inaccurate manual seeding, the technological complexity for a microrobotic solution involves robust microdroplet generation, precise microwell positioning, and effective cell concentration. Existing micromanipulation systems have achieved notable progress and shown promise for flexible biological organism grasping (21), low-damage cell injection (22), and precision actuator development (23). However, they lack the perceptual and control sophistication required for scalable, multicell assembly in complex organoid fabrication. Notably, sensing and mitigating nonlinear fluid perturbations during cell seeding is critical for reproducible manipulation, and achieving programmable manufacturing demands ultrahigh-precision positioning and robust sensing for indistinct phase objects like transparent hydrogel microwells. Therefore, micromanipulation systems must enhance their perception and control ability to meet the novel challenges presented by organoid manufacturing.

Here, we report the development of a robotic micromanipulation platform that harnesses visual-servo control to achieve homogeneous and reproducible cell self-assembly, enabling the programmable manufacturing of organoid arrays (Fig. 1). By modeling the cellular hydrodynamics and integrating sophisticated control algorithms informed by real-time cell distribution sense, it mitigates fluid perturbations from cell seeding and restrains cell spatial distribution at the microscale. Furthermore, its enhanced perception capabilities ensure superior positioning and motion control, even for indistinct phase-contrast objects, by establishing a high-fidelity 3D spatial coordinate system relative to a microwell array chip. This technology enables an unprecedented level of control over organoid: (i) compositional complexity, which refers to the capacity to deterministically integrate multiple distinct cell types at defined ratios, and (ii) spatial complexity, which denotes the ability to dictate the precise spatial arrangement of these cells with the desired pattern. This level of control is critical for recapitulating the intricate architecture of native tissues.

Through experiments, the precise control of cell distribution has been identified as a critical factor in enhancing the homogeneity of the organoids, significantly surpassing what can be achieved in conventional 3D culture methods. Moreover, it enables the accurate and flexible construction of various types of cells in complex patterns to explore collective organoids behaviors upon cell-cell and cell-microenvironment interactions and better mimic microenvironments containing tissue regeneration. In conclusion, we demonstrate how robotic micromanipulation with integrated sensing and advanced control can precisely and reproducibly manage the spatial dynamics of cell distribution, serving as a powerful platform to revolutionize organoid array construction and accelerate organoid-based research and development.

RESULTS

Seeding-induced fluid perturbation leads to a random cell distribution

In 3D cell culture, the ECM hydrogel may restrict material exchange and causes inconsistent local microenvironments, where smaller droplets of matrices can minimize the differences in the shape, size, and distribution of the organoids. Therefore, we can restrict cell distribution by reducing the cell embedded droplet volume to improve organoid consistency (Fig. 2A). However, the minimum volume that a pipette can process is limited, and it is difficult to obtain consistent organoids even in the smallest volume (Fig. 2B and fig. S2A). This highlights the need for a tool that can achieve microdroplet generation.

To address this problem, the micromanipulation is an excellent option for generating sufficiently small droplets to produce uniform, neatly arranged organoids (Fig. 2A, bottom). It offers precise control at the picoliter scale and is commonly used in intracellular microdose drug injections (22, 24), making it highly suitable for precise microfluidics control. Unlike traditional microinjection procedures, where liquid is preloaded into the micropipette, we designed a reservoir dish to load different cell types (Fig. 2C). This allows convenient seeding of multiple cell types without the need to replace the micropipette. Once the cells are loaded, they can be seeded in the incubator chip, facilitating the formation of consistent and well-organized organoid arrays.

However, manual manipulation to generate a large number of organoids is time-consuming and labor-intensive. Therefore, we automated the process and implemented a volume-quantitative seeding strategy. We identified that the inconsistent cell distribution persisted in the microwells. This inconsistency arose from the uneven cell distribution within the micropipette, which caused nonlinear density of cellular fluid during seeding (Fig. 2D and fig. S2B). Therefore, we designed an algorithm to determine the cell density variation pattern within the micropipette. Through experiments, we found that there were four stages during the seeding process, where uneven cell density fluctuates at different stages. In addition, the distribution of cells in the micropipette before extrusion was also segmented (fig. S2C). This cell density variation may induce the differences in the intra- and interwell for cell self-assembly (Fig. 2, D and E, and fig. S2D), where insufficient or dispersive cell density can hinder robust cell aggregation and result in self-assembly failure. The successfully assembled spheroids also had inconsistent sizes due to the uneven initial density. Furthermore, when the cells are relatively scattered in a microwell, the local accumulation of cadherins can lead to the formation of multiple spheroids. Typically, the proportion of failed assemblies was ~62% (fig. S2E).

In addition to the uneven trend of cell density in the micropipette, we considered that the physical boundary of the microwell makes the cell distribution more complex, and to illustrate this, we used a particle tracking model and fluid simulations to simulate the different stages of the cell seeding process, as shown in Fig. 2F. When the cell density underwent minimal variation, as shown in the left three panels of the figure, the cells tended to increase in concentration. A low density may result in a sparse cell distribution, which is unfavorable for cell assembly. For the density transition cases, as depicted in the right two columns, the cells were more concentrated when the density increased. In contrast, the cells were scattered, which may have resulted in the formation of multiple spheroids. The simulation results were consistent with the cell distributions observed during the seeding process (Fig. 2G). These results indicate that the uneven trend of cell density in the micropipette introduces fluid perturbation and influences the consistency and reproducibility of organoids.

Establish a visual-servo–driven robotic micromanipulation platform

To minimize cell seeding-induced fluid perturbations, we developed a visual-servo robotic micromanipulation platform (Fig. 1, B to D, and fig. S3A). It can achieve customizable and automated organoid fabrication by establishing constraint equations and solving corresponding control schemes for robotic and microfluidic control. The general process for programmable organoid manufacturing consists of several steps (fig. S3B), where we need to address two fundamental challenges: (i) localization and control of the robotic micromanipulation end-effector and (ii) perception and regulation of cell distributions.

First, phase objects (e.g., transparent cells and microwell arrays) under bright-field illumination exhibit low imaging contrast due to their refractive index matching with surrounding media. This may lead to localization inaccuracies. To overcome this limitation, we developed a region-predictive cascaded localization method that exploits the focusing clarity-dependent characteristics of phase-contrast imaging (see section S2). By constraining feature detection within defined regions and refining positional estimates, this approach achieves improved detection accuracy and efficiency (Fig. 3B and fig. S4, A to D). Meanwhile, inter- and intrawell cell distribution heterogeneities critically influence the developmental fidelity of multicellular assembloids. Manual pipetting often induces stochastic self-assembly nucleation points within individual wells, disrupting programmable patterning. Our platform addresses this challenge by leveraging 3D visual-servo control to enhance the positioning accuracy and repeatability of the micromanipulation end-effector (Fig. 3C and fig. S4E).

Furthermore, to address the nonuniform cell distribution within micropipette caused by cell aggregation and sedimentation during cell seeding, we propose a visual-servo cell distribution control method to improve the manufacturing consistency. This approach achieves cell enrichment and distribution consistency control through strategic switching of cell seeding process combined with real-time distribution monitoring (Fig. 1, B to D). We draw inspiration from the detection methodology of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) readers, which use absorbance measurements of samples to enable quantitative analysis of cells within the microwells. We delved deeper into analyzing the spatial distribution of the measurement data, thereby successfully advancing the detection capability from 1D density measurement to 3D spatial distribution (fig. S5A). On the other hand, the cell spatial distribution controller consists of two parts. One of them was to solve the uneven density distribution problem, where the fluctuated cell density curve is similar to a rope filled with wrinkles (fig. S5B). By compressing multiple wrinkles into a single section and smoothing out prominent wrinkles, we can obtain a uniform rope. Consequently, stacking cells in the microwell with multiple seeds can result in a more consistent distribution (Fig. 3, D and E). The other control scheme was the cell displacement boundary control in each stacked layer. Stacking cells to be more concentrated can increase cell-cell interactions and establish local gradients of endogenous signals (4), promoting cell aggregation. However, predicting cellular trajectories and distributions as cells transition from the micropipette into an open reservoir presents a considerable challenge due to the abrupt change in flow regime and boundary conditions. Here, we present a comprehensive theoretical framework to model cell motion, both within the micropipette and upon subsequent ejection as a submerged jet into a culture medium–filled reservoir (fig. S5C; detailed modeling descriptions are in section S3). We demonstrate that cells develop a nonuniform radial distribution and velocity profile, dictated by the pipette Reynolds number and the ratio of cell size to pipette diameter. Therefore, the controller was designed to resist the disturbances and optimize cell seeding systems, enabling precise control over cell placement and concentration in the receiving environment (fig. S6; see section S4).

In a typical cell seeding process (Fig. 3D), the cells were evenly distributed within the microwells during the first round of seeding. By adjusting the position of the end-effector of the micromanipulator and controlling cell extrusion, the cell distribution was found to be close to the ideal curve, exhibiting a higher-density central region and lower-density periphery. Through multiple rounds of seeding, the deviation of the distribution curve was reduced by 84%. With the above methods, we can programmable construct cells with various geometric arrangements within the microwell array, which is challenging for the manual seeding process. These included consistent seeding, alternating seeding, gradient seeding, pattern seeding, and mixed seeding (Fig. 3, F to J, and fig. S7D). In these applications, micromanipulator function as a pen-like tool, transforming cells into ink and petri dishes into canvases, enabling precise arrangement of cellular patterns at the microscale (Fig. 3, H and I, and fig. S7, A to C). These results indicate that the visual-servo–driven robotic micromanipulation can address uneven cell distributions caused by fluid perturbations and control the spatial distribution and spatiotemporal patterns of cells.

Precision and efficiency in organoid fabrication through micromanipulation

Before organoid fabrication, we systematically evaluated the positioning precision of different cell seeding approaches and their effects on cellular organization. The benchmark includes pipetting in manual and micromanipulation under both precalibration and visual-servo control (Fig. 4A). The results indicated that manual pipetting exhibited random droplet deposition with positioning variability (SD = 118 μm), whereas micromanipulation-based methods demonstrated enhanced accuracy (precalibration: SD = 77 μm; visual-servo control: SD = 8 μm). Moreover, precalibrated method showed distance-dependent error accumulation (Fig. 4B), where angular deviations as minimal as 1° induced ~250-μm positional displacements. Notably, cellular distribution fidelity proved sensitive to positioning accuracy, with manual pipetting causing interwell contamination (Fig. 4A, right), whereas fluid dynamics–optimized micromanipulation maintained discrete microwell confinement.

Intrawell cellular organization displayed spatial dependency on micropipette positioning precision. Off-center deposition (Fig. 4C) led to heterogeneous cell aggregation patterns, particularly evident in multicellular systems containing self-assembly competent cells (e.g., fibroblast; fig. S8E). This further emphasizes the importance of precise positioning. However, sequential seeding experiments demonstrated cumulative positioning errors for repeated positioning accuracy (149 μm for pipetting, 92 μm for precalibration, and 12 μm for visual-servo control; Fig. 4D). In addition, pipetting often disturbs previously seeded cells during subsequent rounds, resulting in misalignment distributions of different cell types. In contrast, visual-servo micromanipulation consistently achieves precise, overlapping distribution centers across multiple seedings, addressing the challenges of cell placement and improving accuracy for complex organoid fabrication.

Microwell operation resolution emerged as a critical determinant for spatiotemporal control of cell distribution. Unlike manual pipetting, which often results in cell displacement across multiple microwells, micromanipulation allows for more precise single-well operations. In addition to the interwell cell distribution control, we further test the intrawell distribution control ability. We used both manual pipetting and micromanipulation to seed cells in gradient volume (Fig. 4E). Manual methods produced disordered cellular dispersion. Micromanipulation achieved linear gradient establishment (R = 0.987) through precise fluidics control, despite minor intrapipette distribution irregularities. These findings establish that visual-servo micromanipulation overcomes fundamental limitations of conventional approaches for high-fidelity organoid engineering.

Systematic implementation of multiconstraint cellular distribution strategies would significantly improve organoid consistency (Fig. 4F). Our platform enables multidimensional control through parameter-specific constraint strategies, achieving precision-tunable organoid fabrication (Fig. 4, G and H). Manual seeding without constrains resulted in consistent local cell densities but decreased density toward the periphery (fig. S8C), whereas automated seeding provided overall consistent densities with customizable distributions (fig. S8D). The 3D control scheme can further concentrate the cells in the center, reducing the distribution variance and promoting self-assembly (Fig. 4, I and J), achieving aggregate scores of 0.83 and 2.2% variability. In contrast, the 1D and 2D schemes produced flatter distributions, complicating cell aggregation, with lower aggregate scores (0.65 and 0.73) and higher variability (3.3 and 4.0%).

Although micromanipulation is more effective for complex organoid fabrication, manually producing hundreds of organoids would be time-consuming. We evaluated the platform operational efficiency for manual and automated cell seeding (Fig. 4K). On average, manual seeding took 7.5 s per well, whereas automated seeding reduced this to 4.8 s (Fig. 4G). Notably, ~50% of manual operation duration involved interwell micropipette relocation, whereas automated workflows optimized motion trajectories. In addition, biological validation confirmed comparable viability between methods (Fig. 4I), establishing automation as a viable high-precision alternative without compromising cellular viability.

A key advantage of micromanipulation is cell utilization efficiency, requiring threefold reduction in cellular resource consumption compared to manual pipette seeding (Fig. 5, A to C, and fig. S9). This stems from precision deposition in predefined microwell coordinates, eliminating off-target cell loss inherent to manual dispensing protocols. Critical for rare sample processing, our platform can effectively concentrate cells through dynamic fluid control (Fig. 5B), enabling organoid generation from ultralow volume specimens. Integration with real-time visual feedback also permitted submicroliter precision (Fig. 5D), consistently producing 200-μm organoids from 1-μl samples at a density of 5 × 105 cells/ml. In addition, when disregarding cell density constraints, our platform demonstrated the nanoscale fluid handling ability to generate droplet volumes as small as 13 nl (~0.6-nl resolution in the micropipette; Fig. 5E).

We also systematic evaluated surface engineering strategies to address the uneven cell distribution in the micropipette for improving consistency. We tested the effects of hydrophobic treatment of the micropipette, the addition of a surfactant, and control algorithms against a baseline with no treatment. As shown in Fig. 5 (F and G), the untreated group exhibited significant variability between microwells during volume-quantitative seeding. The addition of a surfactant was more effective than the hydrophobic treatment in reducing interwell differences. Among these methods, automated operation provided the best consistency, reducing interwell variability by 70% compared with the control group, where the inconsistent spheroids are mainly from the insufficient number of cells for multiple rounds of adjustment.

To establish robust operational parameters for precision cell seeding, we systematically mapped the interdependencies between fluidics parameters and stage kinematics. As shown in Fig. 5H, lower movement speed with a higher aspiration rate may reduce the overall cell density in the micropipette, where more culture medium than cells is aspirated. Conversely, higher movement speeds increase cell capture efficiency but can lead to incomplete loading, decreasing cell utilization. To optimize this process, incorporating visual feedback allows real-time adjustments to the platform parameters. During cell extrusion, the extrusion speed also affects the cell distribution. We tested various speed (50, 150, 250, 500, and 1000 nl/s) under a fixed volume to observe changes in the cell distribution. The results indicate that lower speeds result in a more concentrated cell distribution (Fig. 5I), whereas increasing the speed broadens the distribution range, where the cell movement trend is more intense. Although slower speeds can avoid cell overflow effectively, more time may be needed to reach the target cell distribution. Automated methods, however, dynamically adjust the speed on the basis of the cell density, minimizing the processing time while ensuring the desired cell distribution. This method can also be extended to different scenarios, where we can control the cell distribution in microwells with various shapes. As shown in Fig. 5J, we successfully generated uniform spheroids on tetrahedral, pentagonal, conical, and spherical microwells. In each case, our method consistently maintained control over the cell distribution, with errors remaining below 5%. Multiscale compatibility was also demonstrated from macroscale tissue to single cells through hierarchical biofabrication (Fig. 5K), which further extends its application.

Precise control of cell distribution achieves consistent regeneration of organoids

In complex organoid fabrication, the initial cellular density, cell type, and cell proportions are critical components (25). To verify the platform performance, we compared our approach with manual seeding to generate spheroids with varied sizes. Cells were seeded in a gradient manner (Fig. 6A). After 48 hours of incubation, we evaluated the resulting organoids through tissue sections and bright-field microscopy (Fig. 6B). Our automated seeding approach consistently yielded spheroids with smaller variations in diameter compared to manual seeding. Quantitatively, the automated method demonstrated an average ratio error of less than 5%. Furthermore, it also exhibited less cell proportion heterogeneity compared to those grown on the hydrogel layer (table S1). In terms of self-assembly efficiency (Fig. 6C), the aggregate measurement score gradually increased with cell density, and the automated seeding method was significantly better than the manual process. These results demonstrate the enhanced reliability and accuracy of the automated method for spheroid shape control, and the automated method is a more reproducible and standardized approach for tissue spheroid production.

Besides the overall consistency in microwells, the morphology of spheroids in individual microwells is also important. For self-assembled spheroids (Fig. 6D), the automated seeding method can generate the desired spheroids, irrespective of cell density variations. However, the assembly status fluctuated with cell density during manual seeding (Fig. 6D). Low-density conditions can obstruct cell aggregation, resulting in the inability to form spheroids (Fig. 6A, #1) and irregular structures (Fig. 6A, #2). These irregular assemblies could be caused by cell dispersal, which resulted in the formation of thin layers instead of the desired 3D spheroid structures. Under high-density conditions, the assembly success rate improved. However, some spheroids still displayed irregular assembly characteristics, with satellite spheroids surrounding a central spheroid (Fig. 6A, #3) and multiple assembly cores within the microwells (Fig. 6A, #4). In conclusion, manual seeding demonstrates not only greater assembly success rates with increasing density but also an increased likelihood of generating multiple spheroids. An automated approach promotes aggregation efficiency by regulating cell distribution. Furthermore, by recording the self-assembly process with fewer cells (fig. S10A), the automated seeding method can promote the establishment of cell junctions and self-assembly. These results demonstrate that the automated seeding method promotes consistent cellular regeneration independent of physical constraints or cellular migration capabilities within the microwells.

The integration of cells from different germ layers in specified proportions is also crucial for generating mature and functional organoid models (26). Manual seeding approaches often struggle to precisely control cell proportions. Our platform deposited predetermined cell quantities and could thus construct spheroids composed of the desired cell compositions. To demonstrate this, we fabricated spheroids with varying cell ratios (Fig. 6E and table S2). The fluorescence intensity ratio confirmed that our method reliably constructs spheroids with predetermined cell compositions (<5% error), demonstrating significantly lower cell proportion variability than manual handling. Moreover, in complex organoid construction, both the proportions and the positioning of different germ layer cells could influence the development and formation of intricate structures (27). Our platform can also control the position of multiple cells (Fig. 3J).

Furthermore, consistent airway organoids arranged in the array configuration had been created as illustrated in Fig. 7 (A and B), which depict their progression process. Proximal airway cells derived from digested organoids or 2D-expanded airway epithelial progenitors were seeded into microwell arrays. Within 12 hours, the cells self-assembled into organoids with 100% colony-forming efficiency. Subsequently, Matrigel was introduced to encapsulate the formed spheroids. We found that organoid encapsulated by the Matrigel exhibited cell migration, and the cells reorganized from a solid spheroid into a structure with lumen structure (fig. S11A). By creating multiple arrays, we observed that the development of airway organoids in Matrigel-wrapped arrays followed a similar progression (Fig. 7C). After about 3 days of culture, these organoid developed petal-like branched structures, and by day 7, the organoids exhibited a morphology resembling densely packed epithelial tips. In addition, by seeding cells at different densities in the array (fig. S11B), we found that the branch formation will not be affected by the density. Notably, certain branched structures displayed a defined lumen and were organized into a single epithelial layer, closely mimicking the in vivo architecture of the tip epithelium (Fig. 7, D and E), which is hardly observed in conventional organoid droplet culture method. This observation aligns with the physiological requirement for high-density 3D branching morphogenesis in the lung airway, which ensures optimal spatial organization of tubes for functional efficiency (28, 29).

To further assess the potential of robotic micromanipulation in improving the functional uniformity of organoids, we performed a quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) experiment. We randomly selected the same quantity of organoids from both the manually seeded hydrogel layer and the robotically seeded microwells. We then analyze the gene expression to quantify the expression levels of key genes associated with basal cells (KRT5), ciliated cells (FOXJ1), and goblet cells (MUC5AC). Consistent with the reduced structural variability observed in microwells, robotically cultured organoids showed significantly lower gene expression heterogeneity compared to hydrogel-grown counterparts (fig. S11C and table S3).

To assess the differentiation capacity of these tip-like organoids, we further cultured them for 1 week in PneumaCult-ALI, an airway organoid differentiation medium. Postdifferentiation (Fig. 7F and fig. S11D), a reduction in basal cell populations was observed, accompanied by the emergence of acetylated tubulin (ACT)+ ciliated cells. Immunostaining revealed increased expression of FOXJ1, a ciliation-associated transcription factor, confirming ciliated cell differentiation. Intriguingly, branched structures exhibited enhanced proliferative activity compared to nonbranched regions (Fig. 7G). Differentiated ciliated cells were also predominantly localized near the luminal regions in branched structures (Fig. 7E). However, organoids cultured in differentiation medium showed marked contraction of branches compared to those maintained in renewal medium (fig. S11E), suggesting biomechanical or signaling changes during differentiation. Furthermore, the gene expression results also demonstrate a decrease in the expression of the basal cell marker KRT5 with increasing culture duration. Concomitantly, we observed an up-regulation of FOXJ1 and MUC5AC. These findings collectively confirm that our airway organoids undergo appropriate functional differentiation over the extended culture period (fig. S11F). These results suggest that the automated seeding method improved the consistency and efficiency of the cell assemblies, facilitating the production of tissue spheroids with specific characteristics. By sequentially depositing predetermined cells and regulating spatiotemporal distribution, our approach enables the fabrication of consistent complex organoids with specific cell compositions.

The robotic micromanipulation supports a customizable organoid regeneration strategy

Current organoids used in clinical translation, drug screening, and personalized medicine rely heavily on the ECM hydrogel, which are constrained by xenogeneic origin, batch-to-batch variability, and scalability barriers (30). Scaffold-free methods are more compatible with high-throughput platforms, standardized production, and enhanced suitability for automated drug screening or disease modeling (10). Moreover, organoids derived from single-cell sources exhibit inherent limitations in achieving physiological maturity and functionality. For instance, functional lung organoids require not only epithelial progenitors but also interactions with supporting stromal cells (e.g., fibroblasts, endothelial cells, or immune cells) to recapitulate the lung in vivo microenvironment. However, current cell culture and 3D printing methods struggle to reliably reproduce complex organoid microwells composed of multiple cell types. Micromanipulation enables the sequential seeding of various cell types, facilitating the formation of intricate cell arrangements with the desired spatial resolution (Fig. 8, A and B). Recognizing their critical roles in airway development and as a stem cell niche in vivo, we investigated how fibroblasts and endothelial cells could enhance the relevance of biofabricated airway organoids.

We separately tested the simultaneous seeding of mouse airway epithelial cells (mAECs), human lung fibroblast cells (HLFCs), and human umbilical cord endothelial cells (HUVECs) in the same microwells (Fig. 8C and fig. S12). Different coculture conditions exhibited varying growth states. To compare their differences, we conducted immunofluorescence analysis on the organoids (Fig. 8B). Ki67-positive cells, which indicate proliferation, were present in nearly all the organoids, particularly concentrated in the more mature or actively growing regions. Compared with those with the pure airway epithelium, the proliferation rate of organoids with mixed culture conditions was greater, indicating that coculture promotes organoid growth. FOXJ1, a marker for ciliated cells, reflects organoid maturity through the presence of cilia. Although cilia have been observed in organoids across various conditions, their maturity varies. In mixed culture, there were relatively more mature cells, especially when vascular cells were included. In contrast, organoids composed solely of epithelial cells displayed localized clusters of mature cells. In addition, in microwells containing both vascular cells and fibroblasts, we observed the formation of vascular sprouts, with these cells enveloping the organoid surface, corresponding to a relatively high level of maturity in that area (Fig. 8D). Compared with organoids only composed of vascular cells, the incorporation of fibroblasts promoted the formation of vascular sprouts. Incidentally, the organoids cocultured with HLFCs had a relatively regular shape in long-term culture, potentially due to the stronger contractile properties of fibroblasts.

In addition, tumors in vivo exhibited a wide range of phenotypes within microenvironments (31) (Fig. 8E). To study its impact on cancer behaviors, we constructed different organoid coculture models that accurately reflected the complexity of tumor development. This involved the establishment of monoculture of tumor cells, coculture of intestinal tumor cells with other cell types, and spatially arranged multicell culture. We developed a tumor vascularization model by controlling the seeding positions and cell types within the microwells (Fig. 8F). During tumor organoid maturation, the cellular components and tissue structures were distinct. We conducted immunofluorescence analysis under different conditions to verify these distinctions (Fig. 8, G and H). For the coculture conditions, the number of Ki67-positive cells was consistently uniform at the center, whereas it increased at the edges of the tumor-endothelium interface. In addition, EpCAM was positively expressed at the tumor-to-endothelium interface, indicating the emergence of certain complex structures within the tumor organoid. For the spacing arrangement condition, the proportion of Ki67-positive cells gradually increased from the center toward the periphery, indicating an enhanced proliferation state compared to that under the coculture condition. Conversely, in the monoculture of tumor cell condition, there was a distinct distribution of Ki67-positive cells, without any noticeable peripheral proliferation. These results demonstrated the impact of microenvironmental variations on tumor growth. This influence could be due to the tumor preference for anaerobic conditions, which is counteracted by the presence of vascular endothelial cells that suppress the tumor growth state. These results emphasize the importance of our platform in investigating tumor microenvironments in a controlled manner to elucidate the mechanisms governing tumor behaviors.

DISCUSSION

Organoid fabrication has emerged as a critical technology for advancing biomedical research as there is an increased demand for physiological recapitulation organ models (32). However, there is a lack of appropriate tools for achieving precise multiscale spatial control of cellular organization, which is crucial for the development of consistent and complex organoids (33). To address this technological gap, we developed a robotic micromanipulation platform on the basis of advanced robotic technology and visual-servo control. This platform can precisely restrain the spatial distribution of cells to promote cell self-assembly and construct consistent and complex organoids to better mimic in vivo tissue regeneration (34).

To enable high-precision and programmable manufacturing of organoids, we introduce robotic micromanipulation. In addition, a critical, yet largely unaddressed, challenge in organoid fabrication is the stochastic nature of cell distribution during seeding, where interactions with the microscale flow field lead to unpredictable outcomes. We conducted in-depth research on these issues on the basis of this platform. The core scientific problem here is to mitigate uncertain fluid perturbation and control cell distribution. In conventional volume-quantitative seeding, phenomena such as cell aggregation, adhesion, and micropipette clogging introduce variability in local cell density. This, in turn, results in inconsistent initial cell arrangements, often characterized by excessive intercellular distances that impede the requisite cell migration and efficient aggregation, thereby compromising the reliability and reproducibility of organoid generation (35). To address these challenges, it is crucial to develop strategies that minimize uncertainties and ensure a more uniform distribution of cells. One promising approach is the use of multiphysics field–assisted fabrication, which can apply external forces to confine cells into predetermined patterns (36, 37). Although effective, these methods typically require specialized device engineering. Our approach, however, focuses on actively directing the flow field distribution on the basis of dynamical models and visual-servo technologies. This method provides a more flexible and accessible alternative for fabricating engineered organoids. Furthermore, conventional macroscale bioprinting, while capable of patterning tissues, lacks the resolution required for constructing intricate architectures at the submillimeter scale. In contrast, our micromanipulation platform achieves superior spatial resolution for programmable intrawell patterning. This capability enables the precise assembly of complex, multicomponent assembloids within confined microenvironments. (For a comprehensive description of the challenges in manufacturing organoids, please refer to section S1.)

The technological cornerstone of our platform is visual-servo control, a sophisticated methodology from industrial robotics that we have innovatively adapted for microscale biological fabrication (38, 39). Unlike conventional mechanical assembly at the centimeter scale, organoid construction demands superior precision to avoid perturbing highly sensitive cells. Our system overcomes the inherent limitations of microscopic optics, such as restricted field of view and depth of field, to achieve robust spatial coordinate alignment within microwell arrays. Previous array-based culture methods, focusing primarily on microwell geometry, have been limited by imprecise cell placement tools. Manual pipetting, for example, is of low resolution and inefficient, often resulting in substantial cell loss and difficulty in managing cell concentrations for small samples. In contrast, our robotic system manipulates picoliter-scale fluid volumes with high resolution, transferring control from a global input to individual microwells. This enhances cell utilization and facilitates the programmable assembly of complex, multilineage organoids. The visual-servo algorithm is specifically designed to counteract in situ uncertainties arising from electrostatic and capillary forces, thereby regulating cell distribution, promoting cell-cell interactions, and establishing local gradients of endogenous signals.

To expedite the industrial translation of this technology, several aspects can be further optimization. The current system—built using standard laboratory equipment—is constrained by motion speed and workspace size. Future iterations could incorporate collaborative robotic arms and multistation parallel processing to scale up production. Furthermore, integrating the cell reservoir with the robotic end-effector would streamline the cell loading process, reducing operational time. All these improvements could advance this technology toward organoid manufacturing at industrial scales.

The precision and consistency afforded by this technology are not merely incremental improvements; rather, they serve as catalysts for transformative advances across several pivotal areas of biotechnology. In high-fidelity drug discovery and personalized medicine, the pervasive issue of experimental variability, stemming from inconsistent organoid growth and resultant phenotypic and gene expression heterogeneity, severely compromises the reliability of drug response data. Our platform directly mitigates this by producing highly consistent organoids. Furthermore, its capacity to construct complex, patient-specific tumor assembloids—complete with relevant stromal and immune cellular components—paves the way for high-throughput screening of personalized chemotherapy regimens (40), promising more accurate predictions of patient responses and ushering in a new era of personalized oncology. In summary, by combining the natural self-organizing properties of organoids with growth regulation, our platform will facilitate the development of the next generation of organoids manufacturing and accelerate progress in disease modeling, organ transplantation, and drug discovery (41).

Organoid microwell chip fabrication process

For the microwell array chip in the incubation dish (fig. S1), a polydimethylsiloxane (1:10 ratio of cross-linking agent:elastomer) (SYLGARD 184 Silicone Elastomer Kit, Dow Corning) male mold is fabricated by a precision micromilling machine. For the chip in the reservoir dish, a resin (Durable Resin, Formlabs) male mold is fabricated by a stereolithography appearance printer (Form3, Formlabs). The shape of the mold is transferred to agarose (2%, w/v) through imprinting. The agarose-based chips are cross-linked for ~5 min in a 4°C environment. Then, the chips are removed carefully, the sterile buffer is added to the chips, and the mixture was stored at 4°C until use.

Cell density statistics in the micropipette

To analyze the density of cells in the micropipette, a rectangle of 150 pixels by 100 pixels is taken as the region of interest (ROI). We take the first image when there are no cells as the background template (fig. S2F). This region is mean filtered to avoid interference. The cell density is detected in real time and is obtained by differencing the density from the background and then we segment it by threshold. The area of the obtained binary image is considered the cell cluster area and compared with the area of the ROI as the final density statistical value.

Organoid micromanipulation platform hardware

Organoid fabrication was performed using a custom-built, automated platform that integrates a micromanipulator (μM Micromanipulator, Sensapex), a motorized stage (H117, Prior), and a modified oil pump (CellTram 4r Oil, Eppendorf). The micromanipulator provides three-axis movement with a 20-mm travel range per axis and 100-nm resolution, actuated by piezoelectric components. The motorized stage offers two-axis movement over a 114 mm–by–75 mm workspace with 3.6-μm resolution, driven by stepper motors. The motorized oil pump, capable of dispensing volumes with a resolution of ~0.1 nl, is actuated by a stepper motor connected to a subdivided motion shaft. A custom control card transmits commands to the motor drivers, which, in turn, control the stepper motors for precise liquid dispensing.

Real-time feedback of cell distribution was acquired using an inverted microscope (IX83, Olympus) coupled with a camera (DP73, Olympus) and transmitted to a host computer. Micropipettes were fabricated from glass capillaries (B100-75-10, Sutter) using a micropipette puller (P-97, Sutter) and subsequently beveled with a microelectrode beveler (EG400, NARISHIGE) to control the size of the nozzle from 70 to 100 μm. To maintain cell viability, a cooling module, comprising copper blocks with integrated flow channels, was embedded within the motorized stage. This module, cooled by a semiconductor refrigeration block, circulates refrigerant via a water pump to maintain a constant temperature 0°C.

The integrated system uses a “macro-micro” hierarchical motion control strategy to achieve both a large operational workspace and high-precision positioning. The motorized stage performs large-scale movements, enabling access to various cell reservoirs and navigation across the microwell plate. Subsequently, the high-resolution micromanipulator executes fine-tuned motions to compensate for any positioning errors and precisely control the cell deposition pattern within individual wells. This hierarchical control strategy is crucial for achieving high-throughput and high-precision organoid manufacturing.

Automated organoid manufacturing process

The hardware setup of the robotic micromanipulation platform mainly includes the establishment of feedback information, motion control for the micromanipulator and the motorized stage, pressure control for cell extrusion, and temperature and CO2 control for providing a suitable cultivation environment (fig. S3A). As the controller, the host computer is responsible for the regulation of the whole platform operation. The host computer takes images from the microscope (as the sensor) and analyzes them to update the platform status in real time. The obtained status is fed into the individual algorithms, and the output of the algorithms is transmitted to the corresponding slave computer controller and actuator. For example, the micromanipulator controller will obtain a set of desired 3D coordinates. The position of the micromanipulator is controlled according to the desired value, and the current actual coordinates are fed to the host computer. The motorized stage controller obtains a set of desired 2D coordinates, where it controls and feeds back the position of the motorized stage. The motorized oil pump controller obtains information about the speed and direction of the motor. The speed of the oil pump is controlled according to the desired value where the cell movement is further controlled. The temperature control unit and carbon dioxide control unit obtain the desired temperature and carbon dioxide concentration, respectively. They maintain the platform environment according to the expected value combined with the feedback information of the temperature sensor and carbon dioxide sensor. The temperature was generally 4°C during the operation, whereas it was 37°C during the observation. The carbon dioxide concentration was maintained at 5%.

System setup

The cooling module was opened 30 min before organoid manufacturing to ensure that the temperature of the copper block was maintained at the ideal value during the experiment. The glass capillary was autoclaved and hydrophobic to prevent adhesion of the cell (Anti-Adherence Rinsing Solution, STEMCELL Technologies).

Cell preparation

Single-cell suspensions were obtained by the following steps: The iPSCs, HUVECs, HLFs, and organoids were digested with TrypLE Express (Life Technologies) into single-cell suspensions, filtered through a 40-μm strainer, and then resuspended in 1.5 mM EDTA/phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cells were resuspended at concentrations ranging from 5 × 105 to 1 × 106 cells/ml, and 1 ml of the single-cell suspension was added to the reservoir dish.

Organoid fabrication

The automated cell seeding process (fig. S3B) is initiated following equipment preparation and an initial one-time precalibration of both the motion stage and the micromanipulator. After calibration, the micromanipulator end-effector is positioned within the field of view. Real-time tracking of the micropipette tip through imaging minimizes the impact of end-position errors on motion precision. The micropipette then moves to the cell reservoir dish for sequential aspiration of cells. Following cell loading, the micropipette transfers to the incubation dish containing the microwell array for organoid culture.

Cell seeding into the microwell array primarily involves microwell location, contact detection, and cell distribution control. The system offers geometric programmability, allowing users to predefine geometric patterns for cell deposition. This enables the selection of microwell formats and desired cell arrangements within each well before initiating the process, facilitating the creation of arrays with complex, user-specified layouts. Following parameter definition for cell seeding within the microwells, the system achieves dynamic programmability through a visual-servo control loop, which enables real-time adjustments during the cell seeding process.

Cell viability measurements

For qualitative imaging, the cells were incubated according to the instructions of the LIVE/DEAD kit (L3224, Thermo Fisher Scientific). It was performed immediately after seeding. Cells were incubated for 15 min at 37°C to ensure positive staining of dead cells. In the automated seeding experiments, the cells were aspirated and extruded at 1.5 μl/s. Three different printing experiments (n = 3) were performed for each time point (immediately after seeding and after 30 min).

Construction of the observer for different constraint strategies

For different constrained control strategies, the corresponding implementation algorithm is as follows (fig. S5A):

For 1D control, the cell density is constrained. First, the cell intensity was calculated in the defined ROI. The results are subsequently compared with the desired values to provide feedback information. Then, it serves as the input for the controller, ensuring that the controlled density is close to the desired number.

For 2D control, both cell densities and boundaries are constrained simultaneously. The method for constraining the cell density is similar to the 1D control. However, the ROI used for cell density calculation dynamically changes. Moreover, to realize boundary control, we detect the boundary of the cell cluster in the boundary ROI. To avoid interference from individual discrete cells, we also calculate the contour circle with 95% of the intensity region and input it into the controller as the actual boundary. The cell density constraint is accompanied by a boundary constraint.

For 3D control, the cell spatial distribution is controlled, which includes constraints on the cell density as well as the boundary. On the basis of the obtained 3D intensity values of the cell distribution, the polar coordinate is established with the center of the intensity distribution as the origin. The accuracy of the observations is determined by subdividing the angles. Here, we take two subdivisions as an example, where each concentric circle is discretized into semiarch regions. By counting the density of cells in each region, we projected the intensity values of cells with various radii to a single point. This process results in a 1D curve that describes the distribution. By comparing the intensity values layer by layer in each concentric circle with the desired value, we can effectively implement constraints on the cell distribution.

Spheroid consistency evaluation metrics

Spheroid consistency was evaluated using two primary metrics: the aggregate measurement score and growth consistency. The aggregate measurement score quantifies the area of the spheroid in relation to the surrounding cell clusters after 48 hours of incubation (fig. S8A). For growth consistency, bright-field images of the microwell arrays were captured. Image analysis was performed using open-source ImageJ (Fiji) software. For each organoid, the diameter of the bounding circle was used as the representative diameter to account for slight asymmetries (fig. S8B). Immunofluorescence staining

Throughout the entire immunofluorescence experiment, organoids were consistently housed in the agarose-made microwell array. The organoid-containing microwell array was rinsed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde/PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. The microwell array was washed three times with PBS, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 [tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.2% Triton X-100 (v/v) washing buffer] for 15 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 hour at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following antibodies: EpCAM (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 36746S) and Ki67 (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog no. 9449). The second day, the microwell array was washed three times with washing buffer and incubated with fluorescently tagged secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog nos.A11029 and A11035) for 1 hour at room temperature. The microwell array subsequently removed DAPI by washing three times with PBS.
