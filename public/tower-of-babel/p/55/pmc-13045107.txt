Association of the estimated glucose disposal rate combined with a body shape index with all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality among individuals with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome
Authors: Chao Fu, Zheng Zhang, Yuxin Li, Fei Wang, Chunlin Li, Zhaohui Xie, Xiangyang Gao, Lulu Sun, Bingqing Han, Rui Wang, Shanshan Liu, Hao Zhang, Jingbo Hou, Qiang Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome exhibit a substantially elevated risk of all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality. Although estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) and a body shape index (ABSI) are commonly used indicators for assessing insulin resistance and atherosclerotic risk, respectively, evidence regarding their combined effect on all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality in patients with CKM syndrome remains insufficient. Investigating this combined impact may help improve risk stratification in this population.MethodsThis study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 1999–2018), including 18,186 individuals with stage 0–4 CKM syndrome. Cox proportional hazards models, Kaplan–Meier curves and subgroup analyses were used to evaluate the associations between eGDR and ABSI and mortality risk. The integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and net reclassification index (NRI) were used to assess the incremental prognostic value of eGDR and ABSI. Finally, six machine learning algorithms were applied to develop predictive models.ResultsDuring the follow-up period, a total of 2536 all-cause mortality and 790 cardiovascular-specific mortality were documented. After multivariable adjustment, both low eGDR and high ABSI independently predicted mortality risk. Combined analysis revealed that individuals with both Low-eGDR and High-ABSI had the highest mortality risk: all-cause mortality hazard ratio (HR) = 2.79 (95% CI 2.30–3.38) and cardiovascular-specific mortality HR = 4.53 (95% CI 2.96–6.92). However, the interaction effect was not statistically significant. Among the six machine learning algorithms, XGBoost demonstrated the best performance, with areas under the curve (AUC) of 0.877 and 0.850 for predicting all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality, respectively.ConclusionBoth eGDR and ABSI are independent and combined predictors of mortality risk among individuals with CKM syndrome. Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), cardiovascular disease (CVD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) represent major global public health challenges [1–3]. Recent studies have demonstrated that these three conditions share close pathophysiological interactions, forming a complex, multi-organ and multi-system syndrome. Accordingly, the concept of the cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome has been proposed to integrate their systemic interdependence [4–6].

Insulin resistance (IR) is not only a fundamental pathological basis of CKM syndrome but also a common risk factor for multiple metabolic diseases, particularly T2DM [7], obesity [8] and CVD [9, 10]. Estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), a novel indicator for assessing IR, integrates waist circumference (WC), hypertension status and Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). Compared with traditional methods, eGDR offers a more comprehensive and clinically meaningful assessment of metabolic status, with strong predictive value for various clinical outcomes [11, 12].

On the other hand, obesity is another critical metabolic risk factor among individuals with CKM syndrome, synergizing with IR and thereby markedly increasing adverse outcomes [13]. Although body mass index (BMI) is widely used to assess obesity, it poorly reflects fat distribution—especially visceral adiposity—and thus inadequately captures true metabolic risk [14]. A body shape index (ABSI), as a novel anthropometric indicator, integrates body weight, height and WC to more sensitively characterize fat distribution, especially visceral fat [15, 16]. Compared with subcutaneous fat, visceral fat is more metabolically active and more likely to trigger IR, chronic inflammation and atherosclerosis, thereby significantly increasing the risk of cardiovascular and metabolic diseases [17]. Previous studies have also demonstrated that ABSI outperforms BMI in identifying visceral obesity and predicting cardiometabolic outcomes [18, 19].

Although the combined cardiovascular effect of eGDR and ABSI has been preliminarily explored in the general population [20], individuals with CKM syndrome represent a more complex pathophysiological state. In this population, IR and visceral adiposity not only coexist but also intertwine with renal dysfunction and metabolic inflammation, potentially amplifying the prognostic value of eGDR and ABSI. Therefore, investigating their joint association with mortality risk in the CKM population is not only necessary but also of significant clinical importance for optimizing risk stratification and guiding early intervention. This study aims to evaluate the associations of eGDR and ABSI with all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality in individuals with stage 0–4 CKM syndrome. Furthermore, we developed integrated prediction models using machine learning methods to capture disease complexity and multi-dimensional interactions, thereby enhancing risk prediction accuracy.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was based on data from 10 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted in the United States between 1999 and 2018, with an initial sample size of 101,316 participants. NHANES is a program funded by American taxpayers and made publicly available for research purposes (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/default.aspx). According to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, participants were excluded sequentially as follows: (1) individuals younger than 20 years (n = 46,235); (2) individuals with missing data required for the calculation of eGDR or ABSI or with incomplete data for CKM syndrome staging (n = 32,005); (3) individuals with missing key covariates or sample weights (n = 4890). Ultimately, 18,186 participants meeting the 2023 American Heart Association (AHA) diagnostic criteria for CKM syndrome were included in the final analysis. Mortality data

To ascertain mortality outcomes among individuals with CKM syndrome, we used the publicly available NHANES-linked mortality files through December 31, 2019. These files were linked by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) to the National Death Index (NDI) using a probabilistic matching algorithm. Causes of death were identified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). The study endpoints included all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality. All-cause mortality was defined as death attributable to any cause. Cardiovascular-specific mortality was defined as death due to cerebrovascular and cardiovascular diseases identified by ICD-10 codes I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51 and I60–I69.

Data collection

Data on demographics, physical examinations, laboratory tests, lifestyle factors and medical conditions were collected from NHANES. Demographic information: age, gender, race, education (below high school, high school or above), marital status, poverty (defined as a family poverty-to-income ratio < 1.3). Lifestyle factors: drinking (Participants who reported having < 12 drinks in their lifetime or in any one year were considered non-drinking and those with ≥ 12 drinks were classified as drinking) and smoking status (current smoker, former smoker, never smoker). Clinical indicators: BMI, WC and other anthropometric measures. Laboratory measurements included total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), triglycerides (TG), glucose (GLU), insulin (INS), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum creatinine (Cr), serum uric acid (SUA), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) and HbA1c.

Statistical analysis

Given the complexity of the NHANES sampling design, all analyses in this study were weighted accordingly. Specifically, we incorporated the fasting subsample 2 year weights (WTSAF2YR), which were rescaled by dividing by 10 when combining ten survey cycles (1999–2018). Analyses also accounted for strata (SDMVSTRA) and primary sampling units (SDMVPSU), with standard errors estimated using Taylor series linearization in R (version 4.4.3). Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Based on the previous study [12], the median values of eGDR (8.19) and ABSI (0.08) were used as cut-off points to categorize participants into four groups: High-eGDR and Low-ABSI, High-eGDR and High-ABSI, Low-eGDR and Low-ABSI and Low-eGDR and High-ABSI. To examine group differences, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Subsequently, the log-rank test and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis were performed to compare survival probabilities across eGDR and ABSI subgroups, as well as across the four combined groups.

To evaluate the independent and joint effects of eGDR and ABSI on all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality among individuals with CKM syndrome, three multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender and race; and Model 3 incorporated important variables identified through three machine learning algorithms (random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) and the Boruta algorithm). Specifically, the top five-ranked predictors from each algorithm were combined to form the covariate subset included in Model 3. The combined variables for all-cause mortality obtained through machine learning include age, cardiovascular diseases, Cr, INS, GLU and BUN (Fig. 2); while the combined variables for cardiovascular-specific mortality include age, Cr, INS, GLU and HbA1c (Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S1). Although some important features were not selected due to low Z-values, based on previous studies and clinical experience, poverty, gender, race, education, marital status, smoking status and drinking were still included in Model 3 for further analysis [22–24]. Additionally, we constructed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) based on prior knowledge of the causal relationships among CKM syndrome, eGDR, ABSI and mortality (Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S2) [25–29]. Using this DAG, we identified the minimal sufficient adjustment set of confounders and refitted the Cox models accordingly.

In addition, integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and categorical net reclassification index (NRI) were used to assess the incremental prognostic value of eGDR and ABSI in the final Cox regression models. IDI measures the change in the integrated sensitivity minus specificity difference between two models across all cutoff values; categorical NRI reflects the improvement in model classification ability based on a continuous risk scale.

To address potential multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were examined and only variables with VIF < 5 were retained in the models. Detailed results of multicollinearity diagnostics are provided in Supplementary Material 1 (Tables S1–S6). All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3) and two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Bonferroni correction for multiple testing

To control the potential inflation of the Type I error rate due to multiple testing, we implemented a stratified Bonferroni correction based on the type of analysis. The specific correction scheme was as follows: (1) For the secondary outcome (cardiovascular-specific mortality) presented in Table 2, considering that two primary outcomes were examined, the significance threshold was adjusted to P < 0.025 (α = 0.05/2); (2) For the interaction tests across nine subgroups in the subgroup analysis, the significance level was set at P < 0.0056 (α = 0.05/9); (3) For the sensitivity analyses conducted in four populations shown in Fig. 3, the threshold was adjusted to P < 0.0125 (α = 0.05/4).

Feature engineering in the context of machine learning analysis

Categorical variables were transformed using either one-hot encoding or label encoding. Continuous variables were standardized (mean of 0 and variance of 1) when applied in linear models, whereas normalization (scaled to a 0–1 range) was employed for tree-based or distance-based methods.

Construction of machine learning prediction models

Based on the covariates in Model 3, as well as eGDR and ABSI, we optimized variable selection through Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. This process reduced unnecessary variables while maintaining predictive performance, resulting in a more streamlined model that enhances its clinical interpretability and practical applicability. For all-cause mortality, 11 variables were ultimately selected from an initial pool of 15 candidate features as the optimal predictors to be included in the machine learning models. These variables were: Cr, ABSI, age, BUN, cardiovascular diseases, smoking, race, GLU, marital status, education and eGDR (Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S3). For cardiovascular-specific mortality, 7 variables were finally selected from 12 candidate features. These variables were: Cr, age, ABSI, race, education, gender and eGDR (Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S4).

Six machine learning algorithms were applied to predict the risks of all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality among individuals with CKM syndrome: XGBoost, support vector machine (SVM), logistic regression (LR), RF, k-nearest neighbor (KNN) and light gradient boosting machine (LGBM). Participants were randomly split into training and testing sets at a ratio of 7:3. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated based on the performance of these models.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

As shown in Table 1, a total of 18,186 participants were included in this study and were categorized into four combined groups based on the median levels of eGDR and ABSI. Among them, the Low-eGDR and High-ABSI group comprised 5829 individuals. Compared with the other groups, this group had a higher proportion of participants with BMI ≥ 30, elevated GLU and INS levels and significantly higher prevalence of diabetes (26.69%), CKD (23.12%), hyperlipidemia (51.52%) and CVD (18.49%). In addition, this group exhibited higher WC, TG levels and liver function markers (ALT, AST and GGT), as well as markedly increased rates of all-cause mortality (23.54%) and cardiovascular-specific mortality (7.95%). Moreover, this group had a higher proportion of males, a lower level of education and a relatively smaller proportion of individuals who had never smoked.

Baseline characteristics by individual indicators are shown in Supplementary Materials 2 (Tables S1 and S2). Participants with eGDR ≤ 8.19 generally showed higher obesity-related measures (BMI, WC), elevated lipid-related parameters (TC, TG) and worse glucose metabolism markers (GLU, INS, HbA1c), along with higher rates of all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality. Similarly, individuals with ABSI > 0.08 demonstrated comparable trends.

Given that WC is a common component in the calculation of both eGDR and ABSI, we further examined their intercorrelation. WC was strongly correlated with eGDR (r = − 0.75) and moderately correlated with ABSI (r = 0.37), reflecting its central role in both metabolic and anthropometric risk assessment (Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S5).

Association of eGDR and ABSI with the risk of mortality

As shown in Table 2, after adjustment for Model 3, the results of the Cox regression analysis indicated that in the joint effect group (Low-eGDR and High-ABSI), the risks of both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular-specific mortality were significantly increased. Specifically, the risk of all-cause mortality was elevated (HR = 2.79, 95% confidence intervals (CIs): 2.30–3.38), while the increase in cardiovascular-specific mortality risk was even more pronounced (HR = 4.53, 95% CI 2.96–6.92), with the latter remaining statistically significant after Bonferroni correction for two primary outcomes (adjusted threshold: P < 0.025).

Compared to the joint effect, the impact of individual risk factors on mortality risk was relatively modest. For example, when analyzed separately, the risk of all-cause mortality for Low eGDR was 1.80 (95% CI 1.55–2.11) and for High-ABSI, the risk was 1.78 (95% CI 1.55–2.01). Regarding cardiovascular-specific mortality, the risk for Low-eGDR was 2.80 (95% CI 2.04–3.82), while for High-ABSI, the risk was 2.05 (95% CI 1.56–2.70).

As shown in Figs. S6 and S7 (Supplementary Material 2), High-eGDR or Low-ABSI was associated with lower all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality, while Low-eGDR or High-ABSI—especially their combination—was linked to significantly higher risks (all P < 0.001).

As shown in Fig. S8 (Supplementary Material 2), the three-dimensional surface plot reveals a consistent pattern in predicting all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality at 3, 5 and 7 years of follow-up. Mortality risk decreased with higher eGDR and increased with higher ABSI. The greatest risk was observed in individuals with low eGDR combined with high ABSI, whereas the lowest risk occurred in those with high eGDR and low ABSI. This trend remained stable across different time points and outcomes.

Comparison of eGDR and ABSI with traditional indices in predicting mortality outcomes

As shown in Fig. S9 (Supplementary Material 2), in the unadjusted models, eGDR demonstrated superior predictive performance for all-cause mortality (areas under the curve (AUC) = 0.672) compared with triglyceride-glucose (TyG), homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and TyG-derived indices. For cardiovascular-specific mortality, eGDR also performed best, with an AUC of 0.696, markedly higher than other glucose metabolism–related indices. After full adjustment for confounders in model 3, eGDR consistently retained its relative advantage, with higher predictive performance for both all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality compared with HOMA-IR, TyG and their derived indices.

As shown in Fig. S10 (Supplementary Material 2), in the unadjusted models, ABSI outperformed BMI, WC and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) in predicting all-cause (AUC = 0.725) and cardiovascular-specific mortality (AUC = 0.720). After full adjustment in model 3, ABSI still maintained its superiority, with the highest predictive performance for all-cause (AUC = 0.851) and cardiovascular-specific mortality (AUC = 0.839), compared with other obesity-related indices.

Interaction between eGDR and ABSI

After comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, the 95% CIs of the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and attributable proportion due to interaction (AP) in all models included 0, while the 95% CIs of the synergy index (SI) and multiplicative interaction effect included 1. These findings indicated that there was no statistically significant additive or multiplicative interaction between the eGDR and ABSI in relation to all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality (Table 3).

The incremental prognostic ability of the eGDR and ABSI

We first evaluated the discriminative ability of the prognostic models using Harrell’s C-index. As shown in Table S3 (Supplementary Material 2), the simultaneous inclusion of eGDR and ABSI significantly improved the predictive performance of the models. For all-cause mortality, the C-index increased from 0.840 (baseline model) to 0.849 (combined model), P < 0.001; for cardiovascular-specific mortality, the C-index rose to 0.872, P < 0.001. The joint incorporation of both markers yielded the greatest discriminative gains across all outcomes.

To further quantify the clinical utility of the new markers, we assessed the incremental prognostic ability using the NRI and IDI at 5 year and 10 year time points (Table 4). The combined model (Basic + eGDR + ABSI) demonstrated superior reclassification capability. For all-cause mortality, the combined model showed significant improvement in risk stratification, with a 5 year categorical NRI of 0.057 (95% CI 0.037, 0.079) and a 10 year NRI of 0.064 (95% CI 0.047, 0.082). The IDI remained consistently significant over the 10 year follow-up period (IDI = 0.013, 95% CI 0.009, 0.016), indicating a robust improvement in the average sensitivity and specificity of the model. For cardiovascular-specific mortality, the combined model exhibited an even more pronounced reclassification benefit, particularly in the short-to-medium term: the 5 year NRI was 0.098 (95% CI 0.064, 0.132).

Sensitivity analyses

As shown in Fig. 3 and Tables S5–S8 (Supplementary Material 2), in the sensitivity analysis, this study compared the stability of predictions for all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality between the Low-eGDR and High-ABSI group versus the High-eGDR and Low-ABSI group across different populations, including individuals at CKM stages 0–3, those with a follow-up period longer than two years and those without hypertension or diabetes. In participants with CKM stages 0–3 (Fig. 3A), this group showed a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.76 (95% CI 2.18–3.50) for all-cause mortality and 4.10 (95% CI 2.50–6.73) for cardiovascular-specific mortality. Among participants with a follow-up period longer than 2 years (Fig. 3B), the HRs were 2.77 (95% CI 2.26–3.40) for all-cause mortality and 4.42 (95% CI 2.88–6.77) for cardiovascular-specific mortality. In the non-hypertensive subgroup (Fig. 3C), this group exhibited an HR of 1.76 (95% CI 1.23–2.53) for all-cause mortality and 2.89 (95% CI 1.18–7.06) for cardiovascular-specific mortality. In the non-diabetic subgroup (Fig. 3D), this group exhibited an HR of 2.49 (95% CI 1.99–3.11) for all-cause mortality and 3.95 (95% CI 2.52–6.19) for cardiovascular-specific mortality. In sensitivity analyses across four populations (Fig. 3A–D), after Bonferroni correction for multiple testing (adjusted threshold: P < 0.0125), the combined effect of low eGDR and high ABSI on both all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality remained statistically significant in all populations except for cardiovascular mortality in the non-hypertensive subgroup (P = 0.020).

Additionally, we reassessed the above associations using a DAG-based adjustment set (Fig. S11, Supplementary Material 2). The results showed no substantial changes in the main findings: participants in the Low-eGDR and High-ABSI group remained at significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.56, 95% CI 1.26–1.93) and cardiovascular-specific mortality (HR = 1.89, 95% CI 1.18–3.04).

Development and comparison of predictive models

As shown in Fig. 4, for the testing set in predicting all-cause mortality, the XGBoost model achieved the best performance, with an AUC of 0.877 (95% CI 0.863–0.890), demonstrating strong predictive ability. The next best performers were the LGBM and LR models, with AUCs of 0.864 (95% CI 0.849–0.879) and 0.863 (95% CI 0.849–0.877), respectively, showing comparable predictive accuracy. The RF model also performed well, with an AUC of 0.856 (95% CI 0.841–0.871). In contrast, the KNN model (AUC = 0.744, 95% CI 0.725–0.763) and the SVM model (AUC = 0.840, 95% CI 0.825–0.856) demonstrated weaker predictive performance for all-cause mortality (Fig. 4A).

Discussion

In this study, we for the first time evaluated the joint effects of eGDR and ABSI on all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality among individuals with stage 0–4 CKM syndrome. The main findings were as follows: (1) Lower eGDR was significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality and higher ABSI was also closely associated with elevated risks of both outcomes; (2) In the joint-effect analysis, individuals with low eGDR combined with high ABSI exhibited a markedly higher risk of mortality; (3) Subgroup analyses revealed that this joint effect was more pronounced in individuals younger than 65 years; (4) The XGBoost model demonstrated robust predictive performance for both all-cause mortality (AUC = 0.877) and cardiovascular-specific mortality (AUC = 0.850).

As an emerging indicator of IR, eGDR has garnered widespread attention in recent years and its predictive value in CKM syndrome has been confirmed. A study by Tian et al. indicated that eGDR is an important predictor of incident CVD in the CKM syndrome stages 0–3 individuals [30]. Additionally, a machine learning study by Dong et al. demonstrated that, compared with other IR indicators such as the TyG index, TyG-related composite indices and the metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR), eGDR exhibited superior performance in predicting cardiovascular diseases among individuals with stage 0–3 CKM syndrome [31]. Regarding prognosis, higher eGDR levels are closely associated with a lower risk of mortality among individuals with CKM syndrome [32]. Our findings are consistent with previous studies, showing that CKM syndrome individuals with low eGDR levels have a higher risk of all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality.

In the context of CKM syndrome, particularly among individuals with IR, the “obesity paradox” is notably prominent [13, 33, 34]. While WC and WHtR are often cited as superior to BMI in reflecting fat distribution, recent evidence shows they are highly correlated with BMI (r ~ 0.9) [35]. This statistical overlap suggests that, similar to BMI, they cannot effectively distinguish between visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue compartments [17, 36]. As ABSI is less affected by the obesity paradox, it better reflects visceral adiposity and enables additional risk stratification beyond IR indicators, thereby improving cardiometabolic risk identification [16]. Our study found that, compared to the group with ABSI < 0.08, the group with ABSI > 0.08 showed significantly higher all-cause and cardiovascular-related mortality (HR = 1.78 and 2.05, respectively) among individuals with CKM syndrome. This result is consistent with the findings reported by He et al. in a general population study [37]. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that ABSI outperforms other obesity indices (such as BMI, WC and WHtR) in predicting mortality in elderly populations, as well as all-cause and cardiovascular-cerebrovascular mortality in populations with metabolic syndrome [38, 39]. However, comparative analyses within the CKM syndrome population have not been reported. This study revealed that ABSI, both as a standalone indicator and after adjustment in Model 3, has significantly higher predictive value for all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality among individuals with CKM syndrome than other obesity indicators.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that, among individuals with stage 0–4 CKM syndrome, those in the high-ABSI group have significantly higher all-cause and cardiovascular mortality rates than those in the low-ABSI group. Further analysis revealed that the combined effect of low eGDR and high ABSI on these mortality outcomes was significantly stronger than that of either factor alone. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of this combined effect in predicting mortality among individuals with stage 0–3 CKM syndrome. Moreover, in subgroup analyses of participants without hypertension and without diabetes, its predictive value remained significant and stable.

However, our study did not find a significant interaction between eGDR and ABSI. Although in the unadjusted model (Model 1), RERI, AP and SI for both were indicative of a positive additive interaction in both all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality, these effects weakened and became nonsignificant as covariates were gradually included (Model 2, Model 3). CKM syndrome is influenced by multiple risk factors (such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, smoking, chronic inflammation, genetics, etc.), which may attenuate the interaction between eGDR and ABSI [40–42]. However, the NRI analysis indicated that the combination of eGDR and ABSI provided significantly superior predictive ability for mortality risk compared with either index alone. Thus, although a biological interaction between the two may not exist, their integration in clinical predictive modeling could nonetheless enhance predictive performance.

In the subgroup analysis of this study, the combined effect of low eGDR and high ABSI on mortality risk appeared more pronounced among individuals aged ≤ 65 years, showing a higher HR. In contrast, the predictive model identified age > 65 years as the most important predictor of mortality. This discrepancy stems from the fundamental difference between the two analytical approaches: subgroup analysis assesses relative risk, where the extremely low baseline mortality rate in younger individuals amplifies the relative effect of metabolic abnormalities. In older adults, however, baseline risk is already substantially elevated and age itself becomes the primary driver of mortality, thereby diluting the relative contribution of metabolic indicators. In comparison, predictive models focus on the ability to discriminate absolute risk and age—as a core variable integrating multiple cumulative risks—naturally assumes a dominant role in the global model. These findings suggest that metabolic abnormalities in younger individuals may indicate an earlier pathogenic process, warranting timely intervention. In older adults, however, attention should be directed toward comprehensive management of multiple risks, with age as the foundational consideration.

In CKM syndrome, IR significantly enhances the risk of CVD and mortality through its interaction with lipid metabolism abnormalities [43–45]. IR increases the oxidation of free fatty acids (FFA), leading to ectopic fat deposition, lipotoxicity and oxidative stress, which ultimately impairs endothelial function and accelerates the process of atherosclerosis [6, 46, 47]. At the same time, the activation of the neuroendocrine axis, particularly the sympathetic nervous system and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), exacerbates metabolic imbalance and leads to further deterioration of cardiovascular outcomes [48, 49].

However, this study has some limitations. Firstly, as the laboratory indicators of eGDR and ABSI in the NHANES study were only baseline data, they cannot reflect the effects of dynamic changes in eGDR and ABSI on clinical outcomes. Furthermore, although this study used various machine learning methods to select key variables as covariates, we cannot completely exclude unmeasured confounders, such as genetic factors, environmental pollution and lifestyle (diet quality, physical activity, etc.), all of which may affect the study results. In addition, the study population was limited to the American population and further validation in different populations is required. Finally, although this study achieved certain predictive performance for all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality through Lasso regression and multiple machine learning algorithms, it should be noted that all models were developed based on an internal validation framework and lacked validation using external independent cohorts. Consequently, the reported discriminative performance (e.g., ROC-AUC values) may be at risk of overestimation. Future studies are required to conduct external validation in more diverse populations to further confirm the robustness and generalizability of the models.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that both low eGDR and high ABSI are independent predictors of all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality among individuals with CKM syndrome. Their combined use significantly improves risk stratification compared with either measure alone. Moreover, machine learning algorithms, particularly XGBoost, further improved predictive performance when incorporating both eGDR and ABSI. Taken together, both eGDR and ABSI are associated with all-cause and cardiovascular-specific mortality among individuals with CKM syndrome and more strongly so when considered jointly.
