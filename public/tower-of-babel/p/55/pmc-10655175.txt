Re-Engineered Pseudoviruses for Precise and Robust 3D Mapping of Viral Infection
Authors: Marvin Jungblut, Simone Backes, Marcel Streit, Georg Gasteiger, Sören Doose, Markus Sauer, Gerti Beliu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractEngineered vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) pseudotyping offers an essential method for exploring virus–cell interactions, particularly for viruses that require high biosafety levels. Although this approach has been employed effectively, the current methodologies for virus visualization and labeling can interfere with infectivity and lead to misinterpretation of results. In this study, we introduce an innovative approach combining genetic code expansion (GCE) and click chemistry with pseudotyped VSV to produce highly fluorescent and infectious pseudoviruses (clickVSVs). These clickVSVs enable robust and precise virus–cell interaction studies without compromising the biological function of the viral surface proteins. We evaluated this approach by generating VSVs bearing a unique chemical handle for click labeling and assessing the infectivity in relevant cell lines. Our results demonstrate that clickVSVs maintain their infectivity post-labeling and present an efficiency about two times higher in detecting surface proteins compared to classical immunolabeling. The utilization of clickVSVs further allowed us to visualize and track 3D virus binding and infection in living cells, offering enhanced observation of virus–host interactions. Thus, clickVSVs provide an efficient alternative for virus-associated research under the standard biosafety levels.Keywords: Genetic Code Expansion, Virus Research, Click Chemistry, Super-Resolution Microscopy, VSV Pseudotyping

Infectious diseases continue to be a leading cause of death worldwide. Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has emphasized the importance of studying virus–host cell interactions for disease prevention and the development of therapeutics.1 Unfortunately, many pathogens can only be handled under biosafety level 3 or 4 (BSL-3/4) conditions, limiting viral research to a few specialized facilities. However, vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) where the endogenous surface protein G has been replaced with eGFP (VSVΔG-eGFP) can be engineered to carry viral envelope proteins from other pathogens and serve as a potent model to study virus–host cell interactions and screen for neutralizing antibodies or other inhibitors.2 Among all viral components, surface binding proteins are a key factor in viral infections. The specificity of these proteins is decisive for host cell binding and can vary considerably; in the case of SARS-CoV-2 infection, the binding of its spike protein to cellular ACE2 receptors is crucial, whereas other virus species use surface proteins with a comparatively broad tropism, such as the G protein of VSV (Figure 1a).3−5

Pseudotyping involves the transfer of viral surface proteins from a pathogenic virus into a viral vector, allowing safe and isolated handling and study of the protein. In this respect, VSVΔG-eGFP has become a very important tool in the study of viruses and their surface proteins.6−8 Nevertheless, efficient fluorescence labeling of pseudotyped VSV for infection studies, e.g., tracking and mapping of viruses, remains challenging. Classical staining protocols often rely on antigen-recognition of antibodies, which can have neutralizing properties and interfere with infectivity.9 In particular, the labeling of structural components can lead to the loss of specificity and thus usually does not represent a suitable labeling strategy for studying key proteins or protein–protein interactions (PPIs). Pseudotyped VSVΔG-eGFP induces fluorescent protein (FP) expression upon host cell infection (VSVΔG-eGFP). Despite significant efforts to improve visualization by introducing FPs into structural components of virus-like particles (VLPs), efficient labeling of proteins or structural elements of VLPs remains insufficient.10 This is because any labeling of pseudoviruses potentially alters the structure or function of the virus, thereby impeding infectivity and potentially resulting in the misinterpretation of experimental results. Furthermore, the specific and direct labeling of surface proteins ensures the target protein presence. Infectious particle counts are commonly assessed by using plaque assays. The ratio between optically determined total particle counts and infectious particles frequently surpasses 1, suggesting that a considerable proportion of produced viruses are non-infectious.11,12 Although morphologically intact viral particles are posited to exhibit infectivity and therefore enable cellular entry, it has been shown that budding of virus particles can occur in the absence of viral surface proteins.13 Consequently, labeling approaches that do not directly label surface proteins may overestimate infectious particle numbers or result in misinterpretation due to the surface protein absence. Therefore, the development of novel labeling methods for efficient and specific labeling of surface proteins on pseudoviruses without compromising infectivity is of critical importance for unperturbed infection studies.11

In recent years, there has been a growing interest in smaller and non-invasive labeling technologies for protein visualization. Genetic code expansion (GCE) by amber codon suppression allows the introduction of unnatural amino acids (uAA) into target proteins and is currently proving to be a useful tool for minimally invasive labeling of target proteins for functional and super-resolution imaging.14 In this approach, target cells are equipped with an orthogonal tRNA/tRNA synthetase pair that, when combined with a target gene containing a TAG stop codon at a selected position, enables the incorporation of a unique uAA. This amino acid can later be addressed, for example, through click chemistry labeling. This enables efficient site-specific labeling of proteins in crowded environments, e.g., extracellular protein domains in confined compartments or small virus particles by bioorthogonal click labeling with small tetrazine dyes.15−18

Results/Discussion

We combined GCE and VSV pseudotyping to allow synthesis of re-engineered functional VSVs bearing a unique chemical handle for click labeling termed “clickVSV” (Figure 1a). To this aim, different incorporation sites for introduction of uAAs were evaluated (Figure S1). For generation of the uAA insertions, sites were chosen to be in the S1 subdomain region, where they do not interfere with ACE2 binding by altering the receptor-binding domain (RBD) or immunolabeling via antibodies (Figure S1). Hence, each trimeric spike protein contains three uAAs for bioorthogonal click labeling with a tetrazine dye. Among the four mutants, K567TAG facilitated the most effective click labeling and was thus chosen for the generation of pseudotyped VSVΔG-eGFP-SARS-CoV-2-Sclick (clickVSVs) (Figure S1). The infectivity of clickVSVs was assessed using Vero E6 cells endogenously expressing ACE2. Cells were left uninfected (Control) or infected either with an unmodified non-clickable VSVΔG-eGFP-SARS-CoV-2-SWT (WT-VSV) or with clickVSV. Analysis of infection demonstrated that both WT-VSV and clickVSV are effective in binding to and infiltrating target cells and inducing GFP expression. In contrast, the absence of VSV (Control) resulted in no GFP expression (Figure 1b). In addition, the infectivity of the clickVSVs remained unchanged after labeling with tetrazine dyes Tet-Cy5 and Tet-ATTO643 (Figure 1c, Figure S2). Next, we conducted a Pseudovirus Neutralization Assay to compare the inhibition of infection of clickVSVs and WT-VSVs. In this assay, WT-VSV and clickVSV were preincubated with a neutralizing antibody solely (+nAB) or additional soluble ACE2 (+sACE2) to evaluate the neutralization of infectivity. Both WT-VSVs and clickVSVs were efficiently neutralized by nAB and sACE2 and are therefore suitable model systems to study SARS-CoV-2 inhibitors and neutralizing agents (Figure 1d, Figure S2).

To compare the efficiency of click-labeling and immunolabeling of VSVs we conducted two-color direct stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (dSTORM) experiments (Figure 2a, Figure S3).19 Here, clickVSVs were click-labeled with Tet-Cy5 and immunolabeled with a non-neutralizing antibody binding to the S2 fragment of the S protein (α-spike-S2-CF568). We employed particle averaging to assess virus morphology and a density-based spatial clustering of applications (DBSCAN) algorithm, combined with a customized localization analysis (LOCAN) to determine the labeling density and the distribution of click-sites and S proteins on the viral surface (Figure 2b–d).20 This allowed us to extract viral shapes and determine the number of spike proteins detected with the two labeling methods from two-color dSTORM experiments (Figure 2c). Our results indicate that the number of S proteins detected by click-labeling is approximately two times higher compared to classical immunolabeling of the same virus population, implying a consistent underestimation of virus surface proteins when stained with antibodies (Figure 2c,d).

Since bioorthogonal labeling does not impede or neutralize viral infection, clickVSVs represent an excellent method for investigations of viral infections in both fixed and living cells. We employed fixed-cell confocal microscopy and live-cell lattice-light-sheet (LLS) microscopy to examine the ability of clickVSV to visualize viral binding and infection (Figure 3a, Figure S4). Live-cell LLS microscopy allowed us to visualize clickVSV infection of HEK293T:ACE2-mGreenLantern cells in three dimensions (3D) for more than 1 h (Supplementary Video 1). In addition, we performed single-particle tracking experiments to study the trajectories of individual clickVSVs (Figure 3b–e). The mobility of viruses was evident in both the click and antibody-labeled channel (Figure 3b), as compared to fluorescent beads suspended in agarose, serving as static controls (Figure 3e). While mean track lengths and individual diffusion constants are similar, more tracks were identified in the click channel compared to those in the antibody channel (Figure 3d). This finding can be explained by the lower labeling density of viruses with antibodies resulting in lower signal-to-noise ratios and ultimately in the imperceptibility of virus particles. Furthermore, due to the smaller fluorophore number per VSV with immunostaining, photobleaching can get more critical and therefore explain the reduced number of detected full-length tracks in the antibody channel (Figure 3c,d). Although the 3D trajectories were in close alignment in both channels, confirming that both labeling methods indeed provide consistent trajectories and virus paths, clickVSVs generally facilitated improved and prolonged observation of virus–host interactions and, thus, more precise and robust 3D virus tracking (Figure 3d,e).

Conclusions

In summary, clickVSVs represent a valuable approach for studying viral infection and screening for antiviral agents. The potential applications of clickVSVs include neutralization assays, the investigation of viral tropism and binding receptors, and the analysis of virus–host interactions. The combination of GCE and VSV pseudotyping is simple, efficient, and cost-effective and can be easily adapted for other viral surface proteins or mutations, making it highly relevant for virus-associated research under the standard biosafety level.

Methods/Experimentals

Molecular Biology

Plasmid amplification was performed via transformation in E. coli (XL1-Blue) and DNA isolation via MIDI-prep (Macherey and Nagel, NucleoBond Xtra Midi, #740410). The respective amber stop mutants were generated by introducing a TAG codon through PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis. To identify the best positions for the insertion of uAAs into the S protein, four amber mutant constructs were generated: K544TAG, K567TAG, S713TAG, and K1095TAG.

Cell Culture

Vero E6 (CLS, 305008) and HEK293T (DSMZ, ACC635) cells were cultivated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in T25-culture flasks (Thermo Scientific, #156340) with DMEM (Dulbeccos’s Modified Eagle’s Medium, Sigma-Aldrich, #D5796) supplemented with 10% FCS (Sigma-Aldrich, #F7524), 1% l-glutamine, and 1% Pen-Strep (Sigma-Aldrich, #P4333). For imaging experiments cells were seeded in CellVis (CellVis, #C8-1.5H-N) 8-well chamber slides at 2.5 × 104 c/w for HEK293T and 1.5 × 104 c/w for Vero cells. Chamber slides for HEK293T cells were coated with 0.01 g/L poly-d-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich, no. P6407) for 1 h at room temperature (RT) prior to seeding; slides for Vero cells are left untreated.

Transfection

HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with jetPRIME (Polypus, no. 114-01) at 60–80% confluency according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded on poly-d-lysine coated coverslips 1 day before transfection. For each well 250 ng of DNA was used, and in coexpression of Sclick and pNEU-hMbPylRS-4xU6M15, a total of 500 ng of DNA in a 1:1 ratio was used. Additionally, in transfection with Sclick, cells were supplemented with 250 μM uAAs (TCO*A, SiChem GmbH, #SC-8008) diluted 1:4 in HEPES buffer. Cells were then incubated for 12–36 h before labeling and fixation.

Pseudotyping of VSV

For the generation of VSVΔG-eGFP-SARS-CoV-2-SWT (WT-VSV), HEK293T cells were transfected with a plasmid expressing the wild-type Spike sequence (Wuhan/Hu-1/2019 isolate). For the generation of VSVΔG-eGFP-SARS-CoV-2-Sclick (clickVSV), HEK293T cells were co-transfected with a plasmid expressing the Spike sequence (Wuhan/Hu-1/2019 isolate) containing the K567TAG mutation and with a plasmid expressing tRNA/tRNA-synthetase pairs. Additionally, cells were supplemented with 250 μM uAAs diluted 1:4 in a HEPES buffer. Spike sequences contain a C-terminal truncation of 18 amino acid residues. Cells were transfected at 60–80% confluency using jetPRIME according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 24 h post-transfection, cells were inoculated with a replication-deficient VSV vector pseudotyped with VSV-G (MOI 3) that contains expression cassettes for eGFP and firefly luciferase instead of the VSV-G open reading frame. After 1 h incubation, the inoculum was removed, cells were washed with PBS, and medium supplemented 1:1000 with anti-VSV-G antibody (Kerafast, EB0010) was added to neutralize residual input VSV-G virus. For the generation clickVSV, the medium was additionally supplemented with 250 μM uAAs diluted 1:4 in HEPES buffer. Pseudotyped particles in the supernatants were harvested 18 h post-inoculation and clarified from cellular debris by centrifugation.

Labeling and Purification of Click VSV

Virions were concentrated from clickVSV containing supernatants by ultracentrifugation (5 h at 21.000g). Pellets were resuspended in 10 mM Tris (pH 9) and labeled with 25 μM methyl-tetrazine-Sulfo-Cy5 (Jena Bioscience, #CLK-1019) or pyrimidyl-tetrazine-ATTO643 (Jena Bioscience, #CLK-101) for 1 h at RT. To remove unincorporated dye, the labeled clickVSV was banded on a 15–45% sucrose gradient (5 h at 21.000g). The clickVSV containing band was collected, resuspended in 10 mM Tris (pH 9), and pelleted for 5 h at 21.000g. Final clickVSV pellets were resuspended in 10 mM Tris (pH 9), aliquoted in 10 μL fractions, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

Infection Assays

Vero E6 cells were infected with VSV 1 day after seeding. For regular infection in 8-well chamber slides, 2.5 μL of purified VSVs was added per well to cells in 250 μL of total volume resulting in a 1:100 dilution of VSV in culture medium. Infections were carried out for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. For blocking assays, VSV solution was first added 1:40 to DMEM, mixed thoroughly, and then split into three equal fractions. One fraction was left untreated and served as a control for regular infection; the two others were incubated with either anti-SARS-CoV-2 Spike RBD (Sigma-Aldrich, #MABF2797) added 1:100 or a mixture of 1:100 concentration anti-SARS-CoV-2 Spike RBD and 4 ng/μL soluble ACE2-biotin (Sigma-Aldrich, #SAE0171). All three fractions were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. For infection, the culture medium on Vero cells was removed and replaced with a 2:5 mixture of preincubated VSV solution and new culture medium, to obtain a 1:100 ratio of purified VSV solution to cell culture medium. Cells were then washed with 37 °C warm PBS three times and fixed with methanol-free 4% formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #28906). After three PBS washing steps, nuclear staining with a 1:5000 dilution of Hoechst-34580 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. H21486) in PBS was carried out for 1 h. Three additional washing steps with PBS followed. Samples were then stored at 4 °C until imaging.

Infection Assay Imaging

To determine the number of infected cells, whole-well tile-scans were taken on a Zeiss Elyra 7 lattice SIM using a 10× (Zeiss, EC Plan-Neofluar 10×/0.3) objective. For excitation of Hoechst, a 405 nm laser diode was used, and a 488 nm laser diode was used for GFP excitation. Appropriate bandpass filters were selected for each channel. Tile-scans were stitched and processed in ZEN 3.0 SR FP2 (black) (Zeiss, Version 16.0.10.306) with SIM and SIM2 processing modules. Further analysis was performed using a custom ImageJ macro for recognizing and counting cells in both channels. Infection data were analyzed and plotted in OriginPro2021b (OriginLab).

Antibody Labeling with NHS Dyes

Primary mouse anti-S2-Spike antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-35946) was transferred into 100 mM NaHCO3 in PBS utilizing Zeba Spin Desalting Columns 40K MWCO (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. 87766) according to the manufacturer suggested protocol. Antibody–dye coupling was performed using a 10-fold molar excess of CF568 NHS-ester (Biotium, #92131) for 3 h in the dark at room temperature. Labeled antibodies were separated from free dye by purification with Zeba Spin Desalting Columns. Directly conjugated primary anti-S2-Spike antibodies showed a DOL (degree of labeling) of ∼3.2.

Confocal Fluorescence Imaging

Confocal imaging was performed with a Zeiss LSM980 (Airyscan 2) using a 63×/1.4 oil objective. For construct comparison, HEK293T cells expressing various Spike mutants were stained with 1.5 μM Tet-Cy5 for 10 min at 37 °C. For fixed infection time-series imaging, HEK293T cells were transfected with hACE2-mGreenLantern as described and then infected at 37 °C with VSVΔG-SCy5 added 1:20 for 5–60 min. Cells were transferred onto ice, washed three times with PBS, and fixed with 4% formaldehyde. Images were captured with suitable lasers and filters for Cy5 and mGreenLantern. Imaging settings were kept constant when different constructs were compared. Final images are displayed at the same brightness and contrast settings. The display lookup table (LUT) is linear and covers the full range of the data.

Sample Preparation for dSTORM Imaging

12 mm coverslips were first cleaned by sonication in ddH2O, 2% Hellmanex (Sigma-Aldrich, #Z805939-1EA), 1 M KOH, and 100% ethanol and then dried under a sterile clean bench. Prior to seeding, the coverslips were coated with 0.01 g/L poly-d-lysine for 1 h at room temperature. VSVs were then diluted 1:10 in PBS and transferred onto coated coverslips for 1–2 h at 37 °C. After two washing steps with PBS, VSVs were fixed on the surface for 15 min with 4% formaldehyde (methanol-free). Samples were again washed with PBS and blocked for 30 min with 5% BSA in PBS. Incubation with 5–10 μg/mL α-Spike-S2-CF568 was performed in 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at RT. After three further washing steps of 10 min each, the sample was post-fixed with 4% formaldehyde (methanol-free) for 10 min at RT and then washed again. Coverslips were transferred into custom-built 12 mm coverslip holders. Then, the dSTORM imaging buffer was added to the coverslips. In order to determine the localizations expected per Spike associated Cy5 and Spike bound α-Spike-S2-CF568 separate dSTORM measurements on sparsely labeled HEK293T:Sclick cells were performed. Cells were seeded and transfected as described, then co-stained with 0.015–0.15 μM Tet-Cy5 and 0.05–0.5 μg/mL α-Spike-S2-CF568 for 1 h on ice, washed three times, and fixed with 4% formaldehyde. Samples were stored in PBS until imaging, when PBS was replaced by dSTORM buffer.

dSTORM Imaging

Two-color dSTORM images were acquired on an inverted wide-field fluorescence microscope (Olympus, IX-71) with a 639 nm diode laser (Coherent, Genesis MX639-1000; Chroma) and a 561 nm diode laser (Coherent, Genesis MX561-500) for excitation of Cy5 and CF568, respectively. The lasers were spectrally cleaned (Chroma, Laser Clean-up filter 640/10 and 561/14) overlaid by a LaserMUX filter (Semrock, LM01-552), widened, and focused on the back-focal plane of an oil immersion objective (Olympus, APON 60× NA 1.45) by a lens assembly of a 25 mm (Qioptiq, G322284000) and a 120 mm (Qioptiq, G322303000) focal achromatic lens. Emission light was separated from excitation light by a polychromatic mirror (Chroma, FF410/504/582/669). Cy5 and CF568 emission was split and filtered by a dichroic beam splitter (Chroma, 630dcxr) and two bandpass filters (Shemrock, HC679/41 and BP607/70) mounted in a dual-camera adaptor (Andor, TuCam). For detection, two EMCCD cameras (Andor, iXon Ultra 897) were used with a measured pixel size of 128 nm for the Cy5 channel and 132 nm for the CF568 channel. Prior to two-color dSTORM imaging both cameras were aligned by centering fluorescent beads in the FOV of both cameras. Imaging was performed in 100 mM MEA (Sigma-Aldrich, #M6500) in PBS at a pH of 7.5–7.7. For both channels 25.000 frames were captured at 20 ms exposure time in epifluorescence at an illumination intensity of approximately 3 kW/cm2. To avoid bleaching of Cy5 by 561 nm light the Cy5 channel was captured first. Image reconstruction was carried out by analyzing the image series with rapidSTORM 3.3.22 To correct for chromatic aberration between Cy5 and CF568 channels five to ten series of 100 frames of fluorescent microspheres (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #T7279) with 100 ms exposure time were captured after dSTORM imaging and reconstructed with rapidSTORM 3.3. Elastic transformations were then created using the ImageJ plugin bUnwarpJ23 and applied to the dSTORM images. Localization data was binned to 10 nm (pixelsize), and bit-depth was scale from 0–106 ADC (analog-to-digital converter) counts.

dSTORM Image and Particle Analysis

dSTORM data was analyzed with python scripts making use of the Locan library20 and OriginPro 2021b. Localization clusters representing individual VSV particles were identified using all localizations from two-color measurements with click-labels and immunolabels by DBSCAN with minPts = 20 and epsilon = 75. For each cluster representing a VSV particle, the convex hull region was computed, and localization counts or densities were determined for each channel separately on identical VSV regions. The elongation of VSV particles was estimated from a combination of inertia moments and the oriented bounding box and used for selecting the elongated VSV particles. In addition, titrated HEK293T:Sclick measurements were analyzed under sparse labeling conditions. The mean number of localizations per VSV was then divided by the mean number of localizations per cluster under sparse labeling conditions in order to quantify the number of S proteins per identified virion.

Lattice-Light-Sheet Imaging and Particle Tracking

For single particle tracking and live cell imaging experiments, HEK cells were seeded and transfected with hACE2-mGreenLantern as previously described. One day after transfection, culture chambers were transferred onto a Zeiss Lattice Lightsheet 7, equipped with two sCMOS cameras (Hamamatsu, ORCA-Fusion C14440) and a high-power laser module. The imaging chamber was preheated to 37 °C with 6% CO2 atm. Illumination was achieved with a dithered sinc3 light-sheet (length, 30 μm; width, 1000 nm). Volumetric time-lapse imaging was performed on hACE2-mGreenLantern transfected HEK293T cells by adding 2.5 μL of purified VSVΔG-eGFP-SARS-CoV-2Cy5 directly before imaging. Volumes of 70–100 μm in range were scanned at 2 min intervals for 60 min. Emission light was filtered by appropriate band-pass filters and detected sequentially on the same camera. Image data was deconvolved, deskewed, and coverglass-transformed in Zen blue 3.7 (Zeiss, Version 3.7.2) by constrained iterative deconvolution. Final images and movies were processed using Imaris 9.2.1 and corrected for photobleaching over a full acquisition time. The LUTs are magenta (clickVSV) and gray (ACE2). Particle tracking experiments were performed with hACE2-mGreenLantern transfected HEK293T cells prepared as described. Purified VSVΔG-eGFP-SARS-CoV-2ATTO643 was incubated with 5 μg/mL mouse anti-S2-Spike-CF568 for 1 h at 37 °C and then washed twice by centrifugation and exchange of supernatant. Infection was carried out on stage by adding an equivalent virion concentration, as described above. Imaging was started immediately following the addition of VSVs. mGreenLantern-Membrane staining was excited by 488 nm and detected on the second sCMOS. Emission light from ATTO643 was also detected on cMOS camera one in the same track as mGreenLantern, and the CF568 signal was detected on camera one in a separate track, resulting in a sequential readout of the emission channels which are later tracked. Volumes of 20–30 μm scan range were imaged at a sample rate of 0.2 μm per step with an integration time of 2–3 ms; hence, these volumes could be imaged once every 1–3 s over a time frame of 15–30 min. Acquired data was deconvolved and deskewed in Zen blue 3.7 by constrained iterative deconvolution. Individual channels were loaded into Fiji, processed, and localized utilizing a custom macro. Virions are localized in 3D utilizing the Fiji plugin Clij224 and performed as follows: Z-stacks of individual time points are extracted, filtered for background, and smoothed by a difference of Gaussian; the maximum of a cubic box is then detected, and its position in x, y, and z is extracted into a separate image file of the same voxel size. The image containing the localized maxima is converted into a .csv file by a python script and then tracked in SWIFT.25,26 Ensemble mean square displacement curves, log10(D) histograms, track length histograms, and 2D track visualization were generated in a custom python script, based on the python scientific stack, trackpy, and the LOCAN library.20 To establish a static control for tracking experiments, fluorescent microspheres were suspended in 2% agarose, seeded in CellVis chamber slides, and imaged using identical settings.
