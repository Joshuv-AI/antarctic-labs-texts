Crystallization Improvements in Studtite by Vapor Diffusion Synthesis
Authors: Nicholas Kaitschuck, Jordan M. Roach, Daniel E. Felton, Tanya Hutter, Sheldon Landsberger, Andrew Miskowiec, Tyler L. Spano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Studtite is a common nuclear fuel cycle material that has been the focus of considerable research due to its role as a uranium ore concentrate. In a laboratory setting, studtite is commonly synthesized via the addition of liquid-phase hydrogen peroxide to an aqueous uranyl nitrate solution. The product produced from this process is a very fine, pale-yellow powder with crystals of ∼0.1 μm in their largest dimension. In this work, we implement a small modification to this process by introducing hydrogen peroxide in the vapor phase. This change eliminates mechanical agitation and increases crystallization time, resulting in crystals measuring 10–20 μm. Due to the thermal decomposition mechanism of studtite, this increased crystal size is preserved through heating, leading to large crystals of both metastudtite and α-UO3. Raman spectroscopy and powder X-ray diffraction are used to determine crystallographic information and structures, and scanning electron microscopy is used to visually compare samples produced via the traditional method and our vapor diffusion method. The samples obtained using this technique open the door to future investigations in which large single crystals are required for full structural characterization.

Introduction

Studtite (uranyl peroxide tetrahydrate, UO2O2·4H2O) is a common uranium ore concentrate (UOC) − that garnered significant interest in early studies due to its use as a precursor in the synthesis of a variety of uranium oxides. − In recent years, studtite has been at the center of numerous morphological studies owing to its easily observable characteristic features. − These investigations have produced material following the basic method originally outlined by Watt et al., in which liquid-phase hydrogen peroxide is added to a solution containing uranyl ions. This method was later standardized by Burns and Hughes to the dropwise addition of 30% H2O2 to a 0.5 M aqueous uranyl nitrate solution in equal volumes. Immediately, a yellow-white powder (studtite) precipitates from solution, which, after being allowed to settle, is rinsed with water and vacuum-filtered. Since then, many studtite-related studies have used this method with only slight variations in the concentration of uranyl nitrate (0.1 < M < 1.0). ,− The rapid introduction of peroxide and the mechanical disturbance it creates causes the formation of crystals with poorly defined, jagged edges that measure only a few tenths of a micrometer along the longest dimension. While these samples are useful to many researchers, Siegel et al. have noted situations in which larger single crystals would be beneficial, such as in the study of α-UO3.

Due to its role as a UOC, discussion of studtite is practically incomplete without also discussing its thermal decomposition products, particularly metastudtite (uranyl peroxide dihydrate, UO2O2·2H2O) and α-UO3. While metastudtite is simply a dehydrated phase of studtite, α-UO3 is a common intermediate material in the nuclear fuel cycle whose complex structural characteristics and signatures have perplexed researchers for decades, and much debate still exists. − Cordfunke and van der Giessen first observed the pseudomorphic thermal decomposition of studtite to α-UO3, noting that the size and shape of studtite crystals are preserved through calcination. In this work, we demonstrate this effect on large crystals of studtite, leading to large crystals of the oxide phase, which could be used in future single-crystal diffraction studies, which are required for further understanding of these materials.

A new study has proposed the introduction of impurities, or “taggants”, to nuclear fuels to act as a fingerprint for a specific batch of nuclear material. These taggants are added early in the fuel cycle by mixing small quantities of transition metal nitrates (Fe, Cr, Ni) with standard uranyl nitrate solutions, then precipitating “tagged” studtite quickly via the addition of liquid hydrogen peroxide. In theory, this allows for a nearly inseparable forensic signature as the transition metal atoms could become part of the larger crystal structure; however, in practice, certain elements show signs of inhomogeneous distribution in tagged samples. Increased crystallization time could lead to better incorporation of taggant elements in studtite and provide a more resilient forensic signature.

In this work, we demonstrate the successful application of the vapor diffusion synthesis (VDS) method by producing studtite with significant crystal size improvements (>20 μm). To show the drastic effect that VDS has on crystal size and shape, we synthesize studtite by exposing four different concentrations of uranyl nitrate solution to vapor phase hydrogen peroxide. The morphology and chemical composition of VDS studtite are compared to studtite synthesized via the traditional liquid–liquid method described by Burns and Hughes and show significant improvement in crystal size. These larger, slowly grown crystals not only show promise for future investigations into studtite, but also for another common nuclear fuel cycle material which forms upon its thermal decomposition: α-UO3.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of Studtite and Decomposition to α-UO3

Caution! Isotopically depleted uranium (238U) was used in this synthesis, following appropriate techniques and controls for handling radioactive materials.

Four vials were partially filled with 10 mL of aqueous uranyl nitrate solutions varying in concentration: 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 M. These vials were then placed in a large crystallization dish, and a quantity of 30% H2O2 (Fisher) was added to the dish such that the level of the liquid did not exceed the rims of the vials and liquid–liquid interactions were avoided. The crystallization dish was then covered with Parafilm to allow for the buildup of hydrogen peroxide vapors within the system. The temperature of the laboratory was approximately 27 °C for the course of exposure, leading to a saturated vapor pressure of ∼0.3 kPa. After 7 days, a buildup of pale, yellow powder (studtite) was observed collecting at the bottom of each vial. The contents of each vial were recovered by vacuum filtration and slightly washed with deionized water to remove unreacted nitrate solution. The resulting powder appeared as a yellow-white, cakey powder, consistent with the reported characteristics of synthetic studtite, and Raman spectra (Section 3.1) of these powders corroborated its structure.

A portion of the studtite precipitated from the 0.5 M uranyl nitrate solution was set aside to produce α-UO3 in order to observe the pseudomorphic decomposition in these larger crystals. This studtite was first dehydrated at 80 °C for 2 h to form metastudtite, which presented as a dry, yellow-white powder. , The metastudtite was then heated at 550 °C for 3 h to produce α-UO3, which was confirmed by Raman spectroscopy and PXRD. Throughout the rest of the work, materials produced via vapor diffusion synthesis will be referred to as “VDS” (i.e., VDS studtite, VDS α-UO3). To create a sample for crystal size and shape comparison, studtite and α-UO3 were synthesized by the methods described by Burns and Hughes and Kaitschuck et al., respectively; however, the grinding step reported in the latter work was omitted here to prevent this mechanical disturbance from affecting the sample morphology. , These traditionally synthesized samples will be referred to as TS (i.e., TS studtite, TS α-UO3).

Scanning Electron Microscopy

At each stage of this synthesis (studtite, metastudtite, and α-UO3), scanning electron micrographs were collected to compare the morphological changes imposed by our new synthesis method (VDS) relative to TS. A Zeiss Gemini 460 field emission scanning electron microscope, equipped with two energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometers (Bruker), was used to acquire these images. Electron currents and accelerating voltages are indicated in each figure shown in the following sections. Particle size distributions were calculated using ImageJ.

Studtite and metastudtite samples experienced significant charging in the electron beam, and samples of these specific materials were observed to be physically changing during scanning due to charging. Therefore, images of these materials proved to be of low visual quality and are included in only a minor quantity in this work. Due to the pseudomorphic thermal decomposition of studtite, morphological features observed in α-UO3 are instead included as they are representative of the same features in studtite and metastudtite.

Powder X-ray Diffraction

A Proto AXRD benchtop diffractometer was used to collect powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns in Bragg–Brentano configuration with a Cu–Kα source (λ = 1.5406 Å). Patterns were collected from 15 to 60° 2θ° with a step size of 0.02° and a dwell time of 30 s per step. A 1 mm slit was used to reduce axial divergence. Prior to data collection, samples were lightly ground with a small amount of NIST 640e silicon powder to act as an internal standard for zero-shift corrections. Rietveld refinement of PXRD data was performed in GSAS II, and the results and refinement parameters are shown in Figures S1 and S2.

Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectra were collected using a Renishaw inVia Raman microspectrometer with a 785 nm excitation wavelength. A holographic notch filter allows sensitivity down to 35 cm–1, and a diffraction grating of 1200 l/mm was used. Spectra were collected at three points on our sample and averaged together to ensure a representative sampling of our material. At each point, 10 accumulations were taken using a laser power of 0.03 mW and an exposure time of 10 s.

Because of limitations in equipment availability during this experiment, a portion of the Raman data used in this study was collected on a second instrument. A Photothermal mIRage was also used to collect Raman spectra using a 532 nm laser at a power of 0.4 mW. At three locations, 50 spectra were collected using an integration time of one second. No direct comparisons are made with data taken on different instruments, and the instrument used for each measurement is indicated, where the data are presented.

Data Analysis

All data analysis, such as normalization, smoothing, and peak fitting, was performed in OriginPro. Raman spectra were smoothed using the Savitzky–Golay (SG) method with a window size of 8 and a polynomial order of 3. Graph figures were created in the same software. Intensity normalization of data was performed after SG smoothing. No editing was performed on scanning electron microscope images apart from resizing and cropping.

Results and Discussion

Chemical Confirmation of Studtite and α-UO3

Band locations in the Raman spectra of VDS studtite produced in solutions of 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 M uranyl nitrate all matched well with TS studtite spectra from recent investigations (Figure ). , Little difference is observed between the Raman spectra for the four samples produced via VDS; however, these spectra have a considerably lower baseline than is observed in the spectrum of TS studtite. The band shape of both the lower-energy ν­(UO2 2+) mode and higher-energy ν­(OO) mode possesses considerably lower Lorentzian contribution (Table ), suggesting less influence from vacancy or defect scattering and improved crystallinity. , Additionally, for the VDS samples, the FWHM of each peak decreases as the concentration of uranyl nitrate decreases, although this trend is rather weak. It is also worth noting that VDS samples had a propensity to present with contributions from uranyl nitrate hexahydrate (UNH) at various locations (Figure S3). In the TS method, a significant excess of peroxide is used, typically leading to few, if any, free uranyl ions remaining in solution. However, in the VDS method, the reaction takes place under conditions in which uranyl nitrate is in severe excess, leading to the recrystallization of some quantity of UNH. While this impurity could likely be removed through thorough rinsing with water, this proved difficult to do in practice as we did not want to mechanically disturb any crystals or morphological features. Despite this small impurity, the upcoming sections show that it had little effect on thermal decomposition products.

The primary goal of this work is to introduce this method as a tool for future researchers to use in investigations of studtite and its thermal decomposition products. Therefore, for the remainder of this work, we will focus only on a comparison between TS materials (formed via the precipitation of studtite from the addition of hydrogen peroxide to 0.5 M uranyl nitrate) and VDS material formed via the vapor phase introduction of hydrogen peroxide to 0.5 M uranyl nitrate. This ensures that differences which arise (such as crystal size) do so only due to differences in peroxide introduction methodnot uranyl nitrate concentration.

The Raman spectrum collected for the decomposition product of VDS (from 0.5 M uranyl nitrate) matches well with the most recently reported spectra of α-UO3 (Figure ). Interpretation of this spectrum has been explored elsewhere. , In this work, we merely use this information as a strong indicator that we have obtained our desired material. The spectra we acquired in this study appear to have a noticeably lower baseline than previously reported, suggesting that a higher degree of crystallinity may be present in the VDS α-UO3 sample. Notably, the shoulders on the strong peak at 572 cm–1 are more well defined, and the complex feature from 800 to 900 cm–1 is better resolved relative to α-UO3 formed from thermal decomposition of TS studtite. When heating uranyl peroxide, amorphous UO3 (Am-UO3) forms at 180 °C and remains until around 500 °C, and α-UO3 forms at only slightly higher temperatures (550 °C). , This narrow temperature range leads to the possibility that some amorphous content is normally present in samples of α-UO3. The lower baseline observed in the VDS α-UO3 sample could also suggest that Am-UO3 formation is a consequence of poor crystallinity found in typical TS studtite precursors.

The PXRD pattern of α-UO3 synthesized from VDS confirms that our material possesses an identical structure to α-UO3 synthesized from TS. The locations of observed reflections match well, and the intensity of said peaks is generally in very good agreement when comparing the two α-UO3 products (Figure ). Slight differences in the intensity of some reflections are likely due to preferred orientation, as the long, needle-like crystals in our VDS sample may pack together in an undesirable ordered manner. Rietveld refinement of each powder pattern was performed (using the structure of Kaitschuck et al. as a starting point), and crystallographic information pertaining to the unit cell, domain size, and strain is shown in Table . While it is impossible to entirely decouple the effects that could lead to reflection broadening, the increase in axial length of α-UO3 of 2 orders of magnitude coincides well with observations from scanning electron microscopy, which will be discussed later.

Upon heating to ∼550 °C, UNH decomposes to form β-UO3. Despite the contributions from UNH observed in the Raman spectrum of VDS studtite, no evidence of the β- phase is present in either the Raman spectrum or PXRD pattern of α-UO3 prepared from VDS, suggesting that the UNH phase contributions to VDS studtite were a very small part of the composition. Given that Raman spectroscopy is a surface technique, it is very likely that this UNH formed as only a very small layer on the exterior of the studtite crystals. With the two sets of data presented here for VDS α-UO3, we feel confident in asserting that using vapor diffusion does not alter the final chemical form of α-UO3.

Crystallization Improvements

Multiple studies have recently explored or noted the morphological features of studtite and its thermal decomposition products. , The needle size in these reports is generally very consistent, with small crystals <1 μm in lengthconsiderably smaller than those observed in Figure . As mentioned previously, our studtite and metastudtite samples unfortunately experienced significant charging during imaging, leading to poor quality images that hindered investigation at higher magnification. Despite this limitation, some details are observable in our images, and it is clear that VDS has altered the crystal size of our precipitation product, and this morphology is preserved to the first thermal decomposition: metastudtite. The individual crystals appear as long (∼10–20 μm) fibrous strands that pack together to form a large conglomeration. It is likely that the individual strands are not fused together forming a single particle, as movement of these fibrous strands was observed between images collected by the SEM as the sample charged.

SEM images of VDS studtite and metastudtite taken at 1 k× magnification.

Unlike its uranyl peroxide precursors, α-UO3 does not experience charging, and images of both TS and VDS α-UO3 can be taken at much higher magnifications to explore a more detailed morphology (Figure ). It is apparent that the features observed in the VDS studtite and metastudtite are mostly maintained upon the final calcination to α-UO3. The improvement in final crystal size of VDS α-UO3 is striking compared to the TS α-UO3 sample. In the TS sample, the blocky, columnar crystals are on the order of a few hundred nanometers and do not become obvious until viewed at high (∼25 k×) magnification (Figure ). In contrast, the large, fibrous VDS α-UO3 conglomerate particle observed at just 500× shows individual columnar crystals nearly 2 orders of magnitude larger (Figure ). Upon closer inspection of these crystals in VDS α-UO3, we observe the characteristic pores of α-UO3, and they appear to be slightly larger in sizelikely due to the large amount of gas needing to escape from these larger-volume crystals. , Small cracks appear to form along the edge of the crystals, suggesting internal strain within the material as it decomposes from studtite to α-UO3, although these features could also be due to mechanical disturbances during sample preparation and transport. Surface debris is absent from these crystals, suggesting that any recrystallized UNH on the surface of our initial studtite precipitant melted or was otherwise removed during calcination. ,

The observed axial crystal size distributions measured in Figure match well with the values obtained via Rietveld refinement in Table . This not only confirms that VDS improves product particle size but also coherent crystallite domain size. When these larger, longer particles are viewed under polarized light, complete extinction is observed, further indicating single-crystal characteristics.

The large, blocky needles formed via VDS are similar in morphology to studtite formed in liquid–liquid reactions where hydrogen peroxide is not in severe excess to uranium, although much higher concentrations of uranyl nitrate (2.0 M) are normally needed to induce a morphology similar to the one documented here (0.5 M). The appearance of similar crystals in liquid–liquid interactions does suggest that increased crystal growth is not promoted by the removal of mechanical agitation but rather by the low concentration of peroxide. Under these low saturation index conditions, the nucleation of new crystals is not favorable, leading to increased availability of reactants for existing crystal growth. In contrast to liquid–liquid interactions in which peroxide is a finite resource, VDS allows for the continuous replenishment of peroxide to solution, potentially allowing for the crystallization to proceed under ideal conditions until uranium concentrations change significantly. Similar low-peroxide conditions are found surrounding nuclear fuel (UO2) in aqueous environments, where the decay of radioactive isotopes leads to the production of hydrogen peroxide via radiolysis. Multiple studies have documented this phenomenon and the resulting needle-like studtite formation; − however, the crystals formed from this process are often too small or unsatisfactory for analytical use.

While crystal size has undoubtedly improved with VDS, several changes to this synthesis could be made in future studies to produce even larger specimens. The key idea of this method is to increase the reaction time between hydrogen peroxide and uranyl solution. Therefore, lowering the vapor pressure of hydrogen peroxide via cooling could lead to a significantly lower atmospheric vapor concentration, lowering the reaction speed, and producing even larger crystals than were observed in this work. Additionally, the large cracks and pores which appear during decomposition of studtite could pose issues for single-crystal study, as they may induce crystallographic disorder. These cracks are likely formed due to the high heating rate applied when the metastudtite is introduced to the preheated 550 °C furnace. We propose that these are likely avoidable features in our material by simply using a slower heating profile to allow for slower gas release and longer relaxation times. Although beyond the scope of this work, parametric studies of studtite crystallization kinetics could lead to further improvements in the quality of crystalline materials within this system.

Finally, the authors would like to recognize the similarities between our work and that reported by Benjamin et al. In their work, they also demonstrate a peroxide vapor interaction to form studtite; however, their vapor is instead induced via nonthermal plasma. Their method occurs rapidly, only taking 150 min to complete, and the small area focused by the plasma beam likely creates a nonuniform reaction interface. While similar, our two studies have significantly different intentions, where theirs focuses on providing a proof of concept for an alternative to in situ leaching, and ours aims to study the thermal decomposition products of studtite in greater detail.

Conclusions

While vapor diffusion synthesis is not a new technique, its application in precipitating large studtite crystals is. We observe that crystal sizes increase by nearly 2 orders of magnitude compared to traditional methods, opening up the possibility of single-crystal diffraction studies to future investigators. Due to the pseudomorphic thermal decomposition of studtite, obtaining large crystals of studtite means obtaining large crystals of its calcination products: metastudtite and α-UO3. Both of these materials are important nuclear fuel cycle materials, and α-UO3 in particular has numerous open questions in the literature. The traditional liquid–liquid synthesis of studtite will certainly remain commonplace as it excels at quickly producing large quantities of pure phase material; however, in situations where sample quality and crystallization are paramount, vapor diffusion synthesis should be considered.
