Relationship between the combination of platelet count and neutrophil‐lymphocyte ratio and prognosis of patients with advanced non‐small cell lung cancer treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors plus chemotherapy: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Saeko Kashimura, Miki Sato, Takahito Inagaki, Masaoki Kin, Ryo Manabe, Sojiro Kusumoto, Atsushi Horiike, Takuya Tsunoda, Mari Kogo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between the combination of platelet count and neutrophil‐lymphocyte ratio (COP‐NLR) and prognosis in patients with advanced non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treated with immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) combination therapy with chemotherapy remains unclear. Thus, we investigated prognostic factors, including the COP‐NLR, to identify patients who could benefit from the therapeutic efficacy of ICI combination therapy for advanced NSCLC. Furthermore, we evaluated the relationship between the COP‐NLR score during ICI combination therapy and treatment response.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 88 patients with NSCLC who initially received ICI combination therapy. The primary outcome was overall survival (OS). The prognostic factors were extracted using the Cox proportional hazards model. The relationship between COP‐NLR score at 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy and a good response (complete response [CR] and partial response [PR]) to treatment was analyzed using the chi‐square test.ResultsThe median OS was 15.7 months. In the multivariable analysis, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS) 2, distant metastatic sites ≥2, and baseline COP‐NLR scores of 1, 2 were extracted as significant poor prognostic factors. The proportion of patients with CR and PR in the 3‐week COP‐NLR score of 0 group was significantly higher than that in scores of 1, 2 group.ConclusionsBaseline COP‐NLR, ECOG PS, and number of distant metastatic sites were prognostic factors in patients with NSCLC with ICI combination therapy. A lower 3‐week COP‐NLR was associated with a good response to treatment.Keywords: combination of platelet count and neutrophil‐lymphocyte ratio, immune checkpoint inhibitors, non‐small cell lung cancer, prognosis, treatment outcome

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide. 1 The most common types of lung cancer are non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer, of which NSCLC accounts for approximately 85% of lung cancer cases. Early‐stage NSCLC is frequently asymptomatic. Therefore, most patients with NSCLC are diagnosed at advanced stages and have a poor prognosis. 2

Cytotoxic chemotherapy agents, molecular‐targeted agents, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are recommended as first‐line treatments for patients with advanced NSCLC; ICIs are selected for patients with negative driver genes. ICI monotherapy or ICI combination therapy with chemotherapy prolongs survival compared with chemotherapy alone. 3

ICIs bind to immune checkpoint molecules or their ligands to inhibit the transmission of immunosuppressive signals, maintain T cell activation, and exert antitumor effects. 4 Furthermore, chemotherapy increases the immunogenicity of malignant cells, inhibits immunosuppressive circuitry, exhibits direct cytotoxic effects, and activates tumor‐targeting immune responses. 5 Thus, ICI combination therapy with chemotherapy is expected to enhance the antitumor immune response and is used as a first‐line treatment regimen for advanced NSCLC. 6 However, some patients who select combination therapy experience a high incidence of adverse events and poor tolerability due to the severe inflammatory response caused by rapid tumor progression. 7 Therefore, it is important to identify patients who can continue with ICI combination therapy with chemotherapy and benefit from its therapeutic efficacy. However, no indicators have been established to identify patients who could benefit from ICI combination therapy.

Inflammatory markers play an important role in the prognosis of patients treated with ICI. Chronic inflammation is associated with tumor growth and metastasis. 8 The persistence of chronic inflammation leads to the suppression of antitumor immunity and drug resistance. 8 Additionally, inflammatory markers, such as neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, platelet count, and C‐reactive protein (CRP) level, are associated with the tumor microenvironment, and they promote tumor growth and progression. The neutrophil‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR) are prognostic factors in patients with advanced NSCLC. 9 Moreover, the systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII) is a stronger prognostic factor than NLR and PLR. 10 , 11 However, SII values vary widely among patients, and the reported cutoff values also vary, making it difficult to use them uniformly in clinical practice.

The combination of platelet count and neutrophil‐lymphocyte ratio (COP‐NLR), which is a composite score of NLR and platelet count, has been reported to be a prognostic factor in various cancers. 12 , 13 , 14 This simple score is useful for stratifying prognostic risk. However, the relationship between the COP‐NLR and prognosis in patients with advanced NSCLC treated with ICI combination therapy with chemotherapy remains unclear.

On the other hand, inflammatory markers may change during ICI combination therapy with chemotherapy, and can be affected by chemotherapy‐induced bone marrow suppression. It has been reported that COP‐NLR within 6 weeks of treatment affected the time to treatment failure in patients with renal cell carcinoma. 15 However, in patients with advanced NSCLC, the relationship between COP‐NLR during ICI combination therapy and treatment response remains unclear.

Therefore, we investigated prognostic factors, including COP‐NLR, to identify patients with advanced NSCLC who could benefit from the therapeutic efficacy of ICI combination therapy with chemotherapy. Futhermore, we evaluated the relationship between the COP‐NLR score during ICI combination therapy and treatment response.

METHODS

Study patients

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of 88 patients with NSCLC who initially received ICI combination therapy with chemotherapy at Showa University Hospital between January 2019 and April 2023. A respiratory medicine specialist evaluated the diagnosis of NSCLC using positron emission tomography, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and a pathological evaluation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: history of ICI monotherapy (n = 6), active multiple cancers (n = 3), failure to complete one cycle of ICI combination therapy (n = 2), and infection requiring antimicrobial therapy (n = 1). After excluding 12 patients, 76 were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Patient flow chart of the present study. ICI, immune checkpoint inhibitor; NSCLC, non‐small cell lung cancer.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Showa University School, Japan (approval no. 22–291–A), which waived the requirement for informed consent owing to the retrospective nature of the study. Patients were informed on Showa University Hospital's website that they could opt out of the use of their data for research purposes.

Immune checkpoint inhibitor combination therapy

The ICI combination therapy with chemotherapy included the following seven regimens and was administered according to the dosage and administration schedule of the clinical trial: Pembrolizumab (Pemb) + Carboplatin (CBDCA) or Cisplatin + Pemetrexed (PEM), 16 Atezolizumab (Atezo) + Bevacizumab + CBDCA + Paclitaxel (PTX), 17 Atezo + CBDCA + nanoparticle albumin‐bound paclitaxel (nab‐PTX), 18 Pemb + CBDCA + PTX or nab‐PTX, 19 and Nivolumab (Nivo) + Ipilimumab (Ipi) + CBDCA + PEM. 20

Data collection

Patient characteristics and laboratory data at baseline were collected from the medical records. The patient characteristics included sex, age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS), smoking history, Brinkman index, surgical history, radiation history, prior epidermal growth factor receptor‐tyrosine kinase inhibitor therapy, programmed cell death‐ligand 1 tumor proportion score (PD‐L1, TPS), metastatic site, number of distant metastatic sites, clinical stage, and histology. Drug‐related data included the type of ICI combination therapy regimen used. Laboratory data included white blood cell (WBC) count, hemoglobin (Hb) level, platelet count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, albumin (Alb) level, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) level, CRP level, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level, modified Glasgow prognostic score (mGPS), NLR, PLR, and COP‐NLR score. Additionally, to assess the response to ICI combination therapy, data including platelet count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, NLR, and COP‐NLR at 3 weeks after starting treatment were collected.

Definitions

To facilitate their clinical application, the continuous variables were dichotomized and treated as categorical variables. Clinical staging was divided into two groups, III and IV, based on the site and classification of malignant tumors (TNM) of the Union for International Cancer Control 8th edition. 21 Patients were divided into two groups based on the cutoff values according to the World Health Organization's definition for age (65 years) and anemia (Hb levels 13 and 12 g/dL in males and females, respectively). Patients were also divided into two groups based on eGFR (cutoff: 60 mL/min/1.73 m2) according to the chronic kidney disease guidelines 22 ; Alb levels (cutoff: 3.5 mg/dL) according to the criteria for hypoalbuminemia; CRP levels (cutoff: 0.5 mg/dL) according to the mGPS classification; and PD‐L1 TPS expression (cutoff: 1%) according to previous studies. 16 Patients were also divided into two groups according to the upper limit of normal values for WBC, AST, LDH, ALP, and CEA levels. Patients were also divided into two groups using Youden's index method for PLR, platelet count, and NLR. 23 The COP‐NLR at baseline was defined as follows: patients with elevated platelet count (>30 × 104/μL) and NLR > 7.0 were allocated COP‐NLR 2; patients with only one factor, COP‐NLR 1; and patients with neither factor, COP‐NLR 0. Similarly, the COP‐NLR at 3 weeks (3‐week COP‐NLR) after starting ICI combination therapy was defined as follows: patients with elevated platelet count (>30 × 104/μL) and NLR > 2.0 were allocated COP‐NLR 2; patients with only one factor, COP‐NLR 1; and patients with neither factor, COP‐NLR 0. mGPS was defined as follows: patients with elevated CRP levels (>0.5 mg/dL) and hypoalbuminemia (<3.5 g/dL) were allocated mGPS 2; patients with only one factor, mGPS 1; and patients with neither factor, mGPS 0. 24 , 25

Assessment of treatment response

CT/MRI scans were performed at 2‐ to 6‐month intervals to confirm treatment response. A medical oncologist and respiratory medicine specialist assessed the treatment response according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors ver.1.1. Additionally, the treatment response was evaluated after one cycle of Nivo + Ipi + CBDCA + PEM and two cycles of other regimens. Response data (complete response, CR; partial response, PR; stable disease, SD; and progressive disease, PD) were collected from medical records.

Adverse events (AEs)

Data on AEs and immune‐related AEs (irAEs) that occurred during ICI combination therapy were collected. AEs were evaluated using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, ver. 5.0.

Outcome

The primary outcome was overall survival (OS). The follow‐up period ranged from the initiation of ICI combination therapy to death. In the case of patients whose deaths could not be confirmed, the follow‐up ended on the last visit or on July 31, 2023. OS curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method.

Statistical analysis

Sample size

We estimated that an event size of 41 patients would be required to detect a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.4, with 80% statistical power at a significance level of 0.05 in an equivalence test of the hypothesis, 16 assuming the overall mortality at 2 years to be 50%. 20 Assuming an overall 2‐year mortality of 50%, the sample size should be approximately 82 patients. Therefore, the sample size of 88 patients in this study was sufficient to achieve a valid conclusion.

Univariate and multivariable analyses

Univariate and multivariable analyses were performed using a Cox proportional hazards model. Factors with high correlations and many missing values were excluded from the multivariable analysis, and clinically important factors were included. Factors highly correlated with COP‐NLR (NLR, PLR, and platelet count) and mGPS (Alb and CRP) were excluded from the analysis. PD‐L1 TPS, clinical stage, histology, and CEA were excluded from analysis due to many missing values. ALP, bone, liver, brain, and pleural metastases were excluded from the analysis because they confounded with the number of distant metastatic sites. The following were selected because they are clinically important factors and have been reported as prognostic factors in patients with advanced NSCLC: age, 26 ECOG PS, 27 smoking history, 26 radiation history, 28 number of distant metastatic sites, 29 anemia, 26 LDH, 29 mGPS, 24 baseline COP‐NLR, and 3‐week COP‐NLR. Significant independent variables contributing to the prognosis of patients with advanced NSCLC treated with ICI combination therapy were identified using stepwise selection.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software ver. 27 (IBM, Tokyo, Japan).

Prognostic model and assessment

The β values of the prognostic factors extracted from the multivariable analysis were divided by the smallest β value among them and approximated to the nearest integer. For each factor, the approximated β values were scored as integers. For each patient, the scores were calculated as the sum of the scores for each factor. Patients were divided into two groups based on the distribution of their scores. Survival curves of the two groups were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test. The performance of the prognostic model was evaluated using Harrell's concordance index (c‐index), which is a measure of discrimination; its maximum value is 1.0, which indicates perfect discrimination. A c‐index of 0.5 indicates a random chance to correctly discriminate the outcome. The c‐index was calculated using R software (ver. 4.4.1).

Relationship between combination of platelet count and neutrophil‐lymphocyte ratio and laboratory values at baseline

Laboratory values of the patients with COP‐NLR score of 0 and those with scores of 1, 2 at baseline were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Changes in inflammatory markers during immune checkpoint inhibitor combination therapy

Inflammatory markers at baseline and 3 weeks after starting the ICI combination therapy were compared using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank sum test.

Relationship between COP‐NLR and treatment response

The relationship between treatment response (CR, PR, SD, and PD) and COP‐NLR at baseline or 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy was analyzed using the chi‐square test.

Relationship between the reduction rates of inflammatory markers and treatment response

The reduction rates of neutrophil, lymphocyte, platelet counts, and NLR among the three groups (CR + PR, SD, and PD) were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Relationship between COP‐NLR and inflammatory markers at 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy

The inflammatory markers and their reduction rates in patients with COP‐NLR score of 0 and those with scores of 1, 2 at 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Relationship between COP‐NLR at 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy and progression‐free survival (PFS)

Progression‐free survival (PFS) was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. PFS was defined as the duration from the initiation of ICI combination therapy until tumor progression, death from any cause, or the last follow‐up visit. PFS curves between the two groups, COP‐NLR score of 0 and scores of 1, 2 group at 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy, were compared using the log‐rank test.

Relationship between COP‐NLR at 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy and AEs

The relationship between COP‐NLR at 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy and AEs was analyzed using the chi‐square test.

ICI combination therapy

The most common regimen was Pemb + CBDCA + PEM in 39.5% of patients (Table S1). The median duration of ICI combination therapy was 2.8 (range, 0.7–4.5) months. Thirty (39.5%) patients discontinued ICI combination therapy because they were unable to complete the scheduled cycles for the following reasons: AEs, 14 (18.4%); poor PS, 4 (5.3%); and disease progression, 12 (15.8%).

The cumulative survival curves for all patients are shown in Figure 2. The median OS was 15.7 months. The 1‐ and 2‐year survival rates were 55.7% and 43.5%, respectively. The median follow‐up period was 10.4 (range, 1.4–53.4) months. During the follow‐up period, 42 (55.3%) patients died, of whom 34 (81.0%) died of cancer‐related causes.

Prognostic model and assessment

ECOG PS 2, distant metastatic sites ≥2, and COP‐NLR scores of 1, 2 were each scored as 1 point. The sum of the scores of the three factors, ranging from 0 to 3, was calculated for all patients. The patients were divided into two groups (0–1 points group and 2–3 points group). The OS in the 0–1 points group was significantly longer than that in the 2–3 points group (median: 40.3 vs. 6.3 months, p < 0.001, Figure S1). Thus, scores were defined as follows: low‐risk group (0–1 points group; n = 52) and high‐risk group (2–3 points group; n = 24). The c‐index of this model was 0.702 (95% confidence interval: 0.639–0.765).

Relationship between COP‐NLR score and laboratory values at baseline

The WBC, platelet, and neutrophil counts; Hb, Alb, ALP, and CRP levels; NLR; and PLR were significantly different between the COP‐NLR score of 0 and scores of 1, 2 group at baseline (Table S3).

Adverse events

The relationship between COP‐NLR at 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy and AEs is shown in Table S6. The most common AEs of all grades were anemia, WBC decreased, neutrophil count decreased, fatigue, platelet count decreased, and constipation in 72 (94.7%), 63 (82.9%), 52 (68.4%), 46 (60.5%), 44 (57.9%), and 43 (56.6%) patients, respectively. The most common irAEs of all grades were severe skin reaction and colitis in 14 (18.4%) and 9 (11.8%) patients, respectively. The most common grade 3/4 AEs were neutrophil count decreased and WBC decreased in 38 (50.0%) and 25 (32.9%) patients, respectively.

The incidence of AEs was not significantly different between the 3‐week COP‐NLR score of 0 and scores of 1, 2 group.

DISCUSSION

Baseline COP‐NLR scores of 1, 2, ECOG PS 2, and distant metastatic sites ≥2 were poor prognostic factors in patients with advanced NSCLC treated with ICI combination therapy with chemotherapy. Additionally, a lower 3‐week COP‐NLR score was associated with a good response to treatment and longer PFS. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to reveal the relationship between the baseline COP‐NLR score and prognosis in patients with advanced NSCLC treated with ICI combination therapy. This is also the first report to demonstrate the relationship between COP‐NLR during ICI combination therapy and treatment response.

The COP‐NLR is a convenient indicator that reflects host inflammatory responses, tumor progression, and metastasis cancer and is easily applicable in clinical practice. Patients who should be treated with ICI combination therapy frequently present with severe inflammatory responses caused by rapid tumor progression. Therefore, in these patient populations, clarifying the relationship between the COP‐NLR score and prognosis has high clinical application value. Thus, the COP‐NLR is an important indicator for selecting patients who could benefit from the therapeutic efficacy of ICI combination therapy. Moreover, the use of the COP‐NLR may lead to the selection of an appropriate treatment regimen.

For the first time, we found that baseline COP‐NLR scores of 1, 2 were associated with poor prognosis in patients with advanced NSCLC receiving ICI combination therapy. Neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets, which are components of the COP‐NLR, are associated with the tumor microenvironment. 30 , 31 Neutrophils promote tumor progression, and lymphocytes play an important role in antitumor immunity. 30 Platelets induce epithelial‐mesenchymal transition in cancer cells and promote metastasis from the primary site. 32 Additionally, platelets release angiogenic and growth factors, which promote angiogenesis, tumor growth, and metastasis. 33 Therefore, baseline COP‐NLR scores of 1, 2 would reflect cancer‐related inflammation due to tumor progression, suppression of antitumor immunity, and metastasis, which influenced the worsening of prognosis.

Baseline COP‐NLR scores of 1, 2 were associated with high levels of inflammatory markers and ALP and low Hb levels. Anemia in patients with cancer is caused by increased inflammatory cytokine levels resulting from the interaction between tumor cells and the host immune system. 34 Elevated ALP levels are associated with bone and liver metastases. 35 They are caused by tumor progression due to increased levels of inflammatory cytokines. 36 Therefore, our findings revealed that patients with COP‐NLR scores of 1, 2 had severe inflammatory responses caused by tumor progression.

ECOG PS 2 is a poor prognostic factor in patients with advanced NSCLC treated with ICI combination therapy. 27 Poor ECOG PS is associated with the discontinuation of ICI therapy or chemotherapy because of a poor general condition. 37 , 38 Therefore, patients with an ECOG PS of 2 had poorer tolerability to ICI combination therapy because of their deteriorating general condition, which resulted in a worse prognosis.

Distant metastatic sites ≥2 have been reported as a poor prognostic factor in patients with advanced NSCLC treated with ICI monotherapy. 29 Metastatic sites are associated with poor prognosis in patients with metastatic urothelial carcinoma treated with platinum‐based therapy. 39 Metastasis is caused by tumor progression related to the tumor microenvironment. 40 Therefore, we considered that patients with distant metastatic sites ≥2 had tumor growth, resulting in a worse prognosis.

In this study, we developed a prognostic model for patients with advanced NSCLC treated with ICI combination therapy with chemotherapy. This model, which integrated prognostic factors identified in multivariable analysis, predicted prognostic risk with high accuracy. The prognosis of patients in the low‐risk group can be further improved by ICI combination therapy. In contrast, other treatment regimens should be considered for patients in the high‐risk group.

Neutrophil counts, lymphocyte counts, and NLR were reduced by ICI combination therapy. This may be due to the influence of the efficacy and AEs associated with ICI combination therapy. First, tumor progression may have improved with ICI combination therapy, resulting in changes in the levels of inflammatory markers. Second, among chemotherapeutic agents, cytotoxic chemotherapies frequently cause bone marrow suppression. Thus, neutrophil counts, platelet counts, and NLR, which indicate inflammatory responses, change due to bone marrow suppression. Particularly, neutropenia most commonly occurs during the first cycle of therapy 41 ; thus, its effects may have been stronger at 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy. Therefore, we considered that changes in the levels of inflammatory markers may reflect the therapeutic efficacy and bone marrow suppression associated with ICI combination therapy.

A 3‐week COP‐NLR score of 0 was associated with a good response to treatment. Additionally, a 3‐week COP‐NLR score of 0 group had a lower neutrophil count, platelet count, and NLR and higher lymphocyte count than scores of 1, 2 group. Therefore, a 3‐week COP‐NLR score of 0 may indicate a tumor shrinkage effect due to lymphocyte activation 42 and an improvement in inflammation due to decreased neutrophil count, platelet count, and NLR. Hence, a 3‐week COP‐NLR score of 0 was considered to more strongly reflect tumor shrinkage effects and improvement in inflammation due to ICI combination therapy.

In contrast, the reduction rates of neutrophil, lymphocyte, platelet counts, and NLR at 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy were not associated with treatment response. This suggests that the baseline inflammation levels in this patient population were very high. Therefore, even if this population responded to ICI combination therapy, inflammation may not have been completely suppressed, resulting in no therapeutic efficacy. As such, therapeutic efficacy may have been strongly influenced by the inflammatory intensity at 3 weeks after starting ICI combination therapy, rather than by the reduction rates of inflammatory markers.

In this study, the reduction rates of neutrophil counts and NLR were significantly different, but lymphocyte counts were not, between a 3‐week COP‐NLR score of 0 and scores of 1, 2 group. Cytotoxic chemotherapies target rapidly dividing cells and therefore act on neutrophils, which are the fastest dividing and proliferating cells in the bone marrow. 43 Therefore, we considered that COP‐NLR during ICI combination therapy was strongly affected by chemotherapy‐induced bone marrow suppression.

A 3‐week COP‐NLR score of 0 was associated with a longer PFS. A lower SII after 6 weeks of platinum‐based therapy in patients with metastatic urothelial carcinoma 39 and a lower SII after 6 weeks of ICI therapy in patients with gastric cancer have been reported to indicate longer PFS. 44 Therefore, patients with a 3‐week COP‐NLR score of 0 may experience a higher antitumor effect with ICI combination therapy, which is expected to lead to longer PFS. Hence, the 3‐week COP‐NLR score is useful for deciding whether ICI combination therapy should be continued in patients with advanced NSCLC.

This study has some limitations. First, owing to the retrospective nature of the study, variability was noted in the timing of clinicians' assessments of treatment response. Second, the external validity of the factors could not be verified, as this was a single‐center study. Third, because the cutoff value of the NLR has not been established, we set it independently. Because inflammatory markers change depending on the severity of cancer, the cutoff value of NLR in this study may not apply to all patients. Therefore, it is necessary to verify the external validity of the NLR cutoff values in the future. Fourth, this study included patients treated with various ICI combination therapy regimens. Therefore, the association between ICI combination therapy regimens and the prognostic factors identified in this study remains unclear. Fifth, 39.5% of patients did not complete the protocol‐specified number of cycles in combination therapy; thus, we could not analyze prognostic factors in patients who completed the ICI combination therapy.

In conclusion, baseline COP‐NLR, ECOG PS, and the number of distant metastatic sites were prognostic factors for OS in patients with advanced NSCLC treated with ICI combination therapy with chemotherapy. Additionally, a lower 3‐week COP‐NLR score was associated with a good response to treatment and longer PFS. Therefore, the COP‐NLR is an important indicator for selecting patients who could benefit from the therapeutic efficacy of ICI combination therapy for advanced NSCLC. Patients with a COP‐NLR score of 0 may experience a higher antitumor effect of ICI combination therapy, which is expected to lead to longer OS.
