The impact of graves’ orbitopathy on anterior segment structures and choroidal parameters
Authors: Neslihan Sevimli, Müzeyyen Eryılmaz, Muhammed Kaan Azarbaz, Güneş Taydaş
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate Schlemm’s canal (SC) dimensions and choroidal vascularity in patients with Graves’ orbitopathy (GO+), and to compare the findings with those of patients with Graves’ disease (GD) without orbitopathy (GO-) and healthy controls using optical coherence tomography (OCT); additionally, to investigate the potential impact of these anatomical and vascular changes on intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation and glaucoma susceptibility.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 164 participants, categorized into three groups: GO+ (n = 38), GO- (n = 76), and healthy controls (n = 50). All subjects were euthyroid for at least six months. SC length (SCL), SC area (SCA), choroidal vascularity index (CVI), luminal area (LA), stromal area (SA), and total choroidal area (TCA) were assessed. Correlations between SCA, SCL, and CVI measurements and other ocular and systemic parameters were analyzed. Demographic, ocular, and systemic parameters were also compared among groups.ResultsThere were no significant differences in age, sex, intraocular pressure, central corneal thickness, retinal nerve fiber layer thickness, or optic disc parameters across groups (p > 0.05). Hertel exophthalmometry values were significantly elevated in the GO + group (p < 0.001). Both SCL and SCA were significantly lower in the GO + group compared to the GO- and control groups (p < 0.001). The GO- group showed significantly reduced SC measurements compared to controls (p < 0.001). While CVI did not differ significantly among groups (p = 0.758), pairwise analysis revealed significantly higher LA (p = 0.028) and TCA (p = 0.035) levels in the GO + group compared to the GO- group. A borderline correlation was observed between SCA and CVI (r = 0.150, p = 0.050), and a significant negative correlation was noted between Hertel values and SCA (r = − 0.394, p < 0.001).ConclusionChanges in SC and choroidal structures in GD may be influenced by both mechanical and systemic factors. While these alterations may contribute to IOP elevation, they do not directly imply increased glaucoma risk but could play a role in its development or progression in susceptible individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04441-w.

Background

Graves’ orbitopathy (GO) is the main extra-thyroidal complication of Graves’ disease (GD), characterized by autoimmune inflammation of the orbital tissues. It commonly leads to eyelid retraction, proptosis, restrictive strabismus, and, in chronic or severe cases, may result in optic neuropathy [1–13]. Elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) and visual-field defects are frequently encountered in both GO and primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), complicating diagnosis and management [5, 14]. Although gaze-dependent IOP spikes are typical in GO, persistent IOP elevation—linked to extraocular-muscle hypertrophy, mucopolysaccharide deposition in the trabecular meshwork, and increased episcleral venous pressure (EVP), which may indirectly compress the optic nerve head—may accelerate glaucomatous damage [2, 5, 8, 10, 11, 15, 16].

The aqueous humor drains from the eye by passing through the trabecular meshwork and subsequently entering Schlemm’s canal (SC) and the episcleral veins. Reduced SC caliber has been associated with diminished outflow facility and higher IOP [2, 16, 17].

The choroid, a highly vascular tissue supplying the outer retina, also undergoes hemodynamic changes in GO [18–23]. Agrawal et al. developed the choroidal vascularity index (CVI), which is determined by dividing the luminal area (LA) by the total choroidal area (TCA) based on binarized images obtained from OCT scans [24]. Recent studies have used CVI to assess venous outflow impairment and vascular congestion in GO [9, 13, 25–28]. Increased choroidal volume suggests venous congestion and stromal edema, which may elevate posterior pole pressure, disturb aqueous flow balance, and indirectly compress the optic nerve head, fostering glaucoma-like neurodegeneration [29].

Spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) offers a high-definition view of the SC, revealing outflow pathway alterations and various layers of the retina, thereby enhancing our understanding of the choroid’s role in eye health [12, 19, 30–32].

Despite these emerging insights, comparative data on SC dimensions and choroidal structures among patients with GO, those with GD without orbitopathy, and healthy controls remain scarce. Clarifying these structural and vascular alterations could illuminate mechanisms of IOP elevation in GO and refine glaucoma surveillance. The study aims to quantify SC parameters and choroidal structures in patients with GD with GO, GD patients without GO, and healthy individuals, and to determine whether these anatomical and hemodynamic changes contribute to higher IOP and increased glaucoma risk in GO.

Materials and methods

This retrospective, cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology and the Department of Internal Medicine at Fatih Sultan Mehmet Training and Research Hospital from March 2023 to April 2024. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee (2025/06/1101) and adhered to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and documented.

We included 38 eyes (2 males, 17 females) diagnosed with Graves’ orbitopathy (GO) who were followed regularly at our hospital’s ophthalmology clinic (GO + group), along with 76 eyes (5 males, 33 females) from patients with GD and thyroid hormone abnormalities without clinical evidence of GO (GO- group), and 50 eyes (5 males, 20 females) from healthy controls who attended the ophthalmology outpatient clinic. GO was diagnosed through a comprehensive approach involving clinical assessment, imaging techniques such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and endocrinological testing. The activity of the disease was evaluated using the Clinical Activity Score (CAS). At the same time, its severity was classified according to the criteria established by the European Group on Graves’ Orbitopathy (EUGOGO) [3].

Examination procedures

For all participants, baseline clinical data, including age, sex, duration of disease, history of steroid use, and smoking status, were collected. Comprehensive ophthalmologic evaluations were performed, which encompassed pupillary light reflexes, best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) assessed with Snellen charts and later converted to the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) scale, color vision testing using Ishihara plates, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and fundus examination after pupil dilation achieved with 1% tropicamide and a 90-diopter lens. Visual field testing was conducted using the Humphrey automated visual field analyzer with the 30 − 2 testing protocol. Eyelid retraction was measured via margin reflex distance (MRD), and proptosis was quantified by an experienced ophthalmologist (NS) using a Hertel exophthalmometer.

IOP measurements were obtained in the primary gaze position using Goldmann applanation tonometry. Gonioscopy was performed employing a Zeiss four-mirror lens. Central corneal thickness (CCT) was measured using a non-contact pneumatic tonometer (RK-1a Auto Ref/Keratometer, Nidek, Japan). Anterior segment images for SC assessment and circumpapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness measurements were acquired using SD-OCT (Maestro, Topcon Co., Tokyo, Japan). All selected OCT images were saved in JPEG format and imported into ImageJ software (version 1.54d, National Institutes of Health) for quantitative analysis.

The diagnosis of Graves’ orbitopathy (GO) was established when patients presented with at least one characteristic clinical sign—such as proptosis, upper eyelid retraction, restrictive strabismus, eyelid swelling with erythema, chemosis, or caruncle edema—and these findings were supported by endocrinological assessments or orbital imaging via CT and MRI. Patients were categorized according to the EUGOGO classification system into mild, moderate-to-severe, or sight-threatening GO [3]. Mild GO was defined by minor eyelid retraction less than 2 mm, mild involvement of soft tissues, proptosis less than 3 mm above normal limits, and absent or intermittent diplopia responsive to lubricants. Moderate-to-severe GO included eyelid retraction of 2 mm or more, moderate to severe soft tissue involvement, proptosis equal to or exceeding 3 mm above normal, and either intermittent or persistent diplopia. Sight-threatening GO was reserved for cases complicated by dysthyroid optic neuropathy and/or corneal breakdown, which require prompt and intensive treatment to avoid permanent vision impairment.

Disease activity in the participants was assessed using the CAS, which consists of seven criteria: spontaneous retrobulbar pain, pain during attempted upward or downward gaze, redness of the eyelids and conjunctiva, swelling of the caruncle or plica, eyelid edema, and conjunctival chemosis. GO was classified as active if the CAS was 3 or higher out of 7. A more detailed ten-item CAS, which also includes recent changes such as an increase in exophthalmos by 2 mm or more, a reduction in eye movement by at least 8° in any gaze direction, and a decrease in visual acuity by one or more lines on the Snellen chart over 1–3 months, is useful for evaluating recent disease progression and activity [33].

This study included patients with inactive GO (CAS less than 3) who were euthyroid for at least six months. Participants were classified as mild or moderate-to-severe based on EUGOGO criteria and had not received prior treatment with steroids, immunosuppressants, or orbital decompression surgery. The control group consisted of age- and sex-matched healthy individuals attending routine annual check-ups. Exclusion criteria for both groups included ocular media opacities (such as cataracts), retinal diseases affecting OCT measurements (e.g., age-related macular degeneration), previous dysthyroid optic neuropathy, history of ocular surgery or trauma within the past three months, best corrected visual acuity worse than 0.1 logMAR, refractive errors greater than ± 3.00 diopters to limit axial length effects on choroidal measurements, IOP above 21 mmHg, glaucomatous visual field loss, diabetes mellitus, systemic hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and cerebrovascular disease. Systemic blood pressure was recorded following a minimum 20-minute seated rest period. Additionally, controls with known systemic diseases were excluded.

Schlemm measurements

AS-OCT imaging was performed consistently between 9:00 and 11:00 a.m. to minimize the effects of diurnal variation. Ambient room temperature was kept stable between 22 °C and 25 °C, with humidity maintained within 50% to 60%. These environmental parameters were regularly monitored throughout the study to ensure consistency. A trained technician, blinded to participants’ clinical information, carried out the scans using an SD-OCT device (Maestro, Topcon Co., Tokyo, Japan) fitted with an anterior segment module (ASM). This module functions at a scan rate of 50,000 A-scans per second and requires no supplementary lenses. Images were obtained at a standardized distance of approximately 62.6 ± 0.1 mm, employing linear scanning to capture the Schlemm’s canal area (SCA) across a 3 to 6 mm span, allowing a ± 5% tolerance in both horizontal and vertical axes.

Following attachment of the anterior segment adapter to the OCT device, SC images were captured by an experienced examiner (GT) in a darkened room before pupil dilation. Imaging focused on the nasal limbus, with fixation directed toward the temporal side. For the left eye, SC images were acquired at the 9 o’clock position, and for the right eye at the 3 o’clock position, utilizing the Line (H) Anterior Segment 3.0 mm capture mode. During the procedure, patients were instructed to keep their eyes fully open. The examiner gently assisted by holding the eyelids open with fingers, taking care not to exert pressure on the globe. No eye speculum was used.

Two independent and experienced investigators (KA and GT), blinded to the participants’ group assignments, conducted the image evaluations. The selected images were saved in JPEG format and then imported into ImageJ software (available publicly at http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/ from the National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) for analysis. Choroid measurements

OCT imaging was conducted without pharmacological pupil dilation, consistently scheduled between 9:00 and 11:00 a.m. to control for diurnal variations in CVI. An experienced ophthalmologist (KA) performed all measurements. To ensure optimal image quality, an internal fixation target was utilized during scanning, and only scans achieving a quality score above 45 were included. Imaging was carried out using SD-OCT (Maestro, Topcon Co., Tokyo, Japan) operating in high-resolution mode at a rate of 50,000 A-scans per second with a 1:2 pixel scaling.

CVI was derived from grayscale images of the choroid obtained from scans centered on and passing through the fovea, and analyzed using ImageJ software version 1.53e (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). First, the scale was set by calibrating pixel length with the line tool in ImageJ. The OCT scan was then converted to an 8-bit image format. Next, binarization was performed using a combination of auto-local and Niblack thresholding techniques. The region of interest (ROI), corresponding to the TCA, was manually outlined from the upper boundary at the choroid-retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) junction down to the lower boundary at the choroid-sclera interface, using the polygon tool, and saved in the ROI manager. The image was subsequently converted to red, green, blue (RGB) format, and the colour threshold setting was applied. Brightness thresholds were adjusted (setting the lower limit to zero and the upper limit to 250). These selections were added to the ROI manager. The combined area was calculated by overlaying the initial and subsequent measurements using the logical AND operation within the ROI manager. TCA and LA were computed automatically by integrating the values associated with each pixel within the segmented choroidal area. The choroidal stroma was delineated based on light pixel intensity, whereas luminal regions were defined by dark pixels. Imaging was performed for each eye, and both eyes of a patient were analyzed individually in the study. For both Schlemm’s canal and choroidal measurements, three scans were obtained for each eye, and the mean value of these measurements was used in the statistical analysis.

The repeatability and reproducibility of SC and CVI measurements were confirmed, with intraobserver and interobserver agreement considered acceptable when the coefficient of variation (CV) was < 15% and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), together with 95% confidence intervals, was > 0.75.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from this study were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for macOS, version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics were presented as frequency and percentage for categorical variables, and as median values for continuous variables. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests.

For group comparisons, appropriate statistical tests were selected based on data distribution and variable type. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare independent quantitative data with non-normal distributions across more than two groups. For normally distributed continuous variables, comparisons among three groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pairwise group differences were assessed using the post hoc test. Comparisons between two independent groups were made using either the independent samples t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Considering the potential correlation between the two eyes of the same subject, we applied a Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) approach to compare outcomes across groups.

Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between continuous variables. Univariable and multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations of SC and choroidal parameters with age, sex, IOP, and CCT, and additional models were constructed with IOP as the dependent variable. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 164 eyes from 82 subjects were included in the study. Thirty-eight eyes in the GO + group, 76 eyes in the GO- group, and 50 eyes in the control group. The mean age was 51.26 ± 9.33 years in the GO + group, 53.53 ± 14.38 in the GO- group, and 52.28 ± 14.36 in the control group, with no significant difference (p = 0.825). Female sex predominated in all groups, with no significant difference (p = 0.532).

None of the GO cases are active or sight-threatening. Among GO + participants, 31.6% had a CAS of 0, 26.3% had a CAS of 1, and 42.1% had a CAS of 2. Based on the EUGOGO classification, 47.4% had mild and 52.6% had moderate-to-severe disease. Ocular and systemic clinical characteristics

All patients have been euthyroid for at least 6 months. There is no corneal breakdown or optic neuropathy. No patients have received steroid treatment or undergone orbital decompression in the past 6 months.

There was no significant difference between the GO- group and the control group (p = 0,301).

Schlemm’s Canal and choroidal measurements

Discussion

In our study, we found that patients with Graves’ disease (GD) exhibited a significant reduction in Schlemm’s canal area (SCA) and length (SCL), along with an increase in the choroidal area, while the choroidal vascularity index (CVI) remained unchanged. Notably, the simultaneous alterations in both SC and choroidal structures may play a role in the elevated IOP, they do not appear to be directly associated with an increased risk of glaucoma in this population. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively assess both anterior and posterior segment changes in patients with GD using OCT to explore their potential impact on glaucoma development.

Several studies have reported elevated IOP, particularly varying with the direction of gaze, in eyes affected by Graves’ orbitopathy (GO). Multiple mechanisms have been proposed to explain IOP elevation in GO, including increased mucopolysaccharide deposition within the trabecular meshwork leading to higher outflow resistance, elevated EVP due to orbital congestion and impaired venous drainage, and mechanical restriction or compression of the globe by fibrotic and hypertrophic extraocular muscles [5, 8, 10, 34–37]. Additionally, Bagatin et al. identified several risk factors for GO-related IOP elevation, including older age, longer duration of thyroid disease, and higher anti-thyroglobulin antibody levels [11].

However, whether the elevation in IOP is a direct consequence of GO or a predisposing factor for the development of glaucoma remains a subject of ongoing investigation. Several studies have suggested that the IOP elevation observed in GO patients is more commonly associated with ocular hypertension (OHT) rather than true glaucomatous damage [10, 15]. The absence of glaucomatous visual field defects, optic nerve cupping, and a family history of glaucoma—along with the observed reduction in IOP and improvement in visual fields following orbital decompression—has been reported in several studies, indicating that GO may not inherently predispose patients to glaucoma [10, 38, 39].

Given that narrowing of SC has been associated with elevated IOP [17, 40, 41], Gün et al. recently investigated SC parameters in patients with GO [2]. They reported significantly higher IOP and Hertel values, along with reduced SCA and SCL using AS-OCT. Interestingly, they also observed a larger SCA in individuals with glaucoma or OHT [2]. In our study, SCA and SCL were also significantly reduced in GO patients; however, none of our participants had a diagnosis of glaucoma or OHT. While SC dimensions are generally believed to influence aqueous outflow facility, our findings—together with those of Gün et al. [2] indicate that SC narrowing in GO may not necessarily be associated with the increase in IOP and development of glaucoma, thus highlighting a dissociation between anatomical SC alterations and functional glaucomatous risk. This may be explained by the exclusion of patients with IOP > 21 mmHg and by the euthyroid status of all participants, which likely reduced orbital inflammation and edema and thereby limited a potential increase in EVP. Furthermore, the inclusion of predominantly mild-to-moderate GO cases may have prevented the development of a full functional impact on IOP. In addition, alternative outflow pathways such as the uveoscleral route, compensatory mechanisms in aqueous humor dynamics, and individual variability may also have contributed to the maintenance of similar IOP levels despite reduced SC dimensions in GO patients.

Moreover, the observation that SC dimensions were also reduced in patients with thyroid dysfunction without clinically evident orbitopathy raises the possibility of a direct influence of thyroid hormones, their receptors, or thyroid-related autoantigens expressed in the SC. These systemic factors may induce structural changes in both anterior and posterior ocular segments without necessarily impairing outflow facility or leading to elevated IOP or glaucoma. This suggests a broader thyroid-related mechanism affecting ocular structures, independent of overt orbital involvement.

Similar to the anterior segment, whether the choroidal changes observed in GO are driven by systemic thyroid hormone and antibody levels or are secondary to orbital structural alterations has been a subject of investigation in several studies. These studies have commonly attributed the observed increase in choroidal thickness (CT) to impaired choroidal drainage resulting from congestion in the ophthalmic veins [12, 13, 19, 21, 22, 42–44].

However, CT is influenced by a variety of physiological and anatomical factors, including age, axial length, IOP, and systolic blood pressure, and it also exhibits diurnal variation. In contrast, the CVI is considered more stable and less susceptible to these variables [24]. Supporting this notion, Goel et al. demonstrated that CVI is more sensitive than CT in differentiating healthy individuals from those with GO, underscoring the clinical utility of CVI as a more reliable and robust biomarker for assessing choroidal alterations in thyroid-associated orbitopathy [26].

Most studies have reported an increased CVI in patients with GO compared to healthy controls [28, 45–47]. Ceylanoğlu et al. also observed a higher CVI within the central 1500 μm subfoveal region in GO patients; however, this increase was not evident in the wider 7500 μm area, indicating a regional variability in vascular changes [25]. In our study, CVI measurements were limited to the subfoveal region.

Yeter et al. found no significant difference in CVI between GO patients and controls, which aligns with our findings [9]. In their study, proportional increases in both LA and TCA were reported, which may explain the unchanged CVI despite overall choroidal thickening.

This pattern suggests that the choroidal changes in GO may not solely reflect vascular congestion secondary to mechanical compression. Instead, the concurrent enlargement of both LA and SA could indicate a generalized choroidal expansion due to a combination of factors such as orbital venous congestion, increased EVP, and inflammatory processes. Specifically, venous stasis may lead to an increase in choroidal blood volume, thereby expanding the LA, while inflammatory cell infiltration and extracellular matrix remodeling could contribute to stromal thickening [48]. Choroidal engorgement and edema may raise posterior pole pressure, disrupt aqueous humor dynamics, and elevate IOP without angle closure. Quigley et al. showed that such expansion can compress the optic nerve head, mimicking glaucomatous damage [29].

Eckstein et al. demonstrated that autoantibody levels and prevalence are associated with the severity and activity of GO, indicating a potential triggering role as well as a contribution to the maintenance of the autoimmune process within the orbit [49]. Ulas et al. reported that all subgroups of hypothyroid patients had increased CT compared to healthy controls, even in the absence of GO, suggesting that thyroid hormone imbalance alone may influence choroidal structure [23]. These findings support the notion that choroidal remodeling in GO is likely driven by complex hemodynamic and inflammatory mechanisms by circulating autoantibodies [49].

In our study, we observed a significant correlation between SCA and the CVI, suggesting that orbital and vascular alterations in GD may simultaneously affect both anterior and posterior segment microvascular structures. Elevated EVP and orbital congestion can impair aqueous humor outflow by compressing SC, while also contributing to venous stasis and choroidal engorgement.

Since CVI is defined as the ratio of LA to TCA (CVI = LA/TCA), a relatively larger stromal expansion may account for the observed reduction in CVI. Several studies have reported that the proportional increase in choroidal SA becomes more pronounced as GD activity progresses [20, 50]. The chronic inflammatory and autoimmune processes characteristic of GO may contribute to endothelial dysfunction and generalized vascular remodeling within the eye. These mechanisms may account for the concurrent changes observed in both SCA and CVI, suggesting a shared pathophysiological process that globally affects ocular microvascular structures in GO. The smallest SCA values observed in GO + patients suggest increased inflammation in advanced GD, potentially driven by abnormal thyroid hormone levels or heightened autoimmune activity. The concurrent decrease in CVI with declining SCA implies that, in progressive disease, the stromal area is more affected than the vascular area. However, all participants in our study were euthyroid for at least six months, and autoantibody levels were not available. Although the earlier thyroid status of these patients remains unknown, it is reasonable to assume that cases with more fluctuating or uncontrolled thyroid dysfunction may exhibit more pronounced stromal alterations.

As Hertel values increase, reflecting greater anterior displacement of the globe, the resulting mechanical and vascular stress may directly compress SC or increase resistance to aqueous outflow, leading to narrowing or even collapse of the canal lumen. The negative correlation between Hertel values and SCA observed in our study may also be attributed to enhanced stromal inflammation seen in more advanced stages of GO.

Although CT was not evaluated in our study, similar to Samadi et al., we found no significant correlation between the CVI and other physiological or disease-related parameters [13]. This lack of association between Hertel measurements and CVI may suggest that the choroid is capable of preserving its vascular-to-stromal ratio despite variations in orbital volume or pressure. No Hertel measurements exceeded 28 mm in our study.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and the limited number of patients with GO may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Although the required sample size was achieved based on the power analysis, the unequal group distribution due to the lower number of eligible GO + patients represents an additional limitation. Second, a more detailed evaluation of SCA and SCL across different quadrants could have been achieved through the acquisition of AS-OCT images from multiple meridians. Additionally, the absence of thyroid receptor autoantibody measurements during the study period limits our ability to explore potential associations between these biomarkers and SC parameters. As a cross-sectional study, it also did not allow for the evaluation of longitudinal changes in SC and choroidal parameters regarding the progression of GD and GO. Regarding posterior segment assessment, CVI was measured from a single horizontal B-scan across the subfoveal region. This limited sampling may not fully capture the overall choroidal vascular status; thus, future studies should consider evaluating a wider macular area for a more comprehensive analysis. One limitation is that IOP was measured only at the central cornea during primary gaze, without evaluating IOP changes in various gaze directions. Although patients with refractive errors greater than ± 3.00 diopters were excluded to minimize the effect of axial length on choroidal measurements, the inclusion of detailed refraction and axial length data would have further strengthened the study. Moreover, the study did not include comparisons between the active and inactive phases of GO or between euthyroid and hyperthyroid states. Lastly, the exclusion of patients with glaucoma precluded assessment of the potential interplay between SC morphology, choroidal vascularity, and glaucomatous changes. Future studies including such subgroups may offer further insight into anterior and posterior segment microvascular dynamics in GO.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that changes in Schlemm’s canal and the choroid in patients with GD may be influenced not only by mechanical compression but also by systemic factors. The observation of the smallest SCA values in the GO + group and the correlation between SCA and CVI indicate that, in advanced stages, stromal inflammation is more prominent than vascular involvement. Although these alterations may contribute to elevated IOP, they do not necessarily indicate an increased risk for glaucoma. But they may contribute to its development or progression in individuals with reduced compensatory capacity and may present with findings similar to glaucoma. Nevertheless, further large-scale studies stratified by GO stage and activity—especially those incorporating detailed thyroid hormone and autoantibody profiles—are needed to clarify these associations. Therefore, careful monitoring of anterior and posterior segment changes via OCT in GD is crucial for early detection and management of potential glaucoma risk.
