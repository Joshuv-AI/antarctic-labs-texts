Symmetry-driven gating of TRPM8 by PIP2 and menthol
Authors: Sun-Hong Kim, Chansik Hong, Hyunwoo Park, Youngchang Ju, Youngsoo Hwang, Jinhyeong Kim, Sangho Ji, Wookyung Yu, Insuk So, Hyung Ho Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTRPM8, a cold-activated ion channel, enables mammals to sense cooling agents such as menthol. While PIP2 is essential for menthol-induced activation of TRPM8, the precise cooperative mechanism and the specific binding mode of menthol have remained elusive. Here, we present cryo-EM structures of mouse TRPM8 in diverse conformations, including a PIP2-induced two-fold symmetric intermediate and an icilin-bound open state. Our results reveal that PIP2 binding initiates a symmetry-breaking event, priming the channel for activation through a noncanonical intermediate states. The subsequent binding of cooling agonists promotes a transition back to four-fold symmetry. Notably, we find that menthol stabilizes the PIP2-bound state, thereby overcoming channel desensitization, while icilin, in concert with calcium, stabilizes a fully open conformation. Together, these structures illuminate a stepwise activation pathway involving distinct symmetry transitions and define the cooperative allosteric mechanism by which PIP2 and cooling agonists gate the channel.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Transient receptor potential channels, Permeation and transport, Protein function predictions

Introduction

Menthol, a natural cooling compound derived from peppermint, is a selective agonist of the transient receptor potential melastatin 8 (TRPM8) channel and is widely used to induce a cooling sensation and relieve pain1. As a topical analgesic, menthol is effective against acute, inflammatory, and neuropathic pain, acting through activation of TRPM8 on cold-sensitive nerve endings2,3. At the molecular level, menthol binding lowers the threshold for TRPM8 activation, shifting the channel’s temperature sensitivity toward higher temperatures and thereby producing a cooling effect under physiological conditions4. This pharmacological modulation underlies menthol’s widespread use as a cooling analgesic in topical formulations and inhalants5,6. Menthol’s ability to sensitize TRPM8 has long been recognized, supported by extensive functional studies. In addition, previous studies using mutagenesis and menthol analogs have identified a binding cavity formed by the S1–S4 transmembrane segments as the likely menthol-binding pocket7–10. However, the precise molecular mechanism underlying menthol’s action remains incompletely understood.

TRPM8 is a calcium-permeable, non-selective cation channel expressed in a subset of peripheral neurons and is activated by cool temperatures, mediating the perception of environmental cold (15–28 °C)11–13. In addition to its role in thermosensation, TRPM8 is implicated in pain modulation and contributes to various pathological states, including inflammatory pain, cold allodynia, migraine, prostate cancer, and dry eye disease2,14–17. Recently, acoltremon (also known as WS-12)—a drug containing a menthol moiety—was FDA-approved for treating dry eye disease by specifically activating TPRM818. Consequently, TRPM8 activation is associated with variety of physiological effects, reflecting its significance and potential as a drug target19,20.

Phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) is a crucial lipid cofactor required for TRPM8 gating21,22. TRPM8 strictly depends on PIP2 for its activity: channel desensitization occurs upon PIP2 depletion or excision of membrane patches, whereas application of exogenous PIP2 can reactivate or even directly open the channel in the absence of other stimuli4,21,22. Notably, PIP2 not only permits TRPM8 activation but also functions synergistically with menthol and other cooling agonists21–23. Electrophysiological studies have suggested that PIP2 and agonist binding are allosterically coupled, such that the presence of one enhances the efficacy of the other21. Therefore, it is essential to disentangle the individual and cooperative roles of PIP2 and menthol in TRPM8 gating.

Here, we present a series of cryo-EM structures of full-length TRPM8 in lipid nanodiscs, captured in complex with PIP2, menthol, and icilin. Our analysis reveals a previously uncharacterized, two-fold symmetric conformation induced by PIP2, suggesting a potential activation pathway initiated by symmetry breaking. Furthermore, our structures in the presence of menthol and PIP2 resolve the menthol-binding pocket and support a model where menthol potentiates PIP2’s effect by stabilizing its association with the channel. This mechanism appears to prevent or reverse the formation of the desensitized state. Finally, the icilin-bound structure captures the channel in an open conformation. Together, these structures provide key mechanistic insights into how PIP2, menthol, and icilin differentially modulate TRPM8, elucidating the complex gating landscape of this polymodal ion channel.

Results

Stepwise cryo-EM analysis captures symmetry remodeling during TRPM8 activation

To elucidate the activation mechanism of TRPM8 by PIP2, menthol, and icilin, full-length mouse TRPM8 was cloned into a pEG BacMam vector, incorporating an N-terminal maltose-binding protein (MBP) tag and a C-terminal FLAG tag to facilitate purification. Baculovirus generated from this construct was used to express the channel in HEK293S GnTI⁻ cells. Following solubilization in lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG) and cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS), the protein was purified and reconstituted into MSP2N2 nanodiscs, yielding four-fold symmetric structures corresponding to the closed and desensitized conformations24,25 (Fig. 1a–c, and Supplementary Fig. 1a–c, e). Notably, CHS was observed near the PIP2-binding pocket at the cleft between the voltage sensing-like domain (VSLD) and the pre-S1 domain, consistent with previous reports23,25,26 (Figs. 1a and 2f). This closed conformation served as a reference for subsequent structural comparisons, and all following samples were prepared in the absence of CHS to minimize its interference with PIP2 binding. Removal of CHS alone resulted in altered conformational diversity; the desensitized conformation persisted without CHS density, while a putative conformation co-purified with endogenous PIP2 became detectable (Supplementary Fig. 2).

We next determined the structure of TRPM8 bound to PIP2 in the absence of CHS by adding diC8-PIP2 during grid preparation. This approach yielded four distinct conformations: one desensitized and three intermediate conformations, referred to as intermediates 1, 2, and 3 (Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Fig. 1d, f). Remarkably, the PIP2-induced intermediate 1 displayed two-fold symmetry, unlike the four-fold symmetric intermediates 2 and 3 and previously reported TRPM8 structures, revealing a conformation that was stably resolved under our experimental conditions without CHS. In all three intermediate conformations, PIP2 density was clearly resolved at the PIP2-binding pocket (Figs. 2g, h and 3c, d). Following the addition of menthol or icilin to the PIP2-bound TRPM8, we captured a series of cryo-EM structures that represent distinct states along a potential pathway of agonist-driven symmetry remodeling during channel activation (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Figs. 3, 4, and Supplementary Video 1).

Overall architecture of TRPM8 in a PIP2-induced two-fold symmetric conformation

Two-fold symmetry has been reported in several TRP channel subtypes, including TRPV227,28, TRPV329–31, TRPV432,33, TRPM234, and TRP135. In most cases, ligand binding induces a symmetry reduction from the four-fold symmetric apo state, driven by rigid-body tilting or local rearrangements of individual subunits within the tetramer. Despite the distinct symmetry changes, the structural similarity and sequential relationship between the two-fold and four-fold symmetric tetramers have limited the biochemical and functional validation of these symmetry-reduced intermediates.

In our structure, the two-fold symmetric TRPM8 conformation (intermediate 1) adopts a rectangular geometry, measuring 34 Å × 40 Å based on the distance between the Cα atoms of I957 in the S6 helices (Fig. 2b, top). In contrast, the closed conformation, which exhibits four-fold symmetry similar to previously determined structures across different species23–26, forms a square-shaped arrangement measuring 34 Å × 34 Å (Fig. 2a, top). The asymmetry between the two protomers (hereafter referred to as chains A and B) in the two-fold symmetric conformation becomes even more apparent in the front view, where chain A adopts a constricted conformation (55 Å), whereas chain B appears expanded (75 Å) (Fig. 2b, bottom). Furthermore, structural alignment of the two subunits reveals that chains A and B adopt markedly distinct conformations in both VSLD and pore domain (PD) simultaneously (Fig. 2c). Specifically, chain A’s VSLD closely resembles that of intermediate 2, the four-fold symmetric conformation coexisted in the same cryo-EM dataset, while its PD undergoes a dramatic conformational change, characterized by a kink at residues V867–F869 in the S5 helix (Fig. 2d, top, and Supplementary Fig. 5d, h). In contrast, chain B’s PD remains similar to that of intermediate 2, whereas its VSLD exhibits a notable upward translation (Fig. 2d, bottom). Collectively, these observations highlight the extent of conformational heterogeneity present in the two-fold symmetric conformation.

The local asymmetry between subunits further propagates into global rearrangements, particularly affecting intersubunit interactions. In the closed conformation, the PD of each subunit closely interfaces with the S4 helix of the adjacent protomer and also lies in proximity to the S1 helix (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). However, in the two-fold symmetric conformation, the reorientation of chain B’s VSLD appears to displace chain A’s S5 helix, leading to three key structural consequences (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). First, the outward displacement of chain B’s S4 helix induces a kink in chain A’s S5 helix, creating an almost vertical alignment between chain A’s S5 and chain B’s S4 helices (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Second, this rearrangement includes the S2–S3 helices of chain B elevating upward, forming an exposed VSLD cavity and increasing their distance from the MHR4 domain of the same protomer to approximately 18 Å—in contrast to ~15 Å in the closed conformation and ~9 Å in chain A of the two-fold intermediate 1 (Supplementary Fig. 5e–g, i–l). Third, the S6 helix of chain A is no longer positioned near chain B’s S4 helix, but instead shifts toward the elevated S3 helix of chain B, disrupting canonical PD–VSLD coupling (Supplementary Fig. 6d). This reconfiguration renders the VSLD of chain B largely solvent-exposed, and notably, calcium binding is not observed (Supplementary Fig. 5k).

From the perspective of chain B, the PD remains engaged with the neighboring protomer chain C’s S4 helix (structurally equivalent to that of chain A), preserving more of the canonical intersubunit architecture (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). Notably, the S1–S4 helices of chain C undergo a clockwise rotation, when viewed from the extracellular side, resulting in closer spatial proximity between chain C’s S1 and chain B’s S5 helices, along with chain C’s S5 helix forming a kink to surround chain B’s S6 helix (Supplementary Fig. 6f). Additionally, chain B’s S6 helix is further displaced from chain C’s S3 helix. These structural changes of various conformations result in altered intersubunit interactions with respect to S5 helix, S6 helix, and their combined contacts with the adjacent subunit (Supplementary Fig. 6g–i). This alteration is quantitatively assessed in the heatmaps, which illustrate the similarity of intersubunit interaction sets across different conformations. Notably, the S5 helix of chain A shows noticeably low similarity (0.14–0.31) to other conformations—unlike that of chain B (0.53–0.82)—as reflected by the lighter colors in the heatmap (Supplementary Fig. 6g). Together, these observations suggest that the two-fold symmetry induced by PIP2 not only shows distinct individual protomer architecture but also reorganized intersubunit interactions at the quaternary level.

When the pore constriction residues—modulated by S6 helix rotation in response to various stimuli—were examined (Fig. 1b), the two-fold symmetric TRPM8 conformation was found to feature a pore constriction formed by F979 and V983, consistent with previously reported PIP2-bound structures10,23,36. This contrasts with the closed conformation, where the constriction involves M978 and F979 (Figs. 1b and 2e, and Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). In detail, the side chains of F979 in chains A and C project inward, tightly constricting the pore to a minimum distance of approximately 3.2 Å (Supplementary Fig. 5b). By contrast, in chains B and D, the phenyl rings are oriented upward, making V983 the primary constriction site, a configuration reminiscent of the four-fold symmetric intermediate conformations of TRPM8 (Fig. 1b, and Supplementary Fig. 5c).

Asymmetric features in the two-fold symmetric conformation

Among the structural differences observed between the two subunits in the two-fold symmetric conformation, PIP2 binding emerged as a notable point of divergence. While an α-to-310 helical transition in the S4 helix—the characteristic structural change associated with PIP2 incorporation10,23—is observed preceding the S4–S5 linker in both subunits, the exact PIP2 binding modes differ (Fig. 2g, h, k, l). In chain A, PIP2 is tightly bound, with clear density for the phosphate head group and well-resolved acyl chains (Fig. 2g). In contrast, chain B exhibits weaker PIP2 binding—head group shifting away from the S4–S5 linker and acyl chains stretching toward the outside of the pocket—although not as far as the previously reported avian TRPM8 structure (PDB: 6NR2)10 (Fig. 2h, and Supplementary Fig. 7a). The PIP2 molecule in that study was positioned distant from the S4–S5 linker, resembling features of our closed conformation, including an α-helix in S4 and a tilted TRP helix (Fig. 2f).

This positional shift of PIP2 in chain B correlates with altered electrostatic interactions. Key positively charged residues—including R851 and N852—are positioned 1–2 Å farther from the phosphate head group compared to chain A (Fig. 2g, h). Consequently, R851 on the S4–S5 linker and N852 lie beyond effective interaction range, indicating a local loosening of PIP2 coordination in chain B. Notably, this displacement enables R851 to form another electrostatic interaction with D991 in the TRP helix—a contact that remains too distant to form in chain A (Fig. 2k, l). While this R851–D991 coupling is also observed in other PIP2-bound conformations (Supplementary Fig. 7d), a separate asymmetric interaction unique to the two-fold conformation was identified: Y808 in the S3 helix (chains B and D) forms a hydrogen bond with the backbone carbonyl of L959 in the S6 helix (chains A and C) (Fig. 2j, and Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Despite this distinct structural feature, a Y808 mutation did not alter the menthol-evoked activation profile of TRPM8 (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e). This suggests that while the Y808–L959 interaction uniquely characterizes the structural framework of this transient, PIP2-induced asymmetric conformation, it might not represent an obligatory requirement for global channel activation.

Another distinction between the two subunits is the presence of calcium within the VSLD. While no calcium-like density was observed in the closed conformation or in chain B of the two-fold intermediate 1, chain A displayed a prominent calcium density positioned between the S2–S3 linker (Supplementary Fig. 5e–g). This calcium ion disrupts the electrostatic triad formed by Y745, R842, and D802 by reorienting R842 and D802 towards the calcium ion (Supplementary Fig. 5i–k). To evaluate whether asymmetric calcium binding contributes to the formation of the two-fold symmetry, we solved the cryo-EM structure of TRPM8 in complex with PIP2 under EGTA treatment (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Among the resulting conformations, calcium densities disappeared in both intermediate 2 and a desensitized conformation, yet the two-fold intermediate 1 conformation persisted—distinguished by the defined kink in the S5 helix of chain A (Supplementary Fig. 9b–d). Overall, these observations suggest that the asymmetric calcium binding between chains A and B is not the primary driver of symmetry reduction, but rather a consequence of PIP2-induced symmetry breaking.

PIP2-induced conformational ensemble of TRPM8

In addition to the two-fold symmetric conformation (intermediate 1)—which represents the predominant population in the same dataset (~47%) (Fig. 1c)—two distinct four-fold symmetric PIP2-bound conformations were also resolved, referred to as intermediate 2 and intermediate 3 (Fig. 3, and Supplementary Figs. 1f and 10). When aligned and viewed from top, the VSLD and PD of intermediate 2 exhibit a counterclockwise rotation relative to the closed conformation, whereas intermediate 3 is rotated even further in the counterclockwise direction compared to intermediate 2 (Fig. 3b). In addition, despite the clear inositol head group density of PIP2 observed in both conformations, the interaction of PIP2 with surrounding residues in intermediate 3 appears weaker than in intermediate 2 (Fig. 3c, d).

Compared to the closed conformation, the four-fold intermediate conformations exhibit several structural changes that parallel those observed in the two-fold symmetric conformation (Fig. 3a). While the closed conformation features an α-helical S4 helix, all PIP2-bound conformations adopt a 310-helix within S4. This transition induces a sharp turn between S4 and S5, bends S5, elongates S6, and tilts the TRP helix (Fig. 3a, h, and Supplementary Fig. 10a). Furthermore, PIP2 binding disrupts the intersubunit interaction between R851 and D866 of the adjacent subunit. This frees R851 to coordinate with the PIP2 phosphate headgroup in intermediate 2, which subsequently shifts to form an electrostatic interaction with D991 in intermediate 3 (Fig. 3f, and Supplementary Fig. 7d).

Additionally, a counterclockwise rotation of the S6 helix repositions the pore constriction site—a directional change consistent with the agonist-induced channel opening previously described for TRPM823 (Fig. 3g). Notably, the intermediate 2 structure incorporates calcium ions within the VSLD, further distinguishing it from the closed conformation and supporting its activated-like features (Fig. 3e).

We propose that the two-fold intermediate represents a transient conformational ensemble along with the four-fold intermediates, based on several structural observations. First, the PIP2 density and its proximity to coordinating residues are more clearly defined in the four-fold intermediate 2, indicating a stronger binding interaction with R851 (Fig. 3c, d). Conversely, the weaker PIP2 coordination observed in chain B of intermediate 1 and in intermediate 3 correlates with R851 shifting to form an electrostatic interaction with D991 (Fig. 2k, l, and Supplementary Fig. 7d). Second, the loss of the intersubunit R851–D866 interaction—a direct consequence of PIP2 binding—occurs at the site of the S5 helix kink, presumably capturing the moment immediately after interaction disruption by PIP2 (Fig. 3f). Third, the S5 helices of the two-fold intermediate 1 share structural features with the four-fold intermediates: chain A exhibits a kink similar to that in intermediate 3, while chain B shows a bend resembling the global S5 shapes of intermediates 2 and 3 (Fig. 3i). Fourth, calcium binding differs between these states: chain B of the two-fold intermediate 1 and intermediate 3 lack calcium ions, whereas the four-fold intermediate 2 exhibits clear coordination of calcium ions (Fig. 3e, and Supplementary Fig. 5f, g). Lastly, the positioning of the pore-lining residues F979 and V983 differs in a manner consistent with a progressive conformational transition—driven by the counterclockwise rotation of the S6 helix—towards channel opening (Fig. 3g).

Indeed, our observation aligns well with an emerging structural model for the activation of several TRP channels, which accommodates the possibility of a stable two-fold intermediate—as exemplified by TRPV2, TRPV3, and TRPM2—despite the absence of a universally shared stimulus (e.g., Ca2+) for channel activation28–30,34. To test a key prediction of this model for TRPM8, we next examined whether TRPM8-specific agonists that bind to the VSLD—such as menthol and icilin—can revert the symmetry to four-fold symmetry and subsequently activate it.

Menthol further drives symmetry transitions following the PIP2-induced conformation

TRPM8 is well established as the primary molecular target of menthol, making the elucidation of its precise binding mode essential for understanding the mechanism of TRPM8 activation by menthol13. However, determining the exact binding pose of menthol has been challenging due to several factors, including its small molecular size, relatively high EC5012,37–39, and the inherent difficulty of resolving small densities in cryo-EM maps.

When we resolved the two-fold symmetric intermediate conformation of TRPM8 in lipid nanodiscs, it was characterized by an elevated VSLD and an unoccupied ligand-binding cavity (Supplementary Fig. 5g, k). We hypothesized that this conformation may represent a pre-entry or primed state in the agonist-binding pathway, in which the expanded VSLD cavity facilitates ligand access, and subsequent binding could restore four-fold symmetry (Supplementary Fig. 5l). Indeed, upon addition of PIP2 and the naturally abundant stereoisomer L-menthol (hereafter referred to as menthol), we observed a well-defined non-protein density within the VSLD, consistent with menthol binding, as well as PIP2 and calcium (Figs. 1d and 4a, b).

Remarkably, in line with our hypothesis, all particles in the menthol-treated dataset exhibited four-fold symmetry, indicating symmetry restoration upon ligand binding (Supplementary Fig. 1g). In particular, all particles adopted a canonical conformation in S2–S3 helices, unlike two-fold intermediate 1’s chain B with elevated VSLD (Supplementary Fig. 5k). However, despite menthol engagement at the VSLD, the overall transmembrane domain (TMD) conformation remained similar to that of intermediate 2 conformation, with moderate translational shifts in individual helices. The minimum pore distance increased by only ~1.0 Å, indicating limited pore dilation (Figs. 1b and 4d). A similar observation was reported in TRPM8 structures treated with Cryosim-3, a compound that also targets the VSLD and exhibits an EC50 of 0.9–10.3 µM17,23. Taken together, these findings indicate that menthol binding further drives the PIP2-induced transition from two-fold to four-fold symmetry, leading us to name the menthol-bound conformation as a sensitized, yet non-conducting conformation.

Menthol recognition and its PIP2-dependent modulation in TRPM8

Previously, the structure of avian TRPM8 was resolved in complex with WS-12 (PDB: 6NR2), a menthol analog exhibiting approximately 1000-fold higher potency (i.e., lower EC50) than menthol10,37,40 (Supplementary Fig. 11a). WS-12 features an extended methoxyphenyl group tethered to the menthol moiety via an amide linkage37,41. This additional amide functionality enables key interactions with residues R842, Y1005, and R1008 (corresponding to R841, Y1004, and R1007 in Ficedula albicollis), thereby stabilizing the ligand within the VSLD binding pocket (Supplementary Fig. 11c). Despite the engagement of both PIP2 and WS-12, the channel remained in a non-conducting conformation (Supplementary Fig. 11b–d). In our structure, menthol binds within the same VSLD cavity—a site previously associated with both agonist and antagonist binding in TRPM810,23,25,36,42 (Fig. 4a). Compared to the WS-12–bound avian TRPM8 structure, the menthol moiety adopts a slightly shifted position, likely due to the absence of the extended amide linkage present in WS-12 (Supplementary Fig. 11c).

Menthol is located between the S1 and S4 helices, primarily engaging R842 on the S4 helix—a residue extensively studied for its essential role in menthol sensing8,9 (Fig. 4b, c). Notably, R842 appears to form a network of polar interactions connecting menthol, D802, and calcium, resulting in a side chain translation of 1.8 Å upon menthol binding (Fig. 4c, g). Likewise, H845—a residue previously reported to have reduced effect on menthol sensitivity8—underwent a rotational shift (displacement of ~1.8 Å) of its imidazole side chain upon menthol binding (Fig. 4g). Compared to the wild-type (WT) TRPM8, alanine substitution of these side chain-translating residues significantly reduced current responses in whole-cell patch-clamp recordings, supporting their contribution to menthol binding (Fig. 4e, and Supplementary Fig. 11e–h).

Another critical residue, Y745, is positioned directly above the menthol binding site, contributing to a hydrophobic environment that stabilizes the ligand43. In our structure, additional hydrophobic residues within the VSLD, such as F738, V742, and I846, further define the binding pocket (Fig. 4b). Beyond the VSLD, Y1005 from the TRP helix contributes to ligand stabilization. This residue has previously been shown to reduce menthol sensitivity when mutated, which is consistent with its role in ligand recognition and channel gating7. Notably, F1013, located immediately after the TRP helix and preceding the connecting helix and coiled-coil domain, is displaced by approximately 3.3 Å upon menthol binding (Fig. 4g). Alanine substitutions at Y745, Y1005, and F1013 significantly impaired menthol-induced TRPM8 activation, further supporting their functional importance (Fig. 4e, f). Furthermore, introducing a polar hydroxyl group at this position (F1013Y) reduced but did not abolish the menthol-induced current, implying that local polarity alterations partially impair menthol-dependent activation by reshaping the binding pocket (Supplementary Fig. 11j).

To further examine the exact menthol binding site and engaged residues, we performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using 500-ns all-atom trajectories in a lipid bilayer environment (Supplementary Fig. 12). The simulations showed that menthol frequently occupies a position near the modeled site, maintaining the distances with the identified residues to a certain extent (Supplementary Fig. 12c, d). Conformational clustering of the menthol poses indicated that four out of six clusters localized to positions similar to the experimentally determined binding pocket (Supplementary Fig. 12e). Given the relatively weak binding affinity of menthol, these alternative poses—which were observed in 25% of the total MD trajectory frames—likely reflect transient or weakly stabilized interactions (Supplementary Fig. 12f). Together, these simulation results support the proposed menthol-binding site by demonstrating overall consistency with our structural model, while also capturing a minor degree of positional heterogeneity.

Subsequently, to validate allosteric modulation of menthol-evoked activation mediated by PIP2, we performed electrophysiological experiments using three complementary approaches. First, attenuation of PIP2 binding via the N852A mutant, which exhibits weak interaction with PIP2 in the two-fold intermediate 1’s chain B but maintains tight binding in chain A, resulted in a reduction of menthol-evoked currents (Fig. 4e, f, and Supplementary Fig. 11i). Second, depletion of cellular PIP2 levels using poly-L-lysine (PLL) suppressed menthol-induced responses (Fig. 4i). Third, stimulation of voltage-sensing phosphatase (VSP) to deplete PIP2 in the plasma membrane similarly suppressed the menthol-induced response (Fig. 4j, and Supplementary Fig. 11k–n). These parallel experiments further support the critical interplay between PIP2 binding and menthol-mediated activation.

Cryo-EM structure of icilin-bound TRPM8 in the open conformation

We next investigated whether the more potent agonist icilin with a reported approximate EC50 of 200 nM39 could induce a distinct conformation compared to menthol, while still promoting the transition from the PIP2-bound two-fold symmetric intermediate to a four-fold symmetric conformation. Of note, while high-resolution structures of icilin-bound TRPM8 have been previously reported and its binding has been identified, one was resolved in a different species10, and the others captured the channel in a non-conducting conformation in the absence of PIP242 (Supplementary Fig. 13a, b).

By treating PIP2 with icilin, we determined the cryo-EM structures of icilin-bound mouse TRPM8, which revealed both an open and a desensitized conformation, each bound to icilin (Supplementary Fig. 1h, j). Regardless of the conformation—open or desensitized—clear density for icilin was observed at the VSLD cavity (Fig. 5b, c, f, m, and Supplementary Fig. 13c, d). Consistent with the menthol-bound structure, icilin also promoted a transition from two-fold to four-fold symmetry.

Although we applied the same incubation protocol as used for menthol (~1 min incubation prior to grid preparation), we observed a subtly distinct feature, characterized by the presence of extracellular pore loop density—a feature typically associated with the desensitized conformation25 (Fig. 5a, d, h, j). Interestingly, after a 10-min incubation of icilin, the same distribution of open and desensitized structures was observed, but the extracellular pore loop (residues 913–950) was no longer visible in the open conformation (Supplementary Fig. 14a–e). This difference in the extracellular pore loop density between the 1- and 10-min incubation samples was consistently reproduced. While the precise origin of this change is unclear, it may reflect factors such as the kinetics of ligand equilibration or shifts in conformational stability over time on the cryo-EM grid, and warrants further investigation.

Detailed analysis of icilin binding revealed similar interacting residues as menthol, including Y745, R842, H845, and Y1005, which contribute to interactions within the VSLD cavity (Fig. 5b). Mutagenesis of these residues abolished icilin-evoked channel activation, whereas the F1013A mutant significantly reduced the maximal current relative to the WT (Supplementary Fig. 15a–c, e). Surprisingly, F1013Y, a residue shown to impair menthol-evoked activation, exhibited comparable to even higher icilin-induced currents (Supplementary Fig. 15f). Furthermore, the PIP2-disrupting N852A mutant displayed a similar reduction in icilin responses as observed for menthol, suggesting that PIP2 engagement is critical for both agonists (Supplementary Fig. 15d).

Calcium, which directly binds to the VSLD cavity, has previously been reported to be essential in icilin-dependent TRPM8 activation44. In our structures, icilin binding induced a ~1.5 Å shift of the calcium ion toward the ligand, reducing the calcium–icilin distance to ~5.3 Å (Fig. 5c, f). This differs from the menthol-bound structure, where the calcium–ligand distance remains at ~7.8 Å (Fig. 4c). These observations suggest that calcium-dependent potentiation of icilin occurs via an indirect interaction network centered on D802, which coordinates both calcium and R842—a critical interaction residue for both menthol and icilin. In this context, calcium may enhance agonist efficacy by stabilizing the binding environment through D802–R842 coupling, thereby modulating the VSLD conformation in an agonist-specific manner.

Unlike menthol binding, icilin binding resulted in α-to-π helical transition in the S6 helix, displacing residues F979 and V983 away from the pore axis, resulting in an expanded pore (~7.7 Å) at V976 (Fig. 5d, i). This dilation widens the pore sufficiently to accommodate hydrated ions, indicative of an open state (Fig. 5e). This icilin-induced pore-opening mechanism resembles that of the previously reported mouse TRPM8 structure (PDB: 8E4L) activated by Cryosim-3 and allyl isothiocyanate (AITC)23 (Supplementary Fig. 15m, n). In that structure, the α-to-π transition occurred nearly one helical turn higher, potentially driven by the presence of the second agonist (AITC) proximal to the PD. Taken together, our icilin-bound TRPM8 structure in the presence of PIP2 represents a distinct open conformation.

Desensitized conformation of TRPM8 observed across datasets

While the icilin- and PIP2-bound TRPM8 represents an open conformation, we also observed a coexisting structural class within the same dataset—namely, a desensitized conformation (Figs. 1c and 5j). The term desensitized was originally introduced following its identification in the calcium-treated avian TRPM8 structure25. Since then, several studies have reported structures resembling desensitized states under varying ligand and lipid conditions36,42. In our study, despite the inclusion of excess PIP2 during grid preparation, a ~25% population of PIP2-deficient desensitized conformations consistently appeared across multiple datasets—a population notably absent in the menthol-treated dataset (Fig. 1c, and Supplementary Fig. 16). This observation suggests that menthol may resist PIP2-depleted desensitization by promoting or maintaining PIP2 binding. Consistently, menthol has been shown to potentiate the agonistic effect of PIP2 by lowering its EC5021.

The desensitized conformations observed across other datasets were nearly identical, regardless of ligand binding in the VSLD, including icilin. A Cα root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis across the TMD, comparing the desensitized and the open conformations from the icilin-treated dataset, revealed an RMSD of ~1.4 Å (Fig. 5j). This structural divergence is driven by a cascade of three coupled rearrangements. First, the absence of PIP2 density suggests its dissociation from the channel (Fig. 5i), allowing the TRP helix to reorient away from the vacant binding site. This uncoupling subsequently propagates to the S6 helix; although it retains a π-helix at the same residues, the helix shifts inward to narrow the pore pathway (Fig. 5k, l). Ultimately, these concerted rearrangements tightly constrict the pore at V976, reducing the minimum diameter to ~5.6 Å—a dimension consistent with a non-conducting state (Fig. 5n).

Overall, the consistent appearance of this conformation across datasets supports the interpretation that it reflects a PIP2-depleted state or a state induced after full activation (Fig. 1c). This also suggests that the various functional states, such as the intermediate, open, and desensitized conformations, can interconvert depending on the presence or depletion of PIP2 or on the agonist engagement, and are therefore predicted to be thermodynamically coupled (Fig. 6).

In this model, the cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS)-bound closed conformation is depicted as capable of transitioning to a PIP2-bound intermediate 1, 2, and 3, which are shown to exist in dynamic equilibrium. Subsequent binding of specific ligands is proposed to induce distinct downstream conformations; menthol binding leads to the sensitized conformation, while icilin binding leads to the open conformation. In the sensitized state, menthol is hypothesized to stabilize PIP2 binding, thereby potentially preventing PIP2 dissociation and inhibiting progression toward the desensitized conformation. Conversely, the open conformation is characterized by the formation of a π-helix within the S6 helix and a dilation of the pore, suggesting a transition toward channel opening. Discussion

Here, we report a PIP2-induced two-fold symmetric conformation of TRPM8 in lipid nanodiscs, revealing a previously uncharacterized intermediate state. The observation of a symmetry-broken state supports a model in which PIP2 binding primes the channel for activation by destabilizing the four-fold symmetric closed conformation. It is also plausible that PIP2 binding enhances agonist engagement, providing an entry pathway into the VSLD cavity. This is supported by our observation of open agonist-binding pockets in the two-fold symmetric intermediate (Supplementary Fig. 5l). While the precise physiological role and kinetics of this transient, two-fold symmetric state await future investigation, our structure provides an unprecedented snapshot of a critical symmetry-breaking event in the TRPM8 activation pathway. It offers crucial mechanistic insight into the dynamic role of PIP2 that could not be understood from static four-fold symmetric structures alone.

The TRP channel family exhibits remarkable conformational plasticity. Recent structural studies have revealed a plethora of architectural diversity, including two-fold symmetric states (TRP1, TRPM2, TRPV2, TRPV3, TRPV4, TRPM8)27–35, heteromeric complexes (TRPC1/TRPC4, TRPC1/TRPC5, Nanchung/Inactive)45–47, and even pentameric assemblies (TRPV3, Nanchung)47–49, as well as inverted domain-swapped (TRPM1)50 and non-domain-swapped (TRPV4, TRPM8) arrangements51,52. Although structural biology has successfully captured these diverse states, delineating their precise functional roles remains challenging due to the difficulty of isolating transient conformations in functional assays. For example, in our two-fold symmetric intermediate, the asymmetric Y808 interaction in chains B and D is clearly resolved in the presence of PIP2 (Supplementary Fig. 8a). However, electrophysiological evaluation of Y808 mutants revealed no significant deficits in menthol-evoked activation (Supplementary Fig. 8d, e). This suggests that standard macroscopic recordings may lack the requisite kinetic resolution to capture the nuances of such pre-active intermediates, underscoring the need for advanced kinetic or single-molecule approaches to fully dissect these transient conformational states. Additionally, this information does not conflict with the previous report of TRPM8 not acting as two dimers, as this represents one of PIP2-bound conformations rather than the activated conducting state53.

Another notable observation is the complete disappearance of desensitized-conformation particles upon menthol binding. TRPM8 desensitization has been reported to be associated with PIP2 depletion or membrane excision4,21,22, potentially mediated by cellular factors such as phospholipase C (PLC). While these desensitized forms persisted in our purified samples even after the addition of excess soluble PIP2, subsequent menthol treatment eliminated them. A plausible explanation for this is that menthol binding promotes or stabilizes the association of PIP2 with TRPM8. Such a synergistic effect would shift the equilibrium away from the unbound (desensitized) state and toward the PIP2-bound conformation. This hypothesis is consistent with previous electrophysiological data showing that menthol potentiates PIP2 by lowering its EC5021, and our structural findings provide a potential molecular correlate for this allosteric regulation.

These observations highlight the distinct agonism of menthol compared to icilin, despite their engagement of overlapping residues within the VSLD cavity and the shared reliance on PIP2 binding. Unlike menthol or its analog WS-12, icilin requires calcium to fully activate TRPM8 and does so with higher potency54. Moreover, our structural analysis reveals that icilin binds irrespective of the channel’s conformational state, accommodating both the PIP2-bound and desensitized forms. In contrast, menthol binding appears to destabilize the desensitized conformation, thereby promoting PIP2 association and facilitating channel activation.

Additionally, icilin binding to the PIP2-bound sample captured an open conformation characterized by an α-to-π transition in the S6 helix—a well-established hallmark of TRP channel gating55. In TRPM8, a π-helix within S6 has been observed in two functional states, the open (PDB: 8E4L and this study) and the desensitized conformations. Interestingly, while the presence of the π-helix is conserved, its register shifts depending on the state: spanning residues 963–967 in the previously reported open conformation, and 966–969 in the icilin-bound open conformation or the desensitized conformation. These observations support a model in which α-to-π transitions contribute to both activation and desensitization, whereas the closed and intermediate states maintain a fully α-helical S6 segment.

During the preparation of our manuscript, two independent groups reported structural insights into TRPM8 activation by menthol and cold stimuli52,56. Choi et al. proposed a semi-swapped architecture in native membranes, and suggested a π-to-α transition in the S6 helix as a key step during channel opening52. In contrast, Lee et al. reported a fully-swapped architecture and observed an α-to-π transition, consistent with the previously reported open-state TRPM8 structure23,56. Taken together, these findings suggest that S6 helical transitions between α and π conformations are bidirectional, and their directionality may be context-dependent, influenced by factors such as membrane environment, ligand identity, or temperature. Within this framework, our structure captures a unique open conformation stabilized by a single agonist (icilin) bound to the VSLD. While further studies are needed to fully elucidate the polymodal gating mechanism of TRPM8, our findings highlight the existence of distinct activation pathways and conformational transitions driven by diverse stimuli, including menthol, icilin, and PIP2.

Methods

Expression and purification of TRPM8 in lipid nanodiscs

The codon-optimized full-length mouse TRPM8 (residues 1–1104) was synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies and cloned into an in-house–modified pEG BacMam vector containing an N-terminal maltose-binding protein (MBP) tag, a human rhinovirus 3C (HRV-3C) protease cleavage site, and a C-terminal FLAG tag for affinity purification. The construct was expressed in HEK293S GnTI⁻ suspension cells (ATCC) via baculovirus-mediated transduction. Cells were cultured in ESF SFM media (Expression Systems) supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin and 1% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), maintained at 37 °C with 8% CO2. Baculovirus was generated using Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) cells and amplified following the Bac-to-Bac Baculovirus Expression System protocol (Life Technologies). HEK293S cells at ~2 × 106 cells ml−1 were infected with 5% (v/v) of P3 virus for 12–16 h. Sodium butyrate (10 mM), 0.2% glucose, and 0.3% tryptone were then added, and the culture was shifted to 30 °C. After 48–50 h, cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and stored at –80 °C.

Cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer containing 50 mM HEPES-NaOH pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 4 µg ml−1 leupeptin, 4 µM pepstatin, 4 µM aprotinin, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Cells were lysed using a Dounce homogenizer, and membrane proteins were solubilized by gentle rotation at 4 °C for 2 h in the presence of 1% lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG, w/v) and 0.1% cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS, w/v; Anatrace). The lysate was centrifuged at 25,000 × g for 30 min, and the clarified supernatant was incubated with anti-FLAG resin for 3 h at 4 °C. The resin was packed into a gravity-flow column (Bio-Rad) and washed with 50 column volumes (CV) of wash buffer (50 mM HEPES-NaOH pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.01% LMNG, and 0.001% CHS). Protein was eluted with 5 CV of wash buffer supplemented with 0.2 mg ml−1 FLAG peptide and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT). The eluate was concentrated to 1 mg ml−1 using a 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) centrifugal filter (Amicon Ultra, Millipore) for nanodisc reconstitution.

MSP2N2 was expressed in Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) cells (Invitrogen) transformed with a plasmid encoding a C-terminal His-tagged construct. Cultures were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium and induced at OD600 = 1.0 with 1 mM IPTG. After 4 h of induction at 18 °C, cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1 mM PMSF, 1% Triton X-100, and 5 mM imidazole, and lysed using a homogenizer (Micronox). The lysate was clarified by centrifugation (25,000 × g, 30 min), and MSP2N2 was purified using Ni-affinity chromatography. Eluted fractions were buffer-exchanged into 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM EDTA, concentrated to 8.5 mg ml−1, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage at –80 °C.

For nanodisc reconstitution, TRPM8 was mixed with MSP2N2 and a lipid mixture of 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC), -phosphoethanolamine (POPE), and -phosphoglycerol (POPG) in a 3:1:1 molar ratio at a final TRPM8:MSP2N2:lipid molar ratio of 1:1:15. The mixture was incubated on ice for 30 min. Detergent removal was achieved by incubation with Bio-Beads SM-2 (Bio-Rad) at 50 mg ml−1 for 2 h at 4 °C, followed by replacement with fresh beads and overnight incubation (~12 h). In-house purified 3C protease was then added at a 1:10 (v/v) ratio to remove the MBP tag. The nanodisc-reconstituted TRPM8 complex was purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated with 20 mM HEPES-NaOH pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT. Peak fractions were pooled and concentrated to 4.5 mg ml−1 for structural studies. For CHS-free samples, the same purification procedure was followed using LMNG alone (without CHS) in all buffers.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

Prior to cryo-EM grid preparation, the homogeneity and stability of purified TRPM8 particles were assessed using negative-stain electron microscopy. A 3 µl aliquot of protein sample at a concentration of 0.05 mg ml−1 was applied to a glow-discharged 400-mesh copper grid coated with a continuous carbon film (Ted Pella, Inc.), followed by staining with 1% (w/v) uranyl acetate. Grids were imaged on a FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 120 kV with a nominal magnification of 13,500× using a Gatan camera at the Seoul National University (SNU) Center for Macromolecular and Cell Imaging (CMCI).

For cryo-EM grid preparation of LMNG/CHS-solubilized samples, 4 µl of purified protein was applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 300-mesh copper holey carbon grids with a 2 nm continuous carbon support layer. Grids were blotted for 7.0 s at 4 °C under 100% humidity and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI). Micrographs were collected on a Glacios transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 200 kV, equipped with a Falcon 4 camera and using the EPU automated data acquisition software at SNU CMCI. Movies were recorded at a nominal magnification of 92,000×, corresponding to a physical pixel size of 1.105 Å, over a defocus range of −0.9 to −2.4 µm. Each movie comprised 40 frames with a total electron dose of 48.83 e⁻/Å2. A total of 1657 micrographs were collected.

For samples incubated without CHS and with diC8-PI(4,5)P2 (Echelon Biosciences), grid preparation was performed at 4 °C or 20 °C after incubation with 1 mM diC8-PIP2. Micrographs were acquired on a Titan Krios G4 electron microscope operated at 300 kV, equipped with a Gatan K3 detector and a BioQuantum energy filter (20 eV slit width), located at the Institute for Basic Science (IBS). Movies were acquired at 130,000× magnification with a physical pixel size of 0.848 Å, using a defocus range of −0.7 to −1.7 µm. Each movie consisted of 57 and 48 frames with a total dose of 67.7 and 69.9 e⁻/Å2, respectively. A total of 1200 and 14,471 micrographs were collected.

For diC8-PIP2/EGTA and diC8-PIP2/menthol conditions, grids were prepared at 20 °C following incubation with 1 mM diC8-PIP2, and subsequent treatment with either 5 mM EGTA or 1 mM L-menthol (Sigma). Data collection was carried out on a Titan Krios G4 microscope operating at 300 kV and equipped with a Falcon 4i detector and a 10 eV slit width energy filter at the SNU National Center for Inter-university Research Facilities (NCIRF). Movies were acquired at a magnification of 130,000× with a physical pixel size of 0.966 Å, and a nominal defocus range of −1.0 to −2.0 µm. A total of 2318 (EGTA) and 6667 (menthol) movies were acquired (40 frames each) with a total dose of 40 e⁻/Å2.

For the diC8-PIP2/icilin condition, grids were prepared at 20 °C by incubating the protein sample with 1 mM diC8-PIP2 followed by treatment with 200 µM icilin (Sigma) for either 1 min or 10 min. Micrographs were collected on a Titan Krios G4 microscope operating at 300 kV with a Falcon 4i detector at the Core Research Facilities of Pusan National University. Movies were acquired at a magnification of 130,000× with a physical pixel size of 1.05 Å, and a defocus range of −1.0 to −2.0 µm. A total of 4003 (1 min) and 13,172 (10 min) movies were collected, each consisting of 49 frames and a total dose of 50 e⁻/Å2.

Cryo-EM data processing

All data were processed using CryoSPARC v4.4.057 and RELION v4.058. For all datasets, movie frames were preprocessed using patch motion correction followed by patch contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation in CryoSPARC. After CTF estimation, micrographs were manually inspected, and poor-quality images and outliers based on defocus values, ice thickness, or astigmatism were discarded. Blob picker was initially used to generate templates, which were then refined through several rounds of 2D classification and used for template-based particle picking.

For the CHS-present dataset, 2D class averages were used to pick particles. After three rounds of 2D classification, 145,410 particles were subjected to ab initio reconstruction and homogeneous refinement with C4 symmetry. The resulting volume was used to generate a transmembrane domain (TMD)-focused mask. Using this mask, 3D classification yielded five classes. Particles corresponding to the closed and desensitized conformations were pooled and refined using non-uniform refinement with C4 symmetry, with optimization of per-particle defocus and per-group CTF parameters. Final resolutions, determined by gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (GSFSC) at the 0.143 criterion, were 3.43 Å for the closed conformation and 3.25 Å for the desensitized conformation.

For the CHS-absent dataset, 2D class averages were used to pick particles, resulting in 83,503 particles after three rounds of 2D classification. These particles were subjected to ab initio reconstruction, homogeneous refinement with C4 symmetry, and 3D classification using a TMD-focused mask. Out of the three classes, 53,106 particles corresponded to a putative intermediate 3-like conformation, and 29,996 particles corresponded to a putative desensitized-like conformation lacking CHS density. The particle sets were independently subjected to reference-based motion correction and non-uniform refinement with C4 symmetry, yielding final GSFSC resolutions of 3.71 Å and 3.95 Å, respectively.

For the PIP2-treated dataset, particles were extracted using Topaz59, resulting in 553,313 particles after six rounds of 2D classification. These were subjected to ab initio reconstruction, homogeneous refinement, and heterogeneous refinement, which classified particles into two major groups corresponding to a two-fold symmetric intermediate and a desensitized conformation. Each class was processed separately. Further 3D classification refined the intermediate into two conformations: a four-fold symmetric intermediate (intermediate 2) at 3.29 Å, and a two-fold symmetric intermediate (intermediate 1) at 2.82 Å. The desensitized particles were classified by heterogeneous refinement and refined into a four-fold symmetric intermediate (intermediate 3) at 3.48 Å and a four-fold symmetric desensitized conformation at 2.81 Å.

For the PIP2 + EGTA dataset, Topaz was again used for particle extraction. Two rounds of 2D classification resulted in 122,820 particles. After ab initio reconstruction, homogeneous refinement, and 3D classification, particles were further refined using non-uniform refinement with the appropriate symmetry. This dataset revealed three major conformations: a two-fold symmetric intermediate, a four-fold symmetric intermediate, and a desensitized conformation. Due to the limited resolution and the dataset’s intended use for population analysis, these maps were not modeled.

For the PIP2 + menthol dataset, Topaz-based particle extraction followed by three rounds of 2D classification yielded 387,797 particles. These underwent ab initio reconstruction and homogeneous refinement to produce an initial structure. One round of 3D classification removed low-resolution particles, yielding 252,980 particles that were subjected to non-uniform refinement with C4 symmetry, reaching a resolution of 3.05 Å. The resulting particles and volume were exported to RELION using csparc2star.py. A TMD mask was generated in ChimeraX60 using the molmap command. In RELION, 3D classification without alignment was performed (K = 4, T = 16), and 40.7% of particles were selected for a second round of classification (K = 4, T = 64). A subset of 75.2% of particles was re-extracted and transferred back to CryoSPARC for non-uniform refinement with per-particle defocus and per-group CTF parameter optimization, followed by local refinement. The final map representing the sensitized conformation reached a resolution of 2.90 Å, with clear densities corresponding to PIP2 and menthol.

For the PIP2 + icilin dataset, the same processing scheme was applied to samples incubated with icilin for 1 min and 10 min. Topaz picking and five rounds of 2D classification yielded 343,227 (1 min) and 827,398 (10 min) particles. These were subjected to ab initio reconstruction, homogeneous refinement, and 3D classification. Particles corresponding to open and desensitized conformations were transferred to RELION for TMD-masked 3D classification without alignment (K = 4, T = 64). Selected particles were re-extracted and re-imported into CryoSPARC, and refined via non-uniform refinement with C4 symmetry, per-particle defocus optimization, per-group CTF parameters optimization, and local refinement. The 1-min dataset yielded maps of the open and desensitized conformations at 3.10 Å and 3.45 Å, respectively, while the 10-min dataset yielded corresponding conformations at 3.08 Å and 3.46 Å. Notably, the 10-min open conformation showed reduced density in the pore helix and pore loop regions compared to the 1-min structure.

Model building, refinement, and validation

For the closed conformation, PDB ID 8E4P was used as the initial model, while PDB IDs 7WRA and 7WRF were used as starting references for the desensitized conformations. For the intermediate 2 conformation, PDB ID 8E4O served as the initial model. This model was also used as the starting reference for the remaining structures, including intermediate 1, intermediate 3, sensitized, and open conformations. Atomic models were manually built in Coot61 and refined using real-space refinement62 in PHENIX63. Model validation was performed using MolProbity64, and ligand restraints were generated using eLBOW65 in PHENIX. All molecular graphics were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX60. Pore cavity calculations were conducted using MOLE266 and HOLE67.

Patch clamp electrophysiology

For electrophysiology experiments, full-length mouse TRPM8 (residues 1–1104) tagged with a C-terminal green fluorescent protein (GFP) and a FLAG affinity tag in the pEG BacMam plasmid was used. Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells were cultured according to the supplier’s guidelines (ATCC). Transient transfection was performed using X-tremeGENE 9 transfection reagent (Sigma) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Single amino acid mutants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis using PrimeStar polymerase, and sequenced to verify the mutation (Supplementary Table 6).

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were conducted 20–40 h post transfection at room temperature. Borosilicate glass pipettes (resistance: 2.5–3.5 MΩ) were pulled using a PC-10 puller (Narishige). Membrane currents were recorded using an Axopatch 200B amplifier and a Digidata 1550B interface (both from Molecular Devices) and analyzed using Origin 2022b (OriginLab).

For menthol-activated TRPM8 currents, the pipette (internal) solution contained (in mM): 140 CsCl, 10 HEPES, and 0.5 EGTA, with the pH adjusted to 7.3 using CsOH. For icilin-activated TRPM8 currents, the pipette solution was prepared to maintain a free intracellular Ca2+ concentration of 200 nM using a Ca2+/EGTA buffering system. The precise composition was calculated with MaxChelator software. In both menthol and icilin activation experiments, a Ca2+-free external bath solution was used, composed of (in mM): 135 NaCl, 5 KCl, 0 CaCl2, 2 MgCl2, 1 EGTA, 10 HEPES, and 10 glucose (pH adjusted to 7.4 with NaOH).

Current–voltage (I–V) relationships were generated by extracting current values at 10 mV intervals from voltage ramp pulses. For statistical analysis, outward current amplitudes at +100 mV were summarized in bar graphs, which display the mean ± SEM, with individual data points shown as scatter dots.

For voltage-sensing phosphatase (VSP) experiments, cells were co-transfected with TRPM8 and pIRES2-DrVSP-EGFP. TRPM8 responses were evaluated in the presence of 500 µM menthol, and current changes following VSP activation were assessed using the pulse protocol described in the figure legend.

For all electrophysiological experiments, n represents the number of biologically independent whole-cell recordings obtained from individual transfected HEK293 cells collected across multiple independent transfections. Statistical analyzes were performed using GraphPad Prism 5. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using two-sided unpaired t-tests with Welch’s correction, whereas comparisons involving multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. The statistical tests used for individual experiments are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m. Exact P values are provided in the Source Data file.

Molecular dynamics simulation on menthol binding mode

Using the cryo-EM structure of TRPM8 in the sensitized conformation with PIP2 and menthol, we modeled the missing loops (residue 50 to 101, 107 to 114, 148 to 155, 182 to 183, 202 to 208, 215 to 216, 227 to 237, 243 to 250, 266 to 271, 331 to 333, 343 to 349, 395 to 401, 534 to 556, 703 to 709, 715 to 722, 760 to 766, 815 to 824, 891 to 961, and 1018 to 1046) via AlphaFold 368. The simulation system was assembled in CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder69,70 with TIP3P water model71, POPC:POPE:Cholesterol = 2:1:1 membrane bilayer, and NaCl 0.15 M. Using ff19SB72 for protein, and CGenFF73 for menthol and diC8-PIP2 with C4-phosphate protonated state as force field. System size 240 × 240 × 177, 1,011,250 atoms, consist for the TRPM8 membrane system.

All molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using AMBER2474,75 package with five replicas. Each system was minimized with different 5000 steepest-descent steps and a maximum of 2500 conjugate gradient minimization steps. Each replica underwent equilibration for 250 ps under NVT and 1625 ps under NPT conditions at 300 K temperature following the standard CHARMM-GUI protocol. Following equilibration, production MD simulations were carried out for 500 ns for each of the five independent replicas without restraints. Temperature was controlled using Langevin dynamics76 with a collision frequency of 1 ps, and pressure was maintained at 1 bar using a Monte Carlo barostat. A time step of 2 fs was used throughout all simulations.

Structure-based analysis of interaction similarity

Residue contacts between subunits were identified using CONTACT/ACT in CCP4i77. Analyses were performed on residues T732–L889 and T956–R1008, corresponding to the S5 and S6 helices, which are consistently modeled in all PDB files across the examined conformations. All inter-residue contacts within a distance of 4.1 Å were extracted.

From these contacts, specific interaction types were filtered based on the following distance-based criteria: hydrogen bonds were defined as donor–acceptor atom pairs within 3.3 Å; ionic interactions as atoms of oppositely charged residues within 4.0 Å; hydrophobic interactions as atoms of hydrophobic residues within 4.0 Å; and van der Waals interactions as atoms of all other residue types within 4.0 Å. Subsequently, only interactions involving residues from specific domains were selected for further analysis.

To quantify the similarity of intersubunit interactions between two conformations, we calculated the harmonic mean of the fraction of shared interactions with respect to each conformation.
