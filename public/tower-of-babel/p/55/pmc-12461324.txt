Adsorption Studies of Fibrin Proteins onto Au- and Ag-Nanoparticles
Authors: Walter Makgoba, Abram Mahladisa, Malili Matshaba, Mulatedzi Gandamipfa, Nikiwe Mhlanga, Teboho Mokhena, Lucky Sikhwivhilu, Thuto Mosuang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Au- and Ag-nanoparticles were synthesized and conjugated with fibrin protein samples through a sodium citrate-induced method for possible ‘AuNPs+fibrin’ and ‘AgNPs+fibrin’ corona formation adsorption studies. Average spherical diameter sizes of 11, 18, 45, and 50 nm of Au-nanoparticles and 12, 14, and 26 nm of Ag-nanoparticles were considered for the conjugation process. The possible formation of ‘AuNPs+fibrin’ and ‘AgNPS+fibrin’ coronas through adsorption of fibrin protein molecules onto Au- and Ag-nanoparticles was verified using UV–vis and FTIR spectroscopies, ζ-potential, and TEM imaging. UV–vis red shift was observed on fibrin-conjugated AuNPs+fibrin/AgNPs+fibrin coronas relative to the unconjugated Au-/Ag-nanoparticles. FTIR spectra provided traces of possible fibrin functional atoms’ bonding with Au- and Ag-nanoparticle surface atoms. TEM imaging was also utilized to observe and estimate nanoparticle distribution and sizes. A different nature of conjugation mechanism was noted on Au-/Ag-nanoparticles and fibrin protein molecules. Unconjugated Au-nanoparticles materialize as agglomerates of nanoclusters, while fibrin-conjugated AuNPs+fibrin coronas appear as dispersed individual random nanoparticles. On the other hand, unconjugated Ag-nanoparticles occur as scattered irregular individual nanoparticles that agglomerate into AgNPs+fibrin corona nanoclusters upon conjugation with fibrin protein molecules. Conjugated fibrin proteins do not lose their conformational structure or functional property.

Introduction

At nanoscale, precious metals, which include gold (Au) and silver (Ag), have been identified as possible nanotools that can be used in medical imaging and drug transportation into human body systems. , Such modern medical applications, often referred to as biomedicine, are informed by the metal nanoparticle’s unique physicochemical properties, such as chemical surface functionalization, optical response, and electronic characteristics. , Moreover, these fascinating properties are mainly attributed to the electron’s density distribution on the metal along the nanoparticle surface. But, the shape as well as size of the metal nanomaterials involved, which can be regulated during the synthesis process, may also play a crucial role. −

When metal nanoparticles infiltrate the physiological fluids environment, some protein molecular species get adsorbed on the nanoparticle’s surfaces. In due course, the ‘nanoparticle+protein corona’ complex gets formed. , The successful phenomenon is accompanied by the reduction of the free energy of the nanoparticles. , Upon the nanoparticle+protein corona formation, possible alteration of the original structure and properties of the nanoparticles may be encountered. In certain instances, when the nanoparticle+protein corona complex reaches the intended intercellular site, the nanoparticles and protein molecules detach. Now, depending on whether the original structure and properties of the nanoparticles have changed, the secondary nanoparticles may be beneficial or toxic at the target site. It is on this scenario that the actual mechanism of the interaction between the nanoparticles and protein molecules with emphasis on the surface functionality becomes crucial. ,

On the other hand, the biological activities of various molecules on different nanoparticles are highly dictated by the stability of the nanoparticle+protein corona complex. Hence, the adsorption of protein molecules onto the nanoparticle surface may bring about some conformational rearrangement, which may in turn alter the configuration structure and the functioning of the protein molecules. Research studies by Galloway et al., Karlsson et al., and Li et al. − indicate that the majority of the nanoparticles have the ability of altering the conformational structure of some protein molecular species. This ultimately leads to a changed purpose or disordered form of the expected protein species function. Such a disordered effect on a given species outcome can be classified as nanotoxicity. −

On drug transportation, the article by Hazarika and Jha acknowledges that nanoparticles-assisted drug delivery requires significant attention, which can be understood through nanoparticle–protein molecule interactions. To comprehend this, important factors, such as the size, shape, surface charge, and type of the nanoparticle, that contribute to conformational changes in the protein molecules must be considered. As the functionality of different protein molecular species is based on their conformational structure, notable changes in this may result in unexpected functional purposes or even detrimental functionality, known as toxicity. According to Zhang et al., the unique physicochemical characteristics of metal nanoparticles produce not only beneficial biological effects but also unexpected toxicity to human cells. Sambalova et al. hinted on how isolated biofilm molecules can stimulate the reduction of Ag ions, which could result in preferred Ag-nanoparticles for a particular biofilm-specific therapeutics. It is for this reason that the reliable application of metal nanoparticles rests with a proper understanding of the possible nanoparticle toxicity, toxicity observations, and possible steps to undertake to avert toxicity. Successful acquisition of such stable, reliable, and useful nanoparticle+protein corona complexes will lead to therapeutic metallic nanoparticle applications that are nanotoxicity free.

Another point of consideration is that standard XRD is generally not preferred in characterizing the nanoparticle+protein setup due to preparation costs and time-consuming measurements. But then, UV–vis and FTIR spectroscopies could track wavelength changes by sensing the peak absorbance in individual nanoparticles as well as nanoparticles loaded with atoms or molecules. Different nanoparticle shapes and sizes can also be analyzed efficiently using the UV–vis spectral wavelength shifts. In biomolecules and polymers, UV–vis and FTIR blue/red wavelength shifts can identify and explain possible structural changes based on peak absorbance intensities. Apart from nanoparticles and protein molecular species, UV–vis and FTIR spectral shifts and peak absorbance can be used to find concentrations of different chemical solutions. , UV–vis spectral shifts over a specific time are used in kinetic studies by measuring color indexes of certain dye products. On the other hand, FTIR spectral peak shifts are instrumental in identifying functional groups as biomolecules.

Sasidharan et al. considered the time-reliant kinetics adsorption approach to investigate the role of Au- and Ag-nanoparticles together with human serum albumin (HSA), fibrinogen, and immunoglobulin G (IgG) in the formation of biomolecular nanoparticle+protein corona complexes. The aim was to find if the formed biomolecular coronas could be used successfully in biomedical applications. HSA and IgG protein species demonstrated good adsorption onto Au- (AuNPs) and Ag-nanoparticles (AgNPs) toward the formation of the corresponding AuNPs+HSA, AuNPs+IgG, AgNPs+HSA, and AgNPS+IgG corona complexes. However, fibrinogen showed less adsorption tendencies toward possible AuNPs+fibrinogen and AgNPs+fibrinogen coronas. It is in this sense that this study identified fibrin protein species to further investigate possible adsorption and corona formation with Au- and Ag-nanoparticles. On top, fibrin protein molecule is a derivative of fibrinogen and has been identified as an emerging and promising species suitable for biomedical applications. So, fibrin protein has been chosen not only to expand on the existing data on fibrinogen interaction with nanoparticles , but also for an extension of ideas on how metallic nanoparticles interact with different protein species. As a main ingredient in wound healing, the interaction of fibrin protein with metallic nanoparticles may pave the way to new wound healing therapy.

A limited understanding and noticeable gap on the physio-chemical properties of metal nanoparticles in the physiological cell environment necessitates further related studies. As such, clinical translation and success of nanoparticles rely on key interactions with protein molecules and other biomolecule cells in the human or even other animal cells body. A study on the nanoparticle+protein interactions could assist in clarifying nanoparticle intercellular interactions, which in turn may lead to administering safe nanomedicines. In this study, different spherical sizes of Au- and Ag-nanoparticles have been synthesized and thereafter incubated with fibrin protein species samples to assist in understanding the possible mechanism of how fibrin protein molecules get adsorbed onto metallic nanoparticles to form nanoparticle+protein corona complexes. Ultraviolet visible (UV–vis), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and ζ-potential (ZP) spectroscopies were utilized to characterize these Au-/Ag-nanoparticles + fibrin protein-based adsorption formations. Even though TEM assisted in the understanding of AuNPs+HSA, AuNPs+fibrinogen, AuNPs+IgG, AgNPs+HSA, AgNPs+fibrinogen, and AgNPs+IgG coronas, UV–vis and FTIR characterizations were not explored by Sasidharan et al. It is envisaged that through UV–vis and FTIR spectral abilities, extra information on how Au- and Ag-nanoparticles bind with fibrin protein molecules in the formation of nanoparticle+protein coronas can be collected.

Materials and Synthesis

Materials

All chemical reagents used in this study were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Such reagents include chloroauric acid (HAuCl4), silver nitrate (AgNO3), and sodium citrate (C6H5Na3O7), as well as fibrin protein powders.

Synthesis of Au- and Ag-Nanoparticles

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) were synthesized via the seeded growth method. , In this method, 150 mL of 2.2 mM sodium citrate was brought to boiling in an Erlenmeyer flask. When the solution was boiling, 1 mL of 25 mM HAuCl4 was introduced into the solution. After a certain period (approximately 1–2 min), the color of the solution turned pink, indicating the formation of AuNPs with an average diameter of 11 ± 3.26 nm. Thereafter, the temperature of the solution was decreased to 90 °C. Furthermore, for the growing step of the nanoparticles, 1 mL of sodium citrate 60 mM and 1 mL of HAuCl4 25 mM were vigorously added to increase the size of the nanoparticles, with each addition leading to an increased size of the nanoparticles. The process of addition of both sodium citrate and HAuCl4 was repeated after every 30 min, which led to the formation of new nanoparticles with increased average diameter sizes of 18 ± 3.15, 45 ± 12.8, and 50 ± 17.4 nm. The high standard deviation was observed in the diameter sizes 45 and 50 nm due to the presence of few small nanoparticles in the background as the size was increased. This is expected as the capping agent’s (sodium citrate) role is to attempt to bring smaller nanoparticles together into forming larger nanoparticle sizes.

Correspondingly, Ag-nanoparticles (AgNPs) were also synthesized via the sodium citrate-induced method. In this case, 50 mL of 1 mM AgNO3 was heated to boiling in an Erlenmeyer flask. Subsequently, 5 mL of 1% sodium citrate was introduced into the solution. During this process, the solution was also periodically heated until a series of color changes were observed, from yellow to brown, to pale brown. During this, Ag-nanoparticles with different average diameter sizes of 12 ± 3.49, 14 ± 7.52, and 26 ± 5.83 nm were observed, with each color change associated with a particular crystal phase.

In both, AuNPs and AgNPs, the initial step involves nucleation of primary nanoparticles, which are normally referred to as nuclei; the second step concerns nanoparticle growth by surface-assisted reduction, where the nuclei coalescence to produce enlarged nanoparticles, which are referred to as seeds.

Fibrin Protein Preparation through Dissolution and Adsorption

Ethical clearance to work with human-based fibrin protein species was obtained from the Turfloop Research Ethics Committee under project number: TREC/71/2023: PG.

Thus, 1.000 g of fibrin protein was acquired in powder form from Sigma-Aldrich. A concentration of 0.193 mg/mL was then prepared by dissolving 0.005 g of this powdered fibrin protein in 25.00 mL of phosphate buffer saline (PBS) at pH 7.4. The dissolution of fibrin powder into PBS took over 48 h at 37 °C. Upon preparation of the fibrin concentration solution, the Au- and Ag-nanoparticles were incubated with fibrin for 12 h. After the incubation processes, how Au- and Ag-nanoparticles reacted in the presence of fibrin protein was further investigated. The possible adsorption of fibrin proteins onto the nanoparticles leading to the formation of ‘AuNPs+fibrin’ and ‘AgNPs+fibrin’ coronas was also studied.

Characterization Techniques

X-ray Diffraction (XRD)

XRD was used to probe the crystal structures of synthesized Au- and Ag-nanoparticles. In particular, an Empyrean X-ray diffractometer (Malvern Panalytical) was used. In this machine, an incident X-ray produced is scattered by the nanoparticle samples and the scattered rays carry information about the nanoparticles. Sufficient data are collected to calculate the nanoparticles’ unit cell dimensions. From these unit cell dimensions, the nanocrystalline symmetries of both Au and Ag were obtained.

Ultraviolet–Visible (UV–vis) Spectroscopy

A Thermo Scientific Multiskan Go UV–vis spectrophotometer via cuvette mode was used to investigate the optical properties of AuNPs and AgNPs and their corresponding nanoparticle+fibrin coronas. UV–vis makes use of discrete ultraviolet (UV) or visible (vis) light wavelengths absorbed or transmitted by a given sample to analyze its composition and concentration. Absorption spectra in the range of 250–900 nm were adopted for AuNPs and AgNPs as well as AuNPs+fibrin and AgNPs+fibrin coronas to establish spectral wavelength shifts.

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) Spectroscopy

A PerkinElmer FTIR TWO spectrometer was identified and utilized to probe functional groups available in the citrate immersed Au- and Ag-nanoparticles as well as conjugated Au-/Ag-nanoparticle+fibrin corona samples. FTIR spectroscopy uses vibrational modes to obtain information about the structure, composition, and functional groups of biomolecules. The associated structural properties of fibrin proteins before and after conjugation with Au- and Ag-nanoparticles were also extracted. For good measurements, the spectral wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm–1 was initiated. In the process, a total of 1801 scans were achieved at a resolution wavenumber of 4 cm–1.

ζ-Potential (ZP)

Au- and Ag-nanoparticles and the corresponding nanoparticle+fibrin coronas ζ-potentials were further characterized using a Malvern Instrument Zetasizer in disposable capillary zeta cells at a temperature of 25 °C. The attained ζ-potential measurements were averaged from three repeated measurements to get informed convincing results. ζ-Potential (ZP) measurements are simple, easy, and reproducible when averaging or estimating and acquiring the particle surface charge. On different nanoparticle sizes, ZP values assist in examining charge distributions on the surfaces of nanoparticles and nanoparticles loaded with atoms or molecules.

Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM)

An FEI Tecnai F20 Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) (Thermo-Fisher Scientific) was used to analyze AuNPs and AgNPs before as well as AuNPs+fibrin and AgNPs+fibrin after conjugation with fibrin proteins. Incident electron waves were released with a voltage of 200 kV from the TEM electron gun. , Acquired electron micrographs were used to find the nanoparticle shapes and sizes for AuNPs/AgNPs and AuNPs+fibrin/AgNPs+fibrin coronas.

XRD Characterization of Au- and Ag-Nanoparticles

The collected XRD plots of Au- and Ag-nanoparticles are shown in Figures and , respectively. In both cases, XRD diffraction peaks exhibit face-centered cubic (FCC) unit cell structured nanoparticles. Given that all of the studied Au-nanoparticles were prepared using only the seeded growth method, only one XRD plot is considered here in Figure . The peak patterns correspond to the Bragg reflections, which can only be indexed based on FCC gold nanocrystals. The characterized samples possess diffraction peaks belonging to 2θ values of 38.184, 44.392, 64.576, and 77.547°, which respectively correspond to (100), (200), (220), and (311) Bragg planes (JCPDS No. 36-1451). Similar studies by Rattan et al. where gold nanoparticles were synthesized using the wet chemical and green chemistry approach revealed similar results. The average spherical diameter of the characterized Au-nanoparticles samples was calculated using the Debye–Scherrer equation. , A spherical diameter value of ±18.14 nm, which corresponds to one of the investigated samples, was obtained. Such mode of analyzing results is relatable to the study conducted by Yang et al. in which Au-nanoparticles were prepared by utilizing the sodium citrate method or Turkevich method.

FTIR Characterization of Au- and Ag-Nanoparticles

FTIR spectroscopy was used to probe possible functional groups available in sodium citrate immersed with Au- and Ag-nanoparticles. Figure illustrates the FTIR spectra of sodium citrate, Ag-nanoparticles, and Au-nanoparticles, respectively. Analysis of this spectrum suggests a traceable sodium citrate capping agent in the surface morphology of both Au- and Ag-nanoparticle samples. The AuNP spectra exhibit a broad peak in the region of 3240 cm–1, which is attributed to OH stretching. The other three peaks appear centered around 1033, 1370, and 1569 cm–1, associated with the stretching modes of carbonate (CO3) complexes. According to the findings of Sylvestre et al. and Mohan et al., the controlled surface properties of Au-nanoparticles demonstrate similar FTIR peaks.

Conjugation of Nanoparticles with Fibrin Proteins

Dissolution of Fibrin Protein Powders

FTIR of powdered fibrin protein was conducted to investigate traces of existing functional groups in the fibrin molecules. The acquired FTIR spectra of this powdered fibrin shown in Figure are found to be in good agreement with the literature studies outlined by Braga et al. and Deepthi et al. Peaks appearing at 1238, 1514, and 1635 cm–1 correspond to the amide functional groups. In this order, the peak at 1238 cm–1 corresponds to the Amide III bond, the peak at 1514 cm–1 corresponds to the Amide II bond, and the last peak at 1635 cm–1 is associated with the Amide I bond. The amide functional group is given as −CONH2. The amide I bond relates to the CO stretching mode, amide II bond is due to the NH deformation, while the amide III bond results from coupled CN stretching and NH bending motions. Lastly, the peaks at 2855 and 2920 cm–1 are associated with −CH2 groups, while the 3269 cm–1 peak is for the OH group vibration.

FTIR spectrum of fibrin protein powdered solution.

Adsorption of Fibrin Proteins onto Au- and Ag-Nanoparticles

The conjugation process was achieved at a ratio of 1:1 between fibrin protein samples and Au-/Ag-nanoparticle solutions. One mL each of the prepared concentrations of fibrin in PBS solution was poured in two separate containers. Thereafter, 1 mL of Au-nanoparticle solution was introduced into one of the fibrin solution containers, while 1 mL of Ag-nanoparticle solution was introduced into the other fibrin solution container. The entire process of combining Au-/Ag-nanoparticles and fibrin protein samples is regarded as conjugation.

UV–vis spectroscopic characterization was later conducted to analyze the possible shift on Au- and Ag-nanoparticles conjugated with fibrin proteins relative to the original Au- and Ag-nanoparticle samples before conjugation. Figure a,b presents Au-nanoparticles conjugated with fibrin protein samples (thereafter referred to as ’AuNPs+fibrin’) relative to the Au-nanoparticles before conjugation (referred to as ‘AuNPs’) with peaks at average spherical diameter sizes of 11 and 45 nm in that order. Indeed, peak shifts of ‘AuNPs+fibrin’ relative to ‘AuNPs’ are observed, even though the absorbance is lower than that of the ‘AuNPs’. Such peak shifts signify wavelengths at which adsorption of fibrin protein molecules onto Au-nanoparticles occurs. Torres-Diaz et al. made use of the UV–vis peak shifts approach to obtain an understanding of the surface functionalization of peptides on gold nanoparticles. A similar peak shift behavior was emphasized by Kaur et al., Amini et al., and Rutherford et al. based on different protein molecular species’ interaction with gold nanoparticles.

UV–vis spectroscopy of AuNPs+fibrin and AuNPs; (a) Au(11)+fibrin and Au(11), (b) Au(45)+fibrin and Au(45), which correspond to spherical diameters 11 and 45 nm after and before conjugation with fibrin proteins.

Peak shifts were evident in all average spherical diameter sizes of the Au-nanoparticles (Figure a,b), as well as the Ag-nanoparticles (Figure a,b). In addition, the peak shifts observed are toward longer wavelengths in all cases of both Au- and Ag-nanoparticle spectra. The prominent, distinct peak shifts in all average spherical diameter sizes studied indicate good and robust adsorption of fibrin molecules onto Au/Ag-nanoparticles toward AuNPs+fibrin and AgNPs+fibrin coronas. Depending on the different spherical diameter sizes of the studied AuNPs and AgNPs, the observed spectral shifts occur due to the structural and physicochemical changes experienced when fibrin protein molecules get adsorbed onto the surface of the nanoparticles. , To complement, the compromised ‘AuNPs+fibrin’ relative to fibrin peaks lead to dispersed individual clusters, while the heightened ‘AgNPs+fibrin’ relative to fibrin peaks result in conglomeration of nanoclusters (Tables and ).

It is quite remarkable to observe that for Au(11)+fibrin, only Amide III and II associated peaks are traceable around 1250 and 1563 cm–1, respectively. The Au(18)+fibrin complex shows Amide III, II, and I peaks (Table ). In both Au(11)+fibrin and Au(18)+fibrin complexes, CH2 and OH functional group-related peaks cannot be observed. Polydispersity index (PDI) was also analyzed to estimate the uniformity of the nanoparticle sizes in the solution. Nanoparticle size distribution and aggregates play an important role when analyzing polydispersity. A given sample is considered monodispersed (a perfectly uniform sample with respect to the particle size) if its polydispersity index (PDI) is less than 0.1. ,, A PDI value greater than 0.7 implies a sample with a broad particle size distribution. Such a sample may not be suitable for analysis. , In this instance, PDI distributions of ‘AuNPs’ and ‘AgNPs+fibrin’ are compared based on their spherical average diameter sizes. Accumulated data on both ‘AuNPs’ and ‘AuNPs+fibrin’ samples are arranged in Table . The values listed suggest PDIs that are moderately polydisperse (a nonuniform sample with respect to the particle size). PDI values decrease for average spherical diameter sizes 11, 18, and 50 nm after conjugation with fibrin, except for 45 nm, in which the PDI increases after conjugation. The overall attained PDI distribution points to the fact that both ‘AuNPs’ and ’AuNPs+fibrin’ samples are moderately polydisperse. Accordingly, Danaei et al. further articulated that the reasonable PDI range of the nanoparticles considered by most researchers is 0.05–0.70.

Before the conjugation process, AuNPs materialize as agglomerates of nanoclusters. Figures a and a present such seeded grown average spherical diameter sizes of 11 and 45 nm AuNP nanoclusters at 100 and 50 nm TEM resolutions. Conjugation of fibrin protein samples into these nanoclusters stimulates the dispersion of nanoparticles as such ‘AuNPs+fibrin’ suspension projects isolated random AuNPs on the fibrin protein fluid. , Figures b and b display such a dispersion relation. Such a behavior may be expected as nanoparticles dunked into a biological environment fluid spontaneously induce adsorption of immediate protein molecules onto their surfaces. , In this case, adsorbed fibrin molecules onto AuNPs induce a repulsive force among individual AuNPs, which leads to AuNPs being randomly dispersed. Devi et al. reported a similar situation on cysteine-capped gold nanoparticles. Their results argue that gold nanoparticles conjugated with cysteine appear as individual nanoparticles diffused in a cysteine fluid. However, D’souza et al. are of the view that aggregates of ascorbic acid-capped gold nanoparticles may be due to hydrogen bonding with dichlorvos. Therefore, in the absence of hydrogen bonding between AuNPs and fibrin molecules (sodium citrate capping), AuNP aggregates disintegrate into individual species within the fibrin-sodium citrate medium.

Looking at Ag-nanoparticles’ interactions with fibrin proteins, Figure b demonstrates appreciable agglomerations on ‘AgNPs+fibrin’ conjugation at 12 nm average diameter size. In addition, a dark film can be observed encompassing the nanoclusters. It is noteworthy that nanoclustered ‘AgNPs+fibrin’ particles have smaller average spherical diameter sizes when compared to disorderly spaced AgNPs before conjugation process (Figure a). Moreover, Figure a further demonstrates dispersed, isolated AgNPs at an average spherical diameter size of 12 nm. An uneven size and shape distribution at 12 nm average diameter aligned with the poor adsorption process can also be observed. This scenario is exactly opposite to that observed with the AuNPs before and after conjugation with fibrin molecules ‘AuNPs+fibrin’. A similar observation was noted by Halamoda-Kenzaoui et al., in which 30 and 80 nm size distribution of Rubipy-SiO2 nanoparticles in CaCo-2 solution indicated agglomeration and precipitation. Evidence of small and large nanoparticles’ agglomerations was also reported by Murugadoss et al., whereby TiO2 nanoparticles are stabilized with bovine serum albumin (BSA). The TEM 200 nm resolution of 26 nm spherical diameter size displayed in Figure a,b provides a good demonstration of how before conjugation, scattered, individual AgNPs have enlarged spherical diameter sizes in comparison with fibrin-conjugated ‘AgNPs+fibrin’ random aggregates. The spherical diameter size reduction in aggregates may be associated with summed attractive forces that reduce individual nanoparticle sizes. It is well known that in a cellular environment, irregularly dispersed ‘AuNPs+fibrin’ may be linked to nontoxicity while the aggregated ‘AgNPs+fibrin’ may be the source of toxicity.

Conclusions

The XRD plots confirm that the Au- and Ag-nanoparticles synthesized with the sodium citrate method exhibit face-centered cubic symmetry. The UV–vis peak shifts of AuNPs and AgNPs relative to conjugated ‘AuNPs+fibrin’ and “AgNPs+fibrin”, respectively, toward longer wavelengths suggest successful adsorption of fibrin molecules onto Au- and Ag-nanoparticles. On the other hand, ‘fibrin’ before conjugation and ‘AuNPs+fibrin’ and ‘AgNPs+fibrin’ after conjugation FTIR spectra peaks remain similar but shifted toward shorter wavenumbers (red shifts), which suggests successful adsorption with no change in the conformational structure of the fibrin protein molecules. This implies that such fibrin proteins do not lose their original functional properties after conjugation with AuNPs and/or AgNPs. The difference in ζ-potential further validates the charge redistribution from ‘AuNPs’ and ‘AgNPs’ before toward “AuNPs+fibrin” and ‘AgNPs+fibrin’ after conjugation. The PDI data indicate that a uniform spherical particle distribution occurs in every diameter size. Uniform AuNP and AgNP agglomerations provide surface environments that are appropriate for fibrin protein molecule adsorption processes. The TEM micrographs suggest aggregates of AuNPs, which disperse into individual AuNPs upon conjugation with fibrin protein powders. In the case of Ag-nanoparticles, the situation is exactly the opposite. Individually scattered AgNPs conglomerate into nanoclusters upon conjugation with fibrin protein samples.
