Glutaminolysis inhibition boosts cuproptosis to eliminate breast cancer stem cells
Authors: Zitao Fan, Shiyou Li, Chen Xiao, Qiang wang, Zhijie Zhang, Ao hua, Qingqing Xu, Tian Yang, Jiayuan Li, Yue Pan, Baizhu Chen, Xiangliang Yang, Zhenwei Zou, Zifu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe metabolic plasticity of cancer stem cells (CSCs) enables them to adaptively switch between glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and alternative fuels such as glutamine and fatty acids, promoting tumorigenesis and progression by ensuring survival under diverse environmental stresses. However, current therapeutic approaches targeting CSCs remain limited, particularly those relying on single metabolic interventions. By leveraging concurrent metabolic modulation and redox disruption to sensitize CSCs to cuproptosis, we prepared a polydopamine/hydroxyethyl starch-based nanoplatform (CH@PHF) for co-delivering copper-diethyldithiocarbamate (CuET) and HYL001. Specifically, HYL001 suppresses glutaminase (GLS) activity, thereby blocking glutamine metabolism and inhibiting glutathione (GSH) biosynthesis. This effect synergizes with CuET to collectively amplify oxidative stress in CSCs. Furthermore, the combination of HYL001 and CuET significantly elevates intracellular copper levels, particularly the cuprous form (Cu+), thereby sensitizing CSCs to cuproptosis. This combined treatment effectively inhibits tumor growth and metastasis by targeting and eradicating breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs), offering a promising avenue for tumor therapy.Key words: Nano drug delivery system, Cancer stem cells, Redox, Cuproptosis, Metabolic therapy, Glutaminolysis, Hydroxyethyl starch, Cancer therapy

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy worldwide and a major threat to women's health, a threat exacerbated in aggressive triple negative breast cancers (TNBC) by cancer stem cells (CSCs) that drive early metastasis and high relapse rates1, 2, 3. These cells are notorious for their formidable resistance to a broad spectrum of therapies, and are widely blamed as the primary culprits for tumor recurrence and distant metastasis4,5. However, this resilience is underpinned by a paradoxical vulnerability: their unique energy metabolism4,6,7. CSCs exhibit remarkable metabolic plasticity, dynamically shifting between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation to meet their bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands depending on the contextual cues from the tumor microenvironment5,6. This adaptability allows them to survive under various stresses but also presents a critical “Achilles' heel”8,9. Consequently, development of therapeutic strategies targeting a broad spectrum of metabolic pathways, including both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, is deemed necessary. Such a comprehensive approach is essential for the potent and durable eradication of CSCs8, 9, 10, 11, 12.

The intricate survival mechanisms of CSCs extend beyond energy production to include a meticulously regulated redox balance13,14. Compared to their more differentiated bulk tumor cell counterparts, CSCs generally maintain a higher reduced state within the cell, characterized by elevated levels of reduced glutathione (GSH) and an enhanced capacity for scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS)15,16. This robust and adaptable redox regulation fortifies CSCs against apoptosis and promotes resistance to numerous therapies. Strategically disrupting this delicate redox equilibrium—by depleting GSH, inhibiting key antioxidant enzymes, or allowing toxic metabolite accumulation—represents a crucial therapeutic strategy to selectively target and eliminate CSCs17. Accordingly, we have developed a novel conjugate of lonidamine with a triphenyl phosphonium cation, designated HYL001, which effectively suppresses CSCs by targeting mitochondria, subsequently downregulating glutaminase levels and disrupting the redox balance10,18,19. The complementary inhibitory profiles of HYL001 (targeting glutaminolysis) and other treatment strategies provide a compelling mechanistic rationale for combination11,20.

Cuproptosis, a recently identified form of regulated cell death, is distinct from known pathways like apoptosis21, 22, 23. This copper-dependent mechanism involves copper ions binding to lipoylated the tricarboxylic acid cycle proteins such as dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase and dihydrolipoamide S-succinyltransferase, leading to their aggregation and subsequent disruption of mitochondrial metabolism24. Disulfiram, an anti-alcoholism drug, demonstrates anti-tumor activity via its metabolite CuET, which induces cuproptosis25,26. Without the use of other drug delivery systems or solubilization strategies, CuET exhibits extremely poor aqueous solubility (far below 1 μg/L), which severely limits its blood circulation and tumor accumulation27, 28, 29. Despite these advances, key questions remain unexplored, particularly regarding how cuproptosis interacts with the unique metabolic adaptations of CSCs. Our research group has long been committed to the rational design of nanotherapeutics based on the “five features” principle for the potent elimination of CSCs30. Previously, we have developed a series of nano drug delivery systems for delivering CuET, including CuET@HES, CuET@PH, and CuET@ICG27,28,31. These nanoplatforms have effectively addressed the issue of poor water solubility. However, when inducing cuproptosis, CuET still faces limitations imposed by the high reducing capacity and metabolic plasticity of both tumor cells and CSCs24,32. By forming GSH-Cu complexes through the binding of GSH to intracellular Cu+, cancer stem cells inhibit cuproptosis. Therefore, combining CuET with other metabolic therapeutic approaches is essential32. Liu et al.33 developed a nano-drug delivery platform co-loaded with a copper ion transporter inhibitor and a glutaminase (GLS) inhibitor, which demonstrated excellent antitumor efficacy. However, little attention has been paid to the role of cuproptosis and glutamine metabolism in CSCs. The unmet need to target the enhanced antioxidant system in CSCs remains unaddressed.

To augment the antitumor efficacy, herein we prepare a systemically administrable, tumor-targeting nanoplatform (CuET@PHF NPs) capable of dual metabolic modulation through coating with PDA and folic acid-modified HES29, followed by its combination with HYL001 (Fig. 1). Inhibiting glutamine metabolism with HYL001 will regulate the cellular redox level, potentiate copper-induced stress, and thereby enhance cuproptosis. We rigorously test this hypothesis using targeted transcriptomics, molecular biology tools, and in vivo orthotopic tumor models, with a focused investigation into the mechanism by which this combination therapy eradicates CSCs in orthotopic 4T1 and breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs) tumor models. Finally, we will comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of this novel CuET and HYL001 combination therapy, aiming to establish a potent new metabolic-targeted regimen for intractable cancers rich in CSCs.

Materials and methods

Materials

HES with average molecular weight (Mw) 130 kDa and hydroxyethyl molar substitution (MS) of 0.4, was provided by Wuhan HUST Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). NAC was purchased from Yeasen Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). DDTC, copric chloride dihydrate (CuCl2‧2H2O), dopamine hydrochloride, ammonia solution was purchased from Aladdin Reagent Inc (Shanghai, China). N-Ethyl-N′-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDCI, 98%), 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, 99%) and folic acid were bought from Aladdin Reagent Inc (Shanghai, China). PBS buffer and RPMI 1640 cell culture medium were purchased from Hyclone Co., Ltd (Cytiva, American). Fetal bovine serum, trypsin and penicillin–streptomycin solution were purchased from Gibco Life Technologies Inc (Grand Island, NY, USA). The annexin V-APC/7-AAD apoptosis detection kit was purchased from Multi sciences Biotech Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). The kit that was used to detect the contents of GSH & GSSG was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). All flow cytometry antibodies were purchased from Biolegend (San Diego, CA, USA). Ultra-low attachment 96 well & six well plates were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). MitoSOX Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (40778es50) was purchased from YEASEN (Shanghai, China). Allophycocyanin (APC) anti-mouse CD133 (141208), fluorescein (FITC) anti-mouse CD24 (101806), and PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD44 (103030) were purchased from Biolegend (San Diego, CA, USA). The glutaminase activity detection kit was purchased from Abbkine (Shangha, China).

Cell culture

The murine breast cancer 4T1 cell line was acquired from Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences. The cancer cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotics (penicillin: 100 U/mL, streptomycin: 100 μg/mL).

Animals

Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Weitong Lihua (Jiangsu, China) and housed in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) facility in the laboratory animal center of Life Science (HUST). The environment was maintained at 21 ± 1 °C with 40%–70% relative humidity under a 12-h light/dark cycle. The mice had ad libitum access to standard food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Application number for experimental animals: SY20250337, IACUC Number:HUST-IACUC-2025-0052).

Synthesis of FA-HES-SH

HES-SH was synthesized with three steps as reported before26. First, HES (1.0 g) reacted with succinic anhydride (0.07 g) in 25 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution, with 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) (0.06 g) catalyzed for 24 h at 45 °C, to obtain carboxylated HES. Second, carboxylated HES (0.5 g) reacted with 2-(pyridyldithio) ethylamine hydrochloride (0.06 g) in 20 mL of aqueous solution with EDCI (0.15 g) and N-hydroxy succinimide (NHS) (0.05 g) for 48 h at 25 °C to obtain HES-PA. Finally, to produce HES-SH, HES-PA (0.3 g) was reduced by dithiothreitol (0.48 g) in 20 mL of DMSO for 24 h at 45 °C under N2 atmosphere. All products were further purified with dialysis (3500 Da, Biosharp, Hefei, China) and freeze-dried after reactions. The successful synthesis of HES-SH was determined by 1H NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance) with a 600-MHz NMR spectrometer.

Preparation of CuET@PHF and CH@PHF NPs

CuET@PHF NPs were prepared with three steps. First, HES (10 mg) and CuCl2 (5.57 μmol) were mixed in 1 mL of aqueous solution, and then DDTC solution (11.4 μmol, 0.2 mL) was added to the mixture under vigorous vortex at 60 °C to obtain CuET@HES NPs. Second, dopamine (5 mg) and ammonia solution (1 mL) were added to the above CuET@HES NP solution and reacted for 12 h at 25 °C. CuET@PDA/HES NPs were thus acquired and purified with dialysis (3500 Da). Finally, FA-HES-SH (20 mg) was added to the above CuET@PDA/HES NP solution and reacted for 48 h at 25 °C. The product CuET@PHF NP solution was purified with ultrafiltration (100 kDa) three times for further usage. Dopamine (5 mg), ammonia solution (1 mL) and HYL001 (5 mg) were added to the above CuET@HES NP solution and reacted for 12 h at 25 °C. CH@PDA/HES NPs solution and reacted for 48 h at 25 °C with FA-HES-SH.

Characterization of CH@PHF NPs

DLS and zeta potential data were collected with Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern, England). TEM (Hitachi HT7700) mapping was used to characterize the morphology of NPs.

In vitro drug release

The dialysis bag (molecular weight cut-off, 3500 kDa) containing 1 mL CH@PHF NPs, were placed in 30 mL of PBS buffer (pH 7.4, 0.5% Tween 80) under stirring at 100 rpm at 37 °C. At determined time, some PBS buffer was taken from the solution to assess the released amount of HYL001. The quantities of HYL001 were separately determined by high-performance liquid chromatography.

Cell viability assay

The 4T1 cancer cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated overnight. Then, cancer cells were treated with NPs for 24 or 48 h. The classical CSCs were cultured using 6-well ultralow attachment, and then digested by trypsin to single cell for seeding at 96-well ultralow attachment plates. After cultured 24 h, added the fresh culture medium contained drugs for 24 or 48 h34. After that, 10 μL of the CCK-8 solution was added to each well and further incubated for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured by a microplate reader (Thermo, Multi Skan, Waltham, MA, USA). The cell viability was calculated as a percentage relative to the negative control.

Apoptosis assay

The apoptosis rate was assessed using a kit from Multi sciences and detected using a flow cytometry (Berkman Coulter Inc., CytoFLEX, Brea, CA, USA), according to the instructions provided by manufacturer. Briefly, 4T1 cancer cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight, and then, cells were treated with various NPs for 12 or 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were collected by digesting with trypsin without EDTA and stained according to the instructions of Annexin V-APC/7-AAD apoptosis kit. Finally, the apoptosis rate was detected using a flow cytometry.

Intracellular ROS measurements

Typically, 4T1 cells or BCSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. Following incubation, the cells were treated with free drugs or various NPs for specified durations and subsequently harvested by trypsinization. The harvested cells were then stained with 10 μmol/L DCFH-DA in RPMI 1640 medium and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry. The intracellular ROS levels were quantified based on the fluorescence intensity of DCFH-DA.

Intracellular GSH assay

The relative concentration of GSH in cell lysates was measured using a commercial kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. In this assay, GSH reacts with the chromogenic agent DTNB to produce yellow-colored TNB and oxidized GSSG. GSSG can be reduced back to GSH by glutathione reductase in the presence of NADPH. Thus, the total glutathione level (GSH + GSSG) serves as the rate-limiting factor for TNB formation. Total glutathione content was determined spectrophotometrically by measuring absorbance at 412 nm. To quantify GSSG specifically, GSH was first scavenged from the sample using masking reagents. The GSH content was then calculated by subtracting the measured GSSG content from the total glutathione. The assay employs kinetic measurement, requiring absorbance readings at 412 nm to be recorded every 5 min. The concentrations of total glutathione and GSSG were calculated based on a standard curve.

Measurement of intracellular GLS activity

4T1 and BCSCs were treated with NPs for 12 h. After centrifugation, 4T1 and BCSCs were resuspended in 200 μL PBS and sonicated. Next, the precipitates were removed. Intracellular GLS activity were then detected by GLS test kit (Shanghai Jining Shiye, China). The mechanism is as follows: GLS catalyzes the hydrolysis of glutamine to glutamate and ammonia. Ammonia reacts with hypochlorite and phenol in alkaline environment to produce the water-soluble indophenol blue which is stable and has a characteristic absorption peak at 630 nm. Thus, GLS activity can be calculated by detecting the increase rate of ammonia. The absorbance of each well was measured at 630 nm with Multiskan SkyHigh (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Flow cytometry analysis

In order to obtain single cell suspensions, tumor tissues were cut into small pieces, digested by adding collagenase IV and DNase I (Biosharp), filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and washed once with PBS. Then, cells were stained with antibody (Biolegend) at predetermined concentrations on ice for 15–20 min in dark. The samples were measured using flow cytometry equipped with 4 lasers (405 nm, 488 nm, 561 nm, 638 nm), 13 fluorescence detectors, and 2 scattered light detectors.

Treatments of BCSCs with NPs

In a typical experiment, 4T1 cancer cells were digested by trypsin and filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer to obtain single cells suspension, which were plated in ultralow attachment plates (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) at a density of 1000 cells/mL and cultured in a serum-free DMEM/F12 (Hyclone), supplemented with B27 (Beyotime), 20 ng/mL EGF and 20 ng/mL bFGF (PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA), and 4 μg/mL insulin (Biosharp), and 0.4% bovine serum albumin (Beyotime). Every 3–4 days, half of fresh medium was added to the plates. On Day 4, NPs were added and incubated for 2 days. The mammospheres were then dissociated using 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco), pipetted up and down 10–20 times with a 1000 μL pipette, and sieved through a 40-μm sieve to generate a single cell suspension. The cells were plated back to the ultralow attachment plates. The number and the size of the formed mammospheres were recorded on Day 7.

Inhibition of TRCs in 3D fibrin gels

In a typical experiment, 4T1 cancer cells were treated with NPs, or co-incubated with GSH for determined times, before cultured into the 3D fibrin gel. 2 mg/mL fibrinogen was mixture with cell suspension in equal volume. The final concentration of fibrinogen and the amounts of cells in the mixture are 1 mg/mL and 5000 cells/mL, respectively. 1 μL of thrombin (0.1 U/μL) was added to each well of 96 well-plate before plating 100 μL of the mixture into each well. The plate was then incubated for 20 min in 37 °C and 200 μL of 1640 medium containing 10% FBS were then added. The sizes of tumor spheroids were measured from Day 1 to Day 7, and the numbers of tumor spheroids were assessed on Day 7.

Results and discussion

Synergistic effect of CuET@HES and HYL001

Since glutaminolysis contributes to GSH synthesis, thereby enhancing cellular antioxidant capacity and suppressing drug cytotoxicity36, we first evaluated the cytotoxic effects of CuET@HES and HYL001 on 4T1 cancer cells and BCSCs prior to constructing the nano-platform co-delivering CuET and HYL001. As shown in Supporting Information Fig. S1, both drugs exhibited stronger cytotoxicity against BCSCs compared to 4T1 cells. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values decreased from 78.14 ng/mL to 66.24 ng/mL for CuET@HES and from 4.38 μmol/L to 3.82 μmol/L for HYL001, indicating superior efficacy of CuET@HES and HYL001 against BCSCs. Subsequently, we investigated the synergistic effect of CuET@HES and HYL001 on 4T1 cancer cells and BCSCs (Supporting Information Fig. S2). The combination index at various concentrations was calculated using CompuSyn software37. The combination of CuET@HES and HYL001 at different concentrations significantly enhanced cytotoxicity. In particular, when CuET@HES was used at a concentration below its IC50 (50 ng/mL), the strongest synergistic effect was observed, with all CI values being less than 1. Therefore, it is rational to combine CuET and HYL001 against intractable CSCs.

Preparation and characterization of CH@PHF NPs

HYL001 is a novel compound previously developed by our group through the modification of lonidamine using triphenyl phosphonium as a mitochondrial-targeting moiety10. This compound exhibits excellent mitochondrial targeting and potent cytotoxicity against CSCs. To co-deliver CuET and HYL001 within a single nanoplatform, CH@PHF was prepared through a multi-step process, as illustrated in Fig. 1. First, CuET@HES was formed via a nanoprecipitation method, leveraging the chelation of copper ions and diethyldithiocarbamate (DDTC) at a 2:1 M ratio to form hydrophobic CuET complexes, stabilized by the steric hindrance of HES. Subsequently, PDA was employed to further stabilize CuET@HES, resulting in negatively charged NPs designated as CuET@PDA/HES26. These NPs readily adsorbed the positively charged HYL001, forming CH@PDA/HES. However, based on previous studies, PDA/HES-based nanoparticles tend to aggregate. To enhance stability and confer tumor-targeting capability, the surface of CH@PDA/HES was modified with HS-HES-FA (Supporting Information Fig. S3), a conjugate of HES linked to folic acid via a thiol group, yielding the final product referred to as CH@PHF NPs38. In addition, control nanoparticles including PHF, CuET@PHF, and HYL001@PHF were separately prepared. As shown in Fig. 2A, all NPs exhibited uniform diameters of approximately 110 nm. PHF and CuET@PHF showed the lowest zeta potentials, ranging between −10 and −15 mV. In contrast, HYL001@PHF displayed a slight positive charged surface (approximately +12 mV), while CH@PHF was nearly electrically neutral (Fig. 2B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations provided consistent results (Fig. 2C). To verify the successful loading of HYL001 in CH@PHF, the distributions of copper, sulfur, and phosphorus within the NPs were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 2D, these elements were co-localized within the NPs. The higher zeta potential of CH@PHF compared to CuET@PHF NPs further confirmed the co-loading of CuET and HYL001. For comparison, CH@BSA was prepared, and the drug loading efficiencies of CuET and HYL001 were evaluated for both types of nanoparticles at similar particle sizes. Although CH@BSA exhibited a lower PDI, its drug loading capacity was inferior to that of CH@PHF (Supporting Information Fig. S4 and Table S1). The as-prepared CH@PHF NPs demonstrated excellent stability and were capable of releasing HYL001 in response to hydrogen peroxide stimulation (Fig. 2E‒G). In the release medium containing Tween 80, NPs exhibited accelerated release of HYL001, which may be attributed to the ability of Tween 80 to promote the dissolution of HYL001. Additionally, under 10 mmol/L H2O2 conditions, CH@PHF was effectively degraded, as indicated by the gradual decrease in the absorbance of CuET at 450 nm over time (Supporting Information Fig. S5).

CH@PHF NPs effectively disrupt redox homeostasis, suppress 4T1 cells and BCSCs, and induce apoptosis

Building upon the demonstrated efficacy of HYL001 and CuET against 4T1 tumor cells, particularly BCSCs, we further investigated their synergistic effects at the cellular level. We first evaluated the cytotoxicity of CH@PHF NPs against 4T1 cells and BCSCs (Fig. 3A and B). CH@PHF NPs, incorporating HYL001, exhibited lower IC50 values (56.31 ng/mL in 4T1 cells and 54.47 ng/mL in BCSCs) compared to CuET@PHF (75.59 ng/mL in 4T1 cells and 74.86 ng/mL in BCSCs). Similarly, CH@PHF demonstrated superior cytotoxicity against MCF-7 and BCSCs from MCF-7 compared to CuET@PHF (Supporting Information Fig. S6). It has been reported that reducing agents, N-acetylcysteine (NAC), an antioxidant, can effectively inhibit cuproptosis and maintain intracellular redox homeostasis39. Considering the mechanisms of HYL001 and CuET, a high concentration of NAC was introduced as an antioxidant to counteract the cytotoxicity of CuET@PHF. The use of a high NAC concentration was primarily considered because lower NAC concentrations might promote the reduction of Cu2+ to Cu + following cellular uptake of CuET@PHF, potentially transiently enhancing its cytotoxic effect (Supporting Information Fig. S7A). The cytotoxic effect of CuET@PHF at a low concentration (20 ng/mL) was indeed inhibited by NAC (Fig. 3C). However, this inhibition was partially reversed upon the introduction of a low concentration of HYL001 (0.5 μmol/L), which itself exhibited minimal cytotoxicity (Fig. S1A). NAC, acting as a precursor for cysteine in GSH synthesis, provides l-cysteine, a rate-limiting amino acid for intracellular GSH generation, thereby increasing GSH levels. Concurrently, NAC upregulates glutathione reductase expression, promoting the conversion of oxidized glutathione (GSSG) to GSH36,39. The introduction of HYL001 likely disrupts this process, impairing cellular redox balance (Fig. S7B). Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis in 4T1 cells under different treatments revealed that HYL001 significantly increased the proportion of late apoptotic cells, from 9.83% in the control group to 24.45% (Fig. 3D and E). The promotion of early apoptosis by the combination of HYL001 and CuET resulted in CH@PHF effectively inducing robust cell apoptosis. Using the JC-1 probe to detect changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), we observed a consistent decrease in the ratio of J-aggregates to J-monomers, with the CH@PHF group showing the lowest ratio (Fig. 3F and Supporting Information Fig. S8).

As shown in Fig. 3G and H, CH@PHF efficiently reduced the proportion of cells with CSCs phenotypes, specifically CD133+ and CD44+CD24- populations, in 4T1 cells. To validate these findings, we assessed the self-renewal and proliferative capacity of 4T1 tumor-repopulating cells (TRCs) treated with various groups using a 3D tumor spheroid model in fibrin gels, as previously reported40,41. 4T1 tumor cells pre-treated with various NPs for 12 h were harvested and seeded into 3D fibrin gels. Spheroid size and number were monitored every two days (Fig. 3I–K). We found that TRCs from the CH@PHF-treated group exhibited the slowest growth rate, with the final number being only half of that observed in the CuET@PHF group. Specifically, the inhibition rate reached 80.3% for the CH@PHF group, compared to 69.8% for the CuET@PHF group. This strongly demonstrates the superior efficacy of CH@PHF NPs in inhibiting tumor spheroid formation and proliferation. Collectively, CH@PHF NPs exert synergistic effects at the cellular level, effectively promoting apoptosis and suppressing the stemness of tumor cells.

Next, we sought to examine redox status changes in cells treated with various NPs. CuET@PHF, HYL001@PHF, and CH@PHF all effectively induced ROS bursts (Fig. 4A and B). Cells treated with CH@PHF exhibited the highest ROS levels, reaching 2.3-fold (4T1) and 2.1-fold (BCSCs) of the control levels. Assessment of total antioxidant capacity indicated that the CH@PHF group had the lowest capacity, approximately 0.27 mmol/L/106 cells (Fig. 4C). A similar trend was observed for GSH levels (Fig. 4D and E). Similarly, GSH levels were significantly inhibited in both MCF-7 cells and BCSCs derived from MCF-7 (Supporting Information Fig. S9). ROS levels in CH@PHF-treated cells peaked later, at 48 h, whereas peaks for CuET@PHF and HYL001@PHF occurred at 12 and 24 h, respectively (Fig. 4F and Supporting Information Fig. S10). Considering the dynamic nature of intracellular GSH and ROS, we investigated the time-dependent profiles of GSH consumption and ROS maintenance. Interestingly, GSH levels in CH@PHF-treated cells reached a minimum at 24 h (only 6.5% of the 0-h level) before beginning to recover. In contrast, GSH levels in CuET@PHF and HYL001@PHF-treated cells reached their nadir earlier, at 12 h (∼27.4% remaining) (Fig. 4G). This prolonged effect of CH@PHF is likely because HYL001 depletes glutamate (an upstream substrate for GSH synthesis) while CuET concurrently consumes existing intracellular GSH, leading to more sustained GSH depletion and ROS maintenance compared to single agents, despite potential compensatory GSH biosynthesis activation in treated cells. Consistent results were observed in BCSCs (Fig. 4H and I). These findings demonstrate that CH@PHF effectively disrupts tumor cell redox homeostasis through prolonged GSH depletion and ROS maintenance.

Transcriptomics reveals that CH@PHF NPs downregulate transcription of genes related to oxidative phosphorylation and glutathione synthesis

The metabolic plasticity of tumor cells and CSCs is a critical factor that must be considered in therapeutic development7,8. Our previous data indicated that HYL001 and CuET inhibit glutaminolysis and tricarboxylic acid cycle10,26. This prompted our interest in further investigating the associated changes in gene expression following treatments. The dual metabolic inhibition by HYL001 and CuET is expected to effectively disrupt the nutrient and energy supply of tumor cells. Therefore, we employed transcriptome sequencing to assess changes in metabolism-related genes in 4T1 cells after 12 h of treatment with CH@PHF NPs (Fig. 5). Using a fold change threshold of 2, we identified 1080 upregulated and 2070 downregulated genes (Supporting Information Fig. S11). Reactome enrichment analysis of the fifteen terms with the lowest Q-values revealed significant alterations in processes related to glutamate and glutathione metabolism42. KEGG and GO enrichment analyses further indicated significant differences in genes associated with cellular response to oxidative stress, regulation of oxidoreductase activity, glutathione biosynthesis and metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation, in addition to genes related to cation transport and homeostasis. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, specific metabolic pathways including oxidative phosphorylation, as well as alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, were significantly suppressed. In contrast, glutathione metabolism showed a tendency towards enhancement, potentially a compensatory feedback response of cells to the redox imbalance induced by CH@PHF treatment43,44. Interestingly, genes related to glycolysis were also downregulated, which might be attributed to HYL001, as a triphenyl phosphonium-conjugated derivative of lonidamine, retaining the ability to selectively inhibit aerobic glycolysis. Furthermore, genes associated with ferroptosis and ROS were upregulated under CH@PHF treatment (Supporting Information Fig. S12). In summary, transcriptomic analysis reveals that CH@PHF NPs downregulate the transcription of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and glutathione synthesis, demonstrating their significant impact on the metabolic regulatory processes of tumor cells.

HYL001 potentiates cuproptosis by suppressing glutaminolysis, disrupting redox homeostasis, and enhancing intracellular Cu+ accumulation

Based on transcriptomic findings regarding CH@PHF's regulation of oxidative phosphorylation, glutathione metabolism, and glutamate synthesis, CH@PHF may alter intracellular copper levels and subsequently induce cuproptosis through the modulation of GSH (Fig. 5C). We first assessed alterations in cellular ATP levels following treatment with various nanoparticles. As shown in Fig. 5D and Supporting Information Fig. S13A, both 4T1 cells and BCSCs treated with CH@PHF exhibited the lowest ATP levels. Our prior research confirmed that HYL001 inhibits GLS activity and suppresses glutaminolysis19. We further investigated the impact of CH@PHF on glutaminolysis (Fig. 5E and S13B) and GLS activity (Fig. 5F and S13C). Interestingly, the baseline Glu/Gln ratio was higher in untreated BCSCs compared to 4T1 cells, suggesting a potentially greater dependence of BCSCs on glutamine metabolism10. Among all NP-treated groups, the CH@PHF group showed the lowest Glu/Gln ratio and GLS activity. CuET@PHF alone had minimal effect on these parameters. Subsequently, we further investigated the effects of CH@PHF on oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis. Specifically, we measured lactate levels in the cell culture supernatant following treatment with different nanoparticles. As shown in Supporting Information Fig. S14, CH@PHF treatment resulted in an approximately 1.5-fold increase in supernatant lactate levels compared to the control group, which was substantially higher than the 1.2-fold increase observed with CuET@PHF and HYL001@PHF45. Additionally, the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase was inhibited by CH@PHF (Fig. 5G). Moreover, cells treated with CH@PHF exhibited enhanced glucose uptake (Fig. 5H and I). These findings indicate that CH@PHF suppresses oxidative phosphorylation while simultaneously forcing cells to rely more heavily on glycolysis for energy production. The synergy between HYL001 and CuET appears to be mediated primarily through the inhibition of GSH biosynthesis and disruption of cellular redox balance. To validate the impact of glutaminolysis inhibition on redox homeostasis, we measured intracellular GSH levels. Both 4T1 cells and BCSCs treated with CH@PHF NPs exhibited the lowest GSH levels compared to other groups (Fig. 4D and E). Exogenous NAC supplementation effectively restored GSH levels depleted by CuET@PHF, while co-administration of HYL001 counteracted this restoration and again suppressed intracellular GSH (Supporting Information Fig. S15). Furthermore, the introduction of HYL001 led to an increase in intracellular copper concentration as detected by ICP-OES. After 12 h, the copper level increased from 1.20-fold (CuET@PHF group) to 1.36-fold (CH@PHF group), while the group receiving a direct combination of CuET@PHF and HYL001 showed an increase to 1.25-fold (Fig. 5J). This enhanced copper accumulation may be attributed to increased cellular uptake of CH@PHF NPs, potentially related to the shift in their zeta potential from approximately −12 mV to near neutral (Fig. 2B). Using the Cu+-specific fluorescent probe Coppersensor-1, we detected intracellular Cu+ levels (Supporting Information Fig. S16)46. As shown in Fig. 5K and L, cells treated with CH@PHF NPs exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity, indicating the highest Cu+ accumulation. Excellent co-localization of Cu+ signal with lysosomes was observed. The GSH depletion coupled with increased intracellular copper promoted by CH@PHF collectively potentiated cuproptosis. Collectively, GSH, as one of the most critical cellular antioxidants and metal chelators, directly binds copper to form non-toxic GSH–Cu complexes, representing a primary cellular detoxification and excretion mechanism for copper. HYL001 effectively inhibits glutaminolysis, suppresses GSH synthesis, and disrupts redox balance. Its combination with CuET synergistically sensitizes cells to cuproptosis (Fig. 5C)19.

CH@PHF NPs suppress tumor growth in a 4T1 orthotopic model and eliminate CSCs

To validate the targeting capability of CH@PHF, we first evaluated the cellular uptake of nanoparticles in vitro. 4T1 cells exhibited higher uptake of CH@PHF compared to CH@PH (Supporting Information Fig. S17). In vitro, the hemolytic activity of CH@PHF was assessed. As shown in Supporting Information Fig. S18, CH@PHF exhibited a favorable safety profile. Subsequently, the biodistribution at 48 h post-administration was examined to evaluate the targeting capability conferred by FA. Nanoparticles accumulated most extensively in the liver and were subsequently excreted into the intestines via bile. After 12 h, significant accumulation of nanoparticles was observed at the tumor site (Supporting Information Fig. S19). Notably, CH@PHF demonstrated the highest tumor accumulation, which may be attributed to the targeting effect of FA and charge modulation (Fig. 6A and S19). Unexpectedly, all nanoparticles co-loaded with HYL001 (HYL001@PHF, CH@PH, CH@PHF) showed marked accumulation in the kidneys (Supporting Information Fig. S20). Similarly, CH@PHF exhibited a shorter blood circulation half-life (2.33 h) compared to CuET@PHF (2.86 h) (Fig. 6B). This may be associated with alterations in the protein corona following charge modulation, though further investigation is warranted. To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of CH@PHF, we first established an orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer model for pharmacodynamic assessment. Tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into seven groups: Saline, CuET, HYL001, CuET + HYL001, CuET@PHF, HYL001@PHF, and CH@PHF. Mice received treatments every other day for a total of three doses. By Day 15, all treatment groups exhibited varying degrees of tumor growth inhibition compared to the Saline group (Fig. 6C and D, and Supporting Information Table S2). The CH@PHF group showed the most potent antitumor effect, with a tumor inhibition rate (TIR) of 48.6% based on tumor volume. Among all treatments, CH@PHF demonstrated the highest efficacy, with a TIR of approximately 47.7% based on tumor weight, compared to TIRs of 16.1%, 14.3%, 23.8%, 28.7%, and 21.6% for the CuET, HYL001, CuET + HYL001, CuET@PHF, and HYL001@PHF groups, respectively (Fig. 6E). This enhanced efficacy is primarily attributed to the synergistic metabolic inhibition from the combination of HYL001 and CuET, as well as improved tumor accumulation facilitated by the HES-FA coating19,29. Consistent with in vitro findings, CH@PHF significantly suppressed glutaminolysis in the orthotopic 4T1 tumors, reducing the Glu/Gln ratio by over 50% (Fig. 6F). Similarly, GLS activity was sharply decreased following treatment with either HYL001@PHF or CH@PHF NPs (Supporting Information Fig. S21). Furthermore, CH@PHF treatment led to decreased intratumoral GSH levels and increased ROS levels (Fig. 6G and H), corroborating that the combined inhibition of glutaminolysis and induction of cuproptosis synergistically promote ROS generation in vivo. CSCs often enter a quiescent state (G0/G1 phase CSCs) contributing to therapy resistance, and their low ROS levels help maintain this quiescence state8,47. Inspired by our in vitro results showing CSCs' susceptibility to ROS and that high oxidative stress aids in CSC elimination (Fig. 4)48, we further investigated the CSC populations in orthotopic tumors after different treatments. As shown in Fig. 6I and J, single-cell suspensions from digested tumor tissues were analyzed by flow cytometry. The results indicated that CuET@PHF, HYL001@PHF, and CH@PHF treatments all effectively reduced the CSC population. Specifically, the CH@PHF treatment reduced the percentage of CD133+ cells from 5.2% (Saline group) to 2.2%, and CD44+CD24– cells from 30.4% (Saline group) to 10.9%. The immunofluorescence section data revealed consistent results (Fig. 6K and Supporting Information Fig. S22). Furthermore, CH@PHF also modulates the immune microenvironment49. Specifically, CH@PHF effectively increased the proportion of CD86+ M1-type macrophages in tumor tissues, while the proportion of M2-type macrophages remained largely unchanged (Supporting Information Fig. S23A–S23E). Additionally, tumor tissues treated with CH@PHF exhibited a significant increase in CD8+ T cells (Fig. S23F–S23H). Additionally, immunohistochemical analysis in Supporting Information Fig. S24 showed that CH@PHF NPs inhibited Ki67 expression (indicating reduced proliferation) and promoted apoptosis in tumor tissues. Subsequently, we systematically evaluated the in vivo safety profile of CH@PHF NPs in the orthotopic 4T1 model (Supporting Information Fig. S25). No significant changes in body weight were observed in any NP-treated group (Fig. S25A). Furthermore, blood biochemical parameters (ALT, BUN, AST, CREA) and complete blood count indices (WBC, RBC, PLT) all remained within normal ranges (Fig. S25B–S25H), demonstrating the excellent biosafety of CH@PHF in vivo. Collectively, CH@PHF NPs co-delivering HYL001 and CuET exhibited the highest antitumor efficacy. Disrupting ROS homeostasis within quiescent CSCs via glutaminolysis inhibition presents a promising adjuvant strategy, which synergizes with CuET to achieve dual metabolic inhibition and effectively eliminate CSCs.

CH@PHF NPs suppress tumor growth and inhibit lung metastasis in an orthotopic BCSCs model

Building upon the demonstrated efficacy of CH@PHF against orthotopic 4T1 tumors, we expanded our in vivo investigation to evaluate whether CH@PHF could effectively impede the growth and metastasis of CSCs in an orthotopic BCSC model32. Briefly, the BCSC tumor model was established by injecting selected highly tumorigenic tumor spheres; this model required only 5 × 104 cells per mouse to form tumors. The treatment regimen was consistent with that used in the orthotopic 4T1 model. Mice were divided into four groups: Saline, CuET@PHF, HYL001@PHF, and CH@PHF. As shown in Fig. 7A, CuET@PHF and HYL001@PHF exhibited limited efficacy in suppressing BCSC tumors, with average tumor volumes reaching 598.9 mm3 and 612 mm3, respectively, by Day 15. In contrast, the average tumor volume in CH@PHF treated group was 334.8 mm3, corresponding to a TIR of 52.7% based on tumor volume. This superior efficacy likely stems from the synergistic action overcoming the metabolic plasticity of CSCs, which often limits single-agent therapies7,8. Furthermore, similar to the findings in the orthotopic 4T1 model, HYL001 effectively suppressed glutaminolysis in the orthotopic BCSC tumors (Supporting Information Fig. S26). CH@PHF treatment also resulted in significantly lower GSH levels within the tumor tissue, reduced to only 57% of the level observed in the Saline group. CH@PHF also demonstrated a remarkable ability to suppress stemness markers. The proportion of CD133+ cells decreased by 60%, and the CD44+CD24– population was reduced by 43.5% (Fig. 7B and C). To assess the effect of CH@PHF NPs on the self-renewal and proliferative capacity of BCSCs, we performed a mammosphere assay by culturing single-cell suspensions from BCSC tumors in 3D soft fibrin gels (Fig. 7D). Fig. 7E and F shows the potent inhibitory effect of CH@PHF on TRCs growth, with both the number and size of TRCs being drastically suppressed in the CH@PHF group, underscoring its robust therapeutic effect against CSCs. Using the orthotopic BCSC model, we further investigated the impact of CH@PHF on lung metastasis. Following the same treatment schedule, mice in the lung metastasis model were maintained for an additional 30 days post-treatment before lung tissues were harvested for evaluation. At the endpoint, no significant differences in primary orthotopic BCSC tumor volumes were observed among the Saline, CuET@PHF, and HYL001@PHF groups, while CH@PHF treatment effectively controlled primary tumor growth (Supporting Information Fig. S27). Analysis of lung images and nodule counts revealed that mice in the CH@PHF group exhibited nearly no visible lung nodules. In contrast, the Saline, CuET@PHF, and HYL001@PHF groups had average counts of 9, 4.5, and 2 lung nodules, respectively (Fig. 7G–J). We also detected circulating CD133+ tumor cells (CTCs) in the blood and found the lowest proportion of CD133+ CTCs in the CH@PHF group (Fig. 7I and Supporting Information Fig. S28). Together, these data demonstrate that CH@PHF NPs effectively suppress the growth of orthotopic BCSC tumors, eliminate CSCs within the tumors and bloodstream, and consequently reduce pulmonary metastasis.

Conclusions

CSCs exhibit remarkable plasticity under nutrient-deficient conditions, a characteristic primarily enabled through metabolic reprogramming that allows them to develop survival advantages under stress5. The low intracellular ROS levels in CSCs help maintain their self-renewal capacity, ability to initiate tumor formation, and resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy4. Therefore, elevating ROS levels to modulate CSC self-renewal represents a promising strategy for augmenting cancer therapy. We constructed a polydopamine/hydroxyethyl starch-based nanoplatform, CH@PHF, for the co-delivery of CuET and HYL001, achieving effective CSC elimination through concurrent metabolic modulation and redox disruption. The incorporation of HYL001 not only suppresses intracellular GSH levels by inhibiting glutaminolysis, thereby mitigating the poor efficacy of cuproptosis caused by GSH-chelated copper, but also, in combination with CuET, implements dual inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation and glutamine metabolism by blocking crucial carbon and nitrogen sources for tumor cells and CSCs. The use of the PHF nanocarrier additionally addresses challenges related to the poor solubility of CuET and the difficulty in achieving tumor-specific targeting for HYL001. These two mitochondria-acting drugs effectively suppress orthotopic 4T1 and BCSC tumors by disrupting redox balance, increasing the concentration of toxic Cu+, and inhibiting ATP synthesis, among other mechanisms. This leads to the elimination of CSCs and suppression of lung metastasis. Overall, this study offers new strategies and insights into antitumor approaches based on redox regulation and metabolic intervention. In previous work, we combined CuET with strategies such as hyperbaric oxygen and photodynamic therapy to reprogram tumor cell metabolism, clear cancer-associated fibroblasts, and sensitize cells to cuproptosis25,26,28. These approaches, which combine glycolysis inhibition with oxidative phosphorylation suppression, also achieved excellent antitumor outcomes. In the present study, we demonstrate that the combination of CuET and HYL001 is both effective and practical. Therefore, in the future, leveraging transcriptomics and single-cell metabolomics to decipher the spatiotemporal dynamics of CSCs, and developing dual metabolic inhibition or nutrient dependency-targeting strategies, hold promise for overcoming CSCs.
