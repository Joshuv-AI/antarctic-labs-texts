Surveillance of gastrointestinal parasites in pheasants reared at farms and zoos in Japan: High prevalence of Eimeria species infection
Authors: Makoto MATSUBAYASHI, Sayaka TSUCHIDA, Atsushi KOBAYASHI, Kazunari USHIDA, Tomoyuki SHIBAHARA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe morphologically examined 148 fecal samples of pheasants reared at farms and zoos in Japan by the sugar flotation method to evaluate infection with gastrointestinal parasites, including Eimeria species. Eimeria oocysts, Capillariid eggs, and Ascaridia/Heterakis eggs were detected in 76.2%, 49.0%, and 26.5%, respectively. These results suggest that these parasites seem to be widespread in pheasants in Japan.Keywords: Eimeria, farm, Japan, pheasant

Pheasants are long-tailed birds belonging to the family Phasianidae (order Galliformes) that are widely distributed worldwide. They are commonly recognized as game birds and their meat is suitable for consumption. To date, 49 species have been identified, including locally endemic ones; however, the identification of subspecies as more detailed classifications is challenging due to crossbreeding [6]. The Japanese green pheasant (Phasianus versicolor) is an endemic species in Japan, comprising four subspecies, all of which are found in the wild and designated as the National Bird of Japan [10]. Additionally, ring-necked pheasants (P. colchicus) as a non-native species also inhabit in Japan. Most pheasants are bred on traditional or commercial farms for use as game birds or meats, whereas others are maintained in zoos for exhibition purposes.

Coccidiosis is an important enteric disease caused by protozoan parasites mainly in the genus Eimeria. The disease is characterized by diarrhea or bloody feces. Nine Eimeria spp. (E. colchici, E. duodenalis, E. phasiani, E. pacifica, E. tetartooimia, E. langeroni, E. megalostomata, E. dispersa, and E. picta) have been reported in pheasants. Three of these species are associated with pathogenicity in the birds. Their differentiation is largely based on oocyst morphology, whereas the use of molecular identification for differentiation remains limited [7].

To date, outbreaks of coccidiosis have been reported at pheasant farms (Japanese green pheasants and ring-necked pheasants) mainly in Ehime prefecture (Shikoku island) in Japan [1, 2, 8, 13]. Clinical symptoms, including depression, poor growth, and death, were observed in some infected birds [8, 13]. Although the Eimeria spp. involved are unclear, heavy infections with parasites were confirmed in pheasant populations, in addition to increasing numbers of Clostridium perfringens and nematode parasite infections. More recently, E. phasiani and E. iyoensis were identified from ring-necked pheasants reared in Ehime using molecular analyses [1, 2]. Additionally, a previous study reported E. tetartooimia infection in a Japanese green pheasant at a zoo in Osaka prefecture [5]; however, the distribution of Eimeria spp. in pheasants in Japan is unclear. In the present study, we surveyed gastrointestinal parasite infections, including Eimeria spp., in pheasants at farms in four regions of Honshu and Kyushu islands of Japan as well as four zoos in Chubu region to investigate their distributions.

We studied freshly dropped feces of 134 pheasants from 12 farms in Tohoku, Chubu, Chugoku, and Kyushu regions of Japan (total 11 prefectures). They included 105 Japanese pheasants from 10 farms, 25 ring-necked pheasants (a farm of Miyazaki) and four unknown (a farm of Iwate-2) at the age from 60 days old to 3 years old or as unknown age (Table 1). Samples were collected in June and November, 2017 (Table 1). Ten fecal samples were collected from chicks (10–20 days old; farms of Iwate-2 and Niigata). Between 1 and 28 fecal samples were collected at the farms. All pheasants were reared by floor feeding. No pheasants showed clinical symptoms, such as diarrhea; however, two farms reported coccidiosis within one year (farms of Aomori and Kagoshima). In addition, 14 fecal samples of Japanese pheasants were collected at four zoos in Chubu region of Japan (total 4 prefectures) during the same period as described above (Table 2). All fecal samples were stored at 4°C until use.

Fecal samples were examined by the modified sugar flotation method as reported previously [3]. Briefly, 1 g of the fecal sample was diluted in distilled water by filtering with mesh. After centrifugation at 1,200 × g for 5 min, 10 mL of sugar solution with a specific gravity of 1.2 was added to the sediment. The tube was centrifuged again, and the upper layer was transferred onto a glass slide using a loop to estimate the number of oocysts per gram of feces (OPGs) and eggs per gram of feces (EPGs) under a light microscope, according to a previous study [11]. Some of detected eimerian oocysts were purified from feces by the sugar flotation method and allowed to sporulate in 2.0% (w/v) potassium dichromate solution at 28°C for approximately 1 week. Sporulated oocysts were morphologically observed under light microscopy at 1,000× magnification. The morphologies of detected oocysts were compared with published data of Eimeria species derived from pheasants as summarized by Norton [7].

Fecal examination showed that all of the 12 examined farms were positive for parasites (Table 1). The prevalences of the parasites among the fecal samples (134 samples) were 85.8%: 82.8% of Eimeria spp., 53.0% of Capillariid parasites, 25.4% of Ascaridia and/or Heterakis spp., and 9.7% of other nematode parasites. Although the species of Eimeria detected in the present study could not be precisely identified, multiple species were morphologically found in most of samples: E. phasiani and E. tetartooimia-like oocysts from a farm of Aomori, E. megalostomata-like oocysts from a farm of Miyazaki, E. phasiani and E. iyoensis-like oocysts from a farm of Kagoshima (Fig. 1). Although the OPGs showed wide variability among the farms, some examined farms were high (over 103 and 104 OPGs) (farms of Iwate-1, Niigata, and Yamaguchi). The OPGs of the farms where sulfa drugs were used (farms of Aomori, Nagano, and Kagoshima), and where pairs of one male and two or three female birds were kept in each separated area (a farm of Toyama) were comparatively low (36.0 to 333.4 on average). Among 10 examined chicks (farms of Iwate-2 and Niigata), only one sample was infected with Eimeria spp. (10 OPG). Only a few studies in pheasants from other countries previously reported the parasitic prevalences of 86.7% in Azerbaijan (54.5% in birds aged 1–30 days, 66.7% in those aged 31–120 days, and 82.3% in those older than 120 days), 37.9% in the United Kingdom, and 0% in Iran [4, 9, 12]. Thus, the prevalence of parasite infections might vary among the countries, which may be affected by the rearing systems of the farms. To date, three Eimeria spp. (E. colchici, E. duodenalis, and E. phasiani) have been identified as highly virulence although further studies of pathogenic are needed [7]. Previously, three eimerian species among them have been reported in Japan: E. phasiani and E. iyoensis from ring-necked pheasants reared at the farms of Ehime prefecture, and E. tetartooimia from a Japanese green pheasant at a zoo at Osaka prefecture [1, 2, 5]. In the present study, coccidiosis has occurred only at two farms within one year when feces were collected although detailed information is not available. Our results suggest that infections of Eimeria spp. are widespread in Japan although further studies are needed to evaluate the affection of the parasites in production of the pheasants at the farms.

Regarding other parasites, Capillariid eggs were most frequently detected (53.0%); moreover, Ascaridia and/or Heterakis spp. eggs (25.4%) and other nematode eggs (9.7%) were identified. Previous studies reported infections of Eucoleus perforans (Nematoda: Trichocephalida: Capillariidae) (8–73%), Capillaria phasianina (Capillariidae) (10–87%), and Heterakis gallinarum (Nematoda: Ascaridida: Heterakidae) (69–88%), based on worms recovered from the esophagus and cecum of pheasants in Ehime prefecture in Japan [2]. Although we could not examine visceral samples, our findings also suggest the presence of these nematodes in pheasant farms in Japan.

The same parasites identified in the pheasants of the farms were also detected in those of zoos. The prevalence was found to be low although the sample size was small. Actually, the populations of birds raised at the zoos were low (3 to 5 birds per zoo) and thus, their OPGs and EPGs might be lower than those of the birds farmed. As an additional reason, this may be attributed to anti-parasitic treatments administered to the zoo-raised pheasants following routine fecal examinations by veterinarians.

In conclusion, we reported the first survey of gastrointestinal parasites using the feces of pheasants taken from a wide area of Japan. High prevalences of Eimeria spp., following Capillariid and Ascaridia and/or Heterakis parasites, were detected. Mixed infections involving the four categorized parasites were found in 64.9% and 42.9% of fecal samples at the farms and zoos, respectively (Tables 1 and 2). Pathogenicity of the detected parasites, especially Eimeria spp., in pheasants has not been fully clarified although birds examined in the present study did not show clear clinical signs. Further epidemiological and molecular studies are needed to investigate whether these parasites affect the poultry industry.
