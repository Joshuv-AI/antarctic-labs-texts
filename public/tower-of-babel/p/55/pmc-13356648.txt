The distinct molecular profile of metastatic prostate cancer in the mixed Brazilian population
Authors: Gabriel S Macedo, Jaqueline Bohrer Schuch, Nathan Araujo Cadore, Angélica Cerveira de Baumont, Giovana Dallaio Curzel, Claudia Bordignon, Mahira Lopes Rosa, Bruna Silveira da Rosa, Daniela Lacerda Costa Louzeiro, Lilian Arruda do Rego Barros, Alessandra Notari, Aline Bobato Lara Gongora, Vitor Fiorin de Vasconcellos, Williams Fernandes Barra, Henrique Guesser Ascenco, Daniel D'Almeida Preto, Juliana Janoski de Menezes, Edilmar de Moura Santos, Vinicius Knackfuss Gonçalves, Pedro Emanuel Rubini Liedke, Gláucio Antônio Bertollo, Eduardo Kowalski Neto, André Luiz Campos Mancini, Francine Hehn de Oliveira, Carmen Liane Neubarth Estivallet, Tiago Finger Andreis, Onco-Genomas Brasil Research Group, Marina Bessel, Daniela Dornelles Rosa

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe carcinogenesis of metastatic prostate cancer results from complex interactions between genetic and environmental factors. The genomic profiles of admixed populations, such as Brazilians, are still poorly explored, offering an opportunity to better understand the contribution of germline and somatic variants in these patients.MethodsIn this multicenter study, 193 men with metastatic prostate cancer, who underwent matched tumor-normal exome sequencing, were enrolled from all five Brazilian macro-regions.ResultsPathogenic and likely pathogenic germline variants in well-known prostate cancer genes were identified in only 5.7% of the patients, with one case (0.5%) harboring the TP53 p.(Arg337His) Brazilian founder variant. Variants in WNT9B, recently associated with familial prostate cancer, were identified in an additional 2.1% of the patients. In contrast to the germline data, somatic results were obtained in only a subset of patients. Regarding homologous recombination deficiency, 21.8% of patients harbored tumor loss-of-function variants in ATM, CDK12, BRCA2, and FANCA.ConclusionEven with the low success rate of tumor samples, our data demonstrated that most loss-of-function variants in homologous recombination pathway genes occur as somatic events. Our findings also highlight the distinct germline genomic profile of the Brazilian population and underscore the challenges associated with performing comprehensive analyses on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples.Keywords: Genetic ancestry, Genomic landscape, Prostate cancer

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PC) accounts for 1 in every 14 cancers diagnosed globally and represents 15% of all male cancers.1,2 At the time of diagnosis, approximately 8% of patients with PC have metastatic disease.2 The carcinogenesis of metastatic PC (MPC) is a consequence of complex interactions between germline susceptibility, acquired somatic alterations, and microenvironmental and macroenvironmental factors.3 Despite its prevalence, the cause of the disease in most men remains unclear. Recent approaches are focused on the interaction between common polymorphisms and the establishment of polygenic risk scores, which require specific knowledge of the genomic landscape of the target population.4,5

Based on current guidelines, including the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Genetic/Familial High-Risk Assessment: Breast, Ovarian, Pancreatic, and Prostate Guidelines, it is recommended that germline and somatic testing be pursued for patients with high-risk features, a family and/or personal history of cancer, Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry, and/or diagnoses of MPC.5,6 Germline pathogenic and likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants in cancer predisposition genes may explain a subset of cases and have considerable implications for screening, treatment, genetic counseling, and cascade testing of family members.7 In a large study of patients with PC at various stages of the disease, 17.2% showed relevant germline variants, of which 30.7% were of BRCA1 (HGNC:1100) and BRCA2 (HGNC:1101) genes.7 In Brazil, a highly prevalent founder germline variant in the TP53 gene (HGNC:11998), commonly referred to as R337H [NM_000546.6:c.1010G>A; NC_000017.11:g.7670699C>T; p.(Arg337His)], has been associated with PC risk and is classically linked to Li-Fraumeni syndrome (OMIM #151623), potentially explaining a subgroup of cases.8

The treatment for metastatic or biochemically recurrent PC commonly includes androgen deprivation therapy, often combined with an androgen receptor pathway inhibitor, with or without docetaxel. Despite advances, patients often experience disease progression even while maintaining castrate levels of serum testosterone, either with metastases (metastatic castration-resistant PC [mCRPC]) or without metastases.9, 10, 11 Multiple randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the efficacy of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors in patients with mCRPC harboring deleterious alterations in BRCA1, BRCA2, or other homologous recombination repair (HRR) genes.9 Therefore, tumor molecular profiling is increasingly being used to biologically characterize and guide the selection of more precisely targeted therapeutic interventions in MPC. Importantly, loss-of-function (LOF) variants in genes with a direct or indirect role in HRR have been detected in 28% of all tumor samples.12 However, this prevalence may vary considerably among races and genetic ancestries. Moreover, successful implementation of somatic genomic testing in clinical practice is challenging, considering the high rate of next-generation sequencing (NGS) failures due mainly to low-quality tumor tissue.13 In addition, clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), defined as the presence of somatic variants in hematopoietic cells without clinical evidence of hematologic malignancy, may act as an important confounder in the interpretation of genomic tests, particularly when using blood-derived DNA or liquid biopsy assays. This confounding role of CHIP in the interpretation of genomic findings has been well described in studies.14,15

It is incompletely understood how and why molecular features of patients with PC differ by self-reported race and genetic ancestry, and studies have been performed mostly in Asian, White, and Black men.13 The Brazilian population has one of the most diverse patterns of admixture in the world, with predominantly three parental populations (African, Brazilian Native American, and European).14,15 Here, we performed prospective exome sequencing (ES) of matched tumor-blood samples from 193 patients diagnosed with MPC, coupled with clinical annotation and genetic ancestry, to define the germline and somatic genomic landscape in the mixed Brazilian population.

Materials and Methods

Study design and participants

In this multicenter study, we included men with MPC, recruited from participating centers across all 5 Brazilian macro-regions between October 2022 and December 2023. The study protocol was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov and approved by the research ethics committee of the coordinating institution and by all local ethics committees of the participating centers. All participants signed an informed consent form. The Onco-Genomas Brasil study is part of the Brazilian Genomes Program (Programa Genomas Brasil), supported by the Brazilian Ministry of Health. Detailed information about the study protocol was previously published by Schuch et al.16

The inclusion criteria were patients aged ≥18 years, with a histologic diagnosis of prostate adenocarcinoma and clinical stage IV according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer classification. Patients with unavailable tumor tissue from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) or blood samples were excluded.

The cohort consisted of 193 patients assisted by the Brazilian Unified Health System (SUS). All participants answered a questionnaire with demographic data and personal and family history of cancer. Family history of neoplasia was defined according to the presence of cancer in relatives, including 1st, 2nd, and 3rd degree relatives. Information regarding initial diagnosis, staging, date of diagnosis of metastatic disease, and treatments performed (prostatectomy, radiotherapy, and hormonal blockade) were also collected. Finally, a follow-up questionnaire was completed at the end of the study, with current information on further treatment and survival (data not shown).

DNA extraction from blood and FFPE

DNA from peripheral blood and FFPE samples was obtained as previously described.15 Briefly, DNA from whole blood was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Kit (Qiagen). For tumor samples, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections underwent pathological review. A trained pathologist selected the tumor area, and only samples with ≥20% tumor cell content were cut into 10-μm sections, and DNA extraction was performed. Genomic DNA from FFPE samples was extracted using a commercial kit (QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit, Qiagen).

ES and data analysis

Library preparation was performed using the Twist Library Preparation EF 2.0 Kit, followed by sequencing on Illumina's NovaSeq 6000 platform. Mean coverage and SD of 149.79 (SD = 29.15) and 536.13 (SD = 153.01) were achieved for normal and tumor samples, respectively.

The Illumina DRAGEN (Dynamic Read Analysis for GENomics) secondary analysis v.3.10.4 tool was used to map and call genomic alterations (single-nucleotide variation [SNV], indel, and copy number alterations) produced by ES with the human reference genome GRCh38 (hg38; GenBank accession GCA_000001405.28). The DRAGEN tumor-normal mode was performed to identify somatic variations and nonsynonymous tumor mutational burden (TMB). The Varstation platform was employed to analyze and visualize germline and somatic variants.

Tumors copy number variants were inferred using the CNVKit v0.9.11 tool by comparing matching tumor-normal samples. A pooled analysis was performed by rounds of sequencing, using blood samples as a reference germline compared with each tumor sample. Discrete copy number segments were generated from the bin-level log2 ratio values using the circular binary segmentation algorithm. The observed log2 copy number ratios were adjusted using the tumor purity to generate the absolute copy number values. An adjusted log2 ratio >2 was considered an amplification, and < −1.2 was considered a deep deletion.

Guidelines and updates from the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and ClinGen (Clinical Genome Resource) were used to interpret and classify germline variants.17 Here, we reported only those variants of uncertain significance (VUS) that were more likely to be causative. The oncogenicity of somatic variants was assessed by searching the OncoKB database.18,19 Since the number of genes with a known role in cancer is limited, our analyses were restricted to 148 and 410 genes in the germline and somatic contexts, respectively (Supplemental Table 1). Tumor loss-of-heterozygosity (LOH) was assessed among selected participants carrying a germline P/LP, considering deletions of at least one allele (log2 < –1.2).

CHIP was considered as the presence of a somatic leukemia-associated variant at a variant allele frequency of at least 2% in the blood samples of patients with PC but without evidence of hematological neoplasm.20 The following genes were analyzed for the presence of likely CHIP variants: ASXL1 (HGNC:18318), ATM (HGNC:795), CBL (HGNC:1541), CHEK2 (HGNC:16627), DNMT3A (HGNC:2978), IDH1 (HGNC:5382), IDH2 (HGNC:5383), JAK2 (HGNC:6192), PPM1D (HGNC:9277), RUNX1 (HGNC:10471), SF3B1 (HGNC:10768), TET2 (HGNC:25941), TP53 (HGNC:11998) and U2AF1 (HGNC:12453).

Global ancestry inference

Global ancestry was inferred using ADMIXTURE software by a supervised analysis with κ = 4. A multisample VCF file containing all the target samples was merged with samples from the 1000 Genomes phase 3 project, including African, East Asian, and European, and from the Human Genome Diversity Project for Native American continental populations, as a reference panel. Principal component analysis was performed using Hail (hail.is) and normalized by Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (hwe_normalized_pca).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1). Descriptive analyses of continuous variables were performed using the mean and SD or the median and interquartile range (IQR), depending on the data distribution, which was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were described as absolute and relative frequencies.

To compare groups, Student’s t-test was used for continuous variables with a normal distribution, and the Mann-Whitney test was applied for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Responses categorized as "Not informed" and "Unknown" were treated as missing data and excluded from the analyses for the respective variables, considering only valid responses for these analyses. The significance level was set at 5%, and all tests were two-tailed.

Results

Cohort characteristics and ancestry composition

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the 193 Brazilian patients with MPC who were prospectively enrolled in the study. The mean age was 68.4 years; 50.8% self-reported as White, and 102 (53.4%) patients reported smoking. The mean age at diagnosis was 64.5 years, with 80.3% of cases diagnosed at stage IV. The distribution of individuals recruited according to the 5 macro-regions of the country can also be seen in Supplemental Figure 1. During treatment, 34 patients (17.6%) underwent prostatectomy, 42 (21.9%) received radiotherapy, of whom 29 (69.0%) were with curative intent, and nearly all patients underwent testosterone production blockade (n = 187, 96.9%). Additional systemic therapy was administered to 151 cases (78.2%).

Ancestry fractions were estimated for all individuals to investigate the composition of their genetic ancestry. The cohort was composed mainly of individuals of European ancestry, followed by those of African and Native American ancestry, showing median proportions of 67%, 14%, and 13%, respectively (Figure 1 and Supplemental Figure 1). Overall, 7 patients (3.7%) were previously diagnosed with another primary tumor, and 121 (66.8%) patients had a familial history of cancer, 53.6% of which were classified as first-degree relatives. The most commonly reported cancers among family members were prostate (79 cases, 43.6%), breast (33 cases, 18.2%), and colorectal (21 cases, 11.6%) cancers (Table 1).

Germline P/LP variants

Germline P/LP variants were identified in 9.8% (19 out of 193) of the patients. The most prevalent mutated genes among the entire cohort were CHEK2 and MUTYH (HGNC:7527), 1.5% (n = 3) each; BRCA2, ATM, PALB2 (HGNC:26144), and FANCA (HGNC:3582), 1.0% (n = 2) each; and APC, ATR, HOXB13 (HGNC:5130), MSH3 (HGNC:7326), PMS2 (HGNC:9122), RAD51D (HGNC:9823), and TP53, each found in one case (0.5%). The TP53 R337H (c.1010G>A; p.Arg337His) Brazilian founder variant was seen in only one patient (0.5%) (Figure 2). Importantly, the prevalence of germline P/LP variants was 5.7% when we limited our analysis to genes with a known association with PC risk (Supplemental Figure 2). The median age at diagnosis of these patients was 61 years (IQR: 55-63.7) compared with 64 years (IQR: 60-70.5) in those who were non-heterozygous for specific P/LP variants (P = .033) (Supplemental Table 3). There was no difference in the genetic ancestry composition between those who were heterozygous and those who were non-heterozygous for P/LP variants, although we observed a slightly lower median for African genetic ancestry in the first group (Supplemental Table 4). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of mismatch repair (MMR) proteins in the tumor of a patient with a PMS2 germline variant showed intact PMS2 expression (Supplemental Figure 3). Additionally, we investigated the presence of p.(Glu152Lys) and p.(Gln47Arg) in the WNT9B (HGNC:12779) gene, recently identified as associated with familial PC. None of the patients carried the WNT9B p.(Glu152Lys), while 4 out of 193 (2.1%) were heterozygous for the WNT9B p.(Gln47Arg), all of whom were negative for P/LP variants in other PC predisposition genes (Supplemental Table 5).

VUS with a higher probability of being causative (at least 3 points) were found in 3.1% of patients, including variants with potential relevance to PC (Supplemental Table 5). For instance, 1 patient diagnosed with MPC at the age of 69 years and with no family history of cancers classically associated with Lynch syndrome (LS) harbored the MLH1 (HGNC:7127) p.(Ile36Thr) variant (ClinVar, variation ID 577034). Further investigation of the tumor sample showed a high TMB (21.9 mut/Mb), but no biallelic inactivation by somatic LOH (Supplemental Table 5). The wild-type allele was also preserved in the tumor of a patient with a VUS in the NF1 (HGNC:7765) gene, associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 (OMIM #162200), suggesting that this variant is unlikely to be pathogenic. Interestingly, one patient from this PC cohort and two other women from a Brazilian breast cancer cohort (unpublished data; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05306600) presented the same c.1028-1A>T splice site variant in the MSH3 gene. The patients with PC showed a higher proportion of Native American ancestry (33%), in agreement with individuals carrying this variant and of admixed American ancestry found in the gnomAD database (SNV 5-80674982-A-T). Additionally, in a patient with concomitant P/LP variants in ATM and CHEK2 genes, LOH was restricted to the ATM gene (Supplemental Table 5).

Somatic mutational landscape and CHIP

Of the 193 patients with available FFPE samples, 47.7% (n = 92) showed low tumor content, failure to extract sufficient DNA, and/or high levels of DNA fragmentation. These tumor samples were not submitted for somatic ES. Additionally, 23.8% (n = 46) of the samples did not meet the minimum quality control metrics for coverage, resulting in NGS failure. Thus, valid NGS results were obtained in only 28.5% (n = 55) of cases (Supplemental Table 5). The mean nonsynonymous mutational load was 3.73 (ranging from 0.5 to 21.9) mutations/Mb, with 4 cases (7.3%) showing more than 10 mutations/Mb. Most of the somatically altered genes occurred in less than 5% of cases. Recurrently mutated genes included well-known PC drivers, such as TP53, SPOP (HGNC:11254), APC (HGNC:583), PTEN (HGNC:9588), and KMT2C (HGNC:13726). In total, 18.1% of the patients carried somatic alterations in the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) pathway, including LOF variants in PTEN, as well as activating mutations in PIK3CA (HGNC:8975) and AKT1 (HGNC:391). Regarding HRR genes, 21.8% (n = 12) of patients harbored tumor LOF variants in ATM, CDK12 (HGNC:24224), BRCA2, and FANCA (Figure 3 and Supplemental Table 5). The genome-wide analysis demonstrated frequent copy number gains of 8q, as well as copy number losses of 8p, 13q, 16p, 16q, and 18q (Figure 4).

Likely CHIP clones were seen in 13 of 193 patients (6.7%), all of whom were older than 60 years. The affected genes were DNMT3A (n = 7), ASXL1 (n = 3), IDH1 (n = 1), RUNX1 (n = 1), and TET2 (n = 2), variant allelic fractions were at least 16%. Seven of 14 likely CHIP clones were investigated in FFPE tumor samples. Two were undetectable, and five had low allelic frequencies (<3%) (Supplemental Table 5).

Discussion

PC has been described as a tumor with remarkable differences in the incidence and prevalence of specific genomic alterations among diverse ethnicities and geographic regions, suggesting potential contributions to region-specific patterns of disease evolution and genetic susceptibility.13,21 To our knowledge, this is the first study to report the genomic features of a well-characterized and miscegenated population of patients diagnosed with MPC from all 5 Brazilian macro-regions.

Implications of germline P/LP variants in Brazilian patients

In our Brazilian cohort, the prevalence of germline P/LP variants was 9.8% when we considered a set of 148 genes; in contrast, it was observed that the prevalence was only 5.7% when analyses were restricted to genes with a known association with PC risk. The lower prevalence of P/LP variants compared with that found in North American and European populations may have several reasons. A plausible explanation is a minor contribution of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry in our cohort and, consequently resulting in a lower prevalence of pathogenic founder variants in BRCA1 (e.g., 185delAG and 5382 insC) and BRCA2 (e.g., 6174delT), which are thought to account for most BRCA1 and BRCA2 P/LP variants in Ashkenazi Jews.22 In fact, in a series of 1682 Brazilian individuals who received a multi-gene NGS panel for hereditary cancer risk, only 1.3% reported Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry.23 In a study with data from a Clinical Laboratory in the USA comprising mostly White men, relevant germline variants were found in 17.2% of patients with PC, of whom BRCA2 and CHEK2 were the most frequent genes, mutated in 4.7% and 2.8% of cases, respectively. The authors also demonstrated that the prevalence was higher in Ashkenazi Jews (22.7%) and lower in Hispanics (6.4%).7 In another study, 11.8% of men with MPC who were unselected for family history of cancer or age at diagnosis harbored relevant germline variants in genes mediating DNA-repair processes; this prevalence is significantly higher than that among men with localized PC.24 Data from European populations have found similar prevalences to those as described above.25,26 In a publication comparing self-reported Hispanic men and non-Hispanic men with PC, 7.1% and 9.7% were heterozygous for P/LP variants, respectively.27 Recently, a multicenter study including patients with MPC from Latin American countries showed that about 5.3% were heterozygous for P/LP variants in HRR genes (plus HOXB13). Consistent with our results, CHEK2 was the most frequently mutated gene, followed by ATM and BRCA2.28 Additionally, in our series, a high proportion of patients did not cluster in any of the ancestry groups, showing a high dispersion (Supplemental Figure 4). As most variants associated with PC were interpreted using data from European and North American populations, the relatively low rate of germline P/LP variants found in this study might also reflect the lack of curated genomic data in populations with African and Native American ancestry.

LS (OMIM #120435), caused by LOF variants in MMR genes, is the most common hereditary cancer syndrome (that occurs in 1 of 300 individuals).29 While MMR variants have been reported in ∼1.7% of PC cases,7 in our cohort, only one patient (0.5%) carried a PMS2 p.(Ser46Ile) variant. IHC showed intact PMS2 expression, suggesting that this variant is unlikely to be causative, consistent with the limited evidence for PMS2 in PC risk.30 LOH analyses in tumor samples have also been used to demonstrate the causality of germline variants in tumor suppressor genes.31 In this sense, biallelic inactivation was not seen in a case with a VUS in MLH1, but was observed in the ATM gene in a patient with concomitant P/LP variants in the ATM and CHEK2 genes.

Somatic alterations, CHIP, and therapeutic implications

In clinical practice, tumor genomic testing is crucial for identifying men with mCRPC who are eligible for olaparib and other emerging targeted treatments.32 Our success rate for tumor-based ES was remarkably low (28.5%), mostly due to low-quality and/or insufficient DNA samples. Manneh et al28 recruited patients with PC from Latin America and the Caribbean countries and, using a panel of 25 HRR genes, also reported a high testing failure rate (79%, 199/251). The PROfound trial, one of the largest studies to investigate the influence of preanalytical parameters in PC, obtained NGS results in 58% (2792/4858) of tested samples. Factors such as higher tumor content and DNA mass increased the NGS success rate.12 The NGS panel used in the PROfound study required at least 50 ng of DNA post-extraction to proceed with library construction and NGS, while in our study, 100 ng of DNA was required for tumor ES.

Paired tumor-normal sequencing is being implemented at various laboratories and provides several advantages over tumor-only or blood-only sequencing approaches, distinguishing between germline and somatic variants and enabling the identification of potential clonal hematopoiesis-related variants.33 The tumor genomic characteristics of primary and MPC tumors have been robustly assessed through comprehensive molecular studies, which have identified recurrently mutated genes and pathways, including DNA repair, androgen signaling, and PI3K/AKT signaling.34, 35, 36 Although we had a small number of cases with tumor exome results (28.5%, 55/193), the prevalence of genomic alterations in some recurrently mutated genes, such as TP53 (30.9%), SPOP (16.4%), APC (9.1%), and KMT2C (7.3%), were similar to that previously described in other populations.13,36 The frequency of somatic LOF variants in HRR genes was remarkably high in our study (21.8%), suggesting the importance of somatic testing and accurate detection of copy number loss in patients with MPC. Integrating somatic data with germline information in HHR genes (n = 13, 6.7%), we found that 28.5% of MPC cases harbored HRR alterations. In 2015, a high response rate to Olaparib was shown in patients with mCRPC who had defects in DNA-repair genes, mostly BRCA1, BRCA2, and ATM.37 More recently, a randomized, open-label, phase 3 trial evaluating the PARP inhibitor Olaparib in men with mCRPC who had disease progression on a new hormonal agent and who had alterations in genes with a role in HRR demonstrated longer progression-free survival and better measures of response and patient-reported endpoints than either enzalutamide or abiraterone. In this study, tumor HRR variants in at least 1 of the 15 pre-specified genes were detected in 28% of cases, mostly in BRCA2, CDK12, and ATM genes, with a similar prevalence and pattern to our data.9 In the only comprehensive study conducted in Latin America, somatic testing results were not reported due to the small number of analyzed samples.28

Genomic studies in PC have underrepresented not only Black but also Mixed populations, such as Brazilians, thereby limiting our understanding of disparities in the diagnosis and prognosis of MPC among these populations.13,38 Unlike other studies, in which PTEN loss has been observed in 15 to 20% of primary PC and 40 to 60% in CRPC,39 here only 14.5% of the patients with MPC showed LOF variants or deep deletion in PTEN. Regarding actionable genes of the HRR pathway, no differences were observed in variant frequency (e.g., BRCA2, ATM, and CDK12) between African American and White men so far. Robinson et al36 found relevant germline and somatic HRR variants in 8% and 11.3% of metastatic CRPC cases, respectively. Recently, an unpublished study including a total of 729 patients found BRCA1/2 variants in 13.2% of patients, with 9.8% having somatic and 3.4% germline variants, respectively. HRR non-BRCA variants were detected in 17.4% of patients, with 13.4% and 4% being somatic and germline, respectively.40 In our study, the somatic/germline ratio seems to be higher, with a high proportion of patients presenting LOF variants and deletions in the tumor.

CHIP variants have been detected in liquid biopsies using circulating free DNA testing in about 20% of patients with advanced PC and are considered potentially interfering factors since treatment decisions can be made based on these findings.15 Our tumor-normal approach allowed us to determine that none of the patients carried a CHIP in genes that could be targeted for the use of PARP inhibitors, such as CHIP variants in ATM and CHEK2.

Limitations and strengths of study

Our study has several limitations. The bioinformatic approach used in the tumor exome detects SNVs, indels, and copy number variants (amplifications and homozygous deletions). Structural chromosomal changes, such as those involving the E26 transformation-specific family of transcription factors identified in 40% to 60% of cases, were not evaluated. Another important limitation was the high rate of somatic exome failure, which limited our ability to evaluate somatic alterations and LOH for specific genes in most of the samples. Although treatment and survival data were collected, the follow-up time was short, and the study does not allow us to draw conclusions about the survival and treatment sequence of the patients. On the other hand, the study has important strengths, including the prospective nature and representativeness of all the diversity identified in the Brazilian population, since patients were recruited from the 5 Brazilian macro-regions. In addition, the study provides insights into the complementary value of germline testing and tumor sequencing for men with MPC and highlights the challenges associated with assessing genomic profiles in FFPE samples.

Conclusion

The current study provides a comprehensive evaluation of both germline and somatic molecular profiles in Brazilian men with MPC. We observed a relatively low prevalence of germline P/LP variants in established risk genes alongside frequent somatic alterations in HRR genes. Our findings also underscore the challenges of performing somatic testing in FFPE samples, given the high rate of NGS failures, and highlight the importance of studying admixed populations to better inform clinical practice and future research.
