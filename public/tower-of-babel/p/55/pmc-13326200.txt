Platypnea-Orthodeoxia Syndrome in a Patient With Aortic Root Dilation and a Patent Foramen Ovale
Authors: AK Prohl, S Kwiatkowski, A Samanta, SN Hong-Zohlman, H Allaham, L Forbess
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlatypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome (POS) is a rare condition characterized by dyspnea and hypoxemia in the upright position that improves when supine.Case SummaryA 75-year-old man presented with dyspnea and chronic hypoxemic respiratory failure of unknown etiology. The patient was dyspneic sitting and standing and was free of symptoms when supine. Supine and upright pulse oximetry and bubble study confirmed upright hypoxemia, right-to-left shunting across a patent foramen ovale (PFO), and aortic root dilation. The patient's dyspnea and hypoxemia resolved after device closure of the PFO.DiscussionAortic root dilation is a key anatomical risk factor for right-to-left shunting in POS. Obesity can increase right atrial pressure and is likely synergistic with aortic root dilation. Percutaneous PFO closure is a low-risk curative procedure.Take-Home MessageIn cases of hypoxemia of uncertain etiology, evaluation for POS with a thorough history and supine and upright pulse oximetry and bubble study can prevent exhaustive diagnostic workups and expedite time to treatment with PFO closure.Key words: hypoxemia, occluder, shortness of breath

History of Presentation

A 75-year-old man presented to our institution with refractory hypoxemic respiratory failure of unknown etiology. The patient first developed dyspnea on exertion and hypoxemia 15 months before the current presentation. He was hospitalized 4 times during this time interval, treated twice for community-acquired pneumonia and once for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation. Two transthoracic echocardiograms were negative for shunting. Six computed tomography scans of the chest were performed and were free of significant parenchymal or vascular abnormalities in the lungs. Pulmonary function tests were within normal limits. Home oxygen requirement increased from 2 to 5 L during this time frame. During his third hospitalization, 2 weeks before the current presentation, transthoracic echocardiography was performed upright in a chair due to patient preference. Bubble study was notable for right-to-left shunting. Subsequent right heart catheterization showed normal intracardiac filling pressures and no shunt. He was transferred to our institution for further evaluation.

On arrival to our institution, the patient's oxygen saturation was 92% on 40 L, 100% fraction of inspired oxygen via high-flow nasal cannula while sitting upright in bed. Based on our review of his history, we suspected POS and assessed his supine oxygen saturation on room air and found it to be 99%. After sitting the patient upright and placing his feet on the floor, his oxygen saturation dropped to 69%. Blood pressure was 140/76 mm Hg, and pulse was 70 beats/min. Examination was remarkable for tachypnea at 20 breaths/min while upright, normal respiratory sounds, regular rate and rhythm without murmur, no elevation of the jugular venous pressure, warm extremities, and no peripheral edema.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, obesity with a body mass index of 42, obstructive sleep apnea, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, mild aortic root dilation, chronic kidney disease stage 3A, stroke, seizure disorder, and immune thrombocytopenia. He denied a history of chest trauma and chest surgery.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential included platypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome (POS) from intracardiac shunt and intrapulmonary shunt from hepatopulmonary syndrome or arteriovenous malformations.

Investigations

Admission laboratory tests were notable for normal hemoglobin of 14 g/dL, platelets 77 × 103/μL, potassium of 2.7 mEq/L after diuresis, and creatinine of 1.36 mg/dL, which was the patient's baseline. Total bilirubin was 2.0 mg/dL without a differential collected. Liver enzymes were normal. N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide was 617 pg/mL. Electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm. Limited transthoracic echocardiography with agitated saline with the patient at 0° and 45° showed no evidence of shunting. At 90° (upright position), right-to-left shunting occurred immediately (Figure 1). Transesophageal echocardiography identified a patent foramen ovale (PFO) measuring 9 mm in length with intermittent right-to-left shunting and bowing of the interatrial septum to the left with periods of coughing. Other findings included dilation of the aortic root up to 4.3 cm, mild-to-moderate aortic regurgitation, and normal biventricular function (Video 1).

Transthoracic Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography apical 5-chamber view in the supine (left) and upright (right) positions. Bubbles are visualized in the left atrium and ventricle in the upright position only, consistent with a right-to-left shunting.

Management

POS was diagnosed. The patient underwent PFO closure with a 30-mm Amplatzer Talisman PFO Occluder device (Abbott). A small residual shunt was noted after device deployment and was expected to close within 6 weeks. The day following PFO closure, he felt well but had persistent upright hypoxemia with an upright room air oxygen saturation of 75%. He insisted on discharge and left against medical advice with 6 L oxygen by nasal cannula.

Outcome and Follow Up

The patient was rehospitalized 1 week after discharge with sepsis caused by Pseudomonas bacteremia. He was weaned off supplemental oxygen on day 13 post PFO closure during that hospitalization. After 12 months of follow-up, he remains normoxic and dyspnea has resolved. He has not had repeat transthoracic echocardiography with bubble study.

Discussion

POS is characterized by hypoxemia that occurs in the upright position and resolves when supine. It is due to a positional right-to-left shunt, where shunt flow is maximal in the upright position and minimal or absent in the supine position. Although POS is most commonly associated with intracardiac defects, such as a PFO, atrial septal defect, or atrial septal aneurysm, extracardiac etiologies, including pulmonary arteriovenous malformations, have also been documented.1

The right-to-left shunting is provoked by an anatomical distortion of the atrial septum that occurs in the upright position. Aortic root enlargement is commonly found in patients with POS and PFO.2,3 Compression of the atrial septum by the enlarged aortic root while in the upright position results in increased mobility of the atrial septum and leads to changes in right atrial flow dynamics, directing blood to flow from right to left across the septum.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Aortic root dilation has been associated with decreased atrial septal length and increased septal mobility.4 In a case series of 14 patients with POS and PFO, the mean aortic root diameter was only 37 ± 6 mm, suggesting that even mild dilation of the aortic root can predispose to right-to-left shunting.3 Other factors associated with POS include prominent eustachian valve, Chiari network, severe tricuspid regurgitation, and prior cannulation of the right atrium for cardiac surgery or pneumonectomy.1

The patient's class III obesity also contributed to an increased propensity for shunting. Severe obesity is associated with increased cardiac output, which can lead to left ventricular enlargement and diastolic dysfunction. These changes result in elevated left atrial pressure and pulmonary venous hypertension, ultimately promoting right ventricular dilation and dysfunction, as well as increased right atrial pressure and volume.7,8 Collectively, these hemodynamic alterations increase the likelihood of right-to-left shunting independent of body position. However, if obesity were the only driver of the shunt, greater shunting would be expected in the supine position, when intrathoracic pressure increases in recumbency. This discrepancy highlights the contribution of a shunt-provoking factor in the upright position, such as aortic dilation, in the pathophysiology of POS. Furthermore, review of the patient's echocardiograms obtained before the onset of dyspnea show progressive enlargement of the aortic root from 3.7 cm to 4.3 cm on presentation, as well as a weight gain of 15 lb, supporting aortic root dilation and obesity as synergistic in the development and progression of his shunt-related hypoxemia.

Catheter based PFO or atrial septal defect closure is an effective and low-risk procedure for the treatment of POS.3,9 In contrast to prior reports of immediate POS resolution after device placement, complete resolution of hypoxemia in our patient occurred 13 days post procedure.3,9,10 Our patient had a known residual right-to-left shunt, which was expected to close over a number of weeks with complete endothelialization of the device. Prior reports indicate complete PFO closure after Amplatzer PFO Occluder placement in 93% at hospital discharge and 91% to 97% at 6 months post procedure. Residual shunts occur more commonly in patients with larger (35 mm) devices compared with smaller (18-25 mm) devices and in patients with an associated atrial septal aneurysm.11,12 Residual shunt in our patient is consistent with these reports, as he had implantation of a 30-mm device.

Conclusions

In the present case, the diagnosis of POS was made 15 months after the patient initially presented with hypoxemia. During those 15 months, the patient underwent a number of diagnostic studies at a significant cost and experienced a marked decline in quality of life. In patients with unexplained hypoxemia, particularly individuals who are obese with a history of aortic root dilation or prior chest surgery, supine and upright oxygen saturation and bubble study are simple diagnostic tools to diagnose POS. Cardiac catheterization and routine echocardiography are performed supine and will not detect a right-to-left shunt in a patient with POS. Revision of shunt echocardiography protocols for unexplained hypoxemia to include both supine and upright positioning may increase detection of this uncommon and likely underdiagnosed condition.

Aortic Root Dilation in POS: A Visual Summary

Schematic of parasternal short axis view at the level of the aortic root in supine (A) and upright (B) positions. In the upright position (B), compression of the interatrial septum (IAS) by the dilated aortic root causes IAS distortion, and right-to-left shunting of deoxygenated blood (blue) across the patent foramen ovale (PFO) mixing with oxygenated blood (red) produces mixed arterial-venous blood (purple) and hypoxemia. In the supine position (A), the IAS is not distorted, and shunting across the PFO does not occur. LA = left atrium; PA = pulmonary artery; RA = right atrium; RVOT = right ventricular outflow tract.
