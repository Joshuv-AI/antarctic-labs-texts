Keep calm and carry on: bites from Trichonephila clavata are unlikely and cause minimal discomfort
Authors: David R Nelsen, Angela Chuang, Aaron G Corbit, Michael I Sitvarin, Hannah E Bergmann, Everaldo P De Araujo, Sarah R Hunter, Jared K Ing, Justin J Park, Kaitlyn A Vasquez, Amani S Wang, David R Coyle, Warren Booth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArachnophobia is a widespread phenomenon, despite the fact that the vast majority of spiders pose no meaningful threat to people. The introduction and spread of an invasive spider (Trichonephila clavata L. Koch, 1878) to the United States has prompted questions about whether it should be considered dangerous. These questions are particularly relevant because the spider is large, builds webs on and near human structures, and has been documented to consume small vertebrate prey. To understand the realistic threat this species represents, we examined spider–human interactions in the field with escalating levels of contact intensity. During these interactions, spiders primarily moved to avoid human contact, and bites were incredibly rare, mainly occurring when spiders were forcibly restrained. To assess the medical significance of a bite by T. clavata, we conducted what we believe to be the first controlled study of spider bites. Spiders were induced to bite volunteers under controlled laboratory conditions while supervised by medical staff. Subjects who were bitten reported reliably low levels of pain and only localized physical symptoms (eg redness and swelling) that attenuated quickly. It is clear that this spider is unlikely to bite someone who encounters it in nature, and the symptoms are minor and fleeting in the event of a bite. Although T. clavata is spreading quickly in the United States, the risk of its bite or resulting symptoms should not be cause for fear.Keywords: invasive species, Jorō, spider, orbweaver, spider bite, Wong–Baker pain scale

Introduction

The vast majority of the nearly 53,000 spider species worldwide (World Spider Catalog 2025) are not dangerous to humans. In North America, only spiders in the genera Latrodectus (widow spiders) and Loxosceles (recluse spiders) are considered to be medically important, possessing venom toxic enough to cause substantial harm in humans (Vetter 2013). However, despite the rarity of being bitten by a dangerous spider, arachnophobia is one of the most common phobias worldwide (Oosterink et al. 2009, Correia and Mammola 2024). This fear may be compounded by spiders that are large in size (Cranshaw 2006, Zvaríková et al. 2021) or tend to inhabit the same general areas as humans (eg homes, structures, gardens).

Orbweaver spiders in the family Araneidae are common in gardens and natural areas and are known for building large, conspicuous webs (Deitsch et al. 2024). While an orbweaver’s appearance alone can invoke fear in people when encountered (Gerdes et al. 2009), this group is not known to have medically significant bites to humans (Vetter and Isbister 2008). Orbweaver bites are poorly studied, however, given the rarity of such bites and lack of lasting impact when they do occur (Gorham and Rheney 1968, Anderson 1997).

Trichonephila clavata (L. Koch, 1878), known colloquially as the Jorō spider, is an invasive orbweaver first documented in northern Georgia, United States, in 2014 (Hoebeke et al. 2015). Over the last decade, T. clavata populations have grown rapidly (Pemberton 2025) and now encompass a large area in the southeastern United States with disjunct populations in several additional states (Chuang et al. 2023, https://john-deitsch.shinyapps.io/joroshiny/). Trichonephila clavata females are large-bodied and colorful, and make expansive, sturdy webs that often occur at heights low enough for people to encounter, especially on hiking trails, in backyard vegetation, and on structures. Trichonephila clavata populations can become very dense in areas where it has been established for several years; it has also become the most common orbweaver in some of these areas, outnumbering native orbweavers by a factor of 1.5 to 10 (Nelsen et al. 2023). As the T. clavata population spreads and becomes more dense throughout eastern North America, human interactions with this species are likely to increase in frequency, either through accidental encounters or intentional management activities (Chuang et al. 2025).

Similar to other orbweavers, T. clavata is not known to cause medically relevant bites in its native range. However, beyond its increasing density in synanthropic spaces (eg building webs in backyards, on porches, or in outdoor recreational areas), T. clavata has other inherent characteristics that could predispose it to posing a higher risk to humans compared to the average North American orbweaver: (1) it belongs to a group of spiders (previously placed in their own family, Nephilidae, sensu Kuntner et al. 2019) that are among the largest web-building spiders in the world and certainly larger than most orbweavers in the southeastern United States (Chuang et al. 2023), (2) it has fangs large enough and chelicerae strong enough to puncture human skin (AC and DRN, personal experience), and (3) nephilid webs are commensurately large and and have high tensile strength (Kitagawa and Kitayama 1997), allowing them to occasionally ensnare and feed on a wide variety of small vertebrates, including amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals (Nyffeler and Gibbons 2022). Altogether, these characteristics raise questions about the likelihood of humans being bitten by T. clavata and the potency of its venom. Thus, understanding the risks associated with T. clavata encounters is both relevant from a human health perspective and necessary to make informed management recommendations for this invasive species.

Since the current invasion of this species in the southeastern United States is still in a relatively early stage, there are many unknowns regarding T. clavata–human interactions. Aside from anecdotal observations, we know very little about the likelihood of a person being bitten by a T. clavata or how different levels of physical contact with a person might influence spider behavior. There is also a paucity of data regarding the pain associated with a T. clavata bite or the medical signs and symptoms following a bite. As such, our objectives were to determine: (1) T. clavata behavior and the likelihood of a bite under escalating levels of human interaction, and (2) short- and long-term pain and discomfort associated with a T. clavata bite.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

The Southern Adventist University (SAU; Collegedale, TN, USA) Institutional Review Board approved all procedures involving human participants (IRB Tracking Number: 2024-2025-020). All participants were informed of the potential risks and signed an informed consent waiver (see Supplementary Appendix 1) before the experiments. All experiments involved live spiders and were designed and conducted to minimize stress and harm to the spiders during handling. No spiders were visibly injured or killed during these experiments.

Experiment 1: Spider Responses to Escalating Human–Spider Interactions

Field experiments occurred in natural settings, and human-spider interactions were observed in situ. Trichonephila clavata webs were located on a forest trail system on the SAU campus [35.043109, –85.056063 (n = 30); 35.050641, −85.058921 (n = 20)], in the Clemson Experimental Forest [34.749464, −82.884866 (n = 15)], and in different natural areas in Georgia [34.1249866, −83.7209941 (n = 4); 33.9398718, −83.1959331 (n = 4)]. In total, we tested 73 adult female T. clavata from 1 to 10 October 2024, as mature T. clavata males do not construct webs (Miyashita 1993) and are unlikely to pose any risk of biting. Although these spiders can build densely packed webs that interconnect, we only tested individuals in solitary webs to reduce the chances of disturbing the spider before initiating the trial. Once an individual T. clavata was located, the spider was subjected to a series of 5 interactions with increasing physical contact, outlined in Table 1 and illustrated in Fig. 1.

We analyzed the videos to determine the presence of these behaviors and their duration, when possible. The movement duration was measured from the video recordings using frame-by-frame changes in position (videos shot at 30 fps on an iPhone 15 Pro using Apple’s camera app). Because we hypothesized that most, if not all, bites would occur when the spiders experienced the pinching grasp (fifth level of interaction), we positioned small squares of parafilm within reach of their fangs. Parafilm allowed us to confirm fang penetration and observe if and when venom was expressed. In the other interaction levels, biting was assessed directly by the handling researcher (DRN and AC). If a bite occurred, venom use was assessed by the development of signs and symptoms (eg pruritus, erythema, edema).

Experiment 2: Signs and Symptoms Following a Controlled Spider Bite

This experiment aimed to document the range of symptoms human subjects experienced following a confirmed bite by T. clavata. Following IRB approval, the experiment was conducted at the SAU Health Center under medical supervision. The researchers documented the progression of symptoms at specific time intervals (0, 5, 15, and 30 min) immediately post-bite and with a 24-h post-bite follow-up survey (Supplementary Appendix 2) to capture delayed effects and recovery. We used convenience sampling to select 23 participants (Table 3). Our sampling efforts excluded any participants with obvious health issues or comorbidities; we also asked and excluded participants if they had a history of arthropod allergies, were diabetic, or had asthma. Participants were given a $10 Amazon gift card upon completion of the post-bite survey.

Collection and Care of Spiders

Adult female T. clavata (n = 23) were collected from Pine Mountain Trail in Cartersville, Georgia, United States (34.175387, −84.752284) on 3 October 2024. They were transported back to SAU and housed in 473 ml clear plastic, lidded cups until the experiment began. Each spider was only used once; they were not fed before the experiment (which was conducted from 7 October 2024 to 10 October 2024) and were released back to their original location after the experiment.

Fang Measurements

We selected 8 previously euthanized female T. clavata to measure fang length. Each specimen was photographed under a Zeiss Discovery V12 microscope with an Axiocam 305 color R2 camera (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, White Plains, New York, United States) at 10× magnification. Using Zeiss’ ZEN core software (version 3.9.102.03000), we measured the curved length from the tip of the fang to the point where it inserts into the cheliceral base.

Spider Bite Procedure

We evaluated a maximum of 5 participants per day across 5 d, with the oldest participants going last. The oldest participants were reserved for last because some research suggests that older individuals are more likely to have comorbidities and/or take medications that increase the severity or complicate the treatment of envenomation (Feitosa et al. 2015, Shackleford et al. 2015, Spyres et al. 2018). Participants were subjected to a T. clavata bite on the back of the hand, chosen as a plausible surface where a bite would take place, with thinner skin than the palm. In total, we evaluated 23 participants (20 volunteers and 3 investigators). The entire experiment would have been terminated if a single participant required medical treatment beyond over-the-counter medications, as determined by the SAU medical staff who were present throughout the entire study to provide medical oversight and treatment, if necessary, to all participants. Each day, participants arrived at the SAU Health Center at 12:00 PM and were assigned to a researcher who worked as the observer. The observer’s duties consisted of monitoring each participant’s symptoms following the bite for half an hour, and documenting the progression of the symptoms through a survey and photographs of the affected area (see Fig. 2).

Illustration of how Trichonephila clavata were handled A) and the location of the bite on a participant’s hand B). Two puncture marks from spider fangs are visible immediately after the bite C) (0 min post-bite) and progression of symptoms following the bite include swelling and redness at 5 min D), 15 min E), 30 min F), and 1,440 min (ie 1 d) post-bite G).

Prior to the bite, participants were taken to an examination room where the observer, principal investigator (DRN), and a medical staff member were present. The principal investigator reviewed the procedure with each participant and, given DRN’s extensive prior experience working with T. clavata, was the only person handling the spiders and positioning them to bite participants. This helped ensure the safety of both participant and spider, and standardized the procedure. The spider was removed from its container, grasped by the legs and abdomen, and positioned so that the fangs were in contact with the back of the participant’s left hand (see Fig. 2). The principal investigator and the observer verified that the spider’s fangs visibly penetrated the skin. While we visually observed fang penetration, we could not confirm that venom was voluntarily released. Spiders were often hesitant to bite; therefore, mild pressure was applied to the abdomen to encourage a bite. Once fang penetration was verified, the principal investigator released the spider and allowed it to remain attached for a maximum of 10 s or until it voluntarily released the bite. The principal investigator gently recaptured the spider and replaced it in its container. Participants only received one confirmed bite, no matter the bite’s duration or the development of subsequent symptoms.

Data Collection

Both the observer and participant evaluated the physical response, including local (eg hemorrhage and edema), systemic (eg vertigo and nausea), visible (eg erythema), and invisible (eg algia) signs and symptoms (Table 4). Because arthropod envenomation has been associated with anxiety (Hoover and Fortenberry 2004, Yildiz et al. 2006), we also asked about this symptom (Supplementary Appendix 2). Symptoms were assessed for presence/absence, and subjects were asked to rate their pain using the Wong–Baker FACES pain rating scale (0 to 10) (Wong and Baker 1988). Additional data points included the time to bite from first skin contact, the duration of the bite, and images of the bite at each time point. We measured the size of each subject’s hand and used this information to quantify the area of edema and erythema at each time point from the photos using ImageJ software (NIH 1.54 g). Lastly, participants completed a follow-up symptom survey 24 h after the bite (Supplementary Appendix 2) and submitted a final picture of their hand to record any delayed or ongoing effects.

Statistical Methods

For both experiments, we used repeated measures data collection; results were considered significant at α = 0.05. Concerning experiment 1 (escalating interactions with T. clavata), we constructed generalized linear mixed models to test for the main effect of the increasing human interactions (ordinal independent variable) on the presence/absence of each behavior (dependent variable). We specified the random effects as the intercept and the grouping variable as the ID of the spider being observed. Despite the simplicity of our models, we chose to run the more sophisticated GLMM because the independent variable often had more than 2 ordinal levels, and the observations were repeated measures; thus, we could use a single test to compare across all levels simultaneously, reducing the risk of a family-wise error.

For experiment 2 (signs and symptoms following a bite), we also constructed simple mixed models (GLMM and LMM) as described above. While many factors may contribute to whether symptoms develop, which symptoms develop, and the severity of the symptoms following a bite from a venomous organism see (Boyer 2015) and (Corbit 2015), we chose to keep our statistical analysis simple for several reasons. First, a moderate sample size (n = 23) is likely to detect only the strongest effects, and our data do not support analysis with more complex models. Second, because of the risks associated with envenomation with a poorly described venom, we aimed to document the range of symptoms someone may experience while minimizing risk (ie participants were primarily young and healthy with no known comorbidities). Finally, we believe that the most important factor contributing to the development of signs and symptoms in our system is whether the subject received a bite with venom versus a “dry” bite. Taken together, we believe our statistical analyses allow us to attribute the symptoms observed as caused by the bite, and we therefore drew conclusions about the symptoms a healthy individual may experience following a bite.

All statistical tests were conducted in R (R Core Team 2021) using the lme4 package for GLMMs and LMMs (Bates et al. 2015) and ggplot2 (Wickham 2016) for the visualizations. We report the odds ratio as a measure of effect size and 95% confidence intervals as a measure of error.

Results

Experiment 1: Spider Responses to Escalating Human–Spider Interactions

T. clavata responded to the treatments using a variety of distinct behaviors: move, drop, remain motionless, regurgitate, bite, and envenomate (for definitions, see Table 2). Although remaining motionless and envenomation were observed, remaining motionless occurred only 12 times across all levels [Web = 0, Abdomen = 3, Unrestrained skin contact (Skin) = 3, and Cupped hold (Cupped) = 6], and envenomation was observed twice (Fig. 3B, F). Because of the rarity of these behaviors, they were excluded from subsequent analyses and are not discussed further.

Percent of Trichonephila clavata (N = 73) that performed each behavior when interacting with a human in its own web during a field study conducted in Georgia, South Carolina, and Tennessee in fall 2024. Treatment levels vary across A to F because certain treatments preclude the ability of an individual to engage in the behavior: for example, a spider being restrained within a closed, cupped hand or pinched between the fingers could not drop.

Move

Movement was the most common behavior across all levels of interaction. Out of all T. clavata evaluated (N = 73), 88% moved when their web was initially touched, 96% when their abdomen was touched, 84% when they were unrestrained and in contact with skin, and 86% moved while they were cupped between 2 hands (Fig. 3A). Our GLMM comparing movement across all interaction levels found a significant effect of treatment on the probability a spider would move, χ2(3) = 8.40, P = 0.038. Our model suggested that 11.7% of the variance observed was explained by treatment, while the combination of treatment and individual explained 45.5% of the variance. Overall, individuals had significantly greater odds of moving when unrestrained on skin (P = 0.034, OR = 6.0, 95% CI = 1.1 to 31.9) than when they remained within their web. While not statistically significant, our results may suggest that spiders had greater odds of moving when their abdomen was touched than when their web was touched (P = 0.068, OR = 3.8, 95% CI = 0.91 to 16.4). However, we did not detect a difference in movement when the spider was cupped between hands compared to when they were touched on the abdomen (P = 0.79, OR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.30 to 2.5). There was no significant relationship between the duration spent moving and the treatment level (web vs abdomen): F(1, 66.1) = 1.93, P = 0.169, treatment (R2 = 0.02), and combined effect of treatment and individual (R2 = 0.10).

Drop

We observed dropping behavior at least once across all treatment levels (Fig. 3C): Web (occurred in 7% of observations), Abdomen (25%), and Open (88%). Our GLMM accounted for a large proportion of the variance, with the treatment having an R2 = 0.53 and treatment and ID combined R2 = 0.58. Our omnibus test found a significant effect of treatment: χ2(2) = 29.5, P < 0.001. Spiders had significantly greater odds of dropping when they were in unrestrained contact with skin compared to when their web was touched (P < 0.001, OR = 128.8, 95% CI = 21.8 to 760.9) or when their abdomen was touched (P = 0.006, OR = 4.7, 95% CI = 1.6–14.1).

Regurgitate

We observed at least one instance of regurgitation in both the cupped hand (1%) and pinched grasp (41%) treatments (Fig. 3D). Our GLMM found that treatment accounted for a moderate to large proportion of the variance (R2 = 0.53), while treatment and individual had a small increase (R2 = 0.58). Our fixed effects omnibus test revealed a significant effect of treatment type on the probability of spider regurgitation, χ2(1) = 13.7, P < 0.001. Pinched spiders had significantly (P < 0.001, OR= 56.74, 95% CI = 6.7 to 480.8) greater odds of regurgitating than when restrained between cupped hands.

Bite

Our GLMM failed to converge because of the rarity with which we observed biting across all interaction levels, except when the spider was pinched (Fig. 3E). Therefore, in subsequent analyses, we excluded treatments where the behavior was never observed (Web and Abdomen) and ran McNemar’s tests comparing Pinched (77%) with unrestrained skin contact (1%) and held between cupped hands (1%), respectively. Our McNemar’s test (χ2(1) = 54.018, P < 0.0001) found that the odds of observing a bite were 256.5 times greater when pinched than the odds of observing a bite when the spiders were restrained in between cupped hands or when unrestrained but in contact with skin.

Experiment 2: Signs and Symptoms Following a Controlled Spider Bite

Hemorrhage (Bleeding)

Of the 23 participants bitten during the study, hemorrhage (ie any sign of bright red blood) occurred in only 39.1% of people immediately following the bite (Fig. 4A). Signs of hemorrhage had mostly resolved by 5 min post-bite, with only 1 participant (4.3%) still exhibiting signs of hemorrhage at the 5 min check. No participants displayed any signs of hemorrhage after 5 min. Likewise, only 3 (13.0%) participants reported experiencing paresthesia around the bite site within the 5 min following the bite (Fig. 4B). By 15 min and continuing to 30 min, only 1 participant (4.3%) was still experiencing paresthesia, with no reports by 24 h. No participants needed medical assistance during the 30 min of observation post-bite, and none reported taking medication within the 24 h follow-up. Because hemorrhage and paresthesia were reported infrequently across all time points, we excluded these symptoms from further statistical analyses.

Percent of participants (N = 23) that reported experiencing each sign and symptom after a Trichonephila clavata bite on the back of the hand during the controlled study. Hemorrhage = bleeding, paresthesia = tingling, dysesthesia = unpleasant sensation, pruritus = itchiness, erythema = redness, and edema = swelling.

Our questionnaire asked participants to report on their emotional state, including feelings of anxiety. Immediately following the bite, participants reported an average anxiety score of 0.7 (median: 0, range: 0 to 4), on a 10-point scale with zero being perfectly calm and 10 being extremely anxious. Only 8 of the 20 participants questioned reported an anxiety score greater than zero. Only 2 participants continued to report anxiety by 5 min, and none thereafter. We also gave participants the opportunity to provide additional comments about their experience; these comments, unaltered, are provided in Supplementary Appendix 3.

Dysesthesia (Unpleasant Sensation)

The majority of participants (60.9%) reported experiencing some dysesthesia (greater than 0) immediately following the bite (Fig. 4C), with a median pain score of 1 (range: 0 to 3, Fig. 5). The number of participants who continued to report dysesthesia decreased to 39.1% by 5 min (median: 0, range: 0 to 4), to 21.7% by 15 min (median: 0, range: 0 to 4), to 17.4% by 30 min (median: 0, range: 0 to 4), and 4.3% by 24 h (median: 0, range: 0 to 1). The overall model was significant (Table 5), with the time interval significantly predicting whether a participant would report experiencing dysesthesia. Dysesthesia was reported significantly less often at 15 min, 30 min, and 1,440 min compared to immediately following the bite (Table 6). There was no significant difference in the proportion of participants who reported dysesthesia at 5 min compared to immediately after the bite.

Pruritus (Itchiness)

Participants commonly reported experiencing pruritus, with 87.0% reporting it immediately following the bite, 73.9% reporting it at 5 and 15 min, 30.4% reporting it at 30 min, and 4.3% at 1,440 min (Fig. 4D). Our overall GLMM was significant (Table 5). We found that participants were significantly less likely to report pruritus after 30 min and 1,440 min compared to immediately following the bite (Table 6). However, there was no significant difference between the odds that a participant would report experiencing pruritus at 5 min or 15 min compared to immediately following the bite.

Erythema (Redness)

Erythema was the most commonly observed symptom. The majority of participants (87.0%) reported it immediately post-bite. This increased to 95.7% at 5 min, decreased to 87.0% at 15 min, remained at 87.0% at 30 min, and was almost absent (4.3%) at 1,440 min (Fig. 4E). Both our GLMM and LMM were significant, and the time interval explained a moderate amount of the variance (Tables 5 and 7). Participants were significantly less likely to report erythema after 1,440 min than immediately following the bite (Table 6). Although there was an increase in the number of participants who reported erythema after 5 min, none of the other time intervals significantly differed from immediately following the bite. The size of erythema (median: 0.47 cm2, range: 0–12.7 cm2) peaked at 5 min post-bite and decreased consistently afterward (Fig. 6). There was a significant difference between the area of skin showing erythema at 5 (P < 0.001), 15 (P < 0.001), and 30 (P = 0.028) min compared with immediately post-bite. By 1,440 min after the bite, the area affected was not significantly different from immediate post-bite (P = 0.28).

Edema (Swelling)

Edema was commonly observed and developed quickly following the bite, with 69.6% of participants reporting it immediately, 73.9% at 5 min, 56.5% at 15 min, 47.8% at 30 min, and only 4.3% still reporting it at 1,440 min (Fig. 4F). Our GLMM and LMM were significant, with the time interval explaining a moderate amount of the variance (Tables 5 and 7). The presence/absence of edema and the area of skin affected followed the same trend as erythema: rapid initial onset, an increase in occurrence and extent by 5 min, and a progressive reduction thereafter. By 30 min post-bite, the edema was resolving (Table 6) and almost completely resolved by 1,440 min compared to immediately post-bite. This trend was also evident in the area of skin affected. The size of the edema (median: 0.036 cm2, range: 0–1.0 cm2) peaked at 5 min post-bite and decreased consistently afterward (Fig. 6). Compared to immediately post-bite, a significantly larger area of skin was affected at 5 min (P < 0.001) and 15 min (P = 0.003). By 30 min (P = 0.057) and 1,440 min (P = 0.22), we could no longer detect a significant difference compared with immediately post-bite.

Discussion

Our experiment of escalating human-spider interaction intensity revealed that T. clavata is unlikely to bite. Escape behaviors like running and dropping were the most common T. clavata reactions to being handled, and biting was largely a response to physical restraint of the spider. In the second experiment, when bites were forced to occur in a controlled environment, recipients of bites reported low levels of pain, and other symptoms associated with the bite were highly localized and transient. Overall, we found no support for the concern that interactions with T. clavata would result in a medically significant bite to otherwise healthy adults.

Bites from T. clavata are unlikely, largely due to the nature of how humans would encounter the spider. This spider and its closest relatives are known for producing large orb webs with a golden tinge, frequently located more than 2 m above ground level (Kuntner et al. 2008). The size, conspicuousness, and height of these webs should make the spider easy for people to notice. In fact, the high visibility of this spider has contributed to remarkably frequent reports of its presence on the community science platform iNaturalist (Deitsch et al. 2024). Despite this, webs built on or around buildings and hiking trails increase the likelihood of a physical human-spider interaction. Fortunately, our results strongly suggest that bites will rarely, if ever, occur as a result of these interactions. Even when spiders were intentionally handled (eg touched or removed from their web and held), the primary response from the spider was movement, dropping to the ground, and freezing, all of which are used to avoid contact with people. Less than 1% of the human-spider interactions in this study, in which the spider was not pinched, resulted in a bite, highlighting that defensive bites are a last resort of disturbed T. clavata. This is consistent with the generally low risk posed by the majority of spiders (Nelsen et al. 2014, Hauke and Herzig 2017). The manner in which any non-arachnologist will encounter T. clavata all but precludes the opportunity for a bite, unless an individual willfully seeks out prolonged and aggressive contact with the spider.

Human interactions with T. clavata differ markedly from those of spiders with medically important bites in North America (ie Latrodectus and Loxosceles species, also known as widow and recluse spiders, respectively). Members of these genera can be found within and around homes (Guarisco 1999, Vetter 2008, Greene et al. 2009, Vetter et al. 2012), where accidental physical interactions with humans are more likely than with T. clavata, which do not enter structures to construct a web or hunt. Latrodectus and Loxosceles species, and their associated silk structures, are not as large or conspicuous as T. clavata and its large orb web (Rose 2022), which increases the risk of accidental encounters with these medically important spiders. In fact, bites from Latrodectus and Loxosceles species typically occur when people reach into a darkened area or press a spider trapped in clothing against their skin (Clark et al. 1992, Vetter 2008). Despite the far greater potential for bites from Latrodectus and Loxosceles species, these spiders also only bite people defensively (Nelsen et al. 2014), and can coexist in great numbers for many years without incident (Vetter and Barger 2002).

When bites did occur, as intentionally encouraged in our second experiment, the reported levels of pain were low, and associated symptoms were quite mild. Nearly 40% of participants reported no dysesthesia upon being bitten, and reports of pain dropped significantly over time. Pain values rarely exceeded 1 on a 10-point scale, and reports of verified spider bites by multiple species in Australia (Isbister and Gray 2002) and Switzerland (Nentwig et al. 2013) also indicate typically low levels of pain. Exceptions for medically important spiders exist, as in an average score of 6.74 (representing extreme pain) after 24 h from a bite by Lo. reclusa Gertsch and Mulaik, 1940 (Araneae: Sicariidae) (Payne et al. 2014), and between 4.5 and 7.8 (adjusted from a 100-point scale) in response to likely envenomation by Latrodectus hasselti Thorell, 1870 (Araneae: Theridiidae) (Ellis et al. 2005). Bites from Latrodectus spp. have been reported to result in pain reaching “intense,” “burning,” and “severe” levels, both locally and systemically (Caruso et al. 2021, Khakh et al. 2024). Confirmed bites from white-tailed spiders (Lampona spp.) resulted in severe pain in roughly a quarter of described cases (Isbister and Gray 2003). The consistently low levels of pain reported from bites by T. clavata highlight the disconnect between our perceptions of their size and the actual effects of their venom on humans. Although T. clavata can be quite large for a spider (nearly 3 cm body length, Kuntner et al. 2019), their fangs are small (median length: 1.51 mm, range: 1.23 to 1.60 mm, n = 8). Penetration into the hypodermis is unlikely, as the combined thickness of the hand’s dorsal dermis and epidermis is 2.3 ± 0.99 mm (Oltulu et al. 2018), and our measurement of fang length along its curvature represents a conservative measure of absolute penetration depth. The fangs are clearly better suited to smaller prey species, where the injected venom could reach muscular, neural, or circulatory tissues to aid in capture and digestion.

Low levels of pain were reported along with localized pruritus, erythema, and edema, all likely in response to the injection of venom within the skin, and comparable to symptoms reported elsewhere for other spiders (Isbister and Gray 2002, 2003; Nentwig et al. 2013). We cannot, however, be sure that venom was injected, as spiders may have delivered a “dry bite” (sensu Nelsen et al. 2014) that only consisted of the fangs penetrating the skin without injecting venom. Importantly, no severe reactions were observed, no medical intervention was required, and symptoms were largely resolved within 24 h. Specifically, only a single participant reported any symptoms beyond 30 min after being bitten. Although previous characterization of T. clavata’s venom isolated at least 1 neurotoxin that blocks glutamate receptors in vertebrates (Kawai et al. 1982, Nakajima 2006), our participants exhibited only mild, short-term reactions to being bitten. Participants actually reported being more uncomfortable before the bite than afterwards, due to anxiety in anticipation of the bite. Furthermore, multiple participants indicated they would have forgotten about the bite within an hour, and some even expressed interest in being bitten again (see Supplementary Appendix 3). Two of us (AC and DN) have been bitten in the field before this study and found the experience far less notable than a bee sting, which typically hurts more, becomes inflamed, and persists much longer.

Symptoms were never observed to spread beyond the hand that was bitten, and the largest extent of erythema was less than 13 cm2. There is, thus, low concern for the impact of a bite from T. clavata, in contrast to potential systemic reactions to envenomation by widows (latrodectism, Khakh et al. 2024) and recluses (loxoscelism, Vetter and Isbister 2008). The primary function of spider venom is to incapacitate prey, which are typically arthropods (Foelix 2011), although some spiders (including T. clavata) certainly are capable of capturing, subduing, and feeding on vertebrates (Nyffeler and Gibbons 2022). The more damaging venom from spiders of medical importance likely evolved in a defensive role as part of a suite of behaviors providing protection from vertebrate predators (Vetter 1980, Nelsen et al. 2014).

We have demonstrated the first empirical evidence for the low likelihood and relative harmlessness of a bite from T. clavata. This implies minimal risk to the increasing number of people living in areas with T. clavata as their invasive range in North America continues to spread and populations become denser. Risks posed to mammalian pets (eg cats and dogs) are likely even lower, due to their low probability of contact with the spider and protective layer of fur. Should individuals wish to minimize contact with T. clavata that have built webs in their vicinity and avoid nontarget impacts to native arthropods associated with chemical management, we suggest physical removal (Chuang et al. 2025).

In what may be the first systematic study of spider bites to humans in a controlled environment, we showed how pain and associated symptoms of the bite are fleeting and negligible. We hope these results will have a calming effect on distorted media reporting (see Chuang et al. 2023) of the impacts this species may have as it continues to spread. Although their broader impacts on the ecosystems they have invaded remain to be fully documented, the direct impacts of T. clavata on the humans it encounters are highly unlikely to result in a bite. Importantly, we now have empirical support for previously assumed claims that the spider’s bite is likely harmless (eg Anderson 2025). Although arachnophobia is common and strongly impacts those affected (Gerdes et al. 2009, Correia and Mammola 2024), it is clear that this spider does not deserve a fearsome reputation from the likelihood or severity of its bite. T. clavata should, thus, be considered among the ranks of the vast majority of spiders for which outsized concern about their bite is not warranted (Vetter and Isbister 2008).
