Alterations in self-related brain networks in autism spectrum disorder: a systematic review of functional and structural magnetic resonance imaging studies
Authors: Liwei Zhou, Junrong Han, Hang Wu, Han Bao, Jiaying Wang, Pengmin Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) involves alterations in social communication and restricted, repetitive behaviors. Emerging evidence highlights atypical self-awareness as a key factor in ASD-related social impairments. However, the neural mechanisms underlying differences in self-processing remain fragmented. This systematic review synthesizes findings from 49 functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) studies (2000–2025) to explore self-related brain networks in ASD, based on the hierarchical self-model comprising interoceptive, exteroceptive, and mental levels. Across all three levels, consistent atypicalities were observed in ASD. The interoceptive level (insula, thalamus) showed altered functional connectivity (FC) and gray matter density, associated with atypical bodily and affective self-awareness. The exteroceptive level, which includes the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), temporoparietal junction (TPJ), and premotor cortex (PMC), exhibited reduced long-range FC and local coherence, potentially reflecting atypical self–other differentiation and communication. The mental level, involving the anterior and posterior cingulate cortices (ACC and PCC), revealed decreased FC and interhemispheric coherence, implicating atypical reflective self-processing. Disrupted cross-level interactions further suggest a breakdown in hierarchical self-integration. These findings emphasize the importance of self-related network alterations in ASD and support their inclusion in neurocognitive models of autism.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by social communication challenges and restricted, repetitive behaviors (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). It is a highly prevalent condition, with recent US data indicating that the prevalence among 8-year-old children exceeds 3% (Anderer, 2025). Despite extensive research, a unified etiological explanation remains unclear (Parellada et al., 2023). A clue lies in the age at which autism develops. The emergence of ASD core symptoms typically occurs around 24 months (Elder et al., 2017). Consistent with this, toddlers with ASD (12–36 months) demonstrate early self-referential differences, including difficulty with the use of the pronoun "I" (Finnegan et al., 2021), impaired recognition of themselves in photographs (Campbell et al., 2017), and a significantly decreased rate of responding to their own name (Zhang et al., 2018). Notably, this period (18–24 months) coincides with the key developmental window for the emergence of self-awareness (Lewis and Ramsay, 2004). Given that a stable sense of self is fundamental to social communication, this evidence strongly suggests that the atypical development of neural circuits for self-awareness may be a core etiological mechanism in ASD.

To rigorously test this hypothesis, a neurobiological framework of the self is required. A recent meta-analysis offers a compelling neurobiological framework for self-related brain network, conceptualizing the self as a multi-level, nested hierarchy. This model delineates three nested neural subsystems, including the interoceptive self, the exteroceptive self, and the mental self (Qin et al., 2020). The first, the interoceptive self, involves the perception and integration of internal physiological states (e.g. heartbeat, hunger) and focuses primarily on core regions such as the insula and thalamus, forming the neural basis for bodily self-awareness. The second level, the exteroceptive self, is responsible for integrating multisensory information (visual, tactile, proprioceptive) to map the body within the external environment and establish the physical boundary between self and other. This level acts as a bridge between the self and the environment, and relies on regions such as the temporoparietal junction (TPJ), the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), and premotor cortex (PMC). Finally, the third level, the mental self, extends self-awareness to abstract concepts, personal attributes, and social roles, and converts the external stimuli into a part of the self without direct bodily contact (e.g. one’s name, possessions, social identity). This highest level engages core hubs of the default mode network (DMN), including the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC). This hierarchical framework is ideal for studying ASD because it allows us to hypothesize how foundational atypicalities, from the interoceptive to the mental self, explain the social communication challenges of ASD.

A growing body of evidence indicates that individuals with ASD exhibit atypicalities across all three levels of self-processing. At the foundational interoceptive level, for example, those with ASD exhibit difficulties in identifying internal bodily states (Garfinkel et al., 2016), and neuroimaging has revealed interoceptive-self atypicalities in the structure and function of the insula (Uddin and Menon, 2009). Similar disruptions are observed at the exteroceptive level, where behavioral challenges manifest in tasks of mirror self-recognition and body ownership (Carmody and Lewis, 2012; Cascio et al., 2012), alongside reports of atypical function in relevant neural nodes such as TPJ and mPFC (Lombardo et al., 2010; Hoffmann et al., 2016). These challenges may extend to the highest mental-self level in ASD. Difficulties in social communication are strongly linked to aberrant activity within the DMN (Murdaugh et al., 2012).

Existing research has identified atypicalities in ASD corresponding to all three levels of the self. However, these findings are often fragmented, with research typically siloed by subfield and neuroimaging modality. A comprehensive synthesis that examines the relationships both within and between these hierarchical systems in ASD is currently lacking. Therefore, the present review aims to review neuroimaging evidence from functional and structural studies to characterize dysfunction within each of the three self-processing levels. Subsequently, we will examine the emerging evidence for atypical interactions and connectivity between these hierarchical levels. By integrating these findings, this review seeks to offer a more cohesive neurobiological account of self-related atypicalities in ASD and propose a structured framework for guiding future research.

Method

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines (Page et al., 2021). The search strategy was theoretically driven, designed to identify human neuroimaging studies on the neural basis of self-related processing in ASD, guided explicitly by the three-level hierarchical model of self (Qin et al., 2020) detailed in the Introduction.

Search strategy and selection criteria

We performed a systematic search of the PubMed and Web of Science databases, restricted to peer-reviewed articles published in English between 1 January 2000 and 16 October 2025. The search query was constructed using four categories of keywords, combined using the “AND” operator to ensure high specificity:

Category 1 (Population): (Autism OR “Autism Spectrum Disorder” OR ASD OR Asperger). Category 2 (Modality): (fMRI OR “functional MRI” OR “resting-state” OR “rs-fMRI” OR “functional connectivity” OR sMRI OR “structural MRI” OR VBM OR “voxel-based morphometry” OR DTI OR “diffusion tensor imaging”). Category 3 (Self-concept): (“self-related” OR “self-referential” OR “self-processing” OR “self-awareness” OR “self-representation” OR introspection OR interoception OR “body ownership” OR mentalizing OR “default mode network” OR DMN OR “autobiographical memory” OR “self-bias” OR “self-other distinction”). We included the term DMN because its core midline structures, specifically the ACC and PCC, serve as the primary neural substrates for the "Mental Self" in the self. Category 4 (A Priori Regions of Interest—ROIs): (insula OR thalamus OR mPFC OR “medial prefrontal cortex” OR TPJ OR “temporoparietal junction” OR “premotor cortex” OR PCC OR “posterior cingulate” OR ACC OR “anterior cingulate”).

This review aims to synthesize evidence specifically within the neurobiological framework of three levels of self-processing. Therefore, our search was intentionally constrained to studies that explicitly mentioned both the relevant self-concepts (Category 3) and the core neural nodes of the self-model (Category 4). To ensure the synthesized evidence strictly aligns with the three-level hierarchical self-model, our search strategy prioritized studies that explicitly examined the a priori ROIs (Category 4) associated with interoceptive, exteroceptive, and mental processing. This approach ensures high anatomical specificity relevant to the theoretical framework.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies identified in the search were screened for eligibility based on the following criteria:

Inclusion criteria: (1) Publication type: original, peer-reviewed research articles. (2) Language: published in English. (3) Participants (population): must include a clinical sample diagnosed with ASD based on established diagnostic criteria [e.g. Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5)] and preferably confirmed using standardized instruments [e.g. Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS)]. (4) Participants (comparator): must include a non-ASD control group [e.g. typically developing (TD), or neurotypical (NT)]. (5) Modality: must employ human structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) or functional MRI (fMRI). fMRI studies must include either task-based (e.g. self-referential tasks, interoceptive tasks) fMRI or resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), analyzing functional activation or connectivity. sMRI studies must include analysis of brain structure [e.g. voxel-based morphometry (VBM), surface-based morphometry (SBM), cortical thickness] or structural connectivity [e.g. diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)]. (6) Reporting of Findings: must report specific findings related to at least one of the a priori ROIs defined in Category 4 (insula, mPFC, TPJ, etc.). This includes studies reporting peak coordinates from whole-brain analyses within our ROIs, or studies using explicit ROI-based or seed-based connectivity analyses.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Reviews, meta-analyses, systematic reviews, commentaries, editorials, conference abstracts, book chapters, or single-case studies. (2) Studies involving non-human subjects (i.e. animal models). (3) Studies where ASD-specific findings could not be isolated (e.g. ASD group combined with other neurodevelopmental disorders). (4) Studies focused on treatment or interventions without a baseline (pre-intervention) comparison. (5) Genetic studies that did not also report relevant fMRI or sMRI findings.

Study selection and data extraction

The study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). After automatic duplicate removal, all remaining titles and abstracts were independently screened for eligibility by two reviewers (Author A and Author B). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion. If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (Author C) was consulted. The full texts of all potentially eligible articles were then retrieved and assessed in detail against the full inclusion/exclusion criteria by the same two reviewers.

Data from all included studies (N = 49) were extracted by one reviewer (Author A) and subsequently verified by the second reviewer (Author B) to ensure accuracy. The following information was extracted: bibliographic details (e.g. first author, year of publication); sample characteristics (e.g. N_ASD, N_Control, age mean/range, sex distribution); neuroimaging modality (e.g. fMRI, rs-fMRI, sMRI, DTI); task/paradigm (e.g. self-referential trait judgment, interoceptive awareness task, resting-state); analysis methods (e.g. ROI-based, functional connectivity, VBM); and key findings: (e.g. main group differences, specific brain regions/networks identified, and correlations with clinical/behavioral scores).

Resting-state fMRI studies reveal a developmentally dynamic pattern of atypical connectivity. At 6 weeks, infants at high likelihood for ASD display hyperconnectivity between the right insula and thalamus (Tsang et al., 2024). Furthermore, within the high-likelihood group at 9 months, the infants with relatively stronger insula–thalamus connectivity showed more severe sensory over-responsivity (Wagner et al., 2023). Additionally, a large-scale study covering a broad age range of children to young adults found evidence for hyperconnectivity between the thalamus and insula in ASD (Tomasi and Volkow, 2019). However, another study reported significant hypoconnectivity between the anterior insula and right thalamus in adolescents with ASD, finding that weaker connectivity correlated with more severe social communication difficulties (Francis et al., 2019).

Findings on local functional organization, measured by regional homogeneity (ReHo), are less consistent but still point to atypical processing. ReHo is a data-driven approach of rs-fMRI, reflecting synchronization activities between a voxel and its nearest neighbor, where higher ReHo suggests more consistent information processing within a functional cluster (Zang et al., 2004). In ASD patients, lower ReHo in the insula but higher ReHo in the thalamus were found (Paakki et al., 2010), whereas a large-scale analysis of the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) dataset reported decreased ReHo in both regions (Di Martino et al., 2014). Despite these inconsistencies, all these previous results collectively point to altered local synchronization within core interoceptive hubs.

Task-based fMRI studies provide further insights into these interoceptive differences. For example, one study found that when adults with ASD performed an interoceptive task (focusing on their breathing), they exhibited hyperconnectivity between the insula and ACC compared to controls (Barttfeld et al., 2012). While individuals with ASD showed no differences in the primary perception of heartbeats (i.e. accuracy in a heartbeat counting task), the activation of their left posterior insula was abnormally correlated with their self-reported social difficulties (Failla et al., 2020). This suggests that the core differences may lie not in the initial perception of bodily signals, but in the subsequent neural processing that integrates these signals into a meaningful social–emotional context.

Structural MRI studies offer a potential anatomical basis for this functional dysregulation, revealing a clear structural imbalance between the insula and thalamus. A large-scale study reported reduced gray matter density (GMD) in the bilateral insula in ASD, which correlated with repetitive, rigid, and stereotyped behaviors and abnormal sensory behaviors (Mei et al., 2020). In contrast, increased GMD has been found in the bilateral thalami across children, adolescents, and adults with ASD (Wang et al., 2025). These sMRI studies may suggest that a less developed insula is unable to integrate bodily signals, while an enlarged thalamus could reflect an overactive sensory system. Together, these findings point to an unstable interoceptive foundation for the self. This imbalance consists of an overactive sensory "input" system (enlarged thalamus) paired with an underdeveloped integration "hub" (reduced insula). This system-level mismatch fundamentally compromises the brain’s ability to accurately process and make sense of its own internal bodily signals.

The exteroceptive self: a blurred boundary

The exteroceptive self, which bridges internal bodily states and the external world, is responsible for delineating the physical boundary between self and other. This level is supported by the TPJ, mPFC, and PMC, building upon the foundation of the insula. In ASD, consistent evidence points to the disintegration of this network, resulting in a weak self and consequently a “blurred” boundary between self and other. This blurring may stem from the unstable interoceptive foundation; without a coherent sense of the bodily self, the brain struggles to establish a clear distinction between internal experiences and external stimuli. Two studies report reduced FC between the mPFC and the TPJ in ASD (Hoffmann et al., 2016; Joshi et al., 2017). The hypoconnectivity between mPFC and TPJ is considered a core neural atypicality, underlying the brain’s intrinsic capacity for both self-referential processing and inferring the mental states of others (Lombardo et al., 2010). The dysfunction within this network appears particularly centered on the TPJ. This is supported by reports of reduced interhemispheric coupling between the left and right TPJ (Hoffmann et al., 2016) and decreased temporal variability in connectivity within the right TPJ, suggesting a more rigid functional organization in ASD (Bravo Balsa et al., 2024). At a more local level, atypical regional activity also points to the disruption of exteroceptive self. This includes findings of reduced ReHo in the mPFC (Kozhemiako et al., 2020) and reduced FC between sub-regions of the PMC (Nebel et al., 2014).

Task-based fMRI studies reveal atypical activation patterns when probing the exteroceptive self. During tasks requiring the inference of others' intentions, individuals with ASD show reduced FC between mPFC and TPJ (Cole et al., 2019). During a task requiring participants to evaluate their own emotional responses to others' facial expressions, adults with ASD exhibited significantly reduced activation in the ventral mPFC compared to controls, suggesting difficulties in intuitive emotional self-processing (Schulte-Rüther et al., 2011). A particularly insightful finding comes from a task where participants identified emotions from their own face. Individuals with ASD showed greater activation in the dorsal mPFC, a region typically associated with monitoring others (Hyatt et al., 2020). Conversely, typically developing (TD) individuals activated the ventral mPFC, a region central to self-related emotion (Hyatt et al., 2020). This suggests that individuals with ASD may apply an effortful, analytical strategy (a “top-down” cognitive process) to a task that is typically automatic and self-reflective (a “bottom-up” emotional process) in TD individuals. This interpretation is strongly supported by foundational evidence from Lombardo et al. (2010). In that study, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) in neurotypical individuals responded specifically to self-referential judgments. The vmPFC in ASD, however, failed to show this specialization, responding equally to both self and other. During a continuous finger-tapping task, children with ASD had significantly lower activation in the PMC and the cerebellum compared to TD individuals (Mostofsky et al., 2009). The lower activation in children with ASD not only hinders motor execution but also may limit their ability to use external sensory feedback to continuously refine motor commands and update their sense of bodily self.

Structural findings, though complex and age-dependent, point to an altered developmental timeline for this network. Studies have reported larger gray matter volume (GMV) in the mPFC in adolescents with ASD compared to TD (Cheng et al., 2011) and reduced gray–white matter contrast (GWC) in this region in ASD, particularly in childhood (Mann et al., 2018). This GWC reduction suggests a more blurred boundary between gray and white matter, a key index of atypical cortical maturation. Developmental sMRI studies have provided a detailed map of these atypical timelines. For instance, the developmental trajectories of these regions in ASD were fundamentally different from controls. For the PMC, while controls showed a U-shaped pattern of GMV development, the ASD group showed only a linear decrease with age (Greimel et al., 2013). This atypical maturation is also reflected in reduced cortical thickness in the PMC of adults with ASD (Zielinski et al., 2014). For the TPJ, both groups showed the typical inverted U-shaped developmental curve. However, in ASD this entire curve was shifted to the left along the age axis. This shift indicates an accelerated maturation process, where regional GMV reaches its peak at an earlier age in ASD compared to TD (Greimel et al., 2013). This evidence suggests that the neural system supporting the exteroceptive self fails to mature typically in ASD. This atypical maturation process results in a persistently blurred boundary between one’s own experience and the external social world.

A hallmark finding in ASD neuroimaging is significantly reduced FC between the ACC and the PCC (Cherkassky et al., 2006; Assaf et al., 2010; Washington et al., 2014). This hypoconnectivity might result in a fundamental failure of integration within the mental self network. Specifically, this failure disrupts the crucial link between the role of ACC in evaluating the personal and emotional relevance of immediate events (Northoff et al., 2011; Gazzaniga et al., 2014) and the role of PCC in integrating experiences with autobiographical memory (Cavanna and Trimble, 2006). As a result, this dysfunction may lead to an incoherent sense of autobiographical self, where personal experiences are not woven into a coherent and emotionally resonant life story. This neural-level explanation aligns with behavioral reports of disjointed autobiographical memory in ASD (Coutelle et al., 2020).

This long-range under-connectivity is coupled with evidence of atypical local processing. For example, local hyperconnectivity has been reported within the ACC in youths with ASD (Jann et al., 2015). Findings on local processing are complex, as other studies reported increased ReHo in the PCC during eyes-closed states but decreased ReHo in both the ACC and PCC during eyes-open states in the ASD group (Nair et al., 2018). These findings suggest that the processing pattern of the mental self, characterized by failed global integration and rigid local processing, is the likely neural mechanism underlying the restricted and repetitive patterns of thought and behavior characteristic of ASD.

Task-based studies reveal multiple dysfunctions of the mental self network. Adults with ASD show reduced activation in the PCC while evaluating the photogenicity (i.e. how photogenic they appeared) of their own face in a photo (Morita et al., 2012). During film viewing, individuals with ASD show significantly lower inter-subject synchronization in the ACC and PCC. This finding indicates that their neural processing of the social world is atypical (Salmi et al., 2013). Furthermore, individuals with ASD exhibited hyperactivation in the PCC when watching their own controlled actions from an external observer’s view (Hur et al., 2025), possibly reflecting the increased neural effort required to integrate the feeling of "self-action" with an other’s perspective.

Structural studies on the cingulate cortex in ASD have been inconsistent. Although one early study using positron emission tomography (PET) and MRI did report a significant volume reduction in the right ACC of ASD (Haznedar et al., 2000), a surface-based morphometry (SBM) study found a significant increase in cortical thickness in bilateral ACC and the right PCC in individuals with ASD (Ecker et al., 2022). This inconsistency may stem from sample heterogeneity, age differences, and technology. Evidence from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) highlights a developmental component to white matter atypicalities. The cingulum bundle is a long-distance joint fiber bundle that runs deep along the cingulate gyrus, directly connecting the front core (ACC/mPFC) and rear core PCC (Gordon et al., 2011). Studies have found reduced fractional anisotropy (FA) in the cingulum bundle in ASD (Catani et al., 2016). This atypicality in the brain’s “wiring” appears to be developmental, with FA values being lower in younger individuals with ASD and this difference diminishing with age, suggesting an immature or delayed developmental process rather than static damage (Ameis et al., 2013). These findings suggest that the structural development of the mental self is atypical, particularly pointing to the delayed maturation of the cingulum bundle as a key structural mechanism for the functional disconnection between the ACC and PCC.

Functional and structural alterations across self-related levels

A coherent self-experience depends on the integration of information across the interoceptive, exteroceptive, and mental levels. While disruptions within each hierarchical level are significant, the most profound pathology in ASD appears to be a systemic failure of integration between these levels. In ASD, this breakdown appears to be 2-fold. From the “bottom-up,” there is a foundational disruption in the processing of sensory and bodily information. Simultaneously, from the “top-down,” the higher-order networks of the mental self may fail to effectively integrate and regulate the atypical input. The “bottom-up” disruption: sensory overload and faulty integration

The foundational “bottom-up” disruption in ASD is not a simple weakness, but rather a core imbalance in the interoceptive system that exacerbates the “unstable foundation,” as described in the section “The interoceptive self: an unstable foundation".. The problem begins with the thalamic sensory gate (Sherman and Guillery, 1996). Instead of properly filtering sensory information, it shows hyperconnectivity with sensorimotor regions (Nair et al., 2013; Woodward et al., 2017; Ayub et al., 2021; Karavallil et al., 2023). Specifically, increased connectivity between the thalamus and the PMC has been directly linked to the severity of social difficulties (Iidaka et al., 2019).

This hyperconnectivity leads to an excessive volume of unfiltered sensory input reaching the cortex, providing a direct neural basis for the sensory hypersensitivity included as a core diagnostic criterion in the DSM-5. This sensory overload may overwhelm the insula. The insula struggles to process this incoming flood of information, in part because it shows weakened connections to higher-level control regions. This is supported by findings of weakened structural covariance between the insula and the prefrontal cortex (Palande et al., 2019) and reduced FC with the PMC (Ebisch et al., 2011; Huang et al., 2018). Consequently, the “isolated” and overwhelmed insula fails to integrate these chaotic bodily signals into a coherent sense of self.

“Top-down” disconnection: a failure of integration

The “top-down” disconnection in ASD reflects a failure of higher-order networks to integrate both internal bodily states and external social information into a coherent mental self. The first aspect of this disconnection is the impaired link between the interoceptive self and the mental self. The FC between the ACC and right insula was significantly reduced and negatively correlated with stereotyped behavior in ASD (Zhou et al., 2016). Expanding on this, the anterior insula also shows reduced connectivity with the PCC and mPFC (Ebisch et al., 2011; Guo et al., 2019).

This top-down failure extends to the highest levels of cognition, where the FC between the PCC and the mPFC was reduced. This specific hypoconnectivity within the mental self is a consistent finding (Assaf et al., 2010; Weng et al., 2010) and is associated with poorer social functioning (Monk et al., 2009). This hypoconnectivity also follows a divergent developmental path; while the connection between PCC and mPFC strengthens with age in typical development, it trends downwards in ASD (Doyle-Thomas et al., 2015). This suggests that the mental self decoupled from the exteroceptive self over time.

Discussion

By synthesizing functional and structural neuroimaging evidence through the hierarchical self-processing model, this review reveals a unifying neuropathology in ASD. The collective evidence points to a core neural atypicality, characterized not only by distinctive function within each level of the self, but also, critically, by reduced systemic integration across these levels. This suggests that the social communication differences that define ASD are deeply rooted in a fundamentally impaired architecture of the self, from the primary sensation of the body to the abstract representation of self.

Disruptions within the self-hierarchy

Atypical self-processing in ASD emerges at the foundational interoceptive level. Structural studies provide a clear basis for the instability of the self function. Individuals with ASD exhibit reduced GMD in the insula, a key integration hub (Mei et al., 2020). At the same time, they show increased GMD in the thalamus (Wang et al., 2025). This “system mismatch” involves an underdeveloped hub trying to process an overactive input system. This fundamentally impacts the brain’s ability to process and understand its own internal bodily signals.

This structural imbalance manifests functionally as a dynamic and non-linear developmental trajectory (Lee et al., 2025). Based on the developmental frameworks of Uddin et al. (2013) and Duan et al. (2025), the seemingly contradictory connectivity patterns in ASD are fundamentally age-dependent. At 6 weeks of age, infants at high likelihood for ASD exhibit thalamus–insula hyperconnectivity (Tsang et al., 2024), which probably represents diffuse “neural noise” that disrupts typical synaptic pruning (Sakai, 2020). By 9 months, although the HL group as a whole begins to transition toward hypoconnectivity—reflecting a decline in early integration capacity—the finding that stronger insula–thalamus connectivity correlates with more severe sensory over-responsivity symptoms suggests that pathologically strong connections may be retained in these infants (Wagner et al., 2023). As development progresses into adolescence, these early disruptions eventually lead to significant hypoconnectivity that is directly associated with social communication challenges (Francis et al., 2019). This non-linear evolution from early “sensory overload” to later “atypical integration” constitutes the core neurodevelopmental mechanism underlying the unstable architecture of the self in ASD.

Variations in the interoceptive self may impact the development of the exteroceptive self in ASD (Loureiro et al., 2024). The core function of the exteroceptive self is to act as a “bridge” between internal states and the external world. It also defines the physical boundary between self and other. However, to define this boundary, the network first needs a clear “self” signal from the interoceptive system to compare against “other” signals. Reduced FC between the mPFC and TPJ (Hoffmann et al., 2016; Joshi et al., 2017) might not just be an atypicality in the exteroceptive system itself. It could also be a downstream consequence of altered signal modulation from the interoceptive system. An important consequence of the exteroceptive self-disorder is the “blurred self-other boundary.” A study by Lombardo et al. (2010) found that in ASD, the vmPFC loses its specific role. This region normally responds to self-related judgments, but in ASD, it responds equally to both self and other. When bottom-up self-perception fails, individuals with ASD appear to adopt an inefficient "compensatory strategy." For instance, Hyatt et al. (2020) found that ASD participants recruited the dorsal mPFC—typically associated with monitoring others—during self-face processing, indicating an effortful "top-down" cognitive strategy for a task that is normally automatic. Finally, the developmental timeline for this network’s structure is also fundamentally atypical in ASD (Greimel et al., 2013). This provides further evidence that the system did not receive the stable input from the interoceptive self to develop typically.

This cascade of dysfunction extends to the highest level: the mental self, where the ACC and PCC integrate to build an abstract, autobiographical identity (Northoff et al., 2024). In ASD, this integration is significantly impaired, primarily evidenced by reduced functional connectivity between these core hubs (Cherkassky et al., 2006; Assaf et al., 2010; Washington et al., 2014). Without a stable "self" signal from the ACC or consistent experience integration from the PCC, the network fails to consolidate a coherent self-representation, aligning with reports of disjointed autobiographical memory in ASD (Coutelle et al., 2020). Furthermore, the loss of global integration may trap the network in rigid local processing loops, manifesting as local hyperconnectivity or atypical activity patterns (Jann et al., 2015; Nair et al., 2018). This offers a potential neural mechanism for the restricted, repetitive thought patterns characteristic of the disorder. Structurally, this functional disconnection is supported by reduced fractional anisotropy in the cingulum bundle (Catani et al., 2016), reflecting a delayed developmental timeline that physically impedes network integration (Ameis et al., 2013).

A systemic failure of cross-level integration

The atypical integration between hierarchical levels of the self manifests as both divergent bottom-up processing and reduced top-down modulation. Specifically, from the bottom-up, a failure of thalamic sensory gating, evidenced by widespread thalamocortical hyper-connectivity, floods the cortex with unfiltered sensory information (Iidaka et al., 2019; Karavallil et al., 2023). This provides a direct neural basis for the sensory overload that is a core clinical feature of ASD. Crucially, this sensory chaos overwhelms the insula. Unlike a neurotypical system where the insula integrates these signals under the guidance of higher-order control regions, the insula in ASD appears structurally and functionally “isolated,” characterized by weakened connectivity with the prefrontal and premotor cortices (Huang et al., 2018; Palande et al., 2019). Unable to filter this input or recruit executive control, the interoceptive foundation of the self remains unstable and reactive.

This bottom-up sensory chaos is then compounded by a “top-down” failure of integration. Higher-order reflective systems appear unable to reach down to modulate this chaotic internal state. This is exemplified by the severed functional link between the ACC (mental self) and the insula (interoceptive self) (Zhou et al., 2016), suggesting that the “mental self” is physiologically decoupled from the “bodily self.” Furthermore, within the mental level itself, the neural circuits show a divergent developmental trajectory. The connectivity between the PCC and mPFC, which strengthens with age in typical development to support coherent self-representation, conversely weakens in ASD (Doyle-Thomas et al., 2015). This model reframes the social difficulties of ASD, not as a primary failure to understand others, but as a secondary consequence of a poorly integrated self. It may provide a compelling explanation for repetitive patterns of behavior as a compensatory strategy to create predictability and regulate a chaotic internal and sensory world.

From sensorimotor atypicality to fragmented self-integration

Recently, the “sensory-first” account has provided a critical theory for understanding the developmental trajectory of ASD (Falck-Ytter and Bussu, 2023). This perspective suggests that early differences in sensory processing are not secondary symptoms, but are the primary cause of subsequent social challenges. Research indicates that infants later diagnosed with ASD exhibit significant sensorimotor atypicalities as early as 6 months, which significantly precedes the manifestation of social difficulties (Robertson and Baron-Cohen, 2017; Falck-Ytter and Bussu, 2023). These early atypicalities primarily manifest as delays in gross motor development (e.g. persistent head lag), reduced visual reception, and generalized hyporesponsiveness to external visual, auditory, and tactile stimuli (Estes et al., 2015).

Physiological evidence shows that neonates later diagnosed with ASD exhibit increased heart rate acceleration (Blackard et al., 2021). By 4 months of age, infants later diagnosed with ASD demonstrate lower respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) during interaction with strangers (McCormick et al., 2018). Furthermore, longitudinal data spanning from infancy to early childhood (1–72 months) suggest that RSA growth in ASD is significantly slower than TD, with the divergence becoming particularly pronounced after 18 months (Sheinkopf et al., 2019). Due to the lack of clear interoceptive signals, the brain may attempt to compensate by strengthening the connectivity between the insula and the thalamus (Tsang et al., 2024), seeking to anchor self-boundaries using external signals. However, the sensory system itself may be characterized by imbalanced excitatory and inhibitory (E–I) signals (Robertson and Baron-Cohen, 2017), and this pathway introduces substantial neural noise. Instability in the sensorimotor system may impede the construction of a stable interoceptive self during early infancy.

Through developmental cascades (Falck-Ytter and Bussu, 2023), an unstable interoceptive self may impact the development of higher-level self. Without a stable internal reference, the brain may exhibit atypical patterns in defining the boundary between “self” and “others,” which is accompanied by decreased connectivity within the exteroceptive self-network (e.g. the mPFC and TPJ) during critical developmental windows of self awareness (Hoffmann et al., 2016; Joshi et al., 2017)). This cascade potentially extends to the mental self, which is responsible for high-level self-representation (the ACC and PCC). Hypoconnectivity between the ACC and PCC is potentially associated with the reduced integration of fragmented self-related signals, limiting the formation of a coherent and unified experience of the self (Cherkassky et al., 2006; Assaf et al., 2010; Washington et al., 2014).

Clinical implications, limitations, and future directions

This hierarchical perspective has significant clinical implications. It offers a potential explanation for restricted and repetitive behaviors as a compensatory strategy to regulate a chaotic internal and sensory world by focusing on predictable stimuli. Therefore, interventions should not only target social–cognitive skills but also bottom-up processes. Therapies that enhance interoceptive awareness and bodily self-regulation, such as mindfulness-based approaches, have already shown promise in altering insula–mPFC connectivity and supporting individuals in managing ASD-related challenges (Pagni et al., 2023).

Several issues regarding the current literature should be mentioned. A limitation of our strict ROI-based search strategy is the potential exclusion of studies focusing on whole-brain network properties (e.g. using independent component analysis) that did not explicitly label findings within our predefined anatomical nodes, despite their relevance to self-processing. Furthermore, most studies are cross-sectional, limiting our understanding of the atypical developmental trajectories that are clearly central to the disorder.

To evaluate the development of the three-level self-model in ASD and its influence on sensory signaling and social communication, future research should prioritize the following directions:

While the “sensory-first” account highlights the primacy of sensory processing differences in ASD, the precise developmental sequence of interoceptive and sensorimotor atypicalities remains unclear. Although research has identified atypical patterns of RSA in neonates later diagnosed with ASD, the longitudinal evolution of early interoceptive patterns and their interaction with sensorimotor systems remain poorly understood (Klein et al., 2025). Future studies should integrate physiological markers—such as heartbeat-evoked potentials (HEPs) and RSA—with neuroimaging to explore the interaction between the stability of the early internal sense of self and sensorimotor processing, potentially revealing the underlying drivers of the E–I imbalance in primary sensory areas.

Conclusion

By organizing diverse neuroimaging findings within a hierarchical model of the self, this review illuminates a central, unifying pathology in ASD: a fragmented self. The evidence converges to show disruptions at every level—from faulty bodily awareness and a blurred self–other boundary to a disconnected narrative identity. Most critically, the failure to integrate information across these levels suggests that the core experience of many individuals with ASD may be one of disconnect between body, environment, and mind. Understanding the neural basis of this fractured self is not only key to deciphering the profound social challenges in ASD but is also essential for guiding the development of novel, targeted interventions that aim to foster a more integrated and coherent sense of self.
