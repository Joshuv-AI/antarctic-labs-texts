Reduced KLF4 expression in endothelial cells triggers pulmonary vascular abnormalities associated with experimental congenital diaphragmatic hernia
Authors: Heleen M. Kool, Petra E. Bürgisser, Gabriela G. Edel, Anne Boerema-de Munck, Marjon Buscop-van Kempen, Panagiotis Liakopoulos, Petros Kolovos, Wilfred F. J. van Ijcken, Robbert J. Rottier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH) is a rare anomaly with an incidence of 1 in 3000 live births and characterized by defective closure of the diaphragm, lung hypoplasia, and pulmonary vascular remodeling. Although several genes associated with the occurrence of CDH, no clear genetic component has been identified. Previously, we showed that CDH patients have vascular abnormalities already early in development, and that perivascular cells are affected in CDH. In the current study, we focused on early abnormalities in the nitrofen-induced CDH mouse model. Transcriptome analysis of FACS-sorted perivascular and endothelial cell populations from lungs of control and experimental mouse CDH revealed a high similarity in GO terms of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between these populations, suggesting a disturbed interaction between endothelial cells and pericytes. The disturbed interaction is the basis for the aberrant development of the pulmonary vasculature in CDH. Furthermore, the RNA sequence data revealed reduced expression of the Kruppel like factor 4 (Klf4) gene in endothelial cells of CDH, which was confirmed with protein expression analysis. Furthermore, we show that KLF4 is an important up stream regulator of genes associated with vascular development and confirm that members of the NOTCH signaling pathway are differentially expressed, indicating that NOTCH signaling is disturbed in CDH. Collectively, our data support the importance of KLF4 in pulmonary angiogenesis and contribute to our previous data that pulmonary vessels in CDH patients are already affected before birth, which in turn may impact therapeutic strategies to reduce pulmonary hypertension associated with CDH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06319-6.

Introduction

Congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH), a rare anomaly with an incidence of 1 in 3000 live births, is characterized by defective closure of the diaphragm with herniation of abdominal organs into the chest cavity, resulting in lung hypoplasia, pulmonary vascular remodeling, and cardiac dysfunction [13, 41, 45, 53, 55]. The diaphragm defect is surgically repaired in the first weeks after birth, but the clinical challenge is the severe cardiopulmonary instability. The associated pulmonary hypertension (PH) is unpredictable regarding the response to current treatment modalities. PH is the primary cause of long-term hospitalization and lifelong morbidity, as well as the major cause of death in newborns with CDH [19, 52, 55]. The pathology of the vascular abnormalities of CDH are characterized by extensive thickening of the smooth muscle cells in the medial layer and neo-muscularization of the small capillaries, which already starts before birth [26, 27, 33]. The pathophysiology of CDH is very complex, in part because of the heterogeneity of the disease [13, 55]. In contrast to the well-described morphological changes in CDH lungs, little is known about the molecular pathways that are involved at the onset of CDH.

Studies with human patient derived materials and CDH rodent models have contributed to the identification of genes that could potentially be involved in the pathogenesis of CDH [5, 8, 12, 22, 29]. Several lines of evidence show that the retinoic acid (RA) pathway contributes to the occurrence of this disease [21, 26, 27, 40]. Moreover, studies in the rodent CDH model, where a CDH-like phenotype is induced by the herbicide nitrofen, an inhibitor of the retinoic acid pathway, have strengthened the idea that the RA pathway is important in the development of CDH [9, 10, 21, 25, 27, 32, 34, 50]. Furthermore, studies in neonates with CDH have shown significant lower levels of retinol and retinol-binding protein [6, 31]. In addition, whole genome sequencing studies of CDH patients have identified chromosomal changes in genes such as COUPTFII, TCF7L2, FOXP4 and GATA6, but it remains elusive how these mutations contribute to the pathogenesis of CDH [8, 12, 20, 29, 51]. Thus, human and animal studies hint to a role of the retinoic acid pathway in the onset of CDH, but it remains unclear what the downstream signaling cascade is that ultimately leads to this severe disease.

Previously, we reported that structural changes in the pulmonary vasculature of CDH patients and in the CDH mouse model are already present early in development [27, 46]. In order to get better insights in the cellular origin of CDH and in the interactions between the different cell types, we analyzed the gene expression of different cell populations in the CDH mouse model at an early time point in gestation to catch the early events that lead to CDH.

Fluorescent Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) was applied to isolate four cell populations from control and CDH mouse lungs early in development and their transcriptomes were determined with bulk RNA sequencing. The analysis of the whole transcriptome data showed a high similarity in GO terms between the NG2 positive perivascular population and the CD31 positive endothelial cell population, suggesting a disturbed interaction between endothelial cells and pericytes. Detailed analysis of the RNA sequence data revealed reduced expression of the Krüppel like factor 4 (Klf4) gene in endothelial cells in the CDH model. We further report that KLF4 binds to the promoters of angiogenesis-related genes, hypoxia-related genes, members of the Ephrin-Eph family and the promoter regions of several members of the NOTCH signaling pathway. The identification of genes and pathways involved in the ontogeny of CDH contributes to the understanding of how interactions between cell types are affected and how this relates to the onset of the pathological conditions associated with CDH.

Materials and methods

Mouse CDH model

Timed-mated pregnant C57/Bl6 mice received 20 mg nitrofen and 5 mg BPCA in 500 µl olive oil (Sigma) per 25 g of body weight at day 8.5 of gestation to induce CDH in the offspring [27]. At different gestational ages pregnant mice were sacrificed, fetal lungs were isolated and their morphology was used to divide the phenotypic appearance of the fetuses into severely affected and mildly affected. Subsequently, lungs from all pups were analyzed and they all appeared hypoplastic and showed comparable lung pathology (hereafter “CDH”), which we previously showed for the nitrofen-induced rat model [32, 34]. All animal experiments were approved by an independent animal ethical committee and performed according to national guidelines.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

IF with the FACS antibodies was performed on 5 μm thick cryo-sections. Tissue was isolated and immediately embedded in OCT. Five µm thick sections were fixed in ice-cold methanol for 10 min. Blocking was performed using 5% heat inactivated donkey serum in PBS-0.5% Tween incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were washed and incubated with the conjugated primary antibody against NG2 and CD31 overnight. The next day, slides were washed and incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody, and with the conjugated antibody against EPCAM, for 2 h at room temperature. IF was performed on 5 μm thick paraffin sections of lungs as previously described [27]. Antigen retrieval was performed in 10mM Tris pH 9.0, 1 mM EDTA. Primary and secondary antibodies used for IF are listed in Table 1. Negative controls were included in all experiments by omitting the primary antibody. Each experiment was performed three times on at least three independent specimens.

FACS

The FACS sorting was performed as described previously [27]. Briefly, Lungs were isolated at different gestational ages, and were collected in 1 ml DMEM (Lonza) in Eppendorf tubes. Collagenases I, II and IV were added and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min in an Eppendorf shaker at 1000 rpm. Then, the mixture was mixed vigorously to completely disrupt the tissue and subsequently the cell suspension was put through a 40 μm cell strainer. The filter was rinsed with 1 ml DMEM 10% FCS and spun down at 4O C for 10 min at 400 g. Supernatant was removed and the pellet was washed with PBS-10% FCS and re-suspended in 300 µl PBS 10% FCS, of which 10 µl was set aside to count the number of cells and to check for doublets and cell aggregates. Samples were centrifuged at 4O C for 10 min at 400 g and re-suspended in 100 µl of either a mix of FACS antibodies (CD31, EPCAM, CD45 and NG2) or 100 µl of a single antibody (CD31, EPCAM, CD45 or NG2) for 40 min at 4o C. Samples were washed by adding 150 µl PBS-FCS, gently mixed and centrifuged. Pellets were re-suspended in 200 µl PBS-FCS and kept in darkness at 4o C until sorted. Prior to sorting 0.1 µl DAPI solution was added and samples were measured on the ARIA FACS machine (B&D). A 100 micron nozzle was used and the gap was kept around 8. Cells were collected in RNAlater (Thermofisher) and stored at 4o C until RNA isolation was performed. Analysis of the FACS data was done using FlowJo software.

For RNA sequencing, lungs of three control and of three CDH fetal mouse were isolated at E13.5, processed and cell populations were FACS sorted according to the above strategy.

RNA isolation, sequencing, amplification and qpcr

RNA was isolated using the mirVana Paris kit (Thermosfisher). Samples in RNA later were 1 on 1 diluted with ice-cold PBS and spun down at 15,000 rpm. Single read 50 bp RNA sequencing was performed on Illumina Hiseq2500 following the Smarter-2 protocol with adjustments [36, 47]. Raw reads were aligned to mm10 reference genome using Hisat2 aligner [24]. Gene counting was performed using HTSeq count [2]. DESeq2 R package was used for differential expression analysis [30].

Cut and tag

Cut and Tag experiments were performed with human endothelial colony forming cells (hECFCs) according to a previously published protocol [23]. In brief, hECFCs were cultured until confluence, and a total of 11 × 106 cells were harvested in 10% FCS/PBS and counted. Cells were isolated using Concanavalin A-coated (BP531-3 ml, Sanbio) beads. Two primary antibodies against KLF4 were used independently (AF3640, goat, R&D, and HPA002926, rabbit, Sigma), and corresponding IgG controls (goat AB-108-C, rabbit AB-105-C, R&D), as well as the antibodies against modified histones H3K4Me2 (Abcam, Ab32356), H3K27Ac (Abcam, Ab4729) and H3K27Me3 (Active Motif, 39155), were incubated overnight at 4 °C, and the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. pA-Tn5 adapter complexes (C01070001, Diagenode) were bound at room temperature for 1 h, and tagmentation was performed for 1 h at 37 °C. DNA was extracted using the chloroform extraction method, and PCR was performed using NEBNext HiFi 2x PCR master mix (M0541S) and indexed i5 and indexed i7 primers. DNA was purified after PCR using SPRI paramagnetic beads (Agentcourt AMPure XP, A63880) and checked using a High DNA Sensitivity DNA assay on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Adapter sequences were removed from the sequence reads. These were aligned to the human GRCh38 reference genome using the HISAT2 aligner. After alignment, duplicate reads were filtered and peaks were called with the MACS2 peak caller. Sequence coverage over the genome was determined. For motif analysis, only peak sequences in promoter regions defined as 1 kb from transcription start sites were used, and motifs were identified with MEME-ChIP [23].

Whole mount immunofluorescence

Whole mount immunofluorescence staining of total fetal lungs was essentially done as previously described [27]. Shortly, freshly isolated lungs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight. The next day, the lungs were gradually dehydrated through washes in increasing methanol and could be stored in pure methanol at −20 °C for up to 3 months. The next day, the lungs were re-hydrated by sequential steps of decreasing concentration of methanol and blocked in PBS, 0.4% triton X-100 and 1% skimmed milk powder for 4 h, blocking buffer was refreshed every hour. Subsequently, the lungs were incubated with primary anti-body for at least 4 days at 4 °C. Then samples were washed with PBS-0.4% Triton for at least 48 h, washing solution was refreshed every 4 h and at the end of the day. Secondary donkey anti-bodies appropriate for the choice of primary antibody were conjugated with Alexa 488, Alexa 594, biotinylated 647 and were incubated for at least 48 h (Table 1). After this incubation, several washes were performed and the lungs were cleared with benzyl alcohol/benzyl benzoate (BABB). Samples were imaged using a Tile Scan option on the Leica Sp5 confocal microscope. Pictures were processed using FiJi software, pictures were adjusted with brightness/contrast option and background was reduced in Photoshop using Levels function.

Results

Distinctive and specific cell populations in the early mouse lung

Previously, we and others showed that the CDH mouse model had comparable pathological pulmonary vascular characteristics to the well-established nitrofen rat model, and resembled the pathology of human CDH patients [9, 27, 32, 34]. The lungs of CDH patients gradually show pathological changes in the vascular smooth muscle cells leading to disturbed development of the pulmonary vascular bed [46]. The pulmonary vessels grow and expand through distal angiogenesis which requires perivascular cells to stabilize newly formed endothelial sprouts [35]. In CDH, the coverage of pulmonary vessels by perivascular cells is denser and extends more laterally compared to control lungs. This coincided with increased expression of contractile markers and reduced deposition of collagen IV by perivascular cells in CDH lungs [27, 45, 46].

In the current study we further delineate the individual cell populations involved in vascular and airway development and focused on the early pseudoglandular stage of normal and abnormal lung development. Therefore, cell populations from early pseudoglandular lungs (E13.5) of control and CDH mouse embryos were isolated using FACS sorting followed by RNA sequencing. Although the CDH mouse model displays a diaphragmatic defect in 40–60% of the pups, most likely due to small differences in the gestational age between littermates, all littermates show pulmonary hypoplasia and vascular abnormalities as previously shown (Fig. 1A) [27]. Based on the hypoplasia, we were able to select lungs which we designated CDH and used for further analysis. Furthermore, some pups showed a strong facial abnormality, which we referred to as severely affected. In order to ascertain the specificity of the antibodies to be used for the FACS sorting strategy, we first performed an analysis of the spatial distribution of the individual cell populations in early mouse lungs (Fig. 1B).

The immunofluorescence labeling revealed that the antibody against the epithelial cellular adhesion molecule (EPCAM) specifically labeled the epithelial cells of the airways (indicated by open arrow heads Fig. 1B). Furthermore, endothelial cells were specifically labeled with the Cluster of Differentiation 31 (CD31, or Platelet Endothelial Cell Adhesion Molecule (PECAM-1)) antibody (indicated with white arrows Fig. 1B) and perivascular cells were specifically labeled with an antibody against the Neuron-Glial antigen 2 (NG2, or Chondroitin Sulfate Proteoglycan 4 (CSPG4); indicated with closed arrow heads Fig. 1B). Perivascular cells are more precisely defined by NG2 and Platelet-Derived Growth Factor Receptor beta (PDGFRB), but as we previously showed [27], at this stage of development NG2 is sufficient to identify this population. Thus, the antibodies specifically identified the cell populations at the expected location in the embryonic lungs, and were subsequently used to purify and isolate these cell populations from single cell suspensions derived from whole embryonic lungs. The CD45NEG fraction was used to purify the endothelial (CD31POS), epithelial (EPCAMPOS) and perivascular (NG2POS) cells, while the heterogeneous CD31NEG, EPCAMNEG, NG2NEG population was also collected as a marker-negative, residual fraction, which we subsequently referred to as the mesenchymal population (Mes). FACS plots showed that the size of these cell populations was variable between control and CDH samples (Fig. 1C). The cell percentages of the endothelial, epithelial and perivascular cells in CDH varied between 3.0% − 6.6%, whereas the mesenchymal cell population was around 86%. As previously shown, the hypoplastic CDH lungs had less endothelial cells, but more NG2POS cells due to a higher coverage of vessels by perivascular cells in CDH (Fig. 1C and D) [27]. The transcriptome of these isolated populations was sequenced and the purity of the populations was determined by the analysis of genes specific for endothelial, epithelial and perivascular cells (Fig. 1E, supplementary Table 1). This revealed that the endothelial specific markers CD34, CDH5, PECAM1 and VEGFR2 had the highest number of reads in the CD31POS endothelial cell population (Fig. 1E). The normalized counts of the perivascular markers Desmin, NG2, PDGFRβ and PDGFRα showed the highest expression in the NG2POS perivascular population, while the gene expression levels of DR1, EPCAM, FOXA1 and NKX2-1 revealed that these markers were expressed the highest by the epithelial (EPCAMPOS) population (Fig. 1E). Taken together, we identified specific populations in situ and subsequently isolated these different cell populations with high purity as indicated by the heatmaps for the different markers.

CDH-associated genes can be assigned to specific cell populations

Several genes have been associated with the pathogenesis of CDH, either through the identification by exome studies in CDH patients or through studies with genetically modified mouse strains [5, 10, 12, 22, 42, 51]. Therefore, we first focused our analysis of the transcriptomes on those genes that have previously been associated with CDH in human and mouse studies (Fig. 2A, supplemental Fig. 1, 2).

Previously de novo mutations were identified in the transcriptional co activators eyes absent 1 and 2 (Eya1, Eya2) and the transcription factor Zinc Finger Protein/Friend Of GATA (FOG) Family Member ZFPM2 (FOG2) of CDH patients [28]. Sequencing studies of CDH patients furthermore revealed mutations in the genes stimulated by retinoic acid gene 6 (STRA6), Glypican3 (GPC3), chromodomain helicase DNA-binding protein 7 (CHD7) and GATA binding protein 4 (GATA4) [8, 12, 20, 26, 38]. The various pathways in which the products of these genes are involved reflect the heterogeneous nature of CDH and the difficulty to unravel the early onset of CDH. A heatmap was generated for these CDH-associating genes to show their differential expression patterns (Fig. 2A). CHD7 seems higher expressed in the endothelial - and perivascular cell populations, but had a lower fold change in the mesenchymal population. Mutations in EYA1 and EYA2 have not only been shown in patient studies, but mice lacking two alleles of the transcriptional co-activators Eya1 and one allele of Eya2 showed diaphragmatic defects, indicating the importance of appropriate expression levels of these genes [17]. The transcriptome analysis showed that Eya1 expression was decreased in the epithelial and perivascular population. Additionally, Eya2 was higher expressed in CDH samples in the epithelial cell population but significantly lower in the endothelial population. So, an imbalance in the expression levels of Eya1 and Eya2 may be related to the development of CDH.

Genes encoding proteins in the Roundabout homolog/Slit guidance ligand (Robo/Slit) pathway have also been implicated in CDH [1, 11]. The expression of Robo is increased in the endothelial cell population and decreased in the mesenchymal population in CDH. Slit3 was expressed significantly higher in the endothelial population of CDH lungs, but lower in the perivascular cell population. Other changes in expression that are noticeable are the higher expression of Gpc3, Kalrn, Aldh1a2, Crabp2 and Gli2. Ald1a2 is interesting as this gene was found to be involved in CDH patients as well as in several CDH models [10].

To further confirm the RNA sequence results, the expression of a few genes that are part of the retinoic acid pathway were analyzed by qPCR. Platelet-derived growth factor receptor α (Pdgfrα), Slit Guidance Ligand 2 (Slit2), Retinoic acid receptor β (Rarβ) and Cellular retinoic acid binding protein B2 (Crabp2) were quantified by qPCR of RNA isolated from independent samples (Fig. 2B). The results confirmed differences in expression between control and CDH samples observed with the RNA sequence results of FACS-sorted cell populations.

Taken together, assigning genes that have been implicated in the pathogenesis of CDH to specific cell population is a step forward in understanding how CDH develops (Fig. 2A, supplemental Fig. 1, 2). The RNA sequence data of the four different cell populations isolated at E13.5 from the CDH mouse model showed that CDH-associating genes are differently expressed in different cell populations. Moreover, the up regulation of Robo1 in the endothelial population in combination with the down regulation of Slit3 in the perivascular population could lead to changes in migration and guidance of these cells and thereby leading to impaired angiogenesis and the alterations in the pulmonary vasculature [11, 54].

Differentially expressed genes in specific cell populations in CDH

In order to further elucidate potential interactions between the cell types, the individual transcriptomes were subjected to pathway analyses tools to reveal whether or not matching pathways would be active in different cell types, suggestive for potential interactions. A Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed to investigate which pathways were differentially regulated in early development (Fig. 2C, D; supplemental Fig. 3 A, supplemental Table 2). The top pathways in the CD31 positive population are related to the development of the respiratory tract (supplemental Fig. 3 A). The top 30 GO terms for the mesenchymal - and epithelial cell populations partly overlapped with each other, although there are a number of shared GO terms (Fig. 2C, supplementary Table 2). As the vascular abnormalities in CDH are an important contributor to the origin of the disease, we focused on the endothelial and perivascular populations [27]. The top 30 differentially regulated pathways of the NG2 and CD31 population showed e a number of shared GO terms (Fig. 2D, supplementary Table 2).We further focused on the expression of few genes from known pathways that previously have been associated with perivascular and endothelial cells [48], such as Slit2/3 and Robo2 that were differentially expressed by both populations, although Robo1 and Paxillin (Pxn) only by the endothelial population. This suggests that signaling between endothelial and perivascular cells seems affected and that a lack in migration and guidance in these populations could potentially lead to the abnormal pulmonary vasculature of CDH pups and eventually leading to lung hypoplasia. A similar analysis was executed for angiopoietin signaling, which acts through the tyrosine kinase 2 receptor (TIE2) and its ligand angiopoetin-1 (ANG1)(Supplemental Fig. 3B). The ANG-1/TIE2 signaling is controlled by angiopoietin-2 and in this manner the responsiveness of endothelial cells towards cytokines is regulated [14]. Analysis of the molecules of this pathway showed that part of the proteins was only differentially expressed in the endothelial population while other proteins were differentially expressed by the perivascular cell population. The Fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (Fgfr2), Fgfr1 and Signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (Stat5) genes were differentially expressed by the endothelial cell population whereas serine/threonine-protein kinase 3 (Akt3), Nuclear factor (Nkfb1) and p21-activated kinase 6 (Pak6) were only differentially expressed by the perivascular population and TEK tyrosine kinase/tyrosine kinase with Ig and EGF homology domains 2 (Tek/Tie2) and Angiopoietin 2 (Angpt2) were differentially expressed by both populations (Supplemental Fig. 3B). This supports our previous hypothesis that the process of angiogenesis is affected in CDH [27], potentially through angiopoietin signaling, and could furthermore explain the inadequate pulmonary vasculature of CDH pups.

Expression of KLF4 is reduced in endothelial cells in CDH

Significant overlap between the GO terms of the endothelial population and the NG2 population was observed. We showed before that increased perivascular cell coverage leads to abnormal vessel development in CDH [27]. Given the close association between endothelial cells and perivascular cells, it is likely that the endothelial cell population is also affected in CDH [3, 4]. Therefore, a more detailed analysis of the gene expression of the endothelial cell population revealed that the transcription factor Krüppel like factor 4 (Klf4) was differentially expressed in the endothelial population (Fig. 2E, supplemental Fig. 4A-C). KLF4 has previously been linked to endothelial cell behavior and angiogenesis and has been suggested to be an upstream regulator of NOTCH signaling [18]. Moreover, increased expression of KLF4 leads to reduced expression of NOTCH genes in tumors [18]. NOTCH signaling is known for being important in the competition between the tip and stalk cells during sprouting of endothelial cells to form new tubes in the process of angiogenesis [7].

First, we performed a spatio-temporal analysis of KLF4 expression in normal and CDH lung samples not only early in gestation when the four main lobes are established (E12.5), but also at two other important time points in lung development, the end of the pseudoglandular stage (E15.5) and at the end of the canalicular stage/early saccular stage (E18.5) (Fig. 3). Co-localization of KLF4 with the endothelial marker endomucin (EMCN) revealed that endothelial-specific KLF4 expression appeared to be decreased in the CDH lung samples isolated at E12.5 compared to control lungs, and remained lower until E18.5 (indicated with white arrows in Fig. 3A, supplemental Fig. 4D). To pinpoint the cell type specificity of KLF4, the expression of KLF4 in embryonic lungs was analyzed using normal and CDH lungs isolated at E15.5 in conjunction with either PDGFRB (Fig. 3B, supplemental Fig. 4D) or NG2 (Fig. 3C) to demarcate the perivascular cells. This showed that KLF4 is specifically expressed in the endothelial cells lining the vessels, within the perimeter of the perivascular cells (Fig. 3B, C). To quantify the observed differences in KLF4 expression between controls and CDH lungs, high resolution images were made of total embryonic lungs after whole mount immunostaining with antibodies to visualize the perivascular cells and endothelial cells (Fig. 3D). This procedure completely maintains the integrity of the lung and even the smallest capillaries can be traced more accurately than in lung sections. At E15.5 a significant lower number of KLF4 positive cells were detected in CDH (Fig. 3D), indicating an early defect in the vascular development in the lungs of CDH pups. The decrease of KLF4 signal was consistent through development, although at later stages these differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 3D).

Having established that KLF4 is significantly downregulated in endothelial cells of CDH lungs, we next determined the genome wide binding sites of KLF4 using human primary endothelial cells. This identified a total number of 2231 specific binding sites, of which 1261 were located in the vicinity of a gene, either at the transcriptional start site (TSS), the promoter, or the gene body (Fig. 4A). In addition, we also performed genome wide analysis to investigate the chromatin architecture using antibodies raised against the modified histones marks H3K27Ac, a marker for active enhancers and promoters, H3K4me2, a marker for active promoters, and H3K27me3, a marker for silenced genes (Fig. 4, supplemental Fig. 5).

KLF4 associated with several promoters of genes encoding factors that are implicated in angiogenesis and may thus potentially be regulated by KLF4, including KLF4 itself and FOXF1 (Fig. 4B, C, supplemental Fig. 4E, 5 A). FOXF1 was shown to interact with KLF4 and to be a direct target of KLF4 in a TGF-β induced model [56]. Interestingly, Foxf1 was also significantly downregulated in the endothelial cells of the CDH mouse model. Aside from general endothelial cell markers, such as CDH5, genes involved in angiogenesis and disease were bound by KLF4. The endothelial cell-specific molecule 1 (ESM1) or endocan, is a protein secreted by endothelial cells and mainly found in lung and kidney. Recently, it was shown that ESM1 positive cells give rise to gCAP, aCAP and arterial ECs [16, 37]. Other examples of genes involved in angiogenesis are TBX3, SOX17 and GATA6, all three have been described to have essential functions in ECs during development, arterial specification and disease (Fig. 4C, supplemental Fig. 5B) [15, 39, 49]. Next, we also found that KLF4 is potentially involved in the regulation of the three hypoxia responsive genes encoding for the alpha isoforms, HIF1α, EPAS1 and HIF3α (Fig. 4D, supplemental Fig. 5 C). As EPHB-EFNB signaling is involved in angiogenesis, we also checked whether KLF4 would bind to the promoters of these genes, and indeed, we found that KLF4 associates with at least EFNB2, EPHB3 and EPHB4 (Fig. 4E, supplemental Fig. 5D) [49]. As indicated, NOTCH signaling is important in angiogenesis, and KLF4 binds to members of the NOTCH pathway, such as HES1, NUMBL, NOTCH1 and JAG1 [18] (Fig. 5A), which in part corresponded with the changes for the statistically significant transcript levels of several members of the NOTCH pathway (Fig. 5B, C). Although the variation in expression in the perivascular and endothelial cell populations is marginal, several genes appear to be significantly different in CDH lungs compared to controls. Disturbed NOTCH signaling may subsequently lead to a decreased number of sprouting endothelial cells, which results in a reduced number of newly formed tubules in the vascular bed in CDH.

Discussion

Pulmonary vascular diseases are characterized by aberrant formation and development of the vasculature, most often resulting in challenging treatment modalities [26]. The pulmonary hypertension associated with CDH results in long hospitalization and potential life-long problems. The onset of the vascular changes that correspond with the disease are not well understood, but occurs during development as patients suffering from CDH already have vascular abnormalities before birth [46]. Studies towards genes involved in CDH have mainly been focused on either patient sequencing or mouse knockout studies [5, 8, 12, 22, 29, 42, 51]. It remains unclear what the effect of the mutations is on the number of cells and their behavior or fate. Our study relates differences in gene expression to individual cell populations. Previously, we showed that the distribution of perivascular cells is affected in CDH and form the cellular basis for the structural vascular changes that accompany CDH [27]. Moreover, transient inhibition of retinoic acid (RA) signaling early during pregnancy resulted in an increased pericyte coverage of pulmonary vessels and reduced expansion of the capillary bed, one of the earliest pathological signs of the structural changes in CDH. In this study, we investigated the intimate and close interaction between endothelial cells and the perivascular population in normal and abnormal development. Analyzing the endothelial and perivascular populations revealed some similarity in GO terms, which indicates that the genes of specific signaling pathways are differentially expressed in CDH. This could point to aberrant communication between perivascular cells and endothelial cells, resulting in abnormal angiogenesis. More specifically, the appearance of the ROBO/SLIT pathway in the GO term analysis indicated that the guidance signaling cues of the endothelial cells and perivascular cells are affected, which may explain the simplification of the pulmonary vasculature observed in CDH.

Besides the changes in genes that are involved in both cell populations, we identified KLF4 as a novel gene that is reduced in expression in the endothelial cell population in CDH. It has been demonstrated that KLF4 expression was increased in smooth muscle cells (SMC) of the pulmonary arterioles of pulmonary hypertension patients without CDH [43]. It was hypothesized that this increase is required for dedifferentiation and clonal expansion of SMCs in the pulmonary hypertension mouse model. This study showed no difference in the expression of KLF4 in the perivascular population but a significant decrease in KLF4 expression in the endothelial population. The observed difference further suggests that pulmonary hypertension of the newborn and pulmonary hypertension secondary to cardiac malformations are intrinsically different from each other. Interestingly, another study showed that the expression of Klf4 was induced by all trans retinoic acid through the retinoic acid receptor α, possibly linking KLF4 to the retinoic acid pathway [44]. The latter could be potentially interesting in light of the retinoid hypothesis that states that altered retinoid signaling is the underlying cause of the development of CDH [40].

The role of KLF4 in angiogenesis has been linked to NOTCH signaling, and KLF4 is an upstream regulator of NOTCH [18]. The importance of KLF4 in the regulation of NOTCH signaling, and thereby the number of sprouting endothelial cells during angiogenesis, together with the observed decrease in KLF4 expression in the CDH mouse model could contribute to the vascular abnormalities associated with CDH. Since a reduced number of capillaries was observed in the lungs of the CDH mouse model we hypothesized that the decreased KLF4 expression in endothelial cells and disturbed NOTCH signaling would lead to a decrease in active sprouting cells during angiogenesis (Fig. 6). A limitation of our analysis is the restricted number of samples resulting in low statistical significance of the qPCR data.

In summary, this study shows a comparative whole transcriptome analysis of four different cell populations of mouse lungs isolated at gestational age E13.5 of control and experimental CDH. The differential expressed genes of the endothelial and perivascular cell populations are associated with similar pathways, which could be the basis of the altered pulmonary vascular development in CDH. Furthermore, we identified a new candidate gene involved in angiogenesis that are reduced in expression in endothelial cells during the development of the pulmonary vasculature in CDH. Furthermore, we showed that KLF4 physically binds to promoters of genes that are associated with endothelial cell function and angiogenesis, implying direct regulation of these genes by KLF4. The latter suggests that KLF4 is an important regulator of early angiogenesis and arterial differentiation, and that reduced expression of KLF4 may lead to altered vascular development in CDH.

Conclusion

In the current manuscript we showed that decreased KLF4 expression in endothelial cells early in development associates with aberrant vascular development in CDH. Additionally, KLF4 controls the expression of genes associated with vascular development, which further supports the importance of KLF4 in pulmonary angiogenesis. These findings support and contribute to our previous data that pulmonary vessels in CDH patients are already affected before birth. Collectively, this concept of premature aberrant vessels in CDH patients may impact the timing of therapeutic strategies to start preterm with interventions to reduce pulmonary hypertension associated with CDH.
