Decoding the immune landscape: KRAS and STK11 genes mutations shape CD8+ T cell response in resectable non-small-cell lung cancer after neoadjuvant therapy
Authors: Kamila Wojas-Krawczyk, Magdalena Knetki-Wróblewska, Maciej Krzakowski
Published: 2025

Over the past few years, growing interest has emerged in the molecular profile of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients eligible for immune checkpoint inhibitor-based treatment. Not only pathogenic variants in the EGFR, ALK and ROS1 genes, but also other abnormalities are important to predict immunotherapy’s efficacy. Given the high prevalence of KRAS gene alterations, patients with KRAS mutations are of clinical importance, notably in the context of immunotherapy selection for advanced-stage NSCLC. A completely new perspective is being directed towards STK11 gene mutations, which often coexist with KRAS what entirely changes the tumor microenvironment, modulate the immune system cells activity and significantly alters the clinical response to immunotherapy, as has been demonstrated in a population with advanced NSCLC (1-6). These findings was strongly highlight in one of the largest studies by Ricciuti et al., in which among KRAS-mutant patients, those harboring STK11 or KEAP1 co-mutations showed the poorest survival outcomes following immunotherapy (7).

It is highly intriguing to consider whether molecular markers may also inform immunotherapy decisions in early-stage NSCLC. An important question is whether we will be able to accurately define the prognosis of patients following neoadjuvant therapy and surgical resection based on molecular and immunological examination?

The paper presented by Rosner et al. addresses the issue by focusing on molecular profiling the patients suitable for preoperative immunochemotherapy (8). This has become an important part of the treatment of patients with early-stage NSCLC in recent years (9-12). Although the results of many trials clearly show a benefit in terms of prolonging disease-free survival (DFS) and an impact on overall survival (OS), early disease progression has been observed in a few percent of patients and it resulted in inability to perform curative resection (13). Therefore, it may be important to gather additional information and optimize patient eligibility criteria for perioperative treatment, considering a more complete molecular profile of the tumour. To date, data from randomized clinical trials assessing the value of preoperative immunochemotherapy against pathogenic variants in the KRAS or STK11 genes are very limited. In a cohort analysis of 30 patients participating in the NeoTAP01 trial, isolated STK11 and KEAP1 variants did not exhibit demonstrable predictive value (14).

Rosner et al. reported the findings of an analysis involving 61 patients considered eligible for preoperative treatment, among whom 21 had KRAS-mutated lung cancer (including 6 patients with KRAS p.Gly12Cys substitution). In the KRASmut group, 7 patients had a coexisting of STK11 mutation. Patients were assigned to 3 different neoadjuvant treatment regimens: cohort 1 received preoperative nivolumab monotherapy, cohort 2 received nivolumab (3 mg/kg) together with ipilimumab (1 mg/kg) intravenously at least 6 weeks before planned resection, followed by two additional doses of nivolumab (3 mg/kg) administered at least 4 and 2 weeks before surgery. Cohort 3 received nivolumab (360 mg every 3 weeks) in combination with carboplatin (area under curve: AUC 5 or 6 every 3 weeks) and paclitaxel (175–200 mg/m2 every 3 weeks) for three preoperative cycles before undergoing planned surgical resection within 6 weeks after the completion of systemic treatment. Postoperative treatment was at the discretion of the investigator. The study included a very interesting molecular analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, as well as an assessment of the clinical efficacy of neoadjuvant treatment and surgery (8). Let us summarize each of these stages.

For 21 patients with mutated KRAS gene in Rosner et al. study, 5-year recurrence-free survival (RFS) and OS rates were 58% and 85%, respectively. The risk of recurrence was comparable regardless of the type of KRAS mutation [p.Gly12Cys versus non-p.Gly12Cys; hazard ratio (HR) =2.74; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.55–13.71; P=0.22] or the co-mutation STK11 status (HR =0.9; 95% CI: 0.18–4.55; P=0.897). However, a significantly higher risk of relapse was observed in patients with STK11 co-mutation (HR =7.09; 95% CI: 1.25–40.1; P=0.027). The median RFS for KRASmut/STK11mut and KRASmut/STK11wt was 14.03 months and NR (not reached), respectively. However, the results suggest the need for prospective follow-up in this area. Other investigators have suggested that patients with KRAS gene abnormalities may benefit more from preoperative immunochemotherapy than KRASwt patients, but data on the value of co-mutations in the STK11 gene are very limited (15).

The experimental part of this study focused on deep single-cell transcriptomics analysis of tumour-infiltrating CD8-positive cells in two groups of patients: with co-mutation of KRAS and STK11 genes (KRASmut/STK11mut) and in patients with KRAS mutation only (KRASmut/STK11wt) (8). Interestingly, this analysis showed that we are facing two different profiles of T lymphocytes infiltrating tumor tissue. As author concluded, paradoxically, despite the clinically inferior response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy of KRASmut/STK11mut tumors, their CD8-positive lymphocytes were enriched in multiple molecules associated with the effector function (e.g., genes for several granzymes), as well as in markers specific for T-cell dysfunction or exhaustion (e.g., TIM-3, LAG-3, TOX2, CD39). The lymphocyte profile was defined as tissue-resident memory (TRM) subsets with high expression of ITGAE (CD103)—typically widely observed on intraepithelial lymphocytes and of EOMES—a marker specific for memory T lymphocytes. These findings implicate a more dysfunctional T-cell phenotype associated with chronic antigen stimulation in KRASmut/STK11mut tumors. In summary, it appears that these lymphocytes were equipped with the necessary functional tools but were exhausted due to the prolonged immune engagement they had undergone. Contrary, CD8+ TILs from KRASmut/STK11wt tumors were enriched in several genes associated with prostaglandin signaling, including the high expression of PTGER4 and PTGER2 genes—encoding receptors for prostaglandin 2 (PGE2), which is known for its strong immunosuppressive effect on T lymphocytes (8). Prostaglandin inhibits the intratumoral activity of T lymphocytes by blocking the receptor for interleukin-2 (IL-2)—a cytokine extremely important for T lymphocyte differentiation and their survival. Increased in genes associated with PGE2 signaling in TILs from KRASmut/STK11wt tumors was accompanied by reduced expression for IL-2 receptors. In contrast to TILs from patients harboring KRAS and STK11 mutations, lymphocytes from patients with a wild-type STK11 gene appear capable of recognizing tumor antigens despite an evidence of functional impairment. The authors clearly indicate the distinction between exhausted and dysfunctional lymphocytes (8). These differences are important for understanding T cell behavior in the tumor microenvironment. Exhausted T cells arise during chronic antigen exposure and are characterized by progressive loss of effector functions, sustained expression of inhibitory receptors, reduced proliferative capacity, but they may retain partial cytotoxic function. Dysfunctional T cells display impaired effector functions but may arise due to defects in activation or priming, not necessarily from chronic stimulation. Their dysfunction is often intrinsic and non-reversible, and they may be less responsive to immunotherapy.

The summarized results of the study are likely to open a new chapter in the investigation of predictive biomarkers in early-stage NSCLC. It clearly indicates that even within this patient group, certain molecular abnormalities are gaining significant importance. However, the study has several limitations. Most notably, the size of the study cohort does not allow to drawn definitive conclusions, but rather suggests emerging trends. Secondly, different KRAS gene mutations may exert varying effects on the tumor microenvironment and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Are we witnessing a gradual shift away from the standard biomarker, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression? In the Rosner et al. study, none of the patients with high PD-L1 expression (≥50% TC) exhibited STK11 aberrations. Could molecular analysis eventually replace immunohistochemical diagnostics? We are undoubtedly facing a paradigm shift in the way early-stage NSCLC is approached, and molecular testing as a basis for selecting neoadjuvant treatment regimens is already knocking on the door of oncology clinics.

We are convinced that specialists in molecular diagnostics will play an increasingly pivotal role as members of multidisciplinary teams responsible for qualifying patients for preoperative immunochemotherapy. Nevertheless, it is evident that further comprehensive data is urgently needed. It should be postulated that the publication of detailed analyses of the immunological and molecular profiles of patients enrolled in pivotal clinical trials would significantly expand our current knowledge. Such data would not only enrich scientific understanding but also have a direct impact on clinical decision-making. Consequently, the discussion in daily oncological practice would extend beyond the traditional assessment of tumour resectability to include an evidence-based evaluation of the potential efficacy of preoperative immunochemotherapy in an individual patient.
