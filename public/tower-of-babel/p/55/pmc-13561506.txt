Standardised versus Individualised Fortification of Expressed Breastmilk for Preterm Outcomes: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Chloe Mary Bell, Barbara Elizabeth Cormack, Mariam Buksh, Frank H. Bloomfield, Luling Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough the precise nutrient requirements of preterm infants remain uncertain, routine human milk fortification is standard practice despite limited evidence of long-term benefit. Whether current fortification strategies optimally balance benefits, risks, and resource use remains unclear. This review evaluates whether individualised fortification improves preterm infant outcomes.MethodsRandomised controlled trials comparing individualised with standardised fortification were included, evaluating outcomes including mortality, neurodevelopment, growth, and neonatal morbidities. Four databases and three trial registries were searched. Screening, data extraction, and quality assessment were conducted independently by two reviewers using Cochrane Risk of Bias-1 tool. Certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE.ResultsTwelve studies including 575 participants were included. Evidence is very uncertain about the effect of individualised fortification on mortality [181 infants, relative risk (RR) 0.20, 95% confidence interval (CI) (0.02, 1.66), p = 0.14, I2 = 0%] and necrotising enterocolitis [193 infants, RR 0.29 (0.05, 1.72), p = 0.17, I2 = 0%], both very low certainty. Effect on neurodevelopment at 18 months remains very uncertain. Individualised fortification likely increases head circumference [224 infants, mean difference (MD) 1.76 (0.76, 2.76) mm/week, p = 0.0005, I2 = 52%], weight gain [454 infants, MD 2.87 (1.36, 4.39) g/kg/day, p < 0.0002, I2 = 79%], length gain [261 infants, MD 3.20 (1.22, 5.18) mm/week, p = 0.002, I2 = 81%] and fat-free mass [106 infants, MD 121.4 (25.6, 217.2) g, p = 0.01, I2 = 0], all with moderate-certainty. It may increase fat mass [106 infants, MD 68.9 (6.1, 131.8) g, p = 0.03, I2 = 63%] with low certainty.ConclusionIndividualised fortification likely increases growth in preterm infants; however, whether this is beneficial to clinical and long-term functional health is uncertain. Further high-quality trials with long-term follow-up are needed.

Introduction

Preterm birth affects 1 in 10 infants worldwide [1] and is associated with nutrient deficiency and suboptimal growth and neurodevelopment [2]. Mothers’ own milk is recommended for preterm infants. Pasteurised donor milk is also sometimes used, but this differs substantially from own mothers milk in both nutrient and non-nutrient content. Evidence for benefit from use of pasteurised donor milk remains weak. Whether own mother’s milk and pasteurised donor milk require routine macronutrient fortification is unknown, as evidence of clear benefit to clinical, long-term neurodevelopmental and growth outcomes remains uncertain [3].

Standard fortification is the common practice, adding a fixed dose of commercial multicomponent breastmilk fortifier to breastmilk, based on an estimated composition of breastmilk [4]. Individualised fortification comes in two forms – targeted, whereby the nutrient composition of breastmilk is measured using a breastmilk analyser, and adjusted, whereby the infant’s blood urea nitrogen (BUN) concentrations are measured, from which modular additives are added to breastmilk accordingly [5]. Although standardised fortification is common practice in most neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), it does not account for inter- and intra-individual variations and therefore may not satisfy the nutritional requirements of all preterm infants [6, 7]. Individualised fortification accounts for these differences; however, there are few data regarding its efficacy on preterm outcomes. We undertook a systematic review to examine the effect of individualised fortification of expressed breastmilk on preterm outcomes compared to standard fortification, with the aim of informing future guideline development and clinical decision-making.

Methods

This review was conducted according to the Cochrane review of intervention methodology [8], reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [9] (online suppl. file 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553366). The protocol was registered prospectively into PROSPERO (registration number CRD42024613603) (online suppl. file 2).

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

We searched Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL Complete and the Cochrane Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and for registered trials in Clinical Trials [10], Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry [11] and WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP [12] (online suppl. file 3) from inception to 11 October 2025.

Inclusion criteria were published and unpublished randomised controlled trials (RCTs), without restrictions on geographical location or language, involving infants born preterm (<37 weeks’ gestational age) where the intervention was individualised fortification of expressed breastmilk via enteral administration at any timepoint from birth until discharge, and the comparator was standard fortification using commercially available fortifiers.

The primary outcome was mortality from birth to discharge. Secondary outcomes were neurodevelopmental impairment, necrotising enterocolitis (NEC), fully breastmilk feeding at hospital discharge, growth (weight, length/height, head circumference at discharge and post discharge), fully breastfeeding at hospital discharge [13], faltering growth/body composition, duration of initial hospital stay, time to full enteral feeds, bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD)/chronic lung disease, sepsis, retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), anaemia of prematurity, adverse effects, cost (cost of intervention, cost of neonatal care, and life-long cost), and time to full sucking feeds.

Data Collection and Analysis

Two reviewers (M.B. and C.M.B.) independently screened titles and abstracts of identified records, while L.L. and C.M.B. assessed potentially eligible full-text articles for inclusion and extracted data into a pre-specified data extraction form using Covidence [14]. Two independent reviewers (L.L. and C.M.B.) assessed the risk of bias of included studies using the Cochrane risk of bias-1 tool (Rob 1) [15]. Discrepancies in any steps were resolved by discussion or by a third author (B.E.C.).

We assessed certainty of evidence for key outcomes using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [8] and created a “Summary of Findings” table using the GRADEPro Guideline Development Tool [16]. The outcomes included in the GRADE assessment were pre-specified: mortality, neurodevelopmental impairment, NEC, fully breastmilk feeding at hospital discharge, faltering growth/body composition, duration of initial hospital stay, and adverse effects. These were selected as the most clinically important and decision-relevant endpoints.

Statistical Analysis

We undertook meta-analysis using Revman 5.4.1 [17] using fixed-effects models and calculated relative risks (RRs) for dichotomous outcomes with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We calculated mean differences (MDs) for continuous outcomes with 95% CIs. Random-effects models were used in sensitivity analyses, particularly for outcomes with substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 50%) or expected variation in effects, and consistency between models was assessed. We included studies in the meta-analysis that reported comparable raw outcome data. We estimated values for the mean and standard deviation for the studies that provided minimum, maximum and median or lower quartile, median and upper quartile so that data could be combined in meta-analysis [18]. Heterogeneity was assed using I2 and X2, with I2 >50 and p < 0.10 in the X2 test indicating significant heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses included only high-quality studies, and publication bias was assessed using funnel plots when >10 trials were available. Subgroup analyses examined fortification type, birthweight, gestational age, and milk source. Results were presented using forest plots and narrative summaries, with all analyses pre-specified unless otherwise stated.

Results

In total, 3,660 records were identified. After removing duplicates, we conducted title and abstract screening for 2,156, followed by full-text screening of 43 records. Three records could not be retrieved. Our inclusion criteria were met by a total of 16 studies. Four studies were ongoing, leaving 12 for inclusion in the review (Fig. 1). The studies were conducted between 1995 and 2025 (Table 1). According to the 2022 World Bank Classification [19], seven were conducted in high-income countries, four in upper-middle-income countries, and one in a lower-middle-income country.

Risk of Bias or Quality of Included Studies

The overall methodological quality of the included studies was of some concern. Of the 12 studies, seven (58%) had unclear risk of selection bias due to insufficient information regarding sequence generation or allocation concealment. High performance bias was found in three (25%) studies due to insufficient blinding of participants and personnel, and three (25%) studies had a high risk of detection bias due to insufficient blinding of outcome assessors. Three (25%) studies had unclear attrition bias, and one (8.3%) had high attrition bias due to insufficient information provided. Three (25%) studies had unclear reporting bias due to missing data, and 5/12 (42%) had unclear other bias due to unclear funding roles (Fig. 2).

Secondary Outcomes: Neurodevelopmental Impairment

The evidence from Rochow et al. [29] using Bayley III at 18 months is very uncertain about the effect of individualised fortification on cognition (51 infants, MD 3.60 [−3.17, 10.37], p = 0.30), language (51 infants, MD 2.70 [−5.97, 11.37], p = 0.54), and motor (51 infants, MD 4.50 [−1.54, 10.54], p = 0.14) development (Table 2).

NEC (as Defined by Author)

Evidence is very uncertain about the effect of individualised fortification on NEC (3 RCTs 193 infants, RR 0.29 (0.05, 1.72), p = 0.17, I2 = 0%, very low certainty (Table 2; Fig. 3b). Three studies found no events in either group [23, 31, 32].

Duration of Initial Hospital Stay (Days)

Individualised fortification may result in little to no effect on duration of hospital stay (2 RCTs, 99 infants, MD 0.33 [−6.54, 7.20] days, p = 0.93, I2 = 0%, low certainty (Table 2), online supplementary file 4, Fig. 1). Ahuja et al. [21] (35 days individualised versus 33.9 days standardised) and Khorana et al. [24] found that it did not differ significantly between groups.

Weight Gain

Evidence from ten RCTs on weight gain (454 infants, MD 2.87 g/kg/day [1.36, 4.39], p = 0.0002, I2 = 79%, Fig. 3c), four RTCs (179 infants, MD 5.08 g/day [1.79, 8.38], p = 0.002, I2 = 56%), and five RCTs (254 infants, MD 214.52 g [32.82, 396.22], p = 0.02, I2 = 70%) consistently supported increased weight gain with individualised fortification with moderate certainty due to small sample size (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 2a,b). However, two RCTs is very uncertain about the effect on weight z-score at the end of intervention (90 infants, SMD 0.06 [−0.35, 0.48], p = 0.76, I2 = 0%, very low certainty) due to small sample size, wide CIs and inconsistency (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 2c).

Agakidou et al. [20] found that weight z-scores at each time point up to 12-month corrected age did not differ significantly between the two groups and evidence from Rochow et al. [29] is very uncertain about the effect of individualised fortification on weight at 18-month follow-up (69 infants, MD -0.10 [−0.76, 0.56] kg, p = 0.77) due to attrition bias, small sample size and wide CI.

Length Gain

Evidence from seven RCTs on length gain (261 infants, MD 3.20 mm/week [1.22, 5.18], p = 0.002, I2 = 81%. Fig. 3d) and four RCTs (196 infants, MD 0.93 cm [0.32, 1.54], p = 0.003, I2 = 0%) supported increased length gain with individualised fortification with moderate certainty due to small sample size (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 3a). Evidence showed an increase in length z-score at the end of intervention with individualised fortification (2 RCTs, 90 infants, SMD 0.41 (−0.01, 0.83), p = 0.06, I2 = 0%, low certainty due to small sample size and wide CI) (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 3b).

Agakidou et al. [20] found that length z-scores at each time point up to 12-month corrected age did not differ significantly between the two groups and evidence from Rochow et al. [29] is very uncertain about the effect of individualised fortification on length at 18-month follow-up (69 infants, MD −0.80 [−2.80, 1.20] cm, p = 0.43) due to attrition bias, small sample size, and wide CI.

Head Circumference Gain

Evidence from five RCTs on head circumference growth (221 infants, MD 0.62 cm [−0.03, 1.28], p = 0.06, I2 = 56%) and six RCTs (224 infants, MD 1.76 mm/week [0.76, 2.76], p = 0.0005, I2 = 52%) supported increased head circumference gain with individualised fortification with moderate certainty due to small sample size (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 4a, b). Evidence showed an increase in head circumference z-score at the end of intervention with individualised fortification (2 RCTs, 90 infants, SMD 0.41 [−0.01, 0.83], p = 0.05, I2 = 0%, low certainty due to small sample size and wide CI) (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 4c).

Agakidou et al. [20] found that z-scores at each time point up to 12-month corrected age did not differ significantly between groups, and evidence from Rochow et al. [29] is very uncertain about the effect of individualised fortification on head circumference at 18-month follow-up (69 infants, MD 0.30 [−0.50, 1.10] cm, p = 0.46) due to attrition bias, small sample size and wide CI.

Body Composition

Rochow et al. [28] reported little to no effect of individualised fortification on fat-free mass index (MD 0.20 [−0.29, 0.69, p = 0.43] at 36 weeks). Evidence from two RCTs suggested that individualised fortification likely increases fat-free mass (g) at the end of intervention (106 infants, MD = 121.4 [25.6, 217.2], p = 0.01, I2 = 0) with moderate certainty due to small sample size (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 5a).

Rochow et al. [28] showed that individualised fortification results in a slight increase in fat mass percentage (MD 3.00 [−0.05, 6.05], p = 0.05) and fat mass index (MD 0.5 [0.03, 0.97], p = 0.04) at 36 weeks, while evidence from two RCTs shows that individualised fortification may increase fat mass (g) (106 infants, MD 68.97 [−34.91, 172.85], p = 0.10, I2 = 63%, low certainty due to small sample size and wide CI) at end of intervention (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 5b). Rochow et al. [28] indicated that individualised fortification may result in a slight increase BMI (MD 0.70 [0.06, 1.34], p = 0.03) at 36 weeks whilst Agakidou et al. [20] reported that z-scores for BMI at each time point up to the 12-month corrected age did not differ significantly between groups.

Parat et al. [26] found that individualised fortification may result in a slight increase in flank skinfold thickness (MD 1.0 [0.21, 1.99] mm, p = 0.02), but little to no effect on mid-arm circumference (MD 0.27 [−0.69, 1.23] cm, p = 0.58), subscapular skinfold (MD – 0.00 [−0.76, 0.76] mm, p = 1.00) and triceps skinfold (MD −0.10 [−0.69, 0.49] mm, p = 0.74) at end of intervention, but evidence for this is very uncertain due to small sample size, wide CI and study limitations.

Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia

Individualised fortification may result in a slight reduction in BPD (4 RCTs, 215 infants, RR 0.82 [0.62, 1.08], p = 0.16, I2 = 0%, low certainty due to small sample size, and wide CI) (online supple. file 4, Fig. 6).

Feeding Intolerance

Evidence is very uncertain about the effect of individualised fortification on feeding intolerance (5 RCTs, 255 infants, RR 0.93 [0.65, 1.33], p = 0.70, I2 = 28%, very low certainty) due to small sample size, wide CI and inconsistency (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 7). Tiew et al. [32] and Ahuja et al. [21] found no significant difference between the two groups.

Sepsis

Evidence is very uncertain about the effect of individualised fortification on sepsis (5 RCTs, 301 infants, RR 0.91 [0.57, 1.45], p = 0.68, I2 = 0%, very low certainty) due to small sample size, wide CI and inconsistency (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 8).

Time to Full Enteral Feeds

Khorana et al. [24] showed little to no effect on time to full enteral feeds with individualised fortification (MD 0.67 [−4.16, 5.50] days, p = 0.79, low certainty due to small sample size and wide CI). Arslanoglu et al. [22] found that it took 9.5 (26) days (median [range]) for the individualised group to reach full feeds and 11 (24) days for the standardised group.

Retinopathy of Prematurity

Evidence is very uncertain about the effect of individualised fortification on ROP (2 RCTs, 87 infants, RR 1.00 [0.45, 2.23], p = 1.00, I2 = 0%, very low certainty) due to small sample size, wide CI and inconsistency (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 9).

Subgroup Analysis

There was no significant interaction between the individualised fortification method (targeted versus adjusted) and length gain (mm/week) (p = 0.92 for interaction), weight gain (g/kg/day) (p = 0.41 for interaction), head circumference (mm/week) (p = 0.16 for interaction), and weight (g/day) (p = 0.12) (online suppl. file 4, Fig. 10a–d). Tiew et al. [32] found that infants <30 weeks’ gestation had significantly more rapid mean head circumference gain (mean [SD] 8.89 [2.31] individualised vs. 6.37 [2.1] mm/week standardised, p = 0.017), weight gain (23.3 [4.6] individualised vs. 18.9 [4.8] g/kg/day standardised, p = 0.039), and length gain (12.53 [4.9] individualised vs. 8.54 [3.08] mm/week standardised, p = 0.042) during intervention with individualised fortification.

Due to insufficient data, we were unable to undertake other preplanned subgroup analyses of targeted versus adjusted fortification, birth weight, gestational age, and donor versus mothers’ own milk. We were also unable to conduct sensitivity analysis for outcomes with significant heterogeneity by excluding studies at high risk of bias, as there were so few studies.

Certainty of Evidence (GRADE Assessment)

The certainty of evidence was assessed as low for duration of initial hospital stay (days) and very low for mortality, NEC, and neurodevelopmental outcomes (Table 2). There were no data for the outcomes of fully breastmilk feeding at discharge and adverse effects, and the outcomes reported for body composition/faltering growth were too heterogeneous to be graded together.

Discussion

Summary of Main Results

This systematic review included 575 infants in 12 studies investigating the effect of individualised versus standardised fortification. This review found that individualised fortification likely increases head circumference, weight and length gain, as well as fat-free mass, and may increase fat mass and slightly reduce BPD for preterm infants at the end of intervention. Evidence is very uncertain about the effect on mortality, NEC, neurodevelopment, feeding intolerance, sepsis and ROP. Individualised fortification may have little to no effect on duration of hospital stay and time to full enteral feeds.

Consistent with previous research demonstrating the effects of individualised fortification, our review suggests increases in incremental growth outcomes. At 18-month follow-up, individualised fortification may have little to no effect on weight, length, and head circumference, though the evidence is very uncertain. Subgroup interactions comparing adjustable versus targeted fortification were statistically significant under a fixed-effect model but were no longer significant when analysed using a random-effects model. However, one study reported greater benefits of individualised fortification for very preterm and extremely preterm infants.

Possible Mechanisms

There are several mechanisms through which individualised fortification might increase growth outcomes in preterm infants. Regarding adjusted fortification, BUN concentrations are reflective of metabolic response. Despite BUN being influenced by fluid balance, immature glomerular filtration, and renal function, it has a highly positive correlation with actual protein intake once full enteral feeding has been established [33]. Protein plays a pivotal role in growth, as amino acids are the building blocks to tissue synthesis and stimulate key sites in metabolic pathways [34].

Targeted fortification is designed to account for variation in breastmilk [35] but assumes the exact requirements of each infant are known and that random analyses of breastmilk macronutrients are an accurate and reliable measurement of actual intake [36]. In practice, macronutrient requirements of very preterm infants remain uncertain, and human milk composition varies widely [37, 38]. Consequently, single or random milk analyses may not reliably reflect true intake, limiting the precision of targeted fortification. The observed potential reductions in neonatal morbidity, such as BPD, may reflect more optimal protein and energy intake [39], supporting organ maturation, and immune function [40].

Potential adverse effects of fortification should also be considered. Increasing protein and energy intake may lead to accelerated growth or excessive protein exposure, linked to metabolic syndrome [41], and cognitive impairment [42]; however, this evidence is of low certainty. Targeted fortification also requires breastmilk analysis, which may have psychosocial impacts if mothers perceive their milk as inadequate. These considerations highlight the need for careful communication and monitoring in individualised fortification.

Strengths and Limitations of This Review

Overall Completeness and Applicability of Evidence

The evidence included in this review directly addresses most of the pre-specified outcomes, including the primary outcome of mortality. No studies reported breastmilk feeding and adverse effects, limiting insight into these important factors. Included studies were from 10 countries; therefore, the findings may be applicable across different cultural contexts. No studies were conducted in low-income countries, and only one study performed follow-up on long-term outcomes.

Quality of the Evidence

Most studies were low or unclear risk of bias in some domains. Some studies either did not report or did not blind outcome assessors. While lack of blinding is less of an issue for outcomes with objective measures such as mortality, it may introduce bias for outcomes susceptible to assessor influence. Additionally, NEC was reported using author-defined criteria, likely including both medical and surgical cases. As medical NEC is subjective, it may be susceptible to ascertainment bias, so results should be interpreted cautiously. Some studies had unclear funding sources, precluding assessment of potential conflicts of interest. Future studies should transparently report funding and methodology to strengthen the robustness of conclusions.

The certainty of evidence was low for duration of initial hospital stay, and very low for mortality, neurodevelopment, and NEC attributed to insufficient sample sizes and wide CIs. Heterogeneity for some outcomes was able to be explored through subgroup analysis; however, none of these explained the heterogeneity. Subgroup analysis could only be explored for the type of individualised fortification method, and not age or weight because there was substantial overlap between infant characteristics within studies.

Quality of the Review

A 2020 systematic review [43] found individualised fortification likely increases weight gain and may increase length gain and head circumference. It found uncertain effect on the incidence of mortality, BPD, NEC, sepsis, ROP, and length of hospital stay [43]. Compared to the previous systematic review, which included 6 studies directly comparing individualised versus standardised fortification, our review includes 12 studies, one of which has 18-month follow-up data. Although the morbidity outcomes from Kadıoğlu Şimşek et al. [31] were included in this previous review, we excluded it from the present analysis. The morbidities reported in the trial were measured at the point of randomisation, prior to any exposure to the intervention. Because our review focused on post-intervention clinical outcomes, the study did not provide outcome data that could be used to assess the intervention effects. This difference in outcome timing likely explains discrepancies in study selection between our review and earlier syntheses. We used a broad search strategy, ensuring all relevant studies were included and that we were able to obtain data from all identified studies. We were able to evaluate whether there were potential harms and adverse effects of individualised fortification to aid healthcare decision-making.

A key limitation is that we were unable to carry out subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis for some outcomes with significant heterogeneity due to the few reported studies. For growth outcomes, reporting varied widely across studies, with different units used, making it difficult to synthesise the data. This variability reduced the certainty of the evidence and highlights the need for standardised outcome reporting [44]. Future research may consider conducting a cost-effectiveness analysis given the costly nature of the intervention.

Clinical and Research Implications

Clinicians may consider individualised fortification to optimise early growth in preterm infants. However, given the low certainty of evidence for several outcomes, decisions should balance potential benefits with resource implications and feasibility. Future research should focus on high-quality RCTs with standardised fortification protocols and longer term follow-up to clarify effects on growth, neurodevelopment, and major clinical outcomes, particularly in very preterm and extremely preterm populations.

While this review compares individualised and standardised fortification, evidence supporting fortification itself remains limited for key clinical outcomes. An important unresolved question is which infants benefit most from fortification, which may depend on gestational age, birth weight, or nutritional risk.

Conclusions

This review demonstrates that individualised fortification likely increases weight, head circumference and length gain, as well as fat-free mass, but whether this equates to improved clinical and functional health benefits is unknown. Effects on mortality, neurodevelopment, NEC, feeding intolerance and sepsis remain uncertain. Individualised fortification likely increases incremental growth in preterm infants, but whether this equates to important clinical and long-term functional outcomes remains unknown.
