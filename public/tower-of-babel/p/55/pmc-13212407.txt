Comments on, “Effect of class IV LASER therapy along with low-dye kinesio-taping technique or plantar fascia stretching on pain, pain pressure threshold, and disability in individuals with plantar fasciitis: double-blinded randomized clinical trial”
Authors: Pınar Özge Başaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis Comment critically evaluates methodological aspects of a randomized clinical trial investigating Class IV LASER therapy combined with Low-Dye kinesio-taping or plantar fascia stretching in plantar fasciitis. Key concerns regarding blinding, inclusion criteria, intervention comparability, and outcome measures are discussed. Clarification of these issues may improve the validity of the reported findings and inform future research.

Dear Editor

I read with interest the randomized clinical trial by Yadav et al. entitled “Effect of class IV LASER therapy along with Low-dye kinesio-taping technique or plantar fascia stretching on pain, pain pressure threshold, and disability in individuals with plantar fasciitis: Double-blinded randomized clinical trial” published in your journal [1]. The authors are to be commended for addressing a common and clinically relevant musculoskeletal condition. Nevertheless, several methodological aspects of the study warrant further consideration.

The study is described as a double-blinded randomized clinical trial. However, based on the methodological description, blinding appears to have been limited to outcome assessment. Therefore, the study design may be more accurately described as a single-blind randomized controlled trial [2].

Several established risk factors and potential confounders associated with plantar fasciitis were not clearly addressed in the inclusion or exclusion criteria. Factors such as obesity, occupations requiring prolonged standing, and high-level or professional sports participation are known to increase mechanical loading on the plantar fascia and may independently influence pain and disability outcomes [3]. Furthermore, uncontrolled diabetes, prior foot surgery, congenital foot deformities, and recent corticosteroid injections were not clearly specified [4].

Clarification is also needed regarding symptom chronicity. Participants were included if symptoms persisted for more than four weeks. However, plantar fasciitis is generally considered a chronic condition when symptoms last for at least three months [5]. A four-week threshold more closely reflects an acute or subacute phase [6]. Importantly, the study does not report the actual duration of symptoms among participants, nor does it account for symptom duration as a potential confounding factor in the analysis. Grouping individuals with substantially different symptom durations may have influenced treatment responsiveness, particularly given the short intervention period.

The manuscript further states that participants underwent physical, radiological, and investigatory assessments; however, it is unclear whether imaging modalities were systematically performed in all participants. Although the Foot Function Index is a valid and widely used outcome measure, it consists of three distinct subscales: pain, disability, and activity limitation [7]. Separate analysis of these subscales could have provided more detailed insight into which functional domains were most responsive to the interventions. Additionally, incorporating objective outcome measures such as functional performance tests or ultrasonographic assessment of plantar fascia thickness could have strengthened the robustness of the findings. However, it is also important to consider that the study’s sample size may not have been sufficiently powered to support multiple outcome measures and detailed subscale analyses.

An additional methodological issue concerns the exercise intervention. Stretching and strengthening exercises targeting the calf muscles and plantar fascia are widely regarded as a cornerstone of conservative plantar fasciitis management and are commonly provided to all participants in clinical trials [8]. In this study, however, stretching exercises were administered only to one group, while the other group did not receive an equivalent baseline exercise program. This imbalance may have compromised comparability between groups and confounded the interpretation of treatment effects.

Intervention dosing also warrants clarification. The intervention duration was relatively short (two weeks), and the study excluded an intention-to-treat analysis. Although Class IV LASER therapy was administered every other day and kinesio-taping three days per week, the exact number of sessions for each intervention was not clearly specified. Moreover, the frequency and “dose” of adjunctive interventions differed between groups, potentially acting as a confounding factor. It is also unclear whether participants were permitted to use non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for acute pain management during the study period, which could have influenced pain-related outcomes.

Finally, caution is warranted when attributing the observed clinical improvements to LASER therapy itself. As both groups received Class IV LASER therapy, the study design does not allow isolation of its independent effects. Conclusions should therefore be interpreted within the context of combined interventions rather than LASER monotherapy. Additionally, although the stretching group demonstrated more favorable outcomes, future research recommendations focused primarily on taping-based adjuncts may not be fully aligned with the observed results.
