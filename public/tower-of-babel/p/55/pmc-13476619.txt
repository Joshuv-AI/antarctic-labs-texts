Engineered GLUT1-targeted STING polyproagonists: Redox-triggered activation and enhanced endosomal escape for cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Arsalan Raza, Anum Kayani, Guopu Huang, Cheng Li, Qinghao Zhou, Khurshed Bozorov, Yuanyuan Ji, Zhishen Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway plays a critical role in bridging innate and adaptive antitumor immunity, representing a promising target in cancer immunotherapy. However, the clinical application of STING agonists is limited by poor pharmacokinetics, low cytosolic delivery efficiency, and immune-related adverse effects. To address these challenges, we developed STING polyproagonist nanoparticles (named GA+S@SR) by self-assembly of an amphiphilic diblock copolymer, P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA, combined with POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA). The galactose (GA) moieties enable targeted delivery to glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) on tumor cells, facilitating cellular internalization. The P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) segments promote endosomal escape, followed by the release of the disulfide-linked SR-717 in the cytosol under reducing conditions. This leads to robust activation of the STING pathway, resulting in dendritic cell maturation, enhanced T-cell infiltration, and potent antitumor immunity. Furthermore, when combined with an αPD-L1, this polyproagonist synergistically enhances the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade, effectively inhibiting primary and distant tumors by counteracting immune evasion. This study highlights the potential of STING polyproagonists in achieving effective cytosolic delivery of STING agonists to boost antitumor immunity and overcome current limitations associated with STING immunotherapy.Keywords: Immunotherapy, STING agonist, GLUT1-targeting, Redox-triggered activation, Endosomal escape

Introduction

Immunotherapy has emerged as a highly promising strategy in cancer treatment, showing significant potential in preventing tumor metastasis and recurrence [[1], [2], [3]]. However, its efficacy remains limited in many patients due to insufficient T-cell infiltration and the presence of an immunosuppressive “cold” tumor microenvironment [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. The limitation underscores the need for novel immunomodulators to effectively transform immune-excluded tumors into T-cell-inflamed phenotypes [8]. Notably, the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway has garnered widespread attention for its ability to bridge innate and adaptive antitumor immunity [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Activation of STING signaling promotes T-cell infiltration into tumors through a cascade involving TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and phosphorylation of interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) [[18], [19]]. The nuclear translocation of IRF3 stimulates the production of type I interferons (IFNs) and the secretion of pro-inflammatory chemokines, leading to dendritic cells (DCs) maturation and enhanced antigen presentation [[19], [20], [21]]. Various STING agonists, including cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs), metal ions (e.g., Mn²⁺, Zn²⁺) and small molecules, have shown promising immunotherapeutic effects [[22], [23]]. However, their clinical translation is hampered by poor bioavailability and short half-life after systemic administration [24], as well as the challenge of traversing physiological barriers to reach the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where the STING proteins are localized [25].

SR-717 is a synthetic non-nucleotide small-molecule STING agonist with favorable drug-like properties, structurally mimicking the natural ligand cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate (cGAMP). It interacts with human STING, showing an inhibitory concentration 50 (IC50) of 7.8 µM and inducing interferon signaling at an effective concentration 50 (EC50) of ∼2.1 µM, indicating its potency as a non-nucleotide STING agonist [[26], [27]]. Nevertheless, like other small-molecule agonists, it also faces issues of low cytosolic delivery efficiency and lack of tumor specificity, which may lead to off-target activation and systemic cytokine release [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]]. These risks highlight the urgent need for delivery strategies that enhance tumor-specific accumulation and minimize extra-tumoral STING activation. Nanotechnology offers a viable approach to improve the delivery and therapeutic index of STING agonists such as SR-717. For example, Zhou et al. utilized metal-organic framework (MOF) nanoparticles to achieve light-triggered release of SR-717, augmenting photodynamic-immunotherapy [33]. Zhao et al. designed a near-infrared II (NIR-II) responsive MOF photosensitizer loaded with the STING agonist SR-717 to induce ferroptosis and STING activation, enhancing tumor immunotherapy [34]. Li et al. developed a T-cell-membrane-coated nanoplatform for the co-delivery of a photothermal agents and SR-717 [35]. Huang et al. also reported ER-targeting polyprodrug nanoparticles for photoactivatable cytosolic delivery of SR-717 [36]. Furthermore, Fu et al. developed cisplatin polyprodrug nanoparticles loaded with the STING agonist SR-717, achieving synergistic chemo-immunotherapy for ovarian cancer [37]. Despite these advances, most current systems rely on physical encapsulation, which often results in low drug loading, premature leakage, and insufficient endosomal escape, limiting cytosolic bioavailability [38]. A promising alternative is to design polymer-drug conjugates connected by stimuli-responsive linkers to obtain the so-called “polyproagonists” [[39], [40]]. To our knowledge, a covalently conjugated, tumor-targeting polyproagonist system aimed to improve the biodistribution and pharmacokinetics of SR-717 has not yet been reported.

Herein, we report a rational design of a STING-targeting polyproagonists to enhance the cytosolic delivery of SR-717. An amphiphilic block copolymer, poly[(oligo(ethylene glycol) methacrylate-co-galactose methacrylate)]-b-[2-((2-hydroxyethyl) disulfanyl) ethyl methacrylate-SR-717], abbreviated as P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA), was synthesized via a reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) polymerization reaction [41]. The hydrophilic block P(OEGMA-co-GAMA) contains galactose (GA) residues for targeting glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), is often overexpressed on tumor cells. The hydrophobic block PSSRMA contains SR-717 conjugated through a glutathione (GSH)-cleavable disulfide bond. This copolymer was co-assembled with the endosomolytic polymer POEGMA-b-poly(2-(diethylamino)ethyl methacrylate-co‑butyl methacrylate) [POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA)] to form nanoparticles designated as GA+S@SR (Fig. 1). The P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) segment facilitates endosomal escape under acidic conditions [[42], [43]], promoting the cytosolic release of SR-717 following disulfide reduction. The resulting STING polyproagonist nanoparticles induce potent and tumor-localized immune activation, with minimal off-target effects. When combined with an anti-programmed cell death ligand 1 antibody (αPD-L1), this system synergistically enhances the immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) efficacy, effectively inhibiting the growth of primary and distant tumors. This work highlights a robust nanoplatform for spatiotemporally controlled delivery of STING agonists, offering a potent strategy to reinvigorate antitumor immunity.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fetal bovine serum (FBS), (RPMI-1640), Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), phosphate buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.4), and trypsin were purchased from Gibco Company (USA) and used as received. Cell Counting Kit-8, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit, the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end-labeling (TUNEL) cell apoptosis detection kit, and 2-(4-Amidinophenyl)-6-indolecarbamidine dihydrochloride (DAPI), 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR), Nile Red, LysoTracker green FM staining solution were purchased from Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). αPD-L1 was purchased from Bioxcell (Ohio, USA), Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits of interleukin-6 (IL-6), interferon-β (IFN-β) and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), antibodies against CD11c, CD80, CD86, CD3, CD8, CD4 for flow cytometry were all obtained from Southern Biotechnology Associates, Inc. All other commercially available solvents and reagents were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. and Energy Chemical (Shanghai, China) and used as received. Female BALB/c mice at the age of 5–6 weeks were purchased from GemPharmatec Co. Ltd. All the animal experiments were performed in compliance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) established by the Health Science Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University. The case number of ethics is 2021–1080. 4T1 cells were obtained from Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). And the 4T1 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS at 37 °C under 5% CO2.

Synthesis of monomers and polymers

The detailed synthetic routes of 1,2:3,4-di-O-isopropylidene-d-galactopyranose methacrylate (GAMA), 6-hyrdohexyl methacrylate linked SR-717 (CSRMA), and 2-((2- hydroxyethyl) sulfanyl)ethyl methacrylate linked SR-717 (SSRMA), poly(oligo(ethylene glycol) methacrylate (POEGMA), poly(oligo(ethylene glycol) methacrylate-b-(2-((2-hydroxyethyl) disulfanyl) ethyl methacrylate-SR-717) (POEGMA-b-PSSRMA), poly(oligo(ethylene glycol) methacrylate-co-galactose methacrylate)-b-(6-hydrohexylmethacrylate-SR-717) (P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PCSRMA), and POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) have been shown in supplementary material (Fig. S1). 1H NMR and ESI-MS spectra of the monomers together with 1H NMR spectra of the polymers, have been shown in (Figs. S2-S10).

Synthesis of P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)

Oligo(ethylene glycol) methacrylate (OEGMA) (1.62 g, 5.42 mmol, 40 equiv.), GAMA (0.66 g, 2.02 mmol, 15 equiv.), and 2,2´-azobis (2-methylpropionitrile) (AIBN) (3.2 mg, 0.02 mmol, 0.14 equiv.) were dissolved in 5 mL anhydrous 1,4-dioxane, and the resulting solution was transferred into a Schlenk flask. The mixture underwent degassing through three freeze-pump-thaw cycles, after which the flask was sealed under vacuum. The reaction was conducted in a 70 °C oil bath for 24 h to facilitate polymerization. Upon completion, the polymerization was quenched by immersion in liquid nitrogen. The polymer was precipitated into cold diethyl ether, collected by centrifugation, and subsequently purified by repeated dissolution-precipitation cycles. The final product, P(OEGMA-co-GAMA), was dried under vacuum to yield a pink sticky solid (1.22 g, yield: 53.1%, Mn = 11.8 kDa, and Mw/Mn = 1.16). The degrees of polymerization (DPs) for OEGMA and GAMA components were estimated to be approximately 27 and 10, respectively, as determined by 1HNMR analysis (Fig. S11).

Synthesis of P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA

P(OEGMA-co-GAMA) (0.4 g, 0.034 mmol, 1 equiv.), SSRMA (0.56 g, 1.01 mmol, 30 equiv.), and AIBN (0.8 mg, 0.0048 mmol, 0.14 equiv.) were dissolved in 5 ml anhydrous 1,4-dioxane. The resulting solution was transferred into a Schlenk flask. The mixture was subjected to degassing through three freeze-pump-thaw cycles, after which the flask was sealed under vacuum. The reaction was conducted in a 70 °C oil bath for 24 h to facilitate polymerization. Upon completion, the polymerization was quenched by immersion in liquid nitrogen. Trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, 5 ml) was introduced to the mixture, which was then stirred for 2 h. Subsequently, the mixture was dialyzed sequentially against N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) and water to eliminate small molecules (MWCO, 3500 Da). The dialyzed solution was then freeze-dried to yield the desired product P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA (0.48 g, yield: 50.2%, Mn = 21.8 kDa, Mw/Mn = 1.10). DP of PSSRMA was determined to be 18 according to the 1H NMR analysis (Fig. S12).

Synthesis of P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-P(SSRMA-co-AEMA)

P(OEGMA-co-GAMA) (0.1 g, 0.0044 mmol, 1 equiv.), SSRMA (72 mg, 0.13 mmol, 30 equiv.), 2-aminoethyl methacrylate hydrochloride (AEMA) (2 mg, 0.014 mmol, 3 equiv.), and AIBN (0.1 mg, 0.0006 mmol, 0.14 equiv.) were dissolved in 3 ml anhydrous 1,4-dioxane. The mixture was subjected to degassing through three freeze-pump-thaw cycles, after which the flask was sealed under vacuum. The reaction was conducted in a 70 °C oil bath for 24 h to facilitate polymerization. Upon completion, the polymerization was quenched by immersion in liquid nitrogen. TFA (5 ml) was introduced to the mixture, which was then stirred for 2 h. Subsequently, the mixture was dialyzed sequentially against DMF and water to eliminate small molecules (MWCO, 3500 Da). The dialyzed solution was then freeze-dried to yield the desired product P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-P(SSRMA-co-AEMA) (0.098 g, yield: 56.3%).

Synthesis of P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-P(SSRMA-co-AEMA-IR820)

P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-P(SSRMA-co-AEMA) (50 mg), IR820 (3 mg, 0.0037 mmol, 1 equiv relative to amine groups), and triethylamine (1.0 ml, 7.19 mmol) were dissolved in dry DMF (3 ml). Subsequently, under N₂ protection, the mixture was reacted for 6 h at 40 °C. After 6 h, the mixture was dialyzed against DMF and water. The solution was lyophilized to get P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-P(SSRMA-co-AEMA-IR820) (0.04 g, yield: 79%).

Self-assembly of STING polyproagonists

Nanoprecipitation method was used to fabricate the STING polyproagonists nanoparticles. For GA+S@SR, 1:1 (w/w) ratio of P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA and POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and then added quickly into a stirred solution of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4, 5 ml). Dialysis (MWCO, 3500 Da) against PBS was used to remove DMSO yielding the GA+S@SR nanoparticles. Next, P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PCSRMA and POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) were used to prepare the GSH-nonresponsive GA+C@SR nanoparticles. Similarly, POEGMA-b-PSSRMA and POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) were self-assembled to fabricate the non-galactosylated S@SR nanoparticles. For in vitro cellular uptake analysis, Nile red loaded micelles S@SR, GA+C@SR and GA+S@SR were prepared via self-assembly. For the endosomal escape study, a Nile red-loaded control micelle OEG+S@SR was obtained via the self-assembly of P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA. For in vitro immunostimulatory activity evaluation, a single-polymer control micelle composed of P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA was assembled and denoted as C@PSSR. Similarly, for in vivo investigation of biodistribution, IR-820 labelled micelles (IR-820+S@SR, IR-820+GA+S@SR) were prepared during self-assembly.

Drug release profiles

The dialysis diffusion method was used to quantify the release of SR-717 from STING polyproagonists. Dithiothreitol (DTT) was selected as a substitute for GSH to simulate intracellular redox conditions in the current study. Briefly, GA+S@SR, GA+C@SR and S@SR (1 ml) were loaded in separate dialysis bags (MWCO, 3500 Da) and immersed in PBS (8 ml, 10 mM DTT) at 37 °C. After taking the medium (2 ml) at different time points, the same volume of medium was replenished. The released SR-717 concentration was measured by ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy and a standard curve was used to calculate the drug release rate.

Cellular uptake

4T1 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells per dish and cultured overnight. The cells were then treated with Nile red-loaded micelles at an equivalent SR-717 concentration of 20 μg/ml and incubated for 2, 6 or 12 h. Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min) and stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (20 min) to visualize nuclei. The cells were analyzed by confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). For flow cytometry analysis, the cells were seeded in 12-well plates at the density of 1 × 106 cells per dish and treated with micelles (SR-717-equivalent concentration, 20 µg/ml) and incubated for 1, 2, 4 or 6 h. After washing with PBS, the cells were treated with trypsin for 1 min, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Moreover, to investigate the GLUT1-dependent internalization of STING polyproagonists, the same protocols were followed for CLSM and flow cytometry analysis after pretreatment with a panel of uptake inhibitors. Specifically, 4T1 cells and RAW macrophages 264.7 were pre-treated with phloretin (100 µM, GLUT1 inhibitor), 2-deoxy-d-glucose (50 mM, glycolysis inhibitor), chlorpromazine (10 µg/ml, a clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor), and nystatin (25 µg/ml, a caveolae-mediated endocytosis inhibitor) for 30 min at 37 °C. A control group without inhibitor was also included.

Endosomal escape

CLSM was used to investigate the endosomal escape capability of STING polyproagonists. The Lysotracker green probe was used to stain lysosomes and visualize the distribution of STING polyproagonist nanoparticles. Briefly, 4T1 cells were incubated into a 6-well plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well for 24 h. Next, the media was replaced by DMEM containing Nile red-loaded OEG+S@SR and GA+S@SR at an equivalent SR-717 concentration of 20 μg/ml in each well. After 0.5, 1.5 and 3 h of incubation, the medium was removed and lysotracker probe (200 nM) was added to counterstain the endosomal compartments. After 10 min of incubation, the cells were washed three times with cold PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with DAPI for 20 min to observe the nuclei. The cells were then examined using CLSM. ImageJ software was used to measure colocalization efficiency of Nile red-loaded OEG+S@SR and GA+S@SR in terms of Pearson’s coefficient.

in vitro immunostimulatory activity

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to determine the EC50 of STING polyproagonist formulations via quantifying the interferon-β (IFN-β) secretion. Briefly, 4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight. The cells were then treated with STING polyproagonists GA+S@SR with endosomal escape component and C@PSSR without endosomal escape component at different SR-717 concentrations and incubated for 24 h. After that, the supernatants were collected, and mouse IFN-β ELISA kit was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured, and IFN-β concentrations were plotted against the logarithm of SR-717-equivalent concentration to get the Dose-dependent curves. GraphPad Prism was used to obtain the EC50 values.

Western blot analysis

4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with different formulations (PBS, SR-717, S@SR, GA+C@SR, and GA+S@SR). After 12 h of incubation, the cells were collected and lysed using lysis buffer to extract total cellular proteins. Afterwards, quantification and denaturation of cellular proteins was performed. Sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) at fixed voltage was used to separate the proteins (25 μg). Lastly, Image J software was used to analyze p-IRF3 and P-STING proteins.

Cytotoxicity

4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight. The cells were then treated with STING polyproagonists at different SR-717 concentrations and incubated for 24 h. After that, the cells were incubated with 100 μl cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) solution for 30 min. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and the relative cell viability was calculated. Similarly, cytotoxicity assay was performed to observe the cytotoxicity of SR-717 by using 4T1 cells.

Evaluation of in vitro DCs stimulation and STING pathway activation

Bone marrow-derived immature dendritic cells (BMDiDCs) were isolated from female BALB/c mice. Specifically, the bone marrow was obtained by flushing femur and tibia with PBS. After the lysis of red blood cells, cells were seeded in cell culture dishes with RPMI 1640 medium (10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 20 ng/ml of GM-CSF). On Day 3, the supernatant medium (5 ml) was removed, and an additional fresh medium (5 ml) was added to the culture dishes. On Day 5, half of the culture supernatant was harvested and centrifuged. The cell pellet was resuspended in fresh culture medium (5 ml). On Day 7, the non-adherent and loosely adherent cells were collected for bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs). For DC stimulation evaluation: supernatant from 4T1 cells treated with: (i) PBS, (ii) SR-717, (iii) S@SR, (iv) GA+C@SR, and (v) GA+S@SR (20 μg/ml SR-717-equivalent) were added. After that, DCs were stained with anti-mouse CD11c, FITC-anti-mouse CD80, and PE-anti-mouse CD86, and then evaluated by flow cytometry. The supernatants were collected and the tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and IFN-β secretion levels were quantified by using ELISA kits.

Tumor models and in vivo biodistribution

4T1 tumor models were established by injecting 4T1 cells (1 × 106) subcutaneously into the left flanks of BALB/c mice. An in vivo imaging system (IVIS) was used to monitor the tumor accumulation of STING polyproagonist nanoparticles by tracking IR-820 fluorescence. When the tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3, IR-820-labeled micelles (IR-820+S@SR, IR-820 +GA+S@SR) were intravenously injected into mice at the dose of 100 μl (0.5 mg/ml). Subsequently, IVIS was used to track the fluorescence signals over time. After 24 h, major organs such as heart, lung, kidney, liver, spleen and tumors were collected for ex vivo IVIS imaging. To evaluate the pharmacokinetics, IR-820+S@SR and IR-820+GA+S@SR at the dose of 100 μl (0.5 mg/ml) were injected intravenously into mouse tail vein. At fixed time intervals of 0, 2, 4, 6, 12 and 24 h, the blood was collected from the eyes orbit. Finally, IVIS was used to analyze the fluorescence intensity of IR-820 in the blood to evaluate the pharmacokinetics.

in vivo antitumor efficacy and immunity activation

When tumor volume reached ∼100 mm³, the mice (n = 30) were allocated into six treatment groups, (G1) PBS, (G2) SR-717, (G3) S@SR, (G4) GA+C@SR+αPD-L1, (G5) GA+S@SR and (G6) GA+S@SR+αPD-L1. On Day 0, 2 and 4, the mice were injected with different treatments at previously established SR-717-equivalent dose of 20 mg/kg body weight [33]. For αPD-L1 groups, an PD-L1 antibody dose of 5 mg/kg body weight were injected intraperitoneally on Day 1, 3 and 5. The body weights and tumor volumes of mice were recorded every other day. The results in all experiments were expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical calculation of experimental data was performed using the One-way and Two-way ANOVA statistical analysis through GraphPad prism. Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of sting polyproagonists

Polyprodrugs that have been proposed by Liu et al. have gained increasing attention for targeted and controlled drug delivery due to their ability to prevent drug leakage, achieve responsive controlled release, and form diverse assembly morphologies [44]. To specifically target the overexpressed GLUT1 in the tumor microenvironment and promote the effective cytosolic delivery of STING agonists to reprogram immunosuppressive tumors, we designed STING “polyproagonist” nanoparticles capable of endosomal escape and GSH-triggered SR-717 release. We first synthesized several functional monomers: GAMA, which contains GA moieties for GLUT1 targeting; SSRMA, a GSH-responsive monomer with disulfide-linked SR-717; and CSRMA, a non-cleavable control monomer linked to SR-717 without a GSH-sensitive bond. The synthetic route of the GSH-responsive monomer SSRMA began with 2-hydroxyethyl disulfide, which reacted with methacryloyl chloride to form the intermediate 2-((2- hydroxyethyl) sulfanyl)ethyl methacrylate. SR-717 was then conjugated with the hydroxyl group of 2-((2- hydroxyethyl) sulfanyl)ethyl methacrylate to yield SSRMA (Fig. S1 and 2A). Similarly, 6-hyrdohexyl methacrylate was synthesized by coupling methacryloyl chloride with hexanediol, followed by SR-717 conjugation to obtain the nonresponsive monomer CSRMA. The targeting monomer GAMA was prepared by modifying 1,2:3,4-di-O-isopropylidene-d-galactopyranose with methacryloyl chloride [55] (detailed synthesis procedure is provided in Fig. S1). Successful monomer synthesis was confirmed by 1HNMR and ESI-MS analysis (Fig. S2–S6).

RAFT polymerization was used to prepare well-defined controlled block copolymers from these monomers, as shown in Fig. S1. 2-Cyano-2-propyl benzodithioate was used as the chain transfer agent, and AIBN served as the initiator. We prepared four well-defined diblock copolymers: non-galactosylated POEGMA-b-PSSRMA, GSH-nonresponsive P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PCSRMA, endosomolytic POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA), and GSH-responsive target polymer P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA. Each copolymer was synthesized through a two-step polymerization process. For POEGMA-b-PSSRMA, the first block, POEGMA, was synthesized as a macro-RAFT agent, followed by the polymerization with SSRMA (Fig. S7). The degree of DP of OEGMA and SSRMA in POEGMA-b-PSSRMA were estimated to be 24 and 14, respectively, from its 1H NMR spectrum (Fig. S8). For the synthesis of P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PCSRMA, the first block P(OEGMA-co-GAMA) was synthesized as a macro-RAFT agent, followed by the polymerization with CSRMA (Fig. S9). The DPs of OEGMA, GAMA, and CSRMA were 27, 10 and 12, respectively, from the 1HNMR spectra (Fig. S10). Similarly, POEGMA was used as a macro-RAFT agent to polymerize with DEAEMA and BMA to prepare POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) (Fig. S11). 1HNMR of P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA in Fig. S12 and 2B demonstrated its successful synthesis with a drug content of up to 47% SR-717. The DPs of OEGMA, GAMA and SSRMA were 27, 10 and 18, respectively, as calculated from the 1HNMR spectrum.

The GPC traces revealed a well-defined P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA block copolymer with a relatively narrow dispersity (Mw/Mn =1.30) (Fig. 2C and 2D). Moreover, the shift of the elution peak of the copolymer relative to the macroRAFT agent confirmed the successful synthesis of a controlled and well-defined polymer. GPC elution peaks and characterization details of other block copolymers have been given in Fig. 2C, 2D and S13. The incorporation of SR-717 into the copolymer was characterized by UV–Vis spectroscopy, which showed an absorbance peak at 286 nm corresponding to SR-717 (Fig. S14). Self-assembly and drug release

Due to their amphiphilic nature, the nanoprecipitation method was adopted as a self-assembly approach to fabricate the STING polyproagonist micelles. P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) segments in the block copolymer POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) promote endosomal escape. In P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA, GA residues were incorporated for GLUT1-mediated tumor targeting, and the SR-717 STING agonists were conjugated through GSH-cleavable disulfide bond. This co-assembly strategy combines precise tumor targeting with efficient cytosolic delivery of SR-717. In brief, P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA and POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) were dissolved in a 1:1 (w/w) mixture in DMSO and rapidly added to stirring PBS, yielding GA+S@SR micelles. Control micelles were prepared by using the same protocol. Specifically, the GSH non-responsive formulation (GA+C@SR) was obtained through the self-assembly of P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PCSRMA and POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA). Micelles lacking targeting ligands (S@SR) were obtained through the self-assembly of POEGMA-b-PSSRMA and POEGMA-b-P(DEAEMA-co-BMA). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed spherical morphology of these polyproagonist micelles as shown in Fig. 3A and S15. GA+S@SR showed an average hydrodynamic radius (Dh) of 122.2 nm with a narrow polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.27 in dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements (Fig. 3A). Similarly, the Dh values for GA+C@SR and S@SR were 130.1 and 105.7 nm, respectively (Fig. S15). S@SR, GA+C@SR, and GA+S@SR exhibited positive zeta potential values at pH 6.5 (Fig. S16). This positive surface charge can be attributed to the protonation of the tertiary amine groups in the DEAEMA units of the P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) segment. The pH-triggered cationization of the micelles is expected to enhance electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged endosomal membrane, thereby promoting membrane destabilization and endosomal escape. Therefore, the positive zeta potentials of all the polyproagonist micelles, confirmed the pH-responsive behavior of the DEAEMA segments.

The stability of the micelles was first evaluated in PBS and DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. After 48 h at 37 °C, GA+S@SR maintained consistent size and PDI in both media (Figs. 3B, 3C and S17), confirming its relatively high stability for subsequent applications. We then investigated the GSH-responsive release of SR-717. Considering that GA+S@SR and S@SR contain disulfide linkers, they were designed to release SR-717 through the disruption of micelle structure upon GSH stimulation. In contrast, DLS analysis confirmed a significant changes in the size distribution of both micelles after DTT treatment because of GSH-responsive micellar dissociation (Figs. 3D and S18). The release of SR-717 was determined by UV–Vis spectroscopy, using a standard calibration curve that showed a linear absorbance-concentration relationship (R² > 0.99) (Fig. S19). To this end, we used 10 mM DTT to mimic intracellular redox conditions (2–10 mM GSH). UV–Vis spectroscopy showed that in GA+S@SR without DTT, there was no absorption peak for SR-717 (286 nm), but a distinct peak appeared after 2 h of stimulation, confirming the cleavage of disulfide bonds and drug release (Fig. 3E). The LC-MS spectrum of the released molecule has been given in Fig. S20. Furthermore, the release profile indicated that approximately 80% of SR-717 was released from GA+S@SR within 24 h, while no significant release occurred in the absence of DTT (Fig. 3F). A similar pattern was observed for S@SR micelles (Fig. S21). Collectively, these findings suggest that GA+S@SR polyproagonist micelles possess favorable size, stability, and a significant GSH-responsive drug release behavior, supporting their potential in tumor-specific STING activation.

Cellular uptake and endosomal escape

GLUT1, as one of the most important members of the GLUT family, has been reported to be overexpressed in the triple-negative breast cancer cell lines [[45], [46], [47]]. Glycosylated STING polyproagonist micelles were designed to target the overexpressed GLUT1 receptors to enhance their accumulation within tumors and improve the cellular internalization efficiency. To analyze cellular internalization, STING polyproagonist micelles were co-incubated with 4T1 tumor cells. CLSM showed that 4T1 cells co-incubated with Nile red-loaded S@SR, GA+C@SR and GA+S@SR micelles exhibited significant intracellular red fluorescence, indicating high cellualr uptake efficiency (Fig. S22). However, GA+S@SR exhibited relatively higher cellular uptake, confirming enhanced uptake through GA-GLUT1 interactions. After 12 h of incubation, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the GA+C@SR and GA+S@SR groups increased by 1.15- and 1.27-fold, respectively, compared to the non-galactosylated micelle S@SR (Fig. S23). Flow cytometry data also confirmed these results, indicating that the fluorescence intensity of GA+C@SR and GA+S@SR gradually increased with longer incubation times (Fig. S24). These results confirmed that these STING polyproagonist micelles have potent tumor targeting toward 4T1 cells for enhanced cellular internalization capacity.

The internalization mechanism of Nile red-loaded GA+S@SR micelles was investigated by conducting cellular uptake studies on various inhibitors pre-treated 4T1 cells and RAW 264.7 macrophages, including phloretin, 2-deoxy-d-glucose, chlorpromazine and nystatin. Notably, the control group exhibited the maximum cellular uptake of Nile red-loaded GA+S@SR micelles (Fig. 4A-4D). This significant phenomenon is attributed to the higher expression of GLUT1 receptors on tumor cells, leading to accelerated transport. Among the inhibitors, phloretin showed the maximum inhibitory effect, confirming that the GLUT1-mediated pathway was the primary route for cellular uptake. Other inhibitors such as chlorpromazine and nystatin also moderately suppressed the uptake of NP. This suggests that the internalization of GA+S@SR is a sequential process, where GLUT1 serves as the initial docking site for recognition and binding, subsequently recruiting the standard energy-dependent endocytic machinery (clathrin and caveolae pathways) to facilitate the actual entry of the polyproagonists into the cells. Furthermore, the observed inhibitory effect of 2-deoxy-d-glucose underscores the energy dependence of this targeted delivery mechanism. Flow cytometry data also confirmed that these results, with the maximum fluorescence intensity of GA+S@SR in the control group (Fig. S25). This GLUT1 targeted tumor accumulation, the covalent conjugation of SR-717, and its GSH-responsive release minimized systemic exposure, and reduced off-target STING activation [48]. Overall, these results suggested that GA primarily participates in the recognition, binding and transport of GA+S@SR across glycolytic cells, in a GLUT1-dependent mechanism.

After endocytosis, the successful translocation of SR-717 into the cytoplasm requires effective endo/lysosomal escape [49]. As shown in Fig. 1B, the P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) segments can efficiently disrupt the endo/lysosomal membranes and promote the release of trapped STING polyproagonists through pH-responsive membrane destabilization effects [50]. Here, the membrane interactive hydrophobic BMA residues act synergistically with the cationic DEAEMA groups to promote effective endosomal membrane destabilization [51]. To verify this, we prepared the control micelle OEG+S@SR from P(OEGMA-co-GAMA)-b-PSSRMA. Furthermore, we used LysoTracker green to stain the endo/lysosome compartments and studied the endo/lysosomal escape capabilities of OEG+S@SR and GA+S@SR. OEG+S@SR and GA+S@SR exhibited bright yellow fluorescence after incubating with 4T1 cells for 0.5 h (Figs. 4E and S26). This yellow fluorescence was due to the overlap between the Nile red-loaded micelles and the LysoTracker green-staining of the endo/lysosome. Compared to OEG+S@SR, GA+S@SR exhibited an obviously lower colocalization with endo/lysosomes at 0.5 h, 1.5 h and 3 h. The variation in fluorescence intensity over time was also interpreted in terms of Pearson’s coefficient (Fig. 4F). After 0.5 h, GA+S@SR showed fluorescence corresponding to a Pearson’s coefficient of (∼0.52). However, after 3 h, the value of Perason’s correlation index significantly decreased to ∼0.34, unlike OEG+S@SR, which showed almost similar values of Perason’s correlation index at different time intervals. This reduction in the colocalization/overlapping index demonstrated that the P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) segments significantly imparted an intrinsic endo/lysosomal escaping tendency in the STING polyproagonists by pH-responsive membrane destabilization effect. These results suggested that GA+S@SR had relatively better endo/lysosome escaping capabilities compared to OEG+S@SR. Next, to quantitatively evaluate the contribution of the endosomolytic segments in STING activation, the EC50 values of C@PSSR and GA+S@SR were determined in 4T1 cells in a dose dependent manner (Fig. 4G). The results showed that the endosomolytic P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) segments enhanced the efficacy of STING activation, with GA+S@SR having an EC50 = 1.19 μM compared to C@PSSR’s EC50 = 2.23 μM. These results emphasize that the introduction of the endosome-escaping segments improved the overall STING activity.

The cytotoxicity of the STING polyproagonists on 4T1 cells was evaluated in vitro using the CCK-8 assay. Fig. S27 shows that the native SR-717 exhibited almost no cytotoxicity in 4T1 cells. Similarly, GA+S@SR also induced negligible cytotoxicity in these cells. Fig. S28 indicates that after treatment with different concentrations of S@SR, GA+C@SR and GA+S@SR, cell viability remained at about 90%. Therefore, the designed STING polyproagonists demonstrated relatively low cytotoxicity.

in vitro sting activation analysis

As the most potent antigen-presenting cells, DCs play a key role in initiating immune responses [52]. Given the established role of STING agonists in activating DC-mediated antitumor immunity via STING pathway [[53], [54]], we next evaluated the ability of various treatments to induce DC maturation. First, BMDCs were differentiated from precursors. Subsequently, 4T1 cells were co-incubated with PBS, SR-717, S@SR, GA+C@SR or GA+S@SR. The supernatant of these pre-incubated 4T1 cells was then collected to stimulate BMDCs for 36 h (Fig. 5A). After stimulation with tumor cells treated with GA+S@SR, 38.5% of DCs exhibited upregulation of maturation markers CD80 and CD86. The GA+S@SR group induced significant DC maturation, which may be attributed to enhanced cellular uptake and subsequent release of STING agonists in the cytoplasm. In contrast, cells treated with the S@SR formulation exhibited a moderately lower but still significant maturation level of 35%. Meanwhile, the maturation rates for the untreated group, free SR-717 group, and GA+C@SR group were 24.8%, 34.1% and 30.3%, respectively (Figs. 5B and S29). To further confirm the activation of the STING pathway, we quantified the levels of IFN-β and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6) in the BMDC supernatant using an ELISA kit. The GA+S@SR formulation significantly upregulated the expression of TNF-α, IFN-β and IL-6, outperforming the polymeric control formulation and achieving potent STING activation. Specifically, the expression levels of TNF-α, IFN-β and IL-6 in the GA+S@SR group were 1.92-fold, 10.2-fold and 1.9-fold higher than those in the untreated control, respectively (Fig. 5C-E). Subsequent Western blot analysis of cell proteins showed that 4T1 cells treated with GA+S@SR exhibited higher expression of p-STING and p-IRF3 proteins (Fig. 5F), indicating that GA+S@SR can more effectively activate the STING pathway [55]. Overall, these findings highlight the potential of GA+S@SR as an effective strategy for enhancing DC maturation and STING pathway activation.

in vivo antitumor efficacy

Inspired by the strong immune response in vitro, we continued to evaluate the therapeutic effects of STING polyproagonists on primary 4T1 breast tumors and determine whether the stimulated immune responses could also inhibit the growth of distant tumors. First, to assess their immunotherapeutic potential, we examined the tumor accumulation of these pro-agonists in vivo. We prepared IR-820-labeled micelles using a nano-precipitation method, namely IR-820+S@SR and IR-820+GA+S@SR, and monitored their biodistribution using IVIS (Fig. 5G). These formulations were administered via intravenous injection to 4T1 tumor-bearing mice to investigate whether GA modification could enhance tumor targeting through GA-GLUT1 association. IVIS imaging at 2, 6, 12, 18 and 24 h post-injection revealed distinct distribution patterns: IR-820+S@SR showed widespread distribution with poor tumor accumulation. In contrast, IR-820+GA+S@SR exhibited significantly stronger fluorescence intensity at the tumor site, attributed to GA-GLUT1-mediated targeting. Subsequent ex vivo imaging of harvested organs (liver, heart, spleen, kidneys, lungs, and tumors) further confirmed this trend. Within the tumor tissue, the fluorescence intensity of IR-820+GA+S@SR was 3.05 times higher than that of IR-820+S@SR.

Quantitative analysis consistently showed that IR-820+GA+S@SR had the highest fluorescence signal in tumors, compared to the liver and spleen (Fig. 5H and 5I). This significant tumor accumulation is attributed to the gradual decay of the STING polyproagonist nanoparticles in the bloodstream, with calculated circulation half-lives (t1/2z) of about 4.77 h for IR-820+GA+S@SR and about 2.71 h for IR-820+S@SR (Figs. 5J and S30). Thus, regardless of the cationic DEAEMA segments, this efficient circulation ensures an extended systemic exposure time for IR-820+GA+S@SR, which is beneficial for its application in cancer immunotherapy. These results clearly indicate that the combination of GA enhances the tumor targeting and accumulation of STING polyproagonist nanoparticles, thereby increasing the potential for stronger therapeutic effects.

Although STING agonists potently activate antitumor immunity, including the stimulation of CD8⁺ T cells, they also concurrently induce adaptive upregulation of PD-L1 on tumor cells [[56], [57]]. This increase in PD-L1 serves as an immune escape mechanism by suppressing cytotoxic T-cell activity [[58], [59]]. To overcome this limitation and enhance therapeutic efficacy, we combined αPD-L1 with STING activation as a synergistic immunomodulatory strategy [47,[60], [61]]. To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of this approach, which features tumor-targeted and controlled delivery of SR-717, we established a 4T1 tumor model in female BALB/c mice. When the primary tumor volume reached ∼100 mm³, the mice were randomly divided into six groups and received the following intravenous injections: (G1) PBS, (G2) SR-717, (G3) S@SR, (G4) GA+C@SR+αPD-L1, (G5) GA+S@SR, and (G6) GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 (Fig. 6A). A distant tumor model was also established by injecting 4T1 cells into a separate site on the 7th day of treatment. Body weights and tumor volumes were recorded every other day. After 16 days, we assessed the ability of each treatment to control tumor burden. In the PBS control group, the primary tumor volume grew rapidly, reaching 1207 mm³ by the endpoint. In contrast, GA+S@SR monotherapy (G5) significantly inhibited primary tumor growth, achieving a 79.36% TGI (Fig. 6B). Free SR-717 (G2) and S@SR (G3) showed only moderate efficacy, with TGI values of 51.49% and 40.37%, respectively, which can be attributed to their poor tumor accumulation. The superior performance of GA+S@SR over S@SR underscores the critical contribution of the GA targeting moiety. Most importantly, the combination of GA+S@SR with αPD-L1 (G6) yielded the strongest antitumor effect, achieving a maximal TGI of 89.01%. A similar pattern of potent suppression was observed for distant tumor growth (Fig. 6C), confirming the systemic efficacy of the combination therapy.

To assess systemic antitumor immunity, a distant tumor model was established on Day 7 by subcutaneously injecting 4T1 cells into pre-treated mice. The combination treatment of GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 demonstrated the most potent effect, limiting the distant tumor volume to only 201 mm³. After the treatment, tumors were harvested and weighed (Fig. 6D and 6E). The average tumor weight in the GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 treatment group was the lowest (0.12 g). Compared to the PBS control group, the primary tumor weight in this group was reduced by 11.17 times. Moreover, the efficacy of GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 was superior to that of SR-717, S@SR, GA+C@SR+αPD-L1, and GA+S@SR monotherapy groups, with average tumor weights reduced by 3.19 times, 5.19 times, 7.04 times and 1.48 times, respectively. Furthermore, all groups maintained stable body weight except for the free SR-717 group, which experienced weight loss, possibly due to systemic toxicity or cytokine release syndrome (Fig. 6F). This comparison highlights the improved safety and controlled release characteristics of the polyproagonist nanoparticles. Survival analysis further confirmed the superior efficacy of the targeted therapy (Fig. 6G). Mice receiving GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 treatment achieved a 100% survival rate by Day 40, while the GA+S@SR group also showed significant survival benefits, with a survival rate exceeding 60%, much higher than the other groups. Overall, these results undoubtedly validate the potential of GA+S@SR and its combination with αPD-L1 in eliminating primary and distant tumors.

Moreover, as shown in Fig. 7A, tumor sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Ki67, and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL). H&E staining revealed that, compared to other groups, GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 treatment significantly increased tumor necrosis and nuclear fragmentation while reducing cell density. Consistent with these findings, Ki67 and TUNEL immunofluorescence staining showed a significant reduction in red fluorescence (indicating tumor proliferation) and a significant increase in green fluorescence (reflecting apoptotic cells) in the GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 group. These results collectively confirm the potent antitumor efficacy of STING polyproagonists. Furthermore, the major organ histopathological examination based on H&E did not reveal significant damage during the treatment period, emphasizing the good biocompatibility and biosafety of these drugs (Fig. S31).

in vivo antitumor immune activation

The tumor-targeting micelles effectively deliver STING agonists to tumor tissues. Upon activation, the STING signaling transforms the immunosuppressive “cold” TME into an immunoresponsive “hot” one, promoting T cell proliferation and infiltration. The combination of STING agonists with αPD-L1 further enhances this antitumor immune response. To validate this hypothesis, we assessed the expression of interferons and cytokines in tumors using ELISA. As shown in Fig. 7B–7D, GA+S@SR significantly increased the levels of TNF-α (1.9 pg/mg), IFN-β (14.2 pg/mg), and IL-6 (5.6 pg/mg) compared to the PBS and S@SR groups. These cytokine levels were further elevated in the GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 group (TNF-α: 2.6 pg/mg; IFN-β: 19.4 pg/mg; IL-6: 8.03 pg/mg). Flow cytometry analysis revealed a marked increase in DC maturation (54.9%) and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration (23.5%) in the GA+S@SR group (Figs. 7E, 7F, S32 and S33). The addition of αPD-L1 further boosted CD8⁺ T cell infiltration to 27.5%, surpassing the GA+C@SR+αPD-L1 (20%) and GA+S@SR (23.5%) groups. Consistent with these findings, immunofluorescence staining confirmed enhanced infiltration of CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells in the GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 group, which exhibited the strongest fluorescence signals (Fig. 8A). These results collectively demonstrate that GA+S@SR synergizes with αPD-L1 to enhance the recruitment of cytotoxic T cell and antitumor immunity.

DCs play a pivotal role in initiating a robust immune responses. Mature DCs can induce the generation of antitumor T cells, thereby promoting effective immunotherapy. We analyzed DC maturation and T cell populations in the lymph nodes of mice from different treatment groups using flow cytometry. As shown in Figs. 8B and S34, the DC maturation in the GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 group reached 60.6%, significantly higher than the 40% in the PBS group. The DC maturation in the GA+S@SR group also showed a notable increase (53.1%). Furthermore, the enhanced DC maturation promoted the expansion of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells. In the GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 group, CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells accounted for 34.8% and 39.7% of lymphocytes, respectively, compared to 22.2% and 39.0% in the PBS group (Figs. 8C, S35 and S36).

As the key cytokines involved in systemic immune activation, serum levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-β were measured to assess potential treatment-related toxicity. While free SR-717 could trigger life-threatening cytokine storms due to rapid and uncontrolled release, the polymer designed with GSH-responsive linkers effectively suppressed its systemic distribution at 4 and 12 h post-treatment. This controlled release profile significantly mitigated cytokine upregulation and prevented immune-related adverse events (irAEs), as confirmed in Fig. 8D-F. In summary, the GA+S@SR+αPD-L1 regimen synergistically integrates tumor-targeted delivery, GSH-responsive STING activation and ICB, collectively enhancing antitumor immunity against primary tumors and inhibiting the growth of distant tumors while maintaining good safety.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a tumor-targeting STING polyproagonist nanoplatform (GA+S@SR) that combines GLUT1-mediated targeting, enhanced endosomal escape via the pH-responsive membrane destabilization effect and redox-triggered drug release to effectively deliver SR-717 into the cytoplasm. The GA moiety enables GLUT1-mediated targeting, while the P(DEAEMA-co-BMA) segments promote endosomal escape. In vitro, GA+S@SR demonstrated improved cellular uptake through GA-GLUT1 interactions, effective endosomal escape facilitated redox-responsive cytosolic release of SR-717, resulting in robust STING pathway activation, increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhanced maturation of DCs. After intravenous injection, GA+S@SR exhibited prolonged blood circulation time, enhanced tumor accumulation and immune activation, accompanied by increased infiltration of CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells and DC maturation in lymph nodes. Importantly, the combination with anti-PD-L1 checkpoint blockade further enhanced therapeutic efficacy, achieving near-complete tumor regression and prolonged survival without significant toxicity. This effective antitumor immune response can be attributed to the synergistic effect of STING activation and ICB therapy. This strategy not only improves the efficacy and safety profile of STING agonists but also establishes a multifunctional platform for the co-delivery of other immunomodulators. Although further studies are needed to comprehensively assess its clinical translatability, this research represents a significant advancement in targeted immunotherapy with reduced systemic toxicity.
