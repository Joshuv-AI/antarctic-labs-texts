Introduction

With a reported incidence of 0.8 %, duplication of the ureter and renal pelvis is the most common congenital upper urinary tract anomaly [1]. Patients with duplex renal anomalies may manifest a wide range of clinical presentations, ranging from asymptomatic to severe urinary tract infections (UTI) and hydronephrosis. The upper pole moiety is frequently associated with an obstructive ureterocele or ectopic ureter, leading to loss of renal function, while the lower pole moiety is often associated with vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) and resultant nephropathy [2,3]. Considering the multifaceted anomalies, treatment strategies vary from observation to transurethral incision (TUI) of ureterocele to definitive surgical reconstruction at the level of kidneys or distal ureters [2]. In other studies, Wang et al., the majority of patients with residual ureteral stumps following upper tract surgery do not need stump resection during long-term follow-up. However, the length of the residual ureteral stump is strongly linked to the development of ureteric stump syndrome. Patients with risk factors should be closely monitored for at least 12 months post-surgery to detect any signs of ureteric stump syndrome [12].

Surgical resection by heminephrectomy is considered the mainstay treatment for duplex kidneys with non-functioning moieties [2,4]. While ureteral remnant may be safely ligated or left in situ during heminephrectomy to avoid the risk of injury to the healthy ipsilateral ureter, the distal redundant ureter may cause long-term complications, albeit rarely [[3], [4], [5]]. These complications include persistent VUR, recurrent UTI, hematuria, calculi, and stump neoplasm. This paper presents a case report of a child with hydronephrosis due to residual ureteral stump after upper pole heminephrectomy. This article has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [6].

Presentation of case

A 3-year-old male child was referred to our department with recurrent febrile UTI for 3 days before admission. The child had a history of similar symptoms, which had been resolved after undergoing a right heminephrectomy for duplicated collecting system in another tertiary center one year prior. His physical examination was unremarkable apart from a surgical scar in the right flank region; no palpable mass nor bulging was found. Laboratory test results were unremarkable. Similarly, kidney, ureter, and bladder (KUB) X-ray findings were unremarkable.

Diethylenetriamine pentaacetate (DTPA) renography showing loss of lower pole function (parenchymal and excretory) of the right kidney along with parenchymal dysfunction of the left kidney with delayed excretion.

A repeat ultrasonographic examination in our center revealed bilateral hydronephrosis. The child experienced recurrent urinary tract infections, right hydronephrosis, and right hydroureter due to the remnant ureteral stump. Additionally, left hydronephrosis and hydroureter were suspected to be due to partial obstruction of the left ureterovesical junction. Voiding cystourethrography revealed a normal bladder wall with no additional shadow, filling defect, Christmas tree appearance, or reflux in both kidneys and ureters. Diethylenetriamine pentaacetate (DTPA) renography confirmed intrarenal findings consistent with prior right heminephrectomy along with severe loss of lower pole function (parenchymal and excretory) with a glomerular filtration rate (GFR) of 10.91 mL/min in the right kidney due to suspected right VUR; it also found parenchymal dysfunction of the left kidney with delayed excretion and GFR of 70.71 mL/min. Subsequently, the child was diagnosed with 1) right hydronephrosis and right hydroureter with right ureteral remnant post upper-pole right heminephrectomy for duplicated collecting system; 2) left hydronephrosis and left hydroureter due to suspected partial obstruction of left ureterovesical junction; and spina bifida occulta.

The child then underwent cystoscopy. Ureteral catheter and Double J (DJ) stent were inserted into the left ureter to preserve the contralateral kidney function and prevent obstruction due to left UVJ stenosis (Fig. 2). The ureteral catheter was inserted into the right ureter under contrast guidance, and the remnant ureteral stump was excised. DJ stent was also inserted accordingly to the remaining right ureter to prevent it from adhesion (Fig. 3).

Ureterography and stent placement of left ureter.

Ureterography and stent placement of right ureter.

During the surgery, a 15-cm-long right dilated ureter was excised. The surgery was uneventful, and the child was discharged within a week. Two months postoperatively, the patient came for follow-up in Sanglah Hospital and underwent USG examination, left and right DJ stents were placed in situ.

Discussion

While most residual ureteral stumps do not cause issues, the length of the residual ureteral stump is strongly linked to the development of ureteric stump syndrome. Patients with risk factors should be closely monitored for at least 12 months post-surgery to detect any signs of ureteric stump syndrome [12].

Residual distal ureteral stump after an upper urinary tract surgery rarely causes significant long-term problems. Excision of the distal ureteral stump is often tedious and risks damaging the bladder neck and sphincter; hence it is common practice to simply ligate and leave them in place [7,8]. If, however, urethral or bladder neck insertion of the ectopic ureter and reflux into the ureter is identified preoperatively, the refluxing, obstructed ectopic ureter may pose a significant long-term risk of recurrent infection and thus it is justified to perform extensive excision of the distal ureter [9]. In this case, excision of distal ureteral remnant must be carried out meticulously to avoid injury to the bladder neck, sphincter, and other surrounding structures, and as much of the ureter should be removed to prevent long-term problems. However, to our best knowledge, to date, there have only been several small case series documenting the natural history of refluxing ureteral stump after nephrectomy.

Cain and colleagues [7] observed 38 patients with a mean age of 37 months who underwent nephrectomy and partial ureterectomy for reflux into a nonfunctioning kidney and reported low risk of morbidity associated with a refluxing ureteral stump, with only 1 patient with an ipsilateral ureterocele and 1 with contralateral reflux had symptomatic infections during postoperative follow-up; no patient with only primary unilateral reflux underwent surgery for the refluxing stump. They concluded that refluxing ureteral stump was associated with low risk of morbidity.

Kim and colleagues [8] followed nine pediatric patients aged one to 20 months with ectopic ureter or ureterocele who had undergone an upper tract surgery, including total nephrectomy, partial nephrectomy, and proximal ureteroureterostomy, leaving distal ureteral stump. They found that among the 9 patients, 5 had their refluxing distal ureteral stumps resolved spontaneously, with markedly reduced stump size in the 6 and 12-month follow-up; 4 patients had considerable improvement within 6 months and 1 by 20 months postoperatively.

Caluwé and colleagues [3] followed 19 patients aged 1 month to 12 years who underwent 20 lower pole heminephrectomies for non-functioning lower pole moieties in duplex kidneys. They reported eight patients showed VUR into the stump postoperatively; two of these patients underwent subsequent subureteral endoscopic correction of VUR with polytetrafluoroethylene paste; two underwent stump excision; and the remaining four of the eight patients demonstrated spontaneous resolution of VUR during follow-up. They concluded most patients with residual ureteral stump after lower pole heminephrectomy do not require later stump excision.

Regarding minimally-invasive techniques, Escolino and colleagues [10] reported significant incidence of symptomatic distal ureteral stump in patients aged 1 to 10 years who had undergone minimally-invasive nephrectomy, either through laparoscopic or retroperitoneoscopic approach. Among 21 patients, they found two patients who had undergone retroperitoneoscopic nephrectomy had symptoms related to a refluxing distal ureteral stump. They noted that the length of distal ureteral remnant was longer after retroperitoneoscopic nephrectomy compared with after laparoscopic nephrectomy (range 3–7 mm vs. 2–5 mm; p < 0.001). Based on these findings, they recommended laparoscopic nephrectomy over retroperitoneoscopic nephrectomy for children with non-functioning kidney due to VUR to prevent problems related to symptomatic distal ureteral remnant.

Only 6 of 182 patients (3 %) who had undergone nephrectomy required a secondary procedure to remove symptomatic ureteral remnant [7]. Other report stated the reoperation rate of 8 % to 10 % [4]. If surgical excision of the ureteral remnant after nephrectomy is indicated, distal ureterectomy is the treatment of choice [11]. Alternatively, laparoscopic distal ureteretomy may be a viable alternative to open approach. Casale and colleagues [11] reported excellent results with either of this approach; they found that distal ureterectomy significantly reduced the incidence of recurrent symptomatic UTI.

Following heminephrectomy, there is a significant decrease of renal function in some patients. Gundeti and colleagues [4] reported a 8 % decrease in renal function among children who undergone heminephrectomy for duplex kidneys, with a small decrease in function in the remaining moiety in other 51 %. Whether this decline in renal function is correlated with stump- related complications is not clearly understood.

Proper surgical excision of the ureteral remnant, either through an open or laparoscopic approach, can significantly reduce the incidence of recurrent symptomatic UTIs and other complications. Surgical excision of residual ureteral stumps is crucial to prevent long-term complications such as recurrent infections and hydronephrosis. This report is limited by the small sample size and the short follow-up period. Larger studies with longer follow-ups are needed to better understand the long-term outcomes of residual ureteral stumps after heminephrectomy.

Conclusion

Distal residual ureter stump after nephrectomy rarely causes long-term complications. While ureteral remnant may be safely ligated or left in situ during heminephrectomy, the benefit of preventing long-term stump-related complications must be balanced with the risk of iatrogenic injury to the bladder neck and sphincter. To prevent future complications, it is recommended not to leave a significant portion of the ureter during heminephrectomy. If symptomatic ureteral remnants are present, distal ureterectomy is the treatment of choice. Proper surgical excision of the ureteral remnant, either through an open or laparoscopic approach, can significantly reduce the incidence of recurrent symptomatic UTIs and other complications.
