Association between air pollution exposure and multimorbidity among middle-aged and older adults in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jing Zhu, Zhenhang Zhao, Bangzheng Yin, Rui Jiang, Xi Chen, Chan Yin, Youde Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As the world’s population ages, multimorbidity is becoming an increasingly significant burden in the elderly population. Environmental exposures, particularly air pollution, may influence the patterns and distribution of multimorbidity, but evidence in older adults in China remains limited. This study aims to investigate the association between various air pollutants and multimorbidity patterns in Chinese adults aged 60 and above, while considering spatial structure and sex differences. This study is a cross-sectional analysis using data from the 2020 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) for individuals aged 60 and above. Four multimorbidity patterns were analyzed: neurocognitive and emotional disorders (NED), cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), respiratory system (RES), and articular-visceral organ (AVO). A linear mixed-effects model was used to evaluate the associations between five air pollution indicators and each multimorbidity pattern, expressed as odds ratio (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Sex-stratified analyses were further conducted to explore potential sex-specific effect modifications. Spatial analysis was performed to examine clustering patterns between air pollution and multimorbidity. In addition, an online prediction platform based on machine learning was developed to assist in sexually transmitted disease screening. For NED, each 10 µg/m³ increase in O₃ exposure was associated with an OR of 0.866 (95% CI: 0.769–0.974). For CMM, each 10 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 exposure was associated with an OR of 1.214 (1.143–1.291), each 10 µg/m³ increase in NO₂ exposure with an OR of 1.175 (1.104–1.251), and each 10 µg/m³ increase in O₃ exposure with an OR of 1.115 (1.047–1.186). For RES, there was no significant correlation observed with any of the five air pollution indicators. For AVO, each 10 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 exposure was associated with an OR of 0.869 (0.817–0.923), each 10 µg/m³ increase in NO₂ exposure with an OR of 0.839 (0.789–0.892), each 0.1 mg/m³ increase in CO exposure with an OR of 0.892 (0.839–0.948), each 10 µg/m³ increase in SO₂ exposure with an OR of 0.931 (0.875–0.991), and each 10 µg/m³ increase in O₃ exposure with an OR of 0.804 (0.758–0.853). Sex-stratified analysis revealed a significant sex effect modification for PM2.5 and O₃ exposure in CMM, with higher ORs in females. Spatial autocorrelation analysis showed a significant positive correlation between NED, CMM, and AVO and air pollution, while no spatial autocorrelation was observed for the RES. In older Chinese adults, air pollution is significantly associated with multimorbidity patterns, with variations depending on sex and geographic factors. Introduction

Chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, chronic respiratory disease, cancer, and kidney disease, are major contributors to global mortality and disease burden1,2. With an aging population and changing lifestyle factors, an increasing number of people are likely to have multiple chronic conditions at the same time, leading to a continuing increase in both the prevalence and mortality rates associated with multimorbidity3–5. Epidemiological data suggest that approximately 180 million older adults in China are affected by chronic diseases, of whom over 75% have multimorbidity6. In clinical practice, multimorbidity is often manifested by pathophysiological interactions between comorbid conditions. For example, patients with diabetes may also have a history of stroke7, while those with liver disease may also have chronic kidney disease8. These interactions can complicate treatment regimens, as different therapeutic approaches may interfere with each other, leading to suboptimal outcomes or worsening side effects. Such complications can have more serious consequences than the presence of a single chronic condition9,10. Identifying multimorbidity in older people is therefore of paramount practical importance.

Air pollution is a growing public health concern and is widely recognized as a significant environmental risk factor for chronic diseases. With rapid urbanization and expanding industrial activity, it has become a leading contributor to premature mortality, increased disease burden, and diminished quality of life11,12. A substantial body of epidemiological evidence links ambient air pollution to chronic cardiovascular, respiratory, and neurological conditions13,14. Reducing greenhouse gas emissions and implementing synergistic control measures can both mitigate climate change and reduce the burden of respiratory diseases15. Short-term increases in ambient pollutant concentrations have also been associated with triggering cerebrovascular events in susceptible populations16. However, compared with the extensive literature on single-disease outcomes, the relationship between air pollution and multimorbidity remains underexplored and lacks systematic evidence.

Marked population and spatial heterogeneity further complicate the health impacts of air pollution. Multiple studies suggest that men and women exhibit differential health responses at comparable exposure levels, with some evidence indicating that women may be more susceptible to pollutants such as PM2.517–19. Simultaneously, climatic, economic, cultural, and social factors jointly shape regional exposure levels, pollutant composition profiles, and the distribution of disease patterns, yielding pronounced geographic variation in health effects20–22. Consequently, it is necessary to evaluate systematically, across both sex and regional dimensions, the association between air pollution and multimorbidity, to elucidate differential impact pathways and effect modification, and to provide a robust evidence base for precision prevention and targeted policy-making.

This study examined associations between ambient air pollution and multimorbidity, among older adults, addressing an evidence gap. Using generalized linear mixed-effects models, we estimated the relationship between ambient pollutant exposure and multimorbidity. We conducted sex-stratified analyses and spatial autocorrelation analyses to assess sex differences and characterize geographic patterns in exposure and multimorbidity. Preliminary findings indicate that air pollution is most strongly associated with cardiometabolic multimorbidity, with effect estimates differing by sex. These findings advance understanding of the environmental determinants of multimorbidity and suggest that sex and geographic context may modify the health risks posed by air pollution to older adults.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a national health survey focusing on Chinese people aged 45 and over23. We conducted a cross-sectional analysis based on the CHARLS wave 2020 data. Excluded individuals under the age of 60 and removed cases with missing data in the covariates, the final sample size was 9,941 participants (Fig. 1). The CHARLS was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). All participants signed informed consent. The present study was conducted following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline [S5].

Chronic diseases and multimorbidity

This study assessed fifteen chronic conditions based on participants’ self-reported health status. Participants were asked whether they had ever been diagnosed by a doctor with any of the following conditions: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or high blood sugar, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, digestive disorders, mental health problems, memory-related disorders, Parkinson’s disease, arthritis or rheumatism, and asthma. Participants were asked about their lifetime history of these conditions. Multimorbidity was defined as reporting two or more of these conditions. To explore multimorbidity patterns, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed on the CHARLS 2020 data. This analysis aimed to identify underlying factors that group the chronic conditions based on patterns of multimorbidity. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s sphericity test were used to assess the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Given the binary nature of the variables, a tetrachoric correlation matrix was used. The minimum residual method (minres) was used for factor extraction, and the Promax rotation method facilitated the interpretation of the factors. Factors with loadings of 0.3 or higher were considered significant, helping to identify the key clusters of chronic conditions that tend to occur together in the population24.

Air pollution data

Air pollution data were obtained from the China Air Quality Online Monitoring and Analysis Platform (https://www.aqistudy.cn/), which provides publicly available, quality-controlled ground-based monitoring for 367 cities in China since December 2013. The database includes daily Air Quality Index (AQI) and daily mean concentrations of PM2.5, PM10, SO2, CO, NO2, and O3. Data are reported at the city level and aggregated from hourly measurements collected at fixed-site monitoring stations.

Each CHARLS participant was linked to the registered city of residence. City-level daily pollutant series were assigned uniformly to all participants residing in the same city. Geocoding used official CHARLS residential codes matched to city centroids. Missing daily pollutant values were imputed by linear interpolation from adjacent days.

For each pollutant, we computed two-year (24-month) moving averages preceding the participant’s interview date during 2018–2020 to represent long-term exposure. This window was chosen to capture chronic exposure rather than short-term fluctuations; no additional lag structures were applied because the two-year moving average already incorporates temporal smoothing and exposure persistence.

Variance inflation factor (VIF) diagnostics (Table S1) indicated severe multicollinearity among AQI, PM2.5, and PM10. Because AQI is a composite index derived from its component pollutants, we excluded AQI from multivariable models. Persistent high collinearity between PM2.5 and PM10 led us to further exclude PM10. After these exclusions, VIF values for the remaining variables were ≤ 5, indicating acceptable multicollinearity for subsequent analyses.

Covariates

Several potential confounders were considered in the analysis, and several sets of covariates were collected. These covariates include: (1) Sociodemographic characteristics: age, sex, marital status, level of education, place of residence, personal annual income and number of children; (2) Behavioral factors: smoking status, alcohol consumption, social activity, sleep duration at night, sleep status during the day and intensity of physical activity.

Statistical analysis

We employed a generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) to assess the relationship between air pollutants and multimorbidity. To test the robustness of the association with individual-level confounders, we defined three models by progressively adding covariates. Model 1 (the baseline model) considers air pollutant concentrations as fixed effects and community ID (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$cit{y}_{i}$$\end{document}) as a random effect. Model 2 builds upon Model 1 by adjusting for age and sex. Model 3 includes all covariates for adjustment. Additionally, we performed a two-sample Z-test to examine the statistical differences in effect estimates across sex strata. This was done by comparing the point estimates (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}) and their corresponding standard errors obtained from Model 3.

To analyze the spatial distribution characteristics of multimorbidity across provinces, we employed Global Moran’s I, Getis-Ord Gi*, and a Bayesian spatial model. These methods assess the spatial association of multimorbidity, identifying regions of spatial clustering and dispersion. Global Moran’s I was used to measure the spatial correlation of the data across provinces. A Moran’s I value greater than 0 indicates positive spatial correlation, with larger values indicating greater spatial clustering. Conversely, a value less than 0 indicates negative spatial correlation, with smaller values indicating more dispersed data25,26. Global autocorrelation statistics highlight the overall presence of clustering, while local autocorrelation reveals the specific spatial locations and types of these clusters. To better understand the distribution patterns of multimorbidity, we applied local autocorrelation analysis to detect localized clusters. The Getis-Ord Gi* index quantifies spatial clustering, identifying hot spots and cold spots based on their high and low values25,27.

To quantitatively evaluate the impact of five environmental pollutants on disease prevalence and effectively address unobserved spatial confounding factors, this study constructed a Bayesian spatial model based on the Besag-York-Mollie (BYM) framework28,29. Given that prevalence rates are bounded between 0 and 1, the model was implemented using a Beta likelihood function with a Logit link. All pollutant concentrations were Z-score standardized prior to analysis to ensure comparability. Spatial effects were decomposed into a structured term (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}_{i}$$\end{document}), modeled via a Conditional Autoregressive (CAR) distribution based on a province-level adjacency matrix, and an unstructured term (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${v}_{i}$$\end{document}) to capture localized random heterogeneity. Parameter estimation was performed using the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS), a high-efficiency Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm. We executed four parallel chains with 4,000 iterations each (including a 2,000-iteration burn-in period). Model performance was evaluated using the Watanabe-Akaike Information Criterion (WAIC), with the posterior mean (β) and its exponentiated Odds Ratio (OR, 95% CrI) serving as the primary metrics for association.

Finally, to predict the likelihood of elderly individuals developing chronic diseases, we developed an online platform based on a machine learning model. The model is built using the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) framework and addresses data imbalance using the Borderline Smote 1 technique. Feature selection is performed using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, while hyper-parameter optimization is performed using the Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA). The practical contribution of this prediction tool is to provide personalized risk assessments for elderly individuals, helping identify those at higher associated with chronic diseases based on their individual health data. Detailed information on this section can be found in the supplementary material.

Software

Data cleaning, preprocessing, and descriptive analysis were performed using R 4.4.1. The EFA was conducted using the “psych” and “GPArotation” packages in R. The GLMM was implemented using the “lme4” package in R. The Global Moran’s I, Getis-Ord Gi*, Bayesian spatial model, and machine learning models were all executed using Python 3.11.14.

Results

Sampling adequacy was evaluated using the KMO statistic and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (KMO = 0.77; Bartlett’s test, P < 0.001), supporting the use of EFA. We estimated EFA solutions specifying three through six factors to identify multimorbidity patterns. A five-factor solution that retained all 15 chronic conditions provided the best fit and clinical interpretability (model-fit statistics in Table S2). Since one of the models only included cancer or malignant tumor, it was excluded, leading to the identification of four distinct multimorbidity patterns: the Neurocognitive and Emotional Disorders pattern (NED, including stroke, emotional and mental problems, memory-related diseases, and Parkinson’s disease), the Cardiometabolic Multimorbidity pattern (CMM, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or high blood sugar, heart problems, and stroke), the Respiratory System pattern (RES, including chronic lung disease and asthma), and the Articular-Visceral Organ pattern (AVO, including liver disease, kidney disease, stomach or other digestive diseases, and arthritis or rheumatism). Participants with two or more chronic diseases from any of the four multimorbidity patterns were classified into the corresponding pattern.

Descriptive characteristics, covariates, the 15 chronic conditions, multimorbidity patterns, and air-pollution exposures used in the analysis are summarized in Table 1. The analytic cohort included 9,941 older adults (50.7% women; 49.3% men) with a mean age of 68.8 years (SD 8.9). Multimorbidity was common: 6,022 participants (60.6%) had two or more chronic conditions, with a slightly higher prevalence among women than men. Among the four patterns, prevalence was highest for CMM (40.2%) and AVO (28.7%), followed by RES (6.6%); NED had the lowest prevalence (3.8%).

Changes in the OR for four multi-disease patterns as air pollutant exposure levels increase, estimated using three GLMM, are presented in Fig. 2. After adjustment for covariates, we observed consistent associations across specifications: positive associations of CMM with PM2.5, NO2, and O3; inverse associations of AVO with PM2.5, NO2, CO, SO2, and O3; an inverse association of NED with O3; and no consistent association for RES. The model adjusted for multiple covariates showed the following results (Table 2): For NED, each 10 µg/m³ increase in O₃ exposure was associated with an OR of 0.866 (95% CI: 0.769, 0.974); RES showed no significant correlation with any of the five air pollutants; for CMM, each 10 µg/m³ increase in PM₂.₅ exposure was associated with an OR of 1.214 (1.143, 1.291), each 10 µg/m³ increase in NO₂ exposure was associated with an OR of 1.175 (1.104, 1.251), and each 10 µg/m³ increase in O₃ exposure was associated with an OR of 1.115 (1.047, 1.186); for AVO, each 10 µg/m³ increase in PM₂.₅ exposure was associated with an OR of 0.869 (0.817, 0.923), each 10 µg/m³ increase in NO₂ exposure was associated with an OR of 0.839 (0.789, 0.892), each 0.1 mg/m³ increase in CO exposure was associated with an OR of 0.892 (0.839, 0.948), each 10 µg/m³ increase in SO₂ exposure was associated with an OR of 0.931 (0.875, 0.991), and each 10 µg/m³ increase in O₃ exposure was associated with an OR of 0.804 (0.758, 0.853). Sex-stratified analyses (Table 2) provided evidence of effect modification for PM2.5 and O3 in relation to CMM (P for interaction < 0.05). Compared with men, women had higher odds per 10 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 (OR 1.223, 95% CI 1.132, 1.321) and per 10 µg/m3 increase in O3 (OR 1.175, 95% CI 1.089, 1.269).

Global Moran’s I results (Table 3) indicated significant positive spatial autocorrelation for NED, AVO, and CMM, with no evidence of spatial autocorrelation for RES. For air pollutants, CO and SO2 showed significant positive spatial autocorrelation, and PM2.5, NO2, and O3 exhibited more pronounced positive spatial autocorrelation. Getis–Ord Gi* cluster analysis (Fig. 3) identified distinct hot spots and cold spots in pattern prevalence across China. NED hot spots were concentrated in northern provinces, with cold spots in southern coastal provinces. CMM hot spots were located primarily in the three northeastern provinces (Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning), while southern regions showed cold spots. RES hot spots were observed in Liaoning and several western provinces, with cold spots in southern coastal provinces. AVO hot spots were concentrated in Heilongjiang and western provinces, with cold spots in Tianjin, Beijing, and the central and eastern coastal provinces.

Bayesian spatial regression models (Table S3) revealed that all five pollutants showed positive associations with the prevalence of NED, CMM, and RES, though none reached statistical significance. For NED and CMM, NO₂ exhibited the highest point estimates of risk, with ORs of 1.123 (95% CrI: 0.686–1.808) and 1.280 (95% CrI: 0.881–1.866), respectively. In the RES model, SO₂ was associated with the highest risk (OR = 1.122, 95% CrI: 0.424–2.676). Regarding AVO, SO₂ showed a positive association (OR = 1.183, 95% CrI: 0.548–2.388), whereas the remaining four pollutants were inversely associated with prevalence. Notably, the spatial fraction remained high across all models (ranging from 0.644 to 0.815), indicating that structured spatial components accounted for over 64% of the residual variance. For each disease outcome, the spatial structural effect maps (Figure S1-S4) remained strikingly consistent across all pollutants. For NED and CMM, positive residuals were predominantly concentrated in northern and northeastern provinces (Heilongjiang, Jilin, and Beijing), while negative residuals were clustered in southern coastal regions. In contrast, for RES and AVO, the geographical patterns shifted westward, significant positive residuals were primarily localized in western regions, while negative residuals were concentrated in eastern coastal and southern provinces.

Using an XGBoost-based predictive model, we developed a web-based tool to assist risk estimation (https://chronic-disease-prediction-tool-for-older-adults-evpjkyimnum2p.streamlit.app/). The platform allows users to input sociodemographic characteristics, behavioral factors, and residential location and returns the estimated probabilities of membership in the four multimorbidity patterns and selected chronic conditions.

Discussion

This cross-sectional analysis of 2020 CHARLS data examined associations between ambient air pollution and multimorbidity patterns among older adults in China. Using EFA, we classified 15 chronic conditions into four clinically coherent patterns—NED, CMM, RES, and AVO—largely consistent with prior studies and supported by plausible pathophysiological mechanisms30–33. In multivariable GLMM, CMM was positively associated with PM2.5, NO2, and O3, whereas AVO showed inverse associations with PM2.5, NO2, CO, SO2, and O3. NED was inversely associated with O3, and no consistent associations were observed for RES across model specifications. Sex-stratified analyses indicated effect modification: associations of PM2.5 and O3 with CMM were stronger among women than men. Our spatial analyses demonstrated positive global spatial autocorrelation for several patterns and pollutants and identified distinct hot- and cold-spot provinces, underscoring geographic clustering and potential spillover. Finally, this study not only validated and extended existing evidence on the link between air pollution and cardiometabolic multimorbidity but also, for the first time, systematically explored the effects of air pollution on other multimorbidity patterns. These findings enhance our understanding of the mechanisms underlying multimorbidity formation in older adults and provide a scientific basis for developing targeted air quality management and elderly health intervention policies. Additionally, leveraging advancements in artificial intelligence technology, we have developed an online prediction platform based on machine learning to provide probability assessments. This platform serves as a convenient tool for chronic disease screening and health management among the elderly population, thereby supporting evidence-based decision-making by governments and public health institutions.

In this study, we observed relatively high prevalence rates for CMM (40.2%) and AVO (28.7%), while the prevalence rates for NED (3.8%) and RES (6.6%) were notably low. The low prevalence of NED and RES may limit statistical power and precision, making it more difficult to detect or precisely estimate associations with environmental exposures. Air pollutants, particularly PM₂.₅, NO₂, and O₃ have been extensively documented to be significantly associated with increased CMM prevalence34–36, a finding consistent with the results of this study. However, the relationship between AVO and air pollutants exhibits a negative correlation, a phenomenon warranting further investigation. AVO typically reflects multifaceted organ system dysfunction encompassing conditions such as arthritis, liver disease, and kidney disease. Air pollutants like PM2.5 are recognized risk factors for these disorders37–39. Several explanations may account for these findings: On one hand, individuals chronically exposed to such air pollutants may mitigate harm through adaptive physiological responses, such as enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity or elevated expression of specific genes, thereby developing immune adaptation or resistance to pollution40,41; On the other hand, the health impacts of air pollution inherently develop progressively. Chronic diseases often result from long-term cumulative exposure, with adverse effects potentially taking years or even longer to fully manifest42,43. The currently observed inverse associations may represent short-term adaptive responses temporarily masking long-term latent hazards. Nevertheless, the inverse associations between the AVO pattern and air pollutants do not imply that air pollution has positive health effects. Rather, it may reflect the interplay of multiple environmental factors, lifestyle choices, individual variations, and exposure time windows44. Therefore, further longitudinal studies and mechanistic investigations are needed to clarify the specific role of air pollution in AVO, particularly regarding changes in individual health following prolonged exposure to highly polluted environments.

Consistent with prior studies, long-term exposure to PM2.5 and O3 was more strongly associated with the prevalence of CMM among women45. This sex difference may be partially attributable to biological factors. For example, estrogen can modulate oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling, potentially amplifying pollutant-induced cellular injury46,47. Sociobehavioral and exposure-related factors also warrant consideratio48. Time–activity patterns may increase women’s exposure to household air pollution (e.g., emissions from cooking), thereby augmenting the overall exposure burden49. Accordingly, prevention and control strategies for air pollution–related multimorbidity should account for sex-specific risk profiles, with enhanced targeted protection and monitoring for women during periods of high pollution.

The spatial distribution of multimorbidity reflects the interplay of geography, culture, economy, and climate. In northern regions, colder climates and distinct dietary habits may be linked to higher prevalence of neurocognitive and emotional disorders27. In the northeastern provinces, high-calorie diets and lifestyle patterns may contribute to a higher prevalence of cardiovascular and metabolic multimorbidity50. Environmental and climatic conditions in western and northeastern areas may likewise exacerbate joint and visceral disease burdens. In-depth analysis using Bayesian spatial models further underscores the predominant influence of geographical context on disease risk distribution, as evidenced by the high spatial fraction parameters. Although the estimated OR for the five pollutants did not attain statistical significance, structured spatial components accounted for more than 64% of the residual variance across all models. This suggests that the identified high-risk clusters remain robust even after adjusting for environmental exposures, indicating that the observed spatial heterogeneity is driven by regional factors beyond ambient air pollution alone. These regional differences underscore the need to tailor prevention and control strategies to local contexts. Future research should integrate individual behavioral data with socioeconomic indicators to conduct multiscale spatiotemporal analyses for more robust validation.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between air pollution exposure and multimorbidity. Second, the prevalence of 15 chronic diseases was self-reported by the participants, which may lead to an underestimation of the actual disease rates. Third, air pollution exposure levels were estimated based on the participants’ residential areas, which may not accurately reflect individual exposure. Furthermore, although our models accounted for data dependence through community-level random effects and spatial structured terms, the explicit inclusion of complex sampling weights was not implemented in the primary analysis, which might potentially affect the precision and national representativeness of some estimates. Finally, the sample is limited to middle-aged and elderly individuals in China, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other populations.

Furthermore, although our models accounted for data dependence through community-level random effects and spatial structured terms, the explicit inclusion of complex sampling weights was not implemented in the primary analysis, which might potentially affect the precision and national representativeness of some estimates.
