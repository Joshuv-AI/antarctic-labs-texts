Comment on “Prognostic value of AI-enabled quantitative coronary CT angiography for major adverse cardiovascular events: A systematic review and meta-analysis”
Authors: Gunji Sreenivasulu, Chilamakuru Nagesh, Ch Rajyalakshmi
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the meta-analysis by Malik et al. [1], examining the prognostic value of artificial intelligence–enabled quantitative coronary computed tomography angiography (AI-QCCTA) for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).

Heterogeneity and the limits of pooled inference

The principal finding of a pooled hazard ratio of 1.95 for AI-derived plaque burden predicting MACE carries an I2 of 99%, which is not merely substantial heterogeneity but near-complete statistical inconsistency across studies. At this level, the pooled estimate does not represent a stable treatment effect; it represents the average of ten potentially non-overlapping associations [2]. The ten included studies had follow-up durations from one to ten years, employed five distinct AI platforms with different plaque segmentation algorithms and training datasets, and defined MACE inconsistently, with some including revascularization and others excluding it [3]. Before a cardiologist can apply the HR of 1.95 to an individual patient, these sources of variation must be resolved, not simply acknowledged as limitations of the study [4].

Platform heterogeneity and the sensitivity analysis

The prespecified sensitivity analysis restricted to Cleerly Labs studies is methodologically sound, but the persistence of I2 = 92% even within a single platform indicates that algorithm standardization alone cannot explain the observed variance. This distinction carries clinical weight: if prognostic performance varies substantially even among studies using identical software, the signal is driven by population and protocol differences that no current AI platform can override [5].

The authors have assembled the most comprehensive prognostic synthesis of AI-QCCTA to date, and the consistent directionality across plaque phenotypes, particularly low-attenuation plaque, is a meaningful contribution to risk stratification methodology. Resolving the sources of heterogeneity through individual patient-level data pooling remains an essential next step before these estimates can inform clinical thresholds.
