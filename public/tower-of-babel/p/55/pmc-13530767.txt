Documented arteriovenous fistula thrill category and Doppler hemodynamics in mature radiocephalic fistulas: A single-center retrospective study
Authors: Lanyu Zhong, Yulin Zhang, Hongqiong Cheng, Mingshan Jiang, Wenfang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the cross-sectional association between routinely documented arteriovenous fistula thrill categories and Doppler hemodynamic parameters in mature radiocephalic fistulas.MethodsThis single-center retrospective observational study included 474 patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis with mature native radiocephalic fistulas who underwent color Doppler ultrasound between January 2021 and March 2025. Each patient contributed one eligible examination with same-visit thrill documentation classified as strong, weak, or absent before Doppler interpretation. Hemodynamic parameters, lesion-related records, exploratory receiver operating characteristic analyses, multivariable associations with abnormal thrill, and Doppler reproducibility were evaluated.ResultsOf 474 patients, 367 had strong thrill, 67 had weak thrill, and 40 had absent thrill. Median brachial artery blood flow decreased across the strong, weak, and absent groups from 783 to 206 and 86 mL/min, respectively, whereas median brachial artery resistance index increased from 0.53 to 0.78 and 1.00 (overall P < 0.001 for both). Stenosis-related records were most frequent in the weak-thrill group, whereas thrombosis-related records were concentrated in the absent-thrill group. For exploratory discrimination of abnormal versus strong thrill, blood flow had an AUC of 0.987 at a cohort-specific cutoff of 376 mL/min (sensitivity 98.1%, specificity 92.4%), and resistance index had an AUC of 0.969 at a cutoff of 0.69 (sensitivity 87.9%, specificity 95.9%). In the principal adjusted model, lower blood flow and higher resistance index remained independently associated with abnormal thrill (adjusted OR 0.25 per 100 mL/min and 2.92 per 0.1 unit, respectively; both P < 0.001). Measurement reproducibility was high.ConclusionsRoutinely documented thrill category was associated with Doppler hemodynamics in mature radiocephalic fistulas. The exploratory cutoffs describe separation between documentation groups and should not be interpreted as validated diagnostic thresholds or evidence of a thrill-based triage strategy.Keywords: arteriovenous fistula, hemodialysis, color Doppler ultrasound, thrill intensity, blood flow, resistance index, stenosis, thrombosis

1. Introduction

Arteriovenous fistula (AVF) remains the preferred vascular access for maintenance hemodialysis because of its superior long-term patency and lower infection risk compared with catheters and grafts. 1 Even so, AVF dysfunction remains common throughout the access lifespan. Maturation failure, progressive stenosis, thrombosis, and difficult cannulation can compromise dialysis adequacy, increase the need for salvage procedures, and result in access loss.2,3 Recent observational studies continue to show that early AVF failure and access-related complications remain clinically consequential in routine dialysis practice. 4

Color Doppler ultrasound has become central to AVF assessment because it provides structural and hemodynamic information noninvasively and can be repeated during follow-up.5,6 Ultrasound-derived parameters such as vessel diameter, access flow volume, and resistance index have been used to evaluate stenosis and access dysfunction.7,8 Recent work has further expanded this field to vascular sound-based monitoring, preoperative arterial assessment, recurrent failure after intervention, postoperative RI-based prediction, Doppler thresholds for distal AVF stenosis, and early stenosis detection using Venturi-effect ultrasonography combined with conventional ultrasound indicators.9–14 Physical examination still matters, because changes in thrill, bruit, puncture quality, or dialysis performance often trigger further targeted imaging before a definitive lesion is documented.5,6

In routine dialysis units, this distinction is clinically important. Physical examination is inexpensive, repeatable, and available at every cannulation encounter, but it is inherently operator-dependent and does not quantify access flow or vascular resistance. Conversely, Doppler ultrasound offers objective assessment but may be performed either periodically or when clinical concern for access dysfunction arises. A clearer understanding of how routine thrill documentation corresponds to contemporaneous Doppler hemodynamics can therefore clarify the relationship between bedside findings and objective imaging measurements in everyday practice rather than define a triage pathway.5,6

Most recent studies have focused on maturation after AVF creation, intervention-defined restenosis, or specific stenotic endpoints. Fewer real-world analyses have examined whether a thrill category documented during routine bedside practice corresponds to contemporaneous Doppler hemodynamics across the spectrum of mature AVF dysfunction. The contribution of the present study is to quantify how routinely recorded bedside thrill categories correspond to same-visit objective hemodynamic measurements in mature radiocephalic AVFs. We therefore evaluated the association between documented AVF thrill category and ultrasound-derived hemodynamic parameters in a retrospective cohort of maintenance hemodialysis patients with mature radiocephalic AVFs. We also described the distribution of stenosis- and thrombosis-related records and explored the discriminatory ability of major ultrasound parameters across thrill categories. Our aim was not to validate a standardized palpation scale, establish a diagnostic threshold, localize stenosis by examination alone, or evaluate a thrill-based triage strategy, but to characterize the cross-sectional association between routine thrill documentation and contemporaneous Doppler hemodynamics in everyday practice.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This single-center retrospective observational study used the AVF ultrasound database of the First People’s Hospital of Longquanyi District, Chengdu. A total of 506 patient records from consecutive patients with kidney failure treated with maintenance hemodialysis who underwent color Doppler ultrasound assessment between January 2021 and March 2025 were initially screened. Doppler ultrasound examinations in the database included both routine periodic AVF assessment and examinations performed when access dysfunction was clinically suspected. Eligible patients had a mature forearm native radiocephalic AVF created by end-to-side radial artery-cephalic vein anastomosis and had used the AVF for more than 3 months. For patients who underwent multiple AVF ultrasound examinations during the study period, only the first examination that met the eligibility criteria and had contemporaneous thrill documentation was included; therefore, each patient contributed only one examination to the analysis. Patients were excluded if they had a non-native access, a non-radiocephalic configuration, unstable dialysis blood flow at the time of assessment, or inadequate vascular condition or image quality for reliable measurement. After eligibility screening and verification of key study variables, 32 records were excluded, including 21 because of missing records or incomplete key study variables and 11 because they did not meet the eligibility criteria, leaving 474 patients with recorded thrill category in the final analysis. The study size was based on all eligible available records during the predefined study period; no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. All patient details were de-identified before analysis. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline, 15 and the completed STROBE checklist is provided as supplementary material.

2.2. Ultrasound examination and data collection

All examinations were performed before hemodialysis with the patient supine and the access arm fully exposed using Philips EPIQ 7, EPIQ 8, and EPIQ 7C systems or Mindray Resona 7, Resona 8, and Resona I8 systems equipped with a high-frequency linear-array transducer (7-12 MHz). The inflow tract diameter was measured in the proximal arterial segment and the outflow tract diameter in the distal venous segment. Brachial artery blood flow was measured 1-2 cm proximal to the antecubital fossa. Three consecutive measurements were obtained at the same site, and the median value was used for analysis. Measurements were acquired with an insonation angle below 60° whenever possible and blood flow was calculated from the velocity-time integral and vessel cross-sectional area. The brachial artery RI was calculated as (peak systolic velocity - end-diastolic velocity)/peak systolic velocity. Additional variables extracted from routine clinical and ultrasound records included midpoint venous pressure during dialysis, distal radial artery steal-related findings, thrill category, and lesion-related descriptors when they were documented during routine assessment. Venous pressure was extracted from dialysis treatment records as a recorded pressure range in mmHg, and the midpoint of the recorded range was used for analysis. At the time of ultrasound examination, AVF thrill was assessed by nephrologists and dialysis nurses who had been trained according to the hemodialysis unit’s standard AVF assessment procedure. Palpation was first performed at the anastomotic region to assess inflow-related thrill. The examiner then palpated sequentially along the venous limb toward the heart, including the whole accessible fistula course and the usual cannulation segment. When clinically indicated, the assessment was extended to the upper-arm outflow tract and cephalic arch to screen for possible central venous outflow abnormality. Bilateral hand temperature and vascular filling were also compared as auxiliary bedside findings. In routine documentation, strong thrill referred to a clearly palpable continuous vibration, weak thrill to a reduced or faint palpable vibration, and absent thrill to no palpable thrill at the assessed segment(s). Thrill category was documented before Doppler interpretation. Doppler measurements were not used to assign the clinical thrill category. Because this was a retrospective database study, the documented thrill category should be interpreted as a pragmatic clinical documentation variable rather than a prospectively validated palpation scale. Examiner identity, the exact number of assessors per patient, and inter-observer agreement for palpation were not systematically captured for every record.

2.3. Reproducibility assessment

To assess reproducibility of the key Doppler measurements, a random subset of 50 stored ultrasound examinations was independently remeasured. Two experienced sonographers, who were blinded to each other’s measurements and to the patients’ group assignments, measured brachial artery blood flow and brachial artery RI using the stored ultrasound images and cine loops. The same sonographer repeated the measurements after a two-week interval to assess intra-observer reliability. Reliability was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

2.4. Definitions and analytic grouping

According to the unit’s Doppler protocol, hemodynamically significant stenosis was defined as at least 50% luminal narrowing on B-mode imaging together with a peak systolic velocity ratio ≥2.5. For the present retrospective analysis, a “stenosis-related record” denoted documentation of a stenotic segment or stenosis-site measurements in the routine ultrasound report; this variable was analyzed descriptively and was not treated as an independently adjudicated diagnosis of hemodynamically significant stenosis. Thrombosis was defined as intraluminal echogenic material with loss of compressibility and absent color or spectral Doppler flow. In the original clinical workflow, stenosis or thrombosis suggested by color Doppler ultrasound was further confirmed by fistulography or surgical findings when intervention was clinically indicated. Because confirmatory procedures were not performed systematically in all patients, stenosis- and thrombosis-specific variables were analyzed descriptively as lesion-related records rather than as independently adjudicated reference-standard outcomes.

Patients were categorized as having strong, weak, or absent thrill according to same-visit routine clinical documentation at the time of ultrasound examination. For exploratory ROC analysis and logistic regression, abnormal thrill was defined a priori as weak or absent thrill, with strong thrill as the reference. Distal radial artery steal-related findings were extracted from the original ultrasound records and analyzed descriptively according to the recorded entries, including steal flow, no steal flow, bidirectional flow, occlusion, or unavailable information. Because distal radial flow redistribution may occur in functioning radiocephalic AVFs, this variable was interpreted descriptively and was not treated as a primary dysfunction marker. Near-complete variables were analyzed in the full cohort, whereas lesion-specific analyses were restricted to patients with the corresponding recorded data. Because segment-specific palpation findings were not available in the retrospective dataset, the present analysis focused on the overall association between documented thrill category and contemporaneous Doppler findings rather than lesion localization.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as number and percentage. Group comparisons across the three thrill categories were performed with the Kruskal-Wallis test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. No formal post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed after the overall three-group comparisons; therefore, no adjustment for multiple pairwise comparisons was applied. ROC curve analysis was performed as an exploratory analysis to quantify the discriminatory ability of ultrasound parameters across documented thrill categories rather than to validate thrill palpation as a standalone diagnostic test. The principal multivariable logistic regression model included age, sex, dialysis duration, brachial artery blood flow, brachial artery RI, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disease or heart failure history. Covariates were selected on the basis of clinical relevance and suitability for full-cohort analysis. Blood flow was modeled per 100 mL/min increment and RI per 0.1-unit increment. Collinearity between the key hemodynamic covariates was assessed using correlation analysis and variance inflation factors (VIFs). Sensitivity analyses included a model restricted to patients with strong or weak thrill only and a model restricted to patients with complete stenosis-specific records. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Review Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Longquanyi District, Chengdu, on 22 April 2024 (AF-KY-SB-2024002). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent had been obtained according to institutional requirements, and all patient details were de-identified before analysis.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics and distribution of documented AVF thrill category

Of 506 initially screened patient records, 32 were excluded after eligibility screening and data verification, including 21 because of missing records or incomplete key variables and 11 because they did not meet the eligibility criteria, leaving 474 patients in the final analysis. The final cohort included 367 patients (77.4%) with strong thrill, 67 (14.1%) with weak thrill, and 40 (8.4%) with absent thrill. The distribution of thrill category and the proportions of stenosis- and thrombosis-related records are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. The median age of the full cohort was 59.0 years (IQR 51.0-68.0), and 293 patients (61.8%) were male. Age differed across the three thrill groups (overall P < 0.001), with the highest median age observed in the weak-thrill group; sex and dialysis duration did not differ across groups. 3.2. Hemodynamic gradient across documented thrill categories

Hemodynamic profiles worsened progressively as documented thrill intensity decreased. Brachial artery blood flow showed a marked stepwise decline from the strong-thrill group to the weak-thrill group and then to the absent-thrill group, with median values of 783.0, 206.0, and 86.0 mL/min, respectively (overall P < 0.001). In contrast, brachial artery RI increased across the same groups from 0.53 to 0.78 and 1.00 (overall P < 0.001). Midpoint venous pressure differed across the three thrill groups (overall P < 0.001), with the highest median value observed in the absent-thrill group. These distributions are illustrated in Figure 1. Spearman analysis showed that thrill category was positively correlated with brachial artery blood flow (r = 0.710, P < 0.001) and negatively correlated with brachial artery RI (r = −0.686, P < 0.001).

3.3. Descriptive dysfunction-related records according to documented thrill category

In the overall cohort, 134 patients (28.3%) had stenosis-related records and 63 (13.3%) had thrombosis-related records. These analyses were descriptive because lesion-specific variables were selectively documented during routine care. Stenosis-related records clustered in the weak-thrill group, whereas thrombosis-related records clustered in the absent-thrill group. Among the 133 patients with stenosis-related records and either strong or weak thrill, stenosis in the forearm cephalic vein segment was the most common pattern in both groups. Compared with the strong-thrill group, the weak-thrill group had a smaller stenotic lumen diameter and a higher peak systolic velocity ratio, whereas peak systolic velocity at the stenotic site did not differ significantly (Table 2).

3.4. Exploratory discriminatory ability of ultrasound parameters for abnormal thrill

For distinguishing abnormal thrill from strong thrill in this exploratory analysis, brachial artery blood flow showed the best discrimination, with an AUC of 0.987 (95% CI 0.978–0.993) and a cohort-specific exploratory cutoff of 376 mL/min (sensitivity 98.1%, specificity 92.4%). Brachial artery RI also performed well, with an AUC of 0.969 (95% CI 0.947–0.984) and a cohort-specific exploratory cutoff of 0.69 (sensitivity 87.9%, specificity 95.9%). Midpoint venous pressure during dialysis showed more modest discrimination (AUC 0.672, 95% CI 0.602–0.733). ROC results are summarized in Supplementary Table S1 and illustrated in Figure 2. These results quantify separation between the documented thrill groups and should not be interpreted as diagnostic accuracy for AVF dysfunction. The reported cutoffs are descriptive values derived from this cohort and are not clinically validated thresholds.

ROC curves for exploratory discrimination of abnormal thrill (weak or absent thrill versus strong thrill).

3.5. Data availability for analytic variables

Data completeness was high for the variables used in the full-cohort analyses. Age, sex, dialysis duration, inflow and outflow diameters, brachial artery blood flow, brachial artery RI, midpoint venous pressure during dialysis, documented thrill category, stenosis-related records, and thrombosis-related records were available for all 474 patients. Distal radial artery steal-related findings were available for 465 patients (98.1%). By contrast, stenosis-specific variables and thrombus-characteristic records were selectively documented. Cannulation difficulty, Kt/V, and anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication records were available in subsets of the cohort and were summarized descriptively. Detailed availability of key variables is shown in Supplementary Table S2.

3.6. Reproducibility of Doppler measurements

The intra- and inter-observer reliability for the two key Doppler parameters was high. For brachial artery blood flow, the intra-observer ICC was 0.92 (95% CI 0.86–0.96) and the inter-observer ICC was 0.89 (95% CI 0.81–0.94). For brachial artery RI, the intra-observer ICC was 0.94 (95% CI 0.89–0.97) and the inter-observer ICC was 0.91 (95% CI 0.85–0.95).

3.7. Multivariable analysis for abnormal AVF thrill

In the principal multivariable logistic regression model, lower brachial artery blood flow and higher brachial artery RI remained independently associated with abnormal thrill after adjustment for age, sex, dialysis duration, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disease or heart failure history. Each 100 mL/min increase in blood flow was associated with lower odds of abnormal thrill (adjusted OR 0.25, 95% CI 0.15-0.43; P < 0.001), whereas each 0.1 increase in RI was associated with higher odds (adjusted OR 2.92, 95% CI 1.59-5.34; P < 0.001). Diabetes mellitus (adjusted OR 1.47, 95% CI 0.47-4.61; P = 0.511) and cardiovascular disease or heart failure history (adjusted OR 1.04, 95% CI 0.35-3.05; P = 0.947) were not independently associated with abnormal thrill. Age, sex, and dialysis duration were also not independently associated with abnormal thrill (Table 3). Although brachial artery blood flow and RI were correlated (Pearson r = -0.613, P < 0.001; Spearman rho = -0.767, P < 0.001), their VIF values in the core hemodynamic covariate assessment remained below 2 (blood flow VIF = 1.64; RI VIF = 1.69), indicating no serious collinearity between these two key Doppler parameters (Supplementary Table S3).

3.8. Sensitivity analyses

A sensitivity analysis limited to patients with strong or weak thrill only was performed to determine whether the association persisted after excluding absent-thrill cases, which were enriched for thrombosis-related records. In this analysis, lower brachial artery blood flow and higher brachial artery RI remained independently associated with weak thrill. Each 100 mL/min increase in blood flow was associated with lower odds of weak thrill (adjusted OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.16-0.44; P < 0.001), and each 0.1 increase in RI was associated with higher odds of weak thrill (adjusted OR 2.83, 95% CI 1.53-5.23; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S4). In the sensitivity analysis restricted to the 134 patients with complete stenosis-specific records, lower brachial artery blood flow (adjusted OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.14-0.52; P < 0.001) and higher brachial artery RI (adjusted OR 3.28, 95% CI 1.47-7.34; P = 0.004) remained independently associated with abnormal thrill (Supplementary Table S5).

4. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of mature radiocephalic AVFs, a routinely documented weak or absent thrill was associated with lower brachial artery flow and higher RI at the same assessment. The main implication is a cross-sectional association between bedside documentation and Doppler hemodynamics in everyday practice, not validation of thrill palpation as a standalone surveillance, diagnostic, or lesion-localizing test. The additional sensitivity analysis limited to strong versus weak thrill supported this interpretation, because the association with lower blood flow and higher RI persisted after excluding absent-thrill cases, which were enriched for thrombosis-related records. Descriptive dysfunction-related records also showed that weak thrill was more often accompanied by stenosis-related documentation, whereas absent thrill more often co-occurred with thrombosis-related documentation. Because lesion-specific records were selectively documented during routine care and were not systematically adjudicated in all patients, these patterns should be interpreted as descriptive associations rather than diagnostic evidence.

These findings support the view that bedside examination and ultrasound should be interpreted together rather than as competing approaches.5,6 Caputo et al. found that abnormal duplex flow studies were more sensitive than physical examination alone for identifying fistulas likely to fail or require reintervention. 5 By contrast, Prabhakaran et al. reported excellent diagnostic performance when clinical examination was combined with Doppler relative to fistulography. 6 The specific contribution of our study is the quantitative assessment of routinely documented same-visit thrill categories against contemporaneous Doppler hemodynamics in mature radiocephalic AVFs. In this setting, bedside thrill documentation reflects a clinical finding recorded during routine care, whereas Doppler provides objective hemodynamic measurements; the present analysis evaluates their cross-sectional association rather than the diagnostic performance of one against the other.

The present data are also consistent with recent work emphasizing reduced access flow and increased RI as hemodynamic features of AVF dysfunction.7,8,12,13,16 Oomens et al. recently compared resistive index and volume flow in ultrasound assessment of AVFs for dialysis access. 8 Khayati et al. validated Doppler ultrasound parameters for detecting venous stenosis in patients with AVFs. 7 Other recent studies have likewise highlighted that Doppler thresholds must be interpreted within the specific anatomic and clinical setting of the access.12–14 In particular, Chen et al. evaluated Venturi-effect ultrasonography combined with conventional ultrasound indicators for early AVF stenosis detection in a prospective multicenter study. 14 In our cohort, brachial artery blood flow and RI showed strong separation across documented thrill categories. Their associations with abnormal thrill remained significant after additional adjustment for diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease or heart failure history, reducing concern that these common comorbidities explained the observed hemodynamic differences. Although blood flow and RI were correlated, variance inflation factors were low, suggesting that they provided related but not statistically redundant information in the multivariable model.

The distribution of stenosis- and thrombosis-related records was clinically plausible but should be interpreted cautiously. Weak thrill may correspond to reduced access vibration in the setting of flow reduction or outflow narrowing, whereas absent thrill may occur in patients with severe flow compromise or thrombosis. However, stenosis and thrombosis records in this study were extracted from routine clinical and ultrasound documentation rather than from a systematically applied reference standard across the full cohort. Therefore, weak thrill should not be interpreted as indicating stenosis, and absent thrill should not be interpreted as indicating thrombosis. Prior studies on vascular sound assessment also support the concept that bedside functional findings reflect underlying access dysfunction. Tsuboi et al. showed that a vascular sound visualization system could aid vascular access monitoring, 9 and Park et al. further reported that an auscultation-based deep learning model could quantify AVF stenosis and predict 6-month primary patency. 17 Even so, our data do not show that palpation alone can determine lesion site or replace standardized ultrasound assessment.

From a practical perspective, the present findings should be interpreted as a cross-sectional association between routine thrill documentation and contemporaneous Doppler hemodynamics. Routinely documented weak or absent thrill coincided with lower brachial artery flow and higher resistance at the same assessment, but this study does not establish that thrill can determine which patients should undergo Doppler ultrasound. Whether a standardized bedside examination can inform subsequent ultrasound use requires prospective evaluation with predefined examination protocols and longitudinal outcomes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, and external generalizability should be interpreted cautiously. The ultrasound database included both routine periodic examinations and examinations prompted by suspected access dysfunction; this mixed referral pattern may have enriched the cohort for abnormal accesses and introduced selection bias. Second, although thrill was assessed by trained nephrologists and dialysis nurses according to the unit’s routine AVF assessment procedure, the categories were abstracted from routine documentation rather than collected with a prospectively validated palpation scale. Examiner identity, the exact number of assessors per patient, and inter-observer agreement for palpation were not systematically captured, so misclassification remains possible. Third, because segment-specific palpation findings were unavailable, our analysis cannot determine whether thrill characteristics localize the stenotic lesion. Fourth, lesion-specific stenosis and thrombosis variables were selectively recorded during routine practice and therefore could not be analyzed as systematically measured full-cohort endpoints; confirmatory fistulography or surgical findings were available only when intervention was clinically indicated. Fifth, although major demographic, hemodynamic, and comorbidity variables were included in the adjusted analysis, residual confounding from other access- and dialysis-related clinical factors cannot be completely excluded. In addition, the study assessed same-visit clinical and Doppler findings and lacked a complete longitudinal intervention dataset, which limited evaluation of access patency and post-intervention outcomes. These features support prospective longitudinal validation of the observed clinical-ultrasound associations. 18

5. Conclusion

A documented weak or absent thrill in mature radiocephalic AVFs was associated with lower brachial artery flow and higher RI during the same assessment. These findings demonstrate a cross-sectional association between routine thrill documentation and Doppler hemodynamics. They do not establish a diagnostic threshold, an independent reference-standard relationship for stenosis or thrombosis, or a thrill-based triage strategy. Prospective studies are required to determine whether standardized bedside examination can inform subsequent ultrasound use.
