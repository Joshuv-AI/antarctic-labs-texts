Analysis of bisphenol A-modulated expression of hypothalamic thyroid, estrogen, and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors and concurrent mitochondrial dynamics following short-term exposure in mice
Authors: Daiana Alymbaeva, Attila Zsarnovszky, Csaba Szabo, David Sandor Kiss, Tibor Bartha, Gergely Jocsak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) are synthetic or natural chemicals, often environmental pollutants, that interfere with the endocrine system when humans and animals are exposed to them. EDCs can block natural hormones, as well as function as agonists and antagonists of specific hormones (particularly estrogen and thyroid hormones) (Benvenga et al., 2020, Alsen et al., 2021, Jugan et al., 2010, Lee et al., 2013, Boas et al., 2012, Shanle and Xu, 2011, Watson et al., 2011) disrupting the normal physiological regulation of the body's homeostatic system. The versatile intracellular mechanisms that divert cellular physiology to pathological outcomes are termed Adverse Outcome Pathways (AOP) and have been recently under intense and broad investigation (Klenke et al., 2016, Michałowicz, 2014).

Two primary and interconnected mechanisms are involved in the context of EDC-modulated AOPs. The first mechanism (#1) involves regulatory pathways that maintain physiological balance, optimizing the body's responses to environmental changes (Brix et al., 2023, Britto, 2023, Pisapia et al., 2012, Rutkowska et al., 2023). The second (#2) mechanism adjusts cellular energy expenditure to support homeostatic processes (Chen et al., 2009, Marroqui et al., 2018, Mukherjee and Maitra, 2023).

Among endocrine disruptors, BPA (bisphenol A) is a prominent xenoestrogen first synthesized in 1891 by A. P. Dianin (Zielińska et al., 2019). It is one of the most widely produced synthetic chemicals globally, with annual production exceeding 3.8 million tons and atmospheric emissions of around 100 tons during the same period (Michałowicz, 2014, Huang et al., 2012, Vandenberg et al., 2009, Mohammed et al., 2024).While decades of research have characterized the chronic and developmental consequences of BPA exposure, far fewer studies have addressed acute hypothalamic responses that could initiate long-term disruption. Recent work shows that BPA and its analog bisphenol S (BPS) can impair mitochondrial respiration, increase oxidative stress, and alter bioenergetics in neural and peripheral tissues (Zhang et al., 2024), but whether such effects arise within hours of in vivo exposure has received little attention. Structurally similar to estradiol (Charitos et al., 2022, Shafei et al., 2018, Pollock et al., 2018, Liu et al., 2021) BPA can bind to multiple estrogen receptors (ER) (Liu et al., 2018, MacKay and Abizaid, 2018), including ERα, ERβ, as well as the membrane-associated G protein-coupled receptor (GPR30) (Molina et al., 2021, Cimmino et al., 2019) and estrogen receptor γ (ERγ) (Tohmé et al., 2014), thereby activating non-classical ER pathways (Carchia et al., 2015, Seachrist et al., 2016, Ariemma et al., 2016). It competes with 17β-estradiol for binding to ERs, triggering endocrine receptor signaling pathways, and disrupting normal physiological processes, even at low doses (Konieczna et al., 2015, Gao et al., 2015, Gassman, 2017, Ullah et al., 2018, Zsarnovszky et al., 2018). Additionally, BPA influences development through non-estrogenic and non-androgenic pathways by binding to other receptors, such as the thyroid hormone receptor (TR), glucocorticoid receptor, and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) (Zoeller et al., 2005, Zhang et al., 2018, Prasanth et al., 2010, Kwintkiewicz et al., 2010).

EDCs may alter cellular metabolism by binding hormone nuclear receptors (estrogen, thyroid) and hijacking transcription of metabolic genes. They disrupt metabolic enzymes, hormones, mitochondrial dynamics and oxidative stress responses, upsetting cellular energy balance (Marroqui et al., 2018, Zhou et al., 2020, He et al., 2024, Rizzo et al., 2023, Nadal et al., 2017). BPA, a prototypical EDC, has strong estrogenic activity (binding ERα/β, GPER, ERRs) (Hafezi and Abdel-Rahman, 2019) and antagonizes thyroid hormone action (Kim and Park, 2019). While the behavioral effects of EDCs are well-documented, neural changes in pre-pubertal animals have been less explored. BPA exposure impairs cellular energy metabolism: in neurons, low-dose BPA disrupts mitochondrial structure, reducing oxidative phosphorylation capacity and ATP production, leading to oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, mitophagy and apoptosis (Wong et al., 2003, Chevrier et al., 2013, Nakamura et al., 2012, Yang et al., 2023, Meng et al., 2024). BPA also reprograms hypothalamic feeding circuits: perinatal BPA downregulates the cannabinoid‐1 receptor and activates anorexigenic neuropeptides, lowering food intake and adiposity. BPA’s modulation of estrogen and thyroid signaling thus alters mitochondrial function and cellular energy expenditure (Meng et al., 2024, Suglia et al., 2016).

Although our previous in vitro studies have revealed aspects of BPA’s disruptive potential (Suglia et al., 2016, Le Corre et al., 2015, Walsh et al., 1982), it remains unclear how these effects begin to manifest in vivo — particularly the very first molecular and cellular changes that may occur within only a few hours of exposure. While BPA’s influence on hypothalamic reproductive peptides has been documented, its prompt impact on hypothalamic pathways regulating energy balance has received little attention. This gap underscores the need to determine how BPA initiates alterations in the hypothalamic control of energy homeostasis (Le Corre et al., 2015).

Positioned in a region of the blood–brain barrier with higher permeability, the arcuate nucleus (ARC) in the hypothalamus is particularly exposed to circulating EDCs. The hypothalamus contains orexigenic neurons, such as agouti-related peptide (AgRP) and anorexigenic pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons, which integrate peripheral signals to regulate energy balance (Walsh et al., 1982, Hall et al., 2012). Exogenous chemicals that influence these neurons can disrupt this delicate system, resulting in the dysregulation of energy homeostasis.

BPA exposure has been shown to increase AgRP and POMC mRNA expression in mouse hypothalamic neurons, suggesting dysregulation of the melanocortin system at the transcriptional level (Loganathan et al., 2021). Notably, the transient upregulation of POMC observed at 4 h was mediated by neuroinflammation and the transcription factor PPARγ. However, inhibiting these pathways failed to suppress BPA-induced AgRP upregulation, pointing to a distinct, PPARγ-independent mechanism. As AgRP is a potent orexigenic neuropeptide, elucidating the pathways underlying its transcriptional activation by BPA is critical to understanding the central mechanisms of BPA-induced metabolic disruption. BPA's obesogenic effects have been well-documented in relation to peripheral metabolism (Legeay and Faure, 2017).

Mitochondria in somatic cells primarily generate ATP to support general metabolic functions. Neurons, however, are particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction due to their high demand for mitochondria near synapses (Devine and Kittler, 2018, Breckwoldt et al., 2014, Misgeld and Schwarz, 2017, Nayak et al., 2022). ARC neurons are highly dynamic in regulating the body’s energy balance, and interestingly, unlike in most somatic cells, mitochondria in AgRP and POMC neurons act as energy sensors for their host neurons. Thus, mitochondria in these neurons not only generate ATP, but also serve as indicators of the organism's overall energy status (Angelogianni et al., 2000).

Hormone receptors like ERs, TRs, and PPARs significantly influence mitochondrial activity through both direct and indirect mechanisms. These effects include interactions with nuclear and extranuclear receptors, as well as non-genomic actions involving other organelles and intracellular processes (Alymbaeva et al., 2024, Herwig et al., 2008). With the abundant presence of ERs and TRs in the hypothalamus, and the ability of EDCs to cross the blood–brain barrier, it is essential to understand both their individual and combined influence on mitochondrial function (Chatterjee et al., 2021). Given that mitochondria are critical regulators of cellular energy, and that ERs and TRs modulate mitochondrial function, the presence of these receptors in the hypothalamus positions mitochondria as primary targets for EDCs and early responders to metabolic disturbances.

We propose that BPA affects intracellular pathways, leading to altered organelle function, particularly in mitochondria, which are highly sensitive to hormone-mimicking chemicals as presently tested in AgRP and POMC cells. We hypothesize that, within a short in vivo timeframe, three environmentally relevant doses of BPA can rapidly influence hypothalamic signaling by modulating the expression of mRNAs of nuclear receptors such as ERα, ERβ, TRα, TRβ, and PPARγ, offering insight into early endocrine and metabolic responses. To explore this, we investigated mitochondrial respiration rates (MRR), a key indicator of mitochondrial metabolic activity, and examined mitochondrial morphology in these neurons within a rapid 6-hour timeframe. A short, 6-hour in vivo exposure window enables the detection of early cellular responses to BPA before compensatory systemic changes occur. Importantly, in this study we aimed to capture such prompt, initiating effects, as they may represent the early events that ultimately lead to the well-described alterations observed in studies employing longer exposure times. This timeframe is particularly valuable for identifying rapid mitochondrial alterations, which may precede shifts in nuclear gene expression or tissue morphology. As mitochondria are often among the first organelles to respond to endocrine disruptors, especially in energy-regulating hypothalamic neurons, capturing these time-sensitive effects provides mechanistic insight into the initial stages of endocrine and metabolic disruption.

Materials and methods

Animals

In this study, 18-day-old C57BL/6 (B6J substrain) mice (purchased from HAS Biological Research Centre, Szeged, Hungary) were used. This developmental stage represents a clearly pre-pubertal period, preceding the onset of sexual maturity (vaginal opening in C57BL/6 females typically occurs at ∼ P25–33). At this age, animals are within a hormone-sensitive window, when steroid target regions of the hypothalamus are still maturing and therefore particularly vulnerable to external influences. Pilot experiments and literature data indicate no major sex differences in hypothalamic parameters at this stage (Blaustein et al., 2016, Diamanti-Kandarakis et al., 2009, Sominsky et al., 2018, Walker et al., 2012, Sokolowski et al., 2024, Cortes et al., 2022, Rubin et al., 2006), allowing both males and females to be included (body weight 9–10 g, a total of 60 animals were used in the study: 20 per analysis, i.e., 5 animals per group across 4 treatment groups for each of the 3 analyses: MRR, electron microscopy, and PCR).

The development of hypothalamic neuroendocrine systems is highly dynamic during this pre-pubertal period: estrogen receptor expression and other steroid-sensitive markers are actively regulated, and key puberty-gate circuits, such as kisspeptin–GnRH signaling, undergo postnatal maturation. Thus, P18 animals provide an informative model for investigating how early exposures may shape later endocrine outcomes (Friedman et al., 1983, Nah et al., 2011, Sengupta et al., 2021, Brydges, 2016, Clarkson and Herbison, 2006). Although this age is relatively under-studied in the context of endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs), it is well-suited to address whether acute BPA exposure elicits organizational (modificational) versus activational effects on hypothalamic circuits. The short exposure window applied here (6 h) is relevant to both categories of effects.

Animals were housed under a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle and provided regular chow (FarmerMix Kft., Zsambek, Hungary) with ad libitum access to tap water. All experimental procedures were performed at the University of Veterinary Medicine (Budapest, Hungary) in compliance with ARRIVE guidelines and EU Directive 2010/63/EU. The study was reviewed and approved by the Animal Health and Welfare Directorate of the National Food Chain Safety Office (permit #.: XIV-I-001/2202–4/2012, PEI/001/665–8/2015, and PE/EA/1252–6/2016).

Animals were assigned to experimental groups based on the administered doses, along with a control group that received only the vehicle (Ctrl).

Six hours prior to sampling, animals received a single intraperitoneal injection of BPA, purity: ≥99 %; 527 CAS: 80–05-7), obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The injections were administered in three environmentally relevant doses: 40 µg, 5 mg, and 10 mg of BPA per 1000 g of body weight, dissolved in a solution of 0.9 % NaCl and 0.1 % dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO).

Hypothalamic samples were analyzed for the mRNA expression levels of ERα, ERβ, TRα, TRβ, and PPARγ.

The selected doses of 40 µg/kg, 5 mg/kg, and 10 mg/kg bw for BPA administration were chosen to represent low to moderate environmentally relevant exposures while allowing for the assessment of potential dose-dependent effects. The 40 µg/kg dose is particularly relevant as it is close to the U.S. EPA reference dose (https://ntp.niehs.nih.gov/sites/default/files/ntp/pressctr/mtgs_wkshps/2000/lowdosepeerfinalrpt.pdf) of 50 µg/kg/day. The EPA has estimated the lowest-observed-adverse-effect level (LOAEL) for oral exposure to BPA in rats to be 50 mg/kg/day (U.S. EPA, 1988). Based on this LOAEL, the U.S. EPA Low-Dose Peer Review Subpanel (2001) established 5 mg/kg/day as a cutoff dose for assessing low-dose effects, regardless of the route of exposure, duration, or life stage at which exposure occurs (EFSA, 2010).

Sampling

Sampling for tissue homogenates

Tissue sampling was conducted through rapid guillotine decapitation under deep isoflurane anesthesia, six hours post-treatment, as previously described (Scalise et al., 2012, Jocsak et al., 2019, Kiss et al., 2018). The removal of hypothalamic tissue adhered to an anatomical protocol detailed in prior studies (Toth et al., 2014). In brief, the hypothalami were dissected from the excised brains in the anterior-posterior direction between the caudal margin of the optic chiasm and the rostral margin of the mammillary body, and in the dorsoventral direction below the upper margin of the fornix. Both sides of the hypothalami were utilized for tissue homogenate preparation. Although efforts were made to develop a method for measuring MRR in relatively small samples (Kiss et al., 2016), using only half of the mouse hypothalamus resulted in a sample size that was insufficient to obtain MRR data reliably. The collected samples were subsequently used to determine the levels of receptor mRNAs and mitochondrial respiration rates, as outlined below.

Sampling for electron microscopy

For electron microscopy, anesthetized animals were perfused through the left cardiac ventricle with 50 mL of 0.9 % NaCl, followed by 250 mL of a fixative solution containing 4 % paraformaldehyde and 0.5 % glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB) at pH 7.4. Following the perfusion, the isolated brains were placed in glutaraldehyde-free fixative (4 % paraformaldehyde) at 4 °C for an additional three hours. Samples were then prepared for immunohistochemistry (IHC) by cutting the isolated brain blocks into 50-μm-thick sections. This thickness ensures optimal preservation of tissue morphology and staining efficiency. Specifically, sections within the 20–50 μm range facilitate effective antibody penetration when employing 4 % paraformaldehyde fixation.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from mouse hypothalami using TRI reagent, following the manufacturer’s protocol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, #AM9738), and subsequently purified with the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA, #R2051). RNA concentrations were determined spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop™ ND-1000 (Wilmington, NC, USA), measuring absorbance at 260–280 nm. A total of 3 μg/μL of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using RT-PCR (Amplitron II, Barnstead/Thermolyne, Dubuque, IA, USA) with M−MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega Corporation, Wisconsin, USA, #M1701), oligo (dt) primers, and a dNTP mix.

For qRT-PCR analysis, 2 μL of the resulting cDNA samples were analyzed in triplicates using Master SYBR Green (F. Hoffmann-La Roche, Basel, Switzerland) on a LightCycler 2.0 device (F. Hoffmann-La Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The stability of candidate reference genes – β-actin (ACTB), beta-glucuronidase (GUSB), glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), β-2-microglobulin (B2M), and transferrin receptor protein 1 (TFRC) – was assessed to normalize transcription levels of target genes. GAPDH was identified as the most consistently expressed gene across experimental conditions, as determined by the GeNorm and NormFinder algorithms, accessed via the online platform RefFinder.

Primer pairs were either designed using NCBI’s Primer-BLAST or sourced from the literature and were used at a concentration of 2 μM. The primer sequences for GAPDH, ERα, ERβ, TRα, TRβ, and PPARγ are listed in Jocsak et al., (2019) (Jocsak et al., 2019) (see Table 1), qPCR cycles and controls were established according to the manufacturer’s instructions and optimized for the specific primer pairs as described by Jocsak et al., (2016) (Jocsak et al., 2016). The real-time PCR threshold cycle (Ct) data were analyzed using REST-XL software version 2.0 (GenEx-BioMcc, TUM, München, Germany) (Pfaffl et al., 2002). Mitochondrial metabolism

Isolation of mitochondria

Hypothalamic samples were prepared for oxygen consumption measurement following the method outlined by Kiss et al. (2016) (Kiss et al., 2016). In brief, unit amounts of hypothalamic tissue homogenates were combined with an isolation buffer for mitochondrial purification using Percoll gradient fractionation. The mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate was then measured with a Clark-type oxygen electrode (Oxytherm, Hansatech Instruments, Norfolk, UK) at 37 °C. The values obtained represent the MRR, expressed in nmol O2 consumed per ml of final volume per minute. It is important to note that mitochondrial samples were prepared using both the left and right sides of the hypothalami together, without separate processing or measurement of each side; samples were taken from animals that received the exact same treatment as described above.

Definition of mitochondrial respiration states

The nomenclature for various mitochondrial respiration states varies across the literature. In this study, we define and explain our terminology for mitochondrial respiration states, which were measured sequentially (60 s for each state) as follows (please see: (Toth et al., 2014).State 1 − Mitochondrial Respiration (St 1): In this initial step, mitochondrial oxygen consumption was measured using a respiration buffer without any added substrates that could influence respiration. Under these conditions, the rate of oxygen consumption per unit time depends on the current metabolic state of the hypothalamic sample and its original oxygen supply.State 2 − Mitochondrial Respiration (St 2): To enhance the Krebs cycle, we added 5 μL of pyruvate and 2.5 μL of malate to the sample. This addition stimulates the Krebs cycle, resulting in increased oxygen consumption due to enhanced terminal oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation, provided that the prior in vivo blood/oxygen supply to the hypothalamic tissue was sufficient and that downregulating mechanisms were inactive.State 3 − Mitochondrial Respiration (St 3): We then introduced 2.5 μL of adenosine diphosphate (ADP) into the sample. ADP significantly upregulates mitochondrial respiration, increasing mitochondrial respiration rate (MRR) if the prior in vivo blood/fuel supply to the hypothalamic tissue was adequate.State 4 − Mitochondrial Respiration (St 4): Following this, we added 1 μL of oligomycin, an ATP-synthase blocker that inhibits oxidative phosphorylation (ATP synthesis) while allowing terminal oxidation to continue. Under these conditions, oxygen consumption reflects the actual uncoupled state and alternative oxidation occurring in the mitochondria. Increased oxygen consumption in this scenario indicates either a reduction in previously upregulated processes or an attempt by the mitochondria to downregulate ATP synthesis.State 5 − Mitochondrial Respiration (St 5): Finally, we added 2.5 μL of carbonylcyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy)-phenyl-hydrazone (FCCP), a cyanide derivative that binds to and blocks cytochrome C oxidase, depleting any remaining oxygen from the sample. The decrease in oxygen levels in this state is contingent upon the actual/initial metabolic state of the sampled tissue and the total amount of oxygen consumed during State 1 through State 4. The total oxygen consumption from State 1 to State 4, combined with the remaining oxygen consumed by FCCP, serves as a good reference for the blood/oxygen supply of the tissue at the time of the animal's sacrifice.

Although all five mitochondrial respiration states were sequentially measured and evaluated, this study primarily presents the mitochondrial respiration rates from State 3 and State 4. These parameters are the most relevant for characterizing mitochondrial and tissue metabolism, as State 3 provides insight into mitochondrial activity regulated by the ADP/ATP ratio. At the same time, State 4 reflects the degree of uncoupling and the activity of alternative oxidases—two crucial factors in the transient downregulation of ATP biosynthesis when cellular energy needs to decrease.

Immunocytochemistry & electron microscopy

50 μm thick tissue slices were rinsed in 1 % sodium borohydride in phosphate buffer (PB) for 15 min to eliminate unbound aldehydes. The primary antibodies used for labeling included a polyclonal primary antibody against POMC (Invitrogen, PA5-18368, diluted 1:1500, incubated overnight) and an antibody against AgRP (Invitrogen, PA5-78739, diluted 1:200, also incubated overnight). A biotinylated polyclonal goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen, 65–6140, diluted 1:200, incubated for 1 h) was applied for detection. The development process utilized avidin–biotin complex (ABC) kits (#Pk.4001) along with diaminobenzidine (DAB, CAS: 91–95-2) as a chromogen.

For the electron microscopic analysis, the sections were osmicated in 1 % osmium tetroxide (OsO4) in PB for 30 min, followed by dehydration through increasing ethanol concentrations. The samples were then embedded in Durcupan ACM resin (Sigma-Aldrich, 44610). After embedding, ultrathin sections, measuring 50 nm in thickness, were cut to facilitate precise examination of the targeted areas.

The prepared sections were analyzed using a JEOL-1011 transmission electron microscope (TEM) (JEOL USA, Peabody, MA, USA). The calibrated electron micrographs were assessed with NIH ImageJ software (version 1.52n). The measured parameters included the occurrence (number per area) and size of mitochondria, as well as the matrix-to-entire mitochondrion ratio.

To confirm the quality of tissue preparation and ultrastructural preservation, representative electron micrographs of hypothalamic neurons from BPA-treated mice were examined. Representative hypothalamic neurons were processed for transmission electron microscopy. Tissue samples were fixed, stained, and embedded following standard protocols to allow visualization of subcellular structures. Representative images illustrating the quality of staining and preservation are provided in the Supplemental Materials (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Data analysis

Statistical analysis for this study was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) in accordance with the guidelines provided by the Department of Biomathematics at the University of Veterinary Medicine, Budapest, Hungary.

Before conducting the analysis, the normality of the data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Since the data sets exhibited a normal distribution, one-way ANOVA was employed, followed by Bonferroni or Dunnett’s tests for post hoc comparisons, depending on the specific analysis: PCR and MRR were analyzed with Bonferroni's test, while structural measurements utilized Dunnett’s test. The homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test.

Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical tests were two-tailed, with p-values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

The complete dataset, including MRR gene expression values, ELMI results, and raw PCR data with related diagrams, has been deposited in Figshare and is accessible via the https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.28955471.v1.

A dose-dependent trend could be observed from the mean values, but these did not reach statistical significance compared with control. In contrast, PPARγ expression was significantly upregulated at 40 µg/kg, whereas the higher doses (5 and 10 mg/kg) showed no difference from control (Fig. 1E). Mitochondrial respiration states 3 and 4

The results from mitochondrial respiration rate (MRR) measurements indicate a linear-like dose response for State 3. Although significant differences were observed among the treatment groups, none demonstrated a statistically significant difference from the controls [16,92 nmol O2/ml/min in State 3 and 6,45 nmol O2/ml/min in State 4, respectively], (Fig. 1F). According to the definitions provided in the Materials and Methods section, the lowest dose of BPA (40 µg/kg) did not affect either State 3 [13.16 nmol O2/ml/min] or State 4 [4.43 nmol O2/ml/min], although the standard deviation values were minimized. In contrast, higher doses (5 mg/kg [23.81 nmol O2/ml/min in State 3 and 6.16 nmol O2/ml/min in State 4] and 10 mg/kg [27.05 nmol O2/ml/min in State 3 and 6.53 nmol O2/ml/min in State 4] significantly increased State 3 compared to the 40 µg/kg group; however, there were still no significant differences when compared to the Ctrl. Mitochondrial ultrastructure

In both AgRP (Fig. 2A, C, E) and POMC (Fig. 2B, D, F) neurons, BPA exposure led to notable alterations in mitochondrial morphology. The size of mitochondria increased significantly only in animals treated with 5 mg/kg BPA (p < 0.001 in AgRP; p < 0.05 in POMC), whereas no significant differences were observed in the 40 μg/kg and 10 mg/kg groups compared to the control (Fig. 2A, B). The number of mitochondria per 10 μm2 section area in AgRP neurons exhibited an inverse dose-dependent decrease (p < 0.1), while in POMC neurons, only the 5 mg/kg group showed a significant reduction compared to the control (Fig. 2C, D). Discussion

The following discussion interprets the acute, juvenile effects of BPA on hypothalamic melanocortin neurons across molecular, metabolic, and ultrastructural levels.

Receptor mRNA levels

Extensive studies have demonstrated that BPA acts in a dose-dependent and cell type-specific manner as either an agonist or antagonist of ERα and ERβ, a phenomenon also observed within the hypothalamus (Nesan et al., 2018, Kuiper et al., 1998, Cao et al., 2014, Gould et al., 1998, Marraudino et al., 2021). However, literature on BPA’s effects on the thyroid system remains sparse and insufficient for a comprehensive discussion here (Chevrier et al., 2013, Nakamura et al., 2012). Following short-term episodic BPA exposure, no significant changes were detected in the expression levels of the examined estrogen or thyroid receptors. Interestingly, however, mean values exhibited a non-significant trend resembling a U-shaped dose–response pattern (Fig. 1A–C), consistent with our previous in vitro findings in cerebellar granule cells where significant differences were observed (Scalise et al., 2012, Kiss et al., 2018). This pattern suggests an underlying complex interplay of hormone receptor interactions and cellular feedback mechanisms, characteristic of many endocrine disruptors. To elucidate the temporal dynamics of BPA’s effects on these hormone systems, future studies should employ a broader range of exposure doses and durations, as well as assess additional functional endpoints of the respective endocrine pathways.

Additionally, our results indicate a dose-responsive effect on PPARγ expression, with a significant increase at the lowest BPA dose. In contrast, ERα, ERβ, and TRα expression levels were not significantly different from controls. The observed PPARγ effect parallels findings in visceral adipocytes and may reflect not only shifts in cellular energy demand but also the activation of protective pathways against oxidative stress and hypoxia, events that are outcomes of impaired mitochondrial function (Rzemieniec et al., 2018).

Our findings are partly corroborated by previous studies on hypothalamic neurons. Salehi et al. 2019 (Salehi et al., 2019) demonstrated that cultured mouse-derived POMC neurons exposed to BPA exhibited a dose-dependent upregulation of POMC expression, accompanied by increased neuroinflammatory marker levels, suggesting direct modulation of hypothalamic circuits through PPARγ signaling. This aligns with our observation of dose-dependent alterations in PPARγ mRNA expression, indicating that even short-term BPA exposure can influence nuclear receptor activity in vivo. Conversely, Marraudino et al. 2021 (Marraudino et al., 2021) reported that chronic BPA exposure in adult male mice reduced hypothalamic POMC and NPY immunoreactivity, suggesting that prolonged exposure impairs neuropeptide signaling. Together, these findings support the notion that BPA affects POMC neurons in a time-dependent manner—acute exposure enhances transcriptional and metabolic activity, while chronic exposure leads to functional downregulation, consistent with our observed mitochondrial adaptations. Future studies should therefore aim to verify whether BPA induces an inverted U-shaped dose–response in PPARγ expression and to further clarify the mechanistic relationship between PPARγ signaling, mitochondrial dynamics, and hypothalamic neuron function under varying exposure conditions.

Mitochondrial respiration State 3–4

In contrast to most related previous findings, mitochondrial metabolism (MRR results) showed no significant changes compared to control data, likely reflecting the short-term exposure window applied. However, the BPA-treated groups exhibited a linear-like dose–response relationship, at least for State 3 respiration (which reflects a robust measure of mitochondrial function and coupling efficiency), suggesting the activation of BPA-responsive metabolic processes and the consequent increase in mitochondrial oxygen consumption. However, the less-affected State 4 respiration suggests that while ATP production increased (with higher BPA-dose), the subsequent down-regulatory processes (e.g., reduced oxidative phosphorylation or energy conservation mechanisms) were not as significantly activated during this early phase of exposure (Fig. 1F). It is also to note that State 3–4 values did not follow the same pattern (lack of effects compared to the control data in the case of ERs and TRs, or the pattern seen in PPARγ) as the examined receptor mRNA expression after BPA treatment. State 3–4 values reflect the overall intensity of mitochondrial respiration (metabolism) rather than individual mitochondrial activity. The higher respiration rates observed in State 3 correlate with an increased ATP demand triggered by BPA exposure, suggesting that the cell responds by ramping up energy production through mitochondrial metabolism. The differential pattern in respiration states reflects time-dependent changes in mitochondrial function after BPA exposure. Initially, mitochondria prioritize ATP production, increasing oxygen consumption (higher State 3 values).

However, this heightened metabolic activity is not immediately followed by significant down-regulation, likely reflecting the cell’s ongoing adjustment to stress. Over longer periods, the mitochondrial response may shift toward adaptive or protective mechanisms, which could explain the lack of significant changes in State 4 values observed in our study. Evidence from studies on chronic BPA exposure supports this idea: prolonged exposure has been shown to affect mitochondrial biogenesis, reduce oxidative phosphorylation capacity, and increase reactive oxygen species production in various cell types (Nayak et al., 2022, Tran et al., 2020, Azevedo et al., 2019). These findings suggest that extended exposure may reveal cumulative mitochondrial adaptations or dysfunctions not captured during shorter experimental periods, highlighting the importance of evaluating mitochondrial responses over longer durations in future studies.

Mitochondrial ultrastructure

Offering a multifaceted view of the rapid effects of BPA exposure on main melanocortin neurons, the ultrastructural changes in mitochondria suggest that mitochondrial dynamics are more sensitive to BPA concentration than indicated by mitochondrial activity measurements alone (Fig. 2). The observed non-linear dose response to BPA treatment suggests a compensatory response to low or moderate mitochondrial stress or dysfunction levels. This interpretation is supported by the results on the number of mitochondria, which indicate impaired mitochondrial biogenesis or enhanced degradation (e.g., mitophagy) in response to BPA exposure (Fig. 2C). The combination of reduced mitochondrial count and altered size suggests a significant disruption in mitochondrial turnover, likely impairing cellular energy homeostasis. An increased matrix-to-total mitochondria ratio suggests a reduction in outer membrane surface area relative to the matrix, which may reflect mitochondrial swelling or loss of membrane integrity (Fig. 2E, F). Such changes indicate mitochondrial stress, potentially driven by oxidative damage or calcium overload, leading to structural remodeling of the mitochondria (Lința et al., 2024). These alterations are consistent with mitochondrial adaptations to stress, possibly aimed at compensating for BPA-induced disruptions in energy demand or ROS production (Gassman, 2017). Interestingly, despite these structural changes, ATP production seems to be preserved. This preservation suggests that compensatory mechanisms may maintain mitochondrial energy output, as the inner mitochondrial membrane's integrity and the electron transport chain remain functionally intact, at least after a short and single BPA exposure.

Comparing the two examined melanocortin neurons, one could observe that both AgRP and POMC neurons respond similarly to varying BPA doses. These shifts indicate disturbed mitochondrial dynamics, which, over time, may impair hunger and satiety signaling and contribute to metabolic disorders such as obesity or diabetes (Loganathan et al., 2021, Salehi et al., 2019). This is in concert with previous findings demonstrating that low- or high-dose perinatal exposure, BPA disrupts the hypothalamic regulation of energy expenditure, leading to either loss or gain of weight, after low- or high-dose perinatal exposure, respectively (Tyl et al., 2008, Tyl et al., 2006). According to these, BPA increases the expression of the orexigenic peptides neuropeptide Y (NPY) and AgRP in hypothalamic cell cultures, while decreasing that of the POMC (Marraudino et al., 2021, Salehi et al., 2019).

Limitations

A limitation of the present study is that receptor protein expression was not assessed alongside mRNA levels. However, both our previous work and the literature indicate that mRNA and protein levels of hormone receptors do not always correlate, reflecting post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms that can buffer gene expression (Jocsak et al., 2016, Somogyi et al., 2016). Future studies should include more downstream readouts, such as assessment of components of the intracellular signaling pathways of ERs and TRs, ideally using time-course analyses. Complementary in vitro approaches may also be valuable to explore temporal dynamics and to elucidate the direct cellular effects of BPA.

Another limitation is that this study was conducted in rodents, caution is warranted when extrapolating the findings directly to humans. Nevertheless, rodent models provide valuable insights into conserved neuroendocrine mechanisms and critical developmental windows that are relevant to public health concerns regarding BPA exposure.

Conclusion

This study confirms that even low doses of BPA trigger immediate biological effects, rapidly altering hormone systems, and acting as an endocrine disruptor targeting the neuroendocrine center. BPA modulates energy expenditure by affecting metabolism, appetite, and thermogenesis via hypothalamic signaling, and impacts mitochondrial dynamics, resembling changes from chronic high-fat diets. These effects, involving intracellular pathways and cellular energy regulation, underscore BPA’s influence on the brain–periphery axis, with potential long-term risks for endocrine and metabolic disorders. The findings highlight the need for further research on BPA’s dose- and time-dependent impacts.
