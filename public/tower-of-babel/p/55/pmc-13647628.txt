Unknotting the nexus of asthma and neuroinflammation: from brain network alterations to therapeutic implications
Authors: Wenjie Hu, Yanan Zou, Junbo Yin, Lei Liu, Yujie Ding, Xingzhe Gao, Yan Li, Xiangyi Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsthma is increasingly recognized as a systemic inflammatory syndrome that extends beyond the respiratory tract, with emerging evidence highlighting its relevance to neuroinflammation. The lung–brain axis—via interconnected inflammatory, vascular, metabolic, neural, and microbial pathways—provides a framework for understanding how chronic pulmonary disease may sustain or exacerbate neuroinflammatory processes. Mechanistically, asthma promotes blood–brain barrier disruption, systemic inflammation that seeds central neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, gut–lung–brain microbial crosstalk, and sleep fragmentation, all of which are biologically plausible drivers of sustained neuroinflammatory states and consequent neurodegenerative vulnerability. Epidemiological studies link asthma to increased risks of all-cause dementia and Alzheimer’s disease, though cohort findings vary. Neuroimaging and biomarker evidence further support neuroinflammatory involvement, revealing altered hippocampal metabolism, white matter abnormalities, elevated plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein and neurofilament light chains, and cerebrospinal fluid markers of synaptic injury in severe or poorly controlled asthma—each reflecting neuroinflammatory or neurodegenerative sequelae. Links with Parkinson’s disease and other neurodegenerative disorders remain more preliminary. Notably, asthma severity, phenotype, exacerbation frequency, and corticosteroid burden modulate neurological risk, suggesting that optimal disease control—potentially enhanced by biologic therapies—may confer neuroprotective benefits by dampening neuroinflammation. In conclusion, asthma should not be viewed solely as an airway disorder but as a potentially modifiable contributor to long-term brain vulnerability via neuroinflammatory pathways within the lung–brain axis. Future longitudinal studies integrating detailed phenotyping, biomarkers, and neuroimaging are needed to establish causality and guide anti-neuroinflammatory therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Asthma, Lung-brain axis, Neuroinflammation, Neuropsychiatric disorders, Blood–Brain Barrier

Introduction

The pathophysiological impact of asthma extends beyond the airway lumen, with contemporary research increasingly conceptualizing this disease as a chronic inflammatory syndrome involving multiple interconnected systems. In clinically stable adults with asthma, vascular dysfunction is already detectable [1]. Early-life allergic asthma has been shown to disrupt endothelial–pericyte interactions [2], while asthma-associated endothelial injury can further exacerbate vascular pathology in experimental models [3]. At the epithelial level, severe T2-high asthma is associated with IgE-FcεRI-driven barrier disruption [4], impaired epithelial attachment and barrier formation [5]. In contrast, transcriptomic comparisons indicate that mitochondrial dysfunction-related epithelial signatures are more prominently associated with the T2-low asthma phenotype [6]. Exposure to house dust mite can induce epithelial ferroptosis [7], and particulate matter exposure further compromises epithelial barrier integrity [8]. Microbiome perturbation constitutes an additional layer of chronic inflammatory priming [9]. This broader conceptual framework is critical, as endogenous circadian rhythms exacerbate asthma symptoms at night [10], nocturnal asthma attacks are associated with altered sleep parameters and inflammatory markers [11], and nighttime respiratory symptoms are shaped by comorbidity burden [12]. Intermittent hypoxia can increase blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability [13], remodel brainstem cellular programs [14], drive neuroinflammation via mitophagy-related mechanisms [15], and contribute to hippocampal inflammatory cognitive injury in sleep apnea models [16]. Corticosteroid exposure introduces an additional neuropsychiatric dimension [17]. From a clinical perspective, asthma should no longer be viewed merely as a respiratory disease with occasional psychiatric comorbidity; rather, it represents a chronic stress state originating in the lungs, capable of reshaping immune, vascular, and neural homeostasis far beyond the bronchial tree.

The lung–brain axis provides a useful framework for this conceptual shift. In its modern formulation, this axis comprises at least four interacting pathways. First, pulmonary inflammation generates circulating cytokines, alarmins, lipids, and injury-related signals, while sensory neurons help maintain immune homeostasis in the lung [18]. Sensory neurons also regulate humoral immunity in experimental asthma [19]. In established asthma, cytokines can directly reprogram airway sensory neurons [20]. Neurogenic inflammatory signaling is sufficiently significant that TRPA1 inhibition can suppress airway contraction and neurogenic inflammation [21]. Second, dyspnea, nocturnal symptoms, and hypoxemia alter cerebral energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and sleep architecture. Third, sensory and autonomic neural circuits connect inflamed airways to the brainstem and higher-order limbic and cortical regions; brainstem Dbh-positive neurons can directly control allergen-induced airway hyperreactivity [22]. Allergen exposure can also disrupt amygdala–respiration coupling [23]. In parallel, allergic airway inflammation can induce depression-like behavior accompanied by altered prefrontal–hippocampal circuitry [24]. Fourth, the respiratory microbiome and the broader gut–lung–brain axis modulate immune tone and the pre-activated state of microglia; the lung microbiome itself can regulate brain autoimmunity [25]. These concepts are no longer speculative but are now supported by direct cellular, circuit, and host–microbe studies.

This axis is intimately associated with neurodegenerative diseases, as the biological processes involved play central roles in conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), Huntington’s disease (HD), multiple sclerosis (MS), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). BBB damage has been demonstrated in experimental cerebral amyloid angiopathy [26]. Experimental models have directly linked asthma to cerebral amyloid pathology. House dust mite-induced asthma promotes peripheral inflammation that drives hippocampal Aβ deposition, astrocyte and microglial activation, neuroinflammation, and blood–brain barrier compromise, thereby accelerating Alzheimer’s disease pathology in susceptible mouse models [27]. Mechanistically, asthma upregulates the NF-κB pathway and LTD4/CysLT1R signaling, which exacerbates hippocampal Aβ deposition, neuroinflammation, and synaptic damage in APP/PS1 mice [28]. Regional BBB permeability also correlates with aging and AD biomarkers in cognitively normal older adults [29]. Broader assessments further link BBB dysfunction with AD pathology, cognitive impairment, and neuroinflammation [30]. Longitudinal work connects BBB disruption with biomarker changes and cognitive decline [31]. Peripheral immunity is associated with the risk of incident dementia and, more broadly, with incident brain disorders [32, 33]. Adaptive immune changes track clinical progression in AD [34]. Sleep deprivation can further exacerbate microglial reactivity and amyloid deposition [35]. If asthma chronically activates these pathways—particularly in elderly or severely ill patients—it may not merely coexist with neurological disorders but may lower the threshold for clinical manifestation, accelerate disease progression, or diminish neurological resilience.

Current human evidence already warrants serious consideration of this possibility. Positive epidemiological signals have been reported in a U.S. Medicare cohort [36], a nationwide longitudinal study [37], and a nationwide adult asthma cohort [38]. Biomarker-linked cognitive studies have pointed in the same direction in severe asthma [39]. First, asthma is plausibly associated with increased dementia risk in at least some older populations, as demonstrated by a large U.S. Medicare cohort analysis [36]. Second, the association is not consistent across all cohorts—a Korean nested case-control study found no significant association after matching and adjustment, suggesting that confounding factors or population differences may modulate the signal [40]. Third, the effect size is likely modulated by disease severity, biomarker context, sleep burden, and comorbidity profiles. Specifically, severe asthma amplifies the cognitive effects of established dementia risk factors in CSF-based analyses [39], nocturnal asthma attacks track with altered sleep and inflammatory markers [11], and nighttime respiratory symptoms are further shaped by comorbidity burden [12]. However, not all cohorts have yielded consistent findings [40]. Imaging and biomarker studies provide biological corroboration. Hippocampal spectroscopy demonstrates metabolic abnormalities consistent with neuronal stress [41]. Diffusion MRI and plasma markers indicate neurodegenerative or neuroinflammatory changes in asthma [42]. Resting-state studies have linked asthma with altered cerebral functional connectivity and cognitive decline, thalamic connectivity changes have also been described as part of this broader pattern of network dysfunction [43]. Structural alterations in reward and salience networks add convergent evidence [44]. Resting-state network dynamics have been linked to depressive symptoms and airway inflammation [45]. White matter degeneration and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker findings further connect asthma with AD-type biology [46]. In animal models also offer significant mechanistic insight. Allergic airway inflammation can induce memory deficits and dendritic remodeling [47]. Exercise-responsive hippocampal inflammation has been demonstrated in sensitized juvenile rats [48]. Anxiety-like behavior and neurometabolic disruption have been shown in allergic asthma models [49]. Early postnatal allergic airway inflammation can produce dystrophic microglia [50]. Maternal allergic asthma can lead to persistent neuroimmune abnormalities in offspring [51].

Nevertheless, given the ongoing heterogeneity in this field and the risk of overinterpretation, a cautious appraisal is warranted. Asthma is not a homogeneous condition. Nocturnal worsening varies with circadian timing [10]. Night attacks affect sleep and inflammatory markers [11]. Nighttime respiratory symptoms are further modified by comorbidities [12]. Hospital delirium may also occur in admitted patients with asthma, and its presence varies with age, asthma control status, and comorbid dementia—illustrating how patient-specific factors (rather than asthma alone) determine neurological risk. This variability contributes to the overall heterogeneity of asthma-related neurological outcomes [52]. Treatment exposure matters because oral corticosteroid burden differs substantially between biologic-initiated and non-initiated severe asthma populations [53]. Corticosteroid exposure also carries cognitive and affective correlates [17]. Neurological outcomes are equally diverse, encompassing mood disorders, fatigue, cognitive decline, white matter damage, perioperative delirium, and disease-specific neurodegeneration. Some epidemiological findings remain discordant. For example, a Korean nested case-control study found no increased risk of neurodegenerative dementia after matching and adjustment [40], whereas a U.S. Medicare cohort reported higher claims-based AD incidence and prevalence among patients with asthma after multivariable adjustment and matching [36]. Systemic immune status may further modify the signal because peripheral immunity predicts incident dementia [32], broader brain disorders [33], and AD progression [34]. Therefore, a comprehensive review must distinguish established clinical associations from mechanistic plausibility and direct causal evidence.

Asthma merits attention in neurology because it is a common, chronic, and modifiable condition. Unlike fixed genetic risk factors associated with many neurodegenerative diseases, asthma severity can often be reduced through therapeutic interventions. Real-world mepolizumab studies demonstrate oral corticosteroid sparing [54]. International severe asthma registry data show that biologic initiation reduces rescue steroid exposure and severe outcomes [55]. Dupilumab has been shown to reduce exacerbations even when biomarker shifts are limited [56]. Tezepelumab has demonstrated favorable real-world effectiveness in retrospective multicenter cohorts [57], and short-term multicenter studies further support its efficacy in severe asthma [58]. Small-airway outcomes also improve in real-world tezepelumab studies [59]. Similar benefits are seen in other real-life severe asthma cohorts [60]. Consequently, even if the impact of asthma on neurological outcomes is moderate rather than dominant, its clinical significance remains substantial. A highly prevalent chronic exposure amenable to therapeutic intervention could exert a significant public health impact if it subtly accelerates cognitive decline or reduces resilience in older adults. This is particularly important as asthma increasingly co-occurs with vascular dysfunction [1], peripheral immune risk for dementia [32], peripheral immune risk for brain disorders more broadly [33], and immune changes associated with AD progression [34].

Current reviews often focus on respiratory neuroimmunology, the microbiome, or the neurological outcomes of specific diseases. However, few articles integrate these areas into a unified framework, using asthma as the primary exposure and tracing its impact on sensory immune homeostasis [18], brainstem autonomic control of airway hyperreactivity [22], microbiome-dependent brain autoimmunity [25], salience network changes during airway inflammation [61], TH17-linked stress circuitry in the lung–brain axis [62], and causal relationships between brain network connectivity and respiratory disorders [63]. The present review aims to integrate and analyze these factors from a neuroinflammatory perspective, assisting readers in discerning which findings related to neuroinflammation are sufficiently robust for risk assessment, which neuroinflammatory mechanisms are plausible but supported by indirect evidence, and which therapeutic interventions—particularly those targeting neuroinflammation—are most likely to modify the course of brain injury. In this sense, the lung–brain axis, through its mediation of neuroinflammatory crosstalk, serves not only as a mechanistic concept but also as an organizing principle for translational medicine aimed at mitigating neuroinflammation-driven neurological damage.

Neurological manifestations of asthma

Clinical evidence

Clinical evidence for neurological involvement in asthma encompasses anxiety, depressive symptoms, sleep disturbance, fatigue, and cognitive complaints. More recent work indicates that these manifestations are accompanied by objective changes in brain chemistry, structure, and connectivity. Resting-state studies have linked asthma with altered connectivity and cognitive decline [43]. Thalamic connectivity changes provide additional support [64]. Structural alterations in reward and salience networks have also been described [44]. Network dynamics vary with depressive symptoms and airway inflammation [45]. Salience network connectivity during airway inflammation has been studied directly [61]. Allergen-provoked TH17 responses have likewise been linked to salience network signaling under psychological stress [62]. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) demonstrates hippocampal metabolic stress [41]. Multimodal studies further link asthma severity to white matter abnormalities and circulating glial injury markers [42]. White matter changes also relate to CSF biomarkers and cognitive decline [46]. Animal models recapitulate memory impairment [47], hippocampal inflammation [48], anxiety-like behavior [49], dystrophic microglial changes [50], and offspring neuroimmune priming after maternal allergic asthma [51]. Collectively, these findings support the view of asthma as a systemic disease with measurable neurological effects.

Direct human evidence derives from biomarker, imaging, and neuropsychological studies. Kroll et al. demonstrated that adults with asthma exhibit lower hippocampal N-acetylaspartate and glutamate-related metabolite levels, with poorer asthma control associated with worse cognitive performance and less favorable metabolic profiles [41]. Rosenkranz et al. reported that asthma, particularly severe asthma, is associated with diffusion MRI patterns suggestive of neuroinflammation or neurodegeneration, along with elevated plasma GFAP and NfL [42]. Nair et al. extended these findings to CSF, demonstrating elevated neurogranin levels in severe asthma [39]. Related work further showed that asthma can amplify adverse white matter effects in the setting of abnormal amyloid and tau biomarkers [46]. These observations position asthma within the biomarker landscape of preclinical brain injury rather than framing it solely as a psychosomatic burden.

The available evidence supports a stratified interpretation. First, asthma is associated with neurobehavioral symptoms, including poor sleep, emotional distress, and cognitive complaints. Second, a subset of patients exhibits objective CNS abnormalities, including altered hippocampal chemistry, white matter injury, CSF signatures of synaptic stress, and AD-linked white matter vulnerability [46]. Third, these abnormalities appear more pronounced in severe or poorly controlled disease, suggesting a dose–response relationship rather than a nonspecific association. This gradient is supported by severe asthma biomarker data [42], severe asthma CSF findings [46], salience network changes during airway inflammation [61], and stress-linked salience network responses in asthma [62]. Thus, asthma-related neurological manifestations span emotional, cognitive, imaging, and biomarker domains, and severe or persistent asthma is more likely than mild disease to influence neurodegenerative vulnerability.

Monitoring may ultimately need to extend beyond symptom questionnaires. Structural imaging abnormalities can be captured with diffusion MRI [42]. Hippocampal metabolic stress can be assessed with MRS [41]. CSF biomarkers reveal synaptic stress in severe asthma [46]. White matter changes can also be interpreted alongside AD-related CSF biomarkers [46]. BBB dysfunction provides another measurable layer [30], and regional BBB permeability offers further granularity [29]. Although currently research-oriented, these tools help explain why some patients exhibit cognitive vulnerability despite mild respiratory symptoms. These manifestations are also likely dynamic. Circadian timing contributes to nocturnal worsening [10]. Nocturnal attacks track with altered sleep markers [11]. Acute sleep loss can impair biomarker clearance [65]. Sleep deprivation can alter microarchitecture and accelerate amyloid accumulation in experimental systems [66]. REM sleep features also correlate with multimodal AD biomarkers [67]. Intermittent hypoxia can open the BBB [13]. Obstructive sleep apnea-like hypoxia can drive hippocampal neuroinflammation and cognitive deficits [16]. High-dose steroid exposure may add neuropsychiatric stress during these vulnerable periods [17].

Epidemiological evidence

Multiple population-based studies have linked asthma to an increased risk of dementia, although the literature remains heterogeneous. Positive associations have been reported in a recent U.S. Medicare analysis [36], an earlier nationwide longitudinal study [37], and a nationwide adult asthma cohort [38]. However, a Korean nested case-control study did not confirm the association [40]. Ajay et al. further demonstrated that individuals with asthma exhibit greater age-related white matter degeneration [46]. In the same clinical space, severe asthma has been linked to worse cognition in the context of adverse CSF biomarker profiles [46].

Nevertheless, this association is not uniform across all cohorts. The Korean national sample cohort found no significant association between prior asthma and neurodegenerative dementia after matching and adjustment [40]. By contrast, the Medicare data revealed important differences in race, Medicaid eligibility, and neighborhood disadvantage between asthmatic and non-asthmatic older adults [36]. Biological plausibility is strengthened by the observation that peripheral immunity predicts incident dementia [32]. A related prospective cohort showed that peripheral immunity is also associated with incident brain disorders more broadly [33].

Biomarker-oriented studies strengthen the epidemiological signal by suggesting that asthma may act less as an isolated trigger than as a chronic sensitizer. Severe asthma amplifies the cognitive effects of established dementia risk factors in CSF-based analyses [46]. Peripheral immune status is associated with incident dementia [32] and with broader incident brain disorders [33]. Adaptive immune changes likewise associate with the clinical progression of AD [34].

In summary, the epidemiological literature supports three cautious conclusions. First, asthma is plausibly associated with increased dementia risk in at least some older populations [36]. Second, the association is not consistent across all cohorts [40]. Third, the effect size is likely modulated by biomarker context and disease severity [46], by sleep burden [11], and by comorbidity burden reflected in nighttime symptom profiles [12].

Neuroimaging evidence

Neuroimaging provides a critical bridge between respiratory symptoms and brain pathology. Impaired interhemispheric synchrony has been reported in bronchial asthma [68]. Network-level attentional abnormalities have also been described [69]. Resting-state studies link asthma to altered connectivity and cognitive decline [43]. Thalamic functional connectivity is likewise abnormal in bronchial asthma [64]. Structural MRI demonstrates alterations in reward and salience networks [44]. Resting-state network dynamics are influenced by depressive symptoms and airway inflammation [45]. Salience network functional connectivity during airway inflammation has been studied directly [61]. Allergen-provoked TH17 responses can also be traced into salience network signaling under psychological stress [62]. These abnormalities are consistent with the effects of dyspnea, airway hyperreactivity, inflammatory burden, and intermittent hypoxemia on central neural networks.

Rosenkranz et al. expanded this picture by demonstrating that asthma severity correlates with diffusion MRI abnormalities suggestive of neuroinflammation or neurodegeneration [42]. Related work further showed that these white matter abnormalities track with CSF amyloid and tau profiles as well as cognitive decline [46]. The limited correlation with p-tau181 in the initial severe asthma study suggests that asthma may initially affect brain health through inflammatory, glial, and white matter mechanisms rather than through classic amyloid–tau pathways alone.

MRS provides complementary metabolic evidence. Reduced hippocampal N-acetylaspartate and altered glutamate-related metabolites in asthma support the presence of neuronal stress in a brain region central to learning and memory [41]. Experimental studies further corroborate the biological plausibility of these findings. Allergic airway inflammation can induce memory impairment and neuronal remodeling [47]. Exercise-responsive models show linked hippocampal inflammation [48]. Allergic asthma can also induce anxiety-like behavior through neuroinflammatory and neurometabolic changes [49]. Early postnatal allergic airway inflammation can produce dystrophic microglia and synaptic surplus [50]. Circuit-level effects of allergen exposure extend to the prefrontal–hippocampal axis [24]. Maternal allergic asthma can also shape offspring neuroimmune environments [51].

In summary, neuroimaging and biomarker evidence support a model in which asthma alters brain function and structure across multiple levels. Network connectivity is abnormal in asthma [43]. White matter injury has been demonstrated in severe disease [42]. Hippocampal metabolism is altered on MRS [41]. White matter vulnerability is amplified in the setting of adverse AD biomarkers [46]. BBB dysfunction offers another intermediate phenotype [30], and regional BBB permeability provides further biological resolution [29]. These measurable phenotypes provide an important link between chronic pulmonary disease and late-life neurodegenerative vulnerability (Fig. 1).

Mechanisms of the lung–brain axis in asthma-related neurodegenerative diseases

Disruption of blood–brain barrier integrity

Damage to the BBB represents a critical pathological link in the bidirectional interaction of the lung–brain axis, involving neuroimmune interactions, inflammatory signaling, and coordinated regulation of specific central sensory regions. Existing evidence indicates that hypoxia and oxidative stress act as independent mechanisms underlying BBB injury. The decoupling of endothelial cell proliferation from barrier integrity maintenance under hypoxic conditions increases susceptibility to BBB damage with advancing age, thereby establishing a foundation for neurological complications [70]. Systemic inflammation and immune cell migration are key drivers of structural BBB damage. Systemic inflammation and immune cell migration are key drivers of structural BBB damage. In experimental models, asthma-related airway inflammation promotes peripheral cytokine responses, brain cytokine altera [25, 71]tions, astrocyte and microglial activation, and reduced BBB integrity. Severe allergic airway inflammation can further engage IL-17 A-sensitive microglia in BBB-devoid interoceptive CNS regions, while lung immune niches and microbiota regulate CNS immune reactivity and immune-cell recruitment [25, 71]. Additionally, allergen-induced type 2 immune responses in asthma elevate circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6 [72, 73]. These cytokines act on the CNS via the blood–CSF barrier, triggering neuroinflammation and resulting in functional hyperactivation of brain regions such as the thalamus and insula, along with dysregulation of the prefrontal cortex [74, 75]. These changes underlie neural circuit dysfunction and may accelerate asthma progression [42, 76]. In summary, asthma-induced BBB damage encompasses rapid neural signaling, delayed immune cell infiltration, and molecular-level degradation of barrier proteins. This understanding suggests that in asthmatic patients with comorbid neuropsychiatric conditions such as anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment, controlling airway inflammation alone may be insufficient to fully interrupt lung–brain crosstalk, highlighting the clinical importance of therapeutic strategies targeting BBB protection and systemic inflammatory pathways.

Hypoxia, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction

Research on the lung–brain axis has elucidated key mechanisms underlying asthma-related neurological disorders, in which hypoxic stress, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction serve as initiating factors. In asthma progression, pulmonary hypoxia and oxidative stress induce mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by reduced ATP synthesis, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, calcium homeostasis imbalance, and apoptosis, which in turn mediate airway remodeling and inflammation, thereby promoting disease progression [77]. Exposure to environmental toxicants such as plastics can induce asthma-like pathological changes and, through lung–brain axis interactions, interfere with energy metabolism enzyme activity in brain tissue, induce mitochondrial oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, alter the expression of apoptosis-related factors, and ultimately lead to neurodegeneration [78]. Hypoxia upregulates hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), promotes p53 ubiquitination via murine double minute 2 (MDM2), enhances airway smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration, inhibits apoptosis, and exacerbates airway inflammation and remodeling, thus contributing to asthma progression [79]. Similarly, intermittent hypoxia activates the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3)/IL-1β signaling pathway through HIF-1α upregulation, inducing neutrophil pyroptosis and inflammatory responses that worsen airway inflammation in asthma [80]. In childhood asthma, the hypoxia-related gene aldo-keto reductase family 1 member C3 (AKR1C3) is closely associated with immune cell infiltration, mediating the role of hypoxia in asthma pathogenesis [81]. Oxidative stress constitutes a central component of lung–brain axis injury. IL-25 promotes ROS production by activating mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I and II/III in airway epithelial cells, upregulates AMPK signaling, and induces mitophagy, thereby driving monocyte polarization toward M2 macrophages and participating in the regulation of airway inflammation and remodeling in asthma [82]. Sirtuin 5 (SIRT5) alleviates airway inflammation in eosinophilic asthma by inhibiting ROS production and activating the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway, influencing severe asthma progression through modulation of oxidative stress [83]. Toluene diisocyanate (TDI)-induced asthma activates the ROS/AMPK/autophagy pathway via high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), upregulates the NLRP3 inflammasome, and induces airway epithelial inflammation and remodeling; inhibition of HMGB1 blocks this signaling axis and reduces ROS generation and autophagic responses [84]. Mitochondrial dysfunction represents a critical execution step in this pathological network. In obese asthma, a high-fat diet combined with allergen exposure induces mixed-type inflammation and oxidative stress, activating the ROS-ASK1-MAPK pathway. The natural compound apigenin interferes with this pathway, inhibits ASK1 phosphorylation, and alleviates airway epithelial apoptosis, inflammatory infiltration, and mitochondrial dysfunction, exerting immunomodulatory effects [85]. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) exacerbates asthma-related airway damage by inducing oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury, and apoptotic pathways. Treatment with salidroside activates the SIRT1/PGC-1α/Nrf2 signaling axis, reducing ROS accumulation, restoring mitochondrial membrane potential, and inhibiting apoptosis, thereby conferring bronchial epithelial protection [86]. Collectively, hypoxia, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction associated with asthma form a self-amplifying remote injury mechanism via the lung–brain axis, providing a critical molecular and cellular basis for the development of asthma-related comorbidities such as cognitive impairment, anxiety, and depression. Mitochondria-targeted antioxidant strategies and other therapeutic approaches aimed at this axis may offer new directions for improving neurological outcomes in asthma patients.

From peripheral inflammation to central immunity

As a chronic inflammatory airway disease, the systemic inflammatory response associated with asthma serves as a core mechanism linking airway pathology with CNS disorders. The systemic dissemination of immune cells and inflammatory factors constitutes a key link in lung–brain crosstalk. Sensory neurons regulate humoral immune responses induced by bacterial infection and allergens through the release of vasoactive intestinal peptide and substance P, thereby influencing B cell recruitment, antibody production, and airway inflammation in asthma [19]. Asthma is characterized by type 2 inflammation mediated by Th2 cells and type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), as well as non-type 2 inflammatory pathways involving Th1/Th17 cells, driving eosinophilic and neutrophilic infiltration, IgE production, and airway remodeling. Precision therapies targeting T cell subsets and their cytokines represent key strategies in asthma management [87]. In type 2 immunity in asthma, IL-9 and IL-21 play non-redundant roles: IL-9 primarily regulates ILC2-mediated innate immunity, while IL-21 drives Th2 cell activation, IgE production, and formation of tertiary lymphoid structures [88]. In the context of asthma, epithelial cells activate NFκB signaling and the unfolded protein response through pattern recognition receptors, driving epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity, which results in barrier disruption, basement membrane thickening, and airway remodeling [89]. Memory ILC2s (ml-ILC2s) localize to the lamina propria of the small intestine during remission under the control of CCR9/CCL25 and S1P signaling. Upon antigen re-exposure, they migrate to the airways and mediate rapid proliferation and IL-13 production via Tox and Tcf-7, driving asthma relapse [90]. In children with asthma, immune dysregulation is characterized by increased ILC2 frequency, insufficient IFN-γ production upon viral and bacterial stimulation, elevated IL-5 levels induced by house dust mite (HDM), reduced IL-1β/IL-6 responses to lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and decreased lung function [91]. Children exposed to PM2.5 exhibit enhanced inflammatory responses to allergens or bacterial stimulation due to histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) modifications in circulating monocytes, with upregulation of TNF, IL-6, and IL-8, indicative of trained immunity [92]. Natural killer T (NKT) cells and chimeric antigen receptor-NKT (CAR-NKT) cell therapies play immunomodulatory roles in allergic asthma by regulating cytokines such as IFN-γ, targeting IgE receptors, and modulating immune checkpoints including CTLA-4 and PDL-2 [93]. In patients with non-type 2 asthma, IL-33 drives neutrophilic and mixed granulocytic inflammation by activating TNF, NFκB, and other signaling pathways and promoting immune cell differentiation; its co-receptor, IL1RAP, is highly expressed in this asthma subtype [94]. Furthermore, bidirectional regulation of neuroimmune interaction pathways constitutes a rapid response mechanism of the lung–brain axis: the vagal TRPA1-pulmonary neuroendocrine cell (PNEC) axis drives airway inflammation in asthma by promoting neuroendocrine cell migration, aggregation, and neuropeptide secretion; bronchial thermoplasty interferes with the NRG1-ERBB signaling pathway, blocking this neuroimmune interaction and alleviating inflammation [95]. In summary, systemic inflammation in asthma acts on the CNS through multiple mechanisms, including immune cell homing, inflammatory factor dissemination, neural pathway activation, and BBB disruption, forming the core molecular and cellular basis for lung–brain axis-mediated neurological disorders. Immunomodulatory strategies may provide new therapeutic directions for improving neuropsychiatric comorbidities in asthma patients.

Microbial mechanisms

Research on the lung–gut–brain axis has revealed that the bidirectional regulatory network of microorganisms and their metabolites represents a key mechanism linking asthma and neurological disorders. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota influences immune homeostasis and chronic inflammation through metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and gut–lung axis immune communication, forming a common biological basis for asthma and its comorbidities (e.g., obesity and gastroesophageal reflux) [96]. The respiratory microbiome in asthma patients is predominantly composed of Proteobacteria, Streptococcus, and specific viruses, which influence immune-inflammatory responses through the gut–lung axis and thereby mediate disease progression [97]. Microbial ecological imbalance serves as an initiating factor in this axis. Dysbiosis of the gut and respiratory microbiota in early life, characterized by reduced Firmicutes abundance and colonization by Moraxella, affects immune development via the gut–lung axis and increases asthma risk; elevated bacterial burden and dominance of Proteobacteria are associated with asthma phenotype and severity [98]. Cluster analyses based on sputum transcriptomics, proteomics, and microbiomics have identified that neutrophilic subtypes of severe asthma are associated with dysbiosis of Moraxella/Haemophilus or activation of the IL-22 pathway, providing a basis for precise subtyping and targeted therapy [99]. Nanoplastics induce gut microbiota dysbiosis (e.g., increased abundance of Pseudomonas) and metabolic disorders, activating a phospholipase A2 (PLA2)-TRPV1 neuroimmune positive feedback loop in the gut–lung axis, thereby enhancing Th2-type inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness, exacerbating asthma progression, and exerting respiratory toxicity [100]. The positive feedback amplification of the microbiota–metabolite–immune network further reveals the pathological closed-loop mechanism of the lung–gut–brain axis. Asthma-related chronic lung inflammation induces gut microbiota dysbiosis via the gut–brain axis, with alterations in the abundance of Clostridia and other taxa correlating with depressive-like behavior, highlighting a key mechanism linking the lung–gut–brain axis to neuropsychiatric comorbidities in asthma [101]. Gut microbiome homeostasis in early life is modulated by factors such as mode of delivery and antibiotic use; its disruption participates in immune-inflammatory responses via the gut–brain axis, contributing to the development of allergic asthma and neurodevelopmental disorders (e.g., autism), representing an important mechanism of lung–gut–brain axis interaction [102]. In a murine model of chronic asthma-related airway inflammation, male mice exhibit hippocampal microglial activation, gut microbiota dysbiosis including alterations in Lachnospiraceae and depressive-like behavior, whereas female mice primarily show astrocyte activation, indicating sex-dependent differences in the mechanisms underlying asthma-associated neuropsychiatric comorbidities [103]. In summary, asthma-related microbial mechanisms constitute a multilevel remote regulatory network via the lung–gut–brain axis, encompassing microbial ecological imbalance, metabolic signaling disruption, neuroimmune interactions, and positive feedback amplification, providing a critical microbiological basis for asthma-related cognitive impairment, anxiety, and depression. Impact of asthma on neurological diseases

Cognitive impairment-related disorders

Recent research on the lung–brain axis has revealed a close association between asthma and neurocognitive disorders, involving systemic inflammation, neuroimmune interactions, and BBB disruption. Bidirectional crosstalk exists between asthma and the brain: systemic inflammation mediates activation of emotion-related brain regions and white matter changes via the lung–brain axis, increasing the risk of dementia and AD [104]. Airborne pollutants can traverse the BBB via the nasal route and accelerate AD pathology by inducing oxidative stress and neuroinflammation [105]. House dust mite-induced asthma promotes peripheral inflammation, which in turn drives hippocampal Aβ deposition, astrocyte and microglial activation, neuroinflammation, and BBB compromise, thereby accelerating AD pathology [27]. Asthma exacerbates hippocampal Aβ deposition, neuroinflammation, and synaptic damage in APP/PS1 mice through upregulation of the NFκB pathway and LTD4/CysLT1R signaling, accelerating AD progression [28]. Mendelian randomization studies indicate that genetic susceptibility to moderate-to-severe asthma increases AD risk (OR = 1.01), an association partially mediated by eosinophils, with genes such as IL7R involved in comorbidity mechanisms through the immune cell interaction pathway MIF-CD74-CXCR4 [106]. In dementia-related cognitive disorders, asthma is positively associated with dementia risk, with risk increasing linearly with the number of comorbidities; the highest increase in all-cause dementia risk is 1.54-fold, suggesting that allergy-related systemic inflammation contributes to neurocognitive dysfunction via the lung–brain axis [107]. Levels of the synaptic degeneration markers neurogranin and α-synuclein are elevated in the CSF of patients with severe asthma and synergize with cardiovascular risk and genetic susceptibility to accelerate cognitive decline, indicating that asthma increases dementia risk through neuroinflammatory and synaptic injury pathways [46]. Leukotriene receptor antagonists (LTRAs) significantly reduce dementia risk in asthma patients (HR = 0.42) by blocking asthma-related neuroinflammatory pathways, suggesting that targeting leukotriene signaling may provide an effective strategy for preventing asthma-related neurocognitive dysfunction [108]. The risk of delirium during hospitalization is significantly elevated in elderly asthma patients (OR = 2.91), associated with poor asthma control, advanced age, and comorbid dementia [52]. In other cognitive function-related disorders, asthma-related cognitive impairment is driven by recurrent hypoxia, systemic inflammation, and comorbidities such as obesity and depression, leading to cognitive decline through hippocampal neurotoxic mechanisms [109]. Resting-state fMRI in asthma patients reveals reduced voxel-mirrored homotopic connectivity in brain regions involved in emotion regulation and cognitive processing (e.g., anterior cingulate cortex and supplementary motor area) and enhanced connectivity in visual-related regions (lingual gyrus), suggesting that lung–brain axis-mediated neural functional reorganization contributes to asthma-related emotional and cognitive disturbances [43]. Sleep disorders exacerbate asthma progression by aggravating immune dysfunction, neuroendocrine imbalance, and cognitive impairment; interventions such as optimizing sleep hygiene and cognitive-behavioral therapy may alleviate asthma-related neurocognitive dysfunction and improve overall health [110]. Elderly asthma patients are at significantly increased risk of cognitive impairment, with the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) playing a mediating role, indicating that systemic inflammation contributes to neurocognitive injury via the lung–brain axis [111]. Asthma diagnosed in childhood is associated with higher cognitive function scores in adulthood, and asthma treatment may mediate this association, suggesting that early-life inflammation control may exert protective effects on neurocognitive development [112]. Poor school ventilation can exacerbate indoor air pollutant exposure, increasing asthma prevalence and cognitive impairment; improved ventilation strategies can reduce respiratory symptoms and enhance academic performance, highlighting the value of environmental interventions in asthma-related neurocognitive protection [113]. In summary, asthma significantly increases the risk of AD, dementia, and other cognitive disorders through multiple mechanisms, including systemic inflammation, neuroimmune interactions, BBB disruption, and hypoxic stress, acting via the lung–brain axis. This understanding provides a theoretical basis for early risk identification and the development of intervention strategies targeting the lung–brain axis, such as anti-inflammatory therapies, immunomodulation, and BBB protection.

Motor dysfunction-related disorders

Asthma is potentially associated with motor dysfunction disorders such as PD and HD through the lung–brain axis, with mechanisms involving systemic inflammation, neuroimmune interactions, and aberrant activation of specific neural pathways. Systemic inflammation and neuroimmune activation are key links in asthma-related motor dysfunction. Pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) released from the asthmatic airway enter the circulation, induce systemic inflammation, and cross the compromised BBB to access the CNS, activating microglia and triggering neuroinflammation. This neuroinflammatory environment may selectively damage dopaminergic neurons in the basal ganglia, thereby contributing to PD pathology [114, 115]. Analysis of data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service–Health Screening Cohort (2002–2019) showed a modestly increased risk of PD in asthma patients (OR = 1.11), an association independent of demographic and lifestyle factors, suggesting that systemic inflammation and lung–brain axis mechanisms may be involved in neurodegenerative processes [116, 117]. Notably, treatment of asthma with LTRAs did not significantly alter PD risk but was associated with increased risks of depression and sleep disorders, indicating neuropsychiatric effects mediated by leukotriene pathway modulation in the lung–brain axis [118]. Drug-targeted Mendelian randomization studies have revealed that genetic mimicry of IL-6 receptor inhibition significantly reduces PD risk (OR = 0.354) but increases asthma risk (OR = 1.327) [119]. Patients with HD have an elevated risk of death following COVID-19 infection (OR = 2.35), and comorbid respiratory diseases such as asthma may exacerbate neurodegeneration through lung–brain axis mechanisms [120]. Comparative etiological studies of ALS and asthma suggest that both diseases involve neurogenic inflammatory mechanisms; neuropeptides such as substance P released from sensory nerve endings may play a common mediating role in airway inflammation and motor neuron pathology [121]. In summary, asthma contributes to the pathological basis of motor dysfunction through multiple mechanisms, including systemic inflammation, basal ganglia dysfunction, hypoxic stress, and neuroimmune interactions, acting via the lung–brain axis. This understanding offers new research directions for early risk identification and lung–brain axis-targeted interventions in motor disorders such as PD.

Other neurological disorders

As a common chronic inflammatory airway disease, the pathological effects of asthma are not limited to the respiratory system. Accumulating evidence indicates that asthma significantly increases the risk of stroke, MS, depression, and epilepsy through the bidirectional lung–brain axis communication network [122]. In the context of stroke, the bidirectional interaction of the lung–brain axis is particularly prominent, with pulmonary complications such as pneumonia significantly worsening neurological outcomes in stroke patients [123]. Prospective cohort studies have shown that stroke patients with asthma exhibit significantly reduced lung function parameters (FEV1, FVC, maximal inspiratory pressure [MIP]) and a higher stroke recurrence rate compared with non-asthmatic patients (32.5% vs. 12.5%), suggesting that asthma-related systemic inflammation and lung–brain axis interactions exacerbate post-stroke neurological impairment and recurrence risk [124]. In MS, T cells can transiently reside and mature in local lymphoid structures of the lung, subsequently migrating to the CNS, where they interact with antigen-presenting cells and become reactivated, ultimately triggering autoimmune neuroinflammation—a mechanism involved in MS pathology [125]. In depression, observational studies and Mendelian randomization analyses consistently demonstrate a significant causal association between asthma and depression (OR = 1.14), with asthma patients showing elevated depression risk [126]. Regression analyses and Mendelian randomization studies indicate a significant genetic correlation (rg = 0.352) and causal association (OR = 1.291) between depression and asthma, with depression increasing asthma risk [127, 128]. Accumulation of Th2/Th17 cytokine-expressing cells in asthma may selectively impair fear extinction behavior through modulation of amygdala neuronal activation [129]. Regarding epilepsy, the comorbidity between asthma and epilepsy has been supported by clinical evidence, with genetic susceptibility to asthma showing a modest association with epilepsy risk [130]. In summary, asthma contributes to the pathogenesis of these neurological disorders through multiple mechanisms, including systemic inflammatory dissemination, immune cell homing, neural pathway activation, and BBB disruption, acting via the lung–brain axis. This understanding provides a critical theoretical basis for early risk identification and integrated intervention strategies targeting the lung–brain axis in neurological comorbidities (Fig. 3).

Proposed pathways of the lung–brain axis mediating neurological effects in asthma. This schematic depicts how asthma-driven lung inflammation may translate into CNS abnormalities through the lung–brain axis. Pulmonary inflammation (mediated, for example, by IL-1β, IL-6, IL-13, TNF-α, and Th2/ILC2 responses) leads to hypoxia, oxidative stress (ROS), and microbial dysbiosis of the lung microbiota. These pathological changes activate vagus nerve pathways and promote systemic neuroinflammation. Peripheral inflammation, together with hypoxia-induced ROS, disrupts the BBB, thereby facilitating the infiltration of immune cells, including Th17 cells, into the brain parenchyma. Within the CNS, microglial activation and subsequent release of inflammatory mediators contribute to neuronal and synaptic damage, as evidenced by elevated levels of synaptic nuclear proteins (e.g., alpha-synuclein) and impaired synaptic integrity. Additionally, the lung–gut–brain axis is implicated, wherein alterations in SCFAs and lung microbiota dysbiosis may indirectly modulate CNS functions. TRPA1-PNECs are highlighted as potential sensors linking airway irritation to vagal signaling. Collectively, these interconnected mechanisms drive neuroinflammation, neuronal injury, and behavioral or cognitive deficits in asthma-associated neurological disorders

Discussion

Asthma should not be viewed as an isolated pulmonary condition but rather as a modulator of chronic neurological vulnerability. Although it is currently unlikely to serve as a sole or sufficient cause of neurodegenerative disease, severe, persistent, or exacerbation-prone asthma may lower the threshold for the clinical manifestation of underlying neurodegenerative processes. This interpretation aligns with the hierarchical structure of current evidence. The most consistent signals from human studies pertain to dementia risk in older adults [36], heterogeneity across cohorts [40], diffusion MRI and plasma biomarker changes [42], CSF-linked cognitive vulnerability and AD-type white matter interactions [46]. In contrast, evidence regarding parkinsonian endpoints remains more preliminary, with a Korean nested case-control signal [116], a nationally representative retrospective cohort signal [117], pharmacoepidemiologic work on beta2-agonists and montelukast [131], and target-trial emulation data in older asthma patients [118]. Together, these findings suggest that asthma is better viewed as a disease amplifier or sensitizing factor than as a definitive pathogenic exposure.

A primary reason for the heterogeneity of evidence in this field is the heterogeneity of asthma itself. Nocturnal biology matters [10], sleep and inflammatory disturbance during attacks matters [11], comorbidity-modified nighttime symptoms matter [12]. Treatment burden matters because oral corticosteroid exposure differs across severe asthma care trajectories [53]. Corticosteroid-associated mood and executive effects matter as well [17]. Cohort design also matters, as shown by the discordance between the Korean nested case-control study [40] and the Medicare analysis [36]. Therapeutic selection can further reshape exposure history, as illustrated by real-world mepolizumab oral corticosteroid sparing and the international severe asthma registry data on biologic initiation [55]. This complexity makes it difficult to disentangle the biological effects of asthma from the effects of its treatment modalities.

This therapeutic dimension is of particular importance for clinical interpretation. Inhaled corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of anti-inflammatory asthma control. Experimental studies have shown that effective airway anti-inflammatory therapy may exert beneficial downstream effects on the brain, and inhaled budesonide attenuated microglial activation and neuronal loss in a murine chronic asthma model [132]. Longitudinal insurance-based data also reported that corticosteroid use was associated with a lower future risk of dementia [133]. However, population-based data indicate that systemic and local corticosteroid exposure is associated with poorer executive function and more mood and anxiety disorders [17]. Real-world severe asthma comparisons further show that oral corticosteroid exposure itself is a major treatment-severity marker [53]. Therefore, while corticosteroids may reduce neurological risk through inflammation suppression, they may simultaneously induce adverse effects, particularly with repeated or long-term systemic exposure.

This same logic extends to contemporary biologic therapies. Real-world mepolizumab studies demonstrate oral corticosteroid sparing [54]. International severe asthma registry data show reductions in exacerbations, emergency care, hospitalization, and steroid burden after biologic initiation [55]. Dupilumab reduces severe exacerbations across patients with differing prior exacerbation histories and type 2 biomarker subgroups even when biomarker shifts are limited [56, 134]. Retrospective multicenter tezepelumab cohorts add further real-world effectiveness evidence [57]. Short-term multicenter tezepelumab studies report similar benefit [58]. Small-airway dysfunction can improve under tezepelumab as well [59]. Other real-life cohorts confirm overall effectiveness in severe asthma [60]. Multicenter real-world follow-up continues to support benefit [135]. Although no definitive clinical trials have demonstrated that biologics can slow cognitive decline or alter neurodegenerative biomarkers, they are biologically relevant to the lung–brain axis because they reduce recurrent systemic inflammation, exacerbation-related sleep disruption, and cumulative corticosteroid toxicity.

Among asthma medications, montelukast warrants separate discussion, as it is one of the few drugs explicitly investigated in the neurodegeneration literature. Preclinical studies have shown protection of nigral dopaminergic neurons in a parkinsonian model [136]. Experimental dementia with Lewy bodies work has also shown reduction of alpha-synuclein load and memory rescue [137]. However, a large pharmacoepidemiologic study found no clear overall inverse association with PD risk [131]. A later target-trial emulation analysis in older asthma patients likewise did not support a clinically meaningful protective effect and raised concern about adverse neuropsychiatric outcomes [118]. Therefore, montelukast should currently be regarded as a mechanistically interesting but clinically unvalidated neuroprotective candidate.

In summary, the most useful clinical insight from a translational perspective is not that asthma therapies can already be considered treatments for neurodegenerative diseases, but that optimal asthma control may hold neurological significance even though direct intervention trials with brain endpoints are still lacking. This hypothesis is biologically plausible because biologic initiation reduces steroid burden and severe outcomes in registry data [55], tezepelumab performs well in retrospective multicenter practice [57], short-term multicenter studies show similar effectiveness [58], small-airway dysfunction can improve in real-world use [59], and other real-life cohorts confirm benefit [60]. Conversely, repeated reliance on corticosteroids may worsen mood and executive function [17], may accumulate within high-burden severe asthma trajectories [53], and may only partly offset risk even when mepolizumab reduces oral steroid use [54]. Thus, recurrent exacerbations, persistent systemic inflammation, chronic sleep disruption, and high steroid burden in older adults or severe asthma should be recognized as markers of possible brain vulnerability rather than purely pulmonary problems (Fig. 4).

Conclusion

In conclusion, asthma should no longer be regarded as a disorder confined to the airways. It can influence the brain through interconnected inflammatory, vascular, metabolic, neural, and microbial pathways operating within the lung–brain axis. The most consistent evidence presently relates to dementia risk in older adults [36], objective neuroimaging and blood biomarker changes [42], CSF-linked cognitive vulnerability and AD-type white matter interactions [46], brainstem control of airway hyperreactivity [22], and microbiome-dependent brain autoimmunity [25]. Although current data do not support a simple one-to-one causal model, they do support the view that asthma may function as a clinically relevant modifier of neurological vulnerability, particularly in patients with severe, persistent, or poorly controlled disease.

From a clinical perspective, recurrent exacerbations, chronic sleep disruption, intermittent hypoxemia, sustained systemic inflammation, and cumulative corticosteroid exposure should not be viewed solely as markers of respiratory disease burden, but also as potential indicators of reduced brain resilience. Circadian worsening of asthma highlights the relevance of biological timing [10]; nocturnal attacks affect both sleep parameters and inflammatory markers [11]; nighttime respiratory symptoms are shaped by comorbidity load [12]; intermittent hypoxia can increase BBB permeability [13]. Chronic intermittent hypoxia also remodels brainstem transcriptomic programs [14], intermittent hypoxia can drive neuroinflammation through mitophagy-linked pathways [15] and obstructive sleep apnea-like hypoxia can produce hippocampal neuroinflammation and cognitive deficits [16]. Conversely, better asthma control may still be neurologically meaningful because biologic initiation reduces severe outcomes and steroid burden [55], tezepelumab is effective in retrospective real-world practice [57], multicenter studies support short-term benefit [58], real-life cohorts confirm effectiveness [60], and excess steroid burden itself remains a plausible source of neurological toxicity [53].

The next stage of research should therefore move beyond broad disease associations and focus on identifying which asthma phenotypes most strongly drive neuroinflammatory processes in the brain, which lung–brain neuroinflammatory pathways dominate in different patient subgroups, and whether these neuroinflammatory effects are reversible. Longitudinal studies should integrate sensory-immune airway biology, brainstem autonomic circuitry, microbiome-linked brain autoimmunity, salience network responses during airway inflammation, stress-linked salience network signaling, and brain–respiratory network mapping at the systems level—all within the context of neuroinflammation. Overall, the lung–brain axis provides a useful translational framework for understanding asthma not only as a pulmonary disease, but also as a potentially modifiable contributor to long-term neurological risk via sustained neuroinflammatory mechanisms.
