The adaptive learning taxonomy for responsible large language model integration in higher education
Authors: Simon Baradziej, Adrianna Kochanska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Large Language Models (LLMs) are being rapidly deployed in higher education, yet institutions lack integrated, theoretically grounded frameworks to guide responsible adoption. While meta-analytic evidence reports large positive effects on academic performance (g = 0.867; Wang and Fan 2025), emerging research reveals a “cognitive paradox”: performance gains may coincide with diminished metacognitive accuracy and self-regulation—a gap no existing framework comprehensively addresses across adaptation design, data governance, pedagogical targeting, and learner agency simultaneously. Methodology. Through a targeted narrative synthesis of 35 empirical studies published between January 2024 and February 2025, supplemented by selected post-window studies incorporated during peer review, and grounded in four learning theories—Connectivism, Distributed Cognition, Cognitive Load Theory, and Self-Regulated Learning—this paper develops the Adaptive Learning Taxonomy for Educational Decision-Making (ALT-ED). The framework structures institutional decision-making across four operational dimensions: Adaptation Trigger, Data Granularity, Pedagogical Locus, and Agency. Key findings. Three central findings emerge: (1) the cognitive paradox requires shifting the pedagogical locus from cognitive scaffolding to metacognitive development; (2) high-resolution data collection amplifies algorithmic bias, supporting a default of progressive data minimisation; and (3) the policy-practice chasm demands transparent, auditable design frameworks rather than prohibition-based approaches. Recommendations. Institutions should prioritise a metacognitive pedagogical locus to foster “learning to learn” skills, employ progressive data granularity to balance personalisation with student privacy, and default to learner-negotiated agency as a structural safeguard against algorithmic determinism. Limitations. The framework has not yet been empirically validated through prospective institutional implementation. Additional limitations include reliance on predominantly short-term quasi-experimental evidence, the absence of standardised metrics for real-time metacognitive efficiency, and a literature base that is overwhelmingly English-language and Western-centric. Future research should focus on cross-institutional pilot validations, longitudinal studies of cognitive entrainment, and the development of standardised instruments for measuring metacognitive efficiency in AI-augmented environments.

Introduction

The rapid integration of large language models such as GPT-4, Claude, and LLaMA has fundamentally transformed the landscape of educational technology. Unlike earlier generations of Computer-Based Instruction or Intelligent Tutoring Systems, which required extensive domain-specific programming and rule-hardcoding, contemporary LLMs arrive pre-trained on vast repositories of human knowledge and demonstrate remarkable versatility across diverse educational contexts. These models offer unprecedented affordances: generating contextually appropriate conceptual explanations, delivering immediate and personalized feedback, simulating one-on-one tutoring at scale, Bloom [7] dynamically adapting content to individual learner characteristics, and creating novel learning materials in response to student-generated inquiries.

Recent meta-analytic evidence substantiates the enthusiasm surrounding these capabilities. Wang and Fan [39] examined 51 peer-reviewed studies investigating ChatGPT interventions in higher education, finding large positive effects on academic performance (g = 0.867), moderate positive effects on learning perception (g = 0.456), and significant improvements in higher-order thinking skills (g = 0.457) including critical thinking and complex problem-solving. This evidence base appears to support claims that LLMs represent transformative educational technology capable of enhancing student outcomes across disciplines. However, this technological capability has significantly outpaced institutional readiness and pedagogical clarity regarding responsible deployment.

Institutions worldwide are rapidly implementing LLM-based tutoring systems, automated content generators, and adaptive learning platforms frequently without clear frameworks for evaluating which adaptation strategies appropriately serve their pedagogical missions, learner populations, and institutional values. Huma et al. [18] highlight in their comparative study of institutional readiness that while technical infrastructure for AI often exists, the necessary ethical frameworks, faculty training, and policy guidelines are frequently absent or underdeveloped Kasneci et al. [19]. This represents a critical implementation gap. Institutional decision-makers confront pressing questions for which existing frameworks provide insufficient guidance:RQ1: What configurations of LLM-based adaptive strategies are pedagogically defensible across different institutional contexts and learner populations?RQ2: How should data governance structures be designed to balance the diagnostic utility of learner data with privacy protection in LLM-enhanced systems?RQ3: What mechanisms enable institutions to detect and mitigate algorithmic bias in LLM-driven personalisation, and why do existing detection approaches prove insufficient?RQ4: How can meaningful learner autonomy be preserved while leveraging the computational optimisation affordances of LLM-based adaptive systems?

These questions transcend technical considerations; they are fundamentally about educational values, student agency, institutional accountability, and social equity.

Popović Šević et al. [30] emphasize that while faculty acknowledge the efficiency benefits of AI, deep-seated ambivalence regarding impacts on academic integrity persists alongside pervasive lack of institutional support for responsible integration. The "policy-practice gap," as identified by Todres and Sun [37], results in a chaotic environment where academics and students engage in "sensemaking" to create informal norms that may conflict with broader institutional goals. As Lim et al. [24] note, the field confronts a profound paradox. LLMs offer genuine potential for democratizing access to high-quality, personalized education, yet the identical mechanisms enabling personalization—continuous data collection, algorithmic optimization, real-time behavioral adaptation, create formidable governance and ethical challenges.

A critical obstacle to responsible integration is the absence of comprehensive, theoretically grounded frameworks specifically designed to guide institutional decision-making about LLM-enhanced adaptive learning. The Adaptive Learning Taxonomy for Educational Decision-Making (ALT-ED) addresses this critical gap by providing a structured framework specifically designed for responsible LLM integration. ALT-ED structures decision-making around four core dimensions: Adaptation Trigger, Data Granularity, Pedagogical Locus, and Agency, that determine the operational and ethical profile of any LLM-enhanced adaptive system. However, the framework's efficacy depends entirely upon the theoretical validity of its dimensions. This paper provides the expanded theoretical grounding necessary to answer fundamental questions: Why does Agency matter? How does Data Granularity affect metacognition? What is the mechanism through which specific adaptation triggers produce learning gains or superficial performance?

Methodological approach

This paper employs a targeted narrative synthesis, a methodology suited to integrating diverse empirical findings under a coherent theoretical structure [28], rather than a systematic review with meta-analytic synthesis. The rationale for this approach is that ALT-ED is a theoretically derived framework requiring illustrative empirical grounding, not an exhaustive cataloguing of all available evidence. Search strategy. We searched Web of Science, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore (January 2024–February 2025) using the query: (‘large language model’ OR ‘ChatGPT’ OR ‘generative AI’) AND (‘higher education’ OR ‘university’) AND (‘learning’ OR ‘pedagog*’ OR ‘metacognition’). Inclusion criteria: (1) empirical studies reporting quantitative or qualitative data, (2) higher education context; (3) focus on learning processes or outcomes related to at least one ALT-ED dimension; (4) published in English in peer-reviewed outlets. Exclusion criteria: (1) non-empirical commentaries, editorials, or opinion pieces; (2) studies focused exclusively on K-12 education; (3) studies addressing AI in education without relevance to LLM-based adaptation, data governance, pedagogical targeting, or learner agency. Selection process. Database searches yielded 127 records. After removing duplicates (n = 14), we screened titles and abstracts against inclusion/exclusion criteria, excluding 64 records. Full-text screening of the remaining 49 studies led to the exclusion of 14 that did not directly inform ALT-ED dimensions, yielding 35 studies retained for synthesis. Each retained study was mapped to one or more ALT-ED dimensions based on its primary empirical contribution (see Fig. 1). Selection was performed independently by both authors, with disagreements resolved through discussion. Post-window supplementation. During the peer-review revision process (March–October 2025), five additional studies published after the original search window were incorporated to address reviewer requests and strengthen specific arguments. These post-window additions are: [11, 13, 16, 23, and Banihashem and Farrokhnia [15]. These studies were not identified through the original systematic search but were incorporated during revision to address specific theoretical and empirical gaps raised by reviewers. They are explicitly excluded from the PRISMA Page et al. [29] flow counts and Fig. 1, which reports only the original search pipeline. Synthesis approach. Retained studies were analysed thematically, with findings organised according to the four ALT-ED dimensions. Theoretical foundations: learning in the age of artificial agents

To ground the ALT-ED framework, we must move beyond traditional constructivist views and embrace theories that account for the networked, distributed, and hybrid nature of cognition in contemporary educational contexts. Four specific theoretical frameworks provide the essential analytical foundation for understanding how LLMs interact with human learning processes.

Broader synthetic work by Farrokhnia et al.’s [15] SWOT analysis of ChatGPT in education comprehensively maps the technology’s strengths (e.g. scalability, personalisation), weaknesses (e.g. hallucination, bias), opportunities (e.g. hybrid learning models), and threats (e.g. academic integrity erosion, deskilling), concluding that impact depends on thoughtful pedagogical integration rather than technology‑first adoption. This SWOT lens validates ALT-ED’s emphasis on Pedagogical Locus (to harness strengths and mitigate weaknesses), Data Granularity (to address bias threats), and Agency (to preserve human oversight and prevent deskilling). Meanwhile, Banihashem et al.’s [6] systematic mapping review at the intersection of artificial intelligence and self-regulated learning synthesises 84 studies, revealing that while AI supports SRL phases like goal‑setting and monitoring, effects are highly variable and context‑dependent, with significant gaps in long‑term transfer and metacognitive accuracy. This evidence base directly underpins ALT-ED’s Pedagogical Locus and Agency dimensions, which prioritise metacognitive targeting and learner negotiation to foster genuine SRL rather than superficial performance gains.

Connectivism and the epistemology of the networked learner

Connectivism, often described as a learning theory for the digital age, posits that learning is fundamentally a process of connecting specialized nodes or information sources. Originating with Siemens and Downes, this theory challenges the notion that knowledge resides solely within individuals, arguing instead that knowledge rests in networks of humans and non-human resources Lave & Wenger [20]. In the context of contemporary educational technology, the Large Language Model represents a fundamental evolution of the conceptual "node." Unlike static webpages or textbooks, an LLM is a dynamic, interactive "super-node" that can synthesize, reformat, and generate information on demand.

Liang and Bai [22] argue that Generative AI transforms the topology of learning networks by enabling new forms of knowledge connectivity. They identify that GenAI facilitates the integration of theory and practice by acting as a bridge between abstract concepts and simulated application, effectively tightening feedback loops within the connectivist network. This theoretical perspective directly informs the Data Granularity dimension of ALT-ED. Connectivism suggests that the "bandwidth" of connection is as important as the content within it. If the connection between the learner and the LLM node is restricted by low-bandwidth data, the capacity for the network to adapt and for the learner to traverse complex information landscapes is diminished. Conversely, a high-bandwidth connection allows for a richer flow of context, enabling the LLM to function as a more effective node in the learner's personal learning environment.

However, Connectivism also provides critical warnings regarding diversity, one of its four core principles. A healthy connectivist network relies upon diverse viewpoints to facilitate critical thinking and innovation. LLMs, trained on averaged datasets, often function as "consensus engines" that provide the statistically mean answer, potentially smoothing over the jagged edges of intellectual debate. If an institution relies too heavily on a single LLM as the primary pedagogical node, it risks "network collapse," where the learner's diverse network of sources is replaced by a single, homogenized interface. From a connectivist standpoint, responsible LLM integration must ensure that the AI acts as a gateway to further connections (citing sources, recommending diverse readings) rather than a terminus that creates epistemic isolation.

Distributed cognition and human-AI teaming

While Connectivism explains the structure of the network, Distributed Cognition explains the mechanism of interaction within that network. Developed by Hutchins and extended by Hollan et al. [17], Distributed Cognition views cognition as a process distributed across members of a social group, cognitive artifacts, and the environment. Traditional educational technologies were viewed as tools,passive artifacts used to offload specific tasks. Generative AI, however, exhibits agency-like behaviors: it reasons, suggests, corrects, and sometimes hallucinates. This shift has led researchers in 2025 to reframe the unit of analysis from "Human–Computer Interaction" to Human-AI Teaming (HAIT).

Sidra and Mason [34] argue that effective interaction with GenAI requires Metacognitive Efficiency—the ability to monitor and regulate the partnership itself. In a distributed cognitive system, intelligence does not lie in the student or the AI alone, but in the emergent interaction between them. The implications for the Agency dimension of ALT-ED are profound: if the system is designed such that the AI makes all executive decisions (System-Directed Agency), the human component of the team is relegated to a passive role, breaking the distributed cognitive loop and potentially leading to de-skilling.

Recent HAIT research by Riedl et al. [33] contributes the concept of the AI as a "social forcefield." The study found that exposure to AI-generated language and logic shapes not only how users speak but how they think and what they attend to, a process known as entrainment. The AI acts as an implicit social actor that reorganizes the distributed cognitive architecture of the team. This suggests that Pedagogical Locus is not merely about what students learn, but how students learn to think with the AI. If the AI consistently provides answers without explanation, students entrain to shallow cognitive processing. If the AI acts as a Socratic tutor, students entrain to critical, interrogative thinking styles. Thus, the design of the AI's interaction mode is a fundamental pedagogical decision that shapes student cognitive habits.

Cognitive load theory and the paradox of ease

The central tension identified in the ALT-ED framework, the "Cognitive Paradox", is best understood through Cognitive Load Theory (CLT). Established by Sweller Sweller, Ayres & Kalyuga [36], CLT distinguishes between three types of cognitive load: Intrinsic Load (the inherent difficulty of the material essential for learning), Extraneous Load (the effort required to process poorly designed instruction), and Germane Load (the effort dedicated to processing, schema construction, and automation). LLMs are powerful engines for reducing Extraneous Load. They can summarize complex text, debug code instantly, and translate jargon. However, empirical work from 2025 indicates that this reduction often comes at a cost.

Yao and Fan [41] developed the Cognitive Load Scale for AI-assisted L2 Writing, identifying four distinct factors of cognitive load in AI tasks: Prompt Management, Critical Evaluation, Integrative Synthesis, and Authorial Core Processing. Their findings suggest a "Load Displacement" effect: while LLMs reduce the load of generating content, they significantly increase the load required for Critical Evaluation and Prompt Management. If students are not skilled in these areas, or if the system essentially completes the work without requiring evaluation, the total cognitive load may drop below the threshold required for Germane processing. This results in the Cognitive Paradox: the task becomes too easy, and schema construction stalls.

A 2025 study in outcome-based design education by Dong et al. implemented a Dual-Path teaching model that integrated structured interactions with the DeepSeek large language model to examine how generative AI affects cognitive load and design performance [13]. In this quasi-experimental design with undergraduate design students, an AI-assisted path was compared to a traditional instructional path. Quantitative results showed that the AI-assisted path significantly improved design quality (mean design score 72.43 vs. 65.60 in the traditional path) while simultaneously reshaping the cognitive load profile of the task. Structural equation modelling indicated that generative AI substantially reduced the mediating role of Extraneous Cognitive Load (from 0.907 to 0.017) and, at the same time, increased investment in Germane Cognitive Load, supporting deeper and more innovative design thinking. The authors interpret this as evidence for a “logical chain externalization → load optimization” mechanism, where AI-supported critical reconstruction behaviours (such as higher iteration counts and more cross-domain references) enable learners to offload lower-order processing while preserving, and even amplifying, the cognitive resources devoted to schema construction. This directly supports the ALT-ED view that adaptation triggers should be designed as “speed bumps” that channel saved extraneous load into germane processing, rather than as “guard rails” that remove intrinsic difficulty altogether.

Self-regulated learning and the engine of autonomy

Self-Regulated Learning (SRL), grounded in the work of Zimmerman and Winne, describes the cyclical process of Forethought (planning), Performance (monitoring), and Self-Reflection (evaluating). In traditional education, the learner monitors their own internal state. In an AI-integrated environment, the learner must regulate the Human-AI Team. A pivotal 2025 study by Sun et al. titled "How Self-Regulated Learning Is Affected by Feedback Based on Large Language Models" illuminates a critical challenge. Sun et al. [35] conducted a quasi-experimental study (N = 63, experimental group n = 32, control group n = 31) with computer programming students examining how LLM-based feedback affects self-regulated learning. Their results revealed a critical divergence: LLM feedback produced a large, statistically significant improvement in academic performance (average assignment score: Cohen’s d = 1.462, p < 0.001) and significant improvements in motivation (Wilcoxon W = 656.0, p = 0.020, rank-biserial r = 0.323) and SRL strategy use (W = 672.5, p = 0.011, r = 0.356). However, these measures aggregate broad motivational and strategic constructs rather than isolating specific self-regulation sub-processes such as goal-setting, self-monitoring, and metacognitive reflection. The divergence between large performance gains and moderate effects on self-regulatory constructs underscores the cognitive paradox central to ALT-ED: students felt more supported but did not necessarily improve their ability to plan or monitor their work independently (Fig. 2). This finding is crucial for the Pedagogical Locus dimension. It suggests that systems optimized purely for Cognitive Scaffolding may inadvertently atrophy Metacognitive Regulation. To foster SRL, the system must explicitly target regulatory processes. Asking "What is your plan?" or "How do you know this is correct?", rather than merely verifying content correctness. Conversely, the "SRLAgent" study (2025) demonstrated that when LLMs are explicitly designed to scaffold SRL using gamification and specific regulatory prompts, students showed significant improvements in goal-setting and time management. This reinforces that the impact of AI on learner agency is not inherent to the technology but is a function of the Pedagogical Locus chosen by the institution.

Constructivist principles for human-AI collaboration

Pavlović [26], Deci & Ryan [10] proposes a framework for "Human-AI Collaboration" based on constructivist principles, arguing for a shift from "transactional" to "dialogical" AI. Pavlović argues that agency in a constructivist system must be "shared, visible, and negotiable." The learner must perceive the AI not as an oracle delivering "truth," but as a partner in "meaning-making." This aligns with the Connectivist view of the network and the Distributed Cognition view of the team. Pavlović's [26] constructivist principles directly inform the Agency dimension of ALT-ED. The requirement for 'visible and negotiable' agency maps to our Learner-Negotiated configuration, where AI proposals remain transparent and subject to learner acceptance. The 'dialogical AI' concept aligns with our Metacognitive Pedagogical Locus, emphasizing process over product. Furthermore, her shared meaning-making principle requires Sparse Data Granularity to prevent the AI from becoming an omniscient oracle that removes the productive ambiguity necessary for construction. For ALT-ED, this implies that Agency should ideally be Learner-Negotiated, where the AI proposes adaptation strategies (such as, "I see you're stuck, want a hint?") that the learner can accept or reject, rather than System-Directed imposition.

The integration of these theoretical frameworks: Connectivism mapping the network, Distributed Cognition defining the team, Cognitive Load Theory managing the struggle, and Self-Regulated Learning preserving autonomy—provides the analytical foundation governing the ALT-ED taxonomy. Theoretical contributions of ALT-ED

Research gap. Existing frameworks for responsible AI in education address isolated dimensions of the problem—fairness and bias [9], instructional design processes [21], high-level governance principles [31], epistemic agency [8], or feedback design [15]—but none provides an integrated taxonomy that simultaneously links learning theory to concrete institutional configuration choices across adaptation timing, data governance, pedagogical targeting, and learner control. This fragmentation leaves institutions without a unified decision structure for configuring LLM-based systems. ALT-ED addresses this gap by organising four theoretically grounded dimensions into a coherent design space that bridges high-level ethical commitments and process-oriented instructional design frameworks to auditable, operationally actionable system configurations.

The Adaptive Learning Taxonomy for Educational Decision-Making (ALT-ED) advances current work on responsible AI in education by providing a multi-dimensional, pedagogically explicit decision structure for configuring LLM-based systems in higher education. Existing frameworks typically emphasise one slice of the problem; fairness and bias, instructional design processes, high-level governance, epistemic agency, or explainability, without offering an integrated taxonomy that directly links learning theory to concrete institutional configuration choices. ALT-ED responds to this gap by organising four theoretically grounded dimensions; Adaptation Trigger, Data Granularity, Pedagogical Locus, and Agency, into a coherent design space that institutions can use for both prospective planning and retrospective auditing.

First, in relation to fairness- and ethics-oriented work such as the FairAIED framework [9], which systematically catalogues bias types (data, algorithmic, interactional) and mitigation strategies in educational AI, ALT-ED incorporates these concerns into the Data Granularity and Agency dimensions rather than treating them as external audit layers. FairAIED provides a vocabulary and checklist for identifying unfair treatment across the lifecycle of educational AI systems, but it does not specify how adaptation frequency, data resolution, or learner control should be configured in response to those risks. ALT-ED uses Progressive Granularity and learner-negotiated agency as structural levers to implement FairAIED’s principles: high-resolution data and more system‑directed adaptation become exceptional, context‑bound choices rather than defaults.

Second, compared to human‑centred instructional design frameworks like ARCHED [21], which define multi-stage workflows for AI‑assisted design aligned with Bloom’s taxonomy and emphasise transparency and educator agency in content creation, ALT-ED operates at the level of system behaviour during learning. ARCHED answers how instructors and AI should collaborate when generating learning objectives and assessments; ALT-ED answers how the resulting AI‑enhanced systems should behave at run‑time: when to adapt, how much learner data to leverage, which psychological processes to target, and who controls adaptation decisions. The two are thus complementary: ARCHED structures the design workflow, whereas ALT-ED structures the operational profile of the deployed system.

Third, at the governance level, the “pro‑actively responsible AI in education” manifesto [31] articulates high‑level commitments and policy principles for AI in education (such as centring human flourishing, interdisciplinary collaboration, and impact-sensitive design), but it deliberately remains agnostic about concrete system configurations. ALT-ED can be read as an operationalisation of these principles for LLM-enhanced adaptive learning: it translates broad commitments (e.g., preserving human agency, avoiding techno-solutionism, minimising harm) into specific choices along the Adaptation Trigger, Data Granularity, Pedagogical Locus, and Agency axes that can be documented, justified, and audited at course or programme level.

Epistemic- and agency-focused accounts such as the “Generative AI as epistemic infrastructure in education” framework [8] foreground the ways AI reshapes knowledge practices, skilled epistemic actions, and habit formation among teachers and students. That work specifies conditions for maintaining epistemic agency (support for skilled epistemic actions, epistemic sensitivity, and habit‑building) but does not provide a configurable taxonomy of adaptation or data practices. ALT-ED directly embeds these epistemic concerns in the Pedagogical Locus and Agency dimensions: for example, prioritising metacognitive locus and learner‑negotiated agency explicitly supports epistemic sensitivity and long‑term habits of critical engagement, while highlighting the risks of purely performance‑oriented cognitive scaffolding.

Banihashem and Farrokhnia's pedagogical framework for hybrid intelligent feedback (2025) provides crucial insights for operationalizing the Agency dimension of ALT-ED. Their framework proposes three approaches to combining human and AI feedback sources:Human-Led Feedback with GenAI Support, where educators use AI to enhance their feedback without ceding authority;Adaptive Human-GenAI Feedback, which dynamically allocates feedback responsibility based on task complexity and learner needs;GenAI-Led Feedback with Human Enrichment, where AI provides initial scaffolding subject to human oversight.

Their five key principles. complementarity (leveraging unique strengths), iterative refinement (allowing multi-stage feedback cycles), dynamic adaptability (context-responsive allocation), enhanced personalization (combining scale with nuance), and shared agency (preserving human oversight), directly inform ALT-ED's Agency and Pedagogical Locus dimensions. Specifically, their 'shared agency' principle operationalizes our Learner-Negotiated Agency configuration, while their 'dynamic adaptability' provides a practical implementation model for Progressive Granularity: initial AI-led feedback (requiring high-resolution data) transitions to human-enriched feedback (requiring only sparse data) as learner competence increases. Integrating their framework addresses the practical challenge of sequencing feedback sources. For novice learners, GenAI-Led Feedback with Human Enrichment aligns with our recommended System-Directed Agency and Cognitive Scaffolding locus. For advanced learners, Human-Led Feedback with GenAI Support aligns with Learner-Negotiated Agency and Metacognitive locus. This integration strengthens ALT-ED's implementation guidance by providing a tested mechanism for managing the human-AI feedback partnership.

Finally, student‑centric XAI frameworks [2] for adaptive learning systems focus on transparency, interpretability, and trust in AI‑driven recommendations, emphasising how learners should be able to understand why a system adapted content or pacing in a particular way. ALT-ED complements this by specifying what kinds of adaptations are on the table, at what temporal granularity, and under what data conditions. Explainability requirements from XAI work can thus be mapped directly onto ALT-ED configurations (e.g., requiring richer explanations when Dynamic, high‑granularity, system‑directed configurations are used in high‑stakes contexts).

Taken together, these relations position ALT-ED not as a replacement for existing frameworks, but as a missing bridging layer that connects:high‑level ethical, epistemic, and feedback‑design commitments, andprocess‑oriented instructional design frameworks, to concrete, theoretically justified choices about when, why, and how LLM‑based adaptive systems act on learners in higher education.

The adaptive learning taxonomy: operational dimensions

The ALT-ED framework comprises four dimensions that define the operational and ethical profile of any LLM-enhanced adaptive learning system. These dimensions are not independent design choices but are interconnected decisions affecting the system's pedagogical value, equity implications, privacy footprint, and alignment with institutional mission.

Dimension 1: adaptation trigger (static vs. dynamic)

Adaptation Trigger defines the temporal pattern and sensitivity of AI intervention. It dictates when and how frequently the system modifies content or feedback in response to learner inputs. Static Adaptation occurs at discrete intervals, such as generating a personalized curriculum at the start of a semester based on diagnostic assessment or learner profile. The AI functions as a "curriculum architect" at the outset but does not interfere with the real-time flow of learning. This approach permits human review of learning paths before implementation, significantly reducing real-time hallucination risks and aligns with "human-in-the-loop" governance models required in high-stakes environments.

Dynamic Adaptation, conversely, involves continuous, real-time adjustments during the learning activity. An LLM tutor might immediately modify explanation complexity upon detecting learner hesitation, or generate a new practice problem instantly based on a specific error. While dynamic adaptation offers responsive scaffolding aligned with Vygotsky's [38] Zone of Proximal Development, it introduces latency challenges and necessitates robust automated safeguards against unmoderated errors. In high-stakes domains (medical education, aviation training, legal ethics), where factual accuracy is paramount, static or semi-static adaptation using verified retrieval-augmented generation is often pedagogically superior despite being less immediately responsive. Mahran and Simbeck [25] demonstrated that dynamic responses in multilingual contexts often degrade in quality, suggesting that dynamic adaptation carries higher risks of introducing bias or misinformation in non-English settings.

Semi-Dynamic (Periodic) Adaptation represents a middle ground, where adaptation occurs at defined checkpoints (weekly reviews, unit completions, milestone assessments) rather than continuously. This approach allows for data aggregation and human oversight while maintaining some responsiveness. In medical education, while high-stakes assessment requires static pre-verified content, simulation-based training scenarios could employ semi-dynamic adaptation that adjusts difficulty after each simulation session based on aggregated performance data, pending instructor review.

From a Cognitive Load Theory perspective, the Adaptation Trigger is the mechanism for managing the Cognitive Paradox. If the trigger is too sensitive, instantly correcting a student's error or simplifying text the moment they hesitate, it risks eliminating the Intrinsic Load required for learning. The system acts as a "Guard Rail" that prevents the productive struggle necessary for schema construction. This aligns with entrainment risks identified in Human-AI Teaming research, where the user becomes passive. Static triggers, conversely, create "Speed Bumps" that force Critical Evaluation and Integrative Synthesis. By introducing latency, the system preserves the Germane Load associated with reflection.

From a Self-Regulated Learning perspective, dynamic triggers that solve problems increase motivation but may fail to build regulation. Triggers should ideally be tuned to prompt Metacognitive reflection (asking "You seem stuck. What strategy have you tried?") rather than Cognitive rescue (providing "Here is the answer."). For foundational knowledge acquisition, Static Adaptation is often superior to allow for human-in-the-loop verification and to prevent hallucination propagation. For skill practice domains such as coding and language learning, Dynamic Adaptation is valuable if designed to prompt strategy rather than provide solutions.

Dimension 2: data granularity (sparse vs. high-resolution)

Data Granularity refers to the resolution and detail of student data used to drive adaptation. Sparse Granularity relies on low-resolution inputs such as quiz scores, completion rates, or explicit learner choices. This approach embodies Data Minimization, reducing privacy burdens and simplifying compliance with regulations such as GDPR. However, it limits the system's ability to diagnose subtle misconceptions. High-Resolution Granularity leverages the full spectrum of learner interaction: natural language queries, keystroke dynamics, cursor tracking, affective sentiment analysis, and extended conversation histories. High-granularity data enables deeply personalized support, such as an AI tutor detecting frustration through sentiment analysis and adjusting its tone.

This dimension represents the central conflict between Connectivism and Self-Regulated Learning theory on one hand, and privacy and ethical considerations on the other. Self-Regulated Learning theory dictates that effective regulation requires monitoring the process, not just the product. To diagnose why a student failed (poor planning versus lack of knowledge), the system needs high-resolution data. Similarly, Connectivism implies that for the LLM to function effectively as a node in the learner's personal learning environment, it requires rich context. A sparse connection limits the AI's ability to provide context-aware connectivity.

Operationalizing Progressive Granularity requires defining competence thresholds that trigger data reduction. For example: Novice Phase (Weeks 1–4): High-resolution tracking including keystroke patterns, query formulation quality, and error types to diagnose specific misconceptions. Developing Phase (Weeks 5–10): Transition to medium-resolution data focusing on task completion rates, help-seeking frequency, and assessment scores. Proficient Phase (Weeks 11 +): Sparse data collection limited to voluntary self-reports and summative assessments. Institutional triggers could include: (1) three consecutive assignments scoring > 85%, (2) reduced help-seeking frequency (< 3 queries per session), (3) explicit learner request for increased privacy. Students should receive transparent notification: 'Based on your demonstrated competence, we're reducing data tracking. You can revert to fuller support anytime.'

However, as argued in the FairAIED framework by Chinta et al. [9], high-granularity collection creates a "Panopticon" effect that may inhibit Agency and Autonomy. High-resolution monitoring acts as a "bias amplifier": if the training data of the LLM contains bias, applying biased patterns to minute student behaviors perpetuates and magnifies systemic prejudice. Institutions must weigh the pedagogical necessity of high-resolution data for Self-Regulated Learning diagnosis against the ethical risk of surveillance. A theoretically sound compromise is "Progressive Granularity": starting with high support for novices and tapering to sparse data as the learner demonstrates competence, thereby returning privacy and agency to the student.

Dimension 3: pedagogical locus (cognitive, metacognitive, affective)

Pedagogical Locus identifies the specific psychological domain targeted by the AI's adaptation. Cognitive Scaffolding focuses on knowledge acquisition (explanations, facts, debugging). This is the default mode for most commercial LLM applications and has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing Extraneous Load. Metacognitive Development targets "learning to learn" processes: helping learners develop awareness of their own thinking, understand problem-solving strategies, and regulate intellectual processes. Instead of answering a question, the AI asks, "What strategy did you try first?" or "How does this connect to the previous concept?".

Wang and Zhao [40] demonstrated that Metacognitive Prompting can be effectively encoded into LLMs to improve both the model's performance and the quality of its interaction with users, fostering deeper understanding. Affective Regulation targets emotional and motivational dimensions: managing frustration, sustaining engagement, building confidence, and maintaining intrinsic motivation. A critical finding in recent literature is the tension between cognitive scaffolding and deep learning. Deng et al. [12] found that while ChatGPT improves performance, it can reduce the mental effort required for tasks. If an LLM reduces extraneous load but also eliminates germane load, it may produce "superficial learning," where cognitive offloading prevents the productive struggle necessary for long-term retention.

The trap of defaulting to Cognitive Scaffolding is evidenced by research on the Dunning-Kruger effect, also known as "Competence Illusion"—a cognitive bias introduced by Dunning et al. [14]. Most commercial LLM tutors provide answers, reducing Authorial Core Processing load but not necessarily building competence. Liebenow et al. [23] found that LLM-generated feedback did not significantly improve students' self-assessment accuracy on average, and exploratory analyses suggested it may have slightly reduced accuracy among students who were already well-calibrated, while helping those with initially poor self-assessment. The AI acted as a crutch that inflated confidence without building commensurate competence. Delikoura et al. [11] identify "superficial learning" risks, where cognitive offloading prevents the productive struggle necessary for retention.

To foster Collaborative AI Literacy, the system must target the Metacognitive Locus. This involves Prompt Management and Critical Evaluation. The AI should act as a "Socratic Mirror," reflecting the student's reasoning back to them. Affective support requires nuance: while appropriate encouragement sustains motivation, "toxic positivity" that prevents realistic self-assessment masks learning gaps. Distributed Cognition warns that "social forcefields" (AI personality) can manipulate user affect. Institutions must ensure Affective support does not become false reassurance that prevents skill development recognition.

Dimension 4: agency (system-directed vs. learner-negotiated)

Agency refers to the locus of decision-making authority regarding the learning path and content selection. System-Directed Agency means algorithms autonomously optimize the path, automatically skipping content deemed known, and assigning remediation without learner input. This maximizes computational optimization but risks "algorithm aversion" and undermines learner autonomy. Learner-Negotiated Agency means the system functions as a recommender or advisor. It proposes options (asking, "You seem to be struggling with X; would you like to review Y or practice Z?"), but the learner retains authority to accept, reject, or modify the path.

From a Constructivist perspective, Pavlović argues that for human-AI collaboration to be constructivist, agency must be "negotiable." System-Directed agency treats the learner as an object to be optimized, violating the Partnered Meaning Making principle. In a Human-AI Team, the human must retain "Superagency"—the executive control to direct the artifact. If the AI drives, the human becomes a passenger, leading to de-skilling and loss of Metacognitive Efficiency. However, a "Novice Paradox" emerges: novices often lack the competence to negotiate effectively. They may choose "easy" paths that avoid learning. Liebenow et al. [23] found that system-directed feedback creates an illusion of competence, while Sun et al. [35] demonstrated that LLM feedback improved performance but not self-regulation sub-scales such as goal-setting and self-monitoring. Together, these findings suggest a Dynamic Agency model: System-Directed for absolute beginners to prevent cognitive overload, graduating to Learner-Negotiated as Collaborative AI Literacy improves.

To counter the illusion of competence identified by Liebenow et al. [23], learners must be active participants in their learning path. System-directed optimization fosters dependency,learner-negotiated paths foster self-regulation. The AI should allow for choice, for example recommend: "80% of students found reviewing this module helpful. Would you like to do that, or attempt the quiz?" The final decision must remain the student's.

Dimensional interdependencies and configuration constraints

While ALT-ED presents four dimensions as conceptually distinct, their practical implementation reveals significant interdependencies. Agency and Pedagogical Locus are particularly entangled: Learner-Negotiated Agency requires that learners possess sufficient Metacognitive capability to make informed choices, suggesting that Cognitive Scaffolding (Pedagogical Locus) may be prerequisite to meaningful Agency transfer. Similarly, High-Resolution Data Granularity enables fine-grained Dynamic Adaptation, but also increases bias amplification risks, constraining Agency options for vulnerable populations.

These interdependencies suggest that ALT-ED configurations are not freely combinable. Certain combinations are pedagogically coherent (e.g., Dynamic + Metacognitive + Learner-Negotiated + Sparse for advanced learners) while others are contradictory (e.g., System-Directed + Sparse data cannot support meaningful personalization). Research landscape and critical evidence synthesis

The cognitive paradox: performance versus competence

The most significant finding from 2024 to 2025 literature is the divergence between performance (the output produced) and competence (the internal capability developed). Wang and Fan [39] demonstrate robust effect sizes for performance improvement (g = 0.867), suggesting LLMs are powerful performance-support tools. However, Liebenow et al. [23] caution that this support can degrade self-assessment accuracy, creating an "illusion of competence" where students believe they understand material simply because they can produce a correct answer via AI. This aligns with Delikoura et al. [11] identification of "superficial learning" risks, where cognitive offloading prevents the productive struggle necessary for long-term retention.

This represents a critical gap requiring institutional attention. Institutions must shift the Pedagogical Locus from Cognitive Scaffolding (getting the answer) to Metacognitive Development (understanding the process). Implementing strategies like Metacognitive Prompting, as validated by Wang and Zhao [40], compels the model to act as a Socratic interlocutor rather than an oracle, reintroducing the necessary friction for deep learning.

The equity trap and algorithmic bias

The pursuit of hyper-personalization through High-Resolution Data Granularity presents a distinct equity trap. While granular data enables responsive adaptation, Chinta et al. [9] argue in the FairAIED framework that it expands the surface area for algorithmic bias. When systems train on historical educational data, they often encode systemic prejudices Baker & Hawn [3], converting past inequities into future predictions. Furthermore, Mahran and Simbeck [25] expose a "linguistic penalty" in LLMs: students interacting in non-English languages receive objectively inferior scaffolding. They evaluated LLM performance on identical math problems across English, German, and Arabic, finding statistically significant performance degradation in non-English languages, confirming that students from different language backgrounds receive structurally unequal educational support from the same AI tools.

Ethical implementation requires a default to Sparse Granularity unless specific safeguards are in place. Furthermore, Agency must remain Learner-Negotiated. If a system is biased against a specific demographic, a System-Directed approach will forcibly route those students into inferior learning paths without their consent. Preserving learner agency acts as a final safeguard against algorithmic determinism. Baldwin [4] proposed an "audit-style framework" for evaluating bias in LLMs used for assessment, providing specific protocols for "stress-testing" models with counterfactual scenarios to reveal latent biases before they affect student grades.

The policy-practice chasm

There is a widening gulf between institutional policy and classroom reality. Alharbi [1] documents a pervasive "shadow pedagogy" where student adoption rates far outstrip faculty awareness, rendering bans and surveillance ineffective. The reliance on plagiarism detection increasingly appears to be a losing battle. Perkins et al. [27] tested six major GenAI text detectors against 805 samples of machine-generated content subjected to simple adversarial techniques such as paraphrasing and translation (n = 805). Their results showed baseline detector accuracy of only 39.5%, dropping a further 17.4% when bypass techniques were applied, leading them to conclude that these tools "cannot currently be recommended for determining academic integrity violations" due to accuracy limitations and the risk of false accusation. Pratama [32] extended this analysis to scholarly publication contexts, evaluating three widely-used AI detection tools on 72 stratified abstracts and finding that two of three tools performed barely better than random guessing (54.63% and 64.35% accuracy). More critically, even the most accurate tool (GPTZero, 97.22% accuracy) exhibited significant bias against non-native English speakers, flagging 25% of AI-assisted abstracts by non-native authors as entirely AI-generated compared to approximately 11% for native authors (p = 0.036). These findings suggest that the arms race between generation and detection is largely futile for educators, and that detection-focused approaches disproportionately penalise linguistically diverse student populations. Todres and Sun [37] describe faculty engaging in ad-hoc "sensemaking" to fill the void left by lagging institutional guidance.

The ALT-ED framework serves as a mechanism to formalize this sensemaking. By explicitly defining the Adaptation Trigger and Agency settings for specific courses, institutions can move from reactive policing to proactive design. The evidence suggests that "Generative AI Literacy," as proposed by Weng and Fu (2025), is a more viable long-term strategy than prohibition. When AI is integrated transparently into language education, bridging the digital divide, it fosters inclusivity and motivation rather than academic dishonesty.

These three empirical tensions—the cognitive paradox (Sect. 4.1), the equity trap (Sect. 4.2), and the policy-practice chasm (Sect. 4.3)—define the landscape that ALT-ED must navigate. Section 5 translates these evidence-based insights into concrete operational recommendations, specifying how each ALT-ED dimension should be calibrated in response to the challenges documented above.

Operationalizing the framework

The Adaptive Learning Taxonomy for Educational Decision-Making (ALT-ED) translates theoretical foundations into institutional practice through explicit dimensional configurations that can be documented, audited, and justified. Three universal principles, grounded in the pro-actively responsible AI manifesto [31], apply across all implementation contexts. First, institutions must always prioritise transparency by requiring systems to explain adaptation rationales, such as "Content adapted due to detected hesitation patterns in problem-solving." Second, data minimisation serves as the institutional default, with collection of additional data permitted only when supported by specific pedagogical evidence, as high-granularity data amplifies proxy bias according to FairAIED analysis [9]. Third, human superagency must be preserved through configurations that maintain instructor and learner veto power over all automated decisions.

Calibrating the adaptation trigger

Context-conditional recommendations calibrate these principles according to learner expertise, domain risk profile, and empirical evidence synthesised from recent studies. For calibrating the Adaptation Trigger, the timing of interventions must balance responsiveness against the preservation of productive struggle, as demonstrated in Dong et al.'s [13] quasi-experimental study with 52 undergraduate design students. Their Dual-Path model showed that generative AI reduced extraneous cognitive load from a mean of 0.907 to 0.017 (p < 0.001 via structural equation modelling) only when paired with critical reconstruction prompts, thereby preserving germane load essential for schema construction. In foundational knowledge domains serving novice learners, such as introductory biology, static or semi-static adaptation proves optimal, generating pre-verified learning paths that undergo human review to mitigate hallucination risks documented in multilingual contexts [25], statistically significant performance degradation). For skill practice among intermediate learners, such as introductory programming, dynamic adaptation becomes viable when constrained by mandatory metacognitive prompts (e.g., "What strategy did you attempt first?"), consistent with meta-analytic evidence of improvements in higher-order thinking across 51 studies [39]. Pure error correction without reflection risks germane load reduction, as evidenced by the cognitive paradox.

Governing data granularity

Governing Data Granularity requires explicit trade-offs between adaptive precision and ethical risk, where high-resolution tracking expands bias surface area [9]. In high-stakes domains like medical education, medium-resolution data, limited to quiz scores and error typologies, supports bounded diagnostics while adhering to FairAIED audit protocols. Low-stakes exploratory contexts, such as advanced seminars, default to sparse granularity capturing only completion rates, addressing privacy gaps prevalent in AI-self-regulated learning studies across 127 studies [5]. Progressive granularity operationalises data minimisation as pedagogical progression: high-resolution tracking applies during initial novice phases for misconception diagnosis, automatically tapering to sparse collection upon meeting competence thresholds such as repeated high performance on assignments.

Shifting the pedagogical locus

Shifting the Pedagogical Locus demands targeting specific psychological domains calibrated to self-regulated learning evidence, where AI effects prove strongest during performance-phase monitoring but reveal gaps in forethought and reflection across 127 studies [5]. Novice learners benefit from cognitive-dominant scaffolding focused on factual acquisition and debugging, yielding substantial performance gains [35] quasi-experimental study of programming students). Intermediate and advanced learners require a metacognitive locus emphasising process reflection ("Explain your reasoning chain"), which improves understanding [40]. Assessment shifts to Prompt Portfolios, complete interaction histories weighted substantially on process quality in grading rubrics, rendering superficial content generation pedagogically transparent [15]. Affective-only targeting risks false reassurance, exacerbating competence illusion where self-assessment accuracy declined substantially [23]

Negotiating agency

Negotiating Agency along the system-directed to learner-negotiated spectrum directly counters deskilling threats identified in ChatGPT's SWOT analysis [15]. Hybrid feedback modes validate graduated control: human-led with AI support preserves oversight for complex tasks, while AI-led with human enrichment suits novices [15]. High-stakes domains or novice cohorts warrant system-directed agency coupled with instructor override, given novices' limited negotiation competence reflected in weak metacognitive effects [35]. Advanced or low-risk settings default to learner-negotiated configurations offering transparent choices (e.g., "80% of peers benefited from review—accept?"), aligning with constructivist requirements for visible, negotiable agency [26].

Institutional decision-making must systematically weigh five empirically-grounded factors: learner prior expertise (novice contexts favour system-directed control), domain risk profile (high stakes demand static triggers), regulatory constraints (GDPR enforcement prioritises sparse data), available human oversight capacity (limited resources constrain complex configurations), and equity implications for vulnerable populations (mandating negotiated agency as bias safeguard). These evidence-based calibrations bridge policy-practice gaps documented in faculty sensemaking amid institutional ambiguity [30] survey, n = 312), enabling defensible LLM deployments that align technical capability with pedagogical mission.

The recommendations in Sect. 5 should not be interpreted as universal prescriptions. Defaulting to Learner-Negotiated Agency, while theoretically optimal for preserving autonomy, may disadvantage novices who lack metacognitive sophistication to negotiate effectively [23, 35]. Similarly, Sparse Data Granularity, while ethically preferable, may prevent early diagnosis of misconceptions in foundational courses. Institutions must conduct context-specific analysis weighing: (1) learner population characteristics, (2) domain risk profile, (3) institutional regulatory requirements, (4) available human oversight capacity, (5) equity implications for underserved populations.

The vignettes that follow illustrate how these calibrated recommendations translate into concrete instructional configurations, demonstrating ALT-ED’s practical applicability across diverse higher education contexts.

Illustrative design vignettes

To demonstrate ALT-ED's practical applicability, three vignettes map the framework onto concrete instructional scenarios. These are theoretically grounded illustrations, not empirical case studies.

Vignette 1: introductory biology (large enrollment, foundational knowledge)

A first-year biology course (N approximately 300) integrates an LLM tutor for foundational concepts. Configuration: Adaptation Trigger = Static (AI generates personalized study guides at unit start, instructor-reviewed before distribution, preventing hallucinated biological facts from entering foundational schemas); Data Granularity = Sparse (quiz scores and completion rates only, minimizing surveillance on first-year students); Pedagogical Locus = Cognitive with embedded Metacognitive prompts (primary goal is accurate knowledge acquisition, but selected modules include "Before reading the AI's explanation, write what you think the answer is"); Agency = System-Directed transitioning to Shared (initially the system recommends study paths; after midterm, students gain the ability to choose between recommended modules). This configuration corresponds to the "Novice learners in high-stakes, fact-heavy domains" cluster in Table 3.

Vignette 2: advanced software engineering (skill practice, senior undergraduates)

A senior-level course uses an LLM coding assistant for debugging and code review. Configuration: Adaptation Trigger = Dynamic (real-time feedback on code errors is appropriate in verifiable domains); Data Granularity = Progressive (high-resolution tracking of error patterns initially, tapering to sparse after students demonstrate consistent debugging competence, operationalized as three consecutive assignments with fewer than two critical errors); Pedagogical Locus = Metacognitive ("What strategy did you use to isolate this bug?" before providing the fix); Agency = Learner-Negotiated (AI recommends review topics but students choose their path; Prompt Portfolio required for assessment). This corresponds to the "Intermediate learners in mixed-stakes analytical courses" cluster.

Vignette 3: medical ethics seminar (high-stakes, regulated domain)

A graduate-level seminar integrates an LLM for case-based reasoning. Configuration: Adaptation Trigger = Periodic (AI generates discussion prompts and counterarguments at each weekly seminar start, instructor-reviewed; no real-time adaptation during discussion); Data Granularity = Sparse (participation metrics and reflection quality only, given sensitivity of ethical reasoning data); Pedagogical Locus = Affective and Metacognitive ("Which stakeholder's perspective have you not yet considered?"); Agency = Learner-Negotiated (students can request the AI to steelman an opposing argument but retain full authority over ethical conclusions). This corresponds to the "High-stakes regulated domain" cluster in Table 3, with conditional coherence requiring strong governance safeguards.

Research gaps and future directions

Limitations

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, ALT-ED is a theoretically derived framework that has not yet been empirically validated through prospective institutional implementation or controlled comparison with alternative frameworks. The retrospective classification presented in Sect. 5.5 provides preliminary evidence of analytical utility, but design-based research across diverse institutional contexts is needed to establish practical efficacy. Second, the four-dimensional structure, while theoretically motivated, has not been subjected to factor-analytic or expert-consensus validation procedures (e.g., Delphi method) that would strengthen claims of dimensional completeness. Third, the evidence base relies predominantly on short-term or quasi-experimental studies conducted in Western, English-language contexts. The generalisability of findings to non-Western educational systems, non-English-language settings, and long-term implementations remains uncertain. Fourth, the narrative synthesis methodology, while appropriate for theory-building, does not provide the exhaustive coverage or quantitative effect estimation of a full systematic review. Fifth, the post-window studies incorporated during revision were selected based on reviewer recommendations rather than systematic search, introducing potential selection bias. Finally, the framework does not address implementation costs, institutional resource requirements, or the technical infrastructure needed for deployment—factors that substantially influence real-world adoption feasibility.

Despite the theoretical progress, substantial gaps persist at the intersection of these theories and practical implementation. While Yao and Fan [41] and Sidra and Mason [34] have proposed scales for "AI-Assisted Cognitive Load" and "Collaborative AI Literacy," these instruments are new and require broad validation across different disciplines. Standardized metrics to assess "Metacognitive Efficiency" in real-time are absent. How can institutions measure if a student is "thinking with the AI" versus "thinking through the AI"?

Most empirical studies are short-term or quasi-experimental. Longitudinal data on the "entrainment" effect are lacking. Does long-term exposure to System-Directed AI tutors permanently alter a student's problem-solving style? Does "Cognitive Offloading" lead to permanent atrophy of skills, or does it free up capacity for higher-order synthesis as proponents claim? The "use it or lose it" hypothesis in the context of cognitive offloading remains under-tested in the GenAI era.

From a Connectivist perspective, bias in an LLM represents a "poisoned node." While general bias research exists, specific research on how adaptive systems amplify this bias is sparse. If an ALT-ED system uses High Data Granularity to profile a student and then applies a biased LLM to adapt content, does it create a feedback loop that structurally disadvantages certain demographics? Methodologies for "Fairness Audit" specifically designed for adaptive GenAI systems require development.

A final methodological limitation concerns the absence of formal empirical validation of the taxonomy itself. ALT-ED has not been applied prospectively to guide institutional decision-making, nor has it been tested through controlled comparison with alternative frameworks. The retrospective system classification presented in Sect. 5.5 offers preliminary evidence of analytical utility, but prospective validation studies, ideally employing design-based research methodology across diverse institutional contexts, are essential to establish the framework's practical efficacy. We also acknowledge that the four-dimensional structure, while theoretically motivated, has not been subjected to factor-analytic or expert-consensus validation procedures (e.g., Delphi method) that would strengthen claims of dimensional completeness.

Discussion and implications

The synthesis of evidence through the ALT-ED framework reveals three critical tensions that define the current state of LLM integration in higher education. These tensions between performance and competence, personalization and privacy, and policy and practice must be navigated deliberately rather than resolved through technical means.

The ALT-ED framework provides structured responses to each of the four institutional questions posed in Sect. 1. First, regarding which LLM-based adaptive strategies are pedagogically defensible, the Adaptation Trigger and Pedagogical Locus dimensions specify that strategies emphasizing metacognitive prompting and periodic or static adaptation are defensible across most contexts, while purely dynamic, cognitive-only configurations risk superficial learning (Sects. 3.1, 3.3). Second, regarding data governance, the Data Granularity dimension establishes that Progressive Granularity; starting with high-resolution data for novices and tapering to sparse collection as competence increases, balances diagnostic utility against privacy risk (Sect. 3.2). Third, regarding algorithmic bias detection and mitigation, ALT-ED integrates the FairAIED framework's bias typology into the Data Granularity and Agency dimensions, mandating multilingual audits and defaulting to Learner-Negotiated Agency as a structural safeguard (Sect. 4.2). Fourth, regarding the preservation of learner autonomy, the Agency dimension's Learner-Negotiated configuration, supported by the Hybrid Intelligent Feedback framework's shared-agency principle, ensures that computational optimization does not override human executive control (Sects. 3.4, 5.4).

The Cognitive Paradox represents the most significant finding from 2024 to 2025 literature: performance gains (g = 0.867) diverge from competence development. While LLMs improve what students produce, they may erode what students learn. The illusion of competence, documented by Liebenow et al. [23], means that students performing well may simultaneously become less accurate in self-assessment. This paradox can be addressed by explicitly designing systems with Metacognitive Development as the Pedagogical Locus, implementing Socratic rather than declarative interaction modes, and requiring students to engage with "Prompt Portfolios" that demonstrate their reasoning trajectory, not just their final outputs.

The Equity Trap emerges from the tension between personalization and fairness. The very mechanisms enabling responsive adaptation, high-resolution data collection and algorithmic optimization, create opportunities for algorithmic bias to encode and perpetuate systemic educational inequities. The multilingual performance penalties documented by Mahran and Simbeck [25] and the "measurement bias" risks identified by Chinta et al. [9] suggest that institutions should default to Data Minimization and preserve Learner-Negotiated Agency as structural safeguards against algorithmic determinism.

The Policy-Practice Chasm reflects the absence of clear institutional frameworks for AI integration. Students adopt AI tools at rates far exceeding faculty awareness or institutional policy. Rather than attempting futile surveillance and detection, institutions should implement transparent integration grounded in the ALT-ED framework, explicitly defining decision parameters for each course and teaching Generative AI Literacy as an essential contemporary competence.

ALT-ED responses to core AI challenges

ALT-ED provides a structured response to the most frequently discussed challenges of LLMs in education, as identified in recent systematic reviews and empirical studies. Each challenge maps to specific dimensional configurations that can be audited and justified.

Algorithmic bias

High-resolution data granularity amplifies bias by enabling LLMs to apply demographic proxies (e.g. writing style, error patterns) to personalised adaptations, as documented in FairAIED [9] where granular data expanded bias surface area. ALT-ED addresses this structurally by defaulting to Sparse Granularity and requiring Learner-Negotiated Agency, where learners can reject biased recommendations, and sparse data limits profiling. Mahran and Simbeck [25] found significant performance degradation for non-English users (p < 0.01 across Arabic/German vs English math tasks), ALT-ED mandates multilingual bias audits before enabling dynamic adaptation.

Academic integrity

Current detection tools fail against sophisticated prompting (false positive rate up to 25% for non-native English speakers versus ~ 11% for native speakers; [32]), with baseline accuracy as low as 39.5% that drops further under simple adversarial techniques [27]. ALT-ED reframes integrity by shifting Pedagogical Locus to Metacognitive assessment: require Prompt Portfolios (full interaction histories) as submissions. Farrokhnia et al. [15] SWOT analysis identifies integrity erosion as a major threat, solvable via hybrid models. Portfolios allow instructors to grade query sophistication and reflection (e.g. 40% rubric weight on process), making "cheating" pedagogically detectable and irrelevant.

Skill atrophy

Dynamic, system-directed adaptation risks undermining self-assessment accuracy. Liebenow et al. [23] conducted a randomized controlled experiment (N = 459 upper secondary students, EG n = 203, CG n = 256) examining LLM-generated feedback effects on self-assessment accuracy (SAA) during argumentative writing. They found no significant main effect of LLM feedback on SAA overall (beta = 0.07, SE = 0.05, p = ns). However, a significant interaction (beta = 0.24, SE = 0.06, p < 0.001) revealed that students with initially low SAA benefited most from feedback (a compensatory "Robin Hood" effect), while exploratory analyses suggested that students with already-high SAA may have experienced slight accuracy declines after receiving feedback (B = -0.66, SE = 0.29, p = 0.03; though estimated at values outside the observed range). Although this study used upper secondary rather than university students, its findings carry implications for ALT-ED: LLM feedback does not uniformly improve metacognitive calibration and may disrupt accurate self-monitoring in students who already possess it. ALT-ED addresses this through Static/Semi-Dynamic Triggers (latency forces reflection) and metacognitive prompting that targets the self-assessment process explicitly.

Privacy erosion

Continuous tracking creates "panopticon" effects inhibiting agency [9]. ALT-ED operationalises data minimisation via Progressive Granularity: high-resolution for novices (1–4 weeks, misconception diagnosis), tapering to sparse as competence thresholds met (e.g. 3 × assignments > 85%). Banihashem et al. [5] mapping review (84 studies) found privacy as a top SRL-AI gap,ALT-ED makes reduction a pedagogical feature with transparent notifications ("Data tracking reduced due to competence").

Teacher De-skilling

Faculty fear irrelevance amid AI efficiency [30] survey, n = 312. ALT-ED preserves authority by making design choices explicit and negotiable: instructors set dimensional defaults per course (e.g. "Semi-Dynamic, Metacognitive Locus"). Banihashem et al. [6] hybrid feedback framework supports "human-led with AI support" modes, aligning with ALT-ED's Agency spectrum.

Epistemic infrastructure risks

LLMs risk "network collapse" by becoming epistemic terminuses [22]. ALT-ED's Connectivist grounding positions AI as network node (e.g. require source citation, diverse readings), with Sparse Granularity preventing over-reliance. Chen [8] warns of eroded epistemic habits,ALT-ED mandates Learner-Negotiated Agency to preserve "superagency".

Beyond these individual risk responses, ALT-ED addresses a systemic challenge frequently noted in the AI-in-education literature: the tendency for institutions to make implementation decisions based on technical capability rather than pedagogical reasoning [37]. By requiring explicit configuration choices across four theoretically grounded dimensions, ALT-ED transforms implicit assumptions into auditable decisions. For instance, an institution deploying an LLM tutoring system must explicitly declare whether it operates with system-directed or learner-negotiated agency, rather than accepting the vendor's default configuration. This forced articulation serves a dual function: it enables meaningful institutional governance (as called for by [31]) and creates documentation that supports the transparency requirements of student-centric XAI frameworks [2]. The framework thus operates as both a design tool and an accountability mechanism—a combination that is absent from any single existing framework identified in our comparative analysis (Table 2).

Conclusion

This paper introduced the Adaptive Learning Taxonomy for Educational Decision-Making (ALT-ED), a theoretically grounded framework for guiding responsible LLM integration in higher education. Synthesizing four foundational learning theories; Connectivism, Distributed Cognition, Cognitive Load Theory, and Self-Regulated Learning, with a narrative synthesis of 35 empirical studies from 2024 to 2025, the framework structures institutional decision-making across four operational dimensions: Adaptation Trigger, Data Granularity, Pedagogical Locus, and Agency.

Three central findings emerged. First, the Cognitive Paradox, the divergence between AI-enhanced performance (g = 0.867) and the erosion of metacognitive competence, requires institutions to prioritize Metacognitive Development as their Pedagogical Locus rather than defaulting to Cognitive Scaffolding. Second, the Equity Trap demonstrates that high-resolution data collection, while enabling personalization, amplifies algorithmic bias and surveillance risks, supporting a default of Data Minimization operationalized through Progressive Granularity. Third, the Policy-Practice Chasm indicates that prohibition and detection strategies are insufficient, and ALT-ED offers a constructive alternative by making implicit institutional decisions about AI integration explicit, auditable, and theoretically grounded.

The dimensional interdependency analysis (Sect. 3.5) and coherent configuration mapping (Table 3) demonstrated that ALT-ED dimensions are not freely combinable, and that pedagogically defensible configurations depend on learner expertise, domain risk, and institutional context. The integration of the Hybrid Intelligent Feedback framework further validated the dimensional structure by showing how feedback configurations correspond to positions along the Agency continuum. Future work should prioritize empirical validation through cross-institutional pilot implementations, the development of standardized instruments for measuring metacognitive efficiency in AI-augmented environments, and longitudinal studies investigating the long-term effects of dimensional configurations on learner development and potential skill atrophy.
