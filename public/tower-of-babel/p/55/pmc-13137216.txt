Deformability-augmented mesenchymal stem cells post-enucleation for efficient mRNA intervention of pulmonary fibrosis in rodent model
Authors: Hao Sun, Xiaojing Qi, Shihan Chen, Yanshuang Huang, Xixi Zheng, Yaosheng Li, Ni Li, Jing Shi, Donghang Xu, Xiaoxu Hao, Yanli Zhao, Xianzhen Yin, Yue Hu, Yinghua Ying, Qin Chen, Yang Xia, Zhen Gu, Xuejin Li, Jianqing Gao, Tianyuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryStem cell therapy has demonstrated significant therapeutic potential. However, the relatively large cellular diameter increases the likelihood of entrapment within capillaries, impeding the migration of stem cells to disease foci. We, herein, propose an enucleation strategy to enhance cell deformability, significantly improving vascular transit efficiency. Computational modeling, combined with experimental investigations using a microfluidic platform, revealed that increased cellular deformability, rather than a reduction in diameter, plays a greater role in facilitating cell transit, thereby challenging traditional perspectives on the cell mechanics. Observations of pulmonary vasculature using optical tissue-clearing and fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography confirmed the extensive distribution of enucleated cells and their reduced entrapment within fibrotic foci. Consequently, enucleated mesenchymal stem cells could efficiently deliver mRNAs to the fibrotic sites, significantly mitigating pulmonary fibrosis. Our findings provide new insights into the role of enucleation in facilitating cellular transit through constricted vasculature and the underlying mechanobiology of enhanced deformability.Subject areas: mechanobiology, fibrosis, drug delivery system, cell engineering, stem cells research

Introduction

Stem cell therapy is an expanding field in medical research,1 offering significant advantages for treating diseases that are challenging to manage with conventional therapies.2,3,4 Among various stem cell types, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have been widely studied and applied in numerous clinical trials.5,6,7,8,9 MSCs exhibit several favorable properties as therapeutic agents, including low immunogenicity, paracrine function, and immune regulation.10 Recently, the inherent homing capability of MSCs toward inflammation sites, mediated by chemokine or cytokine gradients, has garnered significant attention.11 We previously demonstrated the potential of MSCs for treating various diseases, including pulmonary fibrosis (PF), ischemic stroke, and lung metastases.12,13,14,15,16 Despite these promising attributes, the clinical application of MSCs faces several challenges. A notable challenge is the risk of cell-induced embolization during infusion, which was also reported in a clinical trial,17 as MSCs tend to be trapped in small blood vessels.18,19 A previous study further suggests that this tendency is largely due to the relatively large size of MSCs, which restricts their subsequent targeting efficiency to diseased foci.20 This issue is exacerbated in pathological conditions where blood vessels are further narrowed, impeding the migration of MSCs to disease foci and ultimately diminishing therapeutic efficacy.21,22,23 Therefore, minimizing cell embolism in blood vessels before the cells reach target sites remains a critical challenge for optimizing stem cell therapy.

The nucleus is the stiffest organelle within a cell and constitutes a substantial portion of its volume.24 A previous study has showed that enucleation can reduce cell diameter without significantly affecting physiological processes in the short term.20 Currently, the migratory ability of MSCs during transendothelial penetration and tissue infiltration has been extensively studied. However, the impact of nuclear removal on MSC traversal of narrowed vasculature remains insufficiently explored. Therefore, we focus specifically on this vascular transit process, in which systemically infused MSCs are passively carried by blood flow and must deform within seconds to traverse constricted microvessels. Such investigations are crucial for examining the dynamic of MSCs in circulation, as well as advancing our understanding of the advantages and mechanisms of the nucleus in cell deformability.

PF is a life-threatening interstitial lung disorder for which efficient treatments remain limited.25,26 One crucial reason for current medical treatment failure is the insufficient accumulation of therapeutic agents in fibrotic lungs.11,27 The fibrotic pathology tends to cause vascular remodeling and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, thereby significantly reducing pulmonary microvascular diameter. Such pathological change occurs extensively in various types of pulmonary vasculature, including the lung capillaries, setting a significant barrier for the efficient drug delivery to fibrotic tissues.21 This issue tends to become more severe when large-sized stem cells are used for PF intervention, highlighting the need for developing novel methods to improve cell deformability or enhance transit capacity through constricted blood vessels.

Herein, our study showed that enucleation significantly enhanced the deformability of enucleated MSCs (Enu-MSCs) derived from human umbilical cord, facilitating the transit through constricted blood vessels (Figure 1). Moreover, a microfluidic platform replicating the geometry of the vascular lumen was developed to elucidate the dynamic behavior of Enu-MSCs during traversing vasculature. The observed cell transit dynamics were then analyzed using computational simulations. Additionally, we investigated the biodistribution and entrapment of Enu-MSCs in pulmonary capillaries, using optical tissue clearing and fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST). Finally, we introduced hepatocyte growth factor (HGF)-encoded mRNA, verified as a promising anti-fibrotic gene,28 into Enu-MSCs and assessed the efficacy of Enu-MSCs in a PF model. The results uncovered in this study show the therapeutic potential of Enu-MSCs for overcoming constricted lung vasculature barriers and elucidate their enhanced navigation capabilities. Furthermore, this study provides a novel analysis of enhanced deformability from a cellular mechanobiology perspective.

Results

Enucleation significantly enhances cell deformability for effective passage in confined environments

An enucleation strategy involving cytochalasin B treatment and Ficoll gradient density ultracentrifugation was employed to remove nuclei from human MSCs (Figure 2A).20,29 As shown in Figure S1, MSC nuclei were separated from the cells, and Enu-MSCs were collected from the top layers. Cytochalasin B was used to partially and reversibly disrupt the cellular cytoskeleton,30 providing a chance for nuclear removal via centrifugation. However, the non-optimal concentration of cytochalasin B may be detrimental to cells. Therefore, the impacts on MSCs were firstly studied to ensure sufficient cytoskeletal disruption and minimal adverse effects. Morphological observations revealed a noticeable cell shrinkage post-cytochalasin B treatment, indicative of cytoskeletal disruption. However, MSCs could recover after cytochalasin B removal (Figure S2). Fluorescence imaging by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) confirmed the inhibition of F-actin assembly post-treatment, resulting in partial cytoskeleton disruption (Figure S3). Moreover, fluorescence observation confirmed the successful enucleation of most MSCs (Figure 2B), with flow cytometry revealing an enucleated rate of up to 80% (Figure 2C). MSC diameter was significantly reduced following nuclear removal (Figure S4), a change attributed to alterations in the pre-stressed cellular structures.24 These findings suggested that this enucleation strategy can effectively remove the nucleus.

On the basis of these findings, we hypothesize that the reduction in cell diameter post-enucleation may contribute to the enhanced passage ability of Enu-MSCs. To test this hypothesis, we employed a microfluidic assessment, a newly developed technique that enables direct observation of cell motion dynamics in confined environments.31 A microfluidic chip embedded with micropillar arrays was designed to create uniform microchannels, each with a channel length of 30 μm along the flow direction (detailed methods are provided in the STAR Methods). This design was posited to effectively simulate conditions typical of narrow capillaries or small venules, as substantiated by previous studies.32,33,34,35 The dynamic passage of intact MSCs and Enu-MSCs through the micropillar arrays was closely monitored using microscopy (Videos S1 and S2). Frame images captured from these dynamic observations are presented in Figures 2D and 2E. As expected, intact MSCs, owing to their larger size, were able to traverse wider microchannels with a cross-sectional area of 15 μm × 15 μm (Figure 2D). In contrast, Enu-MSCs, being smaller, could navigated much narrower microchannels, even when the cross-sectional area was reduced to 5 μm × 5 μm (Figure 2E). These experimental findings were further analyzed through numerical simulations using a computational model, which revealed significant cell deformation during the microchannel transit. This deformation process can be delineated into three distinct temporal phases (Figure 2F):

These results underscored the critical role that cell deformation plays in facilitating passage through microchannels. Notably, this raises an important question: is the enhanced passage ability of Enu-MSCs attributable solely to their reduced diameter, or does their improved deformability play a crucial role? To address this, we calculated the passage index by evaluating the ability of both MSCs and Enu-MSCs to traverse microchannels of varying widths. The results demonstrated that Enu-MSCs exhibited consistently superior passage capabilities compared with MSCs across all tested microchannel widths. Notably, MSCs were completely obstructed in a microchannel with a width of 5 μm (Figure 2G). Additionally, Enu-MSCs exhibited markedly shorter transit times through narrow microchannels (5–6 μm), in contrast to the prolonged passage times observed for intact MSCs in wider microchannels (10 μm) (Figure 2H). The mean diameters of intact MSCs and Enu-MSCs were determined to be approximately 17.8 and 10 μm, respectively, by microfluidic studies (Figure S5). Notably, while 17.8 μm-sized MSCs showed limited ability to traverse microchannels with widths of 10 μm (approximately half their diameter), the 10 μm-sized Enu-MSCs demonstrated impressive passage capability, even through microchannels with widths half their diameter. This finding suggested that the enhanced passage ability of Enu-MSCs was not solely due to their reduced diameter but also stemmed significantly from their improved deformability attributable to enucleation.

To further elucidate the contributions of cell diameter and deformation to the passage capabilities of these cellular vehicles, numerical simulations based on dissipative particle dynamics (DPD) were employed to model the behavior of intact MSCs and Enu-MSCs. Details of the construction of the DPD model are provided in the supplemental information. The membrane of the modeled MSCs was set at a diameter of 18.0 μm, and the nucleus was modeled at a diameter of 11.25 μm, consistent with previous measurements (Figure S5). The viscoelastic properties of the MSC membrane were defined by four energy components36: elastic energy (Us), bending energy (Ub), surface area constraint (Uarea), and volume constraint (Uvolume).

These four components determined the mechanical properties of the cell model (Figure 2I). DPD simulations were employed to quantitatively assess the minimum microchannel width that MSCs or Enu-MSCs could traverse under a constant pressure differential. The results presented in Figure 2J indicate that a single MSC with a diameter of 18 μm could successfully pass through a microchannel as narrow as 11 μm, whereas a single Enu-MSC with a diameter of 10 μm could traverse a microchannel as narrow as 5.5 μm. These simulation results are consistent with experimental observations from microfluidic assays. To further elucidate the influence of cell diameter differences, a simulation model of a single Enu-MSC with a diameter of 18 μm (depicted by light blue triangles in Figure 2J) was developed to estimate the minimum microchannel width it could traverse. The simulation results revealed a minimum width of approximately 8.5 μm. These findings indicated that even when the diameter of Enu-MSCs was equal to that of intact MSCs, the former still exhibited a superior passage capability through narrower microchannels. This observation deviates from our initial hypothesis, suggesting that the enhanced passage capability of Enu-MSCs is not solely attributable to their smaller diameter but is primarily influenced by their increased deformability.

Figure 2K depicts the local shear strain distributions of the membranes of MSCs and Enu-MSCs, as well as the nucleus of MSCs, during their passage under the same λ ratio (defined as the ratio of the projected area of a cell, in either suspended state or static state, to the cross-sectional area of the microchannel), which reflects the degree to which the cell is confined. Upon entering the microchannel, intact MSCs exhibited maximum shear strain at the posterior region, whereas Enu-MSC experienced maximum shear strain at the anterior section. These results indicated that MSCs experience higher peak shear strains than Enu-MSCs, independent of diameter modifications. Moreover, the contrasting locations of maximum shear strain can be attributed to the nucleus’s inherent rigidity and limited deformability relative to the cell membrane, potentially increasing the likelihood of embolism within the microchannel. To further quantify differences in cell deformability, the stretch ratio, defined as the ratio of cell elongation length to its diameter, was calculated. The results indicated that intact MSCs exhibited a lower stretch ratio than Enu-MSCs when passing through microchannels with an identical λ of 1.7, further supporting the improved deformability of Enu-MSCs (Figure 2L). In addition, the flow resistance, expressed as the ratio of pressure difference to average cell velocity (∇P/v), was investigated as a functional metric for characterizing the resistance encountered by cells traversing microchannels. Figure 2M presents both experimental and computational assessments of flow resistances for intact MSCs and Enu-MSCs, demonstrating a close alignment between the two and indicating that MSCs encountered significantly greater flow resistance than Enu-MSCs under equivalent λ conditions. To further validate our conclusion regarding the resistance encountered during vascular transit, the microfluidic experiments using MSCs and Enu-MSCs of comparable diameters (∼17 μm) were performed. When passing through identical constricted microchannels (5 μm in width), the two cell types exhibited markedly different behaviors (Figure S6; Videos S3 and S4). MSCs required more than 130 s to traverse the channel and displayed pronounced morphological deformation after passage. In contrast, Enu-MSCs crossed the constriction in less than 2 s, with minimal shape alteration. By employing cells of similar size, this experiment excluded the confounding effect of diameter and provided direct experimental support for numerical simulations, demonstrating that enucleation reduces not only cell size but also the flow resistance experienced during vascular transit. Therefore, the combined datasets in Figures 2K–2M demonstrate that removing the nucleus, with its high Young’s modulus, rendered MSCs more deformable, thereby enhancing the passage of Enu-MSCs through constricted vascular environments.

Enu-MSCs exhibited enhanced migration toward fibrotic lungs

Previous studies have indicated that pulmonary remodeling and other pathological alterations of PF progression tend to induce statistically significant narrowing of blood vessels.21,37,38 The present observations further confirmed this pathological alteration, in which reduced vascular diameter was observed in fibrotic lung sections from human biopsy samples (Figure 3A, indicated by “∗”). The observations were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval number: 2024-1037). This vascular constriction was also observed in the PF murine model, which demonstrated a decreased diameter of blood vessels (Figure 3B, indicated by “∗”). These pathological observations underscored the importance of investigating the potential embolism of cellular vehicles within blood vessels, which could significantly impact delivery efficiency. To access the potential entrapment of MSCs in the pulmonary capillaries, we examined the biodistribution of systemically administered MSCs and Enu-MSCs in murine models. Fluorescence signals analysis showed that both MSCs and Enu-MSCs effectively targeted fibrotic lungs. However, compared with MSCs, Enu-MSCs demonstrated more rapid lung clearance in both healthy and fibrotic lung (Figure S7), suggesting reduced lung entrapment of Enu-MSCs. Furthermore, the local distributions of MSCs and Enu-MSCs within fibrotic foci were compared. Enu-MSCs showed widespread fluorescence signals throughout most fields of fibrotic lung sections, whereas MSCs displayed uneven fluorescent signals (Figures 3C, 3D, and S8). In lung tissue sections, the central regions, which are closer to the trachea and thus more directly exposed to bleomycin (BLM), generally exhibit a more severe degree of fibrosis. Consequently, Enu-MSCs showed a broad distribution in highly fibrotic areas, suggesting an enhanced capacity to traverse confined spaces. Moreover, the biodistribution of MSCs and Enu-MSCs throughout lungs was investigated for a comprehensive overview. The vasculature in fibrotic lungs was visualized through α-smooth muscle actin staining and reconstructed in three-dimensional (3D) images, using optical tissue clearing (Figure 3E). Administered MSCs and Enu-MSCs were visualized by labeling their mitochondria using Cy3-conjugated anti-mitochondria antibody, following a previous report.20 Enu-MSCs exhibited a more extensive distribution than MSCs, which suggested a more efficient migration of Enu-MSCs to fibrotic foci (Figure 3E; Videos S5 and S6). We hypothesized that the improved targeting of Enu-MSCs compared with intact MSCs in fibrotic lungs might be attributed to their enhanced passage ability through constricted blood vessels. To support this hypothesis, we further applied high-precision cross-scale fMOST to visualize the pulmonary vascular structure, as previously reported.39 The observations revealed that most MSCs were trapped in vessels with a diameter of 19.6 μm. Enu-MSCs were trapped only upon encountering much narrower vessels (diameter of 14.3 μm) (Figures 3F and 3G; Videos S7 and S8). These in vivo observations are consistent with findings from microfluidic chip experiments, which demonstrated that Enu-MSCs possess an improved ability to traverse constricted vessels, thereby facilitating the targeting toward fibrotic foci.

Minimal impact of enucleation on primary characteristics of MSCs

Because enucleation significantly improved the traversal ability of MSCs through constricted vasculature, we further investigated the impacts of enucleation on key functions of MSCs. MSCs exhibited high viability following cytochalasin B treatments, with survival rates reaching 97% (Figure 4A). Enu-MSCs maintained relatively high viability (>70%) for up to 72 h post-enucleation (Figure 4B), indicating sustained cell viability over 3 days. Furthermore, the potential impact of enucleation on other major organelles, including mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, and Golgi apparatus, was determined. No detectable adverse effect on these organelles in Enu-MSCs was observed compared with the nucleated parental MSCs (Figure 4C). Additionally, protein production capacity by Enu-MSCs remained unchanged (Figure S9). Similarly, Enu-MSCs retained the ability to produce extracellular vesicles (EVs), exhibiting comparable cup-like structures (Figure 4D), surface zeta potential (Figure 4E), particle size (Figure 4F), and size distribution (Figure S10) to those of intact MSCs.

The expression of homing-related receptors, including very late antigen-4 (VLA-4) and C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4),15,40 is believed to underlie the inherent homing ability of MSCs. Therefore, the expression levels of the two receptors and the chemotaxis of Enu-MSCs were evaluated. Western blot analysis demonstrated that the expression of VLA-4 and CXCR4 remained unaltered following enucleation (Figures 4G–4I). Evaluation of the stromal cell-derived factor 1α (SDF-1α)-induced chemotaxis, using a transwell migration assay, confirmed that the chemotaxis activity was retained in Enu-MSCs. Notably, the number of migrated Enu-MSCs was significantly higher than that of MSCs (Figures 4J and S11), suggesting an improved ability of Enu-MSCs to traverse confined pores. The enhanced migration of Enu-MSCs might be attributed to their decreased cell diameter.20 In summary, we have shown the feasibility of an enucleation treatment for MSCs that is not only efficient but also retain major cellular functions.

Enu-MSCs preserve mRNA loading and translation capability

As a potential cellular vehicle, cargo loading capability is also essential for cellular vehicles. Herein, the mRNA loading potential of Enu-MSCs was initially investigated using a reporter mRNA encoding enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP). Following gene transfection with Lipofectamine 3000, MSCs successfully expressed eGFP and maintained the expression over 48 h (Figure S12). Notably, the enucleation strategy did not affect mRNA expression in MSCs (Figure 5A). Additionally, the smaller size of Enu-MSCs affects the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) quantification. At equivalent total cellular fluorescence expression, this smaller cell volume leads to significantly higher MFI values in Enu-MSCs compared with nucleated MSCs (Figure 5B). Having confirmed that Enu-MSCs retained mRNA loading and expression capabilities, we subsequently loaded them with therapeutic mRNA encoding HGF. HGF is an endogenous cytokine known for its anti-fibrotic properties, primarily through the modulation of TGF-β signaling.28,41,42 HGF expression analysis verified the successful loading of HGF mRNA into Enu-MSCs, demonstrating an approximately 150-fold increase in HGF secretion post-gene transfection, with no significant difference compared with intact MSCs (Figure 5C). These results further indicated that Enu-MSCs retained the ability to express mRNA-encoded protein. Moreover, the determination of secreted protein in the culture medium revealed similar secretion levels between Enu-MSCs and intact MSCs post-mRNA transfection (Figure 5D), suggesting that mRNA transfection did not significantly affect the paracrine function of Enu-MSCs. Proteomic profiling identified a total of 2,717 proteins, of which fewer than 2% exhibited significant changes in expression following enucleation (Figure S13). Importantly, proteins associated with fibrotic pathways showed no detectable alterations, indicating that enucleation preserves the protein expression landscape relevant to the anti-fibrotic function of MSCs (Table S1). To further validate that Enu-MSCs inherited the drug delivery function from parental MSCs, therapeutic potential was compared in vitro (Figure 5E). Both MSCs and Enu-MSCs loaded with HGF mRNA demonstrated the ability to reduce the concentrations of matrix metalloprotein-9 (MMP-9) (Figure 5F) and TGF-β (Figure 5G) produced by BLM-treated TC-1 cells (BLM-TC-1), indicating promising potentials to intervene the fibrotic process. In addition, apoptosis of BLM-TC-1 cells was inhibited (Figure 5H) and proliferation of these cells was restored (Figure 5I) following treatment with MSCs or Enu-MSCs loaded with HGF mRNAs. Collectively, these results suggested that Enu-MSCs are capable of substituting the drug delivery functions of intact MSCs.

Enu-MSCs achieved more efficient mitigation of PF than intact MSCs in murine models

Having demonstrated the feasibility and advantages of using Enu-MSCs for efficient mRNA delivery to the fibrotic foci, we next investigated whether their enhanced passage ability would improve PF intervention. A classical murine model of PF was induced via the intratracheal BLM challenge, as described in previous studies.43,44 HGF-loaded Enu-MSCs (indicated as Enu-MSCs + mRNA) or MSCs (inidcated as MSCs + mRNA) were systemically administered on the day following the BLM challenge. MSCs without additional engineering (indicated as MSC) were also administered as a control (Figure 6A). Survival rate monitoring revealed that stem cell therapy prolonged the survival of PF mice, particularly those treated with HGF-loaded Enu-MSCs (Figure 6B). Micro-CT imaging further validated the therapeutic potential of using Enu-MSC- or MSC-based mRNA treatments, demonstrating that fibrotic progress was significantly mitigated on day 28 post-treatment (Figure 6C). Lung section examinations using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s trichrome staining also confirmed this efficient intervention of PF (Figure 6D). The fibrotic ratio of each lung sample was subsequently calculated based on the sections stained with Masson’s trichrome, demonstrating significant mitigation following mRNA-loaded Enu-MSC treatments. Notably, Enu-MSCs loaded with mRNA achieved the most efficient intervention of fibrotic progress among all groups (Figure 6E). Moreover, the determination of the expression levels of MMP-9 (Figure 6F) and TGF-β (Figure 6G) in lung homogenates further supported the improved PF intervention by using MSCs and Enu-MSCs to deliver HGF-encoded mRNA. Additionally, hydroxyproline concentration, a typical indicator of PF severity, revealed similar results, with Enu-MSCs demonstrating the most effective inhibition of hydroxyproline (Figure 6H). In addition, mRNA-loaded Enu-MSCs even showed the ability to treat established PF to some extent, while MSCs and mRNA-loaded MSCs exhibited comparatively weaker therapeutic effects (Figure S14). Collectively, these therapeutic results demonstrated that Enu-MSCs were more effective than intact MSCs in PF intervention. Given the similar HGF expression capability of Enu-MSCs and MSCs (Figure 5C), these improved therapeutic outcomes are likely attributed to the more efficient mRNA delivery ability of Enu-MSCs in fibrotic lungs. In addition, the potential risks of using Enu-MSCs as mRNA vehicles were examined. Neither abnormal changes in body weight (Figure S15) nor negligible side effects in blood routine tests (Figure S16) and histological examinations of major organs (Figure S17) were observed following the administration of Enu-MSCs, indicating the relative safety of using Enu-MSCs for mRNA delivery.

Discussion

Over the past decade, numerous studies have highlighted the promising potentials of MSCs as cellular vehicles in targeted disease treatment, partly owing to their pronounced tropism toward sites of tissue injury.45,46,47 However, most of these studies have primarily demonstrated the presence of MSCs within diseased tissues without adequately examining the dynamic process by which these relatively large cells navigate blood vessels. It is well established that MSCs can transmigrate through barriers narrower than their size via deformation mechanisms.48 Nevertheless, comprehensive investigations into the deformability of MSCs traversing constricted vascular regions and the influence of shear stress remain limited. Notably, intravenously administered MSCs have been reported to become entrapped within the pulmonary capillaries due to their larger diameter relative to those of the capillaries and insufficient deformability.49,50,51 Therefore, overcoming the barrier posed by pathological vascular constriction remains a major challenge in the effective therapeutic application of stem cells as drug delivery vehicles.

A pioneering study by Wang and colleagues demonstrated that the enucleation strategy of MSCs offers the advantage of reducing cell diameter and maintaining cell activities for a few days. They also showed that this reduction in cell size facilitated the transendothelial migration and interstitial tissue infiltration of Enu-MSCs.20 This finding inspired the development of deformability-augmented MSCs through enucleation, designed to surmount the obstacles posed by narrow vasculature in fibrosis. Based on these observations, the present study further investigated the circulation of Enu-MSCs in blood vessels under physiological blood flow conditions. Our results showed that enucleation also enhanced cell deformability under physiological shear stress, thereby facilitating their passage through narrow vessels. Furthermore, the limited lifespan of Enu-MSCs potentially reduces the long-term risks associated with their application. Thus far, direct in situ observation of how these Enu-MSCs traverse blood vessels has remained challenging. To address this obstacle, we developed a microfluidic device designed to replicate the confined spaces of pulmonary capillaries, enabling direct observation of MSC dynamic behavior within constrained channels. By applying the DPD-based computational simulations, in conjunction with the microfluidic experiments, we found that the enhanced passage efficiency of Enu-MSCs was attributed not only to their reduced size post-enucleation but also to a decrease in resistance encountered during passage through confined spaces. This reduction in resistance is primarily related to an increased stretch ratio of the cells, which correlates closely with cell deformability. Consequently, our finding reveals a new insight: enhanced cell deformability resulting from enucleation may play a more crucial role than size reduction alone in influencing the passage capabilities of cellular vehicles. To corroborate findings from our microfluidic experiments, we further applied optical tissue clearing and fMOST to assess the in vivo distribution of MSCs in fibrotic lungs. These technologies provide the opportunity to precisely observe the in situ distribution and condition of MSCs in blood vessels.

In summary, this study introduces Enu-MSCs as deformability-augmented stem cells, which showed advantages in facilitating the passage of stem cells through narrow capillaries with blood flow. Moreover, it elucidates the underlying mechanisms of this enhanced deformability, including decreased mechanical resistance and reduced cell diameter, during transit through constricted spaces. These findings provide insights into the importance of cell deformability in overcoming challenges associated with cell entrapment, as well as mechanobiological explanations and therapeutic advantages regarding improved deformability and enucleation. The results suggest a potential strategy for designing stem cell-based drug delivery system tailored to vascular stenosis pathological conditions.

Limitations of the study

Microfluidic chips have been used as a promising tool for analyzing cellular mechanobiology and monitoring the dynamic transit behavior of cell vehicles through constricted vessels.31,32,33,34,35 However, present microfluidic setup has limitations in capturing interactions between MSCs and the capillary endothelium and in replicating the elastic properties of blood vessel walls. Therefore, further advancements of microfluidic technology and more suitable materials are warranted to improve the fidelity of these models. Due to current technical limitations, this study did not fully separate and independently investigate the effects of cell size and cell deformability on the ability of cells to traverse narrow microchannels. To eliminate the impacts of cell size, we tried to compare the dynamics of MSCs and Enu-MSCs of similar size and demonstrated that Enu-MSCs have better deformability in traversing through constricted spaces. However, a more quantitative analysis of this difference is still necessary to provide more convinced evidence for the augmented deformability of Enu-MSCs.

Besides, owing to the imaging resolution and fluorescence penetration depth, our experiments could not precisely monitor the dynamic process of MSCs as they traversed narrow vessels in vivo, necessitating the further development of enhanced in situ observation techniques. Moreover, the present study showed that Enu-MSCs exhibited a short lifespan and accelerated pulmonary clearance following systemic administration, with no detectable adverse effects noted. This accelerated lung clearance of Enu-MSCs relative to MSCs may reduce the risk of vascular occlusion17 and long-term engraftment-related safety concern.52 However, further experiments to better demonstrate this advantage are still required. For the therapeutic application, HGF was used as the model medicine because it has been extensively reported to exert potent therapeutic effects in early-stage PF, which relies on the modulation of TGF-β signaling.28 This mechanism explains why HGF is more effective in the early phase of fibrosis, whereas targeting the TGF-β pathway shows only limited efficacy in advanced fibrosis. Overall, further investigation of whether Enu-MSCs can ameliorate advanced PF is required. For example, combined therapeutic strategies or innovative therapeutic agents should be employed to address both early fibrotic transformation and established fibrotic tissue. In addition, more comprehensive safety studies, particularly regarding the long-term risk of Enu-MSCs in the fibrotic lungs, remain imperative in the future. Moreover, studying human cell behavior in murine models presents inherent limitations due to interspecies differences in vascular and cellular dimensions. Particularly, human blood vessels and cells are typically larger than those of mice, which may affect the translatability of findings related to cell deformability. The diameters of human MSCs (>15 μm), considered in the present study, are significantly larger than the capillaries in both humans and mice (<8 μm).53,54 Therefore, the evaluation of Enu-MSCs traversing through murine vasculature may provide useful insight into their behavior in human capillaries. In the present study, we did not quantitatively analyze the narrowed microvasculature in human fibrotic lungs due to the insufficient number of lung samples. Further research is necessary to validate whether observations regarding the cellular mechanical properties in murine models can be reliably extrapolated to human physiology, and quantitative determination of the pathological changes in vascular dimension reduction is suggested.

Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (referred to as ‘MSC’) were provided by SinoCell technology Ltd. (Ningbo, China) and were authenticated before the study. Method details

Preparation of enucleated MSCs (Enu-MSCs)

Enucleation strategy was modified from the previously reported articles.19,28 Briefly, 50% Ficoll PM400 (Cytiva, 17030010) was prepared with ultrapure water by magnetic stirring at room temperature. Ficoll solution was disinfected by autoclaving. Afterward, 50% Ficoll solution was diluted to 25%, 17%, 16%, 15%, and 12.5% solutions with α-MEM (Vivacell, C3060-0500), respectively. Cytochalasin B (Sigma, C6762) was added into all Ficoll solutions at 10 μg ml−1 as the final concentration. Then, 2 mL 25%, 2 mL 17%, 0.5 mL 16%, 0.5 mL 15%, and 3 mL 12.5% Ficoll solutions were added into centrifuge tubes (Beckman coulter, 331374) gently. Tubes were balanced at 37°C, 50% humidity incubator overnight. MSCs were collected with TrypLE Select Enzyme (Gibco, 12563011), and resuspended with 2 mL 12.5% Ficoll solution. Furthermore, MSCs were layered on the top of density gradient and finally filled with α-MEM. Ultracentrifuge (Beckman coulter, XPN-100) using SW41 rotor was set with level 7 acceleration and deceleration. Tubes were centrifuged at 31°C for 1 h for 26,000 rpm. Enu-MSCs were collected from top layers and washed with α-MEM medium 3 times. Enucleated rate was analyzed by flow cytometry. See Figure S18 for gating strategy. Feret’s diameters of MSCs and Enu-MSCs were determined by the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, 1.53e).

Microfluidic platform

Most previous experimental observations of 3-dimensional (3D) cell deformation typically adopt long and narrow rectangular microchannels through which cells must travel. Its geometric shape is quite different from the constrictions of the physiological capillary-like micro-environment.50,51 In this study, the microfluidic designs used a row of PDMS pillars to mimic the constricted blood vessels. The contractions formed by adjacent pillars mimic the narrow gaps created by extracellular matrix fibers and other cells, making possible high-resolution imaging of cell deformation. The microfluidic chamber is fabricated by Suzhou Chip Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd, using soft lithographic techniques. The primary mold is made of SU8 resist with two-layer mask and two-layer process. The first layer defines the central area of interest in the MSC deformation behavior experiment. A row of PDMS pillars forms confined 3D constriction micropores to mimic the narrow vascular structure in extracellular matrices and interstitial spaces (2–30 μm).52 The second layer defines the vaster reservoirs for input/output ports and easier interfacing with cell solutions with a width of 1000 μm.

Western blot

Western blot studies were performed to analyze the expression of VLA-4 and CXCR4. Briefly, MSCs were collected by trypsin (Cienry, CR25200), and were lysed in RIPA (Boster, AR0102) with 1% PMSF (Boster, AR1179) in ice bath. The lysates were then boiled in SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Boster, AR1112) at dry bath (Joanlab, DB100) for 15 min, and quantified by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein concentration determination kit (Boster, AR1189). The protein samples were separated in precast polyacrylamide gels (Boster, AR1203), and transferred to PVDF films (Boster, AR0136-04). Afterward, the films were blocked with 5% nonfat powdered milk (Beyotime, P0216), and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight with gentle shaking. After HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies incubating, films were developed on Bio-Rad Chemidoc MP (Bio-Rad, 12003154) with chemiluminescent substrate (Boster, AR1170).

Transwell assay

Transwell migration studies were applied using culture plates with 8 μm porous filters (Corning, 3422). In brief, MSCs and Enu-MSCs were placed in the upper chamber, which was filled with serum-free culture medium. The lower chamber was filled with serum-free medium with 0.1 μg mL−1 stromal cell derived factor-1α (SDF-1α). The culture plate was incubated at 37°C overnight and the upper surface of filter was wiped using cotton swabs. After fixing with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Aladdin, C104188) for 10 min, the membrane was stained with crystal violet solution for 15 min (Beyotime, C0121) and washed twice. The migrated cells were then counted by microscopy.

Immunofluorescence staining of cells

MSCs and Enu-MSCs were cultured in glass bottom culture dishes (Saining, 1051000). The cells were then fixed in 4% PFA solution (Aladdin, C104188), and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, T8200). Goat normal serum (Yeasen, 36119ES03) was used to block cells. Afterward, cells were stained with indicated primary antibodies at 4°C overnight and washed twice, and then stained with fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI solution (Solarbio, C0065) at room temperature for 2 h. Images were taken by a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Leica, TCS SP8).

Isolation and characterization of extracellular vesicles (EVs)

EVs were collected and purified by ultracentrifugation as previous reported.55 Culture medium was centrifugated at 3,000 g for 30 min and 10,000 g for another 30 min to remove cells and debris by a centrifuge (Beckman coulter, Aliegra 64R centrifuge). Afterward, the supernatant was precipitated using ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 70 min. After resuspending and washing by serum-free culture medium, EVs were further ultracentrifuged at 100,000 g for another 70 min by an ultracentrifuge (Beckman coulter, XPN-100). The EV pellets were resuspended in PBS and tested immediately. For morphology, EVs were observed by transmission electron microscope (Thermo FEI, Tecnai G2 spirit). EVs were stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 30 s. The zeta potential, particle size, and size distribution of EVs were detected by dynamic light scattering (Malvern Panalytical, Nano ZS90).

Model formulation of dissipative particle dynamics

Dissipative particle dynamic (DPD), a coarse-grained mesoscopic simulation method, employs a simplified representation in which clusters of molecules are treated as single units.56 In this framework, the position and momentum of the particle are updated in continuous phases but spaced at discrete time steps. The MSC nucleus is similarly modeled as a viscoelastic shell, consisting of a 2D triangular network with multiple vertices. The MSC membrane is represented by Nk,MSC = 3994 particles, with a diameter of 18.0 μm, as supported by the corresponding experimental data (Figure S5). The MSC nucleus is represented by Nk,nucleus = 1994 particles, with a diameter of 11.25 μm. All simulations were performed by a modified version of the LAMMPS,63 with a time step Δt = 3.78 ms. Fluorescence micro-optical sectioning tomography (fMOST) lung sample preparation

Lung sample preparation was followed with previous report with several modifications.37 Briefly, mice were anesthetized and perfused with 0.01 M PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde. Then, the lung samples were removed from the bodies carefully and incubated in 0.01 M PBS at 4°C for 24 h. The lung samples were further dehydrated in ethanol solution of 50%, 75%, 95%, 100% (each for 2 h) accordingly, and soaked in 0.3% Sudan black B (Sigma, 199664) ethyl alcohol solution overnight. Afterward, the lung samples were immersed in an LR White resin solution with 50%, 75%, 100% for 2 h in each gradient, and finally immersed in 100% LR White resin solution for 60 h at 38°C.

Whole-lung imaging by fMOST

The whole lung samples were immobilized on a 3D nano-precision translation stage and imaged with the fMOST system (Wuhan OE-Bio, BioMapping5000 N-S), consisting of Olympus microscope (Olympus, LUMPLFLN NA 0.5), two lasers (Cobolt, Cobolt 06-MLD) and TDI-CCD. The lung samples were cut into ultra-thin slices at a resolution of 0.325 μm by 0.325 μm by 1 μm with simultaneous propidium iodide (PI) staining and then acquired images via mosaic scan.

High-precision 3D visualization of MSCs and Enu-MSCs within blood vessels

Image pre-processing was performed for the two channels by steps of seamless mosaic stitching and illumination correction. The raw data were saved in 8-bit depth TIFF format using LZW compression. The corresponding algorithm was implemented in the MATLAB platform and optimized in parallel for-loop. The optimized slice dataset collected by the fMOST system was resampled at a voxel resolution of 1 μm by 1 μm by 2 μm and analyzed by Amira (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Version 2023.2). The PI channel was executed with volume rendering to visualize the intricate internal structure of lungs. The iso-surface was generated to segment and reconstruct MSCs or Enu-MSCs in another fluorescent channel. The virtual vascular endoscopy function was used to explore the localization of MSCs within small vessels.

Biodistribution of stem cells in PF mice after systemic administration

In order to visualize MSCs and Enu-MSCs in vivo, DiR Iodide (Yeasen, 40757ES25) was used to label stem cells following manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, stem cells were collected and incubated with 2.5 μM DiR Iodide for 20 min. Afterward, stem cells were washed with PBS for 3 times. These fluorescence-labelled cells (3 × 105 cells per mouse) were then intravenously injected into PF mice 24 h after the BLM treatment. Mice were sacrificed at indicated time point. Major organs, including heart, lung, liver, spleen, and kidney were subjected to fluorescence imaging immediately using an In Vivo Imaging System (Caliper PerkinElmer, IVIS Spectrum).

Tissue clearing and imaging

Tissue clearing experiments followed CUBIC tissue-clearing method with slight modification. Briefly, mice were sacrificed to harvest their lung tissues. The collected lung tissues were then fixed in 4% PFA (Aladdin, C104188) at 4°C overnight, then washed 3 times with PBS for 4 h under gentle shaking. Prewarmed 50% CUBIC-L reagents (Tokyo chemical industry, T3740) were used to pre-delipidated tissues for 24 h. Then, the tissues were treated with 100% CUBIC-L reagents for 7 days, and fresh reagents were changed every 2 days. Clearing tissues were washed 4 times for 4 h. After that, tissues were treated with 20% sucrose (Sinopharm, 10021463) for 24 h and embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura Finetek, 4583) at −80°C overnight. Then, the tissue samples thawed at room temperature and were washed for 3 times. 5% BSA (Solarbio, A8020) solution were used to block tissue samples for 24 h at 4°C. These samples were treated with anti-alpha smooth muscle actin antibody for 3 days at 37°C and goat anti mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 647) for 3 days at 37°C. The tissues were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% PFA (Aladdin, C104188) overnight. The lung tissues treated with anti-Mitochondria (Cy3 conjugate) antibody were handled following similar methods. Before imaging, 50% and 100% CUBIC-R reagents were used to treat tissue samples for 1 day separately. The images were taken by a lightsheet microscope (Light innovation technology, LiTone XL) and analyzed by Imaris software (Oxford, Version 10.0).

Proteomic analysis

MSCs or Enu-MSCs were cultured in complete medium for 24 h, and the supernatant was then collected (n = 6 biological replicates). To extract total protein, the collected cell culture supernatant was ultrafiltrated and balanced with DB buffer (6 M Urea, 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate, pH = 8.5). Afterward, 1 M DL-Dithiothreitol was added to the supernatant and reacted for 1 h at 56°C. Subsequently, alkylation was perfored with excess iodoacetamide for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Protein quality test was conducted using BSA standard solution and SDS-PAGE. LC-MS/MS analysis of protein was performed using Vanquish Neo UHPLC -Astral (Novogene, China). The raw files were searched and analyzed using the DIA-NN library search software, according to protein database from "UniProt: Homo sapiens Swiss-Prot" (downloaded on 11 June 2025, included 20,422 sequences). DEPs were used for Volcanic map analysis, cluster heatmap analysis and enrichment analysis of GO, IPR and KEGG.

Mouse model of PF

PF mouse model was established as previously described.12,13 Briefly, male 6-week-old C57Bl/6 mice were housed in an individually ventilated animal feeding system (Suzhou Fengshi, ZJ-4 IVC system) with standard housing conditions. After 1 week of feeding in the system, C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized and intratracheally injected BLM at a dose of 0.8 mg kg−1 in 50 μL of normal saline solution. Healthy group mice were anesthetized but administrated in normal saline solution with the same volume.

Gene transfection of synthesized mRNA

Synthesized human hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) mRNA and enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) were designed and synthesized by RNAlfa Biotechnology, Hefei, Anhui, China. MSCs and Enu-MSCs were collected and resuspended in a serum-free culture medium. Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermofisher, L3000001) and synthesized mRNA were mixed separately with pre-warmed Opti-MEM (Thermofisher, 31985070) and cultured for 5 min at room temperature. Then, they were mixed and incubated for another 15 min. Mixed reagents containing 3 μg synthesized mRNA were added into 1 × 106 cells and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Afterward, cells were washed twice with serum-free culture medium and applied in in vitro or in vivo studies.

Anti-fibrosis treatments in fibrotic lung cell models

TC-1 cells were induced to fibrosis via Bleomycin (BLM) (Aladdin, B107423) treatments as the in vitro cellular model to evaluate the anti-fibrotic effects of Enu-MSCs. In brief, TC-1 cells were seeded in 24-well culture plate before the treatment of 10 μg mL−1 BLM at 37°C for 24 h. These cells were washed twice with culture medium to remove the excess BLM. Meanwhile, MSCs with or without enucleation and mRNA transfection engineering were plated in Boyden chamber porous filters (Corning, 3413). The upper compartments of chamber were moved to culture plates containing BLM-injured TC-1 cells. Culture medium were collected at 48 h after co-incubation and analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to evaluate anti-fibrotic capacity of MSCs with different treatments.

PF intervention in murine model

In brief, different MSC treatment groups were intravenously administered into PF model mice at a dosage of 3 × 105 cells per mouse on the day following the initial BLM challenge. The survival of all mice was monitored until their sacrifice on Day 28 post-BLM challenge. At the end of the treatment period, mice were firstly imaged by micro-CT. Then, all mice were sacrificed for further histological and pathological evaluations. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining of lung samples were performed to estimate the fibrotic pathology and quantify the fibrotic ratio. Meanwhile, quantitative studies of the expression levels of TGF-β and MMP-9 lung homogenates were determined by ELISA. Furthermore, the levels of hydroxyproline (HYP) in lung samples were determined by HYP content assay kit (SolarBio, BC0255).

Histological assessment of lung sections

For paraffin sections, lung samples were fixed with 4% PFA (Aladdin, C104188) and embedded in paraffin (Sinopharm, 69019361). The samples were then cut into 5 μm-thick sections. H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome staining were performed in automated slide stainer (ThermoFisher, Gemini AS). Slices were observed and analyzed with a virtual digital slice scanning system (OLYMPUS, VS120). Fibrotic ratios were quantified by counting the ratio of fibrotic area using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, 1.53e).

For frozen sections, lung samples were fixed with 4% PFA (Aladdin, C104188) and embedded in O.C.T. (Sakura Finetek, 4583) at −80°C overnight. The samples were then cut into 10 μm-thick sections, and blocked with normal goat serum (Yeasen, 36119ES03). After staining with indicated primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, slices were washed with PBS twice and incubated with indicated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Images were observed with a virtual digital slice scanning system (OLYMPUS, VS120).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

HGF, matrix metalloprotein-9 (MMP-9), and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) were measured by ELISA kit. Briefly, murine lungs were homogenized in PBS with 1% PMSF (Boster, AR1179) using a homogenizer (Jingxin, JXFSTPRP-24). The lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 rpm, 4°C for 15 min. The supernatant was diluted with buffer and loaded into ELISA plates. After culturing with primary and secondary antibodies, the absorbance was determined using a μQuant plate reader (Biotek, ELx800) following manufacturer’s instruction. The concentrations were calculated from standard curves in CurveExpert 1.40.

Cell apoptosis determination

Cell apoptosis rates were detected by Annexin V-Alexa Fluor 647/PI apoptosis detection kit (Yeasen, 40304ES50). In brief, TC-1 cells were collected by trypsin without ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, Yeasen, 40124ES60) and washed with PBS twice. The cell samples were suspended in binding buffer and incubated with annexin V and PI reagents for 15 min at 25°C. Afterward, cell samples were analyzed by flow cytometry immediately. The corresponding gating strategy could be found in Figure S17B.

Cellular proliferation assessment

The proliferation rates were estimated by BeyoClick EdU-647 cell proliferation detection kit (Beyotimes, C0081S). Briefly, prewarmed EdU reagents were added into TC-1 culture medium at a final concentration of 10 μM. After 2 h culturing, culture medium was removed, and TC-1 cells were resuspended and fixed with 4% PFA (Aladdin, C104188) for 15 min. Meanwhile, click additive reagents were resolved with ultrapure water, and mixed with click reaction buffer, CuSO4, and Azide 647 following the kit instruction. Afterward, cells were cultured with click reaction solution for 30 min, and analyzed by flow cytometry. The corresponding gating strategy could be found in Figure S17C.

Cell viability

Cell Counting Kits (CCK)-8 (Beyotime, C0038) were applied to determine the cell viability. Briefly, 10 μL CCK-8 reagents were added into 100 μL culture medium and co-cultured with cell samples for 2 h at 37°C. Absorbance was detected using a μQuant plate reader (Biotek, ELx800) and data were analyzed in Microsoft Excel software (Microsoft, 16.0.14332.20848).

Micro-CT scanning

A micro-CT scanner (Milabs, U-CT-XUHR) was applied to observe the fibrotic pathology of PF mice after the treatments. Briefly, mice were anesthetized by isoflurane during scanning. The scanner system was set to accurate mode. The voltage of scanning was 50 kV. The current of scanning was 0.21 mA, and the exposure time of scanning was 75 ms.

Preliminary safety evaluation of Enu-MSC in ICR mice

The major side effects on ICR mice after the administration of Enu-MSC were determined to evaluate the safety concerns of Enu-MSCs. In short, Enu-MSCs and HGF-mRNA loaded Enu-MSCs were intravenously administered to healthy ICR mice at a dosage of 3 × 105 cells per mouse. Normal saline intravenous injection was set as a control group. The body weight was monitored every 4 days. On day 28, major organs and blood samples were collected and analyzed to estimate the potential risks. In addition, H&E staining was performed on major organs sections to study the potential pathological changes caused by Enu-MSCs.

Quantification and statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, LLC) was applied to analyze the data and calculate the statistical differences between indicated groups. Unpaired Student’s t test was performed to access differences between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with multiple comparisons was performed to access differences among multiple groups. p value > 0.05 was considered as having no significance.
