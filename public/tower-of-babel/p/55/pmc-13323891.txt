Gintonin prevents paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain via spinal LPA3 receptors and oligodendrocyte precursor cells in mice
Authors: Keun-Tae Park, Heejoon Jo, Seung-Yeol Nah, Young-Ho Jin, Ik-Hyun Cho, Woojin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) remains a major clinical challenge with limited treatment options. Gintonin, a glycolipoprotein complex derived from Panax ginseng Meyer, acts as a ligand for lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) receptors and has been reported to exert neuroprotective effects. This study investigated gintonin's actions in paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain (8 mg/kg, i.p.), focusing on a previously unreported LPA3–OPC mechanism.MethodsGintonin (50–200 mg/kg, p.o.) was repeatedly administered in a paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain model. Mechanical and cold allodynia were assessed, and spinal tissues were analyzed for LPA receptor expression. Receptor-specific siRNAs were intrathecally delivered to identify the subtype involved. Immunofluorescence and Cy5.5-labeled gintonin were used to examine LPA3 receptor localization in NG2+ oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs).ResultsGintonin attenuated paclitaxel-induced mechanical and cold allodynia, with the greatest effects observed at 100 and 200 mg/kg, and upregulated spinal gene expression of LPA1 and LPA3, but not LPA2 receptor. LPA3-specific siRNA abolished its analgesic effect, confirming LPA3 receptor dependence. Gintonin also preserved NG2+ OPCs, with immunofluorescence and Cy5.5-labeled gintonin demonstrating co-localization with LPA3 receptors.ConclusionThese findings suggest that gintonin prevents paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain through LPA3 receptor–mediated regulation of spinal OPCs.Keywords: Antinociceptive effect, Gintonin, Lysophosphatidic acid receptor, Neuropathic pain, Oligodendrocyte precursor cell, Paclitaxel, Panax ginseng

Introduction

Paclitaxel, a representative chemotherapy agent used to treat ovarian and lung cancer by targeting microtubules, has also been reported to have a very high incidence of cold and mechanical allodynia [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. These abnormal sensations, characterized by sensory disturbances such as numbness and paresthesia, represent one of the most common and debilitating adverse effects of paclitaxel treatment [5,6]. This disruption is known to predominantly affect the sensory nervous system, whereas the motor and autonomic systems are relatively spared [[7], [8], [9]].

Lysophosphatidic acid (LPA; 1-acyl-sn-glycerol-3-phosphate), a member of the lysophospholipid family, is a bioactive lipid mediator that plays a pivotal role in intercellular signal transduction. It is predominantly generated from membrane-derived phospholipids and exerts its effects through six specific G protein-coupled receptors, termed LPA receptors 1 to 6 (LPA1–6). These receptors are widely distributed across various cell types in both the central (CNS) and peripheral nervous systems (PNS) and are implicated in diverse neural signaling pathways and physiological functions [7]. Signal transduction through LPA receptors modulate a wide range of cellular processes, including cell motility, migration, neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and angiogenesis under both physiological and pathological conditions within the nervous system [8,9]. Among the six known LPA receptors, LPA1–LPA3 belong to the endothelial differentiation gene (EDG) family, whereas LPA4–LPA6 are members of the P2Y purinergic receptor family. These receptor subtypes differ in their downstream signaling properties, such as regulation of intracellular Rho activity, calcium mobilization, phosphoinositol turnover, and activation of intracellular kinases [10]. LPA1-LPA3 receptors are also known to be present in the spinal cord [11,12] and they are also involved in various physiological and pathological processes, including cell proliferation [13], migration [14], and differentiation [15]. Their role in pain modulation is complex, with evidence suggesting both pronociceptive [16,17] and antinociceptive [18] effects depending on the receptor subtype involved. Since LPA receptors signaling can exert either beneficial or detrimental effects in the CNS depending on the specific receptor pathways activated, further clarification of their protective roles in the spinal cord, particularly in chemotherapy-induced neuropathic pain, is needed.

Panax ginseng Meyer (P. ginseng) contains various bioactive compounds such as alkaloids, ginsenosides, phenols, and polysaccharides [19,20]. Although the physiological activities of ginsenosides have been extensively investigated, research on non-saponin components of P. ginseng has been relatively limited. Recently, a novel non-saponin fraction designated ‘gintonin’ has been identified [21], and several studies, including our own, have reported its effects in various animal models of multiple sclerosis [22], Parkinson's disease, and Alzheimer's disease [23]. Gintonin is a glycosylated protein complex derived from P. ginseng that contains LPAs, which are recognized as potent mitogenic lipid mediators [24]. Its biological actions are mediated through the activation of LPA-specific G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) [25]. Although gintonin has been studied with respect to progressive neurodegeneration and nervous system dysfunction, this study investigates gintonin in a paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain model, focusing on an LPA3–OPC-associated mechanism. In the present study, we assessed the effects of gintonin on chemotherapy-induced neuropathic pain and investigated potential mechanisms involving spinal LPA1 and LPA3 receptors and OPCs.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice weighing 20 to 22 g (seven weeks old at the start of study; Daehan Biolink, Daejeon, South Korea) were used in this study. They were housed six per cage under controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and humidity (60 ± 5%). Food and water were provided ad libitum, and behavioral experiments were conducted after a one-week adaptation period. Experimental protocols were approved by the Kyung Hee University Animal Care and Use Committee (approval No. KHUASP-24-383, 7 August 2024). Mice were routinely monitored for adverse drug effects.

Preparation of gintonin

Gintonin was produced by the fermentation ethanol extraction methods [26] [27]. 1 kg of 4-year-old ginseng (Korea Ginseng Corporation, Daejeon) was cut into approximately 3 mm pieces and extracted eight times with 70% fermented ethanol at 80 °C for 8 h each. The extract was concentrated to 340 g and diluted 1:10 with cold distilled water. The precipitate and supernatant obtained through water fractionation after ethanol extraction were separated by centrifugation at 845×g (rcf) for 20 min. The precipitate was freeze-dried (lyophilization) to obtain gintonin. Gintonin contains 30.3% protein, 30% carbohydrate, and 20.2% lipid of the total composition, and small amounts of other components are also present. Trace qualitative analysis results showed that gintonin also contains small amounts of triacylglycerol and diacylglycerol.

Experimental groups

Male C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups (n = 6 per group). Paclitaxel-induced peripheral neuropathic pain was induced by intraperitoneal injection of paclitaxel. The experimental groups were organized as follows: the Control group received only the vehicle without paclitaxel or gintonin; the Paclitaxel group was administered paclitaxel alone without gintonin; Gintonin was orally administered at doses of 50, 100, and 200 mg/kg to the respective treatment groups following paclitaxel injection. Agonist and antagonist studies and siRNA intrathecal injection studies were also performed with at least six mice per group.

Paclitaxel, gintonin, and LPA1, 2 or 3 receptors antagonists administrations

Paclitaxel (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved at 6 mg/mL in a 50%: 50% mixture of Kolliphor EL (Sigma-Aldrich) solution and ethanol. Paclitaxel stock was diluted to 0.2 mg/mL in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), volume corresponding to 2 mg/kg, according to body weight, was injected intraperitoneally to mice to induce neuropathic pain. The vehicle group was administered the same volume of PBS dilution without paclitaxel via the same route. Paclitaxel and vehicle solution were injected intraperitoneally once every two days at a concentration of 2 mg/kg a total of 4 times (D0, 2, 4, 6, a cumulative dose of 8 mg/kg) (Fig. 1A). Gintonin was dissolved in saline and administered orally at doses of 50, 100, and 200 mg/kg using an oral sonde (Jungdo-BNP, Seoul, South Korea), a total of 8 times every other day. On D6, Ki16425 (LPA1 and 3 receptors antagonist; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and H2L5186303 (LPA2 receptor antagonist; Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK) were administered via intraperitoneal injection at doses of 10 mg/kg and 1 mg/kg, respectively. In the LPA receptor knockdown study (Fig. 3), intrathecal administration of Lpa1 receptor or Lpa3 receptor siRNA was followed by behavioral assessment to evaluate the cumulative analgesic effect of gintonin, after which gintonin was administered and behavioral testing was repeated 24 h later.

siRNA administrations

Oligonucleotide sequences were synthesized in-house at Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea) and purified via RP HPLC using an acetonitrile gradient over an UniPS column. siRNA targeting the cDNA sequences of mouse Lpa1 receptor (GenBank accession number NM_1351397.1) and Lpa3 receptor (GenBank accession number NM_012152.2). The Lpa1 receptor siRNA sequences are 5′-GAA AUG AGU GAA ACG AAC U-3” (siRNA1), 5′-AGU UCG UUU CAC UCA UUU-3′ and the Lpa3 receptor siRNA sequences are 5′-CAG UUC UUG UCC ACC AUG-3′, 5′-UCA UGG UGG ACA AGA ACU G-3’. The realtime PCR primers for amplifying the full-length cDNA of mouse Lpa1 receptor were 5′-TCT CGG CAT AGT TCT GGA CC-3’ (forward) and 5′-CAT AGG CCA GGA CAT CGC A-3’ (reverse), and the primers for Lpa3 receptor were 5′-TCT TAT CTC CAC ACA CCA GTG-3’ (forward) and 5′-GCA CGT TAC ACT GCT TGC-3’ (reverse). To identify the knockdown efficacy of the two siRNA oligonucleotides, mice spinal cords were co-transfected and Lpa1 receptor, Lpa3 receptor gene expression was observed. As a result, the expression of Lpa1 receptor and Lpa3 receptor in the mouse spinal cords was significantly reduced on day 7 (Fig. 1A). siRNA was administered intrathecally at a concentration of 50 pmole for Lpa1 receptor siRNA and 100 pmole for Lpa3 receptor siRNA at 10 μL into the lumbar 4-5 region of mice. Administration was performed three times before paclitaxel injection. Non-targeting siRNA control was administered using siRNA without the RNA sequence of Lpa1 receptor and Lpa3 receptor.

Behavior assessments

To evaluate paclitaxel-induced cold and mechanical allodynia, behavioral tests were conducted using the acetone drop method and von Frey filament assay. Prior to testing, mice were acclimated for 30 min in a wire mesh chamber to reduce environmental stress. For cold allodynia assessment, 10 μL of acetone was gently applied to the plantar surface of the hind paw. Behavioral responses—such as paw withdrawal, flinching, and licking—were monitored and recorded for 30 s [28]. The average number of responses was calculated for analysis [29,30]. Mechanical sensitivity was assessed using calibrated von Frey filaments. Testing began with a filament of 3.22 g force, which was applied perpendicularly to the mid-plantar surface of the hind paw for 2–3 s or until bending occurred. A positive response prompted testing with the next lower force filament, while a negative response led to application of the next higher force. Responses were denoted as ’X’ (positive) or ’O’ (negative). The 50% paw withdrawal threshold was calculated using the up-down method described by Dixon and the formula modified by Chaplan [27].

Tissues preparations

Animals used for quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation. After confirming that the mice were unresponsive, they were perfused transcardially with cold PBS at pH 7.2. Blood samples for reactive oxygen species (ROS) analysis were collected before PBS perfusion, centrifuged at 15,000×g (rcf) for 10 min, and serum was separated and stored at −80 °C. Spinal cord tissue was excised after additional perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for IHC. Mice used for qPCR or Western blot were perfused via the heart under isoflurane anesthesia without PFA fixation, and spinal cords were removed. Tissues were frozen at −80 °C until further processing.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total ribonucleic acid (RNA) was isolated from the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and spinal cord using an AccuPrep RNA kit (Bioneer, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Only RNA samples with an OD260/OD280 ratio greater than 2.0 were used for reverse transcriptase PCR. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 0.4 μg of total RNA using the Maxime RT Premix kit (Intron Biotechnology, Seongnam, Republic of Korea), according to the manufacturer's protocol. The synthesized cDNA was used for quantitative PCR (qPCR) with the SensiFAST SYBR kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Gene-specific primers were used to amplify each target. The qPCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s, 58 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. Relative gene expression compared to the control group was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method [31], and gene expression value of the control was expression as 1. The primer sequences were as follows: Lpa1 receptor: forward 5′-GAG GAA TCG GGA CAC CAT GAT-3′; reverse 5′-ACA TCC AGC AAT AAC AAG ACC AAT-3′; Lpa2 receptor: forward 5′-GAC CAC ACT CAG CCT AGT CAA GAC-3′; reverse 5′-CTT ACA GTC CAG GCC ATC CA-3′; Lpa3 receptor: forward 5′-TTG CCT CTG CAA CAT CTC GG-3′; reverse 5′-CAT GAC GGA GTT GAG CAG TG-3′; Lpa4 receptor: forward 5′-CAG TGC CTC CCT GTT TGT CTT C-3′; reverse 5′-GAG AGG GCC AGG TTG GTG AT-3′; Lpa5 receptor: forward 5′-GCT CCA GTG CCC TGA CTA TC-3′; reverse 5′-GGG AAG TGA CAG GGT GAA GA-3’; Lpa6 receptor: forward 5′-ACA GTG ATG GGA GGA AGT GC-3′; reverse 5′-CCG CTG GAA AGT TCT CAA AG-3′; Gfap: forward 5′-GAA TCG CTG GAG GAG GAG AT-3′; reverse 5′-GCC ACT GCC TCG TAT TGA GT-3′; Iba1: forward 5′-GGA CAG ACT GCC AGC CTA AG-3′; reverse 5′-GAC GGC AGA TCC TCA TCA TT-3′; Ng2: forward 5′-ACC TGC TCT ACC GGG TGG T-3′; reverse 5′-CTG GCA CAC TGG CTA GGA GG-3′; Olig2: forward 5′-TGG AGA GAT GCG TTC GTT CC-3′; reverse 5′-GTG CTC TGC GTC TCG TCT AA-3′; Mbp: forward 5′-ATC CAA GTA CCT GGC CAC AG-3′; reverse 5′-CCT GTC ACC GCT AAA GAA GC-3′; Actin: forward 5′-CTG TCT GGC GGC ACC ACC AT-3′; reverse 5′-GCA ACT AAG TCA TAG TCC GC-3.

Western blot

The protein levels in the spinal cords were determined from the 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels. The protein content of cell lysates was assayed by the method of Bradford (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) using bovine serum albumin as standard protein. The proteins were transferred to the membranes of nitrocellulose at 120 V for 80 min. After diluting the primary antibodies, the obtained membranes were kept overnight at 4 with the diluted primary antibodies of anti- LPA1 receptor (ab23698, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and anti-LPA2 receptor (bs-10368R, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) and anti-LPA3 receptor (bs-2882R, Thermo fisher, Waltham, USA) and anti-NG2 (sc-166251, Santa Curz, Dalas, USA) and anti-Olig2 (ab9610, Merk-millipore, Cambridge, UK) and anti-MBP (ab749, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and anti-actin (1:1000, #NB100-1617, Novus Biologicals, Littleton CO, USA), respectively. They further incubated with secondary antibody (1:5000, horseradish conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, #31460, Thermo scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. The band signal intensities of proteins were measured using ImageJ software (Version 1.53, NIH, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to β-actin within each lane. Normalized values were expressed relative to the control group (set as 1). Relative expression values represent fold changes within each protein and are not intended for direct comparison between different proteins.

Immunofluorescence staining

After dissection, mouse spinal cords were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for 4 h. The tissues were then equilibrated in 30% sucrose solution for 48 h, embedded in OCT compound, and sectioned at a thickness of 20 μm. Sections mounted on slides were blocked at room temperature for 2 h in PBS containing 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Bovogen Biologicals, BSAB 0.1, Australia), 1% normal goat serum (NGS; Abcam, ab7481, UK), and 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, X100, USA) to prevent nonspecific binding. The sections were then incubated overnight with the following primary antibodies: mouse anti-NG2 (1:500, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-166251, USA) and rabbit anti-LPA3 receptors (1:3000, Bioss Antibodies, bs-2882R, USA). After three 10- minute washes in PBS, sections were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 h with the following secondary antibodies: Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Thermo Scientific, A-11008, USA) and Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor™ 594 (Thermo Scientific, A-11005, USA). The stained sections were mounted using Mounting Medium with DAPI-Aqueous, Fluoroshield (Abcam, ab104139, UK) and imaged using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM900, Germany). Quantitative analysis of the percentage area (% area) was performed using ImageJ version 1.54f (National Institutes of Health, USA). For analysis, regions of interest (ROIs) were defined in the dorsal horn region of each section. Following blocking and LPA3 receptors immunostaining as described above, gintonin conjugated with a Cy5.5 fluorophore was prepared at a final concentration of 62.5 μg/mL and applied to the surface of each section at 200 μl per slide glass. The sections were then incubated in the dark for 3 h, washed with PBS for 10 min, and mounted with aqueous mounting medium containing DAPI to perform mounting and nuclear counterstaining simultaneously.

Results

Gintonin alleviates paclitaxel-induced cold and mechanical allodynia and upregulates LPA receptor expression in the spinal cord

Paclitaxel injections, with a cumulative dose of 8 mg/kg (Fig. 1A), significantly induced both cold and mechanical allodynia in mice (Fig. 1B and C) from day 4(D4) to day 14 (D14) compared with control mice (Fig. 1B and C). Oral administration of gintonin (50, 100, or 200 mg/kg) significantly attenuated paclitaxel-induced cold and mechanical allodynia with the greatest effects at 100 and 200 mg/kg (Fig. 1B and C). Although a dose-dependent trend was observed, no significant differences were found between the gintonin-treated groups. Furthermore, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and western blot were performed to evaluate the gene and protein expression of LPA receptors (LPA1 receptor-LPA6 receptor) in gintonin-treated mice with paclitaxel-induced pain. In the paclitaxel-treated group, Lpa1 and Lpa3 receptor gene expressions were significantly increased, and gintonin further amplified this upregulation (Fig. 1D). As a result of protein analysis, both LPA1 receptor and LPA3 receptor protein expressions were increased in the paclitaxel-only group. In gintonin-treated mice, both proteins showed further increases, consistent with the mRNA results, indicating that gintonin enhances the paclitaxel-induced upregulation of LPA1 receptor and LPA3 receptors (Fig. 1E & F).

Blockade of spinal LPA1 and LPA3 receptors attenuates the antinociceptive effects of gintonin in paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain

To evaluate the role of LPA receptors in the analgesic effects of gintonin, antagonists for LPA1/3 receptor (Ki16425, 10 mg/kg, i.t.) or LPA2 receptor (H2L5186303, 1 mg/kg, i.t.) were administered prior to gintonin treatment. Behavioral testing showed that paclitaxel-treated mice exhibited significantly higher cold response whereas gintonin administration markedly reduced hypersensitivity, demonstrating an analgesic effect. However, co-administration of LPA1/3 receptor antagonist, Ki16425 abolished this effect, as an increased number of responses to acetone drop test was shown. Mechanical allodynia testing also revealed that gintonin prevented the decrease in the withdrawal threshold in paclitaxel-treated mice, whereas this effect was eliminated in the LPA1/3 receptor antagonist group. LPA2 receptor antagonism had no significant impact on pain behavior compared with gintonin alone, suggesting that LPA2 receptor does not play a major role in gintonin-mediated analgesia (Fig. 2A and B). Gene expression analysis showed significant upregulation of Lpa1 and Lpa3 receptors in the paclitaxel group, with further increases in the paclitaxel with gintonin group. This upregulation was attenuated by co-administration of the LPA1/3 receptor antagonist (Ki16425)(Fig. 2C). Protein analysis confirmed these findings as paclitaxel increased protein expression of LPA1 receptor and LPA3 receptors, which was further enhanced by gintonin. Co-administration of Ki16425 reduced the expression of both proteins to near paclitaxel-alone levels (Fig. 2D and E). These results suggest that the analgesic action of gintonin is primarily mediated through spinal LPA1 or LPA3 receptors.

LPA3 but not LPA1 receptor mediates the analgesic effect of gintonin in paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain

To determine whether LPA1 or LPA3 receptor is responsible for mediating the analgesic effect of gintonin, siRNA was employed to selectively silence each receptor. In paclitaxel-treated mice, gintonin maintained its analgesic effect when Lpa1 receptor was silenced, but this effect was completely lost when Lpa3 receptor was silenced, indicating that gintonin's action is depended primarily on the Lpa3 receptor pathway (Fig. 3A and B). Behavioral testing showed that gintonin induced a cumulative analgesic effect, with significant improvement in both cold and mechanical allodynia beginning on day 8 (D8).

Gene expression analysis confirmed effective knockdown, as Lpa1 and Lpa3 receptors mRNA levels were significantly reduced compared with the paclitaxel group (Fig. 3C). Moreover, protein analysis verified selective knockdown of Lpa1 and Lpa3 receptors by their respective siRNAs (Fig. 3F and G). Paclitaxel markedly decreased NG2, Olig2, and Mbp expression, [32], changes that are consistent with a loss of OPC- and myelin-related markers. [33,34]. These reductions were reversed by gintonin, except when combined with Lpa3 receptor siRNA, suggesting that the preservation of these OPC/myelin-related markers by gintonin is dependent on Lpa3 receptor signaling (Fig. 3D). Expressions, which are well-known markers for astrocytes and microglia, were both significantly increased by paclitaxel. After gintonin treatment both GFAP and Iba-1 expression were significantly normalized by gintonin and Lpa1 receptor siRNA but not by Lpa3 receptor siRNA, further supporting the critical role of LPA3 receptor in gintonin-mediated analgesia (Fig. 3E).

All three markers were downregulated following paclitaxel administration and upregulated after treatment with 100 or 200 mg/kg gintonin (Fig. 4A–C). Restoration of NG2 and LPA3 receptors expression by gintonin in the spinal dorsal horn after paclitaxel treatment

Using immunofluorescence analysis, we evaluated the expression and co-localization of NG2+ and LPA3 receptors in the spinal cord following paclitaxel and gintonin treatments. The analysis was conducted in the superficial laminae (I-III) of the dorsal horn (Fig. 5A). Paclitaxel administration alone resulted in a marked decrease in NG2 expression and a concomitant increase in LPA3 receptors expression (Fig. 5B). However, co-administration of paclitaxel and gintonin (200 mg/kg) restored the NG2 expression suppressed by paclitaxel, suggesting that gintonin exerts a protective or reactivating effect on OPCs (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, LPA3 receptors expression was further upregulated in the gintonin-treated group (Fig. 5D). Merged images and quantitative co-localization analysis revealed an increased overlap between NG2 and LPA3 receptors signals in the gintonin-treated group (Fig. 5E). Discussion

In the present study, the analgesic effects of gintonin and its underlying molecular mechanisms in a paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain model were elucidated, with its role on spinal LPA receptors and OPCs. The preventive effect of gintonin on pain development was abolished by Ki16425, an LPA1/3 receptor antagonist, indicating that its actions are mediated through these receptors. To further identify the specific receptor subtype involved, siRNAs targeting Lpa1 and Lpa3 receptor were employed and only Lpa3 receptor knockdown, but not Lpa1 receptor knockdown, effectively inhibited the effects of gintonin. Subsequently, gene and protein expression analyses were performed following paclitaxel and gintonin treatment. Finally, incubation with Cy5.5-labeled gintonin revealed its co-localization with LPA3 receptors in OPCs, providing evidence that gintonin may exert its action on spinal LPA3 receptors.

The effects of gintonin have been documented in several studies, including previous work by our group. Its therapeutic potential has been evaluated in animal models of Huntington's disease [35], Parkinson's disease [24], and encephalomyelitis [36]. In these models, gintonin demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties and exerted neuroprotective effects mediated through LPA receptors in the CNS. However, while the pharmacological properties of gintonin have been widely documented in several papers [[21], [22], [23], [24],37], this is the first study, to demonstrate its therapeutic potential in neuropathic pain. Previous studies have demonstrated that gintonin can transiently increase BBB permeability via LPA receptor dependent modulation of endothelial junctions. Systemically administered, fluorescently labeled gintonin has been detected in brain endothelial cells, neurons, and glial (including microglia, astrocytes, and oligodendrocyte lineage cells) cells supporting its ability to access the CNS [38]. However, future studies with quantitative pharmacokinetic analyses will be required to precisely establish spinal cord exposure.

In our study, the expression of LPA receptors at both the gene and protein levels was elevated in the paclitaxel-treated group and further enhanced after gintonin administration. Blocking these receptors, either pharmacologically or through siRNA-mediated knockdown, diminished the analgesic effects of gintonin, indicating that activation of spinal LPA receptors is essential for its action.

In contrast, previous work by Uchida and colleagues showed that mice lacking LPA1 (Lpar1−/−) or LPA3 (Lpar3−/−) receptors did not develop pain behaviors, suggesting that these receptors promote nociceptive signaling [39,40]. They further demonstrated that LPA3 receptor activity in sensory neurons facilitates pain by strengthening excitatory transmission. Although it is difficult to fully explain the discrepancies between the two results, LPA receptors may play distinct role during the developmental and chronic phase of the pain. In our experiments, the protective effect of gintonin was most apparent between days 4 and 8 (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). This aligns with the observation of Uchida et al., who found spinal LPA receptors levels elevated within 24–72 h after injury but declining thereafter in the dorsal horn, which may explain the reason why in our study receptor activation in the early phase did not reduce pain. Additional evidence supports this time-dependent role: administration of the LPA1 receptors antagonist Ki-16425 blocked LPA-induced neuropathic pain behaviors when given shortly after peripheral nerve injury, with maximal efficacy at 3 h but not maintained beyond this time. Treatment at 3 h, but not 6 h, also lowered substance P levels in the dorsal horn and increased expression of the calcium channel α2δ-1 subunit in the DRG, further indicating a narrow temporal window for LPA-mediated modulation of pain [40].

Furthermore, the release of nuclear factor erythroid-2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) following strong activation of LPA receptors may also help explain these discrepancies, as administration of the Nrf2 activator oltipraz dose-dependently attenuated paclitaxel-induced mechanical allodynia [41]. In our previous study, activation of LPA1/3 receptors was associated with Nrf2 activation, which prevented cell death and consequently ameliorated 3-nitropropionic acid (3-NPA)–induced Huntington's disease through subsequent inhibition of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathways [35]. However, the roles of LPA receptors in different neuronal and glial cell types—such as astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes—need further investigation to fully elucidate their contributions to pain mechanisms.

In our study, prevention of NG2+ OPC loss by gintonin may have protected neurons from functional damage of spinal circuits, thereby lowering pain sensitivity. Although the precise role of OPCs in pain remains to be clarified [42], their loss and the ensuing demyelination are known to impair axonal conduction, promote ectopic discharges, and heighten susceptibility to neuroinflammation [43,44]. Beyond serving as oligodendrocyte progenitors, OPCs have been reported to play active roles in neural circuit regulation in the CNS. Accumulating evidence suggests that OPCs are not only precursors for myelinating oligodendrocytes but also active participants in neural circuit regulation, where their interactions with neurons and glia can directly influence pain processing pathways [[45], [46], [47]]. Moreover, promoting OPC expansion resulted in increase in remyelination [48]. Although only few papers have examined the role of OPC in pain. In a rat spinal cord injury (SCI) model, OPC transplantation promoted locomotor recovery and attenuated neuropathic pain [49]. Likewise, OPC grafting enhanced neuronal survival and remyelination while modulating microRNA expression, facilitating recovery after contusive SCI [50]. In addition, although changes in pain behavior were not assessed, previous studies by Keirstead et al. [51] consistently demonstrated that transplantation of human embryonic stem cell-derived oligodendrocyte precursor cells (hESC-OPCs) in spinal cord injury models attenuated pathological progression by sparing white and gray matter, preserving motor neurons, generating mature oligodendrocytes, and ultimately promoting functional recovery with histological evidence of neuroprotection Together with our findings, these data suggest that preserving the function or number of the OPCs integrity—either pharmacologically by gintonin or via cell replacement—could help neuroprotection, and circuit stabilization. Our study identified changes in GFAP and Iba1 expression following gintonin treatment; however, these observations are limited to gene expression. Elucidation of the potential simultaneous or coordinated roles of astrocytes and microglia will require more in-depth studies, including pharmacological blockade, cell-type–specific modulation, or functional assays, to determine whether and how glial regulation contributes to the analgesic effects of gintonin. In the paclitaxel-injured spinal cord, activated microglia and astrocytes are reported to release cytokines, chemokines, ATP, and glutamate that compromise OPC survival and impede differentiation [52]. Conversely, loss of OPCs and demyelination increase axonal metabolic stress and ectopic firing, further recruiting and activating glia OPC [53]. OPCs are capable of releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including HMGB1, which can increase blood–brain barrier permeability, promote immune cell infiltration, and activate glial cells, thereby amplifying neuroinflammatory responses [54]. In addition, platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRα+) OPCs and immature oligodendrocytes have been shown to secrete chemokines such as C–C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) and C–X–C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) upon inflammatory stimulation, with CCL2 in particular reported to recruit microglia [55]. Thus, although, it remains challenging to determine which glial cell populations were initially activated, gintonin-mediated preservation of NG2+ OPCs and engaging OPC LPA3 receptors signaling, may have permit facilitated their timely differentiation into myelinating oligodendrocytes, thereby improving myelin integrity and axonal conduction [22]. In accordance with this, gene markers of mature oligodendrocytes (Mbp and Olig2) were also increased following gintonin injection. However, although not observed in our study, a simultaneous reduction in microglial and astrocytic activation would be expected to decrease the release of proinflammatory mediators, thereby alleviating stress on OPCs and promoting sustained remyelination [56,57]. Future studies are needed to delineate these mechanisms more precisely.

Although we did not directly assess OPC function in vivo, prior in vitro studies have shown that gintonin enhances OPC proliferation, promotes late-stage differentiation, and improves survival via LPA1/3 receptor–dependent signaling [58]. Importantly, in vivo studies have demonstrated that disruption of LPA receptor signaling impairs oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination, underscoring a critical role for LPA pathways in regulating OPC fate and oligodendrocyte lineage maintenance [59].

Conclusion

In conclusion, gintonin attenuated paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain in mice, and this effect required spinal LPA3 signaling. Gintonin was associated with preservation of OPC- and myelin-related markers in the spinal cord, although direct functional modulation of OPC activity was not assessed. These findings support gintonin as a candidate modulator of LPA receptor–related pathways in CIPN.
