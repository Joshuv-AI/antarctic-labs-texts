Tricuspid vs Bicuspid Aortic Valve Endocarditis
Authors: Geoffrey D Huntley, Juan A Quintero-Martinez, Edward El-Am, Zi Ye, Christopher G Scott, Christophe Tribouilloy, Maurice Enriquez-Sarano, Vuyisile T Nkomo, Daniel C DeSimone, Larry M Baddour, Hector G Michelena
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBicuspid aortic valve infective endocarditis (BAV-IE) patients exhibit more perivalvular complications as compared to tricuspid aortic valve (TAV) IE.ObjectivesWe examined symptom-to-diagnosis time in BAV-IE vs TAV-IE and its effect on outcomes.MethodsConsecutive adult patients (≥18 years) with native aortic valve IE were retrospectively reviewed from all Mayo Clinic US sites from 2015 to 2021. Clinical features, microbiology, and outcomes were extracted from electronic health records. IE-related major adverse cardiovascular events (IE-related MACE) were perivalvular infection extension, septic embolism, heart failure, and/or surgery.ResultsThere were 52 BAV-IE patients (mean age 47 ± 15 years, 85% [44/52] male) and 244 with TAV-IE (mean age 65 ± 14 years, 72% [177/244] male). Compared to TAV-IE, BAV-IE patients showed higher prevalence of streptococcal infections (54% [28/52] vs28% [69/244]; P = 0.0004), and higher suspected dental procedure source (10 [5/52] vs 2% [5/244]; P = 0.006). BAV-IE patients exhibited delayed symptom-to-diagnosis time (median 34 vs7 days; P < 0.0001). IE-related major adverse cardiovascular event (IE-MACE) was higher in BAV-IE (96% [50/52] vs 64% [156/244]; P < 0.0001). Age (OR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.59 to 0.92 per 10-years; P = 0.006), BAV(OR: 4.91; CI: 1.08-22.38; P = 0.04), and symptom-to-diagnosis time (OR: 1.23; CI: 1.09-1.38; P = 0.001 per week) were independently associated with IE-MACE. There was a linear 23% increase odds of IE-MACE per week of diagnosis delay. BAV-IE patients had superior 4-year survival but became similar after age and sex adjustment.ConclusionsBAV-IE patients incur delayed symptom-to-diagnosis time resulting in high IE complication burden, despite predominance of less virulent streptococcal causative microorganisms. These findings call for enhanced IE clinical suspicion in BAV patients and suggest antibiotic prophylaxis may be indicated in these patients.Key words: bicuspid aortic valve, complications, diagnostic delay, infective endocarditis, streptococci

Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) is the most common congenital heart defect, with an estimated prevalence of 0.5% to 0.77% in the general population.1,2 The BAV condition is a valvuloaortopathy with high morbidity burden; of which, aortic valve replacement is the most common and life-threatening complications the least common: infective endocarditis (IE) and aortic dissection.3 The incidence of IE in patients with native BAV is approximately 10 per 10,000 patient/years, which represents 17 to 23 times the IE incidence for the general population.4,5 Furthermore, the lifetime risk (from birth to 90 years old) of IE in BAV (BAV-IE) is not rare at approximately 6%, which is 4 times higher than the lifetime risk of aortic dissection of approximately 1% to 2%.3 Few publications have directly compared BAV-IE vs tricuspid aortic valve IE (TAV-IE);6, 7, 8 these studies showed BAV-IE patients were younger and presented with more perivalvular complications requiring surgery, and with higher incidence of streptococcal infections; however, reported in-hospital and late mortality were variable. If BAV-IE patients are younger and thus healthier than TAV-IE patients, with a high proportion of infections associated to less virulent bacteria (ie, streptococci), why do they develop more IE perivalvular complications? And, does this excess perivalvular extension result in more IE-related complications? In a recent multicenter study, we reported clinical IE presentations among BAV phenotypes and noted subsets of BAV patients incurring delayed referral for IE after symptom onset.9 Therefore, the objective of the current study was to investigate the differences in timing from onset of symptoms-to-diagnosis of IE in BAV-IE vs TAV-IE patients, the effect of these differences on outcomes, and their association with microbiological findings. We hypothesized that as compared with TAV-IE, patients with BAV-IE incur delayed symptoms-to-diagnosis which results in complicated IE.

Methods

The Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board approved this study.

Patients

Consecutive adult (≥18 years) patients with IE from January 2015 to December 2021 at all Mayo Clinic sites (3 main hospitals; Rochester, Florida, and Arizona; and 16 Health-system hospitals) were queried from institutional databases. Medical charts were retrospectively reviewed to identify BAV or TAV definite or possible IE. IE was defined according to the modified Duke criteria10 and current U.S. and European guidelines,11,12 and only the first episode of native aortic valve IE for each case was included, resulting in a total of 296 consecutive patients; 244 TAV-IE and 52 BAV-IE with clinically confirmed native aortic valve definite or possible IE.

Clinical data

All echocardiographic examinations were reviewed by a level III-trained echocardiologist to define valve morphology. Clinical characteristics, comorbidities, microbiology, and outcomes were extracted from electronic health records. The diagnosis of septic embolism, including cerebrovascular embolism, was based on clinical evaluation plus noninvasive imaging (ie, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging). The diagnosis of perivalvular extension included periaortic phlegmon/abscess or periaortic pseudoaneurysm and was based on noninvasive imaging (transthoracic echocardiography, transesophageal echocardiography, computed tomography, and/or magnetic resonance imaging). The diagnosis of heart failure was based on the universal definition of heart failure.13 Symptom-onset date was based on exhaustive review of the electronic medical record, and diagnosis date was based on when the criteria for definite or possible IE were met; clinical notes by cardiology attending physician and infectious disease attending physician were comprehensive and reviewed exhaustively for each patient. Specifically, symptom-onset date was defined by the presence of typical IE symptoms such as fever, chills, fatigue, malaise, weight loss, and night sweats. If these symptoms were absent, then any symptom that prompted the infectious disease workup that led to diagnosis of IE was considered to represent symptom-onset date. Blood stream infection culture isolates were identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometer. Streptococcal blood isolate were classified as anginosus, bovis, mitis, mutans, pyogenic, salivarious, and other.14,15

Outcomes

Outcomes studied were: 1) IE-related major adverse cardiovascular event (IE-MACE); 2) in-hospital mortality; and 3) 4-year mortality. An IE-MACE was defined as perivalvular infection extension, and/or septic embolism (anywhere), and/or heart failure, and/or cardiovascular surgery for IE. Most IE-MACE events were diagnosed in hospital but their onset could have preceded the hospitalization, whereas cardiac surgery for IE occurred exclusively in hospital. The mortality endpoint was all-cause death. In-hospital mortality was defined as occurring within 30 days of hospital admission. The long-term survival status was retrieved from electronic records and by Accurint (LexisNexis; RELX Group), a proprietary resource combining multiple national sources (queried in September 2023.).

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were examined by group. For categorical variables, statistical comparisons were made using chi-square or Fisher exact test, as appropriate depending on prevalence. For continuous variables, comparisons were made using nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Logistic regression was used to examine the association of in-hospital mortality and MACE with BAV after adjustment for known risk factors. These associations were summarized with OR and associated 95% confidence limits. A restricted cubic spline with varying number of knots based on quantiles was used to examine the association of MACE with time from symptoms to diagnosis. Given similar results for other number of knots, the plot was based on fitting the spline with 4 knots.

Mortality was analyzed as a time-to-event outcome; patients not known to be deceased were censored at time last known to be alive. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to plot survival by group. An age- and sex-adjusted Kaplan-Meier curve was also plotted using a method of direct adjustment proposed by Zhang.16 This method averages individual-predicted survival curves from the Cox model. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate the HR and associated 95% confidence limits of BAV with and without adjustment. The proportional hazards assumption was examined by plotting Schoenfeld residuals against time and testing for correlation between residual and time. A matched cohort was created by matching on sex and age within 5 years as a secondary analysis to further control for differences in these factors. The HR for death in the matched cohort was computed by stratified Cox regression. Subgroup analyses in patients with definite IE was also performed. Two-sided P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute) and R (version 4.2.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing17) were used for analyses.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. Of a total of 296 patients, there were 52 patients with BAV-IE (mean age 47 ± 15 years, 85% [8/52] male) and 244 patients with TAV-IE (mean age 65 ± 14 years, 73% [177/244] male). As compared to patients with TAV-IE, those with BAV-IE were significantly younger (P < 0.0001), had less hypertension (17% [9/52] vs 47% [115/244]; P < 0.0001), less diabetes (21% [11/52] vs 47% [114/244]; P = 0.0007), less chronic kidney disease (4% [2/52] vs 28% [69/244]; P < 0.0001), and were more likely to have definite IE (100% [52/52] vs 84% [204/244]; P = 0.002) and at least moderate aortic regurgitation at the time of IE diagnosis (79% [41/52] vs 51% [125/244]; P = 0.0003). There was no significant difference in antibiotic use before IE diagnosis (BAV-IE 35% [18/51] vs TAV-IE 25% [60/237]; P = 0.15).

When stratifying patients’ baseline characteristics by time-to-IE diagnosis (Supplemental Table 1), those with IE diagnosis after 7 days of symptoms-onset were over 3 times more likely to be BAV-IE patients (P < 0.0001), more likely to have definitive IE (P = 0.006), more likely to have moderate aortic regurgitation (P = 0.007), and less likely to have some comorbidities like chronic kidney disease and chronic pulmonary disease, all similar to Table 1. Infective endocarditis characteristics and complications

Patients with BAV-IE incurred delayed IE diagnosis after symptom-onset compared to TAV-IE (median 34 vs 7 days; P < 0.0001) and only 23% (12/52) of patients with BAV-IE were diagnosed within 1 week of symptom-onset compared with 55% (135/244) of TAV-IE (P < 0.0001), as shown in Table 2. Patients with BAV-IE had more perivalvular extension of infection (40% [21/52] vs 17% [42/244]; P = 0.0002), septic emboli (64% [33/52] vs 38% [93/244]; P = 0.0008), heart failure (56% [29/52] vs 26% [62/243]; P < 0.0001), and required more cardiovascular surgery (79% [41/52] vs 41% [100/244]; P < 0.0001) compared with TAV-IE, resulting in total IE-MACE rates of 96% (50/52) for BAV-IE vs 64% (156/244) for TAV-IE, P < 0.0001 (Table 2, Figure 1, Central Illustration). In addition, BAV-IE had larger vegetations and more cerebrovascular embolism and atrioventricular block (all P ≤ 0.002) (Table 2). The suspected source of infection was more likely to be a dental procedure in BAV-IE vs TAV-IE (10% [5/52] vs 2% [5/244]; P = 0.006). Notably, no BAV-IE or TAV-IE patients received antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures.

Infective endocarditis microbiologic characteristics

As shown in Table 3, patients with BAV-IE were more likely to have streptococci as the cause of IE as compared to TAV-IE (54% [28/52] vs 28% [69/244]; P = 0.0004), with a predominance of Streptococcus mitis in BAV-IE (64% vs 41%; P < 0.0001). The symptoms-to-diagnosis time in the subgroup of patients with streptococcal infection was significantly longer for BAV-IE as compared to TAV-IE (median 78 vs 10 days; P < 0.0001) (Table 3). Conversely, there was no significant difference between BAV-IE and TAV-IE in symptoms-to-diagnosis time in the subgroup with staphylococcal infection (P = 0.08) (Table 3). Remarkably, the symptoms-to-diagnosis time of nonstreptococcal vs streptococcal infections was not different for patients with TAV-IE (median 6 [IQR: 3, 14] vs 10 [3, 31] days, respectively; P = 0.11), yet, significantly delayed symptoms-to-diagnosis time for BAV-IE patients with streptococcal infections (median 11 [ IQR: 5, 34] days for nonstreptococcal vs 78 [IQR: 30, 106] days for streptococcal; P < 0.0001).

Risk factors associated with IE-MACE

As aforementioned, IE-MACE was significantly more common in BAV-IE patients (Table 2, Figure 1, Central Illustration). Older age (OR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.59-0.92 per 10-years; P = 0.006) was independently protective of IE-MACE, whereas BAV (OR: 4.91; CI: 1.08-22.38; P = 0.04) and symptoms-to-diagnosis time (OR: 1.23; CI: 1.09-1.38; P = 0.001) were independently associated to IE-related MACE occurrence; model that included age, sex, BAV, symptom-to-diagnosis time, and microbiology (Table 4). There was a linear 23% increase in odds of IE-MACE per week of delay from symptoms-to-diagnosis for the entire cohort (Figure 2, Central Illustration), with no evidence that the association was different for BAV and TAV (P = 0.92 for interaction). Neither streptococcal infection nor staphylococcal infection was associated with IE-MACE.

Mortality and associated risk factors

In-hospital mortality was similar between BAV-IE and TAV-IE (15 [8/52] vs 16% [38/244]; P = 0.97) (Table 2). Multivariable analyses for in-hospital mortality (Table 5) demonstrated that older age, BAV, and IE-MACE were independently associated with worse in-hospital mortality, whereas longer symptoms-to-diagnosis time and surgery were protective. Neither streptococcal infection nor staphylococcal infection was associated with in-hospital mortality.

Age- and sex-matched analysis

An age- and sex-matched analysis was performed to further account for major inherent differences within the BAV vs the TAV population. As shown in Supplemental Table 2, after matching, the mean age was 49 years in each group (P = 0.95) and 83% were male (BAV 88% [43/49] vs TAV 78% [38/49]; P = 0.18). A delay in symptoms-to-diagnosis time persisted in BAV-IE compared with TAV-IE (median 44 vs 8 days; P = 0.003). In addition, IE-MACE continued being more common in BAV-IE vs TAV-IE (96% [47/49] vs 65% [32/49]; P < 0.0001). Furthermore, streptococcal IE continued being more common in BAV-IE (57% [28/49] vs 25% [12/49]; P = 0.001). There was no significant difference in 4-year survival in the age- and sex-matched cohort (HR: 0.63; CI: 0.32-1.23; P = 0.17) (Figure 4).

Subanalysis in patients with definite IE exclusively

We examined the results after excluding nondefinite IE cases. There were 52 patients with definite BAV-IE (mean age 47 ± 15 years, 85% [44/52] male) and 204 patients with definite TAV-IE (mean age 64 ± 13 years, 76% [154/204] male). Compared to TAV-IE, BAV-IE patients continued to show higher prevalence of streptococcal infections (54% [28/52] vs 28% [58/204]; P = 0.0005), and higher suspected dental procedure source (10% [5/52] vs 2% [4/204]; P = 0.008). BAV-IE patients continued to exhibit significant delayed symptom-to-diagnosis time (median 34 vs 8 days; P < 0.0001), and the IE-MACE continued to be higher in BAV-IE (96% [50/52] vs 70% [142/204]; P < 0.0001). Again, there was a linear 24% increase odds of IE-MACE per week of diagnosis delay, and BAV status remained associated with in-hospital mortality.

Discussion

The principal findings of our study are: 1) as compared to TAV-IE patients, those with BAV-IE showed higher prevalence of streptococcal infections, and the suspected infection source in BAV-IE was more commonly a dental procedure; 2) BAV-IE patients exhibited significant delay in symptoms-to-diagnosis time as compared to TAV-IE, and the time delay increased in the streptococcal infection subgroup; 3) IE-MACE was significantly higher in BAV-IE including each separate component (perivalvular extension, septic emboli, heart failure, and surgery); 4) symptoms-to-diagnosis time conferred a linear 23% increase in IE-MACE per week of delay; 5) after age and sex matching, streptococcal infection predominance, delayed time to diagnosis, and higher IE-MACE persisted in patients with BAV-IE; and 6) 4-year survival was better for BAV-IE, a difference that attenuated after age and sex adjustment and matching. Neither symptoms-diagnosis time-delay nor IE-MACE was statistically associated with 4-year mortality.

Prior studies

Contemporary studies directly comparing BAV-IE and TAV-IE patients are few.4,6, 7, 8 Tribouilloy et al.6 retrospectively compared 50 BAV-IE patients vs 260 TAV-IE patients from 2 French tertiary-referral hospitals and conversely to our study, found that TAV-IE incurred more streptococcal infections (TAV 51% vs. BAV 34%; P = 0.028) as well no difference in symptoms-to-diagnosis time (TAV 57 days vs. BAV 48 days; P = 0.55). The prevalence of culture negative IE in the BAV-IE patients was high (24%), which may explain the differing streptocccal infection rates from our study. The authors suspected this was a result of prehospital antibiotic use in 28% of BAV-IE patients; however. prediagnosis antibiotic use was present in 35% of BAV-IE patients in our study with only an 8% prevalence of culture negative IE. Tribouilloy et al.6 confirmed for the first time a higher frequency of perivalvular abscess in BAV-IE compared to TAV-IE patients as well as a tendency for higher rates of surgery in BAV-IE patients, yet BAV did not associate with in-hospital mortality. The findings from Zegri-Reiriz et al.7 have greater similarities to our study; they compared 54 patients with BAV-IE against other low-moderate and high-risk IE patients from 31 Spanish hospitals and found an increased rate of intracardiac IE complications, more streptococcal infections, and more surgeries in BAV-IE patients; unfortunately, symptoms-to-diagnosis time was not evaluated in that study. Likewise, Kiyota et al4 also reported higher likelihood of root abscess and higher prevalence streptococcal infections in BAV-IE patients. Le et al.8 compared 51 BAV-IE to 159 TAV-IE patients, all of whom had undergone surgery for the treatment of IE; reporting again higher prevalence of streptococcal infections in BAV-IE, no significant difference in in-hospital mortality, and higher 10-year postoperative survival rates for BAV patients without matching for age. Finally, a recent European multicenter study18 reported similarities to our study in terms of perivalvular extension, higher rate of embolism, higher rates of surgery for BAV-IE, and same in-hospital mortality as compared to TAV-IE; however, time from symptoms to diagnosis was not explored.

Taken together, this prior literature is supportive of the most critical findings from our study. In regards to time from symptoms-to-diagnosis and its consequences, these have essentially not been studied before.

WHY do patients with BAV-IE present with more perivalvular extension and IE complications?

Turbulent flow and increased shear stress across a BAV, even when “normally functioning”,19 leads to increased endothelial damage, inflammation,20 and gradual valve calcification, which may promote sterile thrombus formation, which may result in noninfective cerebral and retinal embolism in BAV patients.21 These initially sterile valvular thrombi may serve as a nidus for bacteria adhesion,20 although primary adhesion of bacteria to inflamed or damaged valves followed by coagulation activation occurs as well,20 both mechanisms resulting in an “infected clot” (ie, vegetation). There is an increased perivalvular extension universally reported in BAV-IE4,6, 7, 8,22 despite a predominance of streptococci (low-virulence microorganism).23 Notably, we report all other major IE-related complications also increased in BAV-IE: larger vegetation size likely leading to more septic embolisms, higher heart failure rates likely related to the presence of significant aortic regurgitation in BAV patients as compared to TAV (Tables 1 and 2), and consequently, increased need for surgical intervention. These findings reflect a more advanced IE stage in BAV patients despite lower-virulence microorganism (ie, streptococci) predominance. For the first time, our study provides a probable primary explanation for these observations; we have demonstrated a significant delay in time from symptoms-to-diagnosis in BAV-IE. In particular, BAV-IE patients with streptococcal infections, with BAV status and time-delay in weeks being independent predictors of IE-MACE (Table 4), and a linear 23% increase in odds of IE-MACE per week of delay from symptoms-to-diagnosis (Figure 2, Central Illustration).

Important prior observations are consistent with our study: A French population-based epidemiologic survey of 486 definite-IE patients, showed Staphylococcus aureus to be a predictor of early diagnosis of IE, and a predominance of streptococci in late IE diagnoses,24 consistent with our study. A study of 151 consecutive patients with definite-IE showed a delayed time to IE echocardiographic diagnosis strongly associated with severe valvular destruction (severe regurgitation, cardiac abscess, or fistula), in-patient embolism, and higher rates of valve surgery,25 consistent with our study.

We propose another possible explanation for the increase in perivalvular infection extension and other IE complications seen in BAV-IE, which may be related to the BAV anatomic phenotypes26 and their impact on blood flow within the root and the ascending aorta:9,26 Right–left cusp fusion BAV is associated with higher flow related shear stress within the root which could accelerate root abscess formation within this particular phenotype. Non-right–left cusp fusion BAV preferentially directs blood flow toward the distal aorta, proximal to supra-aortic vessels, which could explain excess brain embolism in this subgroup.9

WHY do patients with BAV-IE incur delayed time from symptom onset to diagnosis?

We hypothesize this time delay is multifactorial in nature: 1) lack of patient and physician suspicion of serious infection, given the young age and low comorbidity-status of BAV patients; as well as the patients’ oligosymptomatic clinical presentation. Indeed, the historical term “subacute bacterial endocarditis” classically refers to viridans group streptococci and can present with nonspecific symptoms such as low-grade fever, malaise, and weight loss over weeks to months, contrasting with Staphylococcus aureus IE, which presents more acutely with high fever, rapid onset of symptoms, and higher likelihood of complications including sepsis.27,28 Moreover, patients with the viridans group streptococci may present without fever, so called “euthermic endocarditis”;29 2) lack of patient and physician education on the significantly higher risk of IE in BAV patients as compared to the general population,4,5 and the not rare incidence of IE in the BAV patient’s lifetime,3 for example, a BAV diagnosis was already known in almost half of the patients (44%) in our study, yet diagnosis was still delayed; 3) part of the physician knowledge-gap may be attributable to the lack of medical society consideration of BAV as a high-risk condition;11,12,30,31 and 4) a gap in patient education may exist whereby BAV patients may be unaware of the signs and symptoms of IE and/or have not received instruction on the importance of proper prevention measures (careful dental health and timely care of infectious symptoms).

Remarkably, the symptoms-to-diagnosis delay persisted even after matching for age in our study, such that the streptococcal-associated paucity of symptoms and additional unknown factors are highly-likely contributing to diagnostic delay, and younger age is a lesser contributor.

Streptococcus pyogenes IE has an acute symptom onset (median time from symptoms-to-diagnosis of 6 days) and is complicated by large vegetations with a high rate of systemic embolism.32 However, only 1/52 BAV-IE patient presented with this streptococcal group, Table 3, the vast majority were Streptococcus mitis.33

IE-MACE and mortality in BAV-IE

Within our IE-related MACE (perivalvular infection extension, septic embolism, heart failure, and cardiovascular surgery for IE), besides including life-threatening events, we also included an event that represents a burden for the health care system and a morbidity burden for the patient, yet it is universally protective of death in IE: surgery.34,35 As expected, surgery was strongly associated with improved in-hospital survival (Table 5) and this may be the reason why time from symptoms-to-diagnosis (which leads to higher surgery rates) appears to be paradoxically protective in 1 of the multivariable models for in-hospital mortality (Table 5). Another possibility for this paradoxical finding is that of sample bias whereby diagnosis-delayed BAV-IE cases die before hospital admission and therefore remain unaccounted for in-hospital mortality, or that the most severe IE cases who are most likely to experience early in-hospital mortality are also diagnosed earlier. Indeed, patients with acutely diagnosed IE due to staphylococcus infection are less likely to undergo surgery36 due to either earlier initiated medical therapy preventing surgical indications37 or a decided conservative approach given the higher comorbidity burden in patients with health care-associated infections23 (ie, hemodialysis patients) or high surgical risk.38

Younger age, significantly less comorbidities, lower virulence microorganisms, and higher rates of surgery should have provided an in-hospital survival advantage for BAV-IE patients, yet in-hospital mortality was not different from TAV-IE patients. Notably, both BAV-status and IE-MACE were associated with in-hospital mortality in multivariable analysis, Table 5. Therefore, the similar BAV-TAV in-hospital mortality is a reason for significant concern for these young BAV patients, as they should have an in-hospital survival advantage, which we would have expected in our cohort. Indeed, in a recent large Danish registry, patients with congenital heart disease and IE (median age 39 years)39 had significantly lower in-hospital mortality (6%) than older IE patients (median age 71) without congenital heart disease (17% in-hospital mortality38,39, similar to our BAV-IE patients) (Table 2). Fortunately, the benefits of youth, less comorbidities, and higher rates of surgery are likely reflected in the 4-year BAV-IE superior unadjusted survival (real-life survival) (Figure 3A), which we hypothesize could have prevailed after age and sex adjustment and matching, but instead was attenuated (Figures 3B and 4).

Clinical implications

IE affects BAV patients 17 to 23 times more often than the general population,4,5 with the longest and largest population-based follow-up of BAV patients showing a lifetime IE incidence of 6%, 4 times higher than aortic dissection.3 Furthermore, compared to the pooled incidence of IE in a systematic review involving 3,738 adult congenital heart disease patients including BAV (1.3 per 1,000 patient-years, age range 30-45 years),40 the population-based incidence of BAV-IE is similar at 1.0 to 1.4 per 1,000 patient-years,5 median age 40 years. The universally-reported higher rate of streptococcal infection in BAV-IE is likely related to the increased suspected dental source of infection as demonstrated in our study and supported by previous studies.7,9,22,33 Our study confirms the universal finding of a higher rate of perivalvular infection in BAV-IE, and demonstrates a significant time-delay to diagnosis in BAV patients which results higher rates of life-threatening IE-related complications as well as surgery, with both IE-related complications and BAV status being associated with in-hospital mortality. These findings suggest BAV patients are high-risk IE subsets41 and begs the question of whether these patients should be reconsidered for antibiotic prophylaxis before dental procedures.41,42 There is an ongoing debate about the use of antibiotic prophylaxis before invasive dental procedures, mostly due to a lack of randomized controlled data. Current American and European guidelines11,12,30 recommend antibiotic prophylaxis before invasive dental procedure for patients only at the highest risk. This does not include patients with BAV, who are assumed to be moderate risk. At a minimum, the findings of our study call for increased education about dental hygiene and higher rates of clinical awareness for recognition of IE symptoms in patients with BAV and their physicians alike, especially as each week in diagnosis delay increases the risk of IE-related complications. Importantly, the latest European guidelines state that antibiotic prophylaxis can be considered on an individual basis for patients at intermediate risk.30 The findings of the current study suggest BAV patients could belong to a higher-risk IE category, in whom dental antibiotic prophylaxis could be necessary. For now, careful dental hygiene should be actively recommended.

Study Limitations

This is a multicenter retrospective study of consecutive patients. Referral bias is possible but rather inevitable because IE is most commonly treated in tertiary referral centers. Patient reported IE-related symptoms are susceptible to recall bias. Symptom-onset may not be fully accurate as symptoms of IE are nonspecific and may have been unrelated to IE in some cases. Although the number of BAV-IE patients in the current study is modest (yet similar to most prior existing studies), the differences detected in this study are clinically and statistically robust. Nevertheless, the limited sample size and retrospective design of our study call for verification of these data through independent prospective multicenter studies.

Conclusions

As compared to TAV-IE, patients with BAV-IE exhibit higher prevalence of streptococcal infections and higher suspected dental source. BAV-IE patients incur significantly delayed symptoms-to-diagnosis time resulting in high IE complication burden despite predominance of less virulent causative microorganisms. These findings call for enhanced IE clinical suspicion in BAV patients and suggest antibiotic prophylaxis may be indicated in these patients.

KEY FINDINGS: As compared to patients with TAV-IE, those with BAV IE incurred a delayed symptom-to-diagnosis time resulting in high IE complication burden, despite a predominance of less virulent streptococci in BAV patients, with higher suspicion of dental source.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS: These findings call for BAV patient education on dental hygiene, and enhanced awareness of IE symptoms in BAV patients and their physicians. These findings suggest BAV patients may belong to a high-risk IE stratum in whom dental antibiotic prophylaxis could be considered.

TRANSLATION OUTLOOK: Although differences detected in this study are clinically and statistically robust, the limited sample size, and retrospective design of our study call for verification of this data through independent, prospective, multicenter studies.
