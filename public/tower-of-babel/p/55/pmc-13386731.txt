The Effect of Nurse Practitioner–Led Care on Health-Related Quality of Life in Adult Patients with Atrial Fibrillation—A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Marcie J. Smigorowsky, Ross T. Tsuyuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is associated with significant morbidity, mortality, and healthcare resource utilization. Health system limitations can result in delays to specialty cardiovascular (CV) care. Nurse practitioner (NP)-led care may improve access and quality of care. The purpose of this study was to assess the effect of NP-led care compared to usual cardiologist care on quality of life in patients with AF.MethodsPatients with AF referred to a specialized cardiac clinic were randomized to NP-led or usual cardiologist care for 6 months. Primary outcome was the difference in change in atrial fibrillation effect on quality of life (AFEQT) scores at 6 months between groups. Secondary outcomes were difference in change of EuroQOL EQ-5D-3L scores at 6 months between groups, composite outcome of death from CV causes, hospitalizations, emergency department visits, and patient satisfaction measured by the Consultant Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ) at 6 months.ResultsWe enrolled 150 subjects, with 10% loss to follow-up. Study subjects were 62% male, with a mean age of 64 years, and an overall AFEQT baseline score of 66.45 ±4:86. No significant baseline differences between groups were present. AFEQT scores, EQ-5D-3L, and CV outcomes were not different at 6 months; NP-led care showed higher patient satisfaction in the CSQ domain of professional care (76.32 ± 11.32 vs 65.75 ± 15.45, P = 0.0006).ConclusionsWe found no difference in quality of life or CV outcomes at 6 months with NP-led care vs usual cardiologist care. NP-led care resulted in higher levels of patient satisfaction. Further research is needed to explore the effect of NP-led AF care in CV settings.Clinical Trial RegistrationNCT02745236.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia and is increasing in both incidence and prevalence worldwide.1,2 In Canada, the estimated prevalence is 500,000 to 1 million individuals, with the risk of developing AF increasing significantly with age—from less than 1% in those under age 50 years to 12% in those aged 80 years and older.2,3 With an aging population, the number of patients with AF is projected to increase substantially over the next decade.4 Although AF is not typically acutely life-threatening, it is associated with devastating complications, including stroke and heart failure, and poorly controlled AF is estimated to cost the Canadian healthcare system $956 million annually in hospital visits.2,5 AF has no cure; treatment aims to decrease symptoms, reduce the risk of complications, and improve health-related quality of life (HRQOL).6

Given the increasing demand, novel models of care delivery are required. Clinical guidelines, including the 2020 Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS)/Canadian Heart Rhythm Society (CHRS) AF guidelines, recommend the utilization of multidisciplinary teams,2,7 and recent criteria for AF centres of excellence encourage the involvement of advanced practice providers, including nurse practitioners (NPs).8,9 In Canada, NPs have advanced graduate-level training, enabling them to independently diagnose, order diagnostic testing, prescribe medications, and provide treatment.10,11 They function as independent healthcare providers, including serving as the “most responsible provider,” defined as the regulated healthcare professional with overall responsibility for directing and coordinating a patient’s care.12

Making a distinction between NP-led care vs nurse-led care is essential. Nurses, although integral members of healthcare teams, generally require physician-directed protocols and oversight when managing patients with AF. In contrast, NPs practice independently with a scope more similar to that of physicians, including the authority to diagnose, prescribe, manage, and refer patients.11 This distinction is important, as randomized trials have evaluated nurse-led AF care,13, 14, 15 but this represents a fundamentally different model from NP-led care. In the landmark nurse-led trial by Hendriks et al.,13 nurses followed AF-specific decision support software, with cardiologists reviewing every patient. Although that trial demonstrated a reduction in cardiovascular hospitalization and death, the care model was physician-dependent, rather than independent.

In comparisons of NP-led care with physician-led care in other clinical populations, patients generally achieve similar health outcomes and report higher levels of satisfaction with NP-led care.16, 17, 18, 19, 20 A systematic review of 13 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing NP-led to physician-led care found either positive or equivalent patient outcomes, with the majority of studies reporting improved patient satisfaction associated with NP-led care.20 Patient satisfaction is a key component of quality healthcare, as it has been shown to be associated with improved healthcare utilization and health outcomes.21,22

To our knowledge, no RCTs of NP-led care in AF have been conducted. The purpose of this study was to assess the effect of NP-led care, compared to usual general cardiologist care, on HRQOL in patients with AF.

Methods

Study design and setting

The full study protocol has been published previously.23 Briefly, this study was a prospective RCT comparing NP-led care (NP as “most responsible provider”) to usual care (general cardiologist-led) in adult patients with AF referred to a specialized cardiac clinic designed to improve health system outcomes24, 25, 26 at a large tertiary-care hospital (Mazankowski Alberta Heart Institute) in Edmonton, Alberta, Canada. The study enrolled patients from July 2016 to January 2020.

The study was registered with ClincalTrials.gov, identifier NCT02745236, and ethics approval was obtained from the University of Alberta Research Ethics Office (reference number Pro00055990).

Study population

All adult patients referred to the cardiac access clinic for assessment for AF were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria comprised patients aged 18 years or older with documented nonvalvular AF who were able to provide informed consent and were willing and able to complete study questionnaires. Patients were excluded if the reason for referral required electrophysiologist involvement (atrioventricular node ablation or AF ablation; patients who had failed rate control or antiarrhythmic medications; patients with moderate to severe mitral or aortic valvular heart disease; patients with unstable AF) and if they were unable or unwilling to attend follow-up appointments. Of note, our study was conducted before the release of the 2020 CCS guidelines,2 which recommended earlier consideration of AF ablation.

Referrals were triaged by nurses (as part of their regular clinic role) who reviewed the referral documentation, the provincial electronic health record (Alberta Netcare, which includes specialty care encounters, emergency room visits, diagnostic testing, laboratory results, and medication dispensing records), and the hospital’s cardiology-specific electronic system. If the referral indicated that the patient needed an electrophysiologist’s involvement, the patient was triaged to an electrophysiologist and excluded from the study. In cases of uncertainty, the nurses contacted the referring provider’s office or the patient for clarification.

Randomization

Following their provision of verbal consent obtained during the telephone triage call, patients were randomized by a nurse using a secure Web site. Blocked randomization (using variable block sizes) was used to ensure equal allocation and to conceal allocation. Clinic visits were booked 4-6 weeks from the date of referral, in accordance with CCS guidelines.2,27

Intervention and control groups

The intervention group received NP-led care from a single experienced cardiovascular NP with additional training and over 10 years of experience in AF management. At the initial visit, a complete history and physical examination were performed, and a care plan was developed based on CCS AF Guideline recommendations.28,29 Congestive Heart Failure, Hypertension, Age 65 years, Diabetes, Stroke/Transient Ischemic Attack (CHADS-65) and/or , Congestive Heart Failure, Hypertension, Age ≥ 75 Years or Age 65-74, Diabetes Mellitus, Stroke, Vascular Disease, Sex Category (CHA2DS2-VASc)2,30 scores were calculated to identify stroke risk. CCS severity of atrial fibrillation (SAF) scores31 were completed to assess symptom severity. The NP provided individualized patient education regarding AF management and complications. Follow-up was scheduled at 3 and 6 months, although timing was adjusted for individual patient needs (earlier if clinically indicated, deferred if not required). If patients developed heart failure, or medication intolerances limiting medical management, required treatment with amiodarone, required AF ablation, or developed other serious complications, a cardiologist or electrophysiologist was consulted. The NP remained the “most responsible provider” and was accountable for ongoing care, including ensuring that further testing, referrals, and follow-up were completed. If patients required ongoing care, the NP continued to manage their care in the AF clinic, or they were referred back to their family care provider if no further follow-up was required.

The NP-led care model was based on 10 years of experience at the AF clinic within the institution. The timing of study clinic visit schedules was designed to reflect the practice patterns of the AF clinic and the Hendriks et al. study.13

The control group received usual care from general cardiologists in the cardiac access clinic. Approximately 13 cardiologists rotated through the clinic during the study period. Cardiologists determined AF management and follow-up according to their usual practice patterns. No protocols were imposed on the cardiologist group. The patient’s care was referred back to the family care provider if no follow-up was required.

Data Collection

Data collected at baseline and follow-up visits included sociodemographic characteristics, physical findings, CHADS-65, CHA2DS2-VASc, CCS AF score, type of AF, symptoms, clinical comorbidities, echocardiogram findings, clinical interventions, testing ordered, and referrals made. Data were abstracted from the electronic chart and Alberta Netcare and entered into REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture). The electronic records were accessed retrospectively from the date of enrollment until November 30, 2020 to determine cardiovascular outcomes, which were adjudicated by a blinded assessor.

Baseline questionnaires (Atrial Fibrillation Effect on Quality of Life [AFEQT]32 and EuroQOL EQ-5D-3L [EQ-5D-3L])33 were collected in person at the time of written consent, prior to the initial clinic visit. Follow-up questionnaires were collected at 3 months (AFEQT and EQ-5D-3L) and 6 months (AFEQT, EQ-5D-3L, and Consultant Satisfaction Questionnaire [CSQ]),34) either by mail or online via REDCap. Nonresponders received either 2 reminder telephone calls or 2 reminder e-mails.

Outcome measures

The AFEQT is an AF-specific 20-question questionnaire based on a 7-point Likert scale covering 3 domains.32 Scores range from 0 (complete disability/no satisfaction) to 100 (no disability/complete satisfaction). The EQ-5D-3L is a validated 5-question general quality-of-life (QOL) questionnaire assessing mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression.33 Canadian value sets (time-tradeoff) were used to convert health states to an index score. EQ-5D-3L Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores records overall health. The CSQ measures consultation satisfaction with the healthcare provider using 18 questions on a 5-point Likert scale.34 Scores range from 0 (greatest dissatisfaction) to 100 (greatest satisfaction).

The primary outcome was the difference in change in overall AFEQT score from baseline to 6 months between NP-led care and usual care. Secondary outcomes included the following: (i) difference in change in EQ-5D-3L VAS scores from baseline to 6 months; (ii) composite cardiovascular outcomes (death from cardiovascular causes, cardiovascular hospitalization, and emergency room visits for ischemic stroke, heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, systemic embolism, major bleeding, severe arrhythmic events, and life-threatening adverse drug reactions); and (iii) patient satisfaction as measured by the CSQ.

Sample size

The original sample size of n = 64 per group was calculated to detect a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 12 in overall AFEQT scores (standard deviation [SD] 20; 2-tailed Student t test; 80% power; α = 5%).23 Allowing for 10% attrition, 70 participants per group were planned. An unplanned interim analysis was completed in February 2019 due to slower-than-expected recruitment and academic requirements for a PhD completion. After adjusting α to 2%, the revised sample size was 68 per group, with allowance for 14 patients lost to follow-up, resulting in a final target of 75 per group.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using means ± SD for continuous variables, and counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Analysis of covariance was used to assess change in overall AFEQT scores over time. Independent Student t tests were used to assess between-group differences. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed for the composite cardiovascular endpoint. Multivariate analysis was used to adjust for possible differences in baseline characteristics. Consultation satisfaction was compared using independent t tests. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed by intention-to-treat. All analyses were performed with SPSS version 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY).

Results

Baseline data

A total of 150 patients were enrolled between July 23, 2016 and January 21, 2020, with 70 analyzed per group after 5 in each group were lost to follow-up or withdrew (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics and comorbidities were similar between groups, with no statistically significant differences (Table 1). Rate control medications were used at baseline in 88% (NP-led) and 87% (usual care) of patients. Follow-up visits

More patients in the NP-led care group attended follow-up clinic visits. At 6 months, 55 of 70 NP-led care patients (79%) completed follow-up, compared with 15 of 70 (21%) in the usual care group. Follow-up visits for both groups after March 2020 occurred virtually, due to COVID-19 pandemic requirements. The patient flow diagram is presented in Figure 1.

Discussion

In this first randomized trial of NP-led care compared to usual general cardiologist care in patients referred for AF, we found no statistically significant differences in AF-related QOL, general QOL, or cardiovascular outcomes at 6 months (Table 2). We did, however, find that patients receiving NP-led care reported significantly higher levels of satisfaction, particularly in the Professional Care domain of the CSQ. This finding is consistent with the broader literature showing that NP-led care is associated with higher levels of patient satisfaction across clinical settings and is often associated with their patient-centred approach.16,17,20, 21, 22,35

Contextualizing these findings within the existing literature on nurse-led AF care is important. Several studies have evaluated nurse-led AF management models.13, 14, 15 In the largest of these, Hendriks et al.13 demonstrated that an integrated nurse-led approach, supported by AF-specific decision support software with mandatory cardiologist review, reduced the composite of cardiovascular hospitalization and death, compared to usual care. Although these findings are important, nurse-led care is fundamentally different from NP-led care. In nurse-led models, care is physician-directed through protocols and algorithms, and the physician retains ultimate clinical responsibility. In contrast, NP-led care, as studied here, involves the NP functioning as the “most responsible provider”—independently performing assessments, establishing diagnoses, initiating and modifying treatment, and coordinating ongoing care, physician consultation is reserved for cases of greater clinical complexity. This distinction is critical for healthcare systems seeking to address workforce shortages and access challenges, as NP-led models offer greater capacity for independent practice without requiring physician involvement in every patient encounter.

Another important point to note is that the NP role varies across jurisdictions. In some US states, NPs work under physician supervision, whereas in others, full independent practice is permitted.36 In Canada, NPs have a legislated independent scope of practice that includes prescriptive authority.11 When evaluating NP-led care studies, readers should consider how the NP role functioned, the degree of independence, and the regulatory context.

Patient satisfaction is recognized as a key component of quality healthcare and has been associated with improved healthcare utilization and health outcomes.21 The finding that NP-led care was associated with significantly higher patient satisfaction in the professional care domain of the CSQ (P = 0.0006) is noteworthy and warrants further investigation. Professional care, as measured by the CSQ, captures the patient’s perception of the quality of the clinical interaction—including the provider’s thoroughness, competence, and patient-centredness.34 Nevertheless, this finding must be considered a hypothesis-generating result for several reasons. First, the CSQ was a secondary outcome in a study powered for a different primary endpoint (AFEQT). Second, the differential follow-up rates between groups (53 NP-led vs 39 usual-care respondents) introduce the possibility of selection bias in the satisfaction results. Third, the CSQ was designed for primary care consultations34 and has not been validated specifically for specialist AF care settings or for comparisons between different provider types (NP vs physician). Future research could prioritize patient satisfaction with professional care as a primary or co-primary outcome in studies of NP-led AF care.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its pragmatic design, conducted within the real-world setting of a tertiary cardiac centre with an established NP-led care model. To our knowledge, this RCT is the first to evaluate NP-led care, with the NP functioning as the “most responsible provider,” compared to general cardiologist care in AF. The high rate of clinic visit attendance and questionnaire completion in the NP-led care group is also a strength.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only one NP participated in the study, whereas 13 cardiologists provided care in the control group. Consequently, the differences observed may partly reflect individual practice style rather than the model of care. Future studies should include multiple NPs to enhance generalizability. Second, recruitment was slower than expected, and an unplanned interim analysis was required due to academic timelines associated with a PhD thesis. We recalculated the sample size accounting for this interim analysis and continued recruitment to ensure adequate enrollment.

Third, the study was powered to detect an MCID of 12 points in the overall AFEQT score, which was the best available estimate at the time the study was designed. At the time, AFEQT was a newly validated instrument, and a well-defined MCID had not yet been established. In 2019, after our study was launched, Holmes et al.37 suggested that a change of ± 5 points represents a clinically meaningful difference. Using this updated MCID, a sample size of approximately 245 patients per group would be necessary to reliably detect the true effect. Given that our study enrolled 70 per group, it was likely underpowered to detect more modest but clinically important differences. Future research utilizing the current MCID estimate, with a correspondingly larger sample size, would more likely reflect the true effect of NP-led care on QOL in patients with AF.

Fourth, the exclusion of patients referred for AF ablation or who had failed rate control or antiarrhythmic therapy restricts the study population to less-complex patients. This design decision was made because, at our institution, such patients would be triaged to an electrophysiologist rather than a general cardiologist, and the NP-led model was designed for independent management without direct electrophysiologist involvement. Additionally, the study was designed and conducted prior to publication of the 2020 CCS AF Guidelines, which recommended earlier consideration of AF ablation. This limits the generalizability of our findings to the broader AF population and should be considered when interpreting results.

Fifth, the differential follow-up rate between groups is an important limitation. In the usual-care group, follow-up was dictated by the cardiologist’s usual practice; we deliberately did not impose study-mandated visits to preserve the pragmatic design. The COVID-19 pandemic also disrupted follow-up, particularly for the last patients enrolled (January 2020, with follow-up extending through August 2020). The lower response rate in the usual-care group raises the possibility of a “healthy responder” bias, whereby patients who did not seek or receive follow-up may have had different QOL outcomes. A recent scoping review found that response shift can occur in RCTs and may compromise trial results; if the trial is powered to identify response shift, it will often identify more substantial benefits than those that were originally found if underpowered.38

Sixth, we did not collect data on visit duration or extensive measures of resource utilization, which limits our ability to assess the economic impact of the NP-led model. Finally, the NP in this study had extensive cardiology experience and mentoring; NPs without similar training may require additional mentoring to provide comparable care, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.10,39

Future directions

Although we did not demonstrate superiority of NP-led care on HRQOL, this does not mean that such care is equivalent to that of a general cardiologist. Nevertheless, given the pressures on our healthcare system, and a signal of potentially greater patient satisfaction, further studies are warranted. These could take the form of a larger, multicentre trial with cardiovascular outcomes, perhaps with an equivalence design, or a focus on patient satisfaction. In the current healthcare landscape, characterized by physician shortages, increasing wait times, and rising patient volumes, NP-led AF care warrants serious consideration as a sustainable model of care delivery.

Conclusion

In this first randomized trial of NP-led care for patients with AF, with the NP functioning as the most responsible provider, HRQOL and cardiovascular outcomes were not statistically different from usual cardiologist care at 6 months. NP-led care did, however, demonstrate a significantly higher level of patient satisfaction with professional care. Given the increasing demands on the healthcare system and the need for sustainable care delivery models, NP-led AF care merits further investigation in adequately powered trials.
