6-Shogaol Complements Levodopa Therapy by Modulating Brain–Gut Metabolomes in a Parkinson’s Disease Model
Authors: Jin Hee Kim, Seungmin Lee, Yujin Choi, Choong Hwan Lee, Myung Sook Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Levodopa remains the primary therapy for Parkinson’s disease (PD); however, its benefits are largely confined to motor symptom relief, without altering disease progression. Growing evidence implicates the gut–brain axis in PD, linking gastrointestinal dysfunction and microbial shifts to neuronal cell death. Gut–brain metabolomics is critical for clarifying these interactions and guiding adjunct strategies to enhance intestinal motility and neuroprotection beyond levodopa. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize gut and brain metabolomic alterations induced by levodopa and to evaluate whether co-administration with 6-shogaol, previously identified as a gut–brain axis modulator, modifies these changes in relation to motor and gastrointestinal functions. Male C57BL/6 mice were assigned to normal, 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)/probenecid, levodopa, or 6-shogaol+levodopa groups. PD was induced by repeated MPTP/probenecid injections, and 6-shogaol was administered orally with levodopa. Motor performance, gut motility, dopamine levels in the brain, and metabolomic profiles were subsequently assessed. Levodopa improved motor deficits in the MPTP/probenecid model, while 6-shogaol co-administration preserved these benefits and additionally restored gastrointestinal motility, a non-motor feature unresponsive to levodopa alone. The combination further increased striatal dopamine and levodopa levels, suggesting enhanced dopaminergic availability. Metabolomic profiling revealed distinct brain–gut signatures, including alterations in energy, lipid, and amino acid pathways, with specific metabolites linked to motor function, dopamine regulation, and gut activity. These findings suggest that 6-shogaol complements levodopa by modulating brain–gut metabolomic networks to improve motor and non-motor features of PD.

INTRODUCTION

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder primarily characterized by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, leading to motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremors (Bloem et al., 2021; Morris et al., 2024). However, increasing evidence indicates that PD is not confined to the central nervous system, but represents a disorder involving diverse non-motor features, including gastrointestinal dysfunction (Kim et al., 2025a; Tan et al., 2022). Alterations in gut physiology often precede motor symptoms, and pathological proteins such as α-synuclein have been reported to propagate from the gut to the brain via the vagus nerve (Chen and Mor, 2023; Kim et al., 2019). These findings support the concept that the gut–brain axis plays a pivotal role in PD pathogenesis, making it a critical target for therapeutic intervention.

Levodopa (LD) remains the gold standard for alleviating motor symptoms in PD, primarily because of its robust and immediate efficacy in restoring motor function (Riederer et al., 2025; Salat and Tolosa, 2013). However, its long-term use is accompanied by considerable limitations, including motor fluctuations, dyskinesia, and insufficient efficacy against non-motor symptoms such as gastrointestinal dysfunction (Manson et al., 2012; Tran et al., 2018). Given that LD is unlikely to be replaced in clinical practice, current research has shifted toward identifying adjunctive strategies that can complement its therapeutic benefits and overcome these shortcomings, particularly by extending its scope to non-motor domains mediated through the gut–brain axis (McFarthing et al., 2024).

6-Shogaol (6S), a bioactive compound derived from Zingiber officinale (ginger), has attracted attention owing to its broad pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective effects (Eo et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2025b). Preclinical studies have suggested that 6S modulates neuroinflammatory pathways and protects dopaminergic neurons, making it a promising candidate for PD therapy (Park et al., 2013). In support of this notion, our group previously reported that 6S alleviates gut inflammation and reduces α-synuclein accumulation in a Proteus mirabilis-induced mouse model of PD, thereby demonstrating its capacity to target the gut–brain axis (Huh et al., 2020, 2023; Kim et al., 2024). Moreover, we showed that the combination of 6S with LD improved non-motor dysfunction and complemented the therapeutic effects of LD, suggesting that 6S holds strong potential as an adjunctive strategy for PD treatment. Nevertheless, the mechanistic basis by which 6S modifies the effects of LD, particularly through brain–gut metabolic alterations, remains to be clarified.

This study aimed to elucidate how 6S complements LD therapy by integrating untargeted metabolomic analyses of the brain and gut with functional assessments in a PD model. To this end, we used a 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine/probenecid (MPTP/p)-induced mouse model of PD. We first assessed behavioral outcomes, focusing on both motor function and gastrointestinal motility, along with changes in striatal dopamine and levodopa levels. Subsequently, we conducted metabolomic profiling, followed by pathway enrichment and correlation analyses, to establish links between metabolic signatures and functional outcomes. Through this integrative framework, we demonstrated that 6S complements LD therapy by reshaping brain–gut metabolomic networks, providing novel insights into adjunctive strategies for PD management.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

HPLC-grade solvents were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). MPTP, probenecid, formic acid, as well as all other reagents, were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St Louis, MO, USA).

Animals

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition, National Institutes of Health, 2011) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Kyung Hee University, Seoul, Korea (Approval No. KHSASP-22-416). A total of 20 male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were purchased from DBL Inc. (Eumseong, Korea). Animals were maintained in a controlled environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle, constant temperature (23 ± 1°C), and relative humidity (60 ± 10%); they were housed in standard cages with free access to food and water.

Mice were randomly allocated into four groups: (1) Normal (NOR) group: oral 10% DMSO with intraperitoneal (i.p.) saline injection (N=5); (2) MPTP/p group: oral 10% DMSO with i.p. MPTP/p injection (N=5); (3) LD group: oral LD with i.p. MPTP/p injection (N=5); (4) 6S+LD group: oral co-administration of 6S and LD with i.p. MPTP/p injection (N=5).

For the induction of PD, mice received nine injections of MPTP (25 mg/kg in saline) combined with probenecid (100 mg/kg in 5% NaHCO₃). Probenecid was administered 30 min before each MPTP injection, and the interval between injections was 3.5 days. From the third MPTP/p injection onward, 6S was orally administered at a dose of 20 mg/kg/day dissolved in 10% DMSO in saline and continued until the end of the experiment. The 6S used in this study was synthesized and provided by Professor Boyoung Y. Park (Kyung Hee University).

Behavioral tests

Pole test: Each mouse was placed in the head-up position on top of a vertical pole (8 mm in diameter, 55 cm in height, with a roughened surface). The time required to turn completely downward (T-turn) and the time required to descend to the base (T-LA) were measured. A maximum trial duration of 60 s was used as the cut-off.

Bead expulsion test: Gastrointestinal motility was evaluated using the bead expulsion test. Briefly, 3 days after the final MPTP/p injection, a 2.5-mm bead lubricated with saline was gently inserted 2 cm into the distal colon of each mouse. Following insertion, the animals were returned individually to their cages, and the time required for expulsion of the bead was recorded. A cut-off time of 60 min was used for each trial.

Preparation of tissues

Mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol (300 mg/kg, i.p.) one hour after drug administration, which was performed on day 5 after the last MPTP/p injection. The animals were decapitated, and the whole brain was collected. For subsequent analyses, the olfactory bulb and the posterior portion of the substantia nigra were removed, and the remaining mid-brain block was stored at –80°C until use.

Extraction of metabolites from mouse tissue

Mouse brain hemispheres and colon tissues were homogenized in 1 mL of 100% methanol containing an internal standard (2-chloro-phenylalanine). The samples were lysed using a mixer mill for 10 min, followed by sonication for 10 min. The homogenates were then incubated at −20°C for 60 min. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatants were collected. The supernatant was filtered and dried using a speed-vacuum concentrator. The dried extracts were reconstituted in 100% methanol to a final concentration of 10,000 ppm and subjected to instrumental analysis.

UPLC-triple Q-MS/MS (Ultrahigh Performance Liquid Chromatography-Triple Quadrupole-Tandem Mass Spectrometry) analysis

L-dopa and dopamine contents were quantified using a UPLC–triple quadrupole MS/MS system. Standards were dissolved by 100% methanol. The UPLC system was equipped with an LC-30AD pump (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan). Chromatographic separation was carried out on a BEH C18 column (100×2.1 mm, 1.7 μm particle size; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The injection volume was 10 μL, corresponding to a final concentration of 10,000 ppm. The column oven temperature was maintained at 40°C, with a constant flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water containing 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid). Gradient elution was applied as follows: 0-5 min, 0% B; 5-6 min, linear increase to 55% B; 6-20 min, linear increase to 65% B; 20-21 min, linear increase to 100% B; 21-22 min, 100% B; 22-23 min, return to 0% B; 23-24 min, 0% B for re-equilibration. The quantification of metabolites was performed using a LCMS-8040 (Shimadzu Corp.). Multiple reaction monitoring was established in the positive ion mode. L-dopa was quantified using a precursor ion at m/z 198.05 and a product ion at m/z 152.10. Dopamine was quantified with a precursor ion at m/z 154.10 and a product ion at m/z 137.10.

Ultrahigh Performance Liquid Chromatography–Linear Trap Quadrupole–Orbitrap–Mass Spectrometry (UPLC–LTQ–Orbitrap–MS/MS) analysis

Metabolomic profiling was performed using a UPLC–LTQ–Orbitrap–MS/MS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The UPLC system consisted of a Vanquish binary pump H system coupled to an RS autosampler. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Kinetex® C18 column (100×2.1 mm, 1.7 μm particle size; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA). The injection volume was 5 μL, corresponding to a final concentration of 10,000 ppm. The column oven temperature was maintained at 40°C, with a constant flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of solvents A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). Gradient elution was performed as follows: 0-1 min, 5% B; 1-10 min, linear decrease to 100% B; 10-11 min, 100% B; 11-13 min, return to 5% B; and 13-14 min, 5% B for re-equilibration. Mass spectra were acquired over an m/z range of 100-1000 for brain samples and 100-1500 for colon samples in both positive and negative ionization modes. Detection was performed on an Orbitrap Velos Pro™ mass spectrometer equipped with an ion-trap analyzer and a HESI-II probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Processing of metabolomic data and multivariate analyses

Raw UPLC–LTQ–Orbitrap–MS/MS data were acquired and converted into CDF files (*.cdf) using Xcalibur software (v2.00; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The converted files were processed using MetAlign (RIKILT Institute of Food Safety, Wageningen, Netherlands) for peak alignment, retention time correction, and intensity normalization. SIMCA-P+ software (v12.0; Umetrics, Umea, Sweden) was used to perform principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA). The robustness of the PLS-DA model was assessed using cross-validated analysis of variance (ANOVA). Differential metabolites were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP>0.7) and statistical significance (p<0.05), as determined by one-way ANOVA, followed by Duncan’s multiple-range test (PASW Statistics 18; SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Candidate metabolites were subsequently annotated by comparing retention times and mass spectral features with reference resources, such as the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, http://www.hmdb.ca/), as well as an in-house library of authentic standards and published references.

Statistical analysis

Differences among the groups were analyzed statistically by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test or student’s t test using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 software (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA). All values are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The differences were considered statistically significant at p<0.05 and are expressed in each figure.

RESULTS

Co-administration of 6S and LD improves motor symptoms, gastrointestinal motility, and modulates dopamine and LD levels in MPTP/p-injected mice

In the pole test, MPTP/p-treated mice showed significant motor impairments compared to the normal group. Administration of LD ameliorated these deficits, and co-treatment with 6S did not further alter the LD-induced motor improvement (Fig. 1A, 1B). In contrast, gastrointestinal motility, assessed using the bead expulsion test, showed a distinct pattern. LD treatment alone failed to improve delayed gastrointestinal transit in MPTP/p-injected mice, whereas co-administration of LD and 6S significantly reduced expulsion time (Fig. 1C).

Neurochemical analysis of half-brain samples revealed a significant reduction in dopamine levels in the MPTP/p group compared to the normal group. LD administration tended to increase dopamine levels relative to those in the MPTP/p group, whereas the co-administration of LD and 6S resulted in a significant elevation. In contrast, endogenous LD was not detected in either the normal or MPTP/p groups. As expected, LD was present in LD-treated mice and its levels were further increased by co-administration with 6S (Fig. 1D, 1E).

Non-targeted metabolomic profiling and pathway analysis in brains of mice co-administered 6-shogaol and LD

To evaluate the effects of the co-administration of 6-shogaol and LD on endogenous brain metabolites in MPTP/p-injected mice, non-targeted metabolite profiling was conducted using UPLC–LTQ–Orbitrap–MS/MS. Multivariate analyses using unsupervised PCA and supervised PLS-DA were performed to identify discriminant metabolites (Fig. 2A). The PCA and PLS-DA score plots of brain metabolites showed a clear separation between the LD-only group and the group that received 6-shogaol and LD. In total, 28 metabolites were identified by non-targeted profiling, and their clustering patterns, along with the overall dataset, were visualized using hierarchical heatmaps (Fig. 2B).

Furthermore, alterations in brain metabolites were analyzed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 for metabolic enrichment and pathway analysis. Enrichment analysis revealed that the brain metabolites were predominantly associated with alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism (Fig. 2C). Pathway analysis consistently identified alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism as the most significantly correlated pathways in the brain (Fig. 2D).

Identification of brain metabolites associated with motor dysfunction and dopamine levels in the half-brain following 6-shogaol and LD co-administration

To identify the metabolites associated with the therapeutic effects of co-administered LD and 6S, correlation analyses were performed between altered brain metabolites, motor performance, and dopamine levels in the half-brain. As shown in Fig. 3A, several metabolites were significantly associated with behavioral and neurochemical outcomes.

Succinic acid, arachidonic acid, docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), LysoPS(22:6), LysoPI(20:4), and LysoPC(22:4) were significantly correlated with pole test parameters (T-LA) (Fig. 3B-3G). These findings suggest that alterations in energy metabolism, lipid mediators, and lysophospholipid pathways are closely related to motor improvements in response to LD and 6S treatment.

Regarding dopaminergic neurochemistry, pyroglutamic acid, N-acetyl-L-aspartic acid (NAA), prostaglandin F2α, LysoPS(18:1), LysoPI(18:1), LysoPC(16:1), and PG(22:6) were significantly correlated with striatal dopamine levels (Fig. 3H-3N). These metabolites represent diverse pathways, including glutamate metabolism, neuronal integrity markers, eicosanoid signaling, and membrane lipid remodeling, indicating that the co-administration of LD and 6S influences dopaminergic function through multiple metabolic processes.

To further characterize the metabolites identified as significantly correlated with motor performance or striatal dopamine levels, a fold-change analysis was performed (Fig. 4). Most of the selected metabolites showed significant increases in the LD+6S group compared to those in the MPTP/p group. In contrast, pyroglutamic acid, NAA, and PG(22:6) levels were significantly lower than those in the MPTP/p group. Notably, LysoPC(16:1) did not exhibit any significant changes among the experimental groups. These results confirm that the co-administration of LD and 6S modulates distinct subsets of brain metabolites in a bidirectional manner, with some metabolites being upregulated and others downregulated.

Non-targeted metabolomic profiling and pathway analysis in colons of mice co-administered 6-shogaol and LD

To evaluate the effects of the co-administration of 6S and LD on endogenous colon metabolites in MPTP/p-injected mice, non-targeted metabolite profiling was conducted using UPLC–LTQ–Orbitrap–MS/MS. Multivariate analyses using unsupervised PCA and supervised PLS-DA were performed to identify discriminant metabolites (Fig. 5A). The PLS-DA score plot of the colon metabolites showed a clear separation between the levodopa-only and levodopa-co-administered 6S and LD groups. A total of 42 metabolites were identified by non-targeted profiling, and their clustering patterns, along with the overall dataset, were visualized using hierarchical heatmaps (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, alterations in colon metabolites were analyzed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0. Both enrichment and pathway analyses consistently indicated that biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids was the most significantly associated pathway (Fig. 5C, 5D).

Identification of gut metabolites associated with gut motility following co-administration of 6-shogaol and LD

Gut motility was evaluated using the bead expulsion test and correlation analyses were performed between gastrointestinal function and altered gut metabolites. As shown in Fig. 6, several metabolites were significantly associated with expulsion time. In particular, phenylalanine, threonic acid, inosine, DHA, multiple lysophospholipids [LysoPC(18:2), LysoPC(20:4), LysoPC(16:0), LysoPC(18:1), LysoPC(18:0), LysoPE(18:2), and LysoPE(18:0)], and a lipid derivative (Lipid derivative 1) were significantly correlated with bead expulsion time.

To further characterize the gut metabolites that were significantly correlated with gastrointestinal motility, fold-change analyses were performed (Fig. 7). Among these metabolites, phenylalanine and DHA levels were significantly higher in the LD+6S group than in the MPTP/p group. In contrast, the remaining metabolites, including threonic acid, inosine, multiple lysophospholipids [LysoPC(18:2), LysoPC(20:4), LysoPC(16:0), LysoPC(18:1), LysoPC(18:0), LysoPE(18:2), and LysoPE(18:0)], and lipid derivative 1, were significantly decreased.

DISCUSSION

Our previous studies demonstrated that 6S, when combined with LD, improved representative non-motor symptoms of PD, such as depression, cognitive dysfunction, and olfactory deficits, without interfering with the potent motor benefits of LD (Kim et al., 2024). Moreover, the co-treatment provided dopaminergic neuroprotection and reduced neuroinflammation, which were not observed with LD alone. These findings highlighted the potential of 6S as a valuable adjunct candidate to LD therapy. The present study was designed as a follow-up study to elucidate the complementary effects of 6S on LD treatment using a metabolomics-based approach.

In particular, we focused on the brain and gut, which are increasingly recognized as critical sites in PD pathogenesis (Munoz-Pinto et al., 2024). Although PD has classically been defined by dopaminergic neuronal loss in the substantia nigra, emerging evidence supports alternative origins, including α-synuclein aggregation in the gut and impaired intestinal barrier integrity. This has led to the proposed distinction between “brain-first” and “gut-first” PD, underscoring the importance of targeting gut-derived factors for disease modification (Borghammer and Van Den Berge, 2019). In line with our earlier findings that 6S reduced gut α-synuclein accumulation and inflammation in a Proteus mirabilis-induced PD model, the current results suggest that 6S may complement LD by regulating gut-associated factors that influence brain pathology (Fig. 8).

Consistent with previous reports, LD robustly improved motor deficits in the MPTP/probenecid model. Importantly, 6S co-administration did not diminish the motor efficacy of LD, but significantly restored gastrointestinal motility, a non-motor feature unresponsive to LD monotherapy. In addition, striatal dopamine and LD levels were significantly further elevated in the combination group, suggesting that 6S potentiates dopaminergic availability and may enhance the therapeutic efficacy of LD. The increased LD levels observed following co-administration with 6S imply a potential influence of 6S on LD bioavailability. One plausible mechanism involves the modulation of key metabolic enzymes, such as catechol-O-methyltransferase or monoamine oxidase, thereby reducing peripheral LD degradation.

Beyond host metabolism, emerging evidence highlights the critical role of gut microbiota in LD pharmacokinetics (Reunanen et al., 2025). For instance, Enterococcus faecalis has been shown to decarboxylate LD into dopamine via tyrosine decarboxylase (TDC), which limits its systemic availability and brain uptake. In this context, 6S may enhance LD levels by modulating gut microbial composition or specifically inhibiting TDC-mediated metabolism. Furthermore, 6S-induced improvements in intestinal barrier integrity or absorptive function may facilitate LD uptake. Although the precise pathways warrant further investigation, these mechanisms offer a robust framework for explaining the synergistic increase in LD and dopamine levels observed in this study.

Metabolomic profiling revealed distinct brain and gut signatures specific to the LD+6S group. In the brain, multiple metabolites across energy, lipid, and amino acid pathways were significantly altered, with succinic acid, arachidonic acid, DHA, and lysophospholipids showing strong associations with motor performance. Similarly, pyroglutamic acid, N-acetyl-L-aspartic acid, prostaglandin F2α, and related metabolites were correlated with striatal dopamine levels. In the gut, phenylalanine and DHA levels were elevated in association with improved motility, whereas the levels of lysophospholipids and nucleotide derivatives were decreased (Chitre et al., 2020; Li et al., 2024).

These correlation patterns indicate that the combination of LD and 6S not only alters metabolite abundance but may also influence key biochemical processes underlying both motor and non-motor regulation. The metabolic pathways identified here provide mechanistic insights into how 6S complements LD therapy. The elevation of DHA and the reduction of arachidonic acid and lysophospholipids imply a shift toward an anti-inflammatory lipid profile, which may contribute to dopaminergic neuron protection. Increased succinic acid indicates restoration of mitochondrial energy metabolism, while modulation of pyroglutamic acid and N-acetyl-L-aspartic acid suggests reduced oxidative stress and improved neuronal integrity. In the gut, upregulation of phenylalanine and DHA may enhance enteric neurotransmission and mucosal resilience, facilitating gut motility. Collectively, these pathway-level changes support the view that 6S acts as a metabolic modulator that rebalances oxidative, lipid, and amino acid metabolism across the brain–gut axis.

One limitation of the present study is the absence of a 6S-alone treatment group. Therefore, although the current findings demonstrate that 6S modifies the effects of LD co-treatment, it remains difficult to distinguish whether these effects arise from a synergistic interaction or are partially attributable to the intrinsic activity of 6S itself. Because the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the adjunctive potential of 6S in the context of LD therapy, the experimental design was focused on combination treatment. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the present results.

Our findings provide novel evidence that 6S acts as an effective adjunct to LD, preserving its robust motor benefits, while ameliorating non-motor symptoms and enhancing dopaminergic efficacy. By revealing distinct metabolomic changes in both the brain and gut, this study highlights the potential relevance of gut–brain metabolic interactions in improving outcomes in PD. Collectively, these findings suggest that dietary or pharmacological incorporation of 6-shogaol could represent a feasible adjunctive approach to enhance LD efficacy and mitigate gastrointestinal complications in PD.
