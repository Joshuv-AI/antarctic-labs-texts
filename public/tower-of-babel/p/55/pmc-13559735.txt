Chronic Pain in Community: A Mixed Methods Evaluation of the Vet‐to‐Vet Program
Authors: Rachael R Kenney, Elise Gunzburger, Drew Herbert, Kyla L Bauer, Joseph W Frank
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThis paper presents findings from a mixed methods evaluation of the Vet‐to‐Vet Program—a virtual peer‐led support group developed to bridge geographic barriers and address social needs for rural Veterans living with chronic pain, which is nowbut open to all Veterans. This study evaluates outcomes and healthcare utilization patterns among Veterans participating in Vet‐to‐Vet.MethodsWe explored outcomes of the Vet‐to‐Vet Program using a sequential mixed methods design with a pragmatic qualitative design and a retrospective observational quantitative design. First, we conducted focus groups and analyzed them with matrix analysis. Then, we calculated standardized mean differences to explore healthcare utilization 6 months prior to and 6 months after a Veteran's first Vet‐to‐Vet encounter.FindingsA total of 40 Veterans participated in six focus groups and 110 Veterans were included in the observational cohort (i.e., attended five or more meetings at one of seven established sites). Focus group participants attributed the following outcomes to their participation in Vet‐to‐Vet: reduced social isolation and increased social connection, increased awareness and utilization of services, and improved physical and mental health. Quantitative findings demonstrated a small decrease in primary care utilization (SMD = 0.12) and a small increase in the use of Complementary and Integrative Health Services (SMD = 0.11) after Vet‐to‐Vet participation. We observed a small decrease in the number of Emergency Department encounters (SMD = 0.14) and hospital length of stay, when hospitalized (SMD = 0.19).ConclusionsThe Vet‐to‐Vet Program may increase social connection, improve health outcomes, and change how Participants navigate their care.Keywords: chronic pain, healthcare utilization, peer support, Veterans

Introduction

Rural American adults and Veterans report experiencing pain at higher rates than their urban and civilian counterparts [1, 2]. Additionally, while rurality may be a protective factor for loneliness [3], Veterans [4], and those living with pain [5] report heightened experiences of loneliness compared to the general population. The Veterans Health Administration (VHA), which provides care to 2.7 million rural or highly rural Veterans [6], takes a person‐centered care approach, called Whole Health [7], toward treating chronic pain. Treatment approaches that grew out of Whole Health and the Congressional mandate [8] to expand pain care appear to increase rural Veterans’ access to pain care [9], and rural/urban disparities in access to pain care appear to be more complex than initially suspected [10]. The Whole Health System also includes group‐based programs and a focus on exploring relationships and community.

Healthcare systems have long recognized a value in peer support groups that bring together peers who have experienced the same health conditions to help them better adjust to those conditions, such as chronic pain [11, 12, 13]. Mutual self‐help for health conditions is a specific model of peer support defined by peers with direct experience leading and running the group to share in their knowledge. Depending on the kind of health condition, it has been found to help participants overcome health problems, empower them despite their health problems, and/or improve their overall wellbeing [14, 15]. Research has been mixed if peer support can improve physical or mental health conditions [16]. However, when combined with narrative‐based interventions, such as storytelling, it shows promise to enhance self‐efficacy and self‐management of chronic conditions [17].

We conducted a patient‐centered sequential mixed‐methods evaluation to explore outcomes that mattered to Veterans who participated in Vet‐to‐Vet. As addressing social needs can have a wide‐ranging impact on health and wellbeing outcomes [18], we started with the question: “What specific outcomes do Veterans report as a result of their participation in the Vet‐to‐Vet Program?” Findings from this qualitative analysis led to our quantitative question: “How does service utilization change after participating in an established Vet‐to‐Vet group?”

Methods

Intervention

The Vet‐to‐Vet Program is a weekly virtual mutual self‐help group that provides social support to Veterans who are living with chronic pain through storytelling and peer support. The program was initially designed for rural Veterans, but it is open to all Veterans who would like to participate. Vet‐to‐Vet Peer Facilitators (Facilitators) run meetings, which explore the shared experience of living with chronic pain and of military/Veteran culture. While meetings are intentionally unstructured, meetings typically include a welcome, a mindfulness exercise, storytelling, and peer support. While there is no formal education, Facilitators are encouraged to weave VHA Whole Health concepts, such as the “MAP” (mission, aspiration, purpose) and the Circle of Health [19, 20] into discussions. The only eligibility criteria for participation are that the Vet‐to‐Vet Participant (Participant) identifies as experiencing chronic pain and is (or becomes) enrolled in VHA services. Unlike other groups that have a limited number of sessions, Participants can attend Vet‐to‐Vet in perpetuity [21]. The Implementation Team nationally coordinates Vet‐to‐Vet and Site Leads, who are commonly coaches and peer specialists, oversee local operations. A more detailed description of Vet‐to‐Vet is published elsewhere [22].

Vet‐to‐Vet Implementation and Evaluation Teams

The Vet‐to‐Vet team is made up of an Implementation Team and an Evaluation Team. The Implementation Team supports implementation and sustainment of Vet‐to‐Vet groups, and the Evaluation Team provides evaluation support for the Vet‐to‐Vet Program. One Evaluation Team member assisted with implementing the pilot group but was not involved in implementation at the time of the activities described here. The Vet‐to‐Vet Program Support Assistant was one of the focus group notetakers; however, we did not use those notes for this analysis. The Implementation Team and four Veteran consultants, who did not personally participate in Vet‐to‐Vet, assisted with designing the evaluation and interpreting results but did not collect or have access to raw data. This work was conducted as a healthcare operations quality improvement and was deemed non‐research by the VA Eastern Colorado Health Care System. The evaluation was funded by REDACTED, which was not involved in designing or conducting the evaluation.

Study Design and Population

Qualitative

The goal of our qualitative evaluation was to answer the question: “What specific outcomes do Veterans attribute to their participation in Vet‐to‐Vet?” We took this approach, rather than pre‐defining outcomes, because there is little consensus about which outcomes to measure for peer‐ support interventions [14, 23].

Sampling and Recruitment

As Vet‐to‐Vet is a relational intervention that requires members to build rapport, we followed common practice in peer support interventions to focus on groups at least 6 months after starting [24] (i.e., established groups). We contacted the Site Lead(s) of established Vet‐to‐Vet groups (i.e., had been meeting for 6 months or more) and asked them if we could conduct a focus group during one of their regularly scheduled Vet‐to‐Vet meetings. Once the group agreed on a date and time, the Site Lead sent the meeting invite to the Evaluation Team. Any Participant who attended the meeting on the day of the focus group was eligible to participate, regardless of how long they had been participating in Vet‐to‐Vet (Table 2). Participants could opt out by not attending on the day of the focus group.

Data Collection

We developed a focus group guide (File S1) with input from the Implementation Team and Veteran consultants. We piloted the guide and process with the first established Vet‐to‐Vet group in December 2023. The pilot activities did not result in substantive changes to the questions or processes.

We then conducted focus groups within each group's existing meeting time and platform (e.g., WebEx or VA Video Connect), emphasized confidentiality, and obtained verbal consent from all focus group participants. We held separate Participant and Facilitator focus groups at most sites; however, in some cases where attendance was low or roles were fluid, Participants and Facilitators participated together in one focus group.

Two Evaluation Team members attended each focus group, with one acting as a moderator and the other as a note‐taker. Moderators took a semi‐structured approach [25], encouraging focus group participants to build off one another's responses and using grounded probes and the focus group moderator and note‐taker debriefed after each focus group. The Evaluation Team audio‐recorded the focus groups and commissioned professional transcription. We conducted focus groups until we achieved thematic saturation.

We conducted the pilot focus group on 12/7/2023 and then reached out to Site Leads at the remaining established sites from December 2023‐January 2025. All groups agreed to participate, and the remaining focus groups occurred from 4/24/2024 to 4/29/2025. At the time of focus group participation, groups had been established for a median of 37 weeks, with a median of seven participants per group. Rurality of participating groups ranged from 23% to 71% (Table 3).

Analyses

We conducted a Matrix Analysis [26], a structured approach where data is organized into a matrix format with columns representing constructs of interest and rows representing data collection events [26]. The first author created a template that captured the constructs from the focus group guide. One analyst reviewed the recording and transcript from a focus group, and summarized concepts for each construct in the template, adding quotes. A second analyst reviewed and refined the initial summaries. The first author then wrote and verified summaries for accuracy with another analyst. We resolved discrepancies through discussion and entered final summaries into the matrix. Then, we extracted participant outcomes from the matrix and inductively coded the outcomes to identify salient outcomes for each focus group.

The analysis included pilot focus group data. Owing to the fluidity of Participant and Facilitator roles, and the fact that focus group data is socially constructed and therefore the group is the unit of analysis [25], we included Facilitators who participated in mixed focus groups in the analysis. We excluded data from Facilitator only focus groups. All data and analysis files were securely saved on the VHA's restricted access drive, accessible only to the Evaluation Team members.

Quantitative

Sample

Informed by focus group findings, we conducted a retrospective observational study of Veterans who participated in Vet‐to‐Vet from April 2022 through August 2025 to answer the question: “How does service utilization change after regular participation in an established Vet‐to‐Vet group?” We identified Participants using both program attendance trackers and administrative data. The analysis excluded Veterans if they had less than 6 months of follow‐up available after their first Vet‐to‐Vet encounter. We also excluded Veterans with less than five Vet‐to‐Vet encounters during the study period. We selected this five encounter cutoff to identify meaningful participation per the guidance of the Implementation Team and previous research [27]. Finally, in order to parallel the qualitative component of the study, we excluded Veterans if they participated at sites that had not met sustainment as of August 2025. This cohort represented regular Participants at established Vet‐to‐Vet sites. The analysis did not include Facilitators.

We created two sensitivity cohorts to determine if changing inclusion criteria would affect our conclusions about care utilization. The first sensitivity cohort expanded the inclusion criteria to Veterans with 6 months of follow‐up and at least five encounters at any site regardless of establishment status of the site. The second sensitivity cohort had the even broader inclusion criteria of all Participants that had at least 6 months of follow‐up regardless of site establishment or Veteran's total number of Vet‐to‐Vet encounters.

Measures and Outcomes

We derived Veteran demographics, comorbidities, and outcomes from the VHA Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW), which is an enterprise‐level data repository system that incorporates data from VHA service utilization. Demographics included sex, race, ethnicity, age, marital status, and rurality.

Rural urban commuting area (RUCA) codes for the census tract of a patient's address defined rurality designating Veterans as urban, rural or highly rural (URH). This field is derived through a spatial intersection process between the geocoded point and URH polygons. Records that do not intersect the polygon layer receive a NULL value. VHA defines urban as RUCA codes 1.0 and 1.1, rural as RUCA codes 2–9 and 10.1–10.3, and highly rural as RUCA code 10.0 [28]. This differs from non‐VA groups, which often classify RUCA codes 1–3 as urban/metropolitan and 4–10 as rural/non‐metropolitan [29].

Comorbidities included any pain diagnoses, any mental health diagnoses, any use disorder diagnoses, and Charlson Comorbidity Index and were defined by presence of associated ICD‐10 codes in the prior two years. Pain diagnoses included back pain, neck pain, neuropathy, migraine/headache, fibromyalgia, and other painful conditions. Mental health diagnoses included depression, post‐traumatic stress disorder, and anxiety. Substance use disorders included alcohol use disorder, opioid use disorder, and other substance use disorders.

The outcomes of interest, as indicated by focus group findings, were VHA care utilization of primary care, mental health, specialty pain management, Whole Health, Complementary and Integrative Health (e.g., acupuncture), and acute care (Emergency Department and hospital care). We defined these types of care utilization using VHA stop codes that are related to the specific service for VHA outpatient encounters. Specifically, we were interested in whether there was a change in the proportion of Vet‐to‐Vet Veterans utilizing care types and whether there was a change in the number of encounters per Veteran for the utilization types. Changes in healthcare utilization could signal either positive or negative effects of Vet‐to‐Vet participation on Veterans’ self‐management of chronic pain.

To examine proportion of Veterans using a care type, we determined the number of Veterans with an encounter of that type in the 6 months prior and 6 months post Vet‐to‐Vet start. The number of encounters of each care type for Veterans was also determined in the 6 months prior and post Vet‐to‐Vet start. We defined utilization care types for VHA outpatient encounters using VistA stop codes related to each specific service. The exposure of interest was participation in Vet‐to‐Vet, which began after Veteran's first Vet‐to‐Vet meeting.

Analyses

Given the exploratory mixed‐methods design, we used descriptive methods to characterize Veterans in our study sample and make exploratory comparisons between pre‐ and post‐data. We did not test pre‐specified hypotheses or perform statistical testing. Instead, we aimed to describe our study participants and probe into possible changes in utilization to inform future quantitative analyses. We examined demographics and baseline characteristics with categorical variables reported as counts and percentages and reported continuous variables as means and standard deviations. We used the descriptive method of standardized mean differences (SMD) [30] to compare proportions of Veterans and mean number of encounters per Veteran prior and post Vet‐to‐Vet start for each care utilization type. Standardized mean difference is a metric of effect size that is independent of sample size and is unadjusted for confounders or bias. A SMD >0.1 is considered an indication of a small difference, and a SMD >0.4 is considered an indication of a moderate difference. We also conducted the same descriptive SMD analysis on the two sensitivity cohorts to explore if our results were affected by different inclusion criteria. We did not use any effect modifiers or confounders, and subgroup interactions were not explored in this descriptive study.

Outcomes

Reduced Isolation and Increased Social Connection

All groups described significant experience of increased social connection. A participant from one group summarized their experience: “[Vet‐to‐Vet] helps me not feel so cut off from everybody, from humanity” (Site 6). The relationships between Participants were not superficial; they discussed truly caring for one another, learning to trust again, and some of the focus groups referred to their Vet‐to‐Vet group as a “family.”

“If this group wasn't here, I know for myself, I would end up going back to being completely isolated, and it would be harder to cope, so it's been that much of a big help to me. Being here with people that I care about.” (Site 6)

Increased Awareness of Services and Self‐Advocacy

Another outcome that all groups discussed was finding out about and trying new formal and informal strategies to manage pain (Sites 2, 4, 5, 6). They explained that hearing about services and strategies from other Veterans made them open to trying new things and gave them hope (Site 4). Groups also discussed feeling empowered to self‐advocate the “labyrinth” of VHA services after participating in Vet‐to‐Vet (Sites 1, 3, 6).

“This group has helped me to start to learn to trust again… and to be willing to stand up for myself, and to self‐advocate when I need care I'm not receiving, and continue to self‐advocate as needed to try to get whatever help.” (Site 6)

Improved Physical and Mental Health

While connection to other people and connection to services were the most prevalent themes, groups also discussed the impact of Vet‐to‐Vet on their physical and mental health. They shared that Vet‐to‐Vet helped them learn about how pain functioned in the body (Site 1), helped them cope with the pain (Site 4), and sometimes even led to reduction in medication use (Site 6). Groups provided examples of improved mental health (Sites 3, 4, 6) and newfound hope (Sites 3, 4).

“The weeks that I'm actually interacted with my own kids and with my wife and work, everybody in my household and my animals, is the weeks that I do attend [Vet‐to‐Vet] … the weeks that I'm in my head, spiraling, in my dark spots, are the weeks that I do miss [Vet‐to‐Vet], so this group does impact my life.” (Site 5)

For our quantitative cohort (N = 110), descriptive unadjusted standardized mean differences (SMD) indicated small differences in VHA care utilization in the 6 months post first Vet‐to‐Vet encounter as compared to the 6 months prior (Table 6). We observed a small decrease in the proportion of Veterans utilizing primary care (91% to 87%, SMD = 0.12) and a small decrease in the mean number of primary care encounters per Veteran (2.8 to 2.2 encounters, SMD = 0.12). Similarly, there was a decrease in the proportion using Whole Health care pre to post (38% to 29%, SMD = 0.19) and small decreases in acute care utilization, including the proportion with hospitalizations (6% to 3%, SMD = 0.18), number of mean hospital days per Veteran (0.50 to 0.15 days, SMD = 0.19), and number of mean Emergency Department encounters per Veteran (0.46 to 0.33 encounters, SMD = 0.14). There were no differences seen in utilization of specialty pain care or mental health care. Descriptive standardized mean difference analyses on our two sensitivity cohorts showed similar trends as our primary analysis with attenuated differences seen (Tables S3 and S4). For our second sensitivity cohort of all Vet‐to‐Vet participating Veterans with 6 months of follow‐up, a difference in utilization was no longer seen for Complementary and Integrative Health care, Emergency Department encounters, and hospitalizations.

Discussion

Our findings align with existing literature that found that peer support groups foster social connection, self‐efficacy, and improved health outcomes. While prior studies link peer support to pain reduction [31], our focus group participants did not identify this as a key outcome. Evaluators and researchers should consider a broad range of outcomes for pain support groups.

Participants valued Vet‐to‐Vet for identifying chronic pain resources and enhancing self‐advocacy. This is consistent with prior research on empowerment and self‐efficacy [21, 23, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36] However, Wilson et al., noted that self‐efficacy outcomes only became evident over time, supporting the need for open‐ended groups like Vet‐to‐Vet within the VHA, as previous research indicates that navigation of the VHA system, especially for non‐pharmacological pain services [37], is challenging.

Although rurality is a protective factor for loneliness [3], once disability becomes an issue, social participation has been found to deteriorate more quickly than in urban areas [38]. Vet‐to‐Vet fostered social connection, echoing prior studies on virtual peer support [14, 24, 39, 40], and supporting the need for virtual peer support that is accessible to both rural and urban Veterans.

Veterans reported improved physical and mental health, an outcome less explored in prior research. Cooper et al.’s systematic review did not report specific physical outcomes but saw a positive effect on emotional function across studies [23], whereas Wilson et al. did not report mental health outcomes but saw small improvements for some clinical outcomes across studies [34]. There is more work to be done in terms of defining and measuring physical and mental health outcomes for support group interventions.

Service utilization following Vet‐to‐Vet participation is nuanced, and further complicated by limited access to pain diagnoses and care. These findings are likely multifactorial. Veterans may lack a pain diagnosis because providers may not routinely ask about pain, Veterans themselves may be reluctant to discuss pain, or they may not engage with VHA primary care, where regular chart review helps ensure that documented conditions match experienced ones. Also, providers may not offer pain services, pain services may not be available, or Veterans may opt out of participating in pain‐specific services.

We observed a small increase in the mean number of Complementary and Integrative Health encounters as well as small decreases in utilization of primary care, the Emergency Department, and inpatient care during the 6 months after their first Vet‐to‐Vet meeting compared to the 6 months prior for those who had meaningful engagement (i.e., attended five or more meetings). These findings reflect the qualitative themes. For example, an increase in Complementary and Integrative Health visits (e.g., acupuncture) and a decrease in Emergency Department visits, while maintaining the same number of primary care visits indicates improved self‐management. Additionally, care utilization differed between Veterans with sustained Vet‐to‐Vet engagement compared to those with limited participation or from sites that had not yet reached sustainability.

Other studies that explore healthcare service utilization patterns of peer support intervention participants generally did not find changes [14], or found variable changes [34, 41]. Most peer support interventions that focus on a chronic disease draw from the population of those with that disease. Instead, Vet‐to‐Vet targets a chronic disease state (chronic pain) amongst a select demographic sample (Veterans) who use the same VHA services. This focused matching is known to facilitate participation in similar groups [42]. It may explain why this study's findings suggest changes in service utilization while similar studies on peer support in chronic disease that found the effectiveness of peer support to be uncertain [14].

Our study findings should be interpreted in the context of their limitations. Self‐selection bias likely influenced focus group participation and the data provided within the focus groups. The exploratory quantitative analysis does not allow for causal inference. Future analyses should account for bias and confounders to clarify Vet‐to‐Vet's impact on utilization. A 6‐month follow‐up may be insufficient given service wait times [43]. Finally, this study did not include services accessed outside of VHA [44].

Finally, while this intervention was initiated by REDACTED and designed to be accessible for rural Veterans, only 34% of participants were rural at the time of this study, which was insufficient to compare differences between urban and rural participants. Efforts are underway to recruit more rural Veterans and explore rural/urban differences.

Conclusions

Vet‐to‐Vet, a virtual peer‐led intervention, may reduce isolation and improve outcomes for Veterans with chronic pain. Experiences in the group may also alter how Veterans navigate VHA services. Future studies should examine how specific components of support groups (e.g., mutual health and storytelling) address loneliness and isolation and the value of interventions that are “For Veterans. By Veterans.”
