Standardizing leave of absence and round-trip protocols for surgical management of pulmonary embolism
Authors: Suzanna Fitzpatrick, Khanjan Nagarsheth, Eleanor Dunlap, Vashisht Madabhushi, David Marcozzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary embolism (PE) remains a significant public health challenge, causing 100,000 to 200,000 deaths annually in the United States. Despite advances in medical technology, mortality rates for high-risk patients with PE remain substantial, highlighting the critical need for standardized interhospital transfer protocols for surgical management. In addition, as hospitals struggle with capacity challenges, other access mechanisms to advanced care for time-sensitive conditions should be explored.ObjectiveThis framework aims to develop detailed, evidence-based recommendations for leave of absence and round trip protocols specific to surgical PE management, addressing current gaps in patient transfer and intervention processes.MethodsThrough a detailed analysis of the large academic medical center’s workflow, this paper examines current interhospital transfer practices for patients with PE. The methodology involves evaluating patient selection criteria, pretransfer medical optimization, transport logistics, and postprocedure care coordination, using validated risk assessment tools such as the simplified Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index score, Bleeding, Age, Cancer, Syncope, echocardiographic evaluations, and the 2026 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology acute PE clinical categories (A-E). This work represents a quality improvement and protocol development analysis; as no primary patient data were collected, Institutional Review Board review was not required. This framework paper does not involve primary patient data collection; sex-disaggregated analysis is therefore not applicable.ConclusionsThe framework offers a systematic approach to improving surgical management of PE through standardized leave of absence and round trip protocols, potentially reducing mortality and improving patient care. The proposed framework includes rigorous patient selection criteria, specialized transfer team composition, thorough pretransfer optimization protocols, and standardized communication and documentation processes. Critical components include careful patient risk stratification, specialized critical care transport teams, and structured postprocedure monitoring and transfer protocols. By establishing a standardized approach to PE patient transfers, this paper aims to provide a construct for timely access to higher-level care to improve clinical outcomes, enhance patient safety, and optimize resource utilization across health care institutions.Keywords: Pulmonary embolism, Interhospital transfer, Surgical management

According to the American Heart Association (AHA)/American College of Cardiology (ACC) clinical practice guidelines, pulmonary embolism (PE) represents a significant public health concern, ranking as the third leading cause of cardiovascular mortality in the United States.1,2 With an estimated 100,000 to 200,000 annually, mortality rates remain high particularly for patients with high-risk presentations requiring advanced interventions—many of whom present to community hospitals without the surgical embolectomy, catheter-directed intervention, or extra-corporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) capability needed to treat them.3, 4, 5, 6

When those patients require a medical intervention that a community hospital cannot provide, the question becomes: how do you get them to the right place, safely, without losing time for intervention? For patients admitted at a referring facility, the answer is often a leave of absence (LOA) or a round trip (RT) transfer—a model that transports the patient to a tertiary center for a time-sensitive procedure and returns them to their original bed the same day. This keeps tertiary capacity available while extending access to advanced surgical care. At the University of Maryland Medical System (UMMS), bed capacity is a primary driver of the LOA model—as a high-volume referral center, permanently admitting all patients requiring advanced PE intervention is not feasible, making the RT model essential to extending access to specialized care while preserving capacity for the highest-acuity patients. The challenge is that, unlike ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or stroke, there is no widely adopted protocol for effectively managing PE.5 Geographic gaps in critical care resources, variable transport capabilities, and inconsistent handoff practices between facilities all contribute to outcomes that could be improved.3,4,7

Catheter-directed thrombolysis is an option for these patients, but these interventions are not without morbidity and mortality risk.1,6 High-risk patients undergoing catheter-directed thrombolysis require specialized anesthesia providers and may need ECMO in the event of clinical decline.8,9 These periprocedural resources are often only available at tertiary referral centers.5,6 The 2026 AHA/ACC guidelines introduced the acute PE clinical categories (A-E) to replace the previous “massive” and “submassive” classification, with categories D and E corresponding to “incipient” and “overt” cardiopulmonary failure states that most often require advanced interventional management and are the primary candidates for LOA and RT transfer protocols.2 The 2026 PE guideline also specifies that low-molecular-weight heparin is preferred over unfractionated heparin for initial parenteral anticoagulation in categories C1-E1, and that direct oral anticoagulants are preferred for eligible patients requiring oral anticoagulation, both of which are relevant considerations for pretransfer medical optimization and appropriate transport unit personnel.2

For patients requiring specialized surgical management of PE, some health care systems have developed protocols involving LOA, interhospital transfer (IHT), and RT procedures to systematically care for these patients. LOA refers to the administrative and clinical process whereby a patient is temporarily transferred to another facility for a same-day procedure before returning to the originating hospital. RT transfers involve the coordinated movement of inpatients to an alternate facility for a specific intervention, with the expectation that they return to the original hospital before midnight on the same day. This calendar-day standard, rather than a rolling 24-hour window, reflects the administrative and billing definition of a LOA and distinguishes it from a standard IHT. These protocols are distinct from standard patient transfers or repatriation processes, which typically involve longer stays at the receiving facility.10

Despite the critical nature of timely intervention for patients with PE, standardized protocols for IHT and management remain underdeveloped compared with other time-sensitive conditions such as STEMI and acute ischemic stroke. The lack of consistent approaches to patient selection, pretransfer optimization, transport logistics, and postprocedure care coordination creates significant variability in patient outcomes. This gap particularly concerns institutions without specialized pulmonary embolism response teams (PERT)—multidisciplinary experts in the care of patients with PE or advanced surgical capabilities, necessitating transfers to tertiary care centers.5,7 Notably, the 2026 AHA/ACC guideline issued its first class 1 recommendation for PERT activation in patients with higher-acuity PE (categories C-E), showing the importance of multidisciplinary coordination to optimize outcomes.2

This study describes the LOA and RT protocol we developed at a large academic medical center for patients with PE requiring surgical management, and makes the case for what a standardized approach should include. We draw on our institutional workflow,10 Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) transport guidelines,8 and the National PERT Consortium consensus,6 as well as the updated 2026 AHA/ACC PE guideline framework.2 The goal of this study is to create a practical set of standards that other institutions can adapt: who qualifies, how to prepare them, who goes on the transport, what handoff looks like, and how to make sure the sending hospital is ready when the patient comes back. We hypothesize that standardizing patient selection, pretransfer optimization, transport logistics, and postprocedure care coordination—as outlined in this framework—will reduce variability in PE IHT practices and could improve associated clinical outcomes (eg, 30-day mortality, major complications, and readmission) relative to nonstandardized approaches. Testing this hypothesis prospectively is outside the scope of the current framework and protocol-development paper, but is left for further research and consideration.

Background and literature review

The LOA and RT transfer process at UMMS follows a structured sequence: a referring facility identifies a patient with PE who requires an intervention unavailable at their hospital, initiates contact through a centralized transfer platform, and the receiving tertiary center evaluates candidacy via coordinated call, coordinates transport, performs the procedure, and returns the patient to the originating hospital the same day. This model depends on four interdependent elements—patient selection, pretransfer optimization, transport logistics, and postprocedure care coordination—each of which must be standardized for the process to work safely and reliably. The literature reviewed below informed how we built each of these elements into the UMMS protocol, drawing on existing frameworks for critically ill patient transport, IHT decision-making, PE risk stratification, and Maryland’s specialized health care system context.

The ELSO transport guidelines are the closest thing the field has to a standardized framework for moving critically ill patients on ECMO or advanced mechanical support.8,9 For PE specifically, these guidelines matter because a subset of the patients that are being transported in this LOA process, are on vasoactive drips, require ventilator management, or could deteriorate to the point of needing ECMO support during or after the procedure. The ELSO framework emphasizes that transport is not a passive handoff—it requires pretransport stabilization, a team with defined roles, equipment capable of managing deterioration in transit, and continuous monitoring throughout. Informed consent is part of that framework, which is particularly important in PE given how quickly a patient’s condition can change between the time consent is obtained and the time the transport team arrives.8

The CHEST consensus on IHT of critically ill patients covers the core decisions that every transfer team faces: is this patient stable enough to move, what mode of transport is appropriate, how urgent is the transfer, and what does informed consent look like when the patient is critically ill and time is short5? For patients with PE, these questions are not theoretical. We have had cases where a patient looked like a reasonable transport candidate at the time of the referral call and was significantly worse by the time the team arrived. The CHEST framework reinforces what we have learned operationally: the stability assessment has to happen as close to transport time as possible, communication between the sending and receiving teams has to be direct and specific, and the transfer plan has to include contingencies, not just best-case assumptions.5,6

The European Society of Cardiology (2019) discussed that the effective management of acute PE relies heavily on risk stratification.7 Identifying circulatory failure is paramount, as it indicates a high risk of immediate death and necessitates rapid diagnosis and reperfusion. Next, 30-day mortality risk is estimated using clinical parameters like the Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (PESI).7 Low-risk patients without right ventricular (RV) dysfunction can be safely treated with outpatient anticoagulation. Hemodynamically stable patients, classified as intermediate-risk PE, require hospitalization, though most recover without complications. Recent efforts have focused on identifying a higher-risk subgroup within the intermediate category (intermediate-high-risk PE) who might benefit from more aggressive interventions, such as reperfusion therapies and monitored unit admission.1,7 Current methods combine markers of RV dysfunction and myocardial injury but are not reliable enough to guide primary thrombolysis. Sensitive markers of circulatory failure, like plasma lactate, have become part of how we practically assess whether a patient is truly stable enough to move.11 The improved safety of reduced-dose thrombolysis may broaden its use in selected intermediate-high-risk PE cases.7

The 2026 AHA/ACC/ACCP/American College of Emergency Physicians/CHEST/Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions/Society of Hospital Medicine/Society of Interventional Radiology/Society for Vascular Medicine/Society for Vascular Nursing Guideline for the Evaluation and Management of Acute Pulmonary Embolism in Adults introduced a significant refinement to PE risk classification through the AHA/ACC acute PE clinical categories system (categories A-E), replacing the previous “massive” and “submassive” terminology.2 This five-category schema integrates clinical, hemodynamic, biomarker, and imaging parameters to more precisely stratify severity and guide therapeutic decision-making. Category A (subclinical/incidental PE) and category B (symptomatic with low clinical severity score, eg, sPESI = 0) are generally managed with outpatient anticoagulation. Category C (symptomatic with elevated clinical severity score, including abnormal biomarkers and/or RV dysfunction) and category D (incipient cardiopulmonary failure, including normotensive shock) require hospitalization and close monitoring. Category E (overt cardiopulmonary failure with persistent hypotension or cardiogenic shock) mandates the most aggressive resuscitative and interventional strategies.

In addition, the 2026 guideline provides the first class 1 recommendation for multidisciplinary PERTs for patients in categories C-E, and shows the importance of coordinated, specialist-driven care delivery.2 For IHT specifically, the guideline recommends that hemodynamically stable patients in categories C3-D with high-risk features (such as RV dysfunction and elevated biomarkers) may be considered for transfer to centers offering advanced therapies such as surgical embolectomy, catheter-directed thrombolysis, or mechanical thrombectomy, whereas hemodynamically unstable patients in category E should not be transferred before stabilization.2 These updated categories provide a more precise framework for patient selection in LOA and RT transfer protocols than previous risk schemes.

The Maryland framework provides unique insights into hospital capacity challenges and IHTs through the state’s comprehensive Health Services Cost Review Commission (HSCRC) data collection system. As the only state with a comprehensive all-payer hospital rate-setting system, Maryland’s HSCRC database captures all patient encounters within the state, offering an important view of transfer patterns and capacity challenges.12 A retrospective analysis of HSCRC data from 2013 to 2015—examining emergency surgical encounters across Maryland’s 45 hospitals—revealed significant variability in IHT practices, with approximately 3.2% of encounters representing incoming transfers accepted by a receiving facility and 2.7% representing outgoing transfers sent from an originating facility. These figures are not two sides of the same transfers but separate statewide counts, and the difference between them partly reflects transfers that cross state lines and are therefore captured on only one side of the Maryland dataset. This variability underscores the system-wide inconsistency in transfer practices that extends to high-acuity conditions including PE.12 The HSCRC’s Maryland Hospital-Acquired Conditions program specifically monitors complications such as PE during inpatient stays, recognizing these conditions as clinical concerns that may necessitate specialized intervention and financial burdens that increase health care costs.13 Within Maryland’s global budget revenue model, hospitals face increasing pressure to optimize resource utilization while ensuring timely access to specialized care for high-risk conditions such as PE, which is associated with mortality rates of 16% to 42% for high-risk presentations requiring advanced interventions,1,14 making standardized LOA and RT protocols particularly relevant for high-acuity conditions requiring surgical management.

Methods

This study describes the LOA and RT protocol developed at the UMMS for patients with PE requiring surgical intervention. The protocol was designed through detailed examination of the UMMS RT surgical LOA process flow,10 with particular attention to how the Ryde Central transfer center platform—a centralized transfer coordination system specific to the UMMS—integrates with the electronic health record (HER) to support communication, documentation, and coordination between facilities. Institutions without a system similar to Ryde Central would need an equivalent centralized transfer platform to replicate this workflow. The goal of this workflow description is to document the standards we developed and make them reproducible for other institutions. Although prospective validation across multiple institutions is needed, the standards described here are grounded in established clinical guidelines and expert consensus frameworks, and are offered as a practical starting point for institutions seeking to develop or refine their own LOA and RT protocols.2,5,6,8

The current workflow begins with a patient identification process that involves a systematic selection approach by providers at the tertiary care center, in collaboration with outside facilities. Although not discussed in detail, distance, transport time, and the clinical capabilities of the referring center—including physicians’ experience, nursing skills, and technical resources—are important considerations when developing transfer protocols. Within our system, three hospitals across different regions of Maryland have been designated as having the appropriate capabilities and resources to participate in this protocol, with transfer decisions informed by both geography and bed availability at the time of referral. There is no formal credentialing or financial requirement that limits which facilities may refer: IHT in Maryland is governed by the Code of Maryland Regulations 10.07.01.23, which requires the receiving hospital’s consent and prohibits transfer decisions based on a patient’s ability to pay.15 Any hospital in Maryland may therefore initiate a transfer or RT request, with the agreement described in the Recommendations section formalizing the clinical and logistical process on top of this regulatory baseline. Referrals from outside Maryland are accepted as well, though in practice these are uncommon, as longer transport distances increase the risk of clinical deterioration before the patient reaches UMMS. This protocol is not restricted to hospitals within a single tertiary health system: any hospital, including nontertiary, community hospitals capable of holding the patient’s original bed and supporting the postprocedure monitoring, is eligible to participate. This includes careful application of the transfer contraindications described in the Recommendations section, taking into account clinical stability, anatomic factors, and patient-specific risk factors. Tools such as sPESI score,7,11 Bleeding, Age, Cancer, Syncope bleeding risk assessment,16 and presence of right ventricle dysfunction are used to make this determination. The 2026 AHA/ACC acute PE clinical categories (A-E) provides an updated framework to complement these tools; patients in categories D1-2 and E1 are generally considered appropriate candidates for advanced intervention, whereas category E2 (refractory cardiogenic shock/cardiac arrest) patients require stabilization before any interfacility transfer or LOA due to the significant risk of patient decompensation during transport.2 The patient transfer center is critical in coordinating these transfers, with clearly defined responsibilities for referring and accepting providers. This coordination encompasses patient assessment, communication between clinical teams, and resolution of logistical challenges that might compromise patient care.5,6

Preoperative preparation encompasses proper documentation, patient consent after understanding the RT procedure, and medical optimization.10 The transport process involves a coordinated team with specific responsibilities, appropriate equipment for monitoring and emergency intervention, and established communication protocols between facilities.8,9 This orchestration continues through the recovery phase: postprocedure monitoring requirements, criteria for return transport, and a structured handback process to the sending facility are all defined in the protocol to support continuity of care.6,8

Proposed recommendations for patient with PE surgical management

Selecting the right patients for RT and LOA procedures is the center point of this protocol. At the UMMS, we identify patients with RV dysfunction, significant clot burden, or those who have failed or cannot receive fibrinolytic therapy—using the PESI score, echocardiographic RV assessment, and bleeding risk evaluation as our core selection instruments.2,5,7 The 2026 AHA/ACC framework adds important clarity here: patients in categories D1-2 and E1 are generally well-suited for transfer and advanced intervention, whereas category E2—those in refractory shock or cardiac arrest—must be stabilized before any movement is considered.2 The following are contraindications to RT or LOA transfer under this protocol:

Once a patient is identified as a candidate, pretransfer preparation addresses airway, breathing, and circulation in a structured sequence.8 We prioritize noninvasive ventilation and are deliberate about avoiding deep sedation or intubation unless necessary, as the 2026 guideline cautions that these maneuvers can trigger hemodynamic collapse in patients with RV dysfunction.2 On the circulatory side, low-molecular-weight heparin is the preferred initial anticoagulant over unfractionated heparin for categories C1-E1, with direct oral anticoagulants as the preferred oral agent once the patient is eligible—both relevant to how we prepare patients before and after transfer.1,2 Volume management and early vasopressor or inotropic support round out the pretransfer plan for higher-acuity presentations.

The transport team needs to be purpose-built for these patients. At minimum, a qualified medical provider leads the team, with a nationally registered paramedic, critical care nurse and respiratory therapist when the patient’s condition warrants it. The team travels with cardiac monitoring, a transport ventilator, the capability for vasoactive medications, and point-of-care testing to monitor the patient throughout transport.8,9 Clear communication between the sending and receiving facilities before, during, and after the procedure is not optional—it is what makes the RT model work. A structured handoff at each transition point documents clinical status, any changes in the treatment plan, and specific monitoring instructions for the return leg.5,6 After the procedure, patients are observed at the UMMS until hemodynamic stability is confirmed, bleeding risk is assessed, and pain is adequately managed. The return transport decision is clinical, not administrative—each patient will have their own duration of observation following the procedure, and the protocol accounts for that.7,10 Upon return to the referring facility, care is handed back to the admitting team. Postoperative adverse events, including access site complications, arrhythmias, or hemodynamic changes, are managed collaboratively: the referring team provides bedside care while having the ability to connect back with the UMMS, who remain available for guidance or retransfer if clinically indicated.5,6 Outpatient follow-up is coordinated by UMMS and scheduled with the operating surgeon before the patient’s discharge from the referring facility, ensuring continuity of surgical care regardless of where the patient ultimately receives ongoing management.6 From a medical liability standpoint, the LOA process includes documented informed consent that outlines the RT plan and the defined roles of each facility; the operating surgeon at UMMS retains responsibility for the procedural episode, whereas the referring team resumes primary responsibility upon the patient's return—a distinction that should be explicitly documented at each handoff with clear delineation of roles.5,10

Securing the patient's original bed before transfer requires formalized institutional agreements. For nonsystem hospitals, this is established through chief medical officer-to-chief medical officer discussions and a written agreement before RT service is initiated, whereas system hospitals operate under an umbrella agreement that covers this process across the different organizations. One of the lessons we learned early in developing this protocol at UMMS is that the RT model only works if the sending facility is ready to receive the patient back. That means both hospitals need agreed-upon criteria for return eligibility, clear monitoring expectations, and a direct line of communication between clinical teams for the hours after the patient returns.5 Nurses and service providers at community hospitals need to know what to watch for after catheter-directed thrombolysis or thromboembolectomy—access site bleeding, hemodynamic changes, arrhythmias, and signs of recurrent thromboembolic events are the primary concerns.6 We have found that preprocedure education, including having community hospital staff observe cases at the UMMS when possible, makes a meaningful difference in the quality of post-transfer care.8 Education is coordinated directly between the referring provider and the receiving unit intensivist on a case-by-case basis for out-of-system transfers. As participation is overseen by existing Code of Maryland Regulations requirements rather than a UMMS-specific credentialing process, incorporating hospitals outside the system has not presented meaningful additional implementation difficulty beyond this case-by-case education. For in-system hospitals, existing LOA workflows for gastroenterology and cardiology patients were already in place, meaning no new teams or additional resources are required at referring facilities to participate in this protocol. Referrals originate from across the region rather than from a single dominant center, which distributes transfer volume broadly and means competency at the referring facility is maintained through the same case-by-case education and routine case reviews described above rather than through high-volume repetition at any one site. We do not yet have granular per-facility referral-frequency data, but early tracking through Ryde Central shows no single hospital as a majority contributor. Standardized order sets for anticoagulation and hemodynamic monitoring, along with nurse-driven deterioration protocols and clear escalation pathways back to the interventional team, provide the sending facility with information and a clear communication pathway back to UMMS without requiring a specialist on-site.1,6 We also use routine case reviews between the two facilities as a quality improvement mechanism—not to assign blame when things go wrong, but to identify patterns and close gaps before they become problems.7 This shared accountability is what sustains the RT model over time.6,8

Implementation

Getting this protocol to work consistently required building it into the infrastructure that clinical teams use. At the UMMS, we integrated the LOA and RT workflows directly into Ryde Central, our transfer center platform, rather than maintaining a separate tracking system. This meant creating standardized documentation templates that capture the required clinical datapoints at each handoff—patient category, hemodynamic status, interventions performed, anticoagulation plan, and return transport criteria—so nothing falls through the cracks between facilities. Tracking is built into the same workflow, which means the team does not need to enter data twice, and supervisors can monitor active transfers in real time. Early data show that approximately 85% of patients with RT are returned to the referring facility within 24 hours; the remaining 15% require admission at the UMMS due to the need for closer monitoring that the referring facility may not be able to provide, or due to the loss of the original bed at the referring facility. The protocol will be measured against patient-level outcomes (such as 30-day mortality, major complications, and readmission rates), process efficiency metrics (ie, door-to-intervention time and transfer coordination time), and team performance through case reviews and competency checks; as LOA already existed for gastroenterology and cardiology at the UMMS, baseline metrics are available for comparison.

Discussion

PE is one of the few conditions where the right intervention can be the difference between a patient walking out of the hospital and not, and yet there is no standardized system for getting that patient to the center that can provide it. LOA and RT protocols exist in some form at institutions across the country, but have never been described with enough specificity to be consistently reproducible. That is the gap this paper is trying to close.

The biggest driver of variability in PE transfer outcomes is not the procedure itself—it is the system around it: which patients get selected, how they are prepared, who is on the transport team, and what happens when they come back. The 2026 AHA/ACC categories provide better language for patient selection—hemodynamically stable patients in categories C3-D are reasonable transfer candidates; category E patients need stabilization first, full stop—and the class 1 PERT recommendation means multidisciplinary coordination is now guideline-endorsed, not just a preference of academic centers.2 Integrating the transfer workflow into the EHR reduced coordination time and handoff errors,10 and explicitly structuring the transport team composition allows the team to manage a patient who deteriorates mid-transport rather than improvising.8,9 The most difficult part of implementation was not the clinical piece—it was operational coordination. The Maryland HSCRC data reflect just how variable transfer practices are across hospitals, even within a single state system,12,13 and smaller hospitals without dedicated transfer coordinators or EHR interoperability will face a steeper ramp than the UMMS did.6,10 That is not a reason to avoid the model—it is a reason to sequence implementation thoughtfully and invest in referring facility relationships before the first case, not after.

The LOA and RT model is not conceptually different from what cardiology and neurology have been doing with STEMI and stroke for decades—get the patient to the right center fast, do the procedure, and have a plan for what comes next. The difference is that PE has never had the same degree of systems-level coordination built around it. What PE adds that STEMI and stroke do not have to the same degree is hemodynamic unpredictability—a patient who looks stable on the referral call can be in shock by the time the transport team arrives, which is why transfer criteria and transport team composition both matter more than they might for a more predictable presentation.1,7 The RT and LOA structure also solves a problem that STEMI and stroke protocols do not face: how to move an inpatient to a tertiary center and back in a single day without turning it into a permanent transfer.5,10 Like our STEMI and stroke protocols, the PE protocol relies on a standardized activation pathway, a designated receiving team with defined roles, and real-time tracking through Ryde Central. The key difference is the RT component—patients with STEMI and stroke who are transferred typically remain at the tertiary center, whereas the LOA model is explicitly designed to return patients to their original beds.5,10

This protocol has several limitations. Its foundation in a single health care system may limit transferability to institutions with different organizational structures, resources, or patient populations, and outcomes data have not yet been finalized.10 The recommendations rely primarily on clinical guidelines and expert consensus rather than large-scale validation studies.2,6,7 The protocol does not fully address all potential clinical scenarios or the continued evolution of interventional techniques, including the new Advanced Therapy recommendations in the 2026 AHA/ACC guideline.2,14 The incremental implementation cost at the UMMS was nominal because the transfer center infrastructure was already in place, but cost-effectiveness in resource-constrained environments requires further investigation. Finally, Maryland’s HSCRC system is unique; institutions in other states would need to identify alternative data sources for transfer pattern analysis.12

What we are sharing here is what worked at the UMMS—not a finished product, but a starting point. The next step is prospective data: outcomes tracked across institutions with enough volume to know which elements of the protocol actually move the needle on mortality and complications.3,6 Preliminary clinical data from the UMMS before and after protocol implementation are the subject of a separate manuscript currently in preparation, which will provide early evidence of feasibility and outcomes.

Conclusions

Patients with PE who need surgical intervention and are sitting in a community hospital bed have a narrow window. The LOA and RT model exists to close the gap between where they are and where they need to be—same day, with a clear plan for coming back. At the UMMS, we have learned that this works when the protocol is built into the infrastructure, when the transport team is prepared for the worst case, and when the sending facility is a real partner in the process, not just a handoff point.6,10 The 2026 AHA/ACC categories have made patient selection more defensible and more consistent, and the class 1 PERT recommendation means multidisciplinary coordination is now an expectation, not an exception.2 What this field still needs is prospective outcome data—tracking what actually happens to patients transferred under standardized protocols vs those managed without them, across institutions and over time.3,7,14 We offer this protocol as a foundation for that work, and as an invitation to other centers to continue to create innovative ways to improve patient care.6,8
