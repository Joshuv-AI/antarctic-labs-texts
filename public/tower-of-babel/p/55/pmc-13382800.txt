ATF4 orchestrates cancer hallmarks: Stress adaptation, metabolic reprogramming and immune suppression
Authors: Zhiyi Peng, Tong Xu, Zhenhua Xia, Haobo Han, Biao Ding, LuXi Yang, Wen Ren, Huili Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryActivating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) has emerged as a central mediator of cellular stress adaptation, exerting context-dependent and often opposing functions in cancer through the coordinated regulation of metabolism and tumor immunity. However, the mechanistic principles governing ATF4’s functional switch between tumor promotion and suppression remain incompletely defined, and the translational challenges of targeting this pleiotropic transcription factor have not been systematically evaluated. In this review, we dissect the molecular frameworks by which ATF4 integrates stress signals from the endoplasmic reticulum, oxidative stress, and nutrient deprivation to orchestrate metabolic reprogramming and immune evasion. We further critically assess the therapeutic landscape, including pharmacological selectivity, toxicity, and druggability challenges, all of which must be addressed to harness ATF4 as a precision target in oncology.

Introduction

Malignant progression originates from a self-reinforcing interplay within the tumor microenvironment (TME) among stress dysregulation, metabolic reprogramming, and immune evasion.1 Persistent homeostatic disruption, driven by hypoxia, nutrient scarcity, and oxidative insults, activates cellular stress programs that concurrently fuel tumor cell proliferation, impair effector T cell function, and recruit immunosuppressive populations.2 Although this vicious cycle underlies invasion and metastasis, the molecular hubs that connect these three domains remain poorly defined.

Activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), a member of the ATF/CREB family containing a basic leucine zipper (bZIP) domain, has emerged as a key signaling hub at this interface. Unlike conventional transcription factors that respond to individual stimuli, ATF4 integrates diverse upstream signals, primarily through the PERK/eIF2α axis of the unfolded protein response (UPR) and, more recently identified, through metabolite-sensing pathways such as the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) cascade.3 Downstream, ATF4 coordinates broad transcriptional programs that remodel amino acid and glucose metabolism, reinforce antioxidant defenses, and induce immune checkpoint molecules including PD-L1 and IDO1.4 A central unresolved question, which forms the theme of this review, is how ATF4 switches between pro-tumorigenic and tumor-suppressive functions depending on stress intensity, cell type, and therapeutic settings.

In this review, we first delineate the molecular mechanisms by which ATF4 integrates endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and oxidative stress signals. We then examine its role as a master regulator of metabolic reprogramming, focusing on amino acid and glucose metabolism and the recently discovered lysosome-to-nucleus signaling axis. Next, we analyze how ATF4 remodels the tumor immune microenvironment through its effects on tumor cells, CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes, and macrophages. Finally, we synthesize the context-dependent duality of ATF4 across different malignancies, evaluate current ATF4-targeted therapeutic strategies including small-molecule inhibitors, PROTAC degraders, and combination regimens, and outline the challenges that must be addressed for clinical translation.

ATF4 as a master regulator of the integrated stress response

Endoplasmic reticulum stress: From canonical UPR to non-canonical enhancer programs

The ER maintains protein folding homeostasis and calcium balance. When ER luminal homeostasis is disrupted by oxidative stress, nutrient deprivation, or drug-induced damage, unfolded or misfolded proteins accumulate—a condition termed ER stress. Cells counteract this by activating the UPR, a tripartite signaling network.5

The PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 branch is among the most precisely regulated arms of the UPR. Mechanistically, unfolded proteins bind the chaperone BiP (GRP78), causing its dissociation from the ER transmembrane sensor PERK. Freed PERK dimerizes, autophosphorylates, and specifically phosphorylates the alpha subunit of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 (eIF2α) at Ser51.6 This phosphorylation event exerts two opposing effects on translation: it globally suppresses cap-dependent mRNA translation, thereby reducing the protein-folding burden on the ER; concurrently, it selectively promotes the translation of ATF4 mRNA through its unique 5′ untranslated region containing upstream open reading frames (uORFs). The resulting rapid accumulation of ATF4 protein—typically within 1–2 h—represents a paradigm of translational control.7

Once translocated into the nucleus, ATF4 binds ATF/CREB or amino acid response element (AARE) cis-sequences in target gene promoters. It activates a suite of stress-adaptation genes: ER chaperones (GRP78, GRP94) to enhance folding capacity; amino acid transporters to sustain substrate supply; and antioxidant genes (H O -1, NQO1) via cooperative interaction with NRF2.8

However, when ER stress is severe or prolonged, ATF4 switches its transcriptional output toward apoptosis. This switch is primarily executed through CHOP (C/EBP homologous protein), a pro-apoptotic transcription factor directly induced by ATF4. CHOP downregulates the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and upregulates Bax and caspase family members, triggering the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway.9 This context-dependent duality—pro-survival under moderate stress, pro-apoptotic under excessive stress—is a recurring theme in ATF4 biology.

Beyond this canonical UPR framework, recent work has uncovered a non-canonical ATF4 function that operates independently of classical ER stress response genes. Using integrative transcriptomic and epigenomic analyses in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), researchers demonstrated that during liver fibrogenesis, ATF4 does not primarily upregulate UPR-target genes. Instead, it binds to and activates a distinct set of enhancer elements that drive epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and pro-fibrotic genes (α-SMA, COL1A1).10 Mechanistically, transforming growth factor β (TGFβ) remodels ATF4’s chromatin occupancy, enabling it to form a unique enhancer complex. ATF4 as a master regulator of the integrated stress response

ATF4 serves as a central effector of cellular stress adaptation by integrating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and oxidative stress signaling. Under ER stress, PERK activation induces eIF2α phosphorylation and selective ATF4 translation, promoting adaptive programs involved in protein folding, amino acid transport, and antioxidant defense. When stress becomes prolonged or unresolved, ATF4 cooperates with CHOP to trigger apoptotic pathways through regulation of BCL2 family proteins and caspase activation. In parallel, oxidative stress activates the PERK-ATF4 axis, which coordinates glutamine-dependent NADPH production, ROS detoxification, and mitochondrial homeostasis. Through regulation of MFN2-dependent mitochondrial integrity and NRF2-mediated antioxidant responses, ATF4 maintains redox balance and cellular survival. These coordinated responses position ATF4 as a key molecular switch controlling the balance between stress adaptation and cell fate determination.

Collectively, these observations establish ATF4 as a stress-responsive transcription factor whose genomic targets are highly context-dependent: canonical UPR genes under acute ER stress versus lineage-specific enhancer programs under chronic pathological conditions. This flexibility has profound implications for cancer, where the TME imposes persistent but heterogeneous stress conditions.

Oxidative stress: A hierarchical network of initiation, cytoprotection, and apoptosis

Oxidative stress arises from an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant capacity.11 A key determinant of this balance is the ATF4-controlled metabolic and mitochondrial network. On one hand, ATF4 orchestrates a glutamine-dependent adaptive program: it upregulates glutamine transporters and metabolic enzymes, and the resulting glutamine catabolism generates NADPH via malic enzyme and isocitrate dehydrogenase, thereby providing reducing equivalents for ROS scavenging.12 On the other hand, ATF4 links oxidative stress to mitochondrial dynamics. H2O2-induced oxidative stress regulates the mitochondrial fusion protein MFN2 through the PERK-ATF4 pathway; disruption of this axis leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, Ca2+ imbalance, and excessive ROS accumulation, ultimately triggering apoptosis.13 Accordingly, the PERK inhibitor 1-deoxynojirimycin (1-DNJ) downregulates ATF4, improves mitochondrial function, and reduces apoptosis.14 Thus, ATF4 serves as a dual hub coupling glutamine-derived NADPH supply with mitochondrial integrity to maintain ROS homeostasis.

Depending on stress intensity and context, this ATF4-centered network produces three distinct cellular outcomes. The PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 axis represents the initial response. Although originally described in ER stress, ROS-induced ER stress also engages this pathway, which rapidly elevates ATF4 translation. Its pathophysiological relevance is illustrated in ischemic stroke, where PERK-dependent ATF4 induction worsens cerebral infarction by promoting ROS accumulation and neuronal apoptosis15; conversely, ATF4 ablation reduces infarct volume in murine models. A separate cytoprotective arm is mediated by ATF4-NRF2 cooperation. Under oxidative stress, ATF4 and NRF2 jointly induce antioxidant genes. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), they upregulate SLC7A11 (xCT), the cystine/glutamate antiporter that fuels glutathione (GSH) synthesis, thereby suppressing ferroptosis and limiting liver inflammation and hepatocarcinogenesis. During anchorage-independent survival, a hallmark of metastatic dissemination, ATF4 and NRF2 coordinately induce heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), mitigating oxidative damage and promoting metastasis.16 When oxidative stress exceeds repair capacity, a pro-apoptotic pathway is engaged. ATF4 drives CHOP transcription, and CHOP orchestrates mitochondrial apoptosis by modulating Bcl-2 family proteins and activating caspases. This pathway is operative in H2O2-induced nucleus pulposus degeneration, where ATF4/CHOP upregulates caspase-9, culminating in apoptosis.16 Collectively, ATF4 acts as a master regulator that integrates glutamine-dependent redox maintenance, mitochondrial dynamics, and a tiered stress response network to orchestrate cell fate decisions under oxidative challenge (Figure 1).

ATF4 as a key regulator of cellular metabolic reprogramming

ATF4 in amino acid metabolism: Integrating stress signaling, epigenetic crosstalk, and disease pathogenesis

ATF4 serves as a central transcriptional regulator that couples amino acid availability to metabolic adaptation, cell survival, and disease progression. Amino acid deprivation leads to uncharged tRNA accumulation, which activates GCN2, promotes eIF2α phosphorylation, and selectively enhances ATF4 translation. This core pathway is conserved from yeast to mammals and controls both anabolic genes (e.g., PHGDH for serine synthesis, ASNS for asparagine synthesis) and catabolic enzymes (e.g., ALT, AST) to reallocate carbon and nitrogen.17 Concurrently, the PI3K-AKT-mTORC1 axis responds to postprandial glucose and insulin, promoting ATF4 translation via 4E-BP phosphorylation and enhancing its nuclear localization.18 Recent work has identified alternative activation routes that operate independently of eIF2α phosphorylation. Lysosomal cystine depletion, as occurs upon CTNS dysfunction, activates the tryptophan-kynurenine-AhR pathway, leading to direct transcriptional induction of ATF4 and sustained upregulation of glutathione synthesis genes (GCLC, GSS) to counteract ferroptosis.19 Similarly, the arginine sensor BAG2 directly binds arginine; upon arginine deprivation, BAG2 releases SAMD4B, leading to β-catenin degradation and stabilization of ATF4 protein.20

Additionally, ATF4 functions within a broader epigenetic and transcriptional network. Histone demethylases KDM4C and KDM2B enhance ATF4 expression and co-occupy target gene promoters together with ATF4 and MYC, thereby orchestrating the expression of metabolic enzymes in serine, glutamate, and glutathione metabolism.21 JMJD6, when stabilized by SPOP mutations in prostate cancer, cooperates with ATF4 to assemble de novo enhancers for glutathione biosynthetic genes (e.g., SLC7A11, GCLM), conferring ferroptosis resistance. The oncometabolite L2HG activates the mTOR-ATF4 axis to promote amino acid availability and survival under nutrient stress. The m6A reader IGF2BP3 stabilizes ATF4 and other serine synthesis pathway transcripts, supporting leukemia stem cell persistence.22 ATF4 also crosstalks with NRF2: ATF4 induces NRF2 transcription and CHAC1 expression, while NRF2 maintains cystine uptake via xCT, balancing glutathione pools.23 In addition, ATF4 collaborates with ZBTB1 to drive ASNS expression specifically upon asparagine deprivation, and with REDD1 to mediate AKT activation under amino acid starvation.24

Functionally, ATF4 exhibits context-dependent duality. In normal hepatocytes, ATF4 suppresses ferroptosis via SLC7A11-dependent glutathione synthesis, thereby limiting inflammation-driven hepatocarcinogenesis; ATF4 ablation promotes ferroptosis and accelerates tumor onset.25 In established cancers, ATF4 promotes metastasis, lineage plasticity, and therapy resistance, particularly in aged tumors where persistent PERK-eIF2α signaling sustains ATF4 expression. In colorectal cancer, ATF4 upregulates both cystine and cysteine transporters under hypoxia and ER stress, driving glutathione synthesis and tumor growth. In lung adenocarcinoma, UPF1 controls ATF4 expression and eIF2α phosphorylation to modulate amino acid levels.26 ATF4 also mediates angiogenesis via GCN2-ATF4-H2S signaling upon sulfur amino acid restriction, independent of HIF1α. In skeletal muscle, methionine cycle disruption leads to DNA hypomethylation and ATF4-dependent DDIT4 transcription, driving cancer cachexia.27 In diabetic neuropathic pain, BCAA deficiency upregulates LAT1 through ATF4, increasing neuronal excitability.28 Senescent cells exhibit impaired ATF4 translation due to uORF-mediated repression, uncoupling stress sensing from adaptive gene expression and promoting a maladaptive inflammatory secretome.29 Finally, dietary restriction of insulinogenic amino acids improves glucose tolerance via ATF4-FGF21 signaling.30

Collectively, ATF4 acts as an integrative node that links amino acid sensing, epigenetic regulation, and redox homeostasis. Its multiple activation modes (GCN2/eIF2α, mTORC1, AhR, and BAG2) and extensive transcriptional networks (KDM4C, KDM2B, JMJD6, MYC, NRF2, and IGF2BP3) enable dynamic and context-specific metabolic reprogramming.31 Targeting the ATF4-SLC7A11-GSH axis, modulating ATF4 stability or its epigenetic collaborators, or exploiting synthetic lethal interactions (e.g., with JMJD6 inhibitors or ferroptosis inducers) represents a promising therapeutic strategy in cancer, metabolic disease, and aging-related disorders (Figure 2).Figure 2ATF4 in amino acid metabolism: Integrating stress signaling, epigenetic crosstalk, and disease pathogenesisATF4 is a central regulator of amino acid metabolism that links nutrient availability to cellular adaptation. ATF4 is a central regulator of amino acid metabolism that links nutrient availability to cellular adaptation. Upon amino acid limitation, ATF4 promotes amino acid uptake, biosynthesis, recycling, and utilization through coordinated regulation of transporters and metabolic enzymes. In addition, ATF4 integrates epigenetic remodeling and transcriptional programs to maintain amino acid homeostasis, sustain biosynthetic capacity, and support cell survival under nutrient stress. These adaptive metabolic responses are particularly important for rapidly proliferating tumor cells exposed to fluctuating nutrient availability.

ATF4 orchestrates glucose metabolism: Switching between energy production and adaptive survival

ATF4 orchestrates glucose metabolism in fundamentally distinct ways depending on glucose availability, switching from an energy-production mode under nutrient-replete conditions to a survival-oriented adaptive program during glucose deprivation.32

Under glucose-sufficient conditions: Supporting efficient ATP production, reducing equivalent generation, and glucose uptake

Under glucose-sufficient conditions, ATF4 primarily supports efficient ATP production and the generation of reducing equivalents. Nuclear ATF4 binds to ATF/CREB elements to upregulate mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I/III subunits (NDUFB8, UQCRC2) and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4). PDK4 promotes the transport of glycolytic pyruvate into mitochondria and its conversion to acetyl-CoA for TCA cycle entry, thereby improving oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) efficiency.33 Concurrently, ATF4 activates the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) by directly upregulating glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) and 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (6PGD), increasing PPP flux by 30%–40% and enhancing NADPH production 2-fold. The generated NADPH provides reducing power for biosynthesis and sustains glutathione (GSH) regeneration, indirectly protecting glycolytic enzymes from oxidative inactivation.34 Furthermore, ATF4 synergizes with NRF2 to reinforce glucose metabolic homeostasis. It activates NRF2 through bidirectional regulation: directly binding the AARE in the NRF2 promoter to promote transcription, and activating the chaperone HSP70, which inhibits KEAP1-mediated NRF2 ubiquitination and degradation. Nuclear NRF2 then cooperates with ATF4 to upregulate GCLC and GSS, maintaining a reduced intracellular environment that protects hexokinase and other glycolytic enzymes from ROS-mediated damage.35 Beyond intracellular metabolism, ATF4 also promotes glucose uptake by inducing the expression of the glucose transporter GLUT1. In glioblastoma (GBM), tumor-derived factors activate the PERK-ATF4 axis in monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs), leading to GLUT1 upregulation and enhanced glycolytic flux, which supports immunosuppressive function.36 This indicates that ATF4 can coordinate glucose influx and catabolism in a cell-context-dependent manner (Figure 3).Figure 3ATF4 orchestrates glucose metabolism: Switching between energy production and adaptive survivalATF4 regulates distinct metabolic programs according to glucose availability. Under glucose-sufficient conditions, ATF4 promotes glucose uptake, glycolytic flux, and pentose phosphate pathway activity to support ATP production, NADPH generation, and redox homeostasis. Conversely, under glucose deprivation, ATF4 initiates adaptive metabolic reprogramming through MTHFD2-dependent one-carbon metabolism, preserving bioenergetic capacity, maintaining mitochondrial function, and optimizing nutrient utilization. These coordinated responses enable cells to sustain metabolic fitness and survive under energetic stress.

Under glucose-deprived conditions: Initiating metabolic reprogramming and preserving residual glycolytic capacity and mitochondrial resources

Under glucose-deprived conditions, ATF4 initiates an adaptive reprogramming that shifts the source of reducing power and preserves residual glycolytic capacity. It directly binds the promoter of methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase 2 (MTHFD2), a key enzyme in mitochondrial one-carbon metabolism, to upregulate its expression. MTHFD2 generates NADPH through the folate cycle, replacing the PPP (whose flux declines due to glucose scarcity) as the primary source of reducing power.37 This NADPH maintains GSH in its reduced state, inhibiting stress-induced ROS accumulation and thereby protecting residual glycolytic enzymes from oxidative damage, which is critical for sustaining minimal glucose utilization. Additionally, ATF4 eliminates dysfunctional mitochondria to reduce unnecessary consumption of scarce glucose-derived intermediates. Under prolonged glucose deprivation, ATF4 induces the expression of autophagy-related genes (ATG5, BNIP3), initiating mitophagy.38 By removing mitochondria with low ATP production efficiency, ATF4 prevents them from consuming limited metabolites such as pyruvate and prioritizes substrate allocation to functionally normal mitochondria, maximizing the utility of available glucose (Figure 3).

ATF4 regulates glucose metabolism in immune cells: Lactylation-mediated immunosuppression

In the tumor microenvironment, ATF4-driven glucose metabolism also shapes immune cell function. In GBM, the PERK-ATF4 axis in MDMs upregulates GLUT1 and promotes high glycolysis and lactate production. Intracellular lactate directly drives histone lactylation, which enhances IL-10 transcription, leading to potent T cell suppression. Genetic ablation of PERK in MDMs abrogates histone lactylation, relieves T cell inhibition, and delays tumor growth.36 This finding reveals that ATF4 links glucose metabolism to epigenetic reprogramming (lactylation) in immune cells, expanding the role of ATF4 beyond tumor cell-autonomous metabolism.

In summary, ATF4 functions as a glucose-metabolic rheostat that adjusts glycolysis, PPP flux, OXPHOS efficiency, NADPH generation, and mitophagy according to glucose availability. Under glucose sufficiency, it promotes efficient ATP production, NADPH supply, and glucose uptake; under glucose deprivation, it switches to MTHFD2-dependent NADPH production and eliminates wasteful mitochondria to preserve residual glycolytic capacity. Moreover, ATF4-driven glycolysis in immune cells can produce lactate that epigenetically reinforces immunosuppression (Figure 3). These insights position ATF4 as a central node in glucose metabolic adaptation, with broad implications for both tumor biology and the immune response.

ATF4 acts as a core regulatory hub in the tumor immune microenvironment

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a complex ecosystem comprising tumor cells, immune cells (T cells, macrophages, and MDSCs), stromal cells (CAFs, endothelial cells), and secreted factors.39 Rather than passively inhabiting their niche, tumor cells actively remodel it into an immunosuppressive state, which is a process coordinated across multiple cell types by ATF4, a key molecular node that is persistently activated by TME stressors such as hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and oxidative stress.

Tumor cells: Coordinating immune checkpoint expression and metastatic adaptation

In tumor cells, ATF4 drives immune evasion through two core transcriptional axes that upregulate distinct immune checkpoint molecules under different stress conditions. The PERK-eIF2α-ATF4-PD-L1/CTLA-4 axis is evolutionarily conserved across diverse cancer types. Mechanistically, hypoxia-induced PERK activation in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) drives a 3.1-fold increase in ATF4 expression40; nuclear ATF4 then directly occupies the PD-L1 promoter to enhance transcription. The resulting PD-L1 upregulation engages PD-1 on CD8+ T cells, triggering functional exhaustion characterized by diminished IFN-γ secretion and concurrent upregulation of TIM-3.41 Beyond these canonical axes, recent chromatin profiling studies have revealed an epigenetic mechanism that amplifies ATF4-driven PD-L1 expression. Using ChIP-seq and CUT&RUN technology, previous study demonstrated that ATF4 not only binds the PD-L1 promoter but also specifically recruits the acetyltransferase p300 to an enhancer region of the PD-L1 gene. p300 mediates acetylation at histone H3K27, which significantly enhances the cis-interaction between promoter and enhancer, achieving a 4.2-fold increase in PD-L1 transcriptional activity compared to promoter binding alone.42 Importantly, ATF4’s immunomodulatory influence extends beyond tumor cells to CAFs. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), CAFs highly express ATF4, which inhibits the expression of microRNA miR-148a-3p by binding its promoter. Reduced miR-148a-3p is packaged into CAF-derived extracellular vesicle (EVs) and transported to tumor cells, where it would normally target the PD-L1 3′ UTR to inhibit translation.43 Thus, reduced miR-148a-3p leads to PD-L1 upregulation in tumor cells. In mouse models, CAF-specific ATF4 knockout reduced EV-mediated PD-L1 upregulation by 70% and increased CD8+ T cell infiltration 2.8-fold. This CAF-tumor cell communication axis breaks the previous tumor cell-centered research paradigm and highlights the immunotherapeutic value of targeting ATF4 in stromal cells.

CD4+ T cells: ATF4-driven immunosuppressive polarization

Arginine is an essential amino acid for T cell activation and proliferation, and its depletion in the TME—frequently observed in cancers due to arginase secretion by myeloid cells—directly triggers stress signals in CD4+ T cells.44

Nuclear-localized ATF4 acts as the core sensor and effector of this stress signal, initiating a progressive regulatory program that remodels CD4+ T cell function toward immunosuppression. Initially, ATF4 binds the AARE in the IL-10 promoter, significantly upregulating IL-10 and TGF-β1 transcription. Under arginine depletion, IL-10 secretion by CD4+ T cells can increase 3.2-fold, directly exerting immunosuppressive effects at the cytokine level.45 Notably, ATF4 further indirectly promotes expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as CTLA-4 on the CD4+ T cell surface by activating the STAT3 signaling pathway, amplifying suppression of effector T cells.46 Finally, ATF4 targets metabolism-related genes: it upregulates the arginine transporter SLC7A1 to enable preferential uptake of limited arginine, while inhibiting the glycolytic enzyme HK2, consolidating the immunosuppressive state at the metabolic level.47 A recently identified mechanism expands this cell-autonomous regulation to include stromal-to-T-cell communication. Under arginine depletion, CAFs in the TME also activate the ATF4 pathway, and activated ATF4 promotes CAF secretion of EVs enriched in miR-21–5p. After uptake by T cells, miR-21–5p targets the PTEN gene and inhibits its expression, thereby activating the PI3K-AKT pathway and enhancing ATF4 nuclear localization. In CD4+ T cells, this miR-21-5p-mediated ATF4 activation further increases IL-10 secretion by 45%. CAF-specific ATF4 knockout or EV secretion blockade significantly reduces T cell dysfunction in the arginine-depleted TME.43 This study is the first to confirm a novel mode by which stromal cells regulate the T cell ATF4 pathway through EVs, expanding the cognitive boundary of immunosuppressive signal transmission in the TME.

CD8+ T cells: ATF4-mediated exhaustion and metabolic dysfunction

CD8+ T cells are the primary effectors of tumor-specific killing, yet their function is frequently compromised in the tumor microenvironment (TME). ATF4 contributes to this dysfunction through both direct transcriptional regulation and indirect metabolic interference, with the ATF4-CHOP axis serving as a core driver of CD8+ T cell exhaustion. ATF4 directly binds the ATF/CREB element in the CHOP promoter to drive its transcription. Elevated CHOP then triggers multiple inhibitory mechanisms: it binds to T-bet, the master transcription factor for CD8+ T cell function, blocking T-bet from activating effector genes such as IFN-γ and granzyme B, thereby reducing cytotoxicity48; it upregulates BIM expression, markedly enhancing apoptosis under arginine depletion; and it suppresses GLUT1 expression, impairing glucose uptake and ATP production, which further exacerbates T cell dysfunction.49 In mouse lung cancer models, CD8+ T cell-specific CHOP knockout significantly restores T cell infiltration and cytotoxicity within the arginine-depleted TME, leading to a 45% increase in tumor growth inhibition.

Beyond this metabolic and functional suppression, the ATF4-CHOP axis also drives immune checkpoint expression under severe ER stress, linking it to chemoresistance. In ovarian cancer, cisplatin-induced ER stress activates the ATF4-CHOP axis specifically in CD8+ T cells, where CHOP binds the LAG-3 promoter to upregulate LAG-3 expression.50 Consequently, MHC-II-expressing tumor cells engage LAG-3 on CD8+ T cells, promoting MDSC recruitment into the TME, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting this stress-induced checkpoint (Figure 4). Another aspect of metabolic regulation involves arginine biosynthesis. Under arginine depletion, ATF4 represses ASS1 transcription, which is a key enzyme for citrulline-to-arginine synthesis, by binding its promoter’s negative regulatory region, reducing ASS1 expression by 75% and blocking endogenous arginine compensation.41 Notably, ASS1 overexpression in CD8+ T cells lowers CHOP activation by 50% and restores cytotoxicity. In line with these in vitro findings, T cell-specific ASS1 overexpression in mouse melanoma models increases tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells by 3.1-fold and significantly boosts immunotherapy efficacy. Finally, other studies have directly demonstrated a cell-autonomous role of ATF4 in CD8+ T cell exhaustion. Intratumoral hypoxia activates ATF4 in CD8+ T cells by inducing eIF2α phosphorylation. Sustained ATF4 activation reshapes the transcriptional program: it downregulates migration-related genes (e.g., CXCR3) and cytotoxicity-related genes (e.g., IFN-γ), while upregulating exhaustion markers such as PD-1 and TIGIT.51 Accordingly, in mouse models of melanoma and lung cancer, CD8+ T cell-specific ATF4 knockout markedly increases T cell infiltration, restores anti-tumor function, and enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy. Macrophages: ATF4-driven M2 polarization and immunosuppression

Macrophages are the most plastic innate immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME), and ATF4 critically skews their polarization toward the pro-tumor M2 phenotype through four interconnected pathways. First, the PERK-ATF4-PSAT1 metabolic reprogramming axis is activated when stressors in the TME disrupt ER homeostasis, triggering the PERK-ATF4 branch of the UPR. Activated ATF4 directly upregulates phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 (PSAT1), a key enzyme in the serine synthesis pathway. PSAT1-mediated serine metabolism not only meets the high energy demands of M2 polarization by enhancing mitochondrial respiration but also induces epigenetic modifications such as histone acetylation, thereby stabilizing the expression of M2-specific genes including Arg1 and CD206.52 Second, the ATF4-CXCL8-CXCR2 cytokine signaling pathway involves ATF4 upregulating CXCL8 expression in tumor cells; secreted CXCL8 then binds to CXCR2 receptors on infiltrating macrophages, activating downstream PI3K-AKT signaling and directly inducing M2 differentiation.53 Moreover, M2 macrophages form a positive feedback loop through autocrine CXCL8, continuously amplifying the immunosuppressive TME. This pathway is particularly critical in advanced tumors, and targeting the CXCL8/CXCR2 axis effectively restores the anti-tumoral capacity of macrophages.54 Third, the ATF4-NRF2 antioxidant axis responds to mild oxidative stress in the TME by activating ATF4, which then forms a complex with NRF2. This complex simultaneously upregulates antioxidant genes such as HO-1 and NQO1 to help macrophages adapt to oxidative stress, while inhibiting NF-κB activation, thereby reducing the expression of M1-associated cytokines and indirectly promoting M2 polarization.55 Finally, the Tim-3 axis operates in the hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) TME, where T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (Tim-3) is highly expressed on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). ATF4 promotes Tim-3 expression by mediating TGF-β-related signal transduction, enhancing its own nuclear localization and forming a transcription factor complex that binds the Tim-3 promoter. High Tim-3 expression drives TAMs toward M2 polarization and promotes secretion of IL-6 and IL-10 via the NF-κB-IL6 signaling axis, accelerating HCC progression.56 Interfering with Tim-3 expression reverses M2 polarization and inhibits tumor growth, establishing Tim-3 as a potential immunotherapeutic target with ATF4 as an upstream regulator (Figure 4). Together, these four pathways illustrate the central role of ATF4 in integrating metabolic, inflammatory, and redox signals to drive M2 polarization of macrophages, offering multiple therapeutic nodes within the TME.

Current landscape and druggability assessment of therapeutic strategies targeting the ATF4 axis

As the central role of the integrated stress response (ISR)/ATF4 axis in tumor drug resistance, metabolic adaptation, and immune evasion becomes increasingly recognized, drug development targeting this signaling axis has emerged as a new frontier in anticancer therapy. Current strategies can be broadly categorized into three directions: targeting upstream ISR kinases (PERK, GCN2); targeting ISR downstream events and ATF4 translation/stability; and indirectly targeting ATF4 downstream metabolic and transcriptional functions. Although impressive progress has been made in preclinical models, these strategies face common challenges in clinical translation, including pharmacological specificity, therapeutic window optimization, and tumor adaptive resistance.

PERK inhibitors—The first to enter clinical development

PERK is the most extensively studied among the four upstream ISR kinases and represents the first ISR target to enter clinical development. Currently, HC-5404, the most advanced PERK inhibitor globally, has entered phase 1 clinical trials and is designed to enhance and prolong the efficacy of anti-angiogenic agents. According to recent reports, LG Chem’s subsidiary AVEO has entered into an exclusive global development agreement with HiberCell for HC-5404. The partnership will initiate a phase 1b clinical trial combining HC-5404 with FOTIVDA (tivozanib) for the treatment of renal cell carcinoma, positioning this as the world’s first PERK inhibitor to reach clinical evaluation.57 Additionally, NMS-03597812, a PERK inhibitor developed by Nerio Therapeutics, initiated a phase 1 study for relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma in 2022, but the trial has reportedly been terminated for strategic reasons.58

Limitation assessment: Despite the promising prospects of PERK inhibitors, systemic PERK inhibition requires careful balancing of toxicological consequences—PERK is indispensable for maintaining normal physiological functions in pancreatic exocrine cells, pancreatic β cells, and bone metabolism. Long-term inhibition may induce pancreatic exocrine dysfunction, glucose metabolism disorders, and osteoporosis. To date, no long-term toxicological data from non-human primates have been made publicly available, leaving uncertainties regarding the clinical therapeutic window.

GCN2 inhibitors—Key nodes in amino acid sensing

Amino acid starvation within the tumor microenvironment potently activates the GCN2-eIF2α-ATF4 signaling axis, driving metabolic adaptation, and immune evasion. Consequently, GCN2 inhibition has emerged as a new approach to enhance immunotherapy efficacy. However, GCN2 inhibitors exhibit substantial off-target risks. Recent research has definitively demonstrated that the early and widely used GCN2 inhibitor GCN2iB directly inhibits ZAK protein kinase, a critical upstream regulator of ribotoxic stress response (RSR) signaling. While GCN2iB inhibits GCN2 with >99.5% activity and targets ZAK with >95% inhibition at 1 μM, this concurrent ZAK inhibition can obscure the therapeutic benefits of GCN2 blockade, confound pharmacological phenotypes, and lead to unanticipated cellular responses.59 This finding carries important implications for all ISR-targeted drug development—any GCN2 or PERK inhibitor must undergo rigorous selectivity validation in non-human primate models to avoid off-target effects that distort pharmacological profiles. Moreover, no GCN2 inhibitor has yet advanced into formal clinical trials.

ISRIB—An eIF2B-targeting strategy with dose-window challenges

ISRIB (integrated stress response inhibitor) does not directly inhibit upstream kinases but rather reverses the downstream effects of eIF2α phosphorylation by allosterically activating eIF2B, reducing selective translation of ATF4 and thereby “restoring” global protein synthesis rates. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that combining ISRIB with imatinib effectively eradicates drug-resistant chronic myeloid leukemia cells.60 However, ISRIB faces two core challenges in clinical translation: first, opposing regulatory requirements in different pathological contexts—such as neurodegenerative disorders, cognitive impairment, and cancer; and second, a severe lack of druggability and toxicological data. Although ISRIB was proposed as a therapeutic agent over a decade ago, news of its clinical trial initiation has only recently surfaced, with no published safety data yet available, and there are rumors that its efficacy-versus-safety profile may be compromised by off-target side effects (Table 1). Pharmacological strategies targeting the ISR-ATF4 axis in cancer

Direct targeting of ATF4—The “undruggable” transcription factor dilemma

Although direct inhibition of ATF4 protein represents the most straightforward strategy, ATF4, as a transcription factor, lacks a conventional small-molecule drug-binding pocket within its DNA-binding domain. Consequently, no highly selective direct ATF4 inhibitor has yet advanced into clinical development. Current strategies achieve functional inhibition primarily through indirect mechanisms, including: (1) degradation/translation regulation: dihydroartemisinin (DHA) induces ATF4 degradation and inhibits SLC7A11, thereby triggering ferroptosis in cancer cells.62 (2) Indirect signaling regulation: mTOR inhibitors such as sapanisertib reduce ATF4 protein expression and downstream MTHFD2 transcription levels, thereby enhancing the efficacy of pan-RAF+MEK combination therapy in melanoma.63 (3) Epigenetic regulation: strategies aimed at modulating ATF4 expression through uORF translation interference or histone modifications remain in early exploratory stages.21 (4) PROTAC-based approaches: a recent study reported the design of two small molecules, #129 and the PROTAC degrader PT-129, which specifically target the NTF2L domain of G3BP1/2—central nodes of the stress granule network. These compounds inhibit stress granule formation in stressed cells and disassemble pre-existing stress granules. Mechanistically, stress granules facilitate the delivery of ATF4 from fibroblasts to tumor cells via migracytosis, and PT-129-mediated disassembly disrupts ATF4 delivery, thereby preventing cancer cell proliferation in vitro and reducing tumor growth in vivo.64 Thus, direct targeting of ATF4 remains the most challenging yet urgently needed direction in targeted therapy. Key ATF4 targets across human cancers

Downstream metabolic and transcriptional nodes—Indirectly targeting ATF4 function

Targeting the downstream metabolites and transcriptional targets of ATF4 represents an indirect but more druggable alternative strategy. Several agents in this category have entered clinical evaluation: (1) GLS1 inhibitors: CB-839 and BPTES target the ATF4-glutamine metabolic axis via glutaminase inhibition, demonstrating antitumor activity in preclinical models while facing challenges such as tumor heterogeneity and on-target toxicity.65 Additionally, glutamine transport inhibitors including KM4012 (an SLC1A5 monoclonal antibody) are under development, but similarly face the bottleneck of metabolic plasticity. SLC1A5 expression levels are significantly elevated in late-stage treatment-resistant tumors compared with early-stage sensitive lesions, and inhibitors targeting SLC1A5 have become a research hotspot in anticancer drug development, though most remain in preclinical stages.66 (2) Nutritional intervention combination therapy: Serine/glycine starvation therapy can reveal ATF4-driven therapeutic vulnerabilities in acute lymphoblastic leukemia.67 This strategy has attracted attention for its potential to exploit tumor-specific metabolic differences and reduce the long-term toxic side effects associated with conventional chemotherapy. (3) Other emerging targets: Recent studies have shown that the ATF4-driven 2xAARE-YB system can be engineered into CAR-T cell designs to achieve tumor microenvironment-specific responses. By restricting CAR expression to ATF4-high tumor tissues, this approach effectively reduces “on-target, off-tumor” toxicity.68 Moreover, neutralizing antibodies targeting ATF4-regulated LCN2 have demonstrated antitumor efficacy and sensitization to PD-1 blockade in multiple solid tumor models.69

Combination therapy strategies—Key to overcoming tumor metabolic plasticity

Due to the redundancy and compensatory nature of the ATF4 network, strategies inhibiting a single node often face drug resistance driven by metabolic and stress-adaptive plasticity. Recent combination strategies have shown synergistic potential: (1) ISR inhibitors + immune checkpoint blockade: Targeting PERK or GCN2 in combination with PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies significantly enhances antitumor immune responses in preclinical models, offering a promising approach to convert “cold tumors” into immune-sensitive phenotypes.70 (2) Metabolic regulation + chemotherapy/targeted therapy: BPTES combined with gemcitabine synergistically eliminates senescent tumor cells; DHA induces ferroptosis through ATF4 degradation.71 (3) Multi-target intervention: SERCA2 inhibition or ONC201 induces ER stress, which combined with the BCL-XL inhibitor ABT-737 synergistically activates the UPR/ATF4/Noxa pathway, effectively inducing apoptosis in chemotherapy-resistant ovarian cancer cells.72

Toxicity assessment and future directions

The primary toxicity risks of targeting the ATF4 axis include disruption of ISR function in normal stem cells (leading to impaired tissue regeneration), pancreatic secretory cell dysfunction, glucose metabolism disorders, and immunosuppression, underscoring the urgent need for tumor-selective targeting strategies. From a druggability perspective, ATF4—a transcription factor lacking a conventional small-molecule binding pocket—remains a challenging target; however, PROTAC-based protein degradation and AI-powered allosteric site discovery offer promising avenues for developing novel chemical tools. In summary, while ATF4-targeted therapies are transitioning from preclinical proof-of-concept to early clinical exploration, successful clinical translation will depend on overcoming three critical bottlenecks: (1) pharmacological selectivity, to eliminate off-target effects and normal tissue toxicity of PERK/GCN2 inhibitors; (2) target druggability, to identify direct ATF4-binding chemical entities; and (3) metabolic plasticity, to circumvent compensatory adaptations within the ATF4 network. Future advances—including spatial multi-omics profiling of ATF4 expression heterogeneity in the tumor microenvironment, AI-driven precision drug design, and ATF4 promoter-based cellular therapies such as CAR-T cells—will collectively drive this field from target discovery toward substantive clinical translation.

Future perspectives and challenges

The recognition of ATF4 as a pivotal hub integrating stress signaling, metabolic adaptation, and immune suppression in cancer belies the formidable obstacles confronting its clinical translation. Foremost among these is the intrinsic pleiotropy of ATF4; its physiological indispensability in osteogenesis and hippocampal function renders systemic inhibition inherently toxic. This liability is compounded by the canonical undruggability of transcription factors, whose shallow surfaces and transient interaction interfaces resist conventional small-molecule engagement. Moreover, upstream pathway blockade—targeting PERK or GCN2—invariably elicits adaptive feedback, curtailing durable response. Emerging technologies offer viable paths forward. Spatially resolved multi-omics can delineate tumor-intrinsic ATF4 dependencies, whereas AI-powered structural prediction may expose cryptic allosteric pockets on ATF4 or its obligate cofactors. Complementary to these, synthetic promoter-driven circuits could spatially constrain ATF4 modulation to malignant tissues. To circumvent adaptive resistance, rational combinations with immune checkpoint or metabolic inhibitors will be indispensable, informed by biomarker-driven patient stratification. Unraveling the cell state-dependent rheostat that governs ATF4’s shift from cytoprotection to apoptosis may unlock additional therapeutic angles. Ultimately, the successful convergence of these multidisciplinary approaches will determine whether ATF4 can be therapeutically exploited, transforming a fundamental biological insight into a clinically meaningful advance in precision oncology.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ATF4 is a master regulator of the ISR, coordinating cellular adaptation to ER stress, oxidative stress, and nutrient deprivation through canonical PERK-eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP signaling and non-canonical enhancer programs. Beyond stress sensing, ATF4 reprograms amino acid and glucose metabolism to sustain tumor proliferation and survival under nutrient scarcity. Within the tumor microenvironment, ATF4 drives immune evasion by upregulating PD-L1 and CTLA-4, promotes M2 macrophage polarization, induces CD8+ T cell exhaustion, and facilitates immunosuppressive crosstalk via CAF extracellular vesicles. Current therapeutic strategies, including PERK inhibitors, GCN2 inhibitors, ISRIB, PROTACs, and GLS1-targeted agents, are constrained by poor selectivity, on-target toxicity, and the undruggable nature of ATF4. Hence, ATF4 stands as a central hub linking stress, metabolism, and immunosuppression in cancer. Future breakthroughs will depend on spatial multi-omics, AI-driven allosteric drug design, and ATF4 promoter-based synthetic biology, paving the way for selective and effective ATF4-directed therapies.

Limitations of the study

The present study is constrained by its predominant reliance on cell-based systems, leaving the in vivo heterogeneity and contextual dynamics of ATF4 signaling, particularly its interplay with the immune microenvironment and adaptive resistance mechanisms, largely unexplored and requiring rigorous validation in physiologically relevant models.
