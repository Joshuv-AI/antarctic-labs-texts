DPY30 Is an Epigenetic Decoupler Linking Replication Stress to Immunoediting in Pancreatic Cancer

ABSTRACT
Loss of DPY30, a replication stress–specific epigenetic regulator that stabilizes stressed forks, promotes genomic instability, inflammation, and immunotherapy sensitivity, highlighting DPY30 as predictive biomarker and therapeutic target in pancreatic cancer.

Introduction

DNA replication is a tightly regulated multistep process that faithfully duplicates the entire genome once per cell cycle. Any alterations in the process can impair DNA replication progression and fork stability, resulting in cumulative DNA replication stress (1).

Cells deploy sequential mechanisms for protecting DNA replication forks and prevent replication-associated DNA breaks, catastrophic chromosomal rearrangements, and aberrant genome architecture. These include the reversal of replication forks, as an initial global stress response at stalled forks whereby the nascent leading and lagging DNA strands are reannealed to form a four-arm “chicken-foot” structure, overall mitigating DNA replication stress (2, 3).

Stalled and reversed forks (RFs) require stabilization, protection against nucleolytic attack, and subsequent resolution to allow replication restart. These steps typically require oncogene inactivation and the action of multiple tumor-suppressors (4, 5). This process is also dependent on histone modifications as critical intracellular signals enabling the recruitment of specific DNA stability factors to modulate the accessibility and processing of stalled replication forks (6–9). Indeed, the local chromatin environment at stalled forks must be temporarily altered to orchestrate the recruitment of DNA stress response factors to remodel DNA replication forks and ensure their stability.

If these mechanisms fail, the accrual of replicative stress can jeopardize genome stability, eliciting a cGAS-dependent inflammatory response that, in normal tissues, promotes the recruitment of immune cells for the destruction of damaged cells (10, 11). In contrast, in cancer cells, genomic instability can favor tumor-intrinsic heterogeneity, phenotypic plasticity, and disease progression when surveillance mechanisms fail to clear the damaged tumor cells (12, 13).

As the activation of DNA stress response has immunomodulatory effects, understanding the cross-talk between the epigenetic control of replication fork stability and the recruitment of immune cells may represent an actionable avenue to potentiate the sensitivity of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), a strategy that so far has been demonstrated ineffective (14, 15).

Yet, it remains unclear how tumor cells protect stalled or RFs from deleterious damages, especially in the context of aberrant oncogenic signaling (e.g., KRAS mutation), loss of cell-cycle checkpoints (e.g., TP53), and cumulative alterations in epigenetic regulators—all of which are commonly observed in aggressive solid tumors such as PDAC (16, 17).

Previously we identified multiple members of the COMPASS complex (WDR5, ASH2L, and Menin) as critical for PDAC tumorigenesis and cancer progression in KRAS/TP53-mutated backgrounds (18, 19). In mammals, COMPASS complexes are composed of one methyltransferase catalytic subunit of the SET1/MLL family and the WRAD core (WDR5, RBBP5, ASH2L, and DPY30). The full complement of these proteins is required for efficient histone-3-lysine-4 (H3K4) methylation as well as the regulation of transcriptional initiation and elongation of target genes (20, 21).

Although DPY30 expression has been described to be essential for maintaining global H3K4 methylation and supporting the differentiation of embryonic, hematopoietic, pancreatic, and neural stem and progenitor cells (22–28), its role in transformed models harboring oncogenic KRAS and defective DNA damage checkpoint remains unexplored. Some reports suggest that DPY30 is necessary for the association with ASH2L to enable histone H3K4 trimethylation, whereas others describe more nuanced effects on transcription, possibly by stabilizing the methylation at low transcribed genes (21, 29). Thus, although the three-component WRA core is the minimally required cofactor to support SET1/MLL methyltransferase activity for global transcription, the precise role of DPY30 remains elusive.

Here, we identify DPY30 as a replication stress–exclusive epigenetic effector. Importantly, this function is independent of the global SET1/MLL methyltransferase activity; mechanistically, DPY30 separates epigenetic processes controlling transcription from the replication stress–specific regulation. Deploying orthogonal context-dependent perturbation experiments in PDAC preclinical models combined with single-cell molecular approaches, we demonstrate that DPY30 promotes H3K4 methylation at stalled and reversed replication forks to stabilize them, thereby safeguarding genome stability. Consequently, loss of DPY30 increases chromosomal instability (CIN) and triggers acute activation of the cGAS/STING pathway and a robust and prolonged CD8+ T cell-dependent antitumor immune response.

Furthermore, the combination of DPY30 genetic ablation with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICB) potently activates response to immunotherapy in PDAC. Our findings implicate DPY30 in preserving genome stability in cancer cells and redefine our understanding of a replication stress–specific epigenetic code, unveiling DPY30 as a chromatin decoupler essential for RF stability. Finally, our evidence suggests that DPY30 inhibition merits further exploration as a therapeutic target to kill tumor cells by genome destabilization and sensitization to ICB therapy.

Materials and Methods

Patient-derived samples

Written informed consent was obtained from patients to generate patient-derived samples (PATC53 and PATC66). Studies were approved by an institutional review board and included in the protocol LAB00-0396 chaired by Dr. Michael Kim at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (UTMDACC). Inclusion criteria: patients diagnosed with PDAC. For this study, attrition is not applicable.

Studies were conducted in accordance with a recognized ethical guideline, for example, Declaration of Helsinki, International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects (CIOMS), Belmont Report, and US Common Rule.

Cell biology experiments

Cell culture

Human 293T (ATCC #CRL3216, RRID: CVCL_0063), patient-derived xenograft (PDX)-derived PATC53 (PDX1, reposited at MDACC), PATC66 (PDX2, reposited at MDACC), and mouse-derived PDAC cells KP−/−C [internally registered as WY5549 and derived from Ptf1aCre;LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-Trp53L/L mouse model) and KPC (internally registered as N150F and derived from Pdx1Cre;LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-Trp53R172H/R172H mouse model and named as N150F, see below “mouse strains were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich)] supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 IU/mL penicillin (Gibco), and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco). All cell lines were authenticated, and Mycoplasma contamination was routinely tested using the MycoAlert Kit (Lonza Bioscience).

Lentiviral production

Viral particles were produced using packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G. Briefly, 293T cells (ATCC) were seeded in a 15-cm diameter plate in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 100 IU/mL penicillin (Gibco), 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco), and 2 mmol/L glutamine (Gibco) and allowed to adhere. Transfection mix was prepared by adding the vectors of interest in Opti-MEM (Gibco) in the presence of polyethyleneimine (PEI). The supernatant was collected 72 hours after transfection, filtered through 0.45-μm low-protein binding filters (Corning), and concentrated in sterile PBS (Sigma-Aldrich) after ultracentrifugation at 25,000 rpm for 2 hours. Concentrated virus was freshly used to transduce target cells.

Cell transduction with lentiviral particles

Human and mouse cells were transduced overnight with concentrated lentiviral particles (see virus preparation) in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene. After 72 hours from infection, the culture medium was replaced with a fresh medium supplemented with antibiotic selection (G418 50 μg/mL or puromycin 1–5 μg/mL). Overexpression, downregulation, or knockout (KO) of the targets was evaluated by Western blot after 1 week of antibiotic selection. For doxycycline-inducible short-hairpin RNAs (shRNA), after 1 week of antibiotic selection, cells were pulsed with doxycycline hyclate (MilliporeSigma, D5207) 1 μg/mL at different time points, as indicated in the figures, and gene expression was evaluated by Western blot.

Cell transfection

Cells were seeded at 50% confluency in a 10-cm diameter plate in complete medium and allow to adhere overnight. Transfection mix was prepared by adding the 10 μg of the indicated plasmids in 600 μL Opti-MEM (Gibco) in the presence of 40 μL PEI. Cells were then transfected for 8 to 12 hours, and protein expression was evaluated by Western blot.

Generation of barcoded KPC (N150F) cell line

KPC cells were infected overnight with CloneTracker XP Lentiviral Expressed Barcode Library (Cellecta) in the presence of polybrene [8 μg/mL; multiplicity of infection (MOI) <0.1]. After infection and puromycin selection, cells were passaged for 10 generations to stabilize barcoded population. After barcode stabilization, KPC cells were split in two groups: one was transduced with a lentiviral vector carrying control single-guide RNAs [sgRNA, (sgCTR)], and the other with sgRNA against Dpy30 (sgDpy30). After 3 days from CRISPR/Cas9 engineering, barcoded sgCTR or sgDpy30 KPC cells were injected into NOD/SCID gamma (NSG) or C57BL/6 mice.

Cell proliferation and clonogenic assay

Human and mouse cells were seeded in a 96-well plate (500–1,000 cells per well) in medium supplemented with doxycycline 1 μg/mL or vehicle. After cell adhesion, cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 in the IncuCyte incubator (Essen Bioscience). Cell confluency was measured and analyzed over a period of 5 to 8 days using, and medium was changed every 48 hours. For clonogenic assay, 100 to 500 cells were seeded in a six-well plate in medium supplemented with doxycycline 1 μg/mL or vehicle and maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2 (medium was changed every 48 hours). After 8 to 15 days, clones were fixed and stained with crystal violet (0.25% crystal violet in methanol 20%). Colonies with more than approximately 50 cells were counted manually, and clonogenic survival fraction was expressed as the relative plating efficiencies of the irradiated cells to the control cells.

Molecular biology experiments

shRNA cloning

For gene silencing, shRNAs from MilliporeSigma were cloned into the constitutive pLKO.1-TRC cloning vector according to the manufacturer’s protocol. These same shRNA sequences were cloned into pENTR-THT and integrated into the inducible Tet-Puro backbones.

sgRNA design and cloning

sgRNAs were designed using the “GenScript CRISPR sgRNA Design Tool” (https://www.genscript.com/gRNA-design-tool.html?a=post). First, 5′-phosphorylated oligos were annealed and diluted at a ratio of 1:20. Then, 1 μL of each annealed and diluted sgRNA was cloned in digested lentiCRISPR-V2 according to Dr. Feng Zhang’s protocol (https://media.addgene.org/cms/files/Zhang_lRRID:AB_LentiCRISPR_library_protocol.pdf). NEB Stable Competent Escherichia coli (C3040I) colonies resistant to ampicillin antibiotic selection were amplified, and the presence of sgRNA was confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Positive clones were transfected individually in 293T cells along with vectors for lentiviral packaging production. Human or mouse cells were infected by lentiviral particles carrying a specific sgRNA and selected for puromycin resistance. The cut efficiency of sgRNA was tested by T7 Endonuclease I (New England Biolabs #M0302L) assay on the DNA of infected cells, according to the manufacturer’s protocol (https://www.neb.com/protocols/2014/08/11/determining-genome-targeting-efficiency-using-t7-endonuclease-i). The sgRNA sequences for knocking out DPY30 are the following: GAG​CCA​GAG​CAG​ATG​CTG​GA (sgDPY30#1), GGG​ACT​TGC​TGT​GCT​TGC​AA (sgDPY30#2) for human, and GAG​TCG​GAG​CAG​ATG​CTG​GA (sgDpy30) for mouse.

Protein extraction, immunoprecipitation, and Western blot analyses

For cellular protein lysates, cells were scraped on ice using cold RIPA lysis buffer (150 nmol/L NaCl, 50 mmol/L Tris HCl, pH 8, 1% Igepal, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS—Boston BioProducts, BP-115) supplemented with a HALT protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell lysates were centrifuged at 13,200 rpm for 20 minutes at 4°C, and supernatants were collected.

For immunoprecipitation (IP), cells were scraped using NP40 buffer (10 mmol/L NaCl, 10 mmol/L Tris HCl, pH 7.5, and 0.5% Nonidet P-40—Boston BioProducts, BP-119) supplemented with a HALT protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (#78440, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell lysates were sonicated twice at 30% amplitude for 15 seconds on ice and centrifuged at 13,200 rpm for 20 minutes at 4°C, and supernatants were collected. IP was performed by binding the primary antibody to Protein G magnetic beads (Dynabeads, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10004D) for 30 to 60 minutes at room temperature (RT). Bs3 (A39266, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to cross-link primary antibodies to Protein G. Cell lysates were diluted with PBS-T buffer (PBS-0.1% Tween 20) supplemented with the protease/phosphatase inhibitor, added to antibody–bead complexes, and incubated overnight at 4°C. IPs were then washed in PBS-T buffer, resuspended in 4× NuPAGE Sample Buffer (Invitrogen) supplemented with 1% β-mercaptoethanol. IPs and proteins were separated in 4% to 20% SDS-PAGE (Criterion Precast Midi Gel, Bio-Rad) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Trans-Blot Turbo Midi 0.2 μm Nitrocellulose Transfer Pack, Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dried milk in PBS-T and incubated at 4°C overnight with primary antibodies. Membranes were washed in PBS-T and incubated 1 hour at RT with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) for enhanced chemiluminescence detection (SuperSignal WEST Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate, Thermo Fisher Scientific) or with the appropriate IRDye 800CW or IRDye 680RD Dye-Labeled secondary antibodies (LICORbio). Band quantification was performed using the Quantity ONE software (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The ReBlot Plus Strong Solution (#2404, EMD Millipore) was used to strip the membranes, when reblotting was needed.

Purification of proteins on newly synthesized DNA (iPOND)

iPOND was performed essentially as described previously (30). Briefly, 293T or KPC cells were seeded in a square at 70% confluency (one square per each iPOND sample). Cells were pulsed with 10 µmol/L 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU, MilliporeSigma, #900584) for 2, 5, and 10 minutes to enable the detection of protein bound to active DNA replication forks. Alternatively, cells were pulsed with 10 µmol/L EdU in the presence of DMSO (vehicle), camptothecin (CPT, Selleck Chemicals, S1288), or hydroxyurea (MilliporeSigma, H8627). Negative controls were included: one sample was not pulsed with EdU, and one sample was pulsed with EdU followed by 60 minutes thymidine chase. All samples were fixed with 1% formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 20 minutes at RT and quenched using 125 mmol/L glycine (MilliporeSigma) solution for 5 minutes at RT. After fixation, samples were collected in a 50-mL tube and washed (once with PBS + 1% BSA and once with PBS). Samples were permeabilized using PBS + 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes in gentle shaking at RT and transferred in a 15-mL tube. Copper(ii) sulfate (MilliporeSigma #451657), (+)-sodium L-ascorbate (Millipore-Sigma #A7631), and biotin azide (Click chemistry tools, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used to perform the click reaction for biotin conjugate the incorporated EdU. After washing steps, cell pellets were resuspended in 1% SDS for protein extraction and sonicated 3 times at 30% amplitude for 30 seconds on ice. After centrifugation, cleared lysates were diluted at a ratio of 1:2 with PBS, and 1% of protein lysate was kept as the input control. Cleared lysates were then incubated with Pierce Streptavidin Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88817) for 2 hours at RT with gentle shaking. Protein complexes were washed 3 times with PBS-T, and purified proteins were resuspended in 4× NuPAGE Sample Buffer (Invitrogen) supplemented with 1% β-mercaptoethanol.

In vitro immunofluorescent staining

Cells were plated at 50% of confluency over a round glass coverslip (Chemglass Life Sciences, cat. n. CLS1763012), allowed to adhere overnight, treated with doxycycline as appropriate to induce gene silencing, and fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA in PBS pH 7.4) for 15 minutes at RT. Cells were then washed twice in PBS and permeabilized (0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 15 minutes at RT. After a second round of washes in PBS, cells were blocked using blocking buffer (3% BSA, 3% goat serum, and 0.01% Triton X-100 in PBS) 1 hour at RT. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer and incubated overnight at 4°C. After washes, secondary antibodies (conjugated with Alexa-488, Alexa-594, Alexa-647, or Alexa-750) were diluted in blocking buffer and incubated 1 hour at RT. DAPI was used to counterstain nuclei. Images were captured with a Hamamatsu C11440 digital camera, using a wide-field Nikon Ni-E fluorescent microscope.

Proximity ligation assay and SIRF assay

For proximity ligation assay (PLA), Duolink In Situ Detection Reagents Red (MilliporeSigma, cat. #DUO92008) were used, and cells were stained according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were plated, fixed, and blocked as described above. After primary antibody incubation, cells were washed with buffer A, and then the Duolink In Situ PLA probe anti-rabbit or anti-mouse were added as appropriate. After ligation and amplification reactions, cells were imaged using Zeiss LSM 880 AiryScan confocal microscope (63× objective) at the Advanced Microscopy Core (MDACC). Bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU)-SIRF assay was performed essentially as described in (31, 32) with minor modifications. Cells were plated on coverslips as described above and were pulsed with 20 µmol/L BrdU for 5 minutes. The primary antibodies used were anti-WDR5 (rabbit), anti-DPY30 (rabbit, Bethyl Laboratories), anti-PCNA (rabbit), and anti-BrdU (mouse). Click-iT reaction was not performed.

RF-SIRF protocol

RF-SIRF assay was performed essentially as described in (33) with minor modifications. Cells were plated on coverslips as described above and pulsed with 125 µmol/L EdU for 8 minutes, washed twice with PBS, and treated with CPT 50 nmol/L or vehicle for 15 or 60 minutes. Cells were then fixed as described above, permeabilized (0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS pH 7.4) for 15 minutes at RT, and washed twice in PBS for 5 minutes. Click-iT reaction cocktail was freshly prepared by mixing 2 mmol/L copper sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich), 9 µmol/L biotin azide (Invitrogen), 1 µmol/L Alexa Flour-488 Azide (Invitrogen), and 100 mmol/L sodium ascorbate (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS (pH 7.4). Click-iT reaction was incubated one hour at RT. After washing the slides, protein block buffer (ab64226, Abcam) was added for 1 hour at RT. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer and incubated at 4°C overnight. After primary antibody incubation, cells were washed with Buffer A, and then the Duolink In Situ PLA probe anti-rabbit or anti-mouse were added as appropriate. After ligation and amplification, slides were incubated with DAPI (1 µg/mL; Life Technologies) at RT for 5 minutes, followed by one wash with PBS (pH 7.4) before mounting with ProLong Gold antifade (Life Technologies). Slides were imaged using a Vectra Polaris 40× objective and analyzed with Fiji software. Per condition, at least 50 nuclei were analyzed using mean fluorescent intensity for DAPI, GFP, and red fluorescent protein channels.

DNA fiber assay

Fiber stretching was performed essentially as described (34–36). Briefly, cells were double-pulsed precisely for 20 minutes with 50 µmol/L 5-iodio-2′-deoxyuridine (IdU, MilliporeSigma, I7125) followed by 50 µmol/L chloro-2′-deoxyuridine (CldU, MilliporeSigma, C6891) in the presence or not of HU or CPT. Cells were harvested and diluted at 2 × 105cells/mL. A 3-μL drop of cell suspension was added onto the slide and mixed with 10 μL of spreading buffer (0.5% SDS in Tris-HCl EDTA) and incubated for 5 minutes, before tilting the slides for the spreading. Fibers were fixed in methanol/acetic acid 3:1 10 minutes, allowed to completely dry, and rinsed with deionized water. DNA was denatured with HCl 2.5N for 30 minutes at RT. After washing steps, slides were blocked in blocking buffer (PBS 5% BSA, 2.5% goat serum, and 0.1% Tween 20) 1 hour at RT. CldU and IdU were recognized by using the following primary antibodies: rat monoclonal anti-BrdU clone BU1/75 and mouse monoclonal anti-BrdU clone B44. The antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer and incubated 2 hours at RT, followed by secondary antibody [goat anti-rat IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 555, goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488, Thermo Fisher Scientific]. Imaging was performed on the Zeiss Axio Observer Z1 fluorescent microscope at the Advanced Microscopy Core (MDACC).

Chromosome missegregation

For the in vitro analysis, cells were plated at 50% of confluency over a round glass coverslip (Chemglass Life Sciences, cat. #CLS1763012), allowed to adhere overnight, and fixed using 4% PFA for 10 minutes at RT. DNA was stained using DAPI 10 minutes at RT. Chromosome missegregation were evaluated using a wide-field Nikon Ni-E fluorescent microscope (40× objective). For in vivo analysis, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) samples from human or mouse-derived tumors grown in recipient mice were evaluated for anaphases with chromosome missegregation events using a wide-field Nikon Ci-L plus light microscope (100× objective). Analyses was performed by evaluating chromosome missegregation in H&E samples (five mice per group, one section per mouse), and for each mouse, at least 20 anaphases were counted.

Preparation of metaphase spreads

Analysis of chromosomal aberrations was performed at the Molecular Cytogenetics Facility (MDACC) as previously described (19). Cells were incubated with Colcemid (KaryoMAX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15212012) at 0.04 μg/mL for 3 hours, trypsinized, washed, and spun down. Cells were incubated at RT for 15 minutes in hypotonic solution (0.075 mol/L KCl). Cells were fixed by adding 3 times 2 mL of fixative solution (glacial MeOH + acetic acid at a 3:1 ratio), followed by centrifugation. Cell suspension was dropped on glass slides. Slides were allowed to air-dry, stained with 4% Giemsa staining solution (KaryoMAX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #10092013) for 5 minutes, and mounted. At least 35 metaphases were analyzed from each sample for chromosomal aberrations. Images were captured using a Nikon 80i microscope equipped with karyotyping software from Applied Spectral Imaging, Inc.

BrdU incorporation assay using flow cytometry

Human cells were seeded in a 6-cm diameter plate (1 × 105 cells per plate) in complete medium and allowed to adhere to the plate for 16 hours. Cells were pulsed with 15 µmol/L BrdU for 2 hours, harvested, and fixed using APC or FITC BrdU kit (#552598 or #559619 BD Biosciences) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Alternatively, human PDAC cells were fixed with ethanol as described above, and DNA was denaturated using 2N HCl solution for 20 minutes. Cells were then washed 3 times with PBS and blocked in PBS with 5% BSA, and PE-conjugated anti-BrdU antibody was incubated 1 hour at RT. After washing with PBS, nuclei were counterstained using DAPI. Data were acquired using BD Celesta analyzer and analyzed by FlowJo (RRID: SCR_008520).

Immunophenotyping using flow cytometry

After tumor dissociation and single-cell extraction (see “Cell isolation from tumors”), staining was performed essentially as described in (37). Briefly, cell surface antigens were stained with the appropriate primary antibodies for 20 minutes at 4°. For the staining of intracellular proteins, samples were washed, fixed, permeabilized for 30 minutes at RT, and subsequently incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies for 1 hour at RT. Dead cells were stained using the fixable viability violet dye Zombie Red (Invitrogen) for 10 minutes at RT followed by the blocking of Fc receptors with TruStain FcX (BioLegend) for 15 minutes at 4°C. Cells were analyzed on LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometers (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software version 10.8.2 (RRID: SCR_008520).

Ex vivo–stimulated cytokine secretion

Single-cell suspensions from tumors were resuspended in RPMI 1640 with 10% FCS, added to anti-CD3 (clone 145-2C11)/anti-CD28 (clone 37.51) antibody-coated (overnight at 10 μg/mL antibody) culture plates for 4 hours at 37°C in the presence of 1 μg/mL GolgiPlug (BD Biosciences) and Monensin (BioLegend), and processed for TNF and IFNγ intracellular cytokine staining.

Histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence

Tumor specimens were fixed overnight in 4% buffered PFA, transferred to 70% ethanol, and then embedded in paraffin using Leica ASP300S processor. For histopathologic analysis, tumors were sectioned (Leica RM2235, 5 μm thickness), and serial slides were collected. For every series, one section was stained with H&E and, remaining sections were kept for either immunofluorescence or immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses. For Alcian blue (Vector Laboratories, H3501) and Masson’s Trichrome staining (VitroView Masson’s Trichrome Stain Kit, VB-3016), samples were processed according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Histologic samples were processed as previously described (38). Briefly, after cutting, baking, and deparaffinization, sections underwent antigen retrieval using Citra Plus Solution (BioGenex) according to specifications.

For IHC staining, endogenous peroxidases were inactivated by 3% hydrogen peroxide, and nonspecific signals were blocked using 5% BSA, 5% goat serum, and 0.1% Triton X-100. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4°C. ImmPRESS HRP IgGs were used as secondary antibodies, and ImmPACT NovaRED (Vector Laboratories, # SK-4805) was used for detection. Images were captured with a Nikon DS-Fi1 digital camera using a wide-field Nikon Ci-L plus light microscope.

Mouse pancreata from KPC mouse model were stained for DPY30 as described above. DPY30 nuclear expression was evaluated and categorized in blind as follows: negative (score = 0), weak (score = 1), moderate (score = 2), strong (score = 3), and very strong (score = 4) expression. Tissue microarray (TMA) slides of PDAC samples with the available survival data (n = 128) collected at MDACC were stained for DPY30 as described above. DPY30 nuclear expression was evaluated and categorized in blind as follows: low (score 0 = negative or score 1 = weak), medium (score 2 = moderate), and high (score 3 = strong) expression. Kaplan–Meier survival curve was generated by clustering patients with PDAC according to DPY30 expression, and the log-rank test was used to compare the survival difference among different groups.

For immunofluorescence staining, secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa-488, Alexa-594, Alexa-647, or Alexa-750 were used. DAPI nuclear staining (#40043, Biotium) was used to counterstain nuclei. Images were captured with a Hamamatsu C11440 digital camera, using a wide-field Nikon Ni-E fluorescent microscope or Vectra Polaris (Akoya Biosciences).

Cyclic immunofluorescence

After the first staining and image acquisition using PE Vectra Polaris, coverslips were removed by incubating slides with 37°C PBS for 20 minutes. Slides were then incubated with stripping buffer (62.5 mmol/L Tris Base/Tris-HCl, 2% sodium dodecyl sulfate, and 0.8% β-mercaptoethanol, 55°C, 30 minutes) and stained with another round of antibodies. RNA extraction

RNA extraction was achieved using QIAGEN RNeasy Micro Kit following the manufacturer’s instruction. Briefly, RNA extraction was performed from KPC and KP-/-C cells or tumors stably knocked out for Dpy30 or control and from PATC53 stably expressing doxycycline-inducible scramble control (shCTR) or shRNAs targeting WDR5 or DPY30 pulsed for 48 hours with doxycycline. Cells grown in vitro were scraped using 350 μL of RLT buffer supplemented with β-mercaptoethanol. For RNA extraction from tumors, tissue specimens (approximately 2 × 2 mm) were snap-frozen and kept at −80°C until further processing. Tumors were then weighted and dissociated in RLT buffer supplemented with β-mercaptoethanol using gentleMAX M Tubes (Miltenyi Biotec) and RNA_02 program. DNA digestion was performed by adding recombinant DNase I (MilliporeSigma, #4536282001) to the purification column. Total RNA was eluted in RNAse-free water, quantified, and submitted for sequencing.

Chromatin IP

Chromatin IP (ChIP) was performed essentially as described in (39) with optimized DNA shearing condition and few modifications. Briefly, 9 × 106 PATC53 cells stably expressing doxycycline-inducible shRNAs against DPY30, WDR5, or scramble control were seeded in a 15-cm diameter plate in complete medium in the presence or not of 1 μg/mL doxycycline for 48 hours. Cells were then harvested, resuspended in 3 mL of complete medium, cross-linked with 200 μL of 16% formaldehyde for 10 minutes at 37°C in gentle stirring, and quenched with 200 μL glycine 2 mol/L for 5 minutes at 37°C in gentle stirring. Tissue lysis, sonication, and ChIP were performed as described in (18, 38).

Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing

Cells were processed as described in (38). Briefly, 100,000 human PATC53 cells were lysed in lysis buffer for 5 minutes on ice. Tn5 tagmentation was carried out for 30 minutes at 37°C. After indexing and PCR amplification, DNA libraries were multiplexed and sequenced on HiSeq X with 150nt PE configuration.

Immunoprecipitate mass spectrometry

KPC, PATC53, and PATC66 cells were seeded in a 15-cm diameter plate at 50% confluency in complete medium. Cells were scraped in RIPA buffer as described above after 24 hour from cell seeding. For each sample, 2 mg of lysate were immunoprecipitated for DPY30 or control IgG, and immunocomplexes were resuspend in 100μL of NuPage sample buffer, and 20 μL were resolved using SDS-PAGE for quality control. For proteomics, 40 μL of each sample were processed by SDS-PAGE using 10% Bis-Tris NuPAGE Mini-gel (Invitrogen) with the (2-N-morpholino)-ethanesulfonic acid (MES) buffer system. The gel was run 1 cm, and the mobility region excised into 10 equally sized bands. Each band was processed by in-gel digestion with trypsin using a robot (ProGest, DigiLab) with the following protocol: (i) washed with 25 mmol/L ammonium bicarbonate followed by acetonitrile, (ii) reduced with 10 mmol/L dithiothreitol at 60°C followed by alkylation with 50 mmol/L iodoacetamide at RT, (iii) digested with sequencing grade trypsin (Promega) at 37°C for 4 hours, and (iv) quenched with formic acid, and the supernatant was analyzed directly without further processing. For mass spectrometry (MS), half of each digested sample was analyzed by nano LC/MS-MS with a Waters M-Class HPLC system interfaced to a Thermo Fisher Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer. Peptides were loaded on a trapping column and eluted over a 75-μm analytic column at 350 nL/minutes; both columns were packed with Luna C18 resin (Phenomenex). The mass spectrometer was operated in a data-dependent mode, with the Orbitrap operating at 60,000 full width at half maximum (FWHM) and 15,000 FWHM for MS and MS-MS respectively. The instrument was run with a 3-second cycle for MS and MS-MS. Five hours of instrument time was used for the analysis of each sample.

In vivo experiments

Mouse strains

Conditional Ptf1aCre;LSL-Trp53fl/+ and LSL-KrasG12D/+ strains were interbred to obtain Ptf1aCre;LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-Trp53fl/fl;double-mutant animals (hereafter KP−/−C mouse model) and kept in a C57BL/6 background (41). Conditional Ptf1aCre; LSL-KrasG12D/+ (obtained from Dr. Haoquang Ying, UTMDACC) and (42) LSL-Trp53R172H/+ (obtained by Dr. Guillermina Lozano, UTMDACC) strains were interbred to obtain Ptf1aCre;LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-Trp53R172H/+;double-mutant animals (hereafter KPC mouse model) and kept in pure C57BL/6 background. Nude (RRID: IMSR_JAX:002019), NSG (RRID: IMSR_JAX:005557), and C57BL/6 (RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664) female mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from Experimental Radiation Oncology at MDACC. For treatment with anti-CD8 and anti–PD-1 monoclonal antibodies (mAb), C57BL/6J female mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. All animal studies and procedures were approved by the UTMDACC Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All experiments conformed to the relevant regulatory standards and were overseen by the institutional review board. No sex bias was introduced during the generation of experimental cohorts. Littermates of the same sex were assigned randomly to experimental arms.

Pancreas orthotopic injections and surgical procedures

C57BL/6 female mice (8 weeks old) were shaved and anesthetized using isoflurane (Henry Schein Animal Health). Analgesia was achieved with buprenorphine SR (0.1 mg/Kg twice a day; Par Pharmaceutical) via subcutaneous injection, and shaved skin was disinfected with chlorhexidine and 70% ethanol. A 0.5-cm incision was performed on the left flank through the skin/subcutaneous and muscular/peritoneal layers. Pancreas was exposed, and 20 μL of cell resuspension (total 1 × 105 KPC or KP−/−C cells per mouse) were injected using a 27-gauge Hamilton syringe. Hemostasis was controlled with a bipolar cautery (Bioseb) if needed. The pancreas was carefully repositioned into the abdominal cavity, and muscular/peritoneal planes were sutured individually by absorbable sutures. The skin/subcutaneous planes were closed using metal clips. Mice were monitored daily for the first 3 days and twice/week thereafter for signs of tumor growth by manual palpation or MRI when appropriate.

Subcutaneous transplantation, tumor volume measurement, and survival study

Tumor single-cell suspension was resuspended in DMEM without phenol red (Sigma-Aldrich) and Matrigel (BD Biosciences, 356231; 1:1 dilution) at a density of 1 × 105 cells/100 μL for KPC and KP−/−C cells and 1 × 106 cells/100μL for PATC53 and PATC66 cells. Cell suspensions were injected subcutaneously into the flank of 8-week-old NSG, nude or C57BL/6 female mice as indicated in figures. Mice were monitored every 5 days, and tumor measurements was calculated according to the following formula: Tumor volume (mm3) = (W2 × L)/2, in which W is the width and L is the length. Mice that underwent orthotopic cell transplantation were monitored over time for tumor development by MRI, and survival was annotated.

Treatments

The following antibodies were purchased from Bio X Cell: InVivoMab anti-mouse PD-1 (CD279; clone 29F.1A12, # BE0273, RRID: AB_2687796), InVivoMab anti-mouse CD8 (clone YTS 169.2, # BP0117, RRID: AB_10950145), and InVivoMab rat IgG2a isotype control (clone 2A3, # BE0089, RRID: AB_1107769). Treatments with mAb against PD-1 or CD8 or control were started 7 days after KPC cell subcutaneous injection in C57BL/6 mice. mAbs were diluted in PBS to a final concentration of 200 μg/100 μL and 50 μg/100 μL, respectively, for anti–PD-1 and anti-CD8. The treatments were performed by intraperitoneal injection of 100 μL of antibody per 25 g mouse, 3 times a week for two consecutive weeks.

Euthanasia, necropsy, and tissues collection

Mice were euthanized by exposure to CO2 followed by cervical dislocation. A necropsy form was filled in with mouse information, tumor size and weight, infiltrated organ annotations, and metastasis number and location.

Noninvasive MRI imaging

A 7T Bruker Biospec (Bruker BioSpin), equipped with 35 mm inner diameter volume coil and 12 cm inner-diameter gradients, was used for MRI imaging. A fast acquisition with relaxation enhancement sequence with 2,000/39 milliseconds repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE), 256 × 192 matrix size, r156 µmol/L resolution, 0.75 mm slice thickness, 0.25 mm slice gap, 40 × 30 cm FOV, 101 kHz bandwidth, and four number of exitations (NEX) was used for acquired in coronal and axial geometries a multi-slice T2-weighted images. To reduce respiratory motion, the axial scan sequences were respiratory-gated. All animal imaging, preparation, and maintenance was carried out in accordance with MD Anderson’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee policies and procedures.

Cell Isolation from tumors

Tumors from mouse xenografts were harvested in HBSS (Gibco) and kept on ice. Isolation of pancreatic tumor cells was performed by mechanical dissociation (mincing the tumor tissue with sterile scissors) and enzymatic digestion 1 hour at 37°C in in gentle rotation (Dispase II 1 mg/mL and collagenase IV 2 mg/mL in DMEM high glucose supplemented with 1% FBS). The single-cell populations were seeded in DMEM high glucose (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 100 IU/mL penicillin (Gibco), and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco). In order to get rid of the murine fibroblasts in the culture, we performed brief trypsinization cycle (0.25% Trypsin-EDTA, Gibco). For single-cell RNA and flow cytometry analyses, tumors from KPC cells were minced into small fragments and digested with 1.5 mg/mL collagenase IV and 50 U/mL DNase I for 30 minutes at 37°C under agitation. For flow cytometry analysis, the lymph nodes were collected and passed through 40-μm cell strainers and used for compensation.

In silico analyses

Analysis of the The Cancer Genome Atlas dataset

For The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) analysis, the TCGA pancreatic adenocarcinoma mRNA dataset is downloaded from Broad GDAC website (http://firebrowse.org/?cohort=PAAD&download_dialog=true; ref. 43). The mRNA data from the Illumina Genome Analyzer platform in the website was used here (PAAD.rnaseqv2__illuminahiseq_rnaseqv2__unc_edu__Level_3__RSEM_genes_normalized__data.data.txt). There were a total of 179 tumor samples and four normal adjacent tissues with their mRNA available. The clinical survival profile of 179 samples was downloaded from the cBioPortal website (http://www.cbioportal.org). The tumor purity of 179 TCGA RNA samples was computed by the ABSOLUTE method developed by Broad Institute (RRID: SCR_007073). Only those samples with purity above 50% were selected. These 70 high-purity tumor samples were classified into high- and low-abundant groups for survival analysis through applying median value as the cutoff. Survival was analyzed according to the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences in their distribution were evaluated by means of the log-rank test.

Analysis of the PRINCE dataset

Median DPY30 mRNA expression from metastatic sites was used to segregate patient into high- or low-DPY30 expression and generate progression-free and overall survival Kaplan–Meier plots (15).

Automatic image segmentation and quantification

Fluorescence quantification was done using MATLAB (The MathWorks, Inc., RRID: SCR_001622). Specifically, Otsu’s thresholding method and marker-controlled watershed algorithm were used to segment and specify nuclear regions of individual cells. Expression of each marker in the individual segmented area was then quantified by pixel intensity.

ChIP sequencing data analysis

Samples were sequenced using NextSeq 500 at UTMDACC Advanced Technology Genomics Core. Raw reads for each ChIP sequencing (ChIP-seq) were processed using Trimmomatic (v.0.39, RRID: SCR_011848) to trim off adapter sequences incorporated in the read and to remove low-quality bases using the following parameters “TRAILING:3 LEADING:3 ILLUMINACLIP:adapters.fasta:2:30:10; SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:50”. Then, the resulting reads were mapped to the human genome build GRCh38/hg38 using Bowtie 2 v.2 (RRID: SCR_016368) with the “–very-sensitive” preset of parameters. Reads that did not align to the nuclear genome or aligned to the mitochondrial genome were removed, and duplicate reads were marked and removed using SAMtools v.1.12 (RRID: SCR_002105). Peak calling versus the corresponding input genomic DNA was performed using MACS2 v.2.2.7.1 (RRID: SCR_013291) for each sample separately, with the “–nomodel,” “–extsize 200,” “–keep-dup all,” and “–q value 0.05” flags and arguments. Peaks blacklisted by the ENCODE consortium analysis of artifactual signals in human cells (https://sites.google.com/site/anshulkundaje/projects/blacklists) were removed using bedtools v.2.30 (RRID: SCR_006646). To obtain a confidence peak set, only intersections between biological replicates were retained and shown as a heat map (generated using deepTools v.2.5.3, RRID: SCR_016366). Finally, differential binding analysis was performed using DiffBind v.3.0 (RRID: SCR_012918) with settings to use the DESeq2 method, full library size, and with subtraction of control input read counts. Binding events with an FDR cutoff of 0.05 were defined as significantly different among experimental conditions. Mean normalized reads across the three replicates obtained from DiffBind analysis were used for scatter plot representation using the ggplot2 R package (RRID: SCR_014601).

Proteomic data analysis

MS data were searched using a local copy of Mascot (Matrix Science) with the following parameters: enzyme: Trypsin/P; database: SwissProt Human or Mouse (concatenated forward and reverse plus common contaminants); fixed modifications: carbamidomethyl (C); variable modifications: oxidation (M), acetyl (N-term), pyro-Glu (N-term Q), and deamidation (N,Q); mass values: monoisotopic; peptide mass tolerance: 10 ppm; fragment mass tolerance: 0.02 Da; and max missed cleavages: 2. Mascot DAT files were parsed into Scaffold (Proteome Software) for validation, filtering, and to create a nonredundant list per sample. Data were filtered using 1% protein and peptide FDR and requiring at least two unique peptides per protein.

Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing data analysis

Paired-end Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) fragments of human cells were aligned to mouse reference build GRCh38/hg38 using Bowtie 2 v.2. Reads that did not align to the nuclear genome or aligned to the mitochondrial genome were removed, and duplicate reads were marked and removed using SAMtools. Read positions were corrected for transposon insertion offset as described (44), and then only uniquely mapped paired-end reads were kept for further analysis. All ATAC-seq peaks that were no more than 3 kb away from annotated gene transcription start site (TSS, GENCODE v19) were selected as promoter (proximal) regions, using annotatePeaks script from the HOMER package.

RNA sequencing data analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of samples was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform at the UTMDACC Advanced Technology and Genomics Core. FastQC v0.11.9 (RRID: SCR_014583, https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC) was used to perform quality control. Raw reads were first aligned to the human genome GRCh38.p14 or mouse genome GRCm38.p6 using STAR2.7.10b v3.0.0 and sorted using samtools v1.16 (RRID: SCR_002105). Gene expression was quantified using Rsubread v2.14.2 (RRID: SCR_016945). Differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR v3.42.4 (RRID: SCR_012802) and limma v3.56.2 (RRID: SCR_010943). Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using R package fgsea v1.26.0, clusterProfiler v4.8.2 (RRID: SCR_016884), msigdbr v7.5.1 (RRID: SCR_022870), and ReactomePA v1.44.0 (RRID: SCR_019316).

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis

Tumors derived from KPC cells were digested, and single-cell suspension was diluted in 2% FBS in PBS for further processing. Chromium single-cell sequencing technology from 10x Genomics was used to perform single-cell separation, complementary DNA amplification, and library construction following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, the cellular suspensions were loaded on a 10x Chromium Single Cell Controller to generate single-cell gel bead-in-emulsions. The single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) libraries were constructed using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Library and Gel Bead Kit v.2 (PN-120237, 10x Genomics). The HS dsDNA Qubit Kit was used to determine the concentrations of both the cDNA and the libraries. The HS DNA Bioanalyzer was used for quality-tracking purposes and size determination for cDNA and lower-concentrated libraries. Sample libraries were normalized to 7.5 nmol/L, and equal volumes were added of each library for pooling. The concentration of the library pool was determined using the Library Quantification qPCR Kit (KAPA Biosystems) before sequencing. The barcoded library at the concentration of 275 pmol/L was sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) S2 flow cell (100 cycle kit) using a 26 to 91 run format with 8 bp index (read 1). To minimize batch effects, the libraries were constructed using the same versions of reagent kits and following the same protocols, and the libraries were sequenced on the same NovaSeq 6000 flow cell and analyzed together.

The raw scRNA-seq data were preprocessed (demultiplex cellular barcodes, read alignment, and generation of gene count matrix) using Cell Ranger Single Cell Software Suite (v7.1.0,10x Genomics). Scrublet v0.2.3, an algorithm to predict doublets in scRNA-seq data, was used to further clean doublets. Seurat (version 5.0.2) was applied to the normalized gene–cell matrix to identify highly variable genes (HVG) for unsupervised cell clustering. Principal component analysis was performed on the top 2,000 HVGs. The FindNeighbors function of Seurat was used to construct the shared nearest neighbor graph, based on unsupervised clustering performed with Seurat function FindClusters and top 60 principal components (PC). Different resolution parameters for unsupervised clustering were then examined, and cluster marker genes were checked to determine the optimal number of clusters with distinct transcriptional profiles. For visualization, the dimensionality was further reduced using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection method with Seurat function RunUMAP. The PCs used to calculate the embedding were the same as those used for clustering. Differentially expressed genes (DEG) were identified for each cluster using the FindMarkers function in Seurat. The top-ranked DEGs and the enrichment of canonical marker genes were integrated, together with global cluster distribution and prior knowledge to infer major cell types and cellular states.

Statistical analysis

In vitro and in vivo data are presented as the median values or mean ± SD of at least 3 biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using Student t test and one-way or two-way ANOVA test as specified in figure legends. The results from survival experiments were analyzed with a log-rank test and expressed as Kaplan–Meier survival curves.

Perturbation of the WRAD core in PDAC reveals different functional roles for complex members

We performed a comprehensive functional analysis of the effects of WRAD core perturbations in preclinical PDAC models. Consistent with our previous observations (18, 19), loss-of-function experiments targeting WDR5 (shWDR5), ASH2L (shASH2L), or RBBP5 (shRBBP5) strongly restrained cell proliferation in human and mouse PDAC cells in vitro (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S1A–S1E). Strikingly, loss of DPY30 did not significantly affect cell proliferation (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S1F–S1Q).

Recently, DPY30 loss has been proposed to have context-dependent effects in glioblastoma, where it restrained tumor expansion in vivo but leaving largely unaffected the cell growth in vitro (23). To explore whether a similar context dependency exists in PDAC, we performed heterotopic transplants of tumor cells derived from a PDAC developed in a Pdx1Cre; LSL-KrasG12D/+;LSL-Trp53R172H/+ mouse (hereafter KPC, see description in the Materials and Methods section). These cells, engineered to express inducible shRNAs targeting Wdr5 (shWdr5), Dpy30 (shDpy30), or control sequence (shCTR), were subsequently injected into either immunocompromised or immunocompetent mouse models (Fig. 1B–F). Although Wdr5 loss strongly inhibited tumor growth in either contexts, Dpy30 depletion by silencing or genetic KO (Dpy30-KO) impaired tumor growth uniquely in immunocompetent mice but had no effects on tumor growth in immunodeficient counterparts (Fig. 1B–F; Supplementary Fig. S2A–S2V). Furthermore, histologic analysis revealed that Dpy30-KO tumors grown in immunocompetent mice seemed more differentiated and characterized by an increased number of mucin-positive ductal-like structures compared with their respective controls (Fig. 1G and H; Supplementary Fig. S2U). Consistently, Dpy30 depletion significantly prolonged animal lifespan in immunocompetent mouse models using orthotopic transplantation (Fig. 1I; Supplementary Fig. S2Q and S2V).

IHC analysis of pancreas tissues from the KPC model at different time points showed low DPY30 expression in normal pancreas and metaplastic lesions but a striking increase in expression in transformed cells that became increasingly higher as the lesions progressed from pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia to high-grade PDAC (Fig. 1J and K; ref. 42). We also noticed that, in normal pancreas, DPY30 was mostly expressed in the endocrine compartment, in line with its role in endocrine cell functional maturation (22, 28), whereas being weakly detectable in pancreatic ductal and acinar cells (Fig. 1J and K).

To explore the translational relevance of our findings, we evaluated DPY30 expression in PDACs on a TMA comprising of human PDAC (n = 128) and matched tumor-adjacent normal pancreas tissue samples (n = 128) collected at the UTMDACC. We found that DPY30 expression was low in normal pancreatic tissues but high in PDAC, particularly in poorly differentiated tumors (Fig. 1L). Mirroring our preclinical observations with KPC tumors, low DPY30 expression in human PDAC was associated with better survival in the cohort of patients included in the TMA (Fig. 1M). To further investigate this association, we interrogated TCGA (43), the International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC; ref. 45), and Chen (46) PDAC datasets and found that high DPY30 expression was associated with significantly shorter overall patient survival (Fig. 1N–P). These data provide robust cross-species evidence that DPY30 expression is associated with PDAC progression. Importantly, loss of DPY30 suppresses proliferation only in immune-competent mice, suggesting that DPY30 promotes an immune-suppressive effect in vivo.

DPY30 is not essential for global H3K4 methylation in PDAC

To molecularly characterize the phenotypic effects of DPY30 loss in PDAC, we explored whether halting DPY30 or WDR5 expression in PDAC cells could differentially affect global H3K4 methylation. To this end, we compared the acute effects of loss of DPY30 or WDR5 on chromatin profiles in patient-derived PDAC cell lines through ATAC-seq, H3K27 acetylation (H3K27ac), and H3K4 tri-methylation (H3K4me3) ChIP-seq, the latter of which provides readouts of COMPASS activity at gene-regulatory regions (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. S3A and S3B; ref. 47). Cluster analysis of open genomic regions, as well as genomic regions marked by H3K4me3 and H3K27ac, revealed that WDR5-silenced cells exhibited chromatin states that were distinct from DPY30-silenced cells, which clustered with controls. This suggests that, unlike the effects observed upon WDR5 loss, the acute depletion of DPY30 is not sufficient to alter global chromatin accessibility and states (Fig. 2B–D). Consistently, WDR5, but not DPY30, downregulation affected the total levels of H3K4me3 and H3K4me1 histone marks (Supplementary Fig. S3B–S3D).

Furthermore, DPY30 silencing did not affect the pattern of H3K4me3 at regulatory regions proximal to gene TSSs (Fig. 2E). In contrast, WDR5 silencing significantly altered H3K4me3 patterns, resulting in the differential repression of thousands of genomic regions (Fig. 2E and F; Supplementary Table S1).

To gain insights into the role of DPY30 on the transcriptome, we then performed bulk RNA-seq analysis of PDAC cells with reduced DPY30 and WDR5 expression, as described above (Fig. 2G). WDR5, but not DPY30, silencing profoundly modulated the transcriptome of PDAC cells (Fig. 2G and H), which is consistent with the essential role of WDR5 in regulating global gene transcription (18, 22, 48). Specifically, and in line with our epigenomics analysis, WDR5 silencing induced a remarkable downmodulation of genes involved in the IFNα and γ response as well as a dysregulation of pathways involved in mRNA translation and RNA processing (Fig. 2H; Supplementary Table S2). Taken together, the data suggested that DPY30 mediates H3K4-independent effects, implying a noncanonical function in evoking an antitumor immune response in PDAC.

The WRAD core localizes to DNA replication fork

To explore putative new roles of DPY30, we immunoprecipitated endogenous DPY30 in human (PATC53 and PATC66) and mouse (KPC) PDAC cells. Unbiased proteomic analysis revealed that multiple top-scoring interactors of DPY30 are involved in chromatin remodeling, RNA metabolism, splicing, and gene transcription (Supplementary Fig. S4A–S4C; Supplementary Table S3), consistent with reported roles for DPY30 as a cofactor supporting H3K4 methylation and gene transcription regulation by COMPASS complexes (22–27). However, proteins involved in DNA replication, DNA damage, and cell-cycle regulation were also identified as top DPY30 interactors (Supplementary Fig. S4A–S4D; Supplementary Table S3). These included multiple members of the DNA replisome, including the MCM complex and WDHD1 (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. S4D). We validated these findings by expressing either mouse or human HA-tagged DPY30 with mouse or human MCM subunits and found that DPY30 binds with high affinity to MCM6 (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Fig. S4E). Additionally, we found that WDR5 also interacts with MCM complex members (Fig. 3C). Notably, PLA showed that both endogenous DPY30 and WDR5 lie in proximity (<40 nm) of MCM6 and WDHD1, validating that DPY30 and WDR5 directly interact with the DNA replisome machinery (Fig. 3D; Supplementary Fig. S4F and S4G). Importantly, endogenous WDR5, ASH2L, and RBBP5 could be coprecipitated with MCM6, suggesting that the whole WRAD core participates in this interaction (Supplementary Fig. S4H–S4J).

Next, we overexpressed a mutant isoform of DPY30 (L69D) known to hinder the DPY30:ASH2L interaction and the proper assembly of the WRAD core (49). Remarkably, in addition to disrupting the assembly of the WRAD core, DPY30L69D expression prevented the DPY30:MCM6 interaction (Fig. 3E; Supplementary Fig. S4K and S4L). We used a similar approach by overexpressing two WDR5-mutant isoforms (L240K and V268E) that have altered WDR5-binding motif domain and are known to disrupt the interaction of WDR5 with RBBP5 and cMyc (18). We consistently found that disrupting the WRAD core assembly strongly reduced the interaction between WDR5 and MCM6 (Fig. 3E and F; Supplementary Fig. S4K and S4L).

As the MCM complex is part of the replisome (50), we sought to test the ability of the WRAD to associate with nascent DNA replication forks. Using iPOND to recover proteins bound to newly synthesized DNA, we found that WRAD core members localize to DNA replication fork initiation sites (2 minutes EdU pulse) and persisted during DNA elongation (5–10 minutes EdU pulse) but disappeared after one-hour chase, demonstrating binding to nascent forks but not behind them at postreplicative sites (Fig. 3G). In contrast, histone H3, which is deposited after replication on newly synthesized DNA, gradually accumulated with strongest signals detected after chase (Fig. 3G). These data suggest that DPY30 associates and travels with ongoing DNA replication forks but shows no preference for global methyl deposition on chromatin.

Interestingly, destabilization of the WRAD core impeded endogenous binding of DPY30 to MCM6, whereas DPY30 loss did not destabilize MCM6 nor WDR5 occupancy at unperturbed replication forks, suggesting that WRAD core mediates DPY30–replisome interactions (Fig. 3H and I; Supplementary Fig. S4M and S4N). Consistent with this idea, analysis of the interaction between proteins and nascent DNA using a single cell-based method analogous to the SIRF assay (31) showed that WDR5 directly interacts with nascent DNA, further supporting a pivotal role of the WRAD core in DNA replication–related processes (Fig. 3J). Despite direct protein interactions between DPY30 and MCM6, the association of DPY30 to nascent DNA using this BrdU-SIRF approach was reduced compared with WDR5, which is likely a reflection of the relative protein position within the complexes (Fig. 3J).

DPY30 distinctly maintains H3K4 methylation at stressed forks

WDHD1, one of the strongest interactors of DPY30, is a replication stress response protein important for the stabilization of stalled forks and protection against their degradation (51). We therefore sought to examine DPY30’s role in affecting stressed DNA structures and performed DNA fiber assay in Dpy30-KO and control KPC cells. In unperturbed conditions, Dpy30-KO shows a marginal increase in replication speed (Fig. 4A). To amplify the phenotypes at intrinsically stressed forks, we challenged cells with hydroxyurea, which further stalls DNA replication forks. These more stringent stress conditions demonstrate that Dpy30-KO caused a strong fork protection defect displayed by hyperresection of stalled forks and impaired DNA replication restart as reported for fork protection defective tumors (Fig. 4B–D; refs. 35, 36, 52, 53).

We next tested fork reversal by fiber analysis using CPT, a topoisomerase I inhibitor, known to potently trigger DNA fork reversal and consequently slowing replication forks (Supplementary Fig. S5A; refs. 54, 55). Consistently with the robust induction of fork reversal, we observed a marked reduction in fork extension rate (CldU pulse, Supplementary Fig. S5A–S5C) accompanied by a mild nascent strand degradation (IdU pulse, Supplementary Fig. S5D) in CPT-treated control cells. In contrast, in Dpy30-depleted cells, the CPT-induced reduction in fork speed (CldU pulse) was significantly attenuated compared with controls, and no detectable fork degradation was observed (Supplementary Fig. S5A–S5D). Together, these findings indicate an impaired fork reversal response upon DPY30 loss.

iPOND experiment conducted by pulsing PATC66 cells with EdU either under in the presence of vehicle or replication stress–inducing agents [hydroxyurea (HU) or CPT] showed that DPY30 and ASH2L as well as MCM6 remained robustly associated with nascent DNA regardless of treatment (56). This strong and persistent retention suggests that DPY30 and the WRAD core play a role in maintaining stable loading of the replicative helicase at stalled and/or RFs, potentially facilitating efficient replication fork protection (Supplementary Fig. S5E). Notably, HU treatment led to an enrichment of PCNA on nascent DNA, reflecting the increased abundance of stalled replication forks, whereas CPT treatment resulted in a reduction of PCNA associated with nascent DNA, consistent with its displacement during replication fork reversal and formation of the regressed arm (57).

We next leveraged a recently developed RF-SIRF (RF - in situ protein interaction with nascent DNA replication forks) assay to detect RFs at a single-cell level (Supplementary Fig. S5F; ref. 33). Strikingly, our data showed that DPY30-KO cells, although incorporating more total EdU compared with control cells, displayed a remarkable impaired RF-SIRF signal, strongly supporting that DPY30 is required to stabilize RFs (Supplementary Fig. S5G–S5I).

Histone modifications play critical roles in transcriptional activation and regulation as well as in recruiting DNA stability factors at stressed DNA replication forks (6–9). We therefore leveraged the spatiotemporal and single-cell resolution of the SIRF assay to test for local chromatin states at stalled and reversed DNA forks in DPY30-KO PDAC cells (Fig. 4E; ref. 31). Strikingly and contrary to our global epigenomic analyses (Fig. 2), H3K4me3—the canonical methyl deposition mediated by the COMPASS complex known to mark transcriptionally active chromatin—was significantly depleted in DPY30-KO PDAC cells at nascent stressed DNA forks under conditions that promote stalled forks and fork reversal (H3K4me3-SIRF, Fig. 4F and G; Supplementary Fig. S5J). As we previously showed that H3K9me3 accumulate at stressed replication forks, consistently with heterochromatin formation causing replication fork slowing (33), we tested this modification in the same setting. Although DPY30 has no known direct role in methylating H3K9, its depletion also strongly reduced H3K9me3-SIRF signal at stalled and RFs, suggesting that the bivalent chromatin modification is necessary for stabilizing RFs (Fig. 4H and I; Supplementary Fig. S5K).

Notably, we found H3K4me3 and H3K9me3 interacting with sufficient proximity to cause robust and significantly elevated PLA signals under conditions that promote replication stress, suggesting that both histone modifications simultaneously occupy stressed forks (Fig. 4J and K; Supplementary Fig. S5L). Furthermore, global H3K4me3 deposition was not affected by DPY30 depletion in untreated and HU-treated cells. Interestingly, we observed an increased expression of H3K9me3 when cells were treated with HU or CPT compared with untreated cells, which was further induced by loss of DPY30 (Supplementary Fig. S5M and S5N). These results demonstrate a role for DPY30 in stabilizing and protecting reversed replication forks. Consistently, we observed that Dpy30-KO KPC cells showed stronger γH2AX (pS139) and pRPA2 (pS4-8) staining compared with control cells, suggesting that loss of DPY30 amplifies the endogenous replication stress conditions of PDAC cells even without additional external damage (Fig. 4L and M; Supplementary Fig. S6A and S6B). As replication stress can lead to CIN, we sought to explore changes in genome integrity in our PDAC models depleted for DPY30. Consistently with its proposed role in replication fork stabilization, DPY30-KO in PDAC models increased the percentage of cells with chromosome anaphase bridges, a consequence of aberrant replication stress resolution, missegregation, and micronuclei in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 4N–P; Supplementary Fig. S6C–S6G). These data support and extend our previous work (18) showing that the WDR5 silencing strongly increased DNA damage in PDAC cells. Overall, these observations suggest that PDAC cells can tolerate the added DNA stress caused by DPY30 loss but cannot survive the global transcriptomic rewiring upon WDR5 depletion.

DPY30 loss strongly recruits tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes by inducing the IFN type I signaling

Loss of replication fork stability activates proinflammatory pathways, enhancing the susceptibility of cancer cells to antitumor immunity (10, 11, 58). Specifically, defects in fork protection or loss of RFs activate a cGAS-mediated inflammation program (10, 59). In line with these observations and our in vivo data (Fig. 1B and C), PDAC cells depleted for DPY30 show an increased activation of the cGAS/STING/TBK1 axis compared with controls in vitro (Fig. 5A–D; Supplementary Fig. S6H–S6M).

Because TBK1 activates the IFN type I response stimulating immunogenicity (11, 60), we next determined the effects of Dpy30 loss on tumor cells and the tumor microenvironment (TME). GSEA from bulk RNA-seq data of Dpy30-KO and control KP−/−C tumors showed a positive enrichment score of pathways involved in DNA damage and repair process in immunodeficient mice, whereas the same cancer cells showed upregulation of gene pathways involved in inflammation, including IFN signaling, the complement cascade, and PD-1 signaling, in an immunocompetent background (Supplementary Fig. S7A and S7B; Supplementary Table S4).

To generate a comprehensive map of the functional effects of Dpy30 loss, we performed scRNA-seq on Dpy30-KO and control KPC tumors in immunocompetent mice (Fig. 5E). Dimensionality reduction analysis of subclusters of tumor cells revealed only a partial overlap between the transcriptomes of Dpy30-KO and control tumor cells (Fig. 5F and G). Specifically, we identified a population of highly proliferative tumor cells that was expanded in control tumors but minimally represented in Dpy30-KO tumors, suggesting that loss of Dpy30 distinctively sensitizes highly proliferative cells (Fig. 5H). Histopathologic analysis of proliferative index (pSer10 H3 and Ki67) analyzed in explanted tumors and expression analysis of cell-cycle regulators corroborated our observation, showing a reduced expression of proliferative genes (Ccnb2, Cdc20, Top2a, Aurka, Aurkb, and Wee1) only in Dpy30-KO tumors grown in immune competent avatars (Supplementary Fig. S7C–S7E). Furthermore, ssGSEA confirmed our bulk RNA-seq data, showing a remarkable upregulation of genes involved in IFN signaling and inflammatory response pathways upon Dpy30 depletion (Il6/Stat3, allograft rejection, and complement; Fig. 5I). Histopathologic and cytologic analyses confirmed a potent activation of STING in Dpy30-KO cells compared with controls in vivo (Fig. 5J). Remarkably, B2m, Cd74, H2-K1, and H2-D1, genes involved in antigen presentation, were among the top-scoring genes in Dpy30-KO cells (Fig. 5K). Altogether, these data suggest that Dpy30 loss can trigger an IFN-mediated immune response in vivo mediated through the cGAS/STING pathway.

Indeed, when comparing Dpy30-KO tumors with controls, immunostaining analysis confirmed an increased infiltration of CD3+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) and cancer associated-fibroblasts (CAF; αSMA+), the latter possibly the result of the depletion of the epithelial compartment (Fig. 5L; Supplementary Fig. S7F). Examination of the TME at the single-cell resolution revealed an increased number of CAFs, B cells, NK cells, and T cells in Dpy30-KO tumors compared with controls (Fig. 5M; Supplementary Fig. S7G and S7H). Importantly, compared with controls, CD8+ T cells in Dpy30-KO tumors displayed lower expression of exhaustion marker Tox and higher expression of cytotoxic markers such as Ifnγ, Gzmb, and natural killer (NK) cell granule protein 7 (Nkg7; Fig. 5N). These findings were corroborated by flow cytometry–based immunophenotyping, which demonstrated a significant increase in the number of all infiltrating T-cell subsets and NK cells in Dpy30-KO tumors. Among the immune cells, the CD8+ T cells were the predominant population, suggesting that they may be the primary contributor to the antitumor response (Fig. 5O and P). These findings suggest that CD8+ T cells are activated and may selectively clear Dpy30-KO tumor cells with increased immunogenicity.

DPY30 depletion unlocks CD8+ T cell–dependent tumor eradication

We next generated genome-tagged, barcoded KPC cells to track context-dependent clonal dynamics in vivo using our established platform (40, 61). Explanted tumors were processed by NGS for barcode detection. Pairwise comparison of common barcode composition of Dpy30-depleted versus control KPC cells grown in immunodeficient or immunocompetent mice showed a dramatic depletion of barcodes in the Dpy30-KO cells transplanted in immunocompetent mice compared with the immunodeficient counterpart (Fig. 6A and B). Furthermore, we injected Dpy30-KO or control KPC cells into immunocompetent mice and collected tumors at different time points. Multiplexed immunostaining analysis showed that, over time, the number of TILs strongly increased exclusively in Dpy30-KO tumors compared with controls (Fig. 6C–F). This immune infiltration, in the Dpy30-KO tumors, was paralleled by a net increase in the cleaved Caspase 3 (CC3) staining, a bona fide marker of apoptotic cell death, as well as a pronounced reduction in the number of Ki67+ tumor cells (Fig. 6C–F). To functionally determine whether CD8+ T cells are responsible for clearing cells Dpy30-depleted cancer cells, we injected Dpy30-KO or control KPC cells into immunocompetent mice and depleted the CD8+ T-cell population using an anti-CD8 mAb (Fig. 6G and H). We found that CD8+ T-cell depletion was sufficient to rescue the phenotype induced by Dpy30 loss, considerably accelerating tumor growth and reducing lifespan when compared with that of control mice (Fig. 6G and H).

To examine whether low levels of DPY30 may be associated with molecular features of activation of IFN signaling in human tumors, we queried the transcriptomic data of the TCGA, ICGC, and Chen PDAC patient cohorts (43, 45, 46). GSEA analyses showed that low DPY30 expression was associated with the increased expression of genes involved in the complement response, IFNα and IFNγ, and IL6/STAT3 signaling, as well as DNA damage and repair pathways (Fig. 6I; Supplementary Fig. S8A and S8B). We corroborated our data by querying the transcriptomic data of 47 PDAC PDXs established at UT MDACC. GSEA analysis showed that PDAC PDXs expressing low levels of DPY30 are enriched in genes belonging to the IFNα and IFNγ response pathways (Supplementary Fig. S8C). Altogether these data indicate that, in a mutated TP53/KRAS genetic background, DPY30 loss enhances the immunogenicity of PDAC cells through the activation of an IFN response and a consequent CD8+ T cell–mediated response.

To explore whether the activation of cytotoxic T cells upon Dpy30 loss may synergize with immunotherapy, we randomized transplanted mice to receive anti–PD-1 mAb or isotype control intraperitoneally 3 times a week for two consecutive weeks. Anti–PD-1 treatment dramatically reduced tumor growth and greatly extended the median survival of mice bearing Dpy30-KO tumors, with 60% of these animals showing complete tumor regression (Fig. 6J and K).

To assess the long-term effect of Dpy30 depletion on immune-mediated cell rejection, we performed a tumor rechallenge in vivo experiment. Briefly, control or Dpy30-KO KPC cells were injected subcutaneously into the left flank of C57BL/6 mice (n = 20 per cell line). One week after tumor implantation, mice were randomized to receive either isotype control antibody (n = 5) or anti–PD-1 mAb (8 mg/kg, 3 times per week for 2 weeks; n = 15). One week after the completion of immunotherapy, a subset of mice was sacrificed for tumor histologic analysis (n = 5 isotype control and n = 5 anti–PD-1), whereas the remaining 10 ICB-treated mice per group were rechallenged by subcutaneous injection of control KPC cells into the contralateral (right) flank (Fig. 6L; Supplementary Fig. S8D).

Consistent with our previous findings, in untreated groups, Dpy30-deficient KPC cells exhibited slower tumor growth compared with control cells. In addition, following anti–PD-1 treatment, only a small fraction of mice (20%) injected with Dpy30-KO KPC cells developed tumors, whereas all mice injected with control KPC cells and treated with anti–PD-1 formed tumors, albeit with delayed growth kinetics relative to untreated controls (Supplementary Fig. S8E and S8F).

Notably, all mice initially injected with control KPC cells and subsequently rechallenged had to be sacrificed shortly after due to excessive primary tumor burden, when secondary tumors were still growing (Fig. 6M–O; Supplementary Fig. S8G).

In contrast, among the 10 mice injected with Dpy30-KO KPC cells and rechallenged with control cells, three animals were sacrificed due to primary tumor burden, yet no secondary tumors were detected. Importantly, in the remaining seven mice, neither primary tumors nor rechallenge tumors developed during the observation period (Fig. 6M–O; Supplementary Fig. S8G).

These results suggest that Dpy30 depletion elicits a robust and durable antitumor immune response that extends beyond the initial tumor context and persists upon tumor rechallenge, even when secondary tumors are intact for Dpy30 expression. This observation is consistent with the establishment of a long-lasting, cross-reacting, tumor-specific immune memory. Importantly, this durable immune protection was observed specifically in the context of Dpy30 loss combined with ICB, supporting that DPY30 depletion enhances tumor immunogenicity and sensitizes otherwise poorly responsive tumors to immunotherapy.

Noteworthy, cross-species analysis of PDAC tumors leveraging the phase II PRINCE trial dataset, annotated for clinical efficacy of PD-1 inhibition (nivolumab) and transcriptomic data (15), demonstrated that patients with PDAC expressing low DPY30 levels showed a prolonged progression-free and overall survival compared with patients with PDAC with high DPY30 expression (Fig. 6P; Supplementary Fig. S8H and S8I).

Taken together, our data strongly suggest that DPY30 depletion activates an IFN-dependent CD8+ T-cell response, sensitizing PDAC to ICB with the single-agent anti–PD-1, thus offering a relevant clinical opportunity to boost antitumor immunity, particularly in advanced malignancies that are intrinsically resistant to ICB.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrate an unanticipated function for DPY30 as an effector of replication fork protection by promoting H3K4 trimethylation and stabilization of stalled and reversed replications forks. Interestingly, our study reveals that both the transcriptionally “active” H3K4me3 and “repressive” H3K9me3 histone marks colocalize at stressed replication forks. Although epigenetic marks control gene expression and play essential roles during DNA stress responses, our results illuminate differences between these epigenetic languages. During stress responses, the simultaneous presence of H3K4me3 and H3K9me3 is essential for sustaining fork stability, highlighting an unappreciated replication stress-specific epigenetic code distinct from that used during global transcriptional control. Importantly, the data identify DPY30 as a molecular switch for WRAD/MLL complexes to act at stressed replication forks, providing an unprecedented opportunity to control epigenetically regulated replication processes without affecting global chromatin state.

Oncogenic KRAS-induced replication stress slows replication by heterochromatin formation (62). Although fork reversal is generally considered a protective mechanism, its imbalance disrupts replication stress homeostasis and promotes CIN, which is frequently observed during PDAC progression, particularly in advanced stages (16, 34, 63). Interestingly, DPY30 expression is upregulated during tumor progression, with poorly differentiated PDACs displaying the highest expression. This increase suggests that DPY30, by facilitating heterochromatin formation at stressed forks, could cooperate with oncogenic KRAS to attenuate oncogene-induced replication stress. Thus, the study of DPY30 in the context of PDAC, characterized by intrinsically stressed forks, may have enabled the discovery of its dynamic function. Importantly, our data show that targeting DPY30, which destabilizes stressed replication forks, can act as a molecular time machine to reactivate the immune response and sensitize tumors to ICB.

DPY30 expression is essential for the normal development and differentiation of embryonic stem cells as well as for hematopoietic, pancreatic, and neural progenitor/stem cells, all cells with proliferation being a common denominator (22, 23, 25–27). The necessity of DPY30 expression in these cell types has been dogmatically attributed to the role of the MLL/WRAD complex for transcription activation (22–27). Yet, replication stress accumulates in proliferative cells, especially during embryogenesis, tumorigenesis, and aging (64). Consistently, reversed replication forks accumulate disproportionally in stem cells, cancer cells, and aged cells (64). Our discovery of DPY30 in selectively stabilizing stressed replication forks supports a reevaluation for its role in stem and progenitor cells, which will be exciting to explore in future studies.

It is well established that genome instability is a double-edge sword, promoting inflammation, while fueling cancer evolution (13, 65). Indeed, although CIN is a hallmark of PDAC genomes, these tumors display an immunosuppressive environment, underscoring that undefined genomic instability is an unsatisfactory biomarker for accurately predicting immunogenicity that drives ICB response (13, 65). Our data show that stressed fork instability is a critical immune-promoting factor, and—as we propose here—it is distinct from broad genome instability.

Taken together our results identify DPY30 as an epigenetic suppressor of DNA fork instability and a distinct immunoediting driver. Accordingly, DPY30 loss leads to the accumulation of PDAC cells with complex karyotypes, which, in turn, triggers the selective clearance of damaged proliferating cells by CD8+ T cells. Considering the paucity of treatment options for patients with PDAC, our study provides a rationale for targeting the SET1/MLL COMPASS, via DPY30, as a precise and selective therapeutic strategy, particularly in combination with immunotherapy.
