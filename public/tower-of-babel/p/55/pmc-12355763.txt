Deciphering the methylation landscape of long noncoding RNAs in hepatocellular carcinoma: a focus on LINC00942
Authors: Zhaoqi Shi, Xiaolong Liu, Duguang Li, Jing Yang, Haonan Chen, Hui Lin, Xiaoxiao Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConduct a comprehensive genome-wide methylomic analysis of long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) genes in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and identify specific lncRNAs for subsequent functional validation.MethodsWe conducted a methylation microarray analysis on 11 HCC samples alongside adjacent non-tumor tissue specimens. This analysis was integrated with The Cancer Genome Atlas data and the Gene Expression Omnibus for a comprehensive methylomic evaluation. Following this, a LASSO retrospective analysis was employed to develop a prognostic model for HCC, utilizing the methylation levels of specific lncRNA genes. Ultimately, LINC00942 was identified as a significant candidate within the model, and its biological effects in HCC cells were investigated through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Additionally, potential downstream target genes of LINC00942 were elucidated using western blot analysis.ResultsGenome-wide hypomethylation and CpG island hypermethylation within lncRNA genes were observed in HCC. Furthermore, a prognostic model utilizing the methylation levels of five specific lncRNA genes has demonstrated high accuracy in predicting the prognosis of patients diagnosed with HCC. LINC00942, an identified lncRNA within the model, exhibits hypomethylation and elevated expression levels in HCC. Methylation within the promoter region has been shown to suppress its expression in HCC cells. Both in vivo and in vitro studies have demonstrated that the silencing of LINC00942 leads to a decrease in the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cells. Flow cytometry analyses revealed a significant increase in cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in HCC cells following the knockdown of LINC00942. The result of western blot indicates that cyclin D1, CDK2, and BAX are probable downstream target molecules of LINC00942.ConclusionsA substantial quantity of lncRNAs is transcriptionally modulated by methylation in HCC. The demethylation of LINC00942 within its promoter region facilitates the upregulation of LINC00942 expression, thereby contributing to its function as an oncogene in HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-01952-7.

Introduction

Long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) is a category of transcripts that exceed 200 nucleotides in length and possess no protein-coding capability [1]. Initially, lncRNAs were regarded as"junk fragments"generated during gene transcription and had no biological functions. In the 1990 s, Borsani identified the involvement of lncRNA Xist in transcriptional silencing of the X chromosome in female mammals [2]. Since then, researchers have focused on these small molecules, and increasing evidence suggests that lncRNAs are not products of transcriptional noise but are specifically regulated and conserved [3]. The functional mechanisms of lncRNAs are diverse; they can act as scaffolds, decoys, or signals and can act through genomic targeting, regulation in cis or trans, and antisense interference [4].

To better identify lncRNAs in the human genome, the GENECODE database comprehensively annotated all lncRNAs in the human genome in 2012. The annotation results included 9277 manually annotated lncRNA genes, yielding 14880 transcripts [5]. The complex functional mechanism and the large number of lncRNAs lead to the diversity of their biological functions in cancer. In HCC, lncRNAs were reported to be associated with cell proliferation, metastasis, and invasion, energy metabolism, stem cell differentiation, immune escape, tumor drug resistance, and other biological processes [6]. While regulating the transcription of coding genes, the transcription of lncRNAs themselves is also regulated by various factors, such as epigenetic regulation, transcription factor activation, and RNA processing [7]. Aberrant methylation modification of certain particular lncRNA genes could be crucial events correlating with unfavorable outcomes in HCC [8]. Promoter hypermethylation of the lncRNA-MEG3 gene is identified, and lncRNA-MEG3 expression is increased with inhibition of methylation with either 5-aza-2-deoxycytidine or siRNA to DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) 1 and 3b in HCC cells [9]. Most of the existing studies focused on the regulation of methylation on a single lncRNA gene, and the genome-wide alterations of lncRNA genes have not been fully elucidated in HCC.

In this study, we performed a genome-wide methylomic analysis based on the Illumina HM450K methylation chip detection of eleven HCC tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues (ANTs). Then, our in-house dataset, the TCGA dataset, and one GEO dataset were jointly analyzed to screen out differentially expressed and differentially methylated lncRNAs in HCC. We further constructed a prognostic model for HCC patients based on the methylation levels of these lncRNA genes. Finally, LINC00942 was selected to verify its transcriptional regulation and biological function in HCC cells.

Methods

Patient samples and clinical data collection

Eleven HCC tissues and ANTs were collected in the Shanghai Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital. The inclusion criteria were patients who were diagnosed with HCC and received curative surgery. All samples underwent strict histopathological evaluation to ensure diagnostic accuracy and ensured the HCC tissues contained more than 80% tumor cells. The exclusion criteria were patients who received preoperative treatment for HCC or had a history of other cancers. All tissues were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until DNA was extracted. The samples were then used for Illumina HM450K methylation chip microarray analysis. Our study was conducted in strict compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013) and was approved by the Ethics Committees of the hospitals. Informed consent was obtained from all included patients.

DNA extraction and bisulfite conversion

A QIAamp DNAMini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) was used to extract genomic DNA from ≥ 25 mg primary cancerous tissues and ANTs. The concentrations and quality of all DNA samples were detected using a Thermo NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Then, 400–500 ng of DNA was taken for bisulfite treatment using an EpiTect Fast DNA Bisulfite Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany).

Methylation microarray analysis

500 ng bisulfite-treated DNA was employed for genome-wide methylation analysis via the Infinium HM450 BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Subsequent normalization and filtration of the raw methylation matrices were executed in R (v3.4.3; https://www.r-project.org/) utilizing the Chip Analysis Methylation Pipeline (ChAMP) package [10]. The microarray results were expressed as the percentage of methylation signal intensity at different sites by β value. β = M/(M + U + O), where U represents the unmethylated signal intensity, M represents the methylated signal intensity, and O is the offset to prevent the occurrence of zero as a denominator. The Infinium HM450 BeadChip contained 485577 CpG sites. After annotation of the BeadChip according to the ENCODE database (human genome GRCh38, version 42), a total of 11928 CpG sites were corresponded to lncRNA genes and selected for further analysis. Differentially methylated CpG sites that corresponded to lncRNA genes were further screened from these 11928 CpG sites by calculating the difference in methylation level of each CpG site between HCC tissues and ANTs. The criteria for the difference were set as: |delta methylation difference|> 10% and P < 0.05. The volcano plot was drawn using GraphPad Prism 9. The heat map was drawn using the R package “heatmap.”

Online datasets and samples

Online HCC methylation datasets from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE77269) were used for conjoint analysis, along with our in-house methylation microarray data. A total of 20 HCC tissues and ANTs were included in the GSE77269 database. In total, 369 HCC tissues and 49 ANTS were included in the TCGA database. These two datasets used Illumina HM450 BeadChip, and methylation differences were analyzed in the same way as our in-house database. For different lncRNA gene expressions in the TCGA database, the limma package in the R software was utilized at the cutoff criteria of P < 0.05 and fold change (FC) > 2.0. The heat map of differentially expressed lncRNA genes was drawn using the R package “heatmap.”

Construction of the prognostic model

LncRNA genes with both methylation and expression differences were screened for prognostic model construction. Since one lncRNA gene often corresponded to multiple CpG sites, we calculated the mean methylation value of all CpG sites that correspond to a single lncRNA gene as the methylation level of that lncRNA gene. The prognostic model was constructed based on the methylation levels of these lncRNA genes and prognostic information of HCC patients in the TCGA database. Patients without lncRNA methylation data or prognostic information were excluded. Patients were allocated to the training and validation groups in a 7:3 ratio. LASSO regression analysis was applied to the training group to reduce the likelihood of overfitting and to narrow down the lncRNA candidates [11]. Next, a multivariate Cox regression model was used to identify lncRNA genes most related to survival, from which the risk models were constructed. The risk score for each HCC patient was calculated using the formula below: Risk score = Coef lncRNA1 × lncRNA1 methylation + Coef lncRNA2 × lncRNA2 methylation + ··· + Coef lncRNAn × lncRNAn methylation. Coef represented the coefficient value of the corresponding lncRNA.

Evaluation of the prognostic model

The prognostic model was evaluated in both the training and validation groups. Patients were categorized into low-risk and high-risk groups based on the median risk score. Then, principal component analysis (PCA) was used to explore potential differences between high-risk and low-risk groups [12]. The model's performance and prognostic ability were evaluated using time-dependent ROC analyses and Kaplan–Meier (K–M) log-rank tests, via the R packages “timeROC” and “survival” [13, 14]. An area under the curve (AUC) of greater than 0.7 was considered predictive.

Cell lines and reagents

The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines HepG2 and Huh7 and tool cell HEK293T were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) at 37 °C in humidified air with 5% CO2. Decitabine (NSC-127716) was purchased from MCE.

Decitabine treatment

HCC cells were pre-cultured to 10–25% confluence and then cultured with decitabine (DAC, Targetmol)-containing medium at different concentrations (0 μM, 5 μM, and 10 μM) for three days. DAC was diluted in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma). The medium was refreshed every three days.

Lentiviral vector construction and transfection

Incorporation of sh-LINC00942-1, sh-LINC00942-2, and scrambled shRNA into the pLKO.1 plasmid was conducted, followed by transient transfection of HEK293T cells with lentiviral vectors, packaging, and envelope plasmids MD2G and PAX2 using Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (L3000015; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) for lentivirus supernatant production. Viral supernatants were subsequently harvested, filtered through a 0.22 μM filter (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), and ultracentrifuged. After lentivirus transfection for 48 h, HCC cells were subjected to puromycin selection at 1 μg/mL. The short hairpin RNA sequences for LINC00942 knockdown were as follows:

sh-LINC00942-1: GCTGGCTCCTGCTTGGAAACT;

sh-LINC00942-2: GCTTGGAATTGATGACGTTTC.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

RNA extraction was performed using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (AG21023, Accurate Biology). Reverse transcription was executed following the protocol of the Eco M-MLV RT Premix kit (AG11706, Accurate Biology). The target gene expression was normalized to the endogenous control gene GAPDH. RT-qPCR was performed using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Tag HS qPCR kit (AG11701, Accurate Biology) on the QuantStudio 1 machine (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The RT-qPCR primer sequences were as follows:

LINC00942-F: GGTGTCTGCGGGAAACAGTAC,

LINC00942-R: GAACAAAGAGTCAGGTTGTGTGG;

GAPDH-F: CTCTGCTCCTCCTGTTCGAC,

GAPDH-R: ACCAAATCCGTTGACTCCGA.

Methylation-specific PCR (MSP)

Firstly, cellular genomic DNA was collected using the Universal Genomic DNA Purification Mini Spin Kit (D0063, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) following the provided instructions. Subsequently, the EpiArt® DNA Methylation Bisulfite Kit (EM101, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was employed to perform the sulfite conversion of DNA and to purify it. To perform the sulfite conversion of DNA, 20 μl of DNA (approximately 100 pg–2 μg) was mixed with 130 μl of CT Conversion Mix. The mixture was then placed in a PCR apparatus and processed according to the program settings. Following this, the product was purified. To collect the purified DNA filtrate, 10–20 μl of E-Elution Buffer was added to the center of the adsorption column membrane. The cells were allowed to stand for 1–2 min at room temperature and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min, and the purified DNA filtrate was collected. Subsequently, primers targeting methylated and unmethylated sequences were designed using MethPrimer (http://www.urogene.org/methprimer/) and amplified through PCR. The methylation status of DNA sequences complementary to the primers was determined by agarose gel electrophoresis. The specific primers targeted methylated and unmethylated sequences were as follows:

LINC00942-M-F: TAATTTTTTTTGTTGTTTTTAGCGT,

LINC00942-M-R: TATAAAAATTTCTCAAACATTCGAT;

LINC00942-U-F: TTTTAATTTTTTTTGTTGTTTTTAGTGT,

LINC00942-U-R: TATAAAAATTTCTCAAACATTCAAT.

Cell viability and proliferation assay

A total of 1000 HCC cells per well were planted into a 96-well plate and allowed to attach for 24 h. Then, 100 mL of fresh medium containing 10% CCK8 solution (MA0218, Meilunbio, China) was added at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 days after planking, and the 450-nm absorbance was detected following incubation for 2 h at 37 °C using a spectrophotometer (Multiskan Spectrum 1500, Thermo, USA).

Transwell cell migration and invasion assay

HepG2 and Huh7 cells were seeded into the upper compartments of 24-well plates at a density of 30,000 cells per well, using 100 μl of serum-free DMEM. In the lower compartment of the plate, 0.6 ml of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS was added as a chemoattractant. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Following the incubation period, the cells on the lower side of the insert membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and then stained with 1% crystal violet for an additional 20 min. The inserts were rinsed with PBS for several seconds to remove excess dye and then allowed to air dry completely. Pictures of the lower side of the filter were captured under a microscope. For the transwell invasion assay, 50 μl of 10% ECM gel (40183ES08, Yeason, China) was added to the upper compartment of the insert, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to allow the liquid ECM gel to solidify. The remaining steps followed the protocol described above.

Cell cycle flow cytometry

A total of 5 × 105 cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, each group was treated with 1 mL of DNA staining solution containing 10 μL of permeabilization solution (Cell Cycle Staining Kit, CCS012, MultiSciences, Hangzhou, China) and incubated for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. The samples were then subjected to flow cytometry analysis using a BD LSRFortessa instrument (New Jersey, NJ, USA). The results were analyzed using the “FlowJo” software.

Cell apoptosis flow cytometry

Cells inoculated in 6-well plates were collected (about 1–10 × 105 cells, including those in the culture supernatant) and washed twice with PBS. Cells were resuspended in 500 μl of 1 × Binding Buffer. 5 μl Annexin V-APC and 10 μl PI were added to each tube (Annexin V-APC/PI Apoptosis Kit, AT107, MultiSciences, Hangzhou, China). After gently vortexing and thorough mixing, the mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 5–10 min. Following this incubation period, flow cytometry analysis was carried out, and the results were analyzed using the “FlowJo” software.

Western blotting and antibodies

Cell protein extraction was performed using RIPA buffer (Fude Biotech, China) with protease inhibitors added. Protein concentrations were measured with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Meilunbio, China). Then, 10–20 μg of protein was loaded onto a sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel for electrophoresis and transferred to a 0.22- or 0.45-µm-pore-sized PVDF membrane. The PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h and then incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were as follows: GAPDH (AC002, Abclonal, Wuhan, China), α-tubulin (ab7291, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Cell Cycle (CDK2, CDK6, Cyclin B1, Cyclin D1) Antibody Sampler Panel (ab228528, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Pro-Apoptosis BCL-Family (Bad, Bax, Bcl-2), Antibody Sampler Panel (ab228527, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and Active Caspase-3 Antibody (SR01-02, Thermo Fisher, MA, USA). Afterward, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) western blotting substrate (Fude Biotech, China).

Establishment of hepatoma xenograft model

The male BALB/c nude mice (n = 5, 4 weeks old, weighing ~ 20 g) were purchased from the Shanghai Laboratory Animal Center and fed in a pathogen-free environment at the Laboratory Animal Research Center of SRRSH. All animal experiments were strictly designed and executed according to the guide approved by the Committee on the Use of Live Animals in Teaching and Research at SRRSH. We injected 1 × 107 control or sh-LINC00942-treated HepG2 cells in 200 μL PBS into the groin of nude mice to form murine subcutaneous tumors. The tumor volumes were measured every two days, which were calculated as follows: V tumor = L × W2/2, where L and W were, respectively, the length and width of the tumor. The mice were killed 15 days after injection, and the tumors were isolated, weighed, and photographed.

Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) staining

H&E staining was used to observe the histological structure of subcutaneous tumors in mice. Tumors were excised and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde. Following fixation, the tissues were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5-µm-thick sections for preservation. After deparaffinization and gradient hydration, the paraffin sections were stained with hematoxylin for 2 min, washed with PBS, and then stained with eosin for 1 min. The stained sections were dehydrated using a gradient of alcohol and preserved with neutral gum sealing. Images were captured under a light microscope for observation.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC was used to evaluate Ki-67 expression in tumoral hepatic tissues with and without LINC00942 knockdown. Following paraffin embedding, 5-mm sections of tissue samples were prepared. The sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated. For antigen retrieval, they were immersed in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and heated in a microwave oven for 10 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was then inhibited using 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. To block non-specific binding, the sections were treated with 10% normal goat serum for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-Ki-67 antibody (1:500, A20018, Abclonal, Wuhan, China). Following this, they were incubated with a secondary antibody, and Ki-67 expression in the tissues was evaluated via microscopy after diaminobenzidine and hematoxylin staining.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 21.0, GraphPad Prism 9, and RStudio were used to conduct all statistical analyses. Statistical significance between the two groups was calculated with a two-sided Student’s t-test. Variances among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. A difference in methylation level was defined as the absolute value of the difference in methylation level between HCC tissues and ANTs greater than 10% and a P value less than 0.05. The correlations between methylation levels and methylation level of lncRNA genes were assessed by Pearson correlation analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a p value < 0.05.

Genome-wide methylation analysis of lncRNA genes in HCC tissues

The average methylation level across all 11 HCC tissues, encompassing 11,928 CpG sites, was determined to be 0.3482 ± 0.046, which is significantly lower than the average observed in ANTs, recorded at 0.3850 ± 0.0071 (p = 0.030, Fig. 1a). Subsequently, a subgroup analysis was conducted on the 11,928 CpG sites, categorized by gene regions and CpG regions, respectively. In terms of gene regions, the average methylation levels of all CpG sites within each region were markedly lower in HCC tissues compared to ANTs (Fig. 1b). Conversely, when examining different CpG regions, the average methylation level of all CpG sites within the CpG island region was significantly higher in HCC tissues than in ANTs (0.1745 vs. 0.2030, P = 0.023, Fig. 1c). No significant difference was observed in the average methylation level of all CpG sites within the N-shore region between HCC tissues and ANTs (Fig. 1c). Subsequently, we analyzed the differentially methylated CpG sites in HCC tissues. Our findings revealed a total of 528 hypermethylated CpG sites and 1887 hypomethylated CpG sites (Fig. 1d). We further investigated the distribution of these differentially methylated sites across various gene and CpG regions. Notably, there was a higher frequency of differential hypomethylation sites compared to differential hypermethylation sites across the various gene regions (Fig. 1E). To validate the results obtained from our database analysis, we performed analyses utilizing the TCGA database and a GEO dataset (GSE77269), employing the same methodological approach. The generated volcano plots indicated that the distributions of differentially methylated CpG sites across the three databases were remarkably similar (Fig. 1g, h). Upon intersecting the differentially methylated CpG sites identified in the three databases, we identified a total of 1130 distinct differentially methylated CpG sites, which were associated with 335 lncRNA genes (Fig. 1I).

This analysis resulted in the selection of 774 differentially expressed lncRNA genes (Fig. 2a). Subsequently, these 774 lncRNA genes were cross-referenced with 335 differentially methylated lncRNA genes identified in a prior analysis. The risk score for each patient within the training cohort was computed using the specified formula, subsequently categorizing all patients into high- and low-risk score groups based on the median risk score. The distribution patterns of these risk scores, along with the survival outcomes of patients in the training cohort, were analyzed (Fig. 2d, e). PCA demonstrated a clear distinction between the high- and low-risk score groups (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, the Kaplan–Meier survival curve revealed that high-risk vs. low-risk groups exhibited a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.45 (95% CI 1.68–3.58; p < 0.001), demonstrating robust prognostic discrimination (Fig. 2g). Subsequently, ROC analysis was conducted to assess the sensitivity and specificity of the prognostic model, yielding AUC values for predicting OS at 1, 3, and 5 years of 0.73 (95% CI 0.65–0.80), 0.75 (95% CI 0.66–0.83), and 0.76 (95% CI 0.63–0.87), respectively (Fig. 2h).

The prognostic model underwent further assessment within a validated cohort comprising 102 HCC patients sourced from the TCGA database. The risk score for each patient was computed utilizing a consistent formula. Subsequently, the patients in the validation cohort were categorized into low-risk and high-risk groups based on the median risk score (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). Notably, the two groups exhibited significant discrimination, as demonstrated by PCA (Supplementary Fig. 1 C). The Kaplan–Meier curve indicated that patients classified in the low-risk group experienced a more favorable prognosis compared to those in the high-risk group (Supplementary Fig. 1D, P < 0.01). The AUC values for predicting OS at 1, 3, and 5 years were recorded at 0.70 (95% CI 0.57–0.82), 0.72 (95% CI 0.58–0.85), and 0.70 (95% CI 0.51–0.87), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1E).

LINC00942 is upregulated by DNA methylation in HCC and associated with poor prognosis of HCC patients

We performed a comprehensive search of the PubMed database concerning the five lncRNAs included in the prognostic model. Our findings indicated a notable absence of studies addressing the regulatory effects of methylation on LINC00942, as well as the biological functions of LINC00942 in HCC. As a result, we have decided to concentrate our efforts on a more in-depth investigation of the characteristics of LINC00942 in the context of HCC.

In the initial phase of our study, we evaluated the methylation and expression profiles of the LINC00942 gene in patients with HCC utilizing data sourced from the TCGA database. Discussion

The biosynthesis process of lncRNAs is highly similar to that of protein-coding transcripts, which is mainly regulated by epigenetic modification, recruitment of transcription factor complexes, and RNA processing [5]. A large part of lncRNAs share promoters with protein-coding genes, and their transcription process is also synergistic with the transcription of protein-coding genes during embryonic development [15]. This indicates that DNA methylation in the promoter region may play an important regulatory role in lncRNA transcription. Up to now, only a few studies have reported the transcriptional regulation of lncRNA genes by DNA methylation in HCC. Ma et al. [16] reported that they identified a lncRNA, termed metabolism-induced tumor activator 1 (MITA1), which is upregulated in HCC and contributes to metastasis. DNA methylation within 2nd intron is involved in energy stress-induced MITA1 expression. Another study reported that MEG3 was a tumor suppressor lncRNA gene in HCC. MEG3 promoter hypermethylation was identified by methylation-specific PCR, and MEG3 expression was increased with inhibition of methylation with either 5-Aza-2-Deoxycytidine or siRNA to DNMT1 and 3b in HCC cells [9]. Zheng [17] found that lncRNA SRHC was hypo-expressed in HCC tissues and played a tumor suppression role in HCC cell lines. The promoter region of SRHC contains a CpG-rich island, and the downregulation of SRHC is caused by DNA methylation in HCC. All these studies focused on DNA methylation regulation of single lncRNA genes, and the epigenomic alterations of lncRNA genes in HCC have not been fully elucidated.

In this investigation, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of genome-wide methylation in 11 patients diagnosed with HCC and subsequently identified lncRNA gene-associated methylation sites for further examination. Our findings revealed that lncRNA genes exhibited a general trend of hypomethylation within HCC tissues. However, within the CpG regions, lncRNA genes demonstrated significant hypermethylation, with the proportion of differentially hypermethylated CpG sites surpassing that of differentially hypomethylated sites. These observations align with previous research on genome-wide methylation patterns in coding genes. For instance, Nishida et al. [18] identified 38,330 CpG sites with notable differences in methylation levels between 59 HCC tissues and 58 ANTs, where 92% of the sites were hypomethylated, and only 3,051 CpG sites (8%) were hypermethylated in HCC. Notably, the hypermethylated CpG sites in HCC were predominantly situated within promoter regions and CpG islands [18]. The global hypomethylation of DNA, frequently observed in cancer, is believed to be linked to genomic instability, which may facilitate carcinogenesis [19]. Conversely, promoter hypermethylation is associated with the inactivation of corresponding tumor suppressor genes. Our study suggests that lncRNA genes may exhibit similar genome-wide methylation alterations as coding genes in the context of HCC carcinogenesis.

In addition to conducting a genome-wide methylomic analysis, we developed a prognostic model based on the methylation levels of lncRNA genes for patients with HCC. Previous models predicting liver cancer prognoses largely relied on clinical and pathological features or gene expression levels. Our model, in contrast, is based on lncRNA gene methylation levels. DNA methylation is a stable and reliable biomarker, less influenced by tissue handling and technical variability. It is detectable in various sample types, especially formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissues. Since methylation changes often occur early in carcinogenesis, they may aid early diagnosis and prognosis. Our model offers a novel approach to HCC prognosis, improving risk stratification and offering insights into lncRNA epigenetic regulation in HCC progression.

Our model also demonstrates higher predictive accuracy compared to similar methylation-based models. A prior investigation by Lin established a prognostic model for HCC patients utilizing the methylation levels of lncRNA gene-associated CpG sites rather than the lncRNA genes themselves [20]. In that study, ten CpG sites were identified through LASSO selection to construct the model, yielding AUC values of 0.709, 0.637, and 0.679 for predicting OS at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively. Notably, the LASSO analysis was conducted using a single training set, and the model's predictive performance was not evaluated using a validation dataset. In contrast, our research employed the methylation levels of specific lncRNA genes for the construction of the prognostic model. We performed differential methylation analysis across three datasets, which demonstrated strong concordance (Fig. 1I), thereby enhancing the reliability of our findings. Our LASSO analysis incorporated both a training set and a validation set, with the prognostic model being developed in the training set and subsequently validated in the validation set. The AUC values for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS of HCC patients were 0.73, 0.75, and 0.76 in the training set and 0.70, 0.72, and 0.70 in the validation set, respectively. Su et al. [21] and Wang et al. [22] developed two prognostic models for HCC based on m6A methylation-regulated genes. Su et al. achieved AUC values of 0.669 for 3 years, 0.685 for 4 years, and 0.59 for 5 years using the same TCGA validation cohort. Meanwhile, Wang et al.'s m6A model obtained an overall survival prediction AUC of 0.667. Consequently, our model exhibited greater reliability and improved predictive efficiency.

LINC00942 is a lncRNA primarily localized in the cytoplasm. It was initially identified as a direct binding partner of the methyltransferase METTL4, facilitating METTL14-mediated RNA N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation in breast cancer [23]. Additionally, LINC00942 has been implicated as an oncogene in lung adenocarcinoma, where it interacts with miR-5006-5p to enhance the expression of FZD1, a direct target of this microRNA [24]. Recent studies have also linked LINC00942 to chemotherapy resistance in gastric cancer cells. Specifically, LINC00942 upregulates MSI2 expression by inhibiting its interaction with the SCFβ-TRCPE3 ubiquitin ligase, thereby preventing ubiquitination. As a potential m6A recognition protein, MSI2 stabilizes c-Myc mRNA with m6A modifications, which contributes to reduced apoptosis and the promotion of stemness in gastric cancer cells [25]. Furthermore, LINC00942 recruits the RNA methyltransferase METTL3 to enhance m6A deposition on DNMT3a transcripts, leading to the recognition of modified mRNA by IGF2BP3/HuR, which reinforces mRNA stability and results in the inhibition of autophagy and chemoresistance in gastric cancer cells [26]. Jin et al. [27] demonstrated that LINC00942 enhances SLC7A11 mRNA stability in an m6A-dependent manner by recruiting IGF2BP3, and promotes hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression via ferroptosis inhibition and Treg-mediated immunosuppression. Previous studies have concentrated on the downstream biological functions of LINC00942 across different cancers. Our study, however, explores its upstream regulatory mechanisms, establishing that LINC00942 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma is modulated by promoter methylation. Furthermore, we provide evidence that LINC00942's oncogenic role in liver cancer is closely associated with the regulation of cell cycle progression and apoptosis.

Our study has certain limitations. Firstly, the 11 patients included were all male, which represents a significant selection bias, and it is currently unclear whether sex impacts lncRNA gene methylation. We plan to include more female patients in future research to validate our findings and ensure their generalizability. Second, in the cross-cohort bioinformatics analysis, substantial discrepancies in sample sizes among the in-house, TCGA, and GEO datasets precluded direct integration of the cohorts. As a result, each dataset was analyzed independently, and the final list of differentially methylated lncRNA genes (including LINC00942) was derived by intersecting the respective results. While this approach enhanced the robustness of findings within individual datasets, it inherently bypassed key statistical adjustments for inter-cohort variability—namely batch effect correction (e.g., ComBat, SVA) and multiple testing correction (e.g., FDR). This limitation introduces the potential for residual confounding, inflated false positive or false negative rates among the 1130 identified CpG sites, and reduced generalizability of the integrative conclusions. Future studies employing harmonized, multi-cohort designs with rigorous correction protocols are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

In HCC, a notable phenomenon of genome-wide hypomethylation alongside hypermethylation of CpG islands within lncRNA genes has been documented. The methylation status of lncRNA genes is correlated with the prognosis of HCC patients and may serve as a basis for developing prognostic models. Specifically, hypomethylation of the promoter region of LINC00942 results in its overexpression in HCC cells. Furthermore, LINC00942 has been shown to enhance the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HCC cells by inhibiting cell cycle arrest and apoptosis.
