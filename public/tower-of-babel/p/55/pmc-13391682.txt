Nociception index–monitored extubation is associated with improved hemodynamic stability and recovery efficiency in the post-anesthesia care unit: a single-center randomized controlled trial
Authors: Qian Qian, Yingjie Zhou, Lulu Chou, Wenmin Hu, Weilian Cai, Huijia Lin, Yinghui Wang, Liuqing Chen, Ping Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtubation in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) is a high-risk transition, frequently accompanied by hemodynamic fluctuations and respiratory events. Current practice relies mainly on subjective assessments and the bispectral index (BIS), which reflects cortical activity but not nociceptive responses. The electroencephalography-derived Nociception Index (NOX) may provide an objective measure of nociception–analgesia balance during extubation. This study aimed to evaluate whether NOX-monitored extubation improves hemodynamic stability and recovery efficiency in the PACU. In this single-center randomized controlled trial (July 2023–March 2025), 120 patients undergoing surgery with postoperative intubation were randomized to NOX-monitored extubation (n = 60) or standard monitoring (n = 60). Extubation criteria included consciousness, tidal volume ≥ 5 mL/kg, SpO₂ ≥90%, hemodynamic stability (SBP < 180 mmHg, HR < 100 bpm). Hemodynamic and respiratory parameters, extubation timing, PACU discharge time, and complications were compared between groups. Baseline characteristics were comparable except for higher hypertension prevalence in the NOX group (21.7% vs. 0%). At awakening, the NOX group showed attenuated blood-pressure responses (SBP − 7.3%, DBP − 9.0% vs. controls; both p < 0.05). PACU discharge was faster in the NOX group (median 27.5 vs. 50.5 min; p < 0.001; ≈42% reduction). However, greater heart-rate increases (+ 14.5 vs. + 7.4 bpm; p = 0.008) and oxygen desaturation (–2.28% vs. − 0.70%; p < 0.001) were observed. Complication rates were similar (11.7% vs. 18.3%; p = 0.306). NOX-monitored extubation improved hemodynamic stability and reduced PACU recovery time but was associated with paradoxical cardiorespiratory trade-offs. Introduction

The post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) represents a critical transition phase where patients shift from mechanical ventilation to spontaneous breathing following tracheal extubation [1, 2]. Extubation is inherently risky: airway stimulation frequently provokes coughing, hypertension, bronchospasm, and other hemodynamic disturbances [3], which account for nearly one-third of PACU complications and may precipitate severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular events [4].

Despite the clinical importance of optimizing extubation timing, current practice relies largely on subjective evaluations of consciousness and vital signs [5, 6]. The bispectral index (BIS) provides an objective measure of anesthetic depth and recovery of consciousness, yet it does not capture nociceptive responses—the principal drivers of hemodynamic instability during extubation [7, 8].

Recently, nociception monitoring tools such as the Nociception Index (NOX, 0–100) and the heart rate variability–derived Analgesia Nociception Index (ANI) have been introduced [9]. NOX quantifies the balance between nociceptive input and opioid-mediated analgesia based on dynamic electroencephalographic signals [10].The NOX index does not serve as an objective measure of pain intensity per se, but rather represents a quantitative indicator of cortical excitability derived from frontal electroencephalographic spectral analysis (1–44 Hz), reflecting the cerebral response threshold to noxious stimulation. Specifically, NOX values below 40 indicate a state of deep cortical suppression, characterized by significantly reduced excitability of the sensory cortex–thalamus–brainstem reticular formation pathway. Under these conditions, patients demonstrate an extremely elevated threshold for responding to noxious stimuli such as tracheal intubation or surgical incision, rendering them unable to generate effective protective reflexes (e.g., cough or swallowing) even when subjected to intense mechanical stimulation. Conversely, NOX values exceeding 60 signify gradual resolution of cortical inhibition and restoration of excitability within these neural circuits. This re-establishment of mechanosensitivity to airway foreign bodies is accompanied by the return of consciousness and the progressive recovery of cough and swallowing reflexes. [28]

Recent investigations have further demonstrated that, during emergence from general anesthesia, the ascending slope of NOX values may serve as an objective biomarker of anesthetic drug washout, with its dynamic changes reflecting the recovery of cortical excitability more precisely than simple time-based calculations or clinical observation alone [27]. Although existing literature has primarily validated the predictive value of NOX for tracheal intubation and intraoperative nociceptive stimuli, tracheal extubation involves qualitatively identical noxious stimuli based on shared physiological mechanisms (including mechanical friction between the endotracheal tube and the vocal cords/tracheal mucosa, as well as mechanical stimulation during endotracheal tube cuff deflation). Therefore, the present study hypothesizes that the qNOX index may likewise be applicable for assessing extubation readiness, potentially providing an objective neurophysiological basis for awake extubation by monitoring the recovery of cortical sensitivity to airway mechanical stimulation.

Evidence suggests that these indices are valuable intraoperatively, particularly for guiding opioid titration (e.g., remifentanil administration) [7, 11–13]. However, their role in extubation management within the PACU remains unclear, and potential benefits for improving extubation safety and enhancing PACU efficiency have yet to be systematically evaluated.

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a randomized controlled trial to investigate whether NOX-monitored extubation management in the PACU could facilitate safer and more efficient recovery.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was a prospective, randomized, open-label, parallel-group controlled trial conducted in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) of a tertiary hospital in Guangzhou, China. Participants were enrolled using consecutive sampling from July 2023 to March 2025; all eligible patients were included sequentially according to their arrival time in the operating room. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated sequence (1:1 ratio) prepared by an independent statistician using SPSS 26.0 software, with block randomization (block size of 4) employed to ensure balanced group sizes. Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened by a research coordinator only after participant eligibility was confirmed and informed consent obtained; the coordinator then notified the study nurse responsible for device connection and data recording.

As blinding was not feasible due to the physical presence of the monitoring devices and the generation of additional data variables in the NOX group that were absent in the control group, this study adopted a fully open-label design. Consequently, blinding of clinical operators, data recorders, and statistical analysts was not possible. To minimize potential biases, we implemented the following measures: (1) All anesthesiologists and anesthesia nurses performing extubation (except the principal investigator) underwent standardized operating procedure (SOP) training and were kept unaware of the specific study hypotheses, to minimize performance bias.; (2) Primary outcome measures comprised objective data automatically recorded by monitors (BIS values and physiological parameters), avoiding subjective assessments; (3) Statistical analyses were performed by two independent statisticians who conducted cross-validation of results to reduce analysis bias; and (4) Primary outcomes and statistical methods were predefined and locked in the study registry prior to analysis commencement to prevent selective reporting.

Study population

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 4 years, (2) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–IV [14], and (3) provision of written informed consent. Patients aged ≥ 4 years were included based on anatomical considerations. Specifically, the laryngeal structures in children aged 4 years and above approximate adult configurations, allowing for the application of standardized extubation protocols consistent with adult practice. In addition, the study intervention (NOX monitoring) was non-invasive and did not alter routine clinical management, enabling the inclusion of a broad age range based on clinical eligibility and informed consent rather than predefined age stratification.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) significant cardiopulmonary dysfunction, (2) immediate extubation upon PACU admission, or (3) inability to cooperate with study procedures.

A total of 120 eligible patients were enrolled and assigned by simple randomization to either the NOX group (n = 60) or the non-NOX group (n = 60). Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio using a simple randomization procedure. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University (approval number: 2022-KY-048-02) and registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on August 02, 2022 (ChiCTR; URL: https://www.chictr.org.cn; registration number: ChiCTR2200062346). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [15].

Monitoring and interventions

NOX group

On PACU admission, patients remained intubated and were connected to mechanical ventilation with parameters adjusted to clinical need. Continuous monitoring included respiratory rate, heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation. After stabilization, baseline vital signs were recorded; subsequent measurements were obtained at awakening, immediately before extubation, and at PACU discharge. The forehead was cleansed with saline-moistened gauze and dried; single-use electrodes were then applied sequentially (mid-forehead, lateral eyebrow, left temple), pressing the edges first and then the center for approximately 5 s. Leads were connected to a Bispectral Index Monitor (Models: BISpro, Jiangxi Meigel Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., China). This single device is capable of simultaneously monitoring both the BIS and the NOX. Real-time values of BIS and NOX were recorded at the same prespecified time points as vital signs.

Non-NOX group

Patients received routine PACU monitoring (respiratory rate, heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation). Baseline and subsequent vital signs were recorded at admission, awakening, extubation, and discharge. NOX/BIS monitoring was not performed.

Extubation criteria

NOX group: Extubation was performed when patients were awake and able to follow simple verbal commands (e.g., blinking or hand squeezing), had stable spontaneous respiration with tidal volume ≥ 5 mL/kg, maintained oxygen saturation (SpO₂) ≥ 90%, and showed hemodynamic stability. BIS and NOX values were recorded at extubation. Importantly, BIS and NOX values were not used to guide or determine extubation decisions in this study. Extubation readiness was assessed solely based on predefined clinical criteria. BIS and NOX measurements were recorded at the time of extubation for observational purposes only, to explore their potential association with extubation timing. When extubation criteria were met, anesthesiologists conducted safety assessments, and tracheal extubation was subsequently carried out by either the anesthesiologist or a certified nurse anesthetist who had successfully completed a standardized extubation competency examination. Prior to extubation, all patients underwent preoxygenation with 100% oxygen (FiO₂ = 1.0) for 3–5 min to maximize oxygen reserves.We then suctioned the airway sequentially, beginning with the trachea via the endotracheal tube, then the oropharynx above the cuff, and finally the nasopharynx, limiting each pass to under 10 s. Continuous pulse oximetry was monitored throughout the procedure; suctioning was immediately terminated if the SpO₂ decreased below 92% or dropped by ≥ 3% from baseline values to prevent hypoxemia. Subsequently, tracheal extubation was performed using the positive-pressure technique: the endotracheal tube cuff was deflated at the end-inspiratory phase (peak lung inflation) and the tube was removed swiftly yet gently in a single motion, utilizing the outward airflow to expel residual secretions. Immediately following extubation, supplemental oxygen was administered via facemask (10–15 L/min), and anesthesiologists assessed chest wall excursion and auscultated bilateral breath sounds to confirm airway patency.

Non-NOX group: Extubation required the same clinical criteria (consciousness and command following, tidal volume ≥ 5 mL/kg, SpO₂ ≥ 90%, and hemodynamic stability). The same extubation protocol established for the NOX group was applied.

For both groups, extubation time and concurrent vital signs were recorded. Patients were transferred to the general ward once the Modified Aldrete Scoring System (MASS) score was ≥ 9 [16]; vital signs at PACU discharge were recorded.

Outcome measures

Vital signs (respiratory rate, heart rate, blood pressure, and SpO₂) were recorded at PACU admission, awakening, extubation, and PACU discharge in both groups. In the NOX group, BIS and NOX values were recorded at the same time points; the non-NOX group did not undergo BIS/NOX recording. Time stamps for PACU admission, extubation, and PACU discharge were collected to calculate extubation duration (extubation time minus PACU admission time) and PACU discharge duration (discharge time minus extubation time).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Patient characteristics (age, weight, height, transfusion volume, urine output, blood loss, and time-related outcomes) were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR, 25th–75th percentile). Between-group comparisons of medians were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Monitoring indices (SpO₂, systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP], heart rate, and respiratory rate) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent Student’s t-tests. Categorical variables were reported as counts (n) and percentages (%) and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate.

Temporal trends across seven time points were visualized with line charts. A two-way mixed-design ANOVA was applied to evaluate group effects, time effects, and group × time interactions. Changes relative to PACU admission (baseline) were calculated at six subsequent time points (awakening, extubation at 0, 5, 10, and 15 min, and PACU discharge) and compared between groups.

For variables recorded only in the NOX group (e.g., BIS and NOX values), within-group comparisons across time points were performed using paired Student’s t-tests with Bonferroni correction.

All analyses were performed with R software (version 4.5.0) [17]. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

One hundred twenty patients were enrolled and randomly assigned to the NOX group (n = 60) or the non-NOX group (n = 60). The median age of the cohort was 54.5 years. Adults (≥ 18 years) comprised 90.83% of the study population, whereas pediatric patients accounted for 9.17% (Table 1). Baseline demographic characteristics were generally balanced between groups (Table 1). An exception was the prevalence of hypertension, which was higher in the NOX group (13/60, 21.7%) versus the non-NOX group (0/60) (P < 0.001). There were no other statistically significant differences in sex, body habitus, ASA class or baseline cardiac function (Table 1).

NOX and BIS dynamics (NOX group only)

In the NOX group, both BIS and NOX indices rose from PACU admission through extubation and reached stable high values after extubation (Table 3). Mean NOX increased from 77.40 ± 17.45 at admission to 95.00 ± 6.88 at extubation; mean BIS increased from 62.42 ± 17.12 to 91.27 ± 8.38 over the same interval. Paired comparisons within the NOX group showed significant increases from admission to awakening and to extubation (all P < 0.01 after Bonferroni correction), whereas the difference between values at extubation and at PACU discharge was not significant (P = 1.000), indicating that both indices rose during recovery and then stabilized after extubation (Table 3).

Oxygen saturation (SpO₂) showed a modest but statistically significant downward trend over time in both groups. At the time of extubation (0 min) and at PACU discharge, mean SpO₂ was higher in the non-NOX group (99.58 ± 0.74 vs. 98.85 ± 2.01 at extubation, P = 0.010; 98.80 ± 1.57 vs. 97.18 ± 2.86 at discharge, P < 0.001).

Both SBP and DBP increased from admission to awakening and then tended to stabilize after extubation. At awakening, mean SBP was significantly higher in the non-NOX group (130.98 ± 21.67 vs. 121.35 ± 20.85; P = 0.014), and mean DBP was also higher in the non-NOX group at awakening (80.58 ± 16.35 vs. 73.32 ± 14.93; P = 0.012). At 5 and 10 min after extubation, SBP remained significantly higher in the non-NOX group (P = 0.014 and P = 0.037, respectively). These findings suggest a comparatively attenuated BP response in the NOX group during the immediate recovery period.

Heart rate rose from admission to awakening in both groups. On initial PACU admission, the non-NOX group had a slightly higher mean HR (67.92 ± 10.68 vs. 63.18 ± 13.75; P = 0.037), and thereafter, group differences in absolute HR were not consistently significant at individual time points (Table 4).

The non-NOX group had a higher respiratory rate at multiple time points (admission, awakening, and discharge; P < 0.05 at each). The NOX group’s respiratory rate peaked at extubation and then stabilized.

At extubation (0 min), mean change in SpO₂ was − 0.62% in the NOX group versus + 0.08% in the non-NOX group (mean difference 0.70%, P = 0.024). By PACU discharge, the NOX group experienced a larger decline from baseline (− 2.28% vs. − 0.70%, P < 0.001). Thus, while absolute SpO₂ values were higher in the non-NOX group at some time points, the NOX group experienced a greater decline from admission to discharge.

Although both groups exhibited SBP and DBP increases after admission, the magnitude of SBP change tended to be larger in the non-NOX group at awakening (median increase 12.12 mmHg vs. 6.88 mmHg) and at several early post-extubation time points; however, these between-group differences in change scores did not reach statistical significance for most time points (Table 5). The pattern is consistent with the absolute BP comparisons above and suggests a relative attenuation of BP rise in the NOX group during awakening and the early post-extubation period.

Heart-rate increases from baseline were greater in the NOX group at multiple post-admission time points (e.g., extubation 10 min change: NOX + 14.52 bpm vs. non-NOX + 7.43 bpm; P = 0.008). Respiration-rate increases were also larger in the NOX group at awakening and early post-extubation time points (all P ≤ 0.013). These findings indicate that changes in HR and respiratory rate differed between groups in direction and magnitude and did not uniformly favor one strategy; the clinical significance of these differences warrants cautious interpretation.

Time-to-event outcomes and complications (efficiency and safety)

Time interval outcomes are shown in Table 2. The interval from PACU admission to extubation did not differ between groups (median 23 min for NOX vs. 22 min for non-NOX; P = 0.964). However, the interval from extubation to PACU discharge was markedly shorter in the NOX group (median 27.5 min vs. 50.5 min; P < 0.001), representing a reduction of approximately 42% in post-extubation PACU stay. Overall complication rates did not differ significantly between groups (7/60 vs. 11/60; P = 0.306).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the feasibility and clinical impact of NOX-monitored extubation management in the PACU. Our randomized trial demonstrates that integrating the nociception index NOX into extubation decision-making is associated with multiple benefits, including improved hemodynamic stability and shorter PACU recovery. Specifically, patients in the NOX group exhibited attenuated blood-pressure responses at awakening (SBP ≈ 7.3% lower, DBP ≈ 9.0% lower vs. control; both p < 0.05), substantially reduced PACU discharge times (27.5 vs. 50.5 min, p < 0.001; ≈42% reduction), and synchronous increases in NOX and BIS within the NOX group (NOX: 77.40 → 95.00; BIS: 62.42 → 91.27). These findings suggest that nociception monitoring may complement standard consciousness indices during the high-risk extubation phase, where BIS alone provides no objective measure of nociceptive activity. Notably, NOX and BIS data were collected exclusively in the NOX group.

Contextually, our results extend the application of NOX beyond intraoperative opioid titration [7, 12] into the PACU setting. Using bispectral index monitors, we observed that combining BIS with NOX (both ≥ 90 thresholds) was associated with superior outcomes relative to BIS-only strategies (Fig. 1). A notable observation is the difference in respiratory rate at awakening (16.50 vs. 15.75 bpm, p = 0.037, Table 4), highlighting the need for continued respiratory monitoring despite hemodynamic improvements. BIS remains the most widely used processed-EEG index for assessing hypnotic depth and predicting return of consciousness, with validation and comparative studies consistently demonstrating strong discriminative performance (prediction probabilities in the mid-0.8 range) [18]. However, its limitation lies in reflecting cortical activity alone. NOX may provide additional insight by estimating the nociception–analgesia balance, particularly relevant during airway manipulation when sympathetic responses are pronounced. Prior studies indicate that EEG-based and autonomic-based nociception indices (ANI, surgical pleth index, SPI) are affected differently by confounders such as heart rate variability and vasomotor tone [19–21]. Although our study did not directly compare these devices, the results support the complementary potential of EEG-derived indices.

A plausible mechanism is that cortical nociceptive markers on EEG emerge prior to autonomic responses such as heart rate or blood pressure changes [22, 23], allowing earlier detection and potentially more timely intervention. In our study, a dual-axis strategy requiring BIS ≥ 90 and NOX ≥ 90 before extubation was associated with greater hemodynamic stability and faster PACU discharge. Nevertheless, optimal thresholds remain to be validated, and our findings should be considered preliminary rather than definitive evidence of an “optimal” cutoff. Clinically, NOX monitoring may be most beneficial in high-risk surgical populations prone to hemodynamic instability. Rather than replacing existing monitoring, NOX should serve as a supplementary tool to enhance early detection of nociceptive responses. Given the observed oxygen desaturation patterns, combining a NOX ≥ 90 threshold with concurrent SpO₂ safeguards (e.g., > 92%) may be reasonable, though prospective validation is required. Institutional assessment across different anesthetic techniques is also necessary before standard adoption.

Although blood-pressure variability was reduced in the NOX group, we observed greater heart-rate increases (+ 14.5 vs. + 7.4 bpm, p = 0.008) and SpO₂ declines (–2.28% vs. − 0.70%, p < 0.001)—an unexpected cardiorespiratory trade-off. Similar concerns regarding physiological confounding have been reported for autonomic-based nociception monitors. For instance, the surgical pleth index (SPI) can be influenced by changes in vascular tone, posture, hypothermia, and volume status [24], while ANI readings may be distorted during episodes of bleeding or vasopressor administration [25]. Unidimensional physiological parameters are susceptible to various confounding factors and may inadequately reflect the patient’s true physiological status. As highlighted by Gentile and Introna, interpretation of heart rate variability (HRV) should be integrated with monitoring of anesthesia depth and nociception [26]; in this context, tools such as processed electroencephalogram (pEEG) and the Nociception Level (NOL™) may provide complementary information. Such a multidimensional approach enables clinicians to differentiate whether physiological fluctuations originate from underlying pathology or iatrogenic interventions (e.g., anesthetic agents, analgesics, or mechanical ventilation). These observations underscore the importance of implementing a comprehensive, multimodal monitoring strategy that synthesizes autonomic, central nervous system, and nociceptive signals to ensure both hemodynamic and respiratory stability during the perioperative period [26]. These observations underscore the importance of integrated monitoring strategies that account for both hemodynamic and respiratory stability.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Methodologically, baseline imbalances—including higher hypertension prevalence in the NOX group (21.7% vs. 0%) and greater surgical complexity in the control group (78.3% vs. 60% Grade IV procedures) —may confound the interpretation of hemodynamic stability and recovery duration. In particular, the non-NOX group had significantly longer surgical and anesthesia durations, which may reflect increased procedural complexity and prolonged anesthetic exposure. Future research should utilize stratified randomization or multiple regression analysis to account for baseline differences and reduce potential bias. Furthermore, although all patients received standardized intraoperative opioid regimens (intravenous sufentanil 0.5 µg/kg followed by remifentanil 0.1–0.3 µg/kg/min), total opioid requirements were substantially greater in the non-NOX group than in the NOX group. Technically, NOX shares common EEG-monitor limitations, including susceptibility to electrode placement variability and electromyographic interference [24]. Clinically, the lack of long-term outcome data (postoperative pain, complications, length of hospital stay) and similar complication rates between groups leaves open questions about the broader clinical impact that require further study. Future investigations should prioritize: (1) threshold optimization across diverse patient populations and anesthetic regimens, and (2) mechanistic studies to clarify the clinical implications of NOX-associated cardiorespiratory responses. Larger multicenter trials will be essential to validate these findings and support standardized clinical protocols.

Conclusion

This trial provides early clinical evidence that EEG-based NOX monitoring can enhance extubation management by improving hemodynamic stability and accelerating PACU discharge. Broader adoption will depend on validation in larger, multi-institutional studies and refinement of threshold strategies to ensure both hemodynamic and respiratory safety.
