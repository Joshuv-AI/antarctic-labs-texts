TANGO: direct optimization of constrained synthesizability for generative molecular design
Authors: Jeff Guo, Philippe Schwaller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConstrained synthesizability is an unaddressed challenge in generative molecular design: in particular, designing molecules satisfying multi-parameter optimization objectives, while simultaneously being synthesizable and enforcing the presence of specific building blocks in the synthesis. This is practically important for molecule re-purposing, sustainability and efficiency. Here we propose the Tanimoto Group Overlap (TANGO) reward function, which uses chemistry principles to transform a binary reward function into a continuous reward function. TANGO can augment molecular generative models to directly optimize for constrained synthesizability using reinforcement learning (RL). Our framework is general and addresses starting-material, intermediate and divergent-synthesis constraints. Contrary to many existing works in the field, we show that incentivizing a general-purpose model with RL is a productive approach to navigating challenging synthesizability optimization scenarios.Subject terms: Machine learning, Cheminformatics

Main

Synthesizable generative molecular design is becoming increasingly prevalent1,2, paralleling the rise in the number of experimentally validated generative-design case studies3. Controlling how generated molecules can be synthesized offers great potential for the push towards closed-loop discovery4,5, as molecules that can be made from specific reagents or reactions are naturally more amenable to robotic synthesis automation, which can be specialized for certain chemistries6,7. Moving beyond methods that optimize for synthesizability heuristics2,8, approaches that explicitly assess synthesizability can be broadly categorized into forward synthesis or retrosynthesis, which builds molecules from simple building blocks or recursively decomposes a target molecule into constituent building blocks, respectively. An example of forward synthesis in the context of molecular design is synthesizability-constrained molecular generation. These methods anchor molecular generation in viable chemical transformation rules, thus promoting synthesizability9–15. On the other hand, retrosynthesis planning16–20 proposes viable synthesis routes to a target molecule, and these models are often used as standalone tools to assess synthesizability. Such models have become increasingly adopted and are now routinely used to filter generated molecules21. Recent work has shown that generative models can directly generate molecules deemed synthesizable by retrosynthesis models by treating them as another oracle (computational prediction) to optimize for22. Subsequently, constrained synthesis planning has become a research focus, whereby proposed synthesis routes incorporate enforced building blocks (EBBs). This is especially relevant for sustainability and efficiency, and examples include semi-synthesis23 (starting from reagents isolated from natural sources) and divergent synthesis24 (passing through common intermediates).

More generally, existing works have introduced the notion of starting-material-constrained synthesis25–27, where a small set of fixed reagents is used in the first step of reaction pathways to probe reactivity. These reagents are specifically selected due to their reactivity25, as primordial substrates26 or as toxic wastes to be degraded 27. Drawing the connection to synthesis planning, existing works have explored reacting a small set of fixed reagents with a larger set of general reagents to achieve valorization. For example, Wołos et al.28 demonstrated that industrial waste reacted with ‘basic’ reagents can be valorized into known drug molecules. Similarly, the concept of ‘bioprivileged molecules’29 was introduced, where molecules specifically derived from biomass could be used to synthesize novel and useful chemicals. Therefore, exploring the ability to valorize a small set of specific reagents is a pertinent problem. More recently, constrained retrosynthesis algorithms have been proposed that guide the identification of synthesis pathways towards specific reagents30–32. However, to date, there are no molecular generative models that can enforce specific building blocks in the proposed routes.

In this work, we show that a general-purpose molecular generative model, without any hard constraints, can be incentivized to generate synthesizable molecules that satisfy multi-parameter optimization (MPO) objectives while jointly enforcing a set of building blocks (Fig. 1). To our knowledge, there are only a few works30–33 that enable some notion of building-block-constrained synthesis planning. In particular, the very recent DESP32 retrosynthesis search algorithm proposes a bidirectional search that can be constrained on a starting material. Our work differs in that we are proposing not a search algorithm, but rather a generative approach that jointly tackles constrained synthesizability and MPO. Our contribution is as follows. (1) We leverage chemistry principles and propose the Tanimoto Group Overlap (TANGO) reward function to generate molecules deemed synthesizable by retrosynthesis models with the presence of EBBs using reinforcement learning (RL). (2) We show that generated molecules satisfy MPO objectives, and by design enable the construction of synthesis networks where common intermediates branch towards diverse, high-reward molecules. (3) We show that letting a general-purpose model freely learn (using incentives) can be a productive approach to optimizing challenging synthesizability objectives.

TANGO guides the generation of molecules directly optimized for constrained synthesizability with EBBs while simultaneously optimizing other properties.

Our method generalizes across starting-material, intermediate and divergent-synthesis constraints.

Results

Making constrained synthesizability learnable

Understanding the optimization dynamics

In Supplementary Section 4, we perform ablation studies to understand the optimization dynamics that enable direct optimization of constrained synthesizability. We define fuzzy matching substructure (FMS) as the number of heavy atoms overlapping between a query molecule (molecule node in a synthesis graph) and an EBB. FMSBlock and FMSQuery denote whether the heavy-atom overlap is normalized by the EBB or the query molecule, that is, divided by the number of heavy atoms in the EBB or the query molecule, respectively. See ‘TANGO reward function’ for details of why we formulated two variants.

We summarize our observations around various reward functions and show synthesizability and molecular property and diversity metrics in Table 1 and Extended Data Table 1, respectively. (1) Standard Tanimoto similarity as the reward function can lead to successful runs, but it may be less stable as only 3/5 seeds were successful, with high variance (Solved (Enforced)). (2) Owing to false positives, FMSBlock is essentially unsuccessful, as on average it generates only one molecule satisfying constrained synthesizability (Solved (Enforced)). FMSQuery circumvents false positives and is more successful in generating Solved (Enforced) molecules. The docking scores (DSs) are much more optimized (lower values), but diversity can be lower (IntDiv1)34 (Extended Data Table 1). (3) TANGO combines Tanimoto similarity with FMS with equal weighting. TANGO-FMSBlock and TANGO-FMSQuery are the most consistent reward functions with 5/5 successful seeds. All seeds successfully generate Solved (Enforced) molecules, and much more than FMSQuery alone. Comparing the use of FMSBlock versus FMSQuery in TANGO, we find that FMSBlock is more performant, with better DSs, ligand efficiency and diversity (Extended Data Table 1). Considering these empirical results, the final implementation of TANGO defaults to computing FMSBlock. Only in the event of a false-positive signal is FMSQuery computed instead. From here on, we refer to TANGO-FMSBlock as TANGO. (4) Through ablation studies, we show that ‘it takes two to tango’ because TANGO’s performance is much more consistent and robust (across seeds) than using just Tanimoto similarity or FMS (Block or Query) alone.

Thus far, TANGO was formulated with equal weighting to FMSBlock and Tanimoto similarity. We next investigated varying the weighting, assigning 0.75 to either FMSBlock or Tanimoto similarity and 0.25 to the other. The results in Supplementary Section 4.5 show that putting more weight on Tanimoto similarity leads to more constrained synthesizable molecules, but at the expense of worse MPO. Since MPO is vital for practicality, we chose to designate TANGO with equal weighting as the default reward function. Next, we found that the default hyperparameters of Saturn35 are too exploitative and disadvantageous in this optimization setting. Relaxing this behavior makes constrained synthesizability much more consistently learnable (under the fixed oracle budget). Finally, once molecules with a specific EBB are generated, Saturn heavily focuses on that building block, such that within the same generative experiment often only one unique block is enforced (but variable across seeds). We do not consider this a disadvantage, as it enables the construction of divergent synthesis networks, where a common block branches towards many optimal molecules. We discuss the implications of this behavior from a generative perspective in the next section.

Constrained synthesizability results

Table 2 shows the synthesizability metrics for the Benf-10 and Benf-100 experiments using TANGO. Extended Data Table 2 shows the molecular property and diversity metrics. We make the following observations. (1) Constrained synthesizability can be learned within the 10,000-oracle budget (approximate 8.5 h wall time). (2) All runs generate non-solvable molecules and many solvable molecules do not contain the enforced blocks, as expected (see Non-solved, Solved and Solved (Enforced) metrics). Nonetheless, generated molecules can achieve favorable DSs (tending towards <−10, which is considered optimal in previous works12) and optimal quantitative estimate of druglikeness (QED)36 values. This demonstrates the capability to perform MPO while also optimizing for constrained synthesizability. (3) The negative control is the ‘Unconstrained’ experiment, which does not explicitly optimize for the use of EBBs. We expect generated molecules not to satisfy constrained synthesizability, and this is affirmed by the results in Table 2, showing that only four molecules in total (across ten seeds), by chance, satisfy this constraint. In this experiment only, we report the diversity metrics for the Solved molecules and observe that the IntDiv1, #Circles and number of Bemis–Murcko scaffolds found is much higher than in constrained experiments (Extended Data Table 2). This is expected because constrained synthesizability, by definition, essentially fixes a portion of the molecule by using EBBs. (4) Number of unique enforced blocks is relatively low, as we observed that once the model incorporates one EBB it focuses on generating molecules whose syntheses can be decomposed to that specific block, since the reward it obtains is high and there is a degree of exploitation. This also contributes to the decreased diversity (see IntDiv1, #Circles and #Scaffolds metrics) compared with the Unconstrained setting, and is an expected outcome (Extended Data Table 2). (5) The starting-material constraint is more difficult for Benf-100, but unexpectedly not necessarily for Benf-10 (there are 9/10 successful seeds compared with 6/10 for the non-starting-material case). We speculate that the reason for this is exactly due to exploitation behavior. Since TANGO returns the maximum reward in the synthesis tree (comparing with all Benf), it is possible that more blocks can be a hindrance when there are specific blocks that are particularly favorable. We emphasize that across different seeds the EBBs can be different, which is important, as we could run multiple experiments in parallel and pool the results. (6) Number of reaction steps is generally small, which shows that optimizing for constrained synthesizability does not lead to inefficient synthesis plans. We note that the number of reaction steps in the divergent synthesis results is greater because it takes one step in the first place to arrive at the divergent blocks. Finally, all runs took 8.5 h on average using a single graphics processing unit (GPU), which is reasonable.

Are the results by chance?

While the results thus far were promising, we noticed that many runs (across seeds) converged to the same three EBBs (Fig. 2). We questioned whether the success was simply due to these ‘lucky’ blocks. Therefore, we performed a set of ablation experiments by removing these blocks and re-running the configurations in Table 2. The results show that the model can generate optimal molecules with other EBBs (Supplementary Section 4.7). These runs were much less successful (across seeds) but recovered when removing the QED objective. This suggests that the model learns to use certain blocks that are more aligned with the objective function. Next, we further push our framework with an EBB set of five molecules dissimilar to the lucky blocks. While much more challenging (most runs are unsuccessful under the strict oracle budget), this is still possible, with the routes containing Suzuki coupling reactions (Supplementary Section 4.8), which is notably different from the amide coupling reactions in Fig. 2.

Synthesis networks

Next, we tackle divergent synthesis by incorporating larger, non-commercial molecules in the EBB set. We curated a set of ten non-commercial blocks from solved synthesis routes (Fig. 2) and ran the same experimental set-up. Table 2 shows that this is also learnable within the oracle budget, albeit much less consistently, as only 4/10 seeds were successful. We still believe that our framework is robust, as this is a much more challenging task and we wanted to show that the model can learn to enforce these large building blocks from scratch. We can also increase the oracle budget, which leads to more successful seeds, as we show in Supplementary Section 4.9. This capability opens up practical applications for late-stage functionalization, commonly employed in drug discovery37. Correspondingly, Fig. 2 shows example synthesis networks using results from the starting-material- and divergent-synthesis-constrained experiments. All generated molecules achieve optimal DSs (although starting-material constrained resulted in slightly worse scores) and QED values. In the divergent-synthesis case, a one-step amide coupling reaction from the enforced block leads to notably improved DSs, though sometimes with lower QED. Examples of full synthesis routes are shown in Supplementary Section 5.1.

Learning a desirable distribution

Fundamentally, generative models learn to model distributions. In this section, we further demonstrate that TANGO is a learnable reward function and that the modeled distribution shifts to satisfy the MPO objective. To do so, we take each final model checkpoint (across the ten seeds) from the experiment in Table 2 with 100 EBBs (and with QED) and sample 1,000 unique molecules. Figure 3a shows that a considerable number of sampled molecules are jointly synthesizable with an EBB (Solved (Enforced)). The distribution shift is apparent when compared with 1,000 unique molecules sampled from the pretrained model (before RL), which mostly generates unsynthesizable molecules (Non-solved). Figure 3b pools (across the ten seeds) all the Solved (Enforced) molecules and shows the density of docking and QED scores that have shifted towards favorable values. Next, we take the sampled molecules from one seed and plot a uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP)38 embedding, comparing with the molecules sampled from the pretrained model. It is clear that the checkpoint-sampled molecules are dissimilar but we show that the learned distribution is not perfect, as the final checkpoint still sometimes samples ill-suited (on the basis of the MPO objective) molecules that are similar to the pretrained model. Subsequently, we take the sampled molecules from the final model checkpoint and compare the negative log-likelihoods (NLLs) as measured by this checkpoint and the pretrained model. A lower NLL means the molecule is more likely to be generated and vice versa. We make two observations: first, the molecules are much more likely under the checkpoint, unsurprisingly. However, second, and more importantly, the NLLs from the checkpoint put more probability mass in a narrower region. We now cross-reference Fig. 3e, which shows the top-ten sampled molecules (by DS), which all share the same EBB. The NLLs are not markedly different, which shows that some exploitation during RL can be advantageous as the likelihoods of molecules that share a common structure can be quite similar. The model learns to use the building blocks in a way that performs local exploration and assigns a relatively similar likelihood to the neighborhood of molecules. Overall, the results show that taking a general-purpose model and incentivizing the learning process with TANGO can shift the modeled distribution to one that captures constrained synthesizability, while simultaneously satisfying MPO objectives. This is practically useful, as one could simply sample molecules from model checkpoints to obtain more desirable molecules (it takes about 5 s to sample 1,000 unique molecules).

Enforcing phenylsulfonamide building blocks (COX-2 case study)

In this section, we apply our framework to a real-world-inspired case study to design selective inhibitors for cyclooxygenase (COX) to treat pain and inflammation39. From existing structure–activity relationship studies, the cis-stilbene and phenylsulfonamide moieties can confer selectivity for COX-2 over COX-1, where inhibiting the latter has been associated with toxicity39. We note that existing selective COX-2 inhibitors containing these specific moieties (rofecoxib and valdecoxib) have also led to cardiovascular complications and were withdrawn from the therapeutics market39,40. This suggests that the presence of cis-stilbene and phenylsulfonamide, while important for selectivity, does not fully account for the efficacy and safety of the potential drug molecule.

Experimental details

This case study mimics a plausible design campaign to pivot from a known selective inhibitor by exploring other scaffolds, while fixing a specific moiety. SC-558 is a selective COX-2 inhibitor possessing the cis-stilbene and phenylsulfonamide moieties41. We focus on phenylsulfonamide and extract all the building blocks containing this moiety from the same ZINC-derived commercial stock42 used in this work. Out of 17,721,980 building blocks in the ZINC stock, 139 contain phenylsulfonamide, and we use TANGO to guide molecular generation to use these specific blocks. We run this experiment across five seeds (0–4 inclusive) with a 15,000-oracle budget and jointly minimize the Gnina43,44 DS and maximize QED36.

Results

To promote the plausibility of generated molecules, we redock SC-558 using Gnina to first verify that the docking protocol can reproduce the crystal binding pose. Figure 4 shows that the redocked pose (green) has a 0.266-Å root mean square deviation from the crystal pose (gray), and places the phenylsulfonamide in the correct area, albeit with a slightly rotated orientation. Extended Data Tables 3 and 4 show that TANGO successfully guides the generation of phenylsulfonamide-containing molecules. We make the following observations. (1) The number of unique enforced blocks is much larger than in the experiments in the previous sections and indicates that many of the 139 phenylsulfonamide-containing blocks were used. This is somewhat expected as the EBBs here have high similarity (Fig. 4), in contrast to the set of EBBs in the previous section, which we randomly sampled from the commercial stock. (2) Diverse molecules are still generated (IntDiv1 and #Circles metrics). (3) The wall time is much longer than in previous experiments because Gnina is more computationally intensive compared with QuickVina2-GPU. (4) The reference ligand, SC-558, is a known selective COX-2 inhibitor with a Gnina DS of −11.57. Generated molecules can achieve similar or better DSs with the docking algorithm placing the phenylsulfonamide in a similar orientation (Fig. 4). We acknowledge that DSs are a crude estimate of binding affinity, and the purpose of this section is to demonstrate the applicability of TANGO. Accordingly, some generated molecules are predicted to be synthesizable with a one-step amide coupling (reactions were labeled with NameRxn45) and the binding pose is plausible. Overall, the results demonstrate that TANGO can be used to fix specific chemical moieties of interest and explore other scaffolds that also satisfy the optimization objective.

Discussion

In this work, we have proposed a reward function called TANGO that can guide a general-purpose molecular generative model to directly optimize for constrained synthesizability. The framework demonstrates the feasibility of a generative approach for constrained synthesizability, and tackles various degrees of constraints that are practically important in real-world applications: starting-material, intermediate and divergent-synthesis constraints (Fig. 2).

The results show that TANGO can generalize across different EBB sets, but there are several important limitations. First, TANGO’s success relies on formulating constrained synthesizability as a continuous reward function, which enables providing a signal on whether we are getting ‘closer’ to achieving the objective. In Supplementary Section 4.3, we support this statement by assessing the brute-force approach, meaning assigning 1 or 0 as the reward, indicating satisfying constrained synthesizability or not, respectively. The results show that this brute-force approach is much less reliable, as more seeds are unsuccessful and the MPO is worse. However, while TANGO itself is a continuous reward function, it requires that a synthesis route is solved, in the first place, by the retrosynthesis model. If a synthesis route is not found for a molecule, TANGO cannot be computed and the reward is defaulted to 0. This imposes a binary aspect in TANGO. In all the experiments performed in this work, we found that this was not detrimental to the optimization process, but we highlight that we used 17,721,980 building blocks (stock) in the retrosynthesis model. Naturally, a larger stock means that more molecules can be solved by the retrosynthesis model. Accordingly, under a more constrained stock, it is possible that the binary aspect of finding a synthesis route becomes an optimization challenge. Another limitation is that ‘true synthesizability’ depends on the accuracy of the retrosynthesis model, and these are not perfect. It is likely that many synthesis routes themselves are not synthetically feasible and/or lack regio- or stereo-selectivity46. This is a limitation of current retrosynthesis models and is an ongoing challenge for improved synthesis planning. As we are not proposing a new retrosynthesis model, this is beyond the scope of this work, but we believe this is important to explicitly acknowledge.

Our framework also provides opportunities to further understand the optimization dynamics and to extend TANGO to more use cases. In this work, we investigated various reward function formulations (Supplementary Section 4.3) and their ability to guide learning towards achieving constrained synthesizability. Another factor that may notably affect optimization is the number of synthesis routes to return from the retrosynthesis model. Typically, retrosynthesis models are tasked to return a set of potential synthesis routes for further prioritization47–50. Here, we terminate route searching if one successful synthesis route has been found, for computational efficiency purposes. In general, there are numerous potential ways to synthesize a target molecule. Since TANGO measures the similarity between molecule nodes in the predicted synthesis route and the EBBs, returning more synthesis routes might make the optimization more tractable, given that some routes may have molecule nodes more similar to the EBBs. We believe that this is an interesting research avenue, as running route searching for longer will also increase the computational cost. Thus, understanding the interplay between optimization efficiency and computational cost would be pertinent. Going further, this work only focuses on enforcing a single building block. In Supplementary Section 5.3, we find that there are cases, albeit rare, where more than one EBB is present. Correspondingly, incentivizing the use of more than one EBB would necessitate further understanding of the optimization dynamics and can have practical applications. For example, generating molecules with synthesis routes incorporating multiple industrial by-products28 or biomass-derived molecules29 could be hugely advantageous in molecular re-purposing efforts.

Methods

In this section, we describe the problem formulation of constrained synthesizability, the generative model, the TANGO reward function and the experimental set-up.

Constrained synthesizability problem formulation

In synthesis planning, the goal is to propose a valid synthetic route to a target molecule using (commercially) available building blocks, B, and a set of reaction rules, R. We define a synthesis graph, G(M, R), where each node represents an intermediate molecule, m, that need not necessarily be an available building block, b, and the edges represent reactions, r ∈ R. The depth of a node is the number of edges from the root node (the target molecule). A valid synthetic route requires that all leaf nodes correspond to commercially available building blocks, b ∈ B. We further define the set of EBBs, Benf ⊆ B. In practice, ∣Benf∣ ≪ ∣B∣, and in this work we consider ∣Benf∣ ∈ {10, 100}. We address three cases of constrained synthesis in this work.

Starting-material-constrained synthesis (case 1)

A synthesis graph is considered starting-material constrained if at least one leaf node, m ∈ G(M, R), satisfies both of the following conditions: (1) m = EBB ∈ Benf and (2) depth(m) = max depth,

A practical reason for enforcing a starting-material constraint is that they may be inexpensive reagents. As such, they can be obtained in larger quantities for use in multi-step synthesis, which necessarily loses material at every synthetic step.

Divergent synthesis (case 3)

A synthesis graph is considered divergent if at least one intermediate node, m ∈ G(M, R), satisfies both of the following conditions: (1) m = EBB ∈ Benf and (2) all EBB ∈ Benf are non-commercial. TANGO reward function

In the context of generative molecular design, previous work has shown that retrosynthesis models can be treated as an oracle and directly optimized for22. The effect is that generated molecules are synthesizable, as deemed by retrosynthesis models (from here on, this will just be referred to as ‘synthesizable’, for brevity). In that work, we adopted a brute-force approach to learning synthesizability, despite the reward signal being binary, meaning R ∈ {0, 1}, denoting whether a molecule is synthesizable or not. This worked because there are enough molecules that are synthesizable, making the optimization landscape not too sparse. However, in the constrained synthesis setting, it is highly unlikely that a synthesizable molecule will also contain an EBB in its synthesis graph, especially when the number of Benf is small, which is common in real-world applications28. Consequently, molecules satisfying constrained synthesizability are sparse: without a way to inform the model if it is getting closer to achieving the goal, learning becomes extremely challenging. Intuitively, defining a reward signal that is not exactly the target objective but is informative to achieving the target objective should guide learning, and can be done through reward shaping51.

In this work, we propose the TANGO reward function that leverages chemistry inductive bias to transform the binary reward environment associated with constrained synthesis to a continuous reward environment (Supplementary Fig. 1). Specifically, for every synthesizable molecule, TANGO provides a signal on whether the model is closer to incorporating Benf. Given G(M, R), this is achieved by a notion of similarity between every node, m, and Benf. We draw inspiration from previous works that leverage Tanimoto similarity (subgraph similarity) for retrosynthesis18,52. However, Tanimoto similarity alone is insufficient, as chemical reactivity is often associated with functional groups and their neighborhoods, which dictate incompatibilities46. Correspondingly, we experiment with augmenting Tanimoto similarity with functional group overlap (does a given molecule have similar functional groups to Benf?) and FMS (what is the maximum substructure overlap by atom count with Benf?). In the most straightforward formulation, substructure matching is a binary verifier: 1 if the substructure is contained and 0 otherwise. FMS, by contrast, formulates verification as a continuous signal: even if the exact substructure is not contained, what fraction of the substructure is? Concretely, the first step is to compute the number of overlapping heavy atoms in a query molecule node of a synthesis graph (an example is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1) with an EBB. Next, we define FMSBlock and FMSQuery, denoting whether the number of overlapping heavy atoms is divided by the total number of heavy atoms in the EBB or the query, respectively. In the case of FMSBlock, if the number of overlapping heavy atoms is equal to the total number of heavy atoms in the EBB, then the molecule node is an exact match, indicating that it has the same chemical structure as the EBB. In initial experiments, we found an edge case that led to false positives being matched. This occurs when the molecule node itself contains a substructure that is an exact match with the EBB. In this case, the overlapping heavy atoms are equal to the number of heavy atoms in the EBB, yet the molecule node is not structurally equivalent. FMSQuery handles this edge case by normalizing by the query molecule. When using FMS as a standalone reward function, we used the FMSQuery formulation to avoid false positives. Finally, it is entirely possible that there exists a scenario where FMSQuery is the one that leads to false positives over FMSBlock. We did not observe this given our building-block sets, but to guard against such a scenario the implementation of FMS we use is to compute FMSBlock first. Next, if FMSBlock is 1, then we verify that the query and EBB are structurally equivalent. If not, this is a false-positive case, and FMSQuery is returned instead. See Supplementary Section 4.1 for a more detailed discussion of this edge case.

Subsequently, FMS (regardless of whether Block or Query) also naturally encodes a notion of conserved substructure, as rarely are molecular structures fragmented during a synthesis. If a molecule is used as a building block, a large portion of the intact structure should still be present in the final molecule. This has important implications, as the likelihood of generating a molecule that conserves a large substructure might mean that a certain level of exploitation in the sampling behavior can be beneficial35. We comment on this in ‘Results’. We further note that FMS (Block or Query), if enforcing exact atom hybridization, atom type, chirality and whether the atom is part of a ring, also implicitly considers functional group overlap. In Supplementary Section 4.3, we systematically evaluated various TANGO formulations and their ability to distinguish ‘goodness’, and conclude that equal weighting (0.5) of Tanimoto similarity (TanSim) and FMSBlock yields the best learnable signal:

To compute Tanimoto similarity, this work uses Morgan fingerprints with radius = 2 and nBits = 1,024. We note that, since the maximum value of Tanimoto similarity and FMS is 1, TANGO is by design already normalized ∈[0, 1]. Note also that using FMSBlock instead of FMSQuery does not lead to false positives in this case. The reason is that the Tanimoto similarity would never equal 1 if the molecule node and EBB are not exactly equivalent. Nonetheless, we compute FMSBlock by default, and fall back to FMSQuery if the FMS is 1 but the EBB and query are not structurally equivalent. The TANGO reward is the maximum value between all non-root nodes, m ∈ G(M, R) (Supplementary Algorithm 1). It follows that the type of constrained synthesizability can be controlled by a simple toggle of whether only nodes at max depth (starting material) or any node (intermediate or divergent) are considered.

Molecular generative model

Here, we build on Saturn35, which is a general-purpose autoregressive language-based model operating on SMILES strings53. Saturn uses the Mamba54 architecture and performs goal-directed generation using RL. The key mechanism is combining SMILES augmentation55 with experience replay56 (Augmented Memory57), which directly controls the exploration–exploitation trade-off. In the original work, we found that aggressive local sampling in chemical space improves sample efficiency across various drug-discovery case studies. By contrast, we show that the constrained synthesizability setting necessitates a more exploratory behavior. We pretrain Saturn on PubChem58 after data preprocessing (Supplementary Section 1).

Retrosynthesis model

In this work, we integrate Syntheseus49, which is a wrapper around various retrosynthesis models and search algorithms, into Saturn. Through Syntheseus, we use MEGAN (graph-edit based)59 as the single-step retrosynthesis model coupled with the Retro*60 search algorithm with default hyperparameters. MEGAN was chosen due to its fast inference speed, but we emphasize that our framework is retrosynthesis model agnostic. All generated molecules are sent for retrosynthetic analysis by MEGAN and a predicted synthesis route is returned, if any.

Commercial building blocks

In this work, B is comprised of the ‘Fragment’ and ‘Reactive’ subsets of ZINC42 (17,721,980), which are part of the commercial building-block stock used in the AiZynthFinder47,48 retrosynthesis model. We note that the size of B is much larger than employed in previous synthesizability-constrained works11,12,14,15. Therefore, an additional result in this paper is showing that our framework can navigate an enormous synthesizable space. We also want to highlight that further increasing the size of B is straightforward, and does not require re-training of the generative model. Next, Benf ⊂ B is randomly sampled with the following criteria: 150 < molecular weight < 200, no aliphatic carbon chains longer than 3, exclude charged molecules, if rings are present enforce size ∈ {5, 6}, and molecules must contain at least one nitrogen, oxygen or sulfur atom. We believe these criteria are a reasonable representation of simple building blocks applicable to drug design (see Supplementary Section 2.2 for more details) We consider ∣Benf∣ ∈ {10, 100} and denote these as Benf-10 and Benf-100, respectively.

Drug discovery case study

The MPO optimization task for method development is to generate molecules with optimized QuickVina2-GPU-2.144,61,62 DSs against ATP-dependent Clp protease proteolytic subunit (ClpP)63 (implicated in cancer) and high QED36, and that are synthesizable with either the starting-material, intermediate or divergent-synthesis constraints. We finally note that in rare cases Saturn can generate radicals due to the SMILES syntax: for example, when a ‘[C@H]’ token is generated in place of ‘c’ when constructing a ring. In principle, undesired radicals would be penalized by the oracle, but we noticed that the docking oracle in this work may be overly lenient. As this is a fundamental limitation of the oracle itself, we believe it does not hinder method development in this work.

Experimental details

For method development, we ran every experiment across five seeds (0–4 inclusive) with varying oracle budgets. Once we identified optimal hyperparameters, we ran all main development experiments across ten seeds (0–9 inclusive) with a 10,000-oracle budget, and reported the wall time to promote practical application. We focus our investigation on the optimization dynamics for learning constrained synthesizability and the implications of TANGO. Finally, we apply our framework on a real-world-inspired case study to design selective COX-2 inhibitors by fixing a specific chemical moiety known to be important for selectivity39. We defer experiment-specific details to the corresponding Results section.

Metrics

We report Non-solved, Solved and Solved (Enforced) as the number of generated molecules that the retrosynthesis model deems unsynthesizable (no route returned), synthesizable but not necessarily containing an enforced block, and synthesizable with the presence of an EBB, respectively. Note that Solved (Enforced) is a much more challenging metric than just synthesizable, which previous work has shown is directly learnable22. We further report N as the number of replicates out of ten seeds where Solved (Enforced) > 0, and the mean and s.d. for the number of unique enforced blocks, denoting how many unique EBBs are in the routes for the Solved (Enforced) molecules. Next, we pool all Solved (Enforced) molecules, de-duplicate and report the mean and s.d. of the number of reaction steps. Among the generated molecules that satisfy constrained synthesizability, we further assess their property profiles and diversity. Accordingly, we report the mean and s.d. of the DSs, QED values and ligand efficiency (DS divided by the number of heavy atoms). To assess diversity, we report the internal diversity (IntDiv1)34, #Circles64 and the number of unique Bemis–Murcko scaffolds (#Scaffolds)65. Both IntDiv1 and #Circles use Morgan fingerprints with radius = 2 and nBits = 1,024. IntDiv1 is defined as 1 − (average pair-wise Tanimoto similarity of the generated set). #Circles is defined as the largest subset of generated molecules where all molecules are dissimilar to one another by a distance metric and threshold (0.75 in this work). Accordingly, all molecules in the subset are <0.25 Tanimoto similarity.
