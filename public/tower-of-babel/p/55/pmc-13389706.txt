Diabetic ketoacidosis induced by long-acting growth hormone in a child without preexisting diabetes
Authors: Manahil Mustafa, Tamara S Hannon, John S Fuqua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-acting growth hormone (LAGH) formulations improve medication adherence in children with growth hormone deficiency. Although transient insulin resistance and mild hyperglycemia are recognized effects of growth hormone therapy, diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) in patients without prior diabetes is rare. We report a case of severe DKA shortly after initiation of lonapegsomatropin in an adolescent with hypopituitarism without preexisting glucose abnormalities. A 13-year-old boy with obesity, normal fasting glucose, and hypopituitarism receiving levothyroxine, hydrocortisone, and desmopressin replacement therapies following treatment of a suprasellar nongerminomatous germ cell tumor initiated treatment with lonapegsomatropin at 0.23 mg/kg/week. Two days after the administration of the first dose, he presented critically ill with hyperglycemia, severe metabolic acidosis, and ketonuria consistent with DKA. Hemoglobin A1c was 5.7% (reference range, <5.7%), type 1 diabetes autoantibodies were negative, and C-peptide was elevated, suggesting preserved endogenous insulin secretion. Diabetic ketoacidosis resolved with insulin and fluid therapy, and the patient remained normoglycemic after discontinuation of growth hormone therapy. This case suggests that acute LAGH-induced insulin resistance, potentially compounded by glucocorticoids and obesity, may precipitate transient DKA even in patients without prior glucose abnormalities. Close glucose monitoring should be considered when initiating LAGH therapy in high-risk patients.Keywords: long-acting growth hormone, diabetes ketoacidosis, lonapegsomatropin, hypopituitarism

Introduction

Growth hormone (GH) is essential for normal linear growth and plays a critical metabolic role in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism. In addition to promoting growth, GH functions as a counter-regulatory hormone that opposes the actions of insulin by reducing peripheral glucose uptake and increasing hepatic glucose production [1].

Recombinant human GH (rhGH) has been used clinically since its Food and Drug Administration approval in 1986, traditionally requiring daily subcutaneous injections. Challenges with adherence led to the development of long-acting GH (LAGH) formulations designed for once-weekly administration while maintaining physiological GH activity [2].

Currently approved LAGH preparations include pegylated rhGH approved in China; depot formulations approved in South Korea; albumin-binding GH analogs, such as somapacitan, and fusion protein formulations, such as somatrogon [3]. Lonapegsomatropin is a long-acting prodrug of somatropin approved in the United States for pediatric growth hormone deficiency (GHD) in 2021. These agents have demonstrated noninferiority to daily GH in maintaining normal insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) concentrations and promoting linear growth, with favorable safety profiles.

Although mild insulin resistance and transient hyperglycemia are recognized effects of GH therapy, glucose intolerance, overt diabetes, and diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) are uncommon, particularly in children without preexisting metabolic disease [4]. We describe a case of severe DKA occurring shortly after initiation of LAGH therapy with lonapegsomatropin in an adolescent with hypopituitarism and no prior history of abnormal glucose metabolism.

Case presentation

A 10-year-and-10-month-old White boy presented to the emergency department with several weeks of headache, blurred vision, polyuria, polydipsia, nausea, vomiting, and decreased appetite. His past medical history was notable for autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and anxiety. He had been treated with a stable dose of aripiprazole for 2 years. He was born full term via repeat cesarean section with a birth weight of 3.8 kg. His family history was notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus in his mother. Magnetic resonance imaging and biopsy results demonstrated a suprasellar nongerminomatous germ cell tumor. At presentation, he was prepubertal and had central hypothyroidism, central adrenal insufficiency, and arginine vasopressin deficiency (AVPD), for which he began treatment with levothyroxine, hydrocortisone, and desmopressin replacement.

His serum IGF-1 concentration was 89 ng/mL (SI: 11.65 nmol/L) (reference range for age and Tanner staging, 52-391 ng/mL [SI: 6.8-51.2 nmol/L]). He received outpatient endocrinology follow-up for hormone replacement while undergoing chemotherapy consisting of alternating cycles of carboplatin/etoposide and ifosfamide/etoposide for 6 cycles, followed by proton beam radiation therapy with 36 Gy craniospinal irradiation and a tumor bed boost to 54 Gy. He continued surveillance with hematology/oncology for recurrence and neurosurgery for a history of obstructive hydrocephalus requiring an external ventricular drain and subsequent ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement.

Two years after his tumor diagnosis and completion of therapy, progressive growth deceleration prompted an evaluation for GHD. His height was 147.5 cm (−1.37 SDS), with a height velocity (HV) of 1.5 cm/year. His midparental target height is 175.4 cm, based on a maternal height of 157.5 cm and a paternal height of 180.3 cm. His body mass index (BMI) was 35.3 kg/m2 (>140% of the 95th percentile, 2.49 SDS). He remained prepubertal with a testicular volume of 3 mL. Laboratory testing revealed an undetectable serum testosterone level (<7 ng/dL [SI: <0.24 nmol/L]) (reference range for his age, 100-1200 ng/dL [SI: 3.5-41.6 nmol/L]) and low gonadotropins, including luteinizing hormone (0.5 mIU/mL [SI: 0.5 IU/L]) (reference range for his age, 2.0-12 mIU/mL [SI: 2.0-12.0 IU/L]) and follicle-stimulating hormone (0.4 mIU/mL [SI: 0.4 IU/L]) (reference range for his age, 1.0-12.4 mIU/mL [SI: 1.0-12.0 IU/L]). His bone age was similar to his chronological age. A repeat serum IGF-1 concentration was 79 ng/mL [SI: 10.3 nmol/L]. His baseline fasting serum glucose was 92 mg/dL [SI: 5.1 mmol/L] (reference range, 70-99 mg/dL [SI: 3.9-5.5 mmol/L]), and there was no prior indication of impaired glucose tolerance or diabetes. Growth hormone stimulation testing using arginine and clonidine revealed severe GHD, with a peak stimulated GH of 0.73 ng/mL [SI: 0.73 µg/L] (reference peak response, >5 ng/mL [SI: >5 µg/L]). Lonapegsomatropin was initiated at 0.23 mg/kg/week after clearance from hematology/oncology. Concurrent medications included guanfacine and aripiprazole.

Two days after the first injection, he developed persistent vomiting and received oral stress-dose hydrocortisone at home. He subsequently presented to the emergency department with hyperglycemia and metabolic acidosis consistent with DKA.

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory evaluation upon presentation to the emergency department revealed marked hyperglycemia with a serum glucose of 333 mg/dL [SI: 18.5 mmol/L], severe metabolic acidosis with a bicarbonate of 6 mmol/L (reference range, 22-28 mmol/L), an anion gap of 20 mmol/L (reference range, 8-12 mmol/L), a venous pH of 7.19 (reference range, 7.32-7.42), a base excess of −20 mmol/L (reference range, −2 to +2 mmol/L), and ketonuria on urinalysis, findings consistent with DKA. Hemoglobin A1c was 5.7% (reference range, <5.7%). Notably, fasting glucose had been normal 3 weeks prior at 92 mg/dL [SI: 5.1 mmol/L].

His electrolytes at presentation were as follows: sodium 144 mmol/L (reference range, 135-145 mmol/L), chloride 118 mmol/L (reference range, 98-107 mmol/L), and potassium 4.7 mmol/L (reference range, 3.5-5.0 mmol/L). Additional serum testing included creatinine 1.3 mg/dL [SI: 115 µmol/L] (reference range, 0.58-1.01 mg/dL [SI: 51-89 µmol/L]), blood urea nitrogen 4 mg/dL [SI: 1.4 mmol/L] (reference range, 7-20 mg/dL [SI: 2.5-7.1 mmol/L]), calcium 10.6 mg/dL [SI: 2.65 mmol/L] (reference range, 8.5-10.5 mg/dL [SI: 2.1-2.6 mmol/L]), and phosphorus 2.7 mg/dL [SI: 0.87 mmol/L] (reference range, 3.0-4.5 mg/dL [SI: 0.97-1.45 mmol/L]).

Hypoalbuminemia and hypoproteinemia were also noted, with an albumin level of 2.9 g/dL [SI: 29 g/L] (reference range, 3.5-5.0 g/dL [SI: 35-50 g/L]) and a total protein concentration of 5.9 g/dL [SI: 59 g/L] (reference range, 6.4-8.3 g/dL [SI: 64-83 g/L]).

An immunologic evaluation for type 1 diabetes was normal, including GAD65, IA-2, ZnT8, and insulin autoantibodies. C-peptide measured during hospitalization was elevated at 26.6 ng/mL [SI: 8.8 nmol/L] (reference range, 1.1-4.4 ng/mL [SI: 0.36-1.46 nmol/L]) consistent with severe insulin resistance. An unstimulated GH concentration 4 days after the lonapegsomatropin injection was 9.6 ng/mL [SI: 9.6 µg/L], and IGF-1 measured at the same time was 333 ng/mL [SI: 43.6 nmol/L].

Treatment

Diabetic ketoacidosis was managed with intravenous fluids at up to twice maintenance rates and a continuous insulin infusion initiated at 0.1 units/kg/hour and titrated as needed (up to 0.3 units/kg/hour). After 48 hours, the DKA resolved, and he was transitioned off the insulin infusion. A single subcutaneous dose of long-acting insulin (insulin glargine, 46 units) was administered, after which serum glucose levels remained within the normal range, and no further insulin was required for the rest of the hospitalization. Because of his underlying AVPD and elevation of his serum sodium level to 149 mmol/L, a vasopressin infusion (0.5 mU/kg/hour) was required during the acute phase of his critical illness and was later tapered. Hydrocortisone dosing was increased to stress-dose levels, up to 175 mg/m2/day on the first day of his pediatric intensive care unit stay and subsequently tapered back to physiologic replacement doses. He was discharged from the hospital 7 days after lonapegsomatropin administration. Growth hormone therapy was discontinued. Chronological laboratory results and medications are outlined in Table 1.

Outcome and follow-up

Subsequent outpatient glucose monitoring demonstrated normal blood glucose values without recurrence of hyperglycemia. Over 2 years of endocrinology follow-up, he did not develop persistent dysglycemia or require insulin therapy.

Despite metabolic recovery, his growth velocity remained poor, with a continued decline in height standard deviation score (Figure 1). Multiple attempts to reinitiate daily GH therapy were unsuccessful because his insurance plan specifically excludes coverage for GH treatment, and he did not qualify for available patient assistance programs.

Ongoing evaluation of pubertal hormones demonstrated persistently low gonadotropins with undetectable testosterone levels, consistent with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. Treatment with testosterone was initiated in conjunction with aromatase inhibition in an attempt to delay skeletal maturation and optimize adult height potential.

Discussion

Growth hormone is a potent counter-regulatory hormone that opposes the effects of insulin by reducing peripheral glucose uptake and enhancing hepatic glucose production. It also stimulates lipolysis, leading to increased circulating free fatty acids, which further interfere with insulin signaling and contribute to insulin resistance [5].

Long-acting growth hormone formulations were developed to improve treatment adherence while maintaining physiologic GH activity. In the phase 3 heiGHt trial, compared to daily somatropin, once-weekly lonapegsomatropin resulted in significantly higher annualized HV (11.2 ± 0.2 cm/year vs 10.3 ± 0.3 cm/year; P = .009) and greater gain in height SDS at 52 weeks (Δ height SDS 1.10 ± 0.04 vs 0.96 ± 0.05; P = .01), with similar safety and tolerability [6]. At 56 weeks, there were no clinically meaningful differences in average hemoglobin A1c and fasting glucose values, which remained within the normal range in both the weekly lonapegsomatropin and daily somatropin groups [6].

However, LAGH formulations produce more sustained GH exposure compared with daily therapy, which may lead to prolonged hepatic glucose production and increased lipolysis. This sustained metabolic effect could reflect GH-induced insulin resistance in susceptible individuals.

Prolonged exposure to excess GH, such as in patients with acromegaly, is well known to impair glucose metabolism [7]. In adult cohorts with acromegaly, the prevalence of diabetes ranges from approximately 16% to 56% [8]. Observational studies in adults have reported increased incidences of diabetes during GH therapy, particularly in individuals with preexisting metabolic risk factors. For example, a study involving over 5000 adult patients found that 10% developed diabetes during treatment [9].

Pediatric studies have generally not demonstrated overt diabetes but have shown increased insulin resistance and mild elevations in hemoglobin A1c over time, rather than clinically significant hyperglycemia. For example, a study by Pellegrin et al assessed the risk of metabolic disturbances in children treated with GH. This retrospective study analyzed data from 101 patients and did not observe any cases of overt diabetes mellitus (DM). However, the investigators found an increase in hemoglobin A1c levels and greater insulin resistance after 1 year of GH therapy [10].

To our knowledge, acute DKA occurring shortly after initiation of LAGH in a child without prior glucose abnormalities has not been previously described. Isolated case reports describe DKA in children treated with daily GH, often in patients with underlying insulin resistance or prediabetes [11]. Only 1 prior case has reported hyperglycemia requiring insulin therapy after LAGH [12]. In that report, the patient, similar to ours, was also started on lonapegsomatropin. However, he had a preexisting history of insulin resistance and an elevated hemoglobin A1c of 6.6% and did not present with DKA [12]. In contrast, our patient had no prior evidence of impaired glucose metabolism and developed acute DKA shortly after initiation of LAGH therapy.

In this patient, several factors likely contributed to acute metabolic decompensation. Growth hormone-induced insulin resistance was likely the primary driver, but obesity-related baseline insulin resistance, exposure to stress-dose glucocorticoids during this acute illness, and concurrent aripiprazole use may have further impaired glucose metabolism and promoted ketosis. The temporal relationship between lonapegsomatropin initiation and DKA onset, normal baseline blood glucose, absence of diabetes autoantibodies, significantly elevated C-peptide, elevated GH, and complete resolution of hyperglycemia without ongoing insulin therapy strongly support a transient, GH-induced insulin resistant state with preserved endogenous insulin secretion.

This case highlights the complex metabolic interplay between GH, insulin sensitivity, and glucocorticoids, particularly in patients with obesity and multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies. Clinicians should be aware that acute metabolic decompensation may occur shortly after initiation of LAGH therapy in susceptible individuals.

Learning points

Long-acting growth hormone therapy can precipitate acute, severe insulin resistance and DKA in high-risk patients, particularly adolescents with obesity and hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction.
