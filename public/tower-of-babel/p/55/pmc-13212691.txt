Cost Effectiveness of First-Line Therapies for Treatment-Naïve Chronic Lymphocytic Leukaemia in South Africa
Authors: Rochelle Woudberg, Edina Sinanovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesChronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) is a common adult leukaemia, and selecting the most effective first-line treatment is crucial for optimising patient outcomes and managing healthcare costs. While chemoimmunotherapy (CIT) has been the standard approach, targeted therapies offer promising alternatives for treatment-naïve CLL patients. The objective of this study was to evaluate the cost effectiveness of chemoimmunotherapy compared to targeted therapies for treatment-naïve CLL patients in South Africa.MethodsA cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted using a Markov model based on three health states: progression-free survival (PFS), progression, and death. The model employed a 15-year time horizon and a 1-month cycle length. Patient-level data were reconstructed, and parametric estimation was used to project long-term clinical outcomes. Cost estimates were derived from national tariffs, reflecting a South African public healthcare perspective, while utilities were sourced from published literature. Outcomes were measured in total costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) were calculated and compared to a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold, and sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of the results.ResultsAmong the evaluated treatment strategies, chlorambucil-plus-obinutuzumab (ClbO) had the lowest cost and served as the reference comparator. Both CIT regimens were cost effective, with fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab (FCR) yielding an ICER of US$1645.52 per QALY gained and bendamustine-plus-rituximab (BR) had an ICER of US$1716.79 per QALY gained, both below the US$3407 WTP threshold, under the model assumptions. Ibrutinib generated the highest QALYs but at a higher cost, resulting in an ICER of US$19,679.52 per QALY gained and a 0% probability of being cost effective at the WTP threshold, while venetoclax-plus-obinutuzumab (VenO) was extendedly dominated, and therefore eliminated from the results. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings across variations in key parameters.ConclusionIn the South African public healthcare setting, CIT regimens (FCR and BR) represent cost-effective first-line treatment strategies for symptomatic, treatment-naïve CLL. Bendamustine-plus-rituximab emerged as the most decision-robust option under uncertainty, while FCR yielded the lowest point-estimate ICER. Among CIT regimens, FCR may be preferred in fit patients, while BR represents a more decision-robust option in older or unfit populations. Targeted therapies such as ibrutinib and VenO, despite superior clinical efficacy, are not cost effective at current prices. Substantial price reductions, generic entry, or targeted use in high-risk subgroups may improve their value and enable equitable access within South Africa’s resource-constrained health system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40261-026-01538-y.

Key Points

Fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab and bendamustine-plus-rituximab were identified as cost-effective treatment options within the South African willingness-to-pay threshold.Our study suggests that targeted therapies such as ibrutinib, despite clinical benefits, were not cost effective due to high costs.There is a need for price negotiations and selective use of targeted therapies in appropriate patient subgroups to improve affordability.

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) is a lymphoproliferative B-cell malignancy of the blood, bone marrow and lymphocytic system and the most prevalent form of leukaemia in adults [1, 2]. Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia is characterised by the clonal accumulation of morphologically mature but immunologically dysfunctional B-cell lymphocytes [1]. In 2019, an estimated 103,467 new cases of CLL and 44,613 related deaths were reported globally, accounting for approximately 22%–30% of all leukaemia cases [3]. Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia is also the most common adult leukaemia in South Africa, comprising around 22% of leukaemia cases, with an age-standardised incidence rate of 1.03 per 100,000 population [2]. In recent years, 5-year survival rates have significantly improved from 78.5% for cases diagnosed in 2000 to 89.3% in 2015, according to US Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) programme data [4].

The clinical course of CLL varies widely among patients. Approximately 70%–80% of patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis and managed with a watch-and-wait approach, while the remaining 20%–30% present with progressive disease requiring intervention [5, 6]. For these symptomatic patients, treatment aims to prolong survival and improve quality of life, although a cure remains elusive [7].

Over the past two decades, the therapeutic landscape of CLL has evolved from traditional chemotherapy to chemoimmunotherapy (CIT), and more recently, to targeted therapies such as Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors and B-cell leukaemia/lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) antagonists [8]. Ibrutinib, the first BTK inhibitor approved for first-line use, marked a significant advancement in CLL treatment [9], followed by venetoclax, a BCL-2 inhibitor approved for adult CLL patients [10]. These targeted agents have demonstrated improved efficacy and tolerability, prompting international guidelines to endorse them as first-line therapy irrespective of age, comorbidity, or genetic risk [6, 11]. While targeted therapies are now recommended in most cases, CIT regimens remain an option in select patients (e.g., fit individuals with mutated immunoglobulin heavy variable chain gene [IGHV] and no high-risk features) when targeted therapies are inaccessible due to cost or availability in resource-limited settings, whereas traditional chemotherapy alone is no longer recommended as a first-line treatment.

Current international guidelines recommend a range of first-line options for treatment-naïve CLL patients, including CIT regimens such as fludarabine-cyclophosphamide-rituximab (FCR), bendamustine-rituximab (BR), and chlorambucil-obinutuzumab (ClbO), as well as targeted therapies like ibrutinib and venetoclax (monotherapy or in combination with obinutuzumab [VenO]) [6, 11]. Although these newer therapies have demonstrated superior efficacy in clinical trials, their high acquisition costs and associated budget impact have raised affordability concerns, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.

To estimate the economic effects of new therapeutic alternatives and personalised treatments in CLL for payers, economic evaluations should be carried out using various modelling methods [12]. In South Africa, targeted therapies for CLL such as ibrutinib and venetoclax have been included on the national Essential Medicines List (EML), enabling access through the public healthcare system [13]. However, their integration into routine care presents a significant economic challenge, and evidence on their cost effectiveness within the local context remains limited.

Given the financial constraints of South Africa’s public health sector, there is an urgent need for economic evaluations that support fair and efficient resource allocation in cancer care. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the cost effectiveness of first-line therapy, including both chemoimmunotherapy and targeted therapies, for treatment-naïve CLL patients in South Africa, from the public healthcare system (government payer) perspective.

Methods

Study Overview

A Markov cohort simulation model was used to evaluate the cost effectiveness of first-line treatment therapies for treatment-naïve patients with CLL from the perspective of the South African public healthcare system [14]. The analysis compared five treatment strategies, including three CIT regimens: (1) fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab (FCR), (2) bendamustine-plus-rituximab (BR), and (3) chlorambucil-plus-obinutuzumab (ClbO), and two targeted therapy regimens: (4) venetoclax-plus-obinutuzumab (VenO) and (5) ibrutinib. These treatments were selected based on their inclusion in international clinical guidelines for first-line CLL therapy, their relevance to the South African treatment landscape, local clinical expert opinions, and their availability or potential for access within the public healthcare sector, including the national EML [6, 11, 13].

In this analysis, ClbO was identified as the lowest-cost strategy among the five modelled strategies and was therefore used as the reference comparator for the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) calculations.

The model simulated a hypothetical cohort of treatment-naïve CLL patients over a 15-year time horizon, which was selected to approximate a lifetime horizon from initiation of first-line treatment, capturing the majority of expected survival benefits and costs over the remaining lifespan of most symptomatic CLL patients [6]. A 1-month (i.e., 28-day) cycle length was used to align with a typical CLL treatment cycle. An annual discount rate of 5% was applied to costs and effects, consistent with South African pharmacoeconomic guidelines, and converted into a monthly discount for the running of the Markov cycles [15]. Model outcomes included total costs, quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), and ICERs. Fully incremental analysis was conducted, including dominance and extended dominance checks. Probabilities, cost estimates, and QALYs were varied to account for uncertainty in our model parameters. The model was developed and analyzed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).

Markov Model

The Markov model was structured with three mutually exclusive health states: (1) PFS, (2) progression (including refractory/relapsed lines) and (3) death. In the model, PFS was defined as the period from treatment initiation until disease progression while receiving or having completed first-line therapy; progression (including refractory/relapsed disease) was defined as patients who experience disease progression or relapse after first-line therapy and enter subsequent lines of treatment or supportive care [6]. This three-state model is consistent with published literature evaluating CLL treatments [16–20], and aligns with ESMO and NCCN guidelines [6, 11, 21]. The baseline characteristics of the model cohort (e.g., age, fitness status, IGHV mutation status, and other prognostic factors) were based on the respective populations enrolled in the five pivotal published clinical trials informing each treatment strategy (Supplementary Table S1). The patient cohorts were derived from the CLL-8 trial (NCT00281918) for FCR (vs fludarabine plus cyclophosphamide), the ALLIANCE A041202 trial (NCT01886872) for BR (vs ibrutinib ± rituximab), the iLLUMINATE trial (NCT02264574) for ClbO (vs ibrutinib plus obinutuzumab), the CLL14 trial (NCT02242942) for VenO (vs chlorambucil plus obinutuzumab), and the RESONATE-2 trial (NCT01722487) for ibrutinib (vs chlorambucil) [22–26]. The model represents a general population of symptomatic, treatment-naïve CLL patients initiating first-line therapy. No exclusions were applied for age, fitness level, or TP53 status, to improve generalisability and consistency with current guideline recommendations.

All patients enter the model in the PFS state and initiate one of the five first-line treatments. Each month, patients can remain in their current state or transition to a more severe health state. Dosing for each line of treatment was based on the ESMO and NCCN guidelines [6, 11].

The progression state represents the time from first treatment relapse until death, therefore including the possible sequence of remission and relapse of subsequent lines of treatment common to this disease area. After progression, a patient can either remain in the same health state or die at the end of each cycle. Transition to death is possible from any health state and includes all-cause mortality.

Furthermore, it was assumed that treatment-induced adverse events (AEs) of grade 3/4 occurred during the first month of treatment in the PFS state, reflecting peak incidence during initiation/ramp-up phases, as observed in pivotal trials for each regimen, and were captured through one-off costs and disutilities rather than separate health states. This approach is consistent with published oncology economic models where severe AEs are short-lived, reversible events that do not define long-term disease states, and therefore do not warrant additional structural health states [16–20]. It was assumed that all patients would be in only one health state in any 1-month single cycle, reflecting the mutually exclusive nature of Markov health states and standard cohort modelling practice.

Transition Probabilities

The annual transition probabilities among the Markov health states were estimated based on data from the results of randomised clinical trials. Specifically, transition probabilities were derived from parametric survival models fitted to PFS and overall survival (OS) data obtained from Phase III randomised clinical trials in treatment naïve symptomatic CLL patients comparing FCR, BR, ClbO, VenO and ibrutinib.

The primary source for FCR strategy survival data was the CLL-8 randomised Phase III trial [22]. For BR, ClbO, and VenO strategies, we used the survival data from ALLIANCE A041202, iLLUMINATE, and CLL14 trials, respectively [23–25]. For ibrutinib, survival data were obtained from the RESONATE-2 Phase III trial [26]. These trials were selected as they represent the pivotal Phase III studies in treatment-naïve CLL populations with broadly comparable eligibility criteria, follow-up duration, and outcome definitions, thereby providing the most clinically robust and methodologically consistent evidence base available for first-line CLL treatment.

Because no head-to-head randomised trials comparing all evaluated first-line regimens were available, formal indirect treatment comparison methods (e.g., network meta-analysis or matching-adjusted indirect comparison) were not undertaken. This was due to heterogeneity in trial designs, inclusion criteria, follow-up durations, and risk-profile distributions across studies, which would violate key assumptions required for valid adjusted indirect comparisons. Instead, a naïve indirect comparison of reconstructed trial-specific survival curves was performed, consistent with approaches adopted in prior cost-effectiveness evaluations of CLL therapies, particularly in resource-limited settings where existing indirect comparisons indicate broadly similar efficacy across treatment strategies. Comparative effectiveness data are unavailable [27–29]. Cross-trial heterogeneity may introduce uncertainty in comparative effectiveness estimates and therefore bias cost-effectiveness results. This uncertainty was explored through deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses applied to all survival model parameters.

Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves for both PFS and OS were digitised using Plot Digitizer (Free Software Foundation Inc., Boston, MA), and individual patient data were reconstructed using the method by Guyot et al [30]. These reconstructed data were extrapolated over a 15-year lifetime horizon using standard parametric survival modelling techniques, including exponential, Weibull, log-normal, log-logistic, Gompertz, and generalised gamma distributions [17, 31]. Model fitting and parameter estimation were conducted in Microsoft Excel®, using Solver® to minimise the sum of squared errors between observed and predicted survival values. Model selection was guided by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and visual goodness-of-fit. Model fit statistics (AIC and BIC) for all candidate parametric survival distributions across treatments are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Using the deviance information criteria and best fit, the log-normal distribution offered the best fit for PFS of FCR, BR, VenO and ibrutinib, whereas the exponential distribution offered the best fit for PFS of ClbO. Additionally, the exponential distribution offered the best fit for extrapolating the OS data across all strategies. These distributions were used for estimating time-varying transition probabilities between health states (Fig. 2). Monthly transition probabilities were time-varying, generated from the parametric survival functions for PFS and OS, rather than assuming constant transition risks over time. They were derived from survival functions using the formula Transition probability =1-(St/S(t-1)), where S(t) is the survival probability at time t [32]. These values informed monthly transitions from PFS to progression and from progression to death. Figure 2 illustrates the fitted survival curves underpinning the transition probability estimation.

The direct medical costs were associated with drug treatment, drug administration, pre-medication and supportive care, routine follow-ups via consultations, laboratory tests, and management of AEs were included (Table 1). For simplification, only AEs that occurred with a frequency of at least 5% and only grade 3/4 events were considered.

All alternatives were assumed to be administered for a fixed time duration of six 28-day cycles for FCR, BR, and ClbO, and twelve 28-day cycles for VenO, consistent with published guidelines. Ibrutinib was modelled as a continuous daily therapy until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity.

Following disease progression, all patients were assumed to receive venetoclax in combination with rituximab (Ven+R) as second-line treatment. This regimen is supported by the NCCN guidelines as the preferred option for patients with relapsed or refractory CLL and was selected because it had not been included among the evaluated first-line treatment strategies [11, 33]. Importantly, in the South African public sector, access to alternative novel agents in the relapsed setting remains limited, while venetoclax-based regimens are increasingly available through tertiary oncology services and the South African Health Products Regulatory Authority (SAHPRA) special access programmes [34]. Therefore, assuming a uniform Ven+R second-line strategy reflects a pragmatic and clinically realistic pathway within current public-sector constraints, while avoiding additional cross-trial comparisons that could introduce further uncertainty.

Prophylactic granulocyte colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) support was considered for all treatment strategies as it aids in the tolerance of side effects of chemotherapy. Since Venetoclax can cause rapid death of CLL cells and tumour reduction, there is potential for Tumour Lysis Syndrome (TLS) in patients treated with VenO [35]. Therefore, in this model all patients in the VenO strategy were assumed to receive a TLS prophylaxis regimen prior to initial dose of VenO. During treatment, patients were assumed to receive routine monthly follow-ups including consultations with a specialist and blood tests. Routine monitoring of progressed patients included an initial CT scan, bone marrow biopsy and cytogenetic testing, and monthly consultations with a specialist and blood tests. The model excludes any indirect costs and costs related to intensive supportive care during a terminal phase of the disease.

Cost inputs were obtained from official public-sector sources. Drug prices were determined from the database of medicine prices [34], consultation and hospitalisation costs were obtained from the SA uniform patient fee schedule (UPFS) [36], and laboratory tests costs from National Health Laboratory Services (NHLS) 2018 price lists and inflated to 2024 prices [37]. All costs are presented in US dollars, using the average 2024 exchange rate (US$1 = ZAR17.85).

Utility Values

Utility values were used to quantify health-related quality of life associated with each CLL health state in the model. Due to the lack of published South Africa-specific data on CLL health utilities, values were sourced from Kosmas et al [38], a multicenter study that elicited societal preferences in the UK general public for in CLL patients using the EQ-5D-3L instrument. This source was selected because it reported utility values for clinically distinct CLL disease phases (progression-free differentiated by route of administration, and progressed disease), directly aligning with the health states in our model, and because the study population was comparable to the target population in terms of age distribution, treatment exposure, and disease stage at therapy initiation. These characteristics support the generalisability of the utility estimates to the present analysis.

Utility values generally range between 0 (death) and 1 (perfect health) [39]. All utility values were assumed to apply per Markov cycle (i.e., 1 month) and remain constant within each cycle. This approach is standard practice in Markov and partitioned survival models, where utilities represent average quality of life weights over the cycle duration. If longer cycle lengths were used, the same utilities would be applied over that cycle interval, with QALYs accrued as the product of utility and time spent in each health state. The utility values were used to calculate QALY endpoints, which were discounted at an annual rate of 5%. Table 2 shows the utilities associated with the various health states modelled.

Cost-Effectiveness Analysis

The model outcomes were evaluated over a 15-year horizon. For each treatment strategy, total accumulated costs and QALYs were estimated. The strategy with the lowest total cost, ClbO in this analysis, was designated as the reference comparator. The ICERs were calculated for each of the remaining strategies by dividing the difference in total cost by the difference in QALYs gained relative to the comparator, with results expressed as cost per QALY gained.

To determine cost effectiveness, this study applied a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of US$3407 per QALY gained, based on the opportunity cost-based estimate proposed by Edoka and Stacey for healthcare decision-making in South Africa [40]. This threshold reflects the marginal productivity of South Africa’s public health sector (i.e., the health benefits forgone by displacing other interventions) and is substantially lower than the historically used WHO GDP-based thresholds of 1–3× GDP per capita (US$6428–19,284). Specifically, it corresponds to approximately 0.53 times South Africa’s 2024 GDP per capita (GDP per capita = US$6428), thereby providing a context-specific benchmark for local resource allocation decisions [40, 41].

Although the Edoka and Stacey estimate was originally derived using disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), the conceptual equivalence between DALYs and QALYs are inverse measures of health outcomes (both representing comparable units of health gain); therefore, a WTP value to avert one DALY is conventionally interpreted as equivalent to the WTP to gain one QALY. This allows a direct application of the DALY-based threshold to QALYs. This approach is widely adopted in cost-effectiveness analyses and routinely used in cost-effectiveness literature given the conceptual similarity to QALYs. Accordingly, a treatment strategy was deemed cost effective if its ICER was below or equal to this WTP threshold.

Sensitivity Analysis

Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the uncertainty of variables used in the model and determine the robustness of the results. Deterministic one-way sensitivity analyses were performed by adjusting parameters within specified ranges and their effects on the ICER were observed. For each parameter, the lower and upper limits were set based on their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) or, in the absence of specific data, using benchmark values adjusted by ±50% of the base-case value, in accordance with South African HTA guidelines [15]. Transition probabilities were capped at 1, regardless of the variations from the +50% range, and drug prices were adjusted according to available literature. Discount rates were varied between 0% and 10%, as recommended in the HTA methods guide [15].

To account for parameter uncertainty, probabilistic sensitivity analysis was conducted involving 1000 Monte Carlo iterations, in which multiple input parameters were varied simultaneously. Gamma distributions were assigned to cost inputs, while beta distributions were used for probabilities and health state utilities. Simulation outputs were visualised using a cost-effectiveness scatter plot displaying incremental costs and QALYs. Additionally, cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs) were generated to illustrate the probability of each treatment strategy being cost effective across a range of WTP ($/QALY) thresholds.

Results

Base-Case Analysis

The results of this study are expressed in terms of total costs, QALYs, and ICER (Table 3). Among the evaluated treatment strategies for treatment-naïve CLL patients, ClbO had the lowest cost of US$8371.98 and was the least effective with 4.75 QALYs. All other strategies were found to be more effective but more expensive than the ClbO strategy within the modelled framework. Overall, the total cost for FCR was US$11,622.93, VenO was US$13,425.52, and BR was US$13,659.94. Ibrutinib is associated with the highest costs of US$62,527.43, which is mainly due to the drug price and continuous treatment regimen.

Conversely, ibrutinib was associated with the highest quality of life with 10.39 QALYs, followed by BR with 7.91 QALYs. When compared incrementally, FCR had an ICER of US$1645.52 per QALY gained over ClbO and was considered potentially cost effective under the model assumptions. However, VenO was extendedly dominated as it provided a very small additional health gain compared with FCR (6.82 vs 6.72 QALYs) at a higher cost, while BR achieved greater health gains at a similar cost. This indicates relative inefficiency within the economic model rather than definitive clinical inferiority. In practical terms, VenO is therefore an inefficient option, as moving from FCR directly to BR yields more health benefit for comparable additional cost. Venetoclax-plus-obinutuzumab was thus excluded from further incremental comparisons. Bendamustine-plus-rituximab emerged as the next non-dominated strategy with an ICER of US$1716.79 per QALY gained compared to FCR. Ibrutinib was associated with the highest health benefit and higher total costs, with an incremental cost of US$48,867.49 and 2.48 additional QALYs over BR, resulting in an ICER of US$19,679.52 per QALY gained. All non-dominated treatment strategies (FCR and BR) for treatment-naïve CLL patients met the WTP thresholds of US$3407 per QALY gained, indicating cost effectiveness in the South African context, conditional on the model structure, parameter inputs, and reconstructed cross-trial survival data. Although ibrutinib offers clinical benefit, given its high cost it was found not to be cost effective in the South African context under current assumptions.

Sensitivity Analysis

The deterministic one-way sensitivity analysis evaluated how uncertainty in key model parameters influenced ICERs relative to ClbO and assessed the robustness of the base-case findings. For example, in the comparison of FCR versus ClbO, the probability of remaining in PFS state was the most influential parameter. Higher PFS probabilities for FCR lowered its ICER, indicating that assumptions regarding durability of first-line treatment response strongly influence the modelled cost effectiveness of FCR relative to ClbO. Similarly, for both BR versus ClbO and ibrutinib versus ClbO, the probability of remaining in PFS was also the key driver, highlighting that uncertainty in relative effectiveness parameters derived from reconstructed survival data is the primary source of model uncertainty. For VenO versus ClbO, the probability of remaining in the progression state and the utility value assigned to PFS had the greatest impact on the ICER. Overall, the results indicate that treatment efficacy parameters, particularly uncertainty in long-term PFS assumptions, exert more influence on cost effectiveness than cost inputs, supporting the robustness of base-case conclusions.

The probabilistic sensitivity analysis, which simultaneously varies all parameters across 1000 simulations, further assessed joint parameter uncertainty. The ICER scatter plots (Fig. 4) show the distribution of simulated ICERs relative to the WTP threshold line, with the 95% confidence ellipse indicating uncertainty around each comparison. At the South African WTP threshold of US$3407 per QALY, BR demonstrated a 100% probability of being cost effective compared with ClbO, while FCR showed an 81% probability, VenO a 61% probability, and ibrutinib a 0% probability. Although Table 3 reports that FCR has the lowest deterministic ICER, the probabilistic sensitivity analysis indicates that BR has the highest probability of being cost effective because a larger proportion of its simulations fall below the WTP threshold. This reflects greater decision robustness under parameter uncertainty rather than definitive comparative clinical superiority.

Discussion

Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia remains incurable, with treatment reserved for symptomatic or progressive disease to improve quality of life and prolong survival [42]. This study provides the first cost-effectiveness analysis comparing established CIT regimens with newer targeted therapies for symptomatic, treatment-naïve CLL patients in the South African public healthcare system. The evaluated options (ClbO, FCR, BR, VenO, ibrutinib) are all in routine clinical use and represent the current spectrum of therapies available or potentially accessible nationally.

In the base-case analysis, ClbO had the lowest cost but also the lowest health benefit under the model assumptions. Both CIT regimens, FCR and BR, produced substantial additional QALYs at ICERs well below the South African opportunity-cost threshold of US$3407 per QALY [40], indicating potential good value for money in the public sector within the modelled framework. Venetoclax-plus-obinutuzumab (VenO) was extendedly dominated because it offered only marginal additional benefit over FCR at higher cost, while BR provided larger health gains for a similar cost increase, making VenO relatively inefficient within the model and therefore excluded from further incremental comparisons. Ibrutinib generated the highest total QALYs but at a substantially higher cost, resulting in an ICER far above the WTP threshold and a 0% probability of being cost effective at current prices.

The sensitivity analysis showed that BR emerged as the most robustly cost-effective strategy in probabilistic sensitivity analysis, with a 100% probability of cost effectiveness at the threshold, reflecting greater decision robustness under parameter uncertainty rather than clear comparative treatment superiority. Fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab also showed strong deterministic cost effectiveness but with greater uncertainty. In contrast, targeted therapies (VenO and ibrutinib) were dominated or highly cost ineffective at current prices, primarily due to substantially higher acquisition costs and (for ibrutinib) continuous administration, under current pricing and model assumptions.

These findings align with prior international economic evaluations in high-income settings, where CIT regimens like FCR and BR have been cost effective for fit patients [18, 19, 43], while targeted therapies such as ibrutinib often exceed thresholds at list prices [44, 45]. Studies in the USA, Canada, and Denmark, have found VenO to be cost effective compared to ClbO [46, 47], although such findings depend heavily on pricing and may not be generalisable to resource-limited contexts. However, a study by Hegde et al [48] demonstrated that generic ibrutinib could be cost effective in low-resource settings, suggesting that generics or negotiated pricing could make BTK inhibitors viable. In South Africa, the low opportunity cost threshold underscores the need for careful resource allocation in the public sector, where displacing other interventions has high health consequences.

From a policy perspective, these findings show that under current pricing, South African payers face a prioritisation choice using CIT regimens as preferred first-line therapies, while broader access to targeted therapies would require substantial price reductions to achieve cost effectiveness. Thus, the results provide quantitative evidence to inform price negotiations and future reimbursement decisions. In practice, FCR may be preferentially used in younger, fit patients with favourable prognostic features, while BR may be favoured in older or unfit individuals due to its more favourable tolerability profile and greater decision robustness under uncertainty.

The study has several limitations. There is a lack of survival data after the follow-up period in the clinical trials; therefore, long-term survival data were simulated by survival modelling. This may introduce some bias into the model results, potentially leading to either overestimation or underestimation of survival outcomes, depending on the extrapolation method and distribution selected [49]. The use of naïve indirect comparisons may introduce bias due to unmeasured cross-trial heterogeneity; this is mitigated through the sensitivity analysis. Furthermore, subgroup-specific modelling (e.g., restricted to older or unfit patients) was not undertaken because published trial-level subgroup survival data were insufficient to reliably reconstruct separate PFS and OS curves for each treatment. This represents an area for future research as more real-world or stratified clinical data become available. Additionally, our analysis only considered AEs with a frequency of at least 5% and only grade 3/4 events. The sensitivity analyses in this study, however, confirmed that changes in the probability of occurrence of these adverse reactions would not affect the results. Lastly, the study did not consider the impact of co-morbidities on the treatment pathway for each treatment strategy, the exclusion of this consideration was due to a lack of available data.

Despite these limitations, model validity was supported through expert clinical input, internal consistency checks, and benchmarking of survival and QALY outputs against published international models, with sensitivity analyses confirming stability of the main findings within plausible parameter ranges.

Conclusion

In the South African public healthcare context, CIT regimens (FCR and BR) represent the most cost-effective first-line treatment strategies for symptomatic, treatment-naïve CLL at the opportunity cost-based threshold of US$3407 per QALY, conditional on the model structure, assumptions, and reconstructed cross-trial survival data. Targeted therapies such as ibrutinib and VenO, despite superior clinical efficacy, are not cost effective at current prices due to high acquisition costs and continuous administration requirements. Probabilistic analysis indicates that BR is the most decision-robust option under uncertainty, while FCR yields the lowest point-estimate ICER but with greater uncertainty. Policymakers may therefore consider either regimen as economically efficient, with selection guided by clinical appropriateness and patient characteristics, with FCR more suitable for younger, fit patients and BR representing a more robust option for older or unfit individuals. Targeted therapies should be reserved for select high-risk cases or made accessible through aggressive price negotiations, generic entry, or managed access programmes. Prioritising CITs could allow broader coverage for more patients in the South African public sector, while targeted therapies, if priced competitively, could address unmet needs in high-risk subgroups. Such strategies could improve equity and sustainability in cancer care within South Africa's resource-constrained public sector.
