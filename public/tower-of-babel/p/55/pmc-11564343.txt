Endoscopic transnasal prelacrimal recess approach via the orbital floor to the infraorbital region: an anatomical study
Authors: Changrui Su, Wenlong Tang, Jinsheng Qiao, Wenchao Liu, Bin Hu, Kangda Huang, Qingguo Liu, Long Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe aim of this study is to describe the maximum exposure of the infraorbital region via the orbital floor using the transnasal prelacrimal recess approach (PLRA), and to provide an anatomical basis for treating lesions in the infraorbital region.MethodsTen freshly injected frozen heads were dissected using the PLRA. The orbital floor was removed along the border of the medial infraorbital quadrangle, and the periorbita was opened to expose the infraorbital region. The areas of the medial infraorbital quadrangles were measured and analyzed. The PLRA was applied separately on the left and right sides of each cadaver head, resulting in a total of 20 prelacrimal recess approaches.ResultsThe PLRA enabled visualization of the optic nerve and the central retinal artery through the orbital floor. By integrating both medial and lateral approaches in relation to the inferior rectus muscle, all crucial anatomical structures within the infraorbital region could be clearly identified. The area of the medial infraorbital quadrangle was 420.65 ± 24.03 mm2.ConclusionThe PLRA provides access through the orbital floor to the maximum boundary of the infraorbital region, including the lateral orbital wall at the outermost level, the superior rectus muscle at the topmost level, and the medial orbital wall at the innermost level.

Introduction

In recent years, the advancement of high-resolution imaging systems and improved endoscopic equipment has led to the gradual application of neuroendoscopy in the treatment of orbital lesions. Endoscopic technology has now expanded its range of applications to include skull bases and cranial orbital interfaces [1–5]. This expansion in the scope of endoscopic surgery has opened new possibilities for treating various conditions.

In 2007, to address the issue of sacrificing the nasolacrimal duct and inferior turbinate during the treatment of maxillary sinus lesions via medial maxillectomy, Zhou et al. introduced the prelacrimal recess approach (PLRA) [6]. This approach was first used to completely remove maxillary sinus lesions. Since then, the PLRA has evolved and is now used to treat conditions involving the maxillary sinus, pterygopalatine fossa, infratemporal fossa, and infraorbital wall [7–10]. However, there are no reports in literature on the application of the PLRA to lesions in the infraorbital region. An anatomical study has demonstrated the feasibility of this approach to the intraorbital space, but it did not describe the maximum boundary of the infraorbital region that can be reached [11].

In this study, we investigated the maximum extent of exposure of the infraorbital region reached by the PLRA via the orbital floor. The aim is to provide an anatomical basis for the safer clinical application of this approach.

Methods

Specimens

Ten freshly injected frozen heads were used to study the endoscopic transnasal PLRA through the orbital floor into the infraorbital region. These heads utilized in this study were provided by the Laboratory of Skull Base Anatomy at Changzhi Medical College. The PLRA was applied separately on the left and right sides of each cadaver head, resulting in a total of 20 prelacrimal recess approaches. In order to better differentiate the anatomical structures, a colored silicone mixture (red for arteries and blue for veins) was injected into the vessels within ten cadaver heads.

Equipment

The anatomical experiment was performed with rigid rod lens endoscopes (0°,30° and 70° angles; Karl Storz, Tuttingen, Germany). These endoscopes are 18 centimeters long and 4.0 centimeters in diameter. The endoscope was connected via optical fibers to a light source and a camera equipped with three charge-coupled device sensors. And the camera was linked to a monitor measuring 27 inches in size. Additionally, a set of suction-irrigation instrument equipment was used during the endoscopic dissections.

Dissection

A five-step dissection of the freshly injected frozen heads was performed to illustrate their anatomical structures.

Secondly, the mucoperiostium was elevated toward the attachment of the IT on the lateral wall of the nasal cavity. The bony attachment of the IT was then excised (Fig. 2C).

Finally, a grinding drill was used to further remove the bone anterior to the nasolacrimal duct, enlarging the surgical entry point (Fig. 2D). This procedure established the entrances for the PLRA.

Entry into the maxillary sinus

Surgical entrances for each specimen were established following the anatomical procedures previously described. In this study, we investigated the efficacy of the endoscopic transnasal PLRA for accessing the maxillary sinus. This minimally invasive surgical corridor provides adequate space for the manipulation of an endoscope and surgical instruments. Upon establishing the surgical entrance, the maxillary sinus was explored using endoscopic techniques. The mucosal characteristics of the maxillary sinuses exhibit interindividual variability. Observations of the parietal wall of the maxillary sinus were conducted using an angled endoscope through the PLRA (Fig. 2E).

Identification of the infraorbital canal

The infraorbital canal serves as a crucial intraoperative anatomical landmark in endoscopic surgery [12]. Following the removal of the maxillary sinus mucosa, the infraorbital canal was exposed beneath the orbital floor (Fig. 2E). Orellana-Donoso et al. categorized four anatomical variants of the infraorbital canal, highlighting variations in the trajectory of the infraorbital neurovascular bundle within the parietal wall of the maxillary sinus [13]. According to metric analysis by Van Rompaey et al., the infraorbital canal typically aligns with the midline to the orbit [14]. This alignment facilitates precise localization, thereby preventing inadvertent transection of the infraorbital nerve. Thus, accurate identification of the infraorbital canal is essential is essential for ensuring surgical safety and efficacy.

Definition of the medial infraorbital quadrangle

Endoscopic endonasal dissection was performed bilaterally on the right and left side of each of the ten freshly injected frozen heads. The medial infraorbital quadrangle on the parietal wall of the maxillary sinus was delineated by the orbitomaxillary segment of the infraorbital nerve [12]. This quadrangle is defined by four points: the infraorbital nerve entering and exiting the orbital point on the upper wall of the maxillary sinus, the intersection of the posterior margin of the nasolacrimal duct with the upper wall of the maxillary sinus, and the intersection of the parietal, medial, and posterior walls of the maxillary sinus.

Opening the orbital floor

Following the identification of infraorbital canal, the orbital floor was excised using a Kerrison punch, guided by artificially defined boundaries. Given the thinness of the orbital floor bone, meticulous care is essential during its surgical removal. Removal of the orbital floor bone exposes the periorbit, delineating the border of the medial infraorbital quadrangle (Fig. 2F). Additionally, caution must be exercised when removing the bone from the medial section of the maxillary roof to avoid breaching the cellulae ethmoidales. After the periorbit is incised with a scalpel, identification of the inferior rectus muscle becomes feasible (Fig. 2G).

Using a Vernier caliper (DL91150, Yuyao, Zhejiang, China) with an accuracy of 0.01 mm, the height and base of each triangle were measured separately, and their respective areas were calculated. Data entry and statistical analysis were conducted using SPSS version 26 software. Results

In this study, we demonstrated the detailed procedural steps of the PLRA to access the infraorbital region. The infraorbital region can be effectively accessed through the medial infraorbital quadrangle via the PLRA. Analysis using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 showed that the area of the medial infraorbital quadrangle was 420.65 ± 24.03 mm2. The complete anatomical sequence for the PLRA to the infraorbital region under nasal endoscopy is illustrated in Fig. 2.

From an endoscopic viewpoint, the inferior rectus muscle serves as a demarcation between the medial and lateral compartments within the infraorbital region. Sequential dissection steps of the PLRA into the medial and lateral compartments are detailed in Fig. 2. Careful attention is required during these procedures to avoid injury to small branches of the central retinal artery. Further dissection shows the anterior and posterior ethmoid arteries in the gap between the medial orbital wall and the medial rectus muscle. The oculomotor nerve, which innervates the inferior oblique muscle, is situated lateral to the inferior rectus muscle. Ciliary nerves and arteries are observed between the lateral rectus muscle and the optic nerve, accessible by medially displacing the inferior rectus muscle (Fig. 2I). Simultaneously, the central retinal artery and optic nerve can be visualized. Additionally, identification of the abducens nerve extending to the lateral rectus muscle is feasible. Furthermore, lacrimal glands, lacrimal gland nerves, and arteries are visible within the space between the lateral orbital wall and the lateral rectus muscle (Fig. 2K).

In summary, the endoscopic PLRA facilitates visualization of intraorbital structures while preserving the integrity of infraorbital nerves and arteries. Careful attention is crucial to prevent inadvertent damage to small arteries during endoscopic dissection. The endoscopic transnasal PLRA via the medial infraorbital quadrangle allows access to the boundaries of the infraorbital region, extending from the lateral orbital wall at the outermost level, the superior rectus muscle at the topmost level, to the medial orbital wall at the innermost level. The optic nerve and the central retinal artery are more readily visualized through the lateral compartment approach compared to the medial compartment approach (Fig. 2J and L). By utilizing both medial and lateral approaches, it is feasible to effectively identify all significant anatomical components within the infraorbital region.

Discussion

The orbit constitutes a narrow anatomical space housing numerous important anatomical structures. Diseases affecting the orbit encompass a spectrum of conditions including inflammatory, neoplastic, vascular, traumatic, congenital, periorbital, and systemic manifestations [15]. Surgical interventions have been effective in addressing various orbital diseases. The history of orbital surgery dates back to Krönlein’s pioneering work in 1889, where he first reported successful removal of endoorbital cysts via a transorbital approach [16]. Subsequently in 1922, Dandy introduced the transcranial approach for treating orbital lesions, marking significant milestones in the evolution of surgical techniques for orbital pathology [17]. Since then, both transorbital and transcranial approaches have undergone progressive development in the treatment of orbital lesions.

Li et al. segmented the orbit into four quadrants—inner-superior, outer-superior, inner-inferior, and outer-inferior—using horizontal and vertical lines passing through the optic nerve as demarcations [11]. The infraorbital region encompasses the inner and outer inferior quadrants. The initial surgical approaches studied for lesions in the infraorbital region were transcutaneous methods [18]. Bourguet first described the transconjunctival approach in 1924, making significant contributions to orbital surgery [19]. Tenzel and Miller later advocated for its use in accessing small orbital floor fractures [20]. Kennerdell et al. proposed the transmaxillary sinus approach for successful resection of apical orbital tumors involving the optic nerve [21]. Saskia and Schultheiß et al. highlighted the transmaxillary approach as providing an excellent perspective of the infraorbital region with fewer postoperative complications compared to transcutaneous and transconjunctival approaches [22, 23]. However, it should be noted that all these approaches involve external incisions of varying sizes, which may impact patients’ maxillofacial aesthetics differently.

Over the years, advancements in endoscopic technology and surgical equipment have led to a gradual increase in the number of endoscopic operations for managing orbital lesions [24]. However, the primary procedure remains the decompression of the optic nerve canal [25]. Endoscopic treatment of intraorbital lesions primarily targets inferomedial intraconal space lesions [26]. Nevertheless, there is a notable absence of literature on endoscopic management specifically focusing on lesions confined to the infraorbital region, particularly the lateral infraorbital region. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the feasibility and safety of endoscopic management for such lesions.

In this anatomical study, we demonstrated the successful application of PLRA to access the infraorbital region. Using a 70° scope, we accurately delineated the maximum boundary of the medial infraorbital quadrangle within the infraorbital region. Additionally, we conducted a comparative analysis of the PLRA with other surgical approaches commonly used for managing infraorbital lesions.

For lesions in the infraorbital region, the PLRA offers several advantages over traditional approaches. Firstly, it avoids the need for external incisions, providing a minimally invasive alternative, Secondly, it allows access to the medial infraorbital region without the risk of traction on the optic nerve, a concern with lateral transorbital approaches. Similarly, compared to the transethmoidal approach, the PLRA reduces traction to the nasal septum while facilitating access to the lateral infraorbital region without risking optic nerve injury.

Furthermore, the PLRA minimizes the extent of external incisions compared to approaches such as the transconjuntival and the transmaxillary approaches. Therefore, the PLRA represents a novel approach for treating lesions affecting the infraorbital region, offering advantages over traditional open approaches.

The PLRA utilizes the natural corridor of the maxillary sinus through the medial infraorbital quadrangle to access the infraorbital region. In our study, we successfully accessed the infraorbital region via the PLRA without causing damage to nerves, blood vessels, or muscles. Importantly, this approach allows for adequate exposure of the lateral compartment without the need to inferiorly transpose the infraorbital nerve. For deeper lesions requiring treatment, selective removal of a portion of the medial wall of the maxillary sinus can be performed to create additional space for manipulation. However, it is essential to note that accessing deeper structures such as the superior rectus muscle through the medial infraorbital quadrangle may necessitate removal of orbital fat. This procedure increases the risk of potential damage to neurovascular structures. As a precautionary measure, we do not recommend using the PLRA for treating lesions above the level of the optic nerve.

Additionally, the PLRA creates two types of bone defects when accessing the infraorbital region. One defect occurs during access to the maxillary sinus through the PLRA, while the other is formed through the orbital floor. The bone defect from accessing the maxillary sinus can be repaired by suturing an inferior turbinate mucosal flap. However, defects in the orbital floor resulting from fat removal may lead to complications such as diplopia and enophthalmos [27]. Autologous fat transplantation is a viable option clinical surgery to mitigate postoperative complications such as enophthalmus [28]. Techniques described in the literature on endoscopic repair of infraorbital wall fractures can also guide effective wall repair, with material selection tailored to the size of the bone defect [23, 29–31].

However, several considerations must be addressed before further discussing the PLRA’s approach to the infraorbital region. This study did not involve specific surgical cases, limiting direct application to clinical scenarios. Moreover, the PLRA crosses the optic nerve, posing limitations in treating lesions located above the anatomical landmark. Additionally, during actual operations, there is a risk of damaging smaller branches of the ophthalmic artery. The removal of fat tissue can also complicate identification and protection of the ciliary nerve, potentially resulting inn nerve damage. Therefore, it is crucial for surgeons to thoroughly understand these anatomical structures when employing the PLRA from an endoscopic perspective. Overall, with advancements in endoscopic techniques and materials for orbital floor repair, the safe application of this research in patient surgeries is increasingly feasible and reasonable.

Conclusion

The PLRA through the orbital floor provides access to the maximum boundary of the infraorbital region, extending from the lateral orbital wall at the outermost level, the superior rectus muscle at the topmost level, to the medial orbital wall at the innermost level. This approach offers excellent cosmetic outcomes and preserves the integrity of the nasal cavity structure. Given these advantages, the endoscopic transnasal PLRA to the infraorbital region holds promise for safe and effective treatment of lesions in the infraorbital region. Future applications of this approach could significantly enhance surgical outcomes in orbital and infraorbital surgery.
