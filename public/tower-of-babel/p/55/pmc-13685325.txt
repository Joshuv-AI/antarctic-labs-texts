Traces of hope in the shadow of war: an exploration of preschoolers’ meaning-making processes of the war concept through visual and verbal narratives
Authors: Pınar Aksoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite increasing global exposure of young children to war and conflict, there is limited understanding of how preschool children perceive and make sense of such complex and emotionally charged phenomena. This gap limits educators' ability to recognize children's emotional expressions, support their meaning-making processes, and develop trauma-sensitive, peace-oriented pedagogical practices. This study aimed to examine how preschool children (n = 556; aged 4–6 years) in Turkey conceptualize war through drawings and narratives, and to explore the symbolic, emotional, social, and experiential dimensions embedded in these expressions, with the broader goal of informing educational approaches that foster emotional literacy, empathy, and resilience in early childhood contexts. Framed by an interpretive–descriptive qualitative design, visual and verbal data were treated as a single integrated corpus, open-coded, and organized inductively into themes. Trustworthiness was enhanced through data triangulation (drawings and narratives), expert review, and intercoder agreement exceeding 90%. Five overarching themes were identified. Children primarily conceptualized war through concrete visual and symbolic elements (weaponry, authority figures, and national symbols), extended it to physical, ecological, and life-related destruction (damaged homes, burned environments, displacement, and hunger), and depicted pronounced emotional and psychological responses (sadness, fear, pain as well as captivity and helplessness). Social relations and interactions were depicted as marked by violence, chaos, and loneliness. In contrast, children articulated peace, solidarity, and healing through symbols such as suns, hearts, rainbows, nature, play, and acts of kindness, signaling resilience and moral imagination. Together, these findings indicate that even young children engage in sophisticated, multilayered meaning-making processes related to war, drawing simultaneously on emotional, social, and hopeful dimensions. The findings highlight the pedagogical value of art- and dialogue-based approaches for emotional literacy, trauma-sensitive practice, and nature- and play-anchored restorative learning in early childhood education.Keywords: War perception, Preschool children, Meaning-making processes, Visual and verbal narratives, Emotional literacy, Psychological resilience

Introduction

Early childhood constitutes a critical developmental period during which children actively construct cognitive, social, and emotional understandings through continuous interaction with their environment [5, 25]. Given their limited capacity for abstract reasoning, young children tend to interpret complex and emotionally charged phenomena through concrete, sensory, and experience-based forms rather than through abstract political or historical frameworks [5, 53]. War represents one such phenomenon—not merely a form of physical conflict but a multidimensional social reality encompassing fear, uncertainty, loss, and disruption—that children encounter through both direct experience and indirect channels, including media exposure, family discourse, and broader sociocultural narratives [10, 22, 51]. From an ecological perspective [9], macrosystem-level influences such as sociopolitical contexts and exosystem-level factors such as media shape children's perceptions of war even in the absence of direct exposure [7, 12]. Accordingly, children's understandings of war are both developmentally constrained and socially mediated, emerging at the intersection of individual developmental processes and broader contextual forces.

Despite a growing body of research documenting the psychological impacts of war on children, comparatively limited attention has been directed toward how young children actively interpret, construct, and represent war within their everyday contexts [22, 39]. Exploring these dimensions represents a vital contribution to the field, offering a more nuanced framework for understanding the integrated interplay of cognitive, symbolic, and emotional processes in conflict-ridden childhoods. Children's perceptions of war are not reducible to a single dimension; rather, they emerge from the intersection of developmental constraints and broader sociocultural forces—dimensions that together form the theoretical foundation of the present study [17, 21]. Advancing understanding in this area, therefore, necessitates a holistic inquiry that moves beyond fragmented analyses to examine these multifaceted processes in their dynamic and reciprocal interaction.

Children's cognitive representations of war

Children's cognitive representations of war are shaped by a complex interaction between developmental constraints and ecological influences. Beyond the specific modes of exposure, children actively transform conflict-related information into internally consistent mental schemas rather than passively absorbing external stimuli [40, 52]. This cognitive engagement is characterized by a significant shift toward perceptual salience, where abstract and multifaceted geopolitical crises are rendered through observable markers of hostility and structural collapse [22, 51].

Such a transition underscores the child's developmental reliance on concrete reasoning to interpret the abstract dimensions of global conflict [21, 28]. By reconstructing war through tangible indicators, children attempt to create a manageable interpretative framework for experiences that otherwise exceed their conceptual reach. At the same time, prolonged exposure to these conflict-related stressors—even in mediated forms—has been associated with significant challenges in executive functioning, including persistent difficulties in attention regulation, working memory, and cognitive flexibility [19, 61, 66]. Consequently, these neurocognitive impairments directly impede the acquisition of core learning skills, such as self-regulation and problem-solving, as the child's cognitive resources are depleted by the continuous processing of environmental threats. These cognitive consequences underscore that children do not merely observe war passively; they actively encode it—externalizing internal schemas through symbolic productions that carry both representational and emotional weight.

Symbolic representations of war

In early childhood, complex social realities are frequently expressed through symbolic and nonverbal modes such as drawing, play, and storytelling [14, 37, 59]. Among these expressive forms, drawings occupy a particularly important position. Through visual elements—including figures, colors, spatial composition, and scale—children communicate aspects of their emotional experience and interpretive frameworks that may not yet be accessible through verbal language alone [11, 32, 37]. In child-centered pedagogical traditions such as Reggio Emilia, drawings are further understood as essential tools of pedagogical documentation and as primary resources for accessing children's meaning-making processes [48, 62].

In these representational frameworks, fragmented figures and chaotic spatial compositions serve as sensory symbols of life-threatening forces, reflecting the child's reliance on concrete operational logic [7, 23, 28, 63]. Critically, these symbolic patterns emerge even among children without direct experience of armed conflict, suggesting that socially mediated imagery plays a constitutive role in shaping children's representational frameworks [17]. These observed symbolic patterns indicate that children tend to construct war through concrete and emotionally salient symbols, while representing peace in more relational and idealized terms—a distinction with important implications for peace education.

Emotional and psychological dimensions of war

Children's perceptions of war are fundamentally rooted in their affective experiences and are deeply intertwined with their psychological well-being. Across the empirical literature, war is consistently associated with a pervasive sense of existential threat, while peace is envisioned through frameworks of relational security and emotional flourishing [23, 28, 35, 40]. These associations represent deep-seated affective responses that emerge from the child’s navigation of conflict-related content. Crucially, these meanings are embedded within representational outputs, where visual motifs and compositional choices serve as a non-verbal language for processing psychological vulnerability [11, 32]. Beyond these affective dimensions, the psychological literature has examined more clinically oriented consequences of war exposure.

At the psychological level, exposure to war-related stressors is associated with heightened risks of post-traumatic stress symptoms and emotional dysregulation [6, 54]. Despite these important contributions, the psychological literature has predominantly approached children and war through a deficit-oriented lens, centering trauma and clinical maladjustment [22, 45]. This orientation, while clinically valuable, often overlooks how children actively construct and interpret war-related experiences through symbolic and interpretive processes. Thus, there is a critical need for integrative frameworks that connect neurocognitive outcomes and overall psychological well-being with the symbolic and emotional dimensions of children's meaning-making.

Peace-oriented pedagogy and education policy

Recognizing that war-related experiences are best understood not only through clinical frameworks but through meaning-making, social interaction, and cultural expression, early childhood education policies have increasingly reflected this broader shift [24, 44]. Within this framework, peace education, trauma-sensitive learning, and social-emotional learning (SEL) programs support children in processing crisis- and conflict-related experiences while fostering emotional resilience and empathic relationships.

These innovative approaches have been operationalized through diverse national and international initiatives. For instance, SEL programs in Finland and Germany incorporate structured activities specifically designed to support emotional expression, trust-building, and social cohesion [30, 55]. Similarly, the Education for Peace program in Bosnia and Herzegovina promotes shared values, conflict resolution, and tolerance among children from diverse ethnic backgrounds [16, 49]. In Belgium, the Refugees Well School program addresses emotional regulation and expressive capacities among migrant children [29], while the Healing Classrooms initiative in the United Kingdom prioritizes safe, participatory, and emotionally supportive learning environments [26, 27].

Importantly, while these initiatives are aligned with the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child [60], their ultimate effectiveness relies on a nuanced understanding of the child's internal representational world. Despite this growing policy emphasis, the empirical foundations informing how young children themselves conceptualize and symbolically process war remain underdeveloped. This disconnect underscores a critical gap: without grounding pedagogical interventions in children's specific cognitive and emotional schemas, such efforts risk remaining superficial. The present study seeks to address this gap by providing an integrative analysis of the symbolic and interpretive mechanisms children employ to navigate the reality of global conflict.

Conceptual gaps and study rationale

A critical review of the existing literature reveals that research on children and war remains conceptually fragmented. Studies have predominantly been framed within clinical and deficit-oriented perspectives—focusing on trauma, behavioral disturbances, and adjustment difficulties [36, 40]—while offering limited insight into the complex interpretive mechanisms through which children construct social reality around conflict [39]. Furthermore, integrative research connecting symbolic expression with psychological and cognitive dimensions is particularly scarce regarding early childhood. This developmental period is crucial, as verbal articulation is still evolving, making nonverbal modes of communication essential for capturing the child’s internal world [1, 7, 17].

Although a growing body of qualitative work has examined children's drawings and narratives in conflict contexts, systematic analyses focusing on how young children structure the abstract concept of war through symbolic representations remain insufficient [28, 63]. A significant empirical gap exists specifically concerning preschool children aged 4–6 years—a developmental window during which foundational capacities for symbolic thinking, emotion regulation, and social meaning-making are consolidated [17, 23].

These collective limitations constrain a holistic understanding of how young children perceive and navigate phenomena as abstract and emotionally demanding as war. Addressing these gaps requires a robust, qualitative inquiry that investigates how young children cognitively construct, symbolically express, and psychologically process war within their unique developmental and sociocultural contexts.

Significance of the study

The present study addresses the aforementioned gaps by adopting a multimodal lens to investigate how children aged 4–6 years conceptualize war. While existing research often isolates visual or verbal data, this study holistically examines the synergy between drawings and accompanying narratives. In doing so, the study captures nuanced dimensions of meaning-making that are often overlooked by single-mode approaches, thereby offering a more holistic understanding of the child’s internal representational world. Positioning children not as passive objects of inquiry but as active social agents who symbolically reconstruct complex realities, the study centers their unique voices and perspectives. This stance directly aligns with contemporary early childhood policies that recognize children as legitimate producers of subjective knowledge. Beyond its theoretical contributions, the findings offer practical implications for developing child-centered and trauma-informed pedagogies. By providing these insights, the study contributes both conceptual and applied value to the field, particularly for educational practices in contexts affected by global conflict.

Purpose of the study

Despite the increasing exposure of young children to war and conflict globally, there remains a limited understanding of how preschool children perceive and make sense of such complex and emotionally charged phenomena. This gap constrains educators’ ability to recognize children’s emotional expressions, support their meaning-making processes, and develop trauma-sensitive, inclusive, and peace-oriented pedagogical practices.

The primary aim of this study is to examine how preschool children aged 4–6 years construct meaning around the phenomenon of war through their drawings and narratives, and to explore the symbolic, emotional, social, and experiential dimensions embedded in these expressions. By doing so, the study seeks to contribute to a deeper understanding of children’s perspectives and to inform educational approaches that support emotional expression, empathy, and resilience in early childhood contexts.

In this context, the study addresses the following research questions:

Methodology

Research design

This study adopts a qualitative phenomenological research design to explore how preschool children perceive and symbolically represent the phenomenon of war through their drawings and accompanying narratives. Grounded in a constructivist paradigm, the study seeks to understand how children construct meaning around an abstract and emotionally salient phenomenon based on their indirect experiences, including family discourse, media exposure, and broader societal narratives [15, 34]. Within this framework, phenomenology provides a lens for examining children’s subjective meanings and sense-making processes as expressed through both visual and verbal modalities. Rather than focusing on direct lived experiences of war, the study attends to how children interpret and internalize the idea of war within the limits of their developmental capacities and sociocultural contexts [46]. In this respect, children’s drawings and narratives are treated as symbolic expressions through which their experiential worlds and meaning-making processes become accessible.

Importantly, this study does not assume that children possess fully developed or abstract conceptual understandings of war; rather, it seeks to explore how elements of their experiences are represented in developmentally constrained symbolic forms. In this sense, it examines how children engage in interpretive processes to make sense of war as a phenomenon by focusing on the interpretive processes through which meanings are organized and expressed in symbolic and narrative representations, without reducing them to a single, unified essence.

Participants

The study group consisted of 556 children attending preschool classrooms in 23 schools located in the central district of Tokat, Turkey, during the spring semester of the 2024–2025 academic year. Participants were selected using a convenience sampling method from children who voluntarily agreed to participate and whose parents provided written informed consent. Children who had directly experienced war, forced migration, or traumatic conflict were excluded from the study due to ethical considerations and the potential risk of psychological harm. It is important to note that children in this sample may have been exposed to war-related content indirectly through everyday contexts such as family conversations, media (e.g., television and digital platforms), and peer interactions. These forms of contextual exposure were not controlled or manipulated within the scope of the study; rather, they are considered part of children’s natural sociocultural environments.

Of the participants, 52.3% (n = 291) were girls and 47.7% (n = 265) were boys. The children were distributed across three age groups: 4 years (n = 199; 35.8%), 5 years (n = 248; 44.6%), and 6 years (n = 109; 19.6%). This distribution reflects a balanced developmental range within preschool education in Turkey. Children with formally identified special educational needs or diagnosed developmental disorders were not intentionally included in the study, based on information available from teachers and school records. However, no independent clinical screening was conducted; therefore, undiagnosed developmental differences may be present in the sample.

Research context

This research was conducted in a medium-sized province in the Black Sea Region of Turkey, characterized by rural–semi-urban settlement patterns and strong traditional community ties. The local economy is largely based on agriculture, small-scale trade, and a modest service sector. In recent years, the region has become more diverse due to internal and external migration (especially refugee mobility); only children without direct war exposure were included, while those from active conflict zones were excluded to focus on indirect exposure (family discourse, media, local narratives).

Data collection tools and procedures

A qualitative research design was employed in this study, with children’s drawings and individual interviews serving as the primary data sources within the framework of document analysis. Initially, each child was invited to produce a drawing reflecting what came to their mind regarding war. The open-ended prompt provided was: “In your view, what does war mean? What comes to your mind when you hear the word ‘war’? Please draw it.” During the data collection process, children were provided with A4-sized drawing paper, pencils, crayons, and an eraser. All drawings were completed in a familiar classroom setting, in a calm atmosphere, without direct instructional guidance from the researcher; however, children were already situated within ongoing classroom environments and prior everyday experiences that may have informed their drawings.

A semi-structured interview form was developed for this study to explore preschool children's perceptions of war through their drawings and narratives. The interview items were informed by prior literature on children's symbolic expression, emotional representation, and meaning-making in art. The English version of the interview form is presented in the Appendix to ensure transparency and replicability.

Following the drawing activity, individual interviews were conducted immediately after the completion of each drawing, within the same session, to capture children’s verbal expressions while the drawing process was still fresh in their minds. During these interviews, open-ended questions such as “What did you draw in your picture?” and “Can you tell me about your picture?” were asked, and the children’s responses were recorded in detail. Through this process, children’s drawings and corresponding verbal explanations constituted the primary data sources, enabling a rich understanding of how preschool children represent and interpret the concept of war. All children were invited to participate in the drawing activity; however, variations in engagement were observed. While the majority of children produced drawings related to the prompt, some children required additional time, and a small number produced drawings that were partially or indirectly related to the topic. These variations were considered as part of natural developmental differences and were included in the analysis accordingly.

Data analysis and coding procedure

Children's perceptions of war were analyzed using an interpretive and descriptive qualitative content analysis approach, informed by the principles of thematic analysis. This approach enables nuanced, context-sensitive interpretation of children's symbolic and narrative expressions and is consistent with the study's focus on subjective meaning-making, without aiming to generate formal theory [8, 18, 41, 56]. The analysis was conducted in three systematic phases: (i) data integration and corpus segmentation, (ii) hierarchical coding structure, and (iii) multiple coding, methodological rigor, and reporting. This structured approach was designed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the cognitive, emotional, and social meaning-making processes reflected in children's drawings and narratives.

Phase I: data integration and corpus segmentation

Visual data (children's drawings) and verbal data (interview narratives) were treated as a single integrated corpus. Each case—comprising a drawing and its accompanying narrative—was examined holistically, with visual features (e.g., objects, figures, spatial organization, and symbolic elements) considered alongside the meanings articulated by each child. The corpus was subsequently segmented into meaning units and subjected to open coding, through which recurrent visual and verbal patterns were identified and consolidated into systematic codes. Importantly, the analysis does not presuppose that children's drawings reflect fully developed or abstract conceptual understanding; rather, they are interpreted as developmentally situated, experience-based representations.

Phase II: hierarchical coding structure

Coding followed a hierarchical analytic structure organized across three levels:

Theme → Category Group → Category name (code).

Theme → Category Group → Category name (code).

Each meaning unit was first coded inductively, and the resulting codes were grouped according to conceptual similarities to form categories, category groups, and higher-order themes. The dominant contextual emphasis of each case was determined and used to assign a primary (dominant) theme, within which recurring content patterns were classified into category groups and corresponding categories. Themes were developed to ensure conceptual coherence and contextual integrity across the dataset, while category groups and categories were employed to capture more fine-grained, recurring representations within each thematic domain. Although themes were derived inductively from the data, their formulation was also informed by existing literature on children's symbolic, emotional, and social representations.

The analysis yielded five overarching and conceptually distinct themes, reflecting the multifaceted nature of children's representations of war across symbolic, emotional, social, and experiential dimensions.

Visual and Symbolic Elements: Includes concrete and symbolic elements such as warfare apparatus, power and leadership figures, symbols of national identity and belonging, as well as symbolic, unusual, and fictional entities.

Physical, Ecological, and Life-Related Destruction: Encompasses representations of the tangible consequences of war, including damage to living spaces, environmental degradation, and restricted access to basic needs.

Emotional Responses and Psychological Effects: Comprises representations of difficult emotions, injury and pain, and experiences of captivity and helplessness.

Emotional Responses and Psychological Effects: Comprises representations of difficult emotions, injury and pain, and experiences of captivity and helplessness.

Social Relations and Social Interaction: Captures interpersonal and societal dynamics, including social isolation and loneliness, violence and conflict, and chaos and disorder.

Social Relations and Social Interaction: Captures interpersonal and societal dynamics, including social isolation and loneliness, violence and conflict, and chaos and disorder.

Peace, Solidarity, and Healing: Encompasses positive and restorative representations, such as symbols of peace and hope, natural and peaceful living environments, social solidarity and helping behaviors, and acts of resistance, healing, and reconstruction.

Phase III: multiple coding, and reporting

When a case included elements corresponding to more than one theme, a multiple-coding approach was applied. Each case was assigned a dominant theme based on its overall contextual emphasis—determined primarily by the relative salience and frequency of content elements within the drawing and narrative—while additional relevant categories from other themes were concurrently coded. Secondary codes were included in frequency calculations to reflect the multidimensional nature of children's representations, while interpretation remained principally guided by the dominant theme. Frequencies (f) and percentages (%) were calculated to indicate the salience of coded categories and to ensure a clear, transparent account of content distribution across themes. Findings are reported using category examples, frequency values, selected verbatim quotations, and illustrative visual excerpts to support interpretation.

Ensuring trustworthiness and methodological rigor

Several strategies were systematically employed to ensure trustworthiness and methodological rigor. All coding was conducted by the researcher and independently reviewed by a child development academic expert with expertise in qualitative research methodology. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus meetings, ensuring transparency, conceptual consistency, and reliability. Inter-coder agreement, calculated using Miles and Huberman's [43] formula, exceeded 90% following independent coding and iterative comparison of coding decisions. Credibility and contextual validity were further strengthened through data triangulation, integration of visual and verbal data, and inclusion of verbatim child statements. Visual elements (e.g., symbols, colors, and spatial organization) were interpreted alongside children's own explanations, enabling cross-validation and deeper contextual understanding; expert feedback additionally informed the refinement of categories and themes [34, 50]. Transferability was supported through thick description of the research context, participants, and analytic process.

To minimize potential bias, a standardized, non-directive approach was maintained consistently across all participants, with comparable prompts and interaction styles employed throughout. Data collection was conducted solely by the researcher, and reflexive awareness was sustained to reduce the influence of researcher subjectivity. The analysis extended beyond descriptive reporting to interpret the semantic, emotional, and social dimensions of children's representations [4], examining multilayered concepts—including fear, destruction, resilience, hope, and peace—across both visual composition and narrative language. An audit trail was maintained to document coding decisions, theme development, and category refinement, thereby enhancing dependability and confirmability. Prolonged engagement with the data and iterative review of codes and themes across multiple analytical stages further supported consistency and conceptual clarity.

As shown in Table 1, under the Visual and Symbolic Elements theme, concrete war apparatus is the most prevalent representation in children’s drawings and narratives about war (f = 508; 91.4%). This was followed by representative power/leader figures (f = 256; 46.04%) and national identity & belonging (f = 116; 20.86%). Less frequent were symbolic & unusual objects (f = 27; 4.86%) and fictional entities (f = 26; 4.68%). The overall distribution (∑f = 933) indicates that children primarily represent war through tangible and readily identifiable visual elements (physical force, authority figures, national symbols), while a subset also appears to employ symbolic, dramatic, and fictional elements to convey more elaborated or imagined threat representations.

Concrete warfare apparatus

Children conceptualized war predominantly through concrete, physical, and destructive tools, depicting a wide array of 18 distinct items. Gun/bullet (f = 115) and bomb (f = 115) were the most common, followed by tank (f = 71),airplane/fighter jet (f = 46), sword (f = 38), fire/flame (f = 35), and helicopter (f = 26). These objects were accentuated with dark tones and sharp contours, and the scenes were infused with a menacing atmosphere through explosion, bullet, and flame effects (as seen in Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Consistent with these figures, several narratives emphasized immediate danger and mechanical force: e.g., “The bomb fell, and people flew into the air.” (C403); “When the armed man shot, the police car arrived.” (C426); “Tanks are attacking; planes are dropping bombs.” (C513). Taken together, these choices suggest that children tend to associate war with immediate physical destruction and threat; in other words, their depictions foreground immediate danger and mechanical force, as reflected in their representations rather than fully developed conceptual understanding.

Representative power and leader figures

Soldier (f = 134) and enemy soldier (f = 73) were the most salient human figures; Atatürk (f = 36) and police (f = 11) were associated with protection/heroism. A very small number referenced international political leaders (f = 2), indicating that, for some children, war is situated within a broader sociopolitical context. These depictions indicate that beyond physical conflict, children also represent war in relation to social roles, power dynamics, and military authority.

As illustrated in Figs. 4, 5 and 6, soldier figures are typically depicted in children’s drawings as armed, uniformed, and powerful. Some children cast soldiers in protective and rescuing roles, whereas others position them as heroes who actively fight the enemy. For example, one child (C459) stated, “The war soldiers are protecting the flag,” defining soldiers as a defensive force; another (C492) remarked, “The thousand-armed soldier fights the enemy in the war,” foregrounding exaggerated representations of power. Similarly, the statements by C545 (“Soldiers are trying to save people”) and C513 (“There are soldiers inside the tank and they are shooting at the enemy soldier”) indicate that the soldier figure is associated with both protection and attack. Another child (C489) said, “To protect themselves from the enemies, our soldiers drew a boundary and put knives on its edge so no one can cross,” describing soldiers as authoritative actors responsible for maintaining order and guarding borders. Notably, some representations also depict soldiers in explicitly protective contexts. For instance, as illustrated in Fig. 5, the scene of “a big soldier protecting a small flower” (C177) indicates that military figures can be associated with safety, guardianship, and the protection of vulnerable life. These narratives reveal that children construct war within a system of both physical and symbolic protection and defense.

A further notable representation following soldier figures is Atatürk. In children’s drawings, Atatürk appears as a historical leader, a protective figure, and a symbol of national liberation. His depictions frequently co-occur with flags, children, and battle scenes, often arranged as an integrated composition. One child (C57) stated, “Children are standing under the flag thanks to Atatürk,” defining him in terms of both leadership and protection; in the same drawing, he is portrayed as fighting with a sword, while flags placed around him and children standing in his shadow reinforce the theme of national belonging. These accounts suggest that, in children’s perceptions of war, Atatürk is understood not only as a historical figure but also as a symbolic representation of security and unity. These examples show that human figures function not merely as individual depictions but as carriers of abstract meanings—protection, threat, leadership, order, and belonging—as reflected in children’s symbolic representations.

National identity and belonging symbols

The Turkish flag (f = 80) was the most prominent national symbol across drawings and narratives, followed by the flags of Palestine (f = 15) and Israel (f = 12). In addition, flags of other countries—such as the United States, Azerbaijan, France, Canada, Switzerland, and Japan—also appeared. These flags were most often depicted in connection with themes of conflict, taking sides, or protection.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, one child positioned both the Palestinian and Turkish flags within the same scene, depicting the allied sides of the conflict through soldiers who protect those flags. In this rendering, flags function not only as national emblems but also as visual markers of collective defense and national unity. Similarly, another child (C7) stated, “A big bomb is exploding; the Turkish flag is on the right and the other country’s flag is on the left,” indicating that war is framed within nation-based oppositions. A further account (C413)—“As the flag was about to fall to the ground, people tried to hold it”—casts the falling flag as a threat and an affront to national honor. In yet another case (C275), “Two countries are fighting each other; their soldiers raised their flags,” opposing forces are defined through national symbols and the conflict is staged via flags. One child (C89) noted, “Here the Turkish soldier is carrying our flag; the other flags are the places the Turkish soldier helps,” depicting the flags of Palestine and Azerbaijan alongside the Turkish flag. These examples show that children use flag imagery not only as identity markers but also as symbolic representations associated with identification, protection, and solidarity. In this context, the flag emerges as a powerful symbolic focal point in children’s representation of war.

Symbolic and unusual objects

Objects such as sea steamer, cars, and horses (f = 6), as well as various sport-related items, show that children constructed their war concepts by mobilizing imagination, conveying strength, or adding dynamism to the scene. Less frequent elements—spaceship, boxing glove, punching bag, stone, stick, axe, and hammer—were likewise used to imbue the drawings with symbolic or imaginal meaning. For example, in Fig. 8, the child’s drawing and statement indicate that war is associated not only with military hardware but also with individual strength, endurance, and bodily struggle. The co-presence of boxing gloves/punching bag with war figures suggests that, for the child, war is internalized as an intense experience of the body, power, and confrontation, rather than merely an external clash.

Fictional entities

Entities coded under this lower-frequency category—war robots, monsters, aliens, dragons, and zombies—exemplify children’s use of abstract and fantastical symbols to represent conflict, even if infrequently. These entities express not only fear and aggression but also symbolic representations of resistance and defense. As shown in Fig. 9, one child (C77) interpreted the fantastical dimension of conflict within a heroic narrative, reconstructing themes of strength, struggle, and defiance through familiar cultural images (e.g., a cartoon character). Another child (C454) noted, “I climbed onto the roof to escape from the monster,” depicting a search for protection and escape from threat, while a different child (C140) said, “There is a dangerous cat-faced man whose goal is to end the world,” conveying threat via abstract, fantastical figures. In related drawings, a large, human-like creature with sharp teeth, oversized eyes, and striking facial features is positioned as a central source of menace, exerting pressure over the other figures in the scene. These findings indicate that children represent war not only through concrete elements but also through imagined and symbolic constructions that are shaped by their experiences and cultural contexts.

As seen in Table 2, within the Physical, Ecological, and Life-Related Destruction theme, damaged living spaces is the most prevalent representation in children’s drawings and narratives (f = 77, 13.85%), followed by environmental degradation (f = 43, 7.73%) and access to basic needs (f = 17, 3.06%). The overall distribution (∑f = 137) indicates that war is understood by children not only as interpersonal conflict but also as a multi-layered threat to the built environment (homes, buildings, neighborhoods), natural ecosystems (polluted air/smoke, burnt plants, parched grass), and basic subsistence (displacement, homelessness, hunger/poverty).

Damaged living spaces

Damaged structures (f = 30) and burned places (f = 26) stand out, vividly portraying the extent of physical devastation and civilians’ traumatic flight (as seen in Fig. 10). The statement, “People whose houses and cars burned are running away” (C136; 5-year-old boy), strikingly reflects both the direct destruction of everyday living areas and the resulting forced displacement. The simultaneous presentation of fear, fire, and flight suggests that, in children’s representations, war is associated not only as a physical threat but also with environmental and life-sustaining disruption. Similarly, one child noted, “A plane belonging to bad people flying in the sky split a house in two, and the house burned” (C542; 5-year-old girl), pointing to airstrikes targeting civilian structures; another said, “The soldier driving the tank destroyed the house” (C500; 6-year-old girl), underscoring direct assaults on living spaces. Such accounts indicate that children’s constructions of war are shaped less by abstract notions and more by concrete, experience-based depictions of destruction affecting homes, streets, and neighborhoods.

Environmental degradation

Polluted air/smoke (f = 23) and burned plants (f = 16) were the most salient indicators, with additional details such as parched grass (f = 2) and noise (f = 2) supporting this theme. Black clouds, charred trees, and scorched landscapes suggest that children represent the environmental impacts of war through concrete visual elements. As the child who produced Fig. 11 explained, “I colored the grass black because it had no water.” Another child (C236) added, “I drew burning houses, burning roads, a car, and black clouds,” depicting environmental harm alongside physical destruction. These accounts indicate that children represent nature as an affected component within their representations of war, rather than merely as a passive backdrop.

Access to basic needs

Representations of displacement/forced migration (f = 8), homelessness (f = 5), and hunger/poverty (f = 4) indicate that children associate war with disruptions to fundamental needs such as shelter, nourishment, and safety (see Fig. 12). Scenes featured people migrating by sea or being transported by trucks, families living in camps or tents, and even animals searching for food. Children’s narratives clustered around feelings of houselessness, hunger, and uncertainty. For example: “People are living in a refugee camp because they have no homes” (C235; 6-year-old boy); “People with colorful houses had to leave their country because of the war” (C543; 5-year-old girl); and “Because people went to war, the cats are looking for food” (C294; 5-year-old girl). These statements reveal that children’s understandings of war encompass a multi-faceted condition associated with disruptions to basic needs and everyday life.

As presented in Table 3, within the theme of Emotional Responses and Psychological Effects the most prevalent representation is difficult emotions (f = 105; 18.88%), followed by injury and pain (f = 93; 16.73%) and captivity and helplessness (f = 78; 14.03%). The overall distribution (∑f = 276) indicates that children’s drawings and narratives represent war through emotional distress (e.g., sadness, fear), bodily harm, and help-seeking, as well as representations of insecurity (e.g., hiding, fleeing, being trapped).

Difficult emotions

Within this category, the most frequent affective expression was sadness (f = 76), followed by fear (f = 18), anger (f = 7), and surprise (f = 4). Visually, these emotions were typically conveyed with black, gray, and other dark tones; isolated figures, teary faces, and vacant gazes were prominent. Figure 13 provides a concrete example of this multilayered emotional state in the face of war. In the narratives, children linked these emotions both to individual experiences (e.g., fear, loneliness, anxiety) and to contextual factors (e.g., separation from family, lack of safety). For instance, one child (C84) stated, “The child in the picture is very unhappy because there is war in their country.” Another (C174) offered a symbolic account: “Bombs were dropped; the clouds became sad, and it rained because the cloud was sad; the bees hid from the sound of bombs; a storm broke out in the sky.” These narratives indicate that children make sense of war through multiple emotional layers, linking emotional harm not only to personal experiences but also to changes in their immediate environment. In addition, some children extended these emotional representations beyond the self, referring to the experiences of others affected by war. Across drawings and narratives, children depicted civilians such as women and children and expressed their sadness, fear, and vulnerability. In some cases, children conveyed not only individual emotional states but also a broader sense of shared emotional experience (e.g., “Children are sad; the world is sad”, Fig. 13). These representations show that children refer to others’ emotional states within their representations of war.

Injury and pain

As illustrated in Fig. 14, the most frequent representations within this category were wounded people (f = 40) and blood (f = 27), demonstrating that children often associated war with bodily harm, pain, and trauma. As seen in the statements “Children were injured in the war; there was blood.” (C175) and “The soldier’s leg was torn off; there’s blood everywhere.” (C178), children represented war in relation to experiences of physical suffering and bodily damage. Following these, depictions of Red Crescent tents (f = 12), health personnel (nurses/doctors) (f = 5), and hospitals (f = 3) appeared, showing that severe injury was often accompanied—within the same depictions—by references to medical response and care. Less frequently, elements such as medical equipment (f = 2), ambulances (f = 2), Red Crescent workers (f = 1), and cries for help (f = 1) further revealed that children sometimes extended their portrayals of bodily trauma to include immediate intervention triggered by injury. For instance, C80 stated, “The man on the ground is shot, and an ambulance is coming,” foregrounding a shooting victim while also noting the ambulance as a direct response to acute injury. Similarly, C505 described, “In the Red Crescent tent, the wounded are being helped, and there is an hourglass showing time,” emphasizing the presence of wounded bodies alongside time-sensitive treatment. According to these accounts, children’s drawings conceptualize war through the lens of physical suffering, where depictions of medical aid, far from providing relief, serve to underscore the prevailing narrative of bodily trauma. Extending this pattern, C498 expressed, “The man’s leg is torn off, there is blood, and an ambulance is coming,” combining traumatic injury with anticipation of medical response, while C427 noted that “There is a Red Crescent tent here because many people were injured,” highlighting the importance of post-war humanitarian support. In drawings under this category, red-colored blood stains, bandaged figures, people lying on the ground, and stretcher-like elements were frequently observed. These visual choices reveal that children represent war as a crisis space associated with immediate medical attention and intervention. The wide range of visual figures depicting injury and pain (9 types) further demonstrates that while the forms of suffering varied, the emotional imprint left by these experiences was commonly shared among children.

Captivity and helplessness

In this category, the most frequent representations were hiding figures of people or animals (f = 37; 6.65%), followed by people escaping (f = 20; 3.60%), trapped individuals (f = 16; 2.88%), and innocent people unrelated to war (f = 5; 0.90%). Based on children’s visual and verbal expressions, this category accounted for 14.03% (f = 78) of all depictions. The drawings commonly portrayed figures hiding in trenches, enclosed, or sheltered spaces, where bodies were rendered invisible as a defense mechanism against perceived threats. This indicates that children tend to represent war in relation to experiences of physical danger and the search for safety. For example, one child stated, “The safest place to hide from war is on the roof of the house.” (C163; 6-year-old boy), reflecting how elevated structures were imagined as shelters against danger. Similarly, another child said, “A woman is running away from bullets, hiding behind a black shield so they don’t reach her.” (C481; 6-year-old girl), emphasizing an individual act of protection. A further example, “A soldier is hiding behind the walls to protect himself from enemies.” (C534; 6-year-old boy), highlights how structural barriers were perceived as means of defense and survival.

In the representations of trapped figures, children illustrated restricted mobility and confinement within closed spaces caused by war. One child expressed this vividly: “This man is looking through locked windows in his house to escape from the bad people.” (C435; 5-year-old boy). In this depiction, the figure is positioned within a closed and secured space while simultaneously attempting to avoid external danger, indicating both restricted movement and an orientation toward safety. In Fig. 15, a character trapped inside a balloon with a tied mouth is depicted—symbolizing an unreachable, abstract refuge from conflict and portraying enclosed spaces as safe harbors. The representations of innocent people further revealed that children perceived war as affecting not only combatants but also civilians uninvolved in fighting. One child explained, “He didn’t do anything, but bad people caught and took him away.” (C409; 6-year-old girl); another said, “This is someone like an angel, watching the war without doing anything.” (C256; 5-year-old girl), expressing perceptions of injustice and undeserved suffering. These drawings and narratives suggest that themes of threat, helplessness, and protection are salient in children’s representations of war, even in the absence of direct exposure.

According to the findings presented in Table 4, within the theme of Social Relations and Social Interaction, the most frequent representational form observed in children’s drawings and narratives was violence and conflict (f = 181; 32.55%). This was followed by chaos and disorder (f = 50; 8.99%) and social isolation and loneliness (f = 22; 3.96%). The overall distribution (∑f = 253) suggests that children tend to represent war in relation to social disintegration, cycles of violence, and emotional loneliness, as reflected in their drawings and narratives.

Social isolation and loneliness

Within this category, the representations of separated family members (f = 9), lonely human figures (f = 7), and restricted or enclosed spaces (f = 6) revealed how children represented the disconnection of social bonds associated with war. The visual portrayal of a mother awaiting her soldier son (Fig. 16) powerfully reflects physical distance, illustrating how children conceptualize the separation between parent and child. In these representations, individuals were frequently shown standing alone, parents separated from one another, or people left within barbed or confined spaces, symbolizing emotional detachment and social fragmentation. These visual narratives suggest that children represent the effects of war on human relationships through themes of separation and distance. For example, one child expressed, “My mother, father, and brother are in another city, and she is waiting for them.” (C490; 6-year-old boy), emphasizing both physical distance and separation among family members. Another child (C274) drew his mother and sibling on the front of the page, while depicting his father—holding a gun—on the reverse side, clearly illustrating how war divided family unity. Similarly, another child stated, “There’s no one with him because there was a war.” (C268; 6-year-old boy), directly expressing the sense of loneliness caused by conflict. These examples demonstrate that children’s understandings of war extend beyond destruction and danger to encompass social fragmentation and affective isolation, revealing a keen insight into how conflict erodes the fundamental pillars of human connection.

Violence and conflict

In this category, the most frequently observed elements in the visual and verbal data included fighting individuals (f = 78) and dead bodies (f = 58), followed by arguing individuals (f = 28),shot individuals (f = 10), and attacking individuals (f = 7). These findings indicate that approximately 32% of participants (f = 181) represented war through direct physical violence between individuals. For instance, Fig. 17 portrays two opposing individuals engaged in close combat with swords, reflecting a face-to-face encounter in which violence is immediate, visible, and embodied. In some cases, this representation extended to the use of firearms, with children depicting shooting scenes that convey more distant yet equally lethal forms of harm. These visual narratives are echoed in children’s own words. As one child stated, “There are two people fighting face to face” (C164; 6-year-old boy), while another expressed a more generalized perception: “In a world with war, everyone fights” (C447; 6-year-old girl). Such statements suggest that children tend to represent war as a human-centered process of confrontation, rather than as an abstract or strategic phenomenon. Taken together, these findings show that children represent war through concrete and observable acts of violence between people. Their focus on confrontation, visible harm, and dead bodies indicates that these elements are particularly salient, rather than reflecting abstract or strategic understanding.

Chaos and disorder

In this category, children’s drawings and narratives frequently depicted chaotic scenes (f = 23), monotony (f = 18), contradictory facial expressions (f = 5), and isolated crowds (f = 4). These visual and verbal representations indicate children’s representations of disorder, uncertainty, and social disruption associated with war. The drawings often included people moving aimlessly, overlapping figures, scenes lacking narrative coherence, and emotionally confused faces. In some drawings, the page was almost filled with dark colors, overlapping shapes, and disoriented lines. This visual intensity and lack of structure may reflect how children express confusion, emotional chaos, and instability in relation to war. For instance, one child explained the group of people standing in their drawing by saying, “They are waiting, but they don’t know what they are waiting for.” (C233; 5-year-old boy), highlighting the sense of uncertainty and aimlessness. Another child (C546; 5-year-old girl) depicted all people as faceless, monochromatic silhouettes and commented, “There are many people, but they don’t know each other.” Such descriptions and visual expressions illustrate how war, in children’s minds, extends beyond physical destruction to encompass social disintegration, emotional detachment, and interpersonal disconnection. These dimensions are illustrated in Fig. 18, where individuals appear physically close yet disconnected, which may reflect children’s representations of social fragmentation. Moreover, the repetitive reliance on uniform colors and monotone compositions suggests that children perceive the war environment as a chronic, persistent threat. This is manifested through a sense of emotional numbness and a perceived loss of agency. Collectively, these patterns reveal that war is conceptualized not merely as physical chaos, but as a profound state of social alienation and fractured human engagement.

As revealed in Table 5, another form of representation developed by children in response to war was the theme of Peace, Solidarity, and Healing. The most frequently observed category was symbols of peace and hope with 26.80% (f = 149), followed by natural and peaceful living spaces with 8.45% (f = 47), resistance, healing, and reconstruction with 4.68% (f = 26), and social solidarity and helping with 4.14% (f = 23). The overall distribution (∑f = 245) reveals that, despite the adversities of war, children also represented themes related to hope, unity, and healing.

Symbols of peace and hope

Under this category, the figures displayed considerable diversity (e.g., sun, heart, peace flags, and rainbow); however, the most frequently used figure was the sun (f = 68). In many drawings, the sun appeared to be associated with themes of new beginning and a sense of hope. The warmth of the sun stood out as a light against darkness and as a symbolic representation of returning to life. For example, one child expressed, “The sun has come out, the weather is very hot, and everyone has made peace.” (C184; 5-year-old girl), associating the sun with the end of war and the arrival of peace. This was followed by other positively connoted symbols such as the heart (f = 27), peace flags (f = 21), rainbow (f = 15), and star (f = 9). The heart figure was often used to express feelings of love, peace, and togetherness; as one child stated, “I drew a heart so that children can smile again.” (C499; 5-year-old boy). Some children also included upward-oriented objects, such as balloons (f = 4) and kites (f = 1), to symbolize hope and the feeling of freedom. For instance, “During the war, red balloons suddenly floated into the sky.” (C487; 5-year-old boy) directly reflects this association. The drawing and narrative in Fig. 19 provide a representative example of peace and hope, depicted through children around the world holding hands despite their injuries. Although relatively rare, raindrops (f = 4) were depicted by some children as symbols of purification and rebirth following the war. Collectively, these representations illustrate that children expressed themes related to hope, goodness, and peace, as reflected in their drawings, in contrast to the dark and destructive atmosphere of war.

Natural and peaceful living spaces

Children primarily developed a peaceful vision of nature through depictions of greenery (f = 18) and flowers (f = 18), highlighting the vitality and colorful texture of nature as an image opposing the darkness of war. Some also embodied their sense of safety and harmony with nature by including peaceful animal figures (f = 8), such as butterflies and swans. For instance, one child stated, “When the tanks left, lots of flowers and grass appeared here.” (C507; 5-year-old boy), describing a serene life built within nature. In a few rare examples, scenes surrounded by elements such as the sea sand, and the lake conveyed a sense of silence and inner peace. The findings suggest that children represented nature as a possible space of safety and restoration amidst the chaos of war.

Social solidarity and helping

People rushing to help (f = 12) and people holding hands or embracing (f = 9) emerged as reflections of children’s representations of solidarity and emotional support within the atmosphere of war. In several drawings, mutual assistance among people in response to destruction was clearly illustrated. As shown in Fig. 20, one child stated, “My father will go by plane to help the soldiers on duty.” (C414; 6-year-old boy), emphasizing the notion of collective responsibility during wartime. Similarly, another child remarked, “Everyone here is helping each other because there has been a war.” (C276; 5-year-old girl), highlighting the theme of helping behavior in the face of trauma. Although less frequent, a few drawings included people engaging in shared activities (f = 2), symbolizing togetherness and cooperation. These depictions highlight that children presented representations of resilience, unity, and communal support as coping mechanisms against the hardships of war. In this sense, it can be inferred that children represented the wartime environment not merely as an individual threat but also as a collective challenge that could be overcome through solidarity.

Resistance, healing, and reconstruction

Within this category, cheerful human figures (f = 15) were among the most frequently depicted elements, often appearing in scenes that symbolized the end of the war and the joyful return to everyday life. One child conveyed this longing for normalcy by saying, “The soldiers arrived, the enemies are gone, and people are happy because they have returned home.” (C90; 6-year-old girl). Several children further enhanced their drawings with colorful houses and animals (f = 3) alongside play materials (f = 5), creating an atmosphere in which life was being joyfully rebuilt. For example, “The war is over, and we went on vacation; I played on the seesaw, swing, and with many toys in the garden, then swam in the pool.” (C182; 5-year-old boy) directly illustrates this sense of renewal and happiness. In a few cases, badge and medal (f = 2) emerged as symbolic representations of achievement, deserved goodness, or protection at the conclusion of war. Similarly, one child’s inclusion of a clown figure (f = 1) highlighted the return of laughter and entertainment as signs of recovery. These findings demonstrate that children represented resilient and optimistic portrayals of post-war life, foregrounding psychosocial healing through play, joy, and vivid color (as seen in Fig. 21). These depictions convey a sense of emotional reconstruction, with creative expression appearing to function as a symbolic means of recovery and hope.

Discussion

The five thematic categories identified in the findings are interpreted below within three analytically distinct yet interrelated frameworks. The theme of Visual and Symbolic Elements is examined within the cognitive–symbolic framework, reflecting children's tendency to construct concrete and observable representations of war through weapons, authority figures, national symbols, and fictional entities. The themes of Physical, Ecological, and Life-Related Destruction, Emotional Responses and Psychological Effects, and Social Relations and Social Interaction are situated within the emotional–psychosocial framework, capturing the affective, bodily, and relational dimensions embedded in children's representations. The theme of Peace, Solidarity, and Healing is interpreted within the peace, resilience, and future-oriented meaning-making framework, reflecting children's active construction of hope, restoration, and moral imagination. Together, these frameworks reveal that children's representations of war constitute a layered meaning system in which cognitive, emotional, and future-oriented processes operate simultaneously.

Cognitive and symbolic representations of war in early childhood

This study re-evaluates established assumptions in the literature regarding the formation of perceptions of war in early childhood by investigating the perceptions of preschool children aged 4–6 years through drawings and narratives. One of the key findings of the research is that even in a context where war is not directly experienced, children produce powerful, consistent, and multi-layered representations of war. This suggests that perceptions of war are not solely the product of traumatic experiences; rather, they are constructed from an early age through cultural, media, and social interaction processes. Therefore, images of war emerge as symbolic manifestations of indirect learning and collective memory mechanisms beyond individual experience.

Blankemeyer et al. [7], in a study conducted within an ecological framework with a total of 491 children from the United States and Northern Ireland during the 2003 Iraq War, revealed that factors at the macrosystem (sociopolitical context) and exosystem (media) levels can shape children's perceptions of war without direct contact. The findings of the current study are consistent with this ecological framework, however, the systematic and repetitive representations in the Turkish sample show that this mechanism operates earlier and in a more structured way among preschool children than previously thought. Deguara's [17] qualitative study with children aged 5.5 years on average in Malta provides a concrete illustration of this process. Even children who were not directly exposed to conflict themes expressed statements such as “I don't want war in my home,” indicating that they conceptualized war as an immediate threat rather than an abstract concept. The present study supports these findings and documents that these representations of fear and desire for peace form stable patterns shared at the societal level across a broader sample.

Nagy-Béni [47], in her study examining the visual representation of the Russo-Ukrainian War in online news media, draws attention to how images of war are directed through digital media and political narratives. The current study takes this theoretical framework a step further, revealing through empirical findings how media and political narratives are transformed into concrete symbolic productions (drawings and narratives) in children's worlds. Hazer and Gredebäck [22], in their review examining the effects of war, displacement, and traumatic events on child development, reveal that direct exposure to war has devastating consequences on mental health and emotional development. The present study, however, offers a new perspective to the literature by revealing that this destructive effect is not limited to direct exposure, even children who have never experienced war exhibit similar symbolic organization tendencies, emphasizing that indirect exposure is an explanatory factor in its own right.

Children's experiences of war are represented as a multi-layered phenomenon—beyond physical conflict, encompassing emotional, social, environmental, and symbolic dimensions—which aligns with studies conducted in different cultural contexts (e.g., [1, 21, 23, 51]). Jabbar and Betawi [28], in their mixed-methods study with Iraqi refugee children (aged 6–16) in Jordan, showed that drawings reflected not only physical violence but also family breakdown, displacement, and humanitarian aid. While this study shows that representations of war are shaped by children's direct experiences as refugees, the current study produces a similar multidimensionality in the context of indirect exposure. This comparison indicates that the production of multilayered representations may arise not only from lived trauma but also from cultural and media circulation.

Children's representations of war, which are mostly constructed through visual and symbolic elements, cannot be explained solely by developmental limitations; this can also be conceptualized as a cognitive-emotional “organization strategy.” From a comparative perspective, Deguara [17] defines this process as an indication of children's efforts to externalize their internal experiences and establish cognitive order through concrete symbols. Abdussalam [1], in his study examining the war-related narratives of children living in temporary shelters in the Boganga and Rorogagus areas of Lanao del Sur, emphasized that children's visual narratives are not only a means of artistic production but also a means of emotional expression, social observation, and cognitive organization. The current study applies this framework to children who have not experienced direct conflict, demonstrating that symbolic expression can function independently of the clinical trauma context. In this context, children's drawings are highlighted not only as “descriptive products” but also as pedagogical indicators that reveal cognitive and emotional regulation processes.

The co-occurrence of leader/authority figures and national symbols in children’s drawings indicates that children interpret war not only as an individual threat, but also through broader categories such as protection, unity, and collective belonging. This finding aligns with previous research showing that national identities and enemy images begin to take shape from an early age within conflict-related contexts (e.g., [3, 42]). Similarly, Kolstø [31] highlights that the function of national symbols—whether unifying or divisive—depends on their political positioning, while Wiltgren [67] shows that young people use symbols such as flags and maps as visual markers in the construction of identity. In line with these perspectives, the symbolic productions of preschool children in the present study suggest that national symbols are not merely used as decorative elements, but function as early tools of ontological security and identity anchoring. References to figures such as Atatürk and Trump can be interpreted as concrete manifestations of these dynamics, indicating that knowledge of political and historical figures is acquired at an early age through domestic discourse and media representations. Furthermore, the construction of military figures within a protective framework by a group of children points to the possibility that military representations carry a dual meaning. While the literature has predominantly approached military imagery from a threat-centered perspective, the present findings indicate that such representations may also reflect notions of protection, security, and resilience. Taken together, these findings suggest that children’s representations of war integrate cognitive, emotional, and symbolic dimensions in complex and interconnected ways, highlighting the inherently multidimensional nature of early meaning-making processes.

Emotional meaning-making and psychosocial dimensions of war

Children's association of war not only with the scene of conflict but also with the destruction of their living spaces, environmental degradation, and the obstruction of access to basic needs constitutes one of the important findings of this study in terms of its psychosocial dimension. Children's conceptualization of war as a destruction encompassing both the built environment (home/neighborhood) and nature aligns with Bronfenbrenner's [9] ecological development theory: The interaction of micro (family/home), meso (school/neighborhood), and macro (political climate/cultural values) levels creates a multi-layered structure that shapes children's meaning-making processes. In this context, Bürgin et al. [10], in their policy-focused studies centered on the context of the war in Ukraine, emphasize that the mental health effects of war on children must be addressed with a multi-level, trauma-informed approach that encompasses family systems, community bonds, and cultural meaning frameworks. In his study evaluating the effects of war, MacDonald [36] found that children's coping capacities in war conditions are significantly more fragile than those of adults and that emotional problems intensify during and after the war. Peltonen [51], in a comprehensive review examining children in conflict-affected areas, emphasizes that the impact of war on children is not limited to individual trauma responses, multi-level social and ecological factors such as family systems, community bonds, and cultural resources shape these processes. McLernon and Cairns [40], in their study with 343 adolescents in Northern Ireland, found that adolescents' understanding of peace and war stabilized developmentally after the 1994 ceasefire, however, the vast majority of participants did not describe their country as being “at peace.” The fact that the themes of security and vulnerability are also evident in children who are geographically and personally distant from the conflict in the current study suggests that this thematic structure is based not only on direct experience but also on collective and cultural narratives.

The co-occurrence of emotional reactions, physical injury, and helplessness in children's drawings and narratives indicates that war creates a multi-layered emotional burden. This burden is reflected in children’s mental representations. The traumatic stress literature reveals that children develop not only cognitive but also intense emotional and physical reactions in response to trauma. Indeed, Pynoos et al. [54] emphasize that traumatic experiences simultaneously trigger a sense of danger, physical response, and helplessness in children, this process is shaped by the child's developmental characteristics and social environment. This theoretical framework provides an important basis for interpreting the emotional findings of the present study. Lieberman et al. [33] note that this type of emotional burden deeply disrupts the parent–child relationship in children exposed to trauma and is often processed through relationships established with attachment figures, while MacDonald [36] also states that children's emotional problems become significantly more intense in war conditions. However, in the present study, alongside representations consistent with these findings, a striking divergence nonetheless emerges. Some children's drawings not only reflect helplessness but also active behavioral patterns aimed at avoiding danger and protecting oneself. For example, representations such as a figure looking out of locked windows imply not only a feeling of being trapped, but also an orientation toward monitoring threats and self-protection. This suggests that traumatic experiences do not always produce a passive helplessness response in children, in some cases, they can also trigger active coping processes aimed at adapting to environmental threats. Indeed, it has been noted that the perceived sense of control in the face of stress supports active coping strategies, goal-directed behaviors, and psychological resilience [57]. Within this framework, the current findings point to the importance of addressing children's traumatic experiences not only from a passive helplessness perspective but also in the context of developmental adaptation and resilience processes.

The breakdown of social relationships, the emergence of categories such as loneliness, chaos, and disorder, reflect early representations of the nature of war that damages social bonds. Vygotsky's [64] sociocultural theory (two-dublication) emphasizes the central role of social bonds in learning and development, so the weakening of these bonds under extraordinary conditions can be represented in children's world of meaning as “the collapse of order.” Garbarino et al. [20] note that war and chronic violence environments can damage children's sense of security and social relationships, leading them to perceive the world as a dangerous and unsafe place. Bürgin et al. [10] also emphasize that war and forced displacement have multi-level effects on children's mental health, being associated with experiences of social isolation and insecurity. While the aforementioned studies cover children who have directly experienced conflict, the present study demonstrates that the categories of social disruption also emerge in children growing up in a relatively stable environment. This finding suggests that children conceptualize war not only as an individual threat but also as a process that disrupts social order and interpersonal relationships. This disruption, in turn, appears to shape the emergence of representations of peace and resilience.

Peace, resilience, and meaning-making in war contexts

The clear emergence of symbols of peace, hope, and healing in the findings shows that children are not actors who passively internalize images of war; rather, they are positioned as active subjects who can make sense of destruction and repair and generate hope. The fact that the theme of violence appears at a higher but similar frequency compared to symbols of peace and hope reveals that the themes of destruction and restoration are not opposites but are interrelated in children's world of meaning. This asymmetrical coexistence indicates that representations of war are organized not only along the axis of threat and harm, but simultaneously along the axis of restoration and healing. This simultaneity is consistent with the literature. Ilfiandra and Saripudin [23], in their phenomenological study conducted with preschool children (mean age 5.7 years) exposed to passive media in the Banten region of Indonesia, revealed that children can represent the concepts of war and peace simultaneously, highlighting that peace-related meanings are already embedded within children’s meaning-making processes. Maagerø and Sunde [35] documented that both Norwegian and Palestinian children expressed happiness and a secure future with images of a bright sun, flowers, and family scenes. In the current study, the sun figure alone attains a distinct value, consistent with this pattern, indicating that the sun is consistently functionalized by children in the context of war as a universal symbol of hope and security. While Jabbar and Betawi's [28] study with Iraqi refugee children shows that peace is established through religion, tranquility, and serenity, the spatial structure of the vision of peace in the present study, based on nature and peaceful living spaces, reveals that cultural and contextual factors deeply shape children's conceptualization of peace. Deguara [17] states that children who have never directly experienced war tend to view peace as the end of war and a safe place integrated with nature, while Walker et al. [65] report that, immediately after the Yugoslavia-NATO conflict, themes of interpersonal interaction and the absence of war emerged prominently in the peace drawings of children in the Midwestern United States. Consistent with these findings, the present study further extends these patterns by showing that images of nature and peaceful living spaces point not only to the end of war but also to a desire for an active community and togetherness.

Equally significant is the fact that the social solidarity category remains markedly lower in frequency compared to other categories. It can be considered that indirect war exposure highlights personal security and the search for meaning rather than the capacity for collective problem-solving, which may explain this asymmetry. However, the literature shows that the level of social support, including peer support, is significantly associated with post-traumatic stress symptoms in children exposed to trauma and has a protective function [2]. Viewed from a framework that conceptualizes resilience not as extraordinary but as a normal product of human adaptive systems (e.g., [6, 39], the presence of images of social solidarity in the drawings also indicates an awareness of children's capacity for collective healing.

The cheerful figures, play materials, and colorful drawings in the resistance, recovery, and reconstruction category can be linked to Tedeschi and Calhoun's [58] post-traumatic growth model. However, the clown figure in this category stands out as a nuanced element that points beyond superficial cheerfulness. Representing joy but also carrying dual meanings and an archetypal structure of masquerade, the clown highlights that for some children, joy may be the product not only of genuine healing but also of a defensive coping strategy. This distinction supports perspectives emphasizing that children's visual narratives are best understood beyond single-layered interpretation [13, 38, 63]. Although the post-traumatic growth model has mostly been tested with adults and individuals directly exposed to conflict, the findings of this study suggest that the model may also apply in the preschool period and in the context of indirect exposure, offering a tentative framework for early childhood as well.

Particularly significant is the meaning structure underlying the theme of Peace, Solidarity, and Healing: While symbols of peace establish a language of longing and hope, images of nature produce the spatial counterpart of this longing; scenes of social solidarity embody the relational dimension of healing, while themes of joy and reconstruction embody individual resilience. This holistic pattern shows that children construct the symbolic, social, and spatial dimensions of a safe community in their minds. Walker et al. [65] revealed that children represent war in a multi-layered way, including activity, group conflict, death, difficult emotions, and fantasy dimensions. The present study also reveals that children's perception of war is complex and multidimensional, however, it is understood that this complexity is balanced by a simultaneous meaning structure centered on peace, solidarity, and healing.

Taken together, this analytical mapping demonstrates that children’s representations of war are not confined to isolated thematic categories; rather, they reflect an integrated and multidimensional system of meaning-making that connects cognitive, emotional, and future-oriented dimensions of experience.

Conclusion, implications, and practical recommendations

The results of this study show that children who have not directly experienced war are nevertheless able to produce strong, coherent, and detailed representations of war. Children conceptualize war not merely as physical conflict, but as a multilayered phenomenon encompassing emotional destruction, environmental devastation, the collapse of social bonds, and dimensions of identity and belonging.

Children primarily represent war through concrete and physical elements. Instruments of war—such as weapons, bombs, tanks, and fire—appear dominantly in these representations, while soldiers and enemy soldiers constitute the most salient human figures. Importantly, the soldier figure does not serve a single function: while some children position soldiers within a framework of attack and conflict, others construct them within a framework of protection and security provision. Atatürk is represented as a symbol of historical leadership, protection, and national liberation. National flags, especially the Turkish flag, are used functionally to signify taking sides, solidarity, and collective defense. To a lesser extent, symbolic objects and fictional entities also appear in children’s representations, suggesting that imaginative resources may accompany concrete depictions of war.

The results further indicate that children do not perceive war solely as a scene of conflict, but also represent physical and ecological destruction within this framework. Damaged structures, burning areas, polluted air, and destroyed elements of nature appear prominently in their drawings and narratives. Threats to basic needs—such as displacement, homelessness, and hunger—are also visible across representations. At the emotional level, sadness emerges as the dominant emotion, followed by fear and anger. Injury and blood are the most frequently observed bodily representations, while elements of medical assistance also appear alongside bodily trauma within the same representations. In addition to themes of captivity, escape, and helplessness, drawings and narratives also include active behavioral patterns aimed at avoiding danger and keeping threats at a distance, indicating that vulnerability is sometimes accompanied by survival-oriented action. Children also reflect expressions of sadness, fear, and vulnerability directed toward others affected by war; however, these expressions should be interpreted cautiously and understood as suggestive of emerging perspective-taking rather than fully developed empathic participation.

At the social level, violence and conflict emerge as dominant themes. Fighting individuals and dead bodies are among the most frequently observed elements, and chaotic scenes, monotony, and emotional confusion are also prominently present. Family separations, solitary individuals, and confined spaces reflect war’s disruptive impact on social bonds. At the same time, symbols of peace and hope are observed to appear together with war representations and with high frequency. While the sun, heart, rainbow, and peace flags are the most commonly used symbols, greenery, flowers, and peaceful animal figures also appear within this scope. Figures of people rushing to help, holding hands, and embracing reflect themes of solidarity and collective support, while cheerful human figures, colorful houses, and play materials symbolize processes of recovery and reconstruction. Notably, representations of violence are more frequent, yet remain relatively close to symbols of peace and hope, highlighting a meaningful proximity—despite this asymmetry—between destructive and restorative themes within children’s meaning-making.

Finally, drawings and narratives are shown to function as complementary channels of expression in the meaning-making process related to war. Children verbally expand the visual elements in their drawings within their narratives; in some cases, narratives also encompass emotional and contextual content not directly visible in the drawing. Overall, these results suggest that preschool children are capable of making sense of complex social phenomena such as war through symbolic expression and emotional imagination. Drawing and narrative thus emerge as tools through which children can cognitively and emotionally organize potentially overwhelming realities related to war and transform them into communicable meanings.

The results of this study carry important theoretical and practical implications for educational practice, clinical intervention, and peace-oriented pedagogical approaches, particularly in light of the coexistence of violence-oriented and repair-oriented representations.

From an educational perspective, the relatively close distribution between violence and peace-related representations indicates that children’s expressions should not be interpreted solely through a deficit- or risk-oriented lens. Instead, children’s drawings and narratives constitute sites in which both distress and constructive meaning-making processes are simultaneously articulated. This necessitates a shift toward analytically grounded interpretive frameworks that take into account symbolic structure, relational positioning, and narrative elaboration. Accordingly, teacher education and professional development should focus on developing competencies for interpreting children’s symbolic productions as structured meaning systems. In addition, classroom environments should be designed to support not only emotional expression but also the elaboration of prosocial meanings such as cooperation, solidarity, and care. Dialogic and reflective pedagogical practices are therefore essential for enabling children to articulate, negotiate, and transform their representations.

In clinical contexts, the results underscore the importance of recognizing both vulnerability and adaptive processes within children’s symbolic expressions. The reconceptualization of “captivity and helplessness” as encompassing active coping and survival-oriented strategies further suggests that children’s representations should not be interpreted solely as indicators of distress but also as manifestations of regulatory effort. Consequently, assessment and intervention processes should incorporate multimodal approaches—including drawing, play, and narrative—in order to capture these layered dynamics. Furthermore, the coexistence of violence and repair-oriented symbols indicates that therapeutic work should engage not only with traumatic content but also with elements of resilience, protection, and reconstruction embedded within children’s symbolic worlds. The ambivalent meanings attributed to authority and military figures further call for interpretive approaches that acknowledge symbolic duality.

In the domain of peace education, the relatively close frequencies of violence and peace-related representations provide a critical pedagogical foundation. Rather than introducing peace as an abstract or externally imposed concept, educational practices can build upon children’s existing symbolic repertoires, in which themes of cooperation, helping, and restoration already coexist with representations of conflict. This suggests that peace education can be grounded in children’s own meaning-making processes, particularly through symbolic modalities such as drawing, storytelling, and dramatic play. Additionally, children’s associations between war and environmental destruction point to the potential for integrating peace education with ecological and sustainability-oriented perspectives. Educational approaches should therefore aim to expand and transform existing representations by supporting the development of inclusive and relational understandings that move beyond binary distinctions such as “us” and “the other.”

At the family level, this study highlights that children actively interpret and reorganize war-related information rather than passively internalizing it. The coexistence of distress-related and restorative representations suggests that parental mediation should extend beyond limiting exposure to also include guided meaning-making processes. Engaging with children’s symbolic expressions through open and reflective dialogue can support both emotional processing and cognitive integration. Recognizing and reinforcing themes of care, protection, and hope within children’s representations may further contribute to the development of resilience. At the same time, maintaining continuity between home and educational environments is essential for supporting coherent and developmentally appropriate responses to complex and potentially distressing content.

Overall, this study underscores that young children construct meanings about war through culturally mediated, symbolically complex, and emotionally nuanced processes in which vulnerability and resilience, as well as destruction and repair, coexist. Recognizing and supporting this dual structure is essential for advancing trauma-informed, developmentally appropriate, and peace-oriented approaches that not only address potential risks but also actively foster children’s capacities for meaning-making, social understanding, and adaptive engagement with complex social realities.

Limitations and methodological precautions

This study explored preschool children’s perceptions of war through their drawings and narratives; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, given that children aged 4–6 years have developing abstract reasoning abilities, their perceptions were primarily expressed through concrete imagery. This was partially addressed through the incorporation of contextual analyses of the narratives accompanying the drawings. These potential influences were considered during data interpretation, although not directly measured. Additionally, the sample was drawn from specific regions of Turkey, which may limit the generalizability of the findings across different sociocultural, economic, and political contexts. Nevertheless, diversity across socioeconomic and institutional backgrounds was maintained to enhance contextual variability. As with all qualitative research, interpretive subjectivity introduced a potential source of bias. This was minimized through the use of multiple coders, inter-coder agreement procedures, and thematic reliability checks.

The findings rely primarily on the symbolic interpretation of children’s drawings and narratives without triangulation with additional perspectives such as parents or teachers. While these symbolic expressions provide valuable insights into children’s internal meaning-making processes, the absence of multi-informant data limits the ability to contextualize these representations within children’s broader social environments. The study did not systematically report cases of children who did not produce drawings or produced content beyond the assigned task. In early childhood settings, variations such as task avoidance, attentional difficulties, or emotional overload may influence children's ability to produce symbolic representations. Therefore, analyses based solely on produced artifacts may limit the representativeness of the sample and should be interpreted with due caution. The study included children who were reported by their schools and families to have no prior clinical diagnoses, and this reported status was accepted as sufficient for the scope of the current qualitative analysis. Additionally, no independent clinical screenings or standardized assessments were conducted by the researcher to verify these developmental profiles. Given that developmental variability can significantly influence children’s representational and narrative capacities, this represents a limitation in interpreting the findings. Therefore, while the results provide deep insights into symbolic representations, they should be interpreted within the framework of this reported developmental status, as unmeasured individual differences in cognitive or special educational needs may influence the meaning-making processes.

Ethical considerations were central throughout the research process. In particular, children who had directly experienced war, forced migration, or traumatic conflict were excluded from the study to prevent re-traumatization and to ensure emotional safety. This ethical decision necessitates interpreting the findings as reflecting the perceptions of children indirectly affected by war-related contexts. Consequently, the potentially more intense or qualitatively distinct perceptions of war held by children with direct traumatic experiences are not fully represented in this study. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insights into how young children symbolically construct meanings related to war, highlighting the complexity of early meaning-making processes in socially and emotionally challenging contexts.

Semi-structured interview form

The semi-structured interview protocol was developed by the author for this study to explore preschool children’s perceptions of war through their drawings and narratives.

The interviews were conducted in two short stages: before and after the drawing activity. All questions were open-ended and asked in a friendly, age-appropriate manner to help children express their ideas freely.

Pre-drawing prompt

Before the drawing activity, each child was asked the following open-ended question to elicit their spontaneous thoughts and feelings about the concept of war:

“In your view, what does war mean? What comes to your mind when you hear the word war? Please draw it.”

Post-drawing questions

After completing the drawing, each child was asked a few short questions to help them describe and interpret their own drawing:

“What did you draw in your picture?”, “Can you tell me about your picture?”

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The research strictly adhered to ethical standards and principles of conducting studies with young children. Ethical approval was obtained from the Tokat Gaziosmanpasa University Ethics Committee, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the American Psychological Association [APA] Ethical Principles. As all participants were under the age of 16, written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. The purpose and procedure of the study were explained to children in an age-appropriate manner, and verbal assent was obtained from all participating children prior to data collection. Participation was entirely voluntary; both parents or legal guardians and the children themselves were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without any negative consequence, and children were further assured that they were free to decline answering any question.

During data collection, children's psychological safety was prioritized. No questions or activities related to war, destruction, or loss were formulated in a leading or traumatic manner. Drawing and interview sessions were conducted in informal, child-friendly, and familiar environments, with the researcher maintaining a non-directive, observant role. Parents were not present during data collection and did not have access to the children's drawings or narratives, in order to ensure independent and uninfluenced expression. Children's drawings and narratives were anonymized and labeled using participant codes (e.g., C41; "The plane is dropping bombs."– 5-year-old boy). All quotations and visual materials were used solely for scientific interpretation purposes, and data were securely stored and not shared with any third parties.

Consent for publication

All visual and verbal data presented in this manuscript were collected and reported in a way that fully preserves participants’ anonymity. No identifying information or personally sensitive details are included. Written informed consent for publication of anonymized drawings and narrative excerpts was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participating children.
