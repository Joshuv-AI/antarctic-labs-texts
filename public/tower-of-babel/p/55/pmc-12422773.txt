The First Microsurgery-assisting Robot Equipped With an Automatic Instrument Exchange System and a Miniature Tool Kit
Authors: Makoto Mihara, Hisako Hara, Kazuhito Wakana, Hirofumi Owaki, Kei Mikami, Atsushi Miyamoto, Munekazu Naito, Ken Masamune
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:Microsurgery requires high precision, stability, and effective teamwork. However, current robotic-assisted surgery faces challenges such as inefficient instrument exchange and dependence on surgical assistants, contributing to prolonged operative times and increased costs. To address these issues and enhance surgical efficiency and surgeon autonomy, we developed a microsurgery-assisting robot equipped with an automated instrument exchange system and a specialized micro tool kit. The robot was tested using chicken-wing arteries and rat vessels with diameters ranging from 0.35 to 0.8 mm. Evaluation parameters included anastomosis time, frequency of instrument exchanges, and vascular patency rates. The automated system completed instrument exchanges accurately within approximately 9 seconds, maintaining a smooth workflow while significantly reducing reliance on assistants. In the rat model, a vascular patency rate of 100% was achieved. Although anastomosis times were slightly longer compared with manual operations performed by skilled surgeons, our system is anticipated to improve accuracy in complex microsurgical procedures involving extremely fine vessels. Conventional microsurgery-assisting robots have primarily focused on performing vascular anastomosis alone; however, our newly developed robot with an automated instrument exchange mechanism effectively facilitates a broader range of tasks, including vessel dissection, trimming, vascular anastomosis, and postoperative patency assessments.

Takeaways

Question: Can robotic assistance with an automatic instrument exchange system improve surgical precision, reduce operative time, and support efficient solo surgery in supermicrosurgery?

Findings: Robotic tool exchange achieved 9-second instrument swaps and 100% patency in rat vascular anastomoses. Although suturing time slightly increased, overall precision, procedural stability, and workflow improved, reducing assistant needs and enabling independent performance.

Meaning: Robotic microsurgery with automated instrument exchange enhances surgical precision, efficiency, and autonomy, minimizing reliance on assistants during complex microsurgical tasks.

INTRODUCTION

Microsurgery requires exceptionally high precision, stability, and meticulous coordination between surgeons and assistants. However, conventional robot-assisted surgery faces challenges such as inefficient instrument exchange, reliance on nursing staff and assistants, and increased surgeon workload, resulting in prolonged operative times and diminished efficiency.1–3 To address these issues, we developed an innovative microsurgery-assisting robot (Fig. 1) incorporating 2 novel features: the world’s first automated instrument exchange system (Fig. 2) and a specialized miniature tool kit (Fig. 3).

This tool kit allows all instruments to be arranged within the operative field, enabling surgeons to execute procedures independently without instrument handovers. This facilitates greater concentration on delicate tasks. We assessed the effectiveness of this robotic system primarily in terms of reduced operative time and enhanced precision.

METHODS

Robot Development

The robot was developed through collaboration between supermicrosurgeons (Mukumi Clinic, Tokyo, Japan, and JR Tokyo General Hospital, Tokyo, Japan) specializing in lymphaticovenous anastomosis (LVA) and engineers (SONY Group Corporation, Tokyo, Japan); the surgeons identified clinical needs and conducted preclinical evaluations, whereas the engineers were responsible for design and refinement.

Surgeons

Two board-certified plastic surgeons (1 man and 1 woman), both specialists in microsurgery, particularly LVA, performed more than 1000 LVA procedures.

Materials

Two arteries and 2 veins from chicken wings, along with 6 arteries and 2 veins from rats, were used in this study. Vessel diameters ranged from 0.35 to 0.8 mm. These vessels were specifically selected to simulate the conditions of supermicrosurgical anastomosis required in LVA procedures.

Equipment

The newly developed microsurgery-assisting robot was used in this study. Vascular anastomoses were performed using 11-0 and 12-0 nonabsorbable monofilament nylon sutures (15 cm in length) fitted with 2.5 mm gently curved taper-point needles (Crownjun; Kono Seisakusho, Co., Ltd., Chiba, Japan). Sutures with 11-0 needles were selected for vessels 0.7 mm or greater in diameter, whereas 12-0 needle sutures were used for vessels 0.7 mm or less in diameter. Each anastomosis was completed with 4–7 stitches, and owing to the 15-cm thread length, no more than 1 suture was consumed per anastomosis. Additionally, an automated instrument exchange system was used, capable of supporting needle holders, forceps, and scissors.

Evaluation Parameters

The following metrics were measured and analyzed: the number of sutures required per anastomosis, the anastomosis time (in minutes), the total number of instrument exchanges performed during the procedure, the duration of each instrument exchange (in seconds), and the postoperative vascular patency rate assessed immediately following the completion of each anastomosis.

Experimental Procedure

Chicken-wing arteries and rat blood vessels were prepared under appropriate experimental conditions. (See Video [online], which displays explanation of the features of the microsurgery-assisting robot and provides demonstration of vessel dissection and vascular anastomosis on a 0.35-mm artery in the rat model.)

Arterial and venous anastomoses were subsequently performed using the microsurgery-assisting robot. During these procedures, instruments were switched using an automatic instrument exchange system. Key data, including the number of sutures used, anastomosis time, number of instrument exchanges, and the duration required for instrument exchange, were systematically recorded. Following the procedures, vascular patency was evaluated through standardized patency tests.

Statistical Analyses

All data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) and OpenAI's ChatGPT (GPT-4; OpenAI, San Francisco, CA). Group comparisons were performed with Student t tests or Mann-Whitney U tests as appropriate, and patency rates were compared by the Fisher exact test. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Results

Analysis of the chicken-wing group showed vessel diameters ranging from 0.4 to 0.8 mm, with a median of 0.65 mm, and a consistently applied suture count of 4 stitches per anastomosis (Table 1). Operative time in these ex vivo procedures varied from approximately 11 to 23 minutes, and the corresponding number of surgical instrument exchanges ranged from 7 to 11. Because these experiments did not involve live tissue perfusion, patency was recorded as not applicable. In contrast, the rat group exhibited smaller vessel diameters of 0.35–0.6 mm (median 0.5 mm) and required 5–7 stitches per anastomosis. Operative times extended from approximately 26 to 47 minutes, whereas the number of surgical instrument exchanges ranged from 7 to 16. All vessels anastomosed in rats remained patent on completion of the procedure. Statistical comparisons indicated that vessel diameter, operative time, and number of stitches differed significantly between the 2 groups (P < 0.05).

System Performance

The robotic system successfully completed each instrument exchange in an average of 9 seconds without any observed errors.

Surgical Outcomes of Our LVA Under Local Anesthesia

In a series of 3 cases (10 LVAs) performed under local anesthesia by M.M. and H.H., the average anastomosis time per LVA site was 9.5 minutes (range, 6.5–17.2 min), with an average of 4.6 suture throws per anastomosis (range, 4–6). The mean diameter of the lymphatic vessels was 0.64 mm (range, 0.3–1.0 mm), and the venous diameter was 0.89 mm (range, 0.5–1.2 mm).

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated that a microsurgery-assisting robot equipped with an automatic instrument exchange system markedly improves procedural efficiency by completing each instrument change in ~9 seconds. Unlike conventional robots,4–6 which require manual exchanges and often interrupt surgeons for several minutes, our system enables precise swaps without assistance, preserving concentration during delicate tasks. Smaller vessel diameters (0.35–0.5 mm) were associated with longer anastomosis times and more frequent exchanges, indicating that vessel size influences procedural difficulty. These findings highlight the need for further improvements to enable the robot to handle ultrafine vessels with greater precision and efficiency.

In ex vivo chicken-wing experiments, arterial anastomoses took longer than venous ones, whereas no significant time difference was observed between vessel types in the in vivo rat model. This discrepancy suggests both vessel diameter and blood flow presence affect operative duration. Quality assessment in the chicken-wing model relied on visual inspection, whereas the rat model allowed more realistic evaluation after clamp removal and blood flow restoration. Anastomotic leakage often required additional suturing, extending operative time; leaks on the posterior wall were especially difficult to handle for surgeons less familiar with robotic systems. Thus, anastomosis times in rats were about twice those in chicken wings.

Despite these challenges, the robot achieved 100% patency in rat experiments, demonstrating reliability and suggesting potential clinical benefits. Compared with manual LVA, robotic procedures took about 2–4 times longer time but offered mechanical standardization and reproducibility of the technique. This standardization may reduce intersurgeon variability and enhance accuracy in ultrafine anastomoses. For clinical adoption, further technological refinements are needed to reduce procedural time, accommodate anatomic differences, and validate performance under real-world surgical conditions.

CONCLUSIONS

This study confirms that a microsurgery-assisting robot equipped with an automatic instrument exchange system and a miniature tool kit enhances procedural efficiency, reduces reliance on surgical assistants, and achieves 100% vascular patency in a rat model. The system performed 128 consecutive instrument exchanges without error and yielded performance metrics comparable to those reported for existing robotic platforms. Further research is warranted to improve anastomotic precision and to extend the robot’s applicability to diverse microsurgical procedures.
