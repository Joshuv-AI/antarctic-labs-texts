Anti-capsid nanobodies function as HIV-1 capsid inhibitors by intracellular capsid degradation
Authors: Florence M. Stel, Charlotte E. J. Verkuijlen, Esther M. Zijlstra-Willems, Gillian Dekkers, Raimond Heukers, Neeltje A. Kootstra, Teunis B. H. Geijtenbeek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antiretroviral therapy (ART) strategies against HIV-1 have been successful in suppressing HIV-1 replication and preventing disease progression. However, drug-resistance development, side-effects and life-long adherence of current drugs have driven the development of novel inhibitors against HIV-1. Inhibition of capsid production by targeting the highly conserved capsid protein (CA) has shown to be promising in blocking viral replication. Here, we developed a capsid-targeting biological composed of a single-domain Llama VHH that binds CA, fused to human IgG1 Fc to activate TRIM21-mediated degradation. This anti-capsid biological (aCA-Fc) efficiently bound CA. When expressed intracellularly aCA-Fc completely blocked HIV-1 production by degrading the capsid precursor Gag. Moreover, transfection of purified aCA-Fc during HIV-1 infection also resulted in degradation of Gag. aCA-Fc inhibited HIV-1 replication to similar extent as clinically approved capsid inhibitor lenacapavir (LEN). Interestingly, aCA-Fc didn’t interfere with viral entry and reverse transcription, but eliminated newly produced HIV-1 Gag via TRIM21-mediated proteasomal degradation, even when added after infection. Gag production was restored by proteasome inhibition, introduction of the H433A mutation in the Fc domain and by TRIM21 knockdown using siRNA. These findings suggest that capsid-targeting biologicals, upon effective intracellular delivery, could serve as novel therapeutic strategies for viral suppression through intracellular protein degradation.

Introduction

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) effectively suppresses HIV-1 viral replication, preventing disease progression and reducing morbidity in people living with HIV-1 (PLWH). However, ART can lead to the development of drug resistance, due to the high mutation rate generated by the poor fidelity of reverse transcriptase1,2. Furthermore, PLWH receiving ART are at a higher risk for co-morbidities and mortality, which can often be attributed to factors related to therapy3–5. Lastly, adherence to the drug regimen in PLWH can be challenging due to the necessity of lifelong, daily treatment, which can be impacted by side effects and complex dosing schedules. Therefore, alternative antiviral therapeutics to provide long-lasting control of HIV-1 replication are urgently needed.

The capsid protein CA is generated from the polyprotein Gag, and is a promising target for antiretroviral therapy6,7. The capsid core consists of CA/p24 monomers that assemble into multimers, forming hexamers and pentamers that make up a conical core. The core particle encapsidates the viral RNA genome and interacts with host cellular factors to support multiple steps in HIV-1 replication, from post-entry trafficking and reverse transcription to nuclear import, integration, and assembly of new viral particles8–10. Moreover, CA is highly conserved across HIV-1 subtypes, and mutations in CA often lead to impaired viral fitness11–13. Currently, the most notable capsid inhibitor is lenacapavir (LEN, GS-6207), a potent and long-acting capsid inhibitor with promising results in both preclinical and early clinical studies14–16. Structural studies have shown that LEN binds at an interface between neighboring CA monomers within the hexamer lattice, which hyperstabilizes the lattice and interferes with its interactions essential for the structure and function of the capsid core15,17,18. Despite its efficacy, resistance to LEN has emerged via CA mutations (Q67H, N74D) in both cell culture-based viral breakthrough assays and in participants of clinical trials who received LEN14,15,19. This underscores the need for additional approaches targeting the capsid.

The use of antibodies as an antiretroviral therapy has been a topic of research for decades, mostly in the form of broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) targeting the viral envelope glycoprotein or proteins expressed on the surface of target cells20,21. Antibodies have various favorable properties, like a long half-life, high specificity, and low toxicity, that make them good candidates for long-acting antiretrovirals22. However, the HIV-1 envelope is extremely variable even within the same person, within the same subtype, and between subtypes23,24. It is therefore difficult to develop effective bnAbs across different variants and subtypes. Antibodies mostly reach extracellular proteins and are therefore limited to viral envelope proteins expressed on the cell and virus surface. Notably, the intracellular presence of antibodies induces an intracellular defense mechanism involving the high-affinity Fc-receptor tripartite motif 21 (TRIM21), which recognizes the human constant Fc domain of antibodies25, thereby targeting the intracellular antibody-bound pathogens or proteins to the proteasome for degradation26,27. Here, we have developed a strategy to exploit this TRIM21 intrinsic proteasome degradation pathway to create a new class of antiviral biologicals that target the capsid.

We have generated an anti-HIV-1 CA biological composed of a single domain Llama VHH (nanobody) fused to human IgG1 Fc (aCA-Fc). This biological efficiently bound HIV-1 CA and intracellular expression of aCA-Fc blocked HIV-1 replication by inducing Gag/CA degradation via the TRIM21-proteasomal pathway. Importantly, delivery of purified aCA-Fc protein to infected cells also degraded Gag/CA and prevented HIV-1 replication. Our study reveals that TRIM21-recruiting biologicals against intracellular Gag/CA represent a potent new approach to combat HIV-1 infection.

Results

Construction and functionality of aCA-Fc biological

The capsid-binding VHH domain 59H10 28 was synthesized fused to the Fc domain of human IgG1, and cloned into a lentiviral vector (Fig. 1A). A similar construct was developed with a signal peptide (sp) sequence before the VHH domain to promote secretion of the protein in the culture supernatant29. The U87.CD4.CCR5 cell line was transduced using lentiviral vectors to express anti-CA-Fc biologicals with (sp-aCA-Fc) or without (aCA-Fc) signal peptide. Binding of sp-aCA-Fc and aCA-Fc to HIV-1 capsid protein CA was determined by p24 ELISA using detergent-treated culture supernatant from HIV-1-infected cells. Both sp-aCA-Fc and aCA-Fc bound to immobilized CA (Fig. 1B). Supernatant of sp-aCA-Fc expressing cells was more efficient than sp-aCA-Fc cell lysate and vice versa cell lysate from aCA-Fc was more effective than aCA-Fc supernatant (Fig. 1B). Similarly, aCA-Fc but not sp-aCA-Fc was detected in cells (Fig. 1C) whereas sp-aCA-Fc was indeed mostly secreted and could be detected in supernatant (Fig. 1D). Expression of aCA-Fc biological blocks HIV-1 replication

Next, we investigated whether aCA-Fc protects against infection with HIV-1. U87.CD4.CCR5 cells stably expressing aCA-Fc and mock-transduced U87.CD4.CCR5 were infected with increasing concentrations of HIV-1 NL4-3-Ba-L, and viral replication was determined by measuring Gag/CA production in culture supernatant and cell lysates at 7 days after infection using a p24 ELISA. Infection of cells expressing aCA-Fc did not lead to detectable Gag/CA production in the culture supernatant of aCA-Fc expressing cells, whereas high levels of Gag/CA were detected in mock-transduced cells (Fig. 2A). Strikingly, intracellular Gag/CA was almost completely absent in aCA-Fc expressing cells, and some Gag/CA was detected at a high multiplicity of infection (MOI). No to very low levels of both Gag (p55) and CA (p24) protein was detected by immunoblotting in the cell lysate after infection of aCA-Fc expressing cells at different MOI, whereas Gag and CA were clearly detected in mock-transduced cells at all MOI (Fig. 2B). Similarly, whereas high levels of Gag/CA were detected in mock transduced cells upon HIV-1 infection at different MOI, no Gag/CA was detected in aCA-Fc expressing cells as measured by flow cytometry using a p24 antibody staining (Fig. 2C). In addition, we observed that the expression of aCA-Fc did not affect cell viability as measured by MTT assay. Upon HIV-1 infection with increasing MOI, mock-transduced cells exhibited decreasing cell viability, whereas cell viability of cells expressing aCA-Fc remained comparable to that of uninfected control cells (Fig. 2D). Intracellular delivery of biological blocks to HIV-1 replication

Sp-aCA-Fc was purified from culture supernatant, and we next assessed whether intracellular delivery of sp-aCA-Fc protein in HIV-1-infected cells inhibits HIV-1 replication. U87.CD4.CCR5 cells were infected with HIV-1, and after 1 h, the cells were transfected with sp-aCA-Fc protein using Pro-DeliverIn transfection reagent. Over 30% of the cells were transfected with the sp-aCA-Fc protein as determined by intracellular Fc staining by flow cytometry (Fig. 2E). Notably, transfection of the sp-aCA-Fc biological significantly reduced HIV-1 Gag/CA in both supernatant (p < 0.001), and cell lysate (p < 0.0001), compared to Gag/CA levels observed in mock transfected and control cells (Fig. 2F). These data show that the anti-CA biological targeted to the cytosol inhibits HIV-1 production in cells that have already been infected by HIV-1.

aCA-Fc blocks HIV-1 infection late in the viral replication cycle

Next, we determined at which stage the anti-CA biological was targeting HIV-1 replication. To assess whether aCA-Fc targeted the capsid proteins of the incoming virus, U87.CD4.CCR5 cells expressing aCA-Fc were infected with a single round-luciferase reporter NL4-3 virus pseudotyped with the HIV-1 Ba-L envelope. The use of this single-round reporter virus allows us to investigate whether aCA-Fc interferes with the incoming HIV-1 by measuring luciferase activity. We observed that aCA-Fc did not block infection of the luciferase reporter virus, as luciferase activity was not decreased (Fig. 3A) indicating that aCA-Fc does not target the incoming virus. To confirm this, we infected U87.CD4.CCR5 cells expressing aCA-Fc with NL4-3-Ba-L, and the level of proviral HIV-1 DNA was determined by measuring integrated Pol 16 h after infection by PCR. In addition, U87.CD4.CCR5 cells infected with NL4-3-Ba-L in the presence of LEN were analyzed in parallel. The amount of Pol proviral DNA copies in cells expressing aCA-Fc was comparable to levels observed in control cells (Fig. 3B). LEN treatment resulted in decreased levels of Pol proviral DNA after infection with MOI of 0.05 and 0.1, showing that LEN blocks incoming HIV-1 (Fig. 3B). High levels of Pol proviral DNA in the presence of LEN were observed after infection at MOI of 1, but this was significantly lower compared to the control (Fig. 3B, p < 0.05). These results strongly suggest that the aCA-Fc biological does not interfere with the incoming virus and HIV-1 integration but that it degrades newly synthesized Gag/CA during HIV-1 replication.

We investigated whether the aCA-Fc biological blocks Gag/CA production when expressed before or after HIV-1 infection. HEK293T cells were transfected with the aCA-Fc expressing vector either 24 h before or after infection with replicating HIV-1 (NL4-3-Ba-L) pseudotyped with VSV-G. HIV-1 Gag/CA production was measured by p24 ELISA. Notably, HIV-1 production was inhibited by aCA-Fc not only when aCA-Fc was expressed before infection but also after infection (Fig. 3C). This was further tested by transfection of a Gag-Pol expression vector, resulting in an overexpression of Gag in target cells without infection or viral replication. Co-transfection of aCA-Fc resulted in a decrease in the production of Gag/CA, as measured by p24 ELISA (Fig. S2). Our data strongly suggest that the aCA-Fc biological targets HIV-1 replication post-integration and prevents the formation of new viral particles even when the cells have already been infected by HIV-1.

Gag/CA degradation by aCA-Fc is proteasome and TRIM21-dependent

To elucidate the mechanism used by aCA-Fc to block Gag/CA production, we investigated whether the interaction between the HIV-1 Gag/CA protein and the biological interfered with the detection of the protein or stability. To this extent, lysates of U87.CD4.CCR5 cells infected with HIV-1 were incubated at 37 °C with lysates from U87.CD4.CCR5 cells that expressed aCA-Fc. The amount of Gag/CA in the cell lysates remained stable over time and was not affected by the presence of the aCA-Fc biological, as measured by p24 ELISA (Fig. 4A) and immunoblot (Fig. 4B). Next, we examined which cellular mechanism was involved in the degradation of Gag/CA. The intracellular, high-affinity Fc-receptor TRIM21 recognizes intracellular Fc domains, leading to the proteasomal degradation of the target Fc protein27,30. We therefore introduced the loss-of-function mutation H433A in the Fc domain, which has previously been shown to abolish TRIM21 binding31. U87.CD4.CCR5 cells expressing either the H433A or wild-type aCA-Fc were infected with HIV-1, and virus production was analyzed by p24 ELISA. Interestingly, the H433A aCA-Fc mutant was less efficient in inhibiting Gag/CA production, as production was partially restored in both supernatant and cell lysate compared to those in aCA-Fc expressing cells (Fig. 4C). To further investigate the function of TRIM21 in Gag/CA degradation, U87.CD4.CCR5 cells expressing either the H433A mutant aCA-Fc, wild-type aCA-Fc, or the vector control were transfected with TRIM21-targeting siRNA and infected with NL4-3-Ba-L. TRIM21 mRNA was strongly decreased, whereas protein levels were partially decreased (Fig. S3A, B). Cells expressing aCA-Fc had lower expression of Gag/CA compared to cells expressing an empty vector control, and the H433A mutation again partially restored Gag/CA expression (Fig. S3C). Knockdown of TRIM21 in mutant aCA-Fc expressing cells significantly increased Gag/CA expression, although not to levels observed in siRNA control cells. Gag/CA degradation in cells expressing wild-type aCA-Fc was not decreased by TRIM21 silencing, which could possibly be explained by residual TRIM21 expression.

TRIM21-mediated immunity is regulated through the proteasomal degradation of the TRIM21-antibody-protein complex upon binding26. Therefore, U87.CD4.CCR5 cells were infected with HIV-1, and after 24 h, were transfected with sp-aCA-Fc protein in the presence or absence of proteasomal inhibitors MG-132 and ALLN. The sp-aCA-Fc biological blocked HIV-1 infection and notably, both proteasome inhibitors fully restored Gag/CA production as measured by p24 ELISA in HIV-1 infected cells (Fig. 4D). These data suggest that the aCA-Fc biological actively eliminates Gag/CA from cells and that both TRIM21 and the proteasome are involved in this degradation pathway.

Discussion

The HIV-1 capsid has been highlighted as a promising target for novel antiretroviral drugs for various reasons. It is highly conserved and has a crucial role during multiple stages of HIV-1 replication, from trafficking and protecting viral RNA, transport to the nucleus and reverse transcription, to the production of progeny virus32. In this study, we propose a nanobody-based biological as a potential antiretroviral therapy. We constructed a biological comprising a CA-targeting Llama VHH (nanobody) fused to the Fc domain of human IgG1. This aCA-Fc biological was able to block HIV-1 replication through intracellular degradation of Gag/CA via the TRIM21-proteasome pathway, specifically by targeting Gag/CA during viral assembly in the late phase of the replication cycle. The aCA-Fc biological was effective before and after HIV-1 infection, suggesting that capsid-targeting biologicals are a novel class of inhibitors that can be used in antiviral strategies.

The HIV-1 capsid can be targeted by antiviral strategies during the early as well as late phases of the replication cycle. In the early phase of infection, the incoming capsid protects the viral RNA from innate sensors and facilitates the transport to the nucleus, while in the late phase, capsid precursor protein Gag is essential for assembly and release of progeny virus32,33. The aCA-Fc biological contains the 59H10 nanobody that has been shown to bind CA with high affinity and specificity28 and also interferes with newly produced Gag processing and capsid assembly in cells co-expressing Gag-Pol and the 59H10 VHH34. These studies suggest that 59H10 interferes with capsid assembly by itself. However, the aCA-Fc biological also contains the human IgG1 Fc domain that is recognized by TRIM21, and our data strongly suggest that it thereby harnesses the TRIM21-mediated proteasome degradation pathway to degrade both Gag and CA. We indeed observed a degradation of both Gag (p55) and CA (p24) in the presence of aCA-Fc. The aCA-Fc biological blocked HIV-1 production entirely, shown by the almost complete absence of Gag/CA from both supernatant and cells. However, the aCA-Fc biological did not interfere with incoming viral capsid and integration, as proviral DNA levels were not affected in cells expressing aCA-Fc. The epitope recognized by the aCA-Fc nanobody is located at the C-terminal domain (CTD) around helices 10 and 1128 and is concealed within mature capsids like those from incoming viruses. However, the epitope is exposed on newly produced Gag, monomeric CA, and immature viral particles, and can then be recognized and bound by the biological. Therefore, it is likely that the aCA-Fc biological targets Gag polyprotein, obstructing capsid assembly and HIV-1 replication via active degradation of Gag/CA even post-infection.

Our results suggest that the antiviral effect of the biological relies on TRIM21-mediated proteasomal degradation, as aCA-Fc did not merely bind Gag/CA, but actively degrades the protein. Indeed, blocking the proteasome as well as disrupting the TRIM21 binding capacity of aCA-Fc rescued Gag/CA levels by preventing degradation. These observations align with the known activity of TRIM21 in intracellular protein degradation30. Previous studies have also highlighted the need for TRIM21 in the degradation of target proteins intracellularly, as the VHH alone did not lead to the degradation of the specific target proteins31,35. However, knockdown of TRIM21 using siRNA only showed partial rescue of Gag/CA in cells expressing aCA-Fc mutant (H443A) with low affinity for TRIM21. This is likely because residual TRIM21 remains sufficient to mediate Gag/CA degradation through the high-affinity wildtype Fc domain, whereas partial rescue becomes only detectable when Fc–TRIM21 binding is reduced by the H443A mutation.

The biological described here shows similar inhibitory capacity as compared to LEN (at 1 nM), even though they have different mechanisms. LEN targets the capsid in both the early and late phases of infection, but seems to have higher potency in the earlier steps15,17, whereas aCA-Fc inhibits HIV-1 replication in the late phase. This also indicates that the biological and LEN target different epitopes, suggesting that our biological will still be effective against identified LEN-resistant mutants like Q67H and N74D14,15,19. Moreover, the biological is targeting a conserved region in the capsid, and no mutations associated with viral escape have yet been identified.

In this study, nanobodies were used to target the capsid. These heavy-chain only, single-domain antibodies possess many qualities, including low toxicity and high specificity, which makes them interesting candidates for drug development36–38. Furthermore, nanobodies have distinct advantages over conventional antibodies that make them more suitable for targeting of intracellular proteins39,40. Nanobodies are significantly smaller (~15 kDa vs. ~150 kDa), allowing them to bind epitopes inaccessible to larger antibodies. Fusion of a nanobody to an Fc domain increases the size, but has also been shown to prolong serum half-life and, in some cases, even the affinity41,42. In addition, nanobodies demonstrate low immunogenicity in animal and human clinical studies, due to the fact that the heavy chain variable domains are highly identical to human VH43. Lastly, nanobodies are easy to manipulate, allowing for the creation of humanized nanobodies as well as contributing to a relatively easy production and creation of customized versions44, like our biological, which can be further improved upon for stability and efficacy. In addition, this creates opportunities for nanobodies to be adjusted to fit new targets or tailored to specific applications, whereas small-molecule drugs are found through extensive screening in order to find novel drug candidates. Employing a nanobody that operates entirely intracellularly and targets the conserved HIV-1 capsid expands the repertoire of therapeutic antibodies with broad HIV-1 neutralizing activity. However, there are some limitations. As these nanobodies target intracellular proteins, delivery into the cell is still an obstacle. We show that delivery of purified anti-CA biologicals into cells, using a lipid-based formulation, still results in effective capsid degradation and inhibition of HIV-1 replication, highlighting their potential as intracellular biologicals. New delivery systems, like lipid nanoparticles for mRNA or protein delivery, could provide a platform for nanobody delivery in specific cell populations. Alternatively, nanobodies can also be tagged with cell-penetrating peptides or other location signals, in order to deliver them to their target cells45.

In conclusion, we developed a nanobody-based biological that effectively blocks HIV-1 production through the intracellular degradation of Gag/CA. HIV-1 replication was blocked by the biological to a similar extent as known capsid inhibitor LEN, but in contrast to LEN, the biological targets and actively degrades progeny HIV-1 at a post-integration level. Lastly, we demonstrate that the biological-mediated degradation is dependent on recognition of the Fc domain by TRIM21 and subsequently induced proteasomal degradation. Taken together, these results show the promise of developing intracellular capsid inhibitors as antiretroviral therapies.

Materials and methods

Construction of biological constructs

Constructs expressing aCA-Fc or sp-aCA-Fc were synthesized (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US) and cloned into an HIV-1-based lentiviral expression vector pLV-hEF1a-IRES-GFP in which expression is driven by the human Elongation Factor 1 alpha promoter, with an IRES and GFP sequence downstream46, using the In-Fusion HD Cloning kit (TakaraBio, San Jose, US). The Fc mutant (H433A) was generated by site-directed mutagenesis of aCA-Fc plasmid using the CloneAmp HiFi PCR Premix (TakaraBio, San Jose, US).

Cell culture

Human embryonic kidney cells containing the mutant version of the SV40 large T-cell antigen (HEK293T cells, ATCC CRL-3216) were cultured at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltman, MA, US) containing 10% fetal calf serum (FCS, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, US), penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 µg/mL).

U87 cells expressing CD4 and CCR5 (U87.CD4.CCR547,) were cultured in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco Medium (IMDM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US) supplemented with 10% FCS, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 µg/mL), and treated once a month with G418 (Invitrogen) and puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, US). Cells were maintained at 37 °C, 10% CO2.

Generation of cell lines stably expressing anti-CA biological

For lentiviral vector production, HEK293T cells were plated in a 6-well plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C in the presence of transfection complexes containing 1 μg lentiviral vector, 0.6 μg pMDLgp, 0.2 μg pRSV-Rev, 0.3 μg pCMV-VSV-G, P3000, and Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US). After 24 h, the medium was replaced, and 48 h after transfection, supernatants were harvested and filtered over a 0.45 μm filter (Satorius Stedim Biotech, Goettingen, Germany). This supernatant was used to transduce HEK293T or U87.CD4.CCR5 cells in a 6-well plate, followed by centrifugation at 1000xg for 3 h at 32 °C. Transduced cells were sorted based on GFP expression by flow cytometry (SH800 Cell Sorter, Sony).

Virus production

NL4-3-Ba-L, Ba-L pseudotyped NL4-3Luc R-E- and VSV-G pseudotyped NL4-3-Ba-L were produced by transfection of plasmid DNA (pNL4-3-Ba-L, pNL4-3Luc R-E-48,49 and pEnv Ba-L26, or pVSV-G and pNL4-3-Ba-L) in HEK293T cells using the calcium phosphate method. In short, plasmid DNA was diluted in 0.042 M HEPES containing 0.2 M CaCl2 and mixed with an equal volume of 2× HEPES-buffered saline (HBS). After 15 min, the mixture was carefully added to HEK293T cells in a 6-well plate. After overnight incubation at 37 °C in a humidified 3% CO2 incubator, culture medium was replaced, and cell cultures were continued at 10% CO2 at 37 °C. Virus was harvested after 2 and 3 days after transfection and passed through a 0.22 µm filter. Virus titers were determined by infecting TZM-bL cells and calculating the TCID50.

Viral infection assay

To analyze the effect of the biological on HIV-1 replication, U87.CD4.CCR5 or HEK293T cells were plated in a 96-well plate, and 24 h later, inoculated with the indicated virus. After 24 h, the medium was replaced, and viral replication was analyzed using an in-house p24 ELISA50 or the RETRO-TEK HIV Type 1 p24 Antigen ELISA (ZeptoMetrix, Buffalo, NY, US) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Luciferase assay

Infection by the Ba-L pseudotyped NL4-3 luciferase single-round reporter virus was determined by luciferase activity 72 h post infection by adding 25 μL substrate (0.83 mM ATP, 0.83 mM d-luciferin (Duchefa Biochemie B.V., Haarlem, The Netherlands), 18.7 mM MgCl2, 0.78 μM Na2H2P2O7, 38.9 mM Tris (pH 7.8), 0.39% (v/v) glycerol, 0.03% (v/v) Triton X-100, and 2.6 μM dithiothreitol) directly to the culture medium in each well. Luminescence was measured using a luminometer.

Pol PCR

Quantification of HIV-1 proviral DNA was analyzed by qPCR. U87.CD4.CCR5 cells infected with DNAse-treated NL4-3-Ba-L (30 min at 37 °C; Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and 16 h after infection, total DNA was extracted using the NucleoSpin Blood kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany). The qPCRs detecting pol proviral DNA and GAPDH were performed using SYBR Green PCR mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and the following primer pairs: Pol (FW: 5′ CTTCTAAATGTGTACAATCTAGTTGCC 3′ and REV: 5′ TGATTTTAACCTGCCACCTGTAGTAG 3′), GAPDH (FW: 5′ CGAGCCACATCGCTCAGACACC 3′ and REV: 5′ CAAATGAGCCCCAGCCTTCTCCATG 3′). The following program was used: pre-incubation: 94 °C for 3 min, amplification: 45 cycles of 94 °C for 20 s, 59 °C for 30 sec, 72 °C for 30 s, melting curve stage: 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, 95°C for 15 s. Relative concentration of pol proviral DNA normalized to GAPDH was calculated using the 2–∆Ct method.

Binding ELISA

To determine the binding of aCA-Fc and sp-aCA-Fc to Gag/CA, a binding assay was performed, adapted from the in-house p24 ELISA50. In short, high-binding plates (Nunc MaxiSorp™ flat-bottom, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US) were coated overnight at 4 °C with a mouse-anti-p24 antibody in PBS. After washing, detergent-treated culture supernatant from HIV-1-infected cells was added and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The detergent removes the viral membrane, resulting in a solution that contains a mix of intact and monomeric HIV-1 Gag/CA proteins. Next, supernatant or cell lysate from cells expressing aCA-Fc, sp-aCA-Fc was diluted in PBS with 5% normal goat serum (NGS) and 0.01% Tween, and added to the plates. After 2 h at 37 °C, plates were washed and incubated for 1.5 h at 37 °C with a secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (goat anti-human AffiniPure F(ab’)2 Fragment (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, US) diluted 1:10,000 in conjugate buffer (PBS with 5% NGS and 0.01% Tween). Nanobody binding was visualized using the substrate solution (0.1 M NaAc + 0.1 M citric acid + 0.84 mg/mL TMB + 0,01% H2O2). 2 M H2SO4 was used to stop the reaction, and optical density was measured using a 450 nm filter.

Protein purification and transfection

For purification, the biologicals were isolated from the supernatant using a HiTRap Protein G column (Cytiva, Wilmington, DE, US). The column was washed with binding buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.0) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Next, the samples were run, and the column was washed with binding buffer. The protein was eluted using an elution buffer (0.1 M glycine-HCl, pH 2.7) and collected in PBS. The purity and yield of the purified biological were checked on sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) protein gel. The samples were separated on 4%–12% Bis–Tris gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltman, MA, US) and stained using Coomassie stain, using SeeBlue Plus2 ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltman, MA, US). Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford Assay (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany).

Transfection of purified biological was performed using Pro-DeliverIN (OZ Biosciences SAS, Marseille, France), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Confocal microscopy

U87.CD4.CCR5 cells stably expressing aCA-Fc or empty backbone constructs were grown on sterile round microscopy cover slides for 48 h and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, US) for at 10 min at room temperature and extensively washed with PBS. Cells were then permeabilised with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 min, washed 2 times for 2 min with PBS and blocked with PBS containing 0.5% BSA for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were incubated for 1 h with primary antibody Goat-anti-human-IgG, Fcy fragment specific (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, US) diluted at 1:150 in PBS containing 0.5% BSA. After washing in PBS for 5 min, cells were incubated for 30 min with secondary antibody diluted at 1:1000 in PBS containing 0.5% BSA, followed by another 5 min wash step in PBS. Cells were mounted in Prolong Gold (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US) with DAPI to stain nuclei. Samples were imaged on a Leica TCSS SP8 X mounted on a Leica DMI6000 and analyzed using LAS X. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability of cells expressing aCA-Fc or control cells was determined using the Thiazolyl blue tetrazolium blue (MTT) assay. MTT solution was added to the cell culture and incubated for 2–3 h at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. After removing the medium with MTT solution, MTT solvent containing 4 mM HCL and 1% Nonidet P-40 (NP40) in isopropanol was added to the cells. Homogenous solution was measured at optical density between 580 nm and 655 nm.

Inhibition of the proteasome

Proteasome inhibitor ALLN (3.125 µM; Sigma-Aldrich) or MG-132 (0.5 µM; Merck) was added to the cells 30 min before inoculation with NL4-3-Ba-L.

Flow cytometry

To measure intracellular Gag/CA or biological, cells infected with NL4-3-Ba-L were fixed for 30 min with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, US). After fixation, cells were permeabilized using Perm/Wash solution (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, US) for 5 min at 4 °C and incubated with antibodies diluted in Perm/Wash solution for 15 min at 4 °C: biological expression was detected using goat-anti-human-IgG, FcY fragment specific (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, US) followed by AF4647-conjugated donkey-anti-goat IgG (H + L) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltman, MA, US): Gag/CA was detected using PE-conjugated mouse anti-p24 (clone KC57, Beckman Coulter). Flow cytometric analyses were performed on a BD FACS Canto II (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, US) and BD FACS Symphony A1 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, US). Data were analyzed using FlowJo vX.10.10 software (TreeStar).

Immunoblotting

To measure immunoblot analysis, cells were lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, 0.5 M NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 0.25% Triton X-100) with protease inhibitors Leupeptin (100 µM) and Pepstatin A (10 µM). Next, NuPAGE LDS 4× sample buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltman, MA, US) and 0.1 M dithiothreitol (DTT; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltman, MA, US) were added, and samples were heated at 90 °C for 10 min. Separation of proteins was done by SDS-PAGE using precast NuPAGE 4–12% Bis–Tris gel and MES SDS running buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US) for 1–1.5 h at 150 V. Proteins were then transferred to a Whatman® Protran® nitrocellulose membrane (Sigma-Aldrich) using NuPAGE transfer buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US) containing 20% methanol. After blotting for 2 h at 40 V, the membrane was blocked for 2 h in PBS containing 1% Protifar (Nutricia) and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-p24 antibody (1∶100; in-house), anti-human Fc antibody (20 µg/mL, Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, US), or anti-Cyclophilin A antibody (1∶100; Affinity Bioreagents). Secondary antibodies used were: IRDye 800CW conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1∶15000, LI-COR), IRDye 680LT conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1∶15000, LI-COR), and IRDye 680LT conjugated donkey anti-goat IgG (1∶15000, LI-COR).

Determination of degradation of Gag/CA in lysed samples

Degradation of Gag/CA without active cellular mechanisms was determined by combining the cell lysate of U87.CD4.CCR5 cells infected with NL4-3-Ba-L (to produce a baseline of Gag/CA protein) and the cell lysate of U87.CD4.CCR5 cells expressing aCA-Fc, in a 1:1 ratio. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for different periods of time, and Gag/CA concentrations were measured using immunoblotting stained for p24 and by an in-house p24 ELISA50.

Overexpression of Gag/CA without infection or viral replication in HEK293T cells was achieved by transfection of Tat-independent Gag-Pol expression vector pMDL/pRRE (gB gag LV)51, together with Rev expression vector pRSV-REV51.

Knockdown of TRIM21 through siRNA transfection

For knockdown of TRIM21, U87.CD4.CCR5 cells were plated in a 24-well plate, and 6 h later were transfected with siRNA targeting TRIM21 (5 nmol) using DharmaFECT reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Horizon Discovery, Waterbeach, UK). After 24 h the medium was replaced and 48 h after transfection, RNA was isolated to confirm knockdown, and in parallel cells were inoculated with NL4-3-Ba-L at MOI 0.1. Two days after infection, the amount of Gag/CA-positive cells was determined using flow cytometry.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.2 (GraphPad Software Inc.). Multiple comparisons of unpaired grouped data were determined using a two-way ANOVA test with a Šidák multiple comparisons test. Comparison of the two groups was performed using a paired t-test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (ns = not significant, *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).
