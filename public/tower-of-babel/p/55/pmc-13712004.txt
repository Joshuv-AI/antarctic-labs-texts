Foot-and-mouth disease virus regulates glycolysis through autophagy to drive viral replication in vivo and in vitro
Authors: Qiao Xue, Huisheng Liu, Zhihua Dong, Yingming Zhu, Fan Yang, Weijun Cao, Zixiang Zhu, Haixue Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTViruses, lacking an intrinsic metabolic network, exploit host cell metabolism in order to hijack resources for their own replication. However, the regulatory relationship between foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) and energy metabolism remains incompletely elucidated. Here, metabolomic analysis was performed on PK-15 cells and tonsils infected with FMDV. Metabolites involved in glycolysis were significantly upregulated, whereas metabolites associated with the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle were markedly downregulated in vivo and in vitro, which indicated that FMDV induced reprogramming of host cell energy metabolism, shifting it from oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to glycolysis. Furthermore, FMDV infection promoted glucose uptake and its subsequent utilization in FMDV-infected PK-15 cells, facilitating viral replication. Mechanistically, on the one hand, FMDV infection activated the AKT-MTOR-dependent macroautophagy/autophagy pathway to suppress the expression of OTUD4 (OTU deubiquitinase 4), upregulating HK2 (hexokinase 2) to facilitate glycolysis. On the other hand, FMDV infection decreased the level of mitochondrial SIRT3 (sirtuin 3) through PINK1-PRKN-dependent mitophagy, leading to the increase of HIF1A (hypoxia inducible factor 1 subunit alpha), HK2, phosphofructokinase 1 (PFK1), and PKM/PKM2 (pyruvate kinases M1/2), promoting glycolysis. Overall, this study elucidates how FMDV modulates glycolysis through two different autophagic pathways, which would contribute to our understanding of how the autophagy-glycolysis axis regulates viral replication, providing new avenues for developing antiviral strategies targeting metabolism.Abbreviations: 3-MA: 3-methyladenine; ACTB: actin beta; ATG: autophagy related; CQ: chloroquine; dpi: days post-infection; EV71: enterovirus 71; FMDV: foot-and-mouth disease virus; HK2: hexokinase 2; HIF1A: hypoxia inducible factor 1 subunit alpha; hpt: hours post-transfection; MOI: multiplicity of infection; MTOR: mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase; OTUD4: OTU deubiquitinase 4; OXPHOS: oxidative phosphorylation; PFK1: phosphofructokinase 1; PFKFB3: 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3; PKM/PKM2: pyruvate kinase M1/2; SIRT3: sirtuin 3; siRNA: small interfering RNA; TCID50: 50% tissue culture infectious doses; TCA: tricarboxylic acid; TUNEL: terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling; WT: wild type.

Introduction

Foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), a member of the genus Aphthovirus in the family Picornaviridae, is the causative agent of foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), a highly contagious illness affecting cloven-hoofed animals such as cattle, swine, sheep, goats, deer, and camelids [1]. The World Organisation for Animal Health has listed it as a notifiable disease, and China has categorized it as a Class I animal disease [2]. FMDV exists as seven immunologically distinct serotypes – O, A, Asia1, C, SAT1, SAT2, and SAT3—each comprising multiple subtypes [3]. Of them, O-type FMDV is the most prevalent strain in China [4]. FMDV contains an ~8.5 kb genome encoding a single polyprotein that is subsequently processed into four structural and eight non-structural proteins [3].

Cellular metabolic processes comprise glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and the metabolism of amino acids, lipids, and nucleotides. Glucose is metabolized via glycolysis into pyruvate that faces two distinct fates: it can enter the mitochondria to fuel the TCA cycle, or it can be converted to lactate by the enzyme LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) [5,6]. The TCA cycle processes acetyl groups to generate high-energy electrons for the respiratory chain, in addition to supplying key intermediates for anabolic pathways [7]. Cells generate adenosine triphosphate (ATP) by glycolysis and the TCA cycle through the breaking-down of pyruvate or fatty acids to meet the growing energy demand of cells [8]. Viruses lack their own metabolic systems. To facilitate replication within host cells, viruses hijack cellular organelles to produce the metabolites necessary for their replication. Several viruses have been demonstrated to reprogram host cell metabolism for this purpose, including african swine fever virus (ASFV) [9], influenza A virus (IAV) [10], and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) [11]. Our previous study showed that FMDV infection significantly enhances aspartate, and the amino acid transporter SLC38A8 (solute carrier family 38 member 8) is responsible for FMDV-upregulated aspartate [12]. However, the regulatory relationship between FMDV and energy metabolism remains unclear.

Autophagy, an evolutionarily conserved self-digestive process in eukaryotic cells, plays a key role in maintaining cellular homeostasis through proteolysis [13]. During autophagy, the autophagosome, which forms when an isolation membrane envelops a portion of the cytoplasm along with its organelles and soluble contents, subsequently fuses with a lysosome. This fusion creates an autolysosome, where the encapsulated materials are degraded [14]. Multiple ATG (autophagy related) proteins (such as ATG5, ATG7, and ATG8) have been proven to contribute to the formation of autophagosomes [15,16]. Autophagy is divided into nonselective autophagy and selective autophagy [17]. Selective autophagy is a specific elimination of certain intracellular components by autophagic pathways, which includes mitophagy, reticulophagy, and ribophagy [18,19]. Studies have shown that autophagy is related to the metabolic process [20,21]. For example, newcastle disease virus (NDV) decreases SIRT3 (sirtuin 3) via PINK1-PRKN-dependent mitophagy to reprogram energy metabolism [22]; zika virus triggers autophagy to inhibit host lipid metabolism for viral replication [23]; lactate serves as a molecular bridge connecting glycolysis to autophagy via lactylation [24]. FMDV infection can induce autophagy through an AKT/protein kinase B-MTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase)-dependent pathway [25–27]. However, the mechanisms underlying the modulation of host metabolism by FMDV through autophagy have not been elucidated.

Herein, we investigated the effect of FMDV infection on the energy metabolism in FMDV-infected PK-15 cells and tonsils by metabolomic analysis using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography/quadrupole time-of-flight tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). The tonsils from uninfected pigs were used as a control. The results showed that FMDV triggered a shift in intracellular energy metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to glycolysis in vitro and in vivo, and FMDV-induced AKT-MTOR-dependent autophagy pathway and mitophagy contributed to such effect. In the present study, we provide evidence for metabolic reprogramming in FMDV-infected cells, a process regulated by autophagy.

Results

Analysis of energy metabolism in FMDV-infected PK-15 cells

Our previous results revealed that FMDV infection can increase the level of aspartate through amino acid transporter SLC38A8, thereby activating the 40S ribosome and promoting FMDV replication [12]. However, the mechanism by which FMDV regulates energy metabolism has not yet been fully understood. To analyze the change of glycolysis and the TCA cycle in PK-15 cells infected with FMDV, we first detected FMDV replication in PK-15 cells. Viral titers gradually increased as the infection progressed, indicating the successful replication of FMDV in PK-15 cells (Fig. S1).

The detailed change in energy metabolites in FMDV-infected PK-15 cells was obtained by metabolomics analysis. It was observed that, whether at 6 h or 12 h post- infection (hpi), some metabolites in glycolysis, including glucose-6-phosphate, fructose-6-phosphate, fructose-1, 6-phosphate, dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, phosphoenol-pyruvate, pyruvate, and lactate, were significantly upregulated. Meanwhile, some metabolites in the TCA cycle, including citrate, isocitrate, 2-oxoglutarate, succinate, fumarate, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP), guanosine diphosphate (GDP), and guanosine triphosphate (GTP), were significantly downregulated (Figure 1, Table S1). These results indicated that FMDV promoted glycolysis while reduced the TCA cycle in PK-15 cells. As a whole, FMDV infection significantly affected host’s energy metabolism. Analysis of energy metabolism in FMDV-infected tonsils of pigs

In pigs, FMDV establishes infection at the epithelium of tonsils, a critical site for its replication, leading to subsequent viremia [28,29]. Therefore, the tonsils from FMDV-infected pigs were collected at 3 and 5 d post-infection (dpi), and FMDV RNA was detectable in the tonsil tissues [29]. We then detected the changes in energy metabolism in tonsils during FMDV infection. The levels of glucose-6-phosphate, fructose-6-phosphate, fructose-1, 6-phosphate, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, phosphoenol-pyruvate, pyruvate, and lactate were significantly increased at 3 or 5 dpi. At 3 dpi, the levels of citrate, isocitrate, fumarate, malate, and oxaloacetate remained unchanged, while the levels of citrate, isocitrate, 2-oxoglutarate, fumarate, malate, oxaloacetate, NAD, NADP, and GTP were significantly reduced at 5 dpi (Figure 2, Table S2), indicating that most metabolites in the TCA cycle undergo significant changes by 5 dpi. Overall, the changes in energy metabolism within the pigs were consistent with those observed in PK-15 cells, although there were minor differences in the changes of some metabolites. These results demonstrated that FMDV can promote glycolysis and decrease the TCA cycle in vitro and in vivo. Glycolysis may play an important role in FMDV propagation in pigs. FMDV promotes glucose uptake for viral replication

To determine FMDV infection can lead to changes in glucose metabolism, PK-15 cells infected with FMDV were incubated with a fluorescent glucose analog 2-(N-(7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-1, 3-diazol-4-yl) amino)-2-deoxyglucose (2-NBDG) to monitor glucose uptake ability. There was no significant difference in the fluorescence intensity within the mock-infected cells. As the viral infection progressed, the fluorescence intensity within the cells gradually increased, indicating that FMDV promoted the uptake of 2-NBDG (Figure 3(A)). Subsequently, we detected the levels of glucose in mock-infected and FMDV-infected cells using a glucose detection kit. The results showed that the levels of glucose in FMDV-infected cells were significantly higher than that in mock-infected cells (Figure 3(B)). HK2 (hexokinase 2), phosphofructokinase 1 (PFK1), and PKM/PKM2 (pyruvate kinase M1/2) are the three rate-limiting enzymes in the glycolytic pathway, which play important roles in the regulation of glycolysis [30,31]. To further determine the ability of FMDV to regulate glycolysis, the expression of HK2, PFK1, and PKM in FMDV-infected cells and tonsils was assessed. The results showed that the abundance of HK2, PFK1, and PKM were gradually increased as the FMDV infection progressed, while their expressions showed no significant changes in mock-infected cells (Figure 3(C)). In addition, the protein expression of HK2, PFK1, and PKM in FMDV-infected tonsils was also enhanced compared to that in mock-infected tonsils (Figure 3(D)). The results indicated that FMDV infection facilitated the expression of HK2, PFK1, and PKM in vitro and in vivo.

With the evidence that FMDV promotes glucose uptake, we then investigated the effect of glucose on FMDV replication. PK-15 cells incubated with different doses of glucose were infected with FMDV, viral titers were detected by TCID50 assay. The results indicated that glucose has the ability to promote the replication of FMDV, and the higher the concentration of glucose, the higher the viral titers (Figure 3(E)). To further assess the impact of glycolysis on FMDV replication, 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) was used to inhibit the activity of HK and PFK15 was used to inhibit PFKFB3 (6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3) [32,33]. Then, the cells were infected with FMDV and viral titers were tested. The addition of 2-DG (Figure 3(F)) or PFK15 (Figure 3(G)) significantly reduced the replication of FMDV. Altogether, FMDV promoted the uptake of glucose, leading to an increase in the downstream metabolic enzymes and driving viral replication.

OTUD4 inhibits the expression of HK2 through apoptosis

HK2 serves as the first rate-limiting enzymes in glycolysis, the central pathway for glucose utilization [34]. We therefore elucidated the mechanism by which FMDV promotes glycolysis through regulating HK2. We first compared the proteomic profiles between mock-infected and FMDV-infected PK-15 cells (Table S3), then screened several differentially expressed proteins to assess their impact on HK2 protein expression. OTUD4 (OTU deubiquitinase 4), TXNRD2 (thioredoxin reductase 2), ZC3H12A (zinc finger CCCH-type containing 12A), and FLAD1 (flavin adenine dinucleotide synthetase 1) proteins were selected in this study. The results showed that knockdown of OTUD4, but not TXNRD2, ZC3H12A, and FLAD1, promoted the expression of HK2 (Fig. S2). Thus, OTUD4 was selected for subsequent experiments.

To further confirm the impact of OTUD4 on the expression of HK2, OTUD4 knockout (OTUD4−/−) PK-15 cells were prepared using CRISPR/Cas9 system. OTUD4−/− cells were successfully acquired (Figure 4(A)). The expression of HK2 in OTUD4−/− cells was elevated compared to wild-type (WT) cells (Figure 4(A)), indicating the function of OTUD4 in inhibiting the expression of HK2. OTUD4, a deubiquitinating enzyme, regulates the expression of many proteins in human cells by modulating the ubiquitination of proteins [35–37]. To determine whether OTUD4 inhibits the expression of HK2 through the proteasome pathway in pig cells, PK-15 cells were transfected with Flag-OTUD4 expression plasmid alongside with the proteasome inhibitor N-benzyloxycarbonyl-L-leucyl-L-leucyl-L-leucinal (MG132). OTUD4 could inhibit the expression of HK2, but adding MG132 could not reverse the inhibitory effect of OTUD4 on HK2 (Figure 4(B)), indicating that OTUD4 did not inhibit the expression of HK2 through the proteasome pathway. OTUD4 is also involved in the regulation of apoptosis [38]. We thus examined the impact of apoptosis on the function of OUTD4 using the pan-caspase inhibitor benzyloxycarbonyl-Val-Ala-Asp-fluoromethyl ketone (Z-VAD-FMK). The suppression of HK2 by OTUD4 was partially reversed by Z-VAD-FMK (Figure 4(C)), demonstrating that OTUD4 inhibited the expression of HK2 through the apoptosis pathway. To verify that OTUD4 can induce apoptosis in pig cells, we transfected PK-15 cells with a Flag-OTUD4 expression plasmid and monitored cleaved-CASP3 levels. Overexpression of OTUD4 induced the cleavage of CASP3 (Figure 4(D)), which indicated that OTUD4 promoted apoptosis in PK-15 cells. The cell states in Flag-OTUD4-transfected cells was also examined using the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay. Increased expression of OTUD4 resulted in a marked increase in cell apoptosis, and approximately 50% of PK-15 cells were observed to undergo apoptosis (Figure 4(E)). These results indicated that OTUD4 can induce apoptosis in PK-15 cells in a dose-dependent manner.

The impact of OTUD4 on glycolysis was investigated using OTUD4−/− PK-15 cells. There were no significant difference in the levels of glucose, pyruvate, and lactate in mock-infected OTUD4−/− and WT cells. The levels of glucose, pyruvate, and lactate in FMDV-infected OTUD4−/− cells were significantly higher than that in FMDV-infected WT cells (Figure 4(F)), indicating that OTUD4 negatively regulated glycolysis. Accordingly, knockout of OTUD4 also markedly boosted the replication of FMDV (Figure 4(G)). Altogether, OTUD4 downregulated HK2 expression by inducing apoptosis, resulting in the negative regulation of glycolysis.

FMDV reduces OTUD4 expression through an AKT-MTOR-dependent autophagy pathway

The potential relationship between OTUD4 and FMDV infection was further investigated. PK-15 cells mock-infected and infected with FMDV were collected and analyzed by western blotting. The expression of OTUD4 gradually decreased as the FMDV infection progresses (Figure 5(A)). Meanwhile, the abundance of OTUD4 were also reduced in FMDV-infected tonsils (Figure 5(B)). To investigate how FMDV downregulates OTUD4, we blocked the two major intracellular degradation pathways – the proteasome and autophagy-lysosome system – using specific inhibitors. Treatment of FMDV-infected PK-15 cells with the autophagy inhibitors chloroquine diphosphate (CQ) and 3-MA, but not with the proteasome inhibitor MG132, rescued OTUD4 expression (Figure 5(C)), indicating that FMDV inhibited the expression of OTUD4 primarily via the autophagy pathway. FMDV can induce autophagy through an AKT-MTOR-dependent pathway [25,39]. Therefore, we investigated whether FMDV inhibits OTUD4 expression by this way. SC79, a specific AKT activator, was employed to promote AKT phosphorylation while inhibiting its membrane translocation [40]. To determine whether FMDV degrades OTUD4 via the AKT-MTOR-dependent pathway, we assessed the expression of OTUD4 in SC79-incubated cells. The results showed that the addition of SC79 promoted the phosphorylation of both AKT and MTOR. Compared with mock-infected cells, FMDV infection could reduce the protein expression of OTUD4, but FMDV-induced decrease in OTUD4 was recovered in SC79-incubated cells (Figure 5(D)).

ATG5 is a key protein involved in the autophagy process [41,42]. Therefore, ATG5 knockout (ATG5−/−) PK-15 cells were further used to investigate the impact of autophagy on OTUD4 expression. ATG5−/− cells were mock-infected and infected with FMDV, and the abundance of OTUD4 was detected. Incubating FMDV in the ATG5−/− cells could no longer induce the degradation of OTUD4 (Figure 5(E)), confirming the role of autophagy in the degradation of OTUD4. Altogether, these findings indicated that FMDV infection activated the AKT-MTOR-dependent autophagy pathway to suppress OTUD4, thereby upregulating HK2 to facilitate glycolysis.

FMDV promotes HK2 expression by stabilizing HIF1A

Given that OTUD4 regulates glycolysis during FMDV infection, we were prompted to investigate whether other proteins also play a role in this process. HIF1A (hypoxia inducible factor 1 subunit alpha), a key transcription factor under hypoxia, positively regulates several glycolytic proteins including HK2, PFK1, and PKM, and it plays a crucial role in energy metabolism [43,44]. Therefore, we investigated whether FMDV promotes HK2 and glycolysis through the HIF1A protein. We found that FMDV promoted HIF1A expression in PK-15 cells, which progressively increased with the duration of infection (Figure 6(A)). In addition, FMDV can also facilitate the expression of HIF1A in the tonsils (Figure 6(B)). These results indicated that FMDV promoted HIF1A expression in vitro and in vivo. We further delved into the underlying mechanism of HIF1A regulation by FMDV.

The hydroxylation at the P564 site (a proline residue) of HIF1A is associated with the stability of its protein [45]. As expected, FMDV infection induced HIF1A stabilization by suppressing its hydroxylation (Figure 6(A)). Elevated mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels are known to stabilize HIF1A [46]. Since our previous results showed that FMDV infection significantly increased mitochondrial ROS [47], we postulated that FMDV promoted HIF1A expression via high ROS levels. Therefore, we employed the ROS scavenger N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) in FMDV-infected cells and then detected HIF1A expression. The results showed that FMDV infection upregulated the expression of HIF1A, while in cells treated with NAC, FMDV lost its ability to stabilize HIF1A (Figure 6(C)).

To further define the connection of HIF1A with HK2, PKM, and PFK1, we measured the expression of these glycolytic enzymes in both HIF1A siRNA-transfected cells and HIF1A−/− cells. The abundance of HK2, PKM, and PFK1 was markedly reduced in HIF1A siRNA-transfected PK-15 cells compared to that in NC siRNA-transfected cells (Figure 6(D)). knockdown of HIF1A in PK-15 cells also significantly inhibited FMDV replication (Figure 6(D)). Furthermore, the levels of HK2, PKM, and PFK1 was reduced in hif1a−/− RAW 264.7 cells compared to that in WT cells (Figure 6(E)). Meanwhile, knockout of Hif1a significantly diminished FMDV replication (Figure 6(E)). Altogether, FMDV promoted the expression of HK2, PKM, and PFK1 by stabilizing HIF1A, facilitating glycolysis.

FMDV decreases mitochondrial SIRT3, stabilizing HIF1A and HK2

We further explored other potential mechanisms that regulate HIF1A expression. SIRT3 (sirtuin 3), located in the mitochondria, is an NAD+-dependent deacetylase linked to HIF1A [22,48,49]. Therefore, SIRT3 was selected for further studies. Our results showed that FMDV induced the reduction of SIRT3 in both PK-15 cells (Figure 7(A)) and tonsils (Figure 7(B)). To delineate the pathway through which FMDV suppresses SIRT3 expression, we employed the proteasome inhibitor MG132 and the autophagy inhibitors CQ and 3-MA in FMDV-infected PK-15 cells. The fact that CQ and 3-MA, but not MG132, could rescue SIRT3 levels specifically (Figure 7(C)), demonstrating that FMDV induced SIRT3 degradation through the autophagy pathway. The impact of autophagy on SIRT3 expression was further verified using ATG5−/− cells. WT and ATG5−/− cells were mock-infected or infected with FMDV, the expression of SIRT3 was compared and analyzed by western blotting. There was no significant difference in the expression of SIRT3 among the different treated cells, which indicated that FMDV lost its ability to inhibit the expression of SIRT3 in ATG5−/− cells (Figure 7(D)), confirming that the reduction of SIRT3 by FMDV was mediated by the autophagy pathway. As SIRT3 is a mitochondrial protein [50], we hypothesized that FMDV might exploit mitophagy to degrade it. To test this hypothesis, we treated FMDV-infected PK-15 cells with Mdivi-1, a specific mitophagy inhibitor. The results showed that FMDV can induce the decrease of SIRT3, while the addition of Mdivi-1 partially reversed the expression level of SIRT3 (Figure 7(E)), indicating that FMDV indeed inhibited the expression of SIRT3 through the mitophagy pathway.

We then utilized SIRT3−/− PK-15 cells to investigate the relationship between SIRT3 and HIF1A. Compared to WT cells, the expression levels of both HIF1A and HK2 were increased in SIRT3−/− cells (Figure 7(F)), which established the connections between SIRT3 and HIF1A as well as HK2. The ability of SIRT3 to regulate HK2 prompted us to investigate its role in FMDV replication. Indeed, knockout of SIRT3 resulted in a notable enhancement of viral production (Figure 7(G)). Altogether, FMDV inhibited the expression of SIRT3 through the mitophagy pathway, stabilizing HIF1A and HK2.

FMDV decreases SIRT3 through PINK1-PRKN-dependent mitophagy

While FMDV has been shown to suppress SIRT3 through mitophagy, the key question of how the virus induces mitophagy remains incompletely understood. Electron microscopy is a widely employed technique for the visualization of autophagy. To determine the ability of FMDV to trigger mitophagy, we examined the morphological changes of mitochondria using electron microscopy. As shown in Figure 8(A), compared with mock-infected cells, FMDV infection induced more vesicles displaying typical characteristics of autophagic vesicles. The mito-QC reporter system (mCherry-GFP-FIS1[mt101–152]), a well-validated tool for monitoring mitophagy [51], was employed to further investigate FMDV-induced mitophagy. Previous studies have revealed that mitochondrial depolarization results in mitophagy [52]. Carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) can inhibit mitochondrial membrane potential, leading to mitochondrial depolarization [53]. Thus, CCCP serves as a positive control for mitophagy in this study. PK-15 cells were transfected with mito-QC reporter, followed by FMDV infection or CCCP treatment. The results showed that FMDV infection of mito-QC-transfected cells induced a shift from yellow to partially red fluorescence. The CCCP-treat cells were consistent with the FMDV-infected cells (Figure 8(B)). These results confirmed that FMDV infection can induce selective mitophagy. It is well established that PINK1 activation triggers mitophagy after mitochondrial depolarization [54]. The contribution of PINK1 to mitophagy after FMDV infection was evaluated. FMDV-infected cells, like those treated with CCCP, showed a significant upregulation of endogenous mitochondrial PINK1 (Figure 8(C)). Further analysis revealed that the expression of PINK1 accumulated predominantly in the dysfunctional mitochondria after FMDV infection, indicating that FMDV promoted PINK1 translocation to mitochondria, leading to the initiation of mitophagy (Figure 8(D)).

To determine the regulatory relationship between PINK1 and SIRT3, we employed the inhibitor Mdivi-1 in CCCP-treated and FMDV-infected cells and assessed the protein expression levels of both PINK1 and SIRT3. CCCP treatment and FMDV infection enhanced the abundance of PINK1, while they reduced the expression of SIRT3. The addition of Mdivi-1 reversed such effect (Figure 8(E)). The impact of PINK1 on SIRT3 expression was further assessed using PINK1−/− PK-15 cells. WT and PINK1−/− cells were mock-infected and infected with FMDV, the expression of PINK1 and SIRT3 was detected. In WT cells, FMDV promoted PINK1 expression and decreased SIRT3 expression. However, FMDV did not affect the expression of SIRT3 in PINK1−/− cells (Figure 8(F)). These results indicated that FMDV decreased SIRT3 through PINK1-PRKN-dependent selective mitophagy.

Given the established link between SIRT3 and HIF1A, we next examined the changes in energy metabolism using PINK1−/− cells. PINK1−/− cells mock-infected and infected with FMDV were collected and subjected to metabolomics analysis. Consistent with the protein expression data, no significant differences in metabolites of glycolysis and the TCA cycle pathways were observed between FMDV-infected and mock-infected PINK1−/− cells (Table S4). The results indicated that knockout of PINK1 abolished the capacity of FMDV to enhance glycolysis and suppress the TCA cycle. Altogether, FMDV infection decreased the abundance of SIRT3 through PINK1-PRKN-dependent mitophagy, leading to the enhancement of HIF1A and HK2, promoting glycolysis and viral replication.

Discussion

FMDV can rapidly evade host innate immunity by suppressing pattern recognition receptors, including RIGI (RNS sensor RIG-I), NOD2 (nucleotide binding oligomerization domain containing 2), and CGAS (cyclic GMP-AMP synthase) [47,55–59]. Upon entry, the virus hijacks the host’s metabolic system and resources to facilitate its efficient replication. Although our study has shown that FMDV reprograms host amino acid metabolism to enhance its replication [12], the specific mechanisms by which FMDV regulates energy metabolism are still not fully elucidated. Here, we revealed for the first time the mechanism by which FMDV regulates energy metabolism in PK-15 cells and pigs. In the process of sample collection, every PK-15 cell can potentially be infected by FMDV. The epithelial cells in the tonsils are susceptible to FMDV. Consequently, not every tonsil cell is infected by the virus, and it is also difficult to determine the proportion of infected cells due to technical limitations. Our data showed that FMDV promoted glycolysis and decreased the TCA cycle in vitro and in vivo. Two distinct mechanisms drive this process: (1) FMDV activated the AKT-MTOR-dependent autophagy pathway to suppress OTUD4, thereby upregulating HK2 to facilitate glycolysis, (2) FMDV decreased the abundance of SIRT3 through PINK1-PRKN-dependent mitophagy, resulting in the increase of HIF1A and HK2, promoting glycolysis. Our findings reveal a novel link between autophagy and energy metabolism in the context of FMDV infection.

Numerous viruses reprogram host metabolism to facilitate their own replication. For instance, NDV regulates host nucleotide metabolism by manipulating mitochondrial MTHFD2 (methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase (NADP+ dependent) 2, methylenetetrahydrofolate cyclohydrolase) [60]; ASFV infection increases host TCA cycle and amino acid metabolism to drive viral replication [9]; west nile virus (WNV) infection reprograms liver metabolism including glutathione metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and sterol metabolism [61]; classical swine fever virus (CSFV) inhibits serine metabolism by the deacetylation of phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) [62]; and porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) promotes lipid synthesis via ROS-dependent AKT-PCK1 (phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 1) signaling axis, promoting viral replication [63]. These results suggested that different viruses hijack distinct metabolic pathways to facilitate their own replication. Previous results have shown that FMDV infection promotes the level of lactate and the expression of HK2 in PK-15 cells, but the mechanism by which FMDV regulates glycolysis remains unclear [64]. Here, we analyzed the effect of FMDV on glycolysis and the TCA cycle both in vivo and in vitro, and elaborated on its mechanism in detail. These results clarify how FMDV utilizes the host’s energy metabolism to facilitate its own replication, highlighting the importance of glycolysis in FMDV replication. Glucose promotes FMDV replication in a dose-dependent manner. Low-dose glucose treatment maintains a low level of viral titer, likely due to reduced cell viability and limited energy supply for viral replication. In contrast, high-dose glucose enhances this effect. The expression of HK2 is regulated through numerous mechanisms. NLRP12 (NLR family pyrin domain containing 12) inhibits TRIM25 (tripartite motif containing 25)-mediated HK2 degradation to promote glycolysis [65]. Circular RNA circRNF20 promotes the binding of HIF1A to the HK2 promoter, enhancing the expression of HK2 [66]. Demethoxycurcumin targets PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homolog)-AKT signaling to inhibit HK2-driven glycolysis [67]. STING1, a putative innate immune signaling adaptor, restricts glycolysis by inhibiting the hexokinase activity of HK2 [68]. In the present study, we observed that FMDV mediated HIF1A stabilization via degradation of SIRT3, promoting HK2-mediated glycolysis, which is similar to the mechanism by which NDV induces glycolysis [22]. Furthermore, our data uncovers a novel mechanism regulating HK2 abundance. We demonstrated that FMDV infection suppressed OTUD4, which in turn led to the upregulation of HK2. This finding not only adds a layer of post-translational regulation to HK2 control but also integrates the OTUD4-HK2 axis into the metabolic reprogramming induced by viral infection.

OTUD4, a member of the OTU deubiquitinating enzyme with multiple functions, is involved in cancer [69], ferroptosis [70], innate immunity [36,71], and apoptosis [38]. The effect of OTUD4 on cell apoptosis remains controversial. Zhiqiang Wu et al [72] and Xiaohui Zhao et al [73] reveal that OTUD4 increases cell apoptosis in human breast, liver, and lung cancer cells. In contrast, a study by Lijuan Luo et al [38] reported that OTUD4 reduces apoptosis in lung epithelia through the upregulation of SERPINE1/PAI-1 (serpin family E member 1) ubiquitination and subsequent degradation. In the present study, our results showed that OTUD4 promoted apoptosis in PK-15 cells, revealing the connection between OTUD4 and apoptosis in pig cells. Furthermore, the role of OTUD4 in glycolysis has also been reported. Deubiquitinase OTUD4 stabilizes SLC5A2 (sodium glucose transporter 2) to promote intermediate product levels in glycolysis, as well as glucose uptake in human cells [69]. OTUD4 promotes the expression of glycolytic enzyme PFKFB3 via OTUD4-mediated deubiquitylation to regulate glycolysis in human cells [74]. Here, we found that OTUD4 inhibited the expression of HK2 through apoptosis in pig cells, which is inconsistent with observations reported in human cells. To explore this discrepancy, we compared the amino acid sequences of human and porcine OTUD4. Comparative analysis revealed significant differences in both sequence length and order of OTUD4 (Fig. S3). In addition, we transfected human OTUD4 into OTUD4−/− PK-15 cells and found that overexpression of human OTUD4 promoted HK2 expression (Fig. S4A) and FMDV-induced glucose uptake (Fig. S4B), confirming the functional difference between porcine and human OTUD4. In addition to OTUD4, other proteins may also exhibit species-specific functions. For example, PPIA/CypA (peptidylprolyl isomerase A) plays an essential role in enterovirus-71 (EV71) entry and replication in human cells [75], while porcine PPIA inhbits FMDV replication by interacting with FMDV 2B protein [76]. Collectively, these results indicate that protein function may be species-specific rather than governed by universal rules, highlighting the adaptive complexity of cellular regulation.

Autophagy is related to a wide range of biological processes including metabolic processes, organelle degradation, and immune responses, which can be regulated by both host proteins and viral infection. Such as, hydrogen sulfide-mediated protein S-sulfhydration modulates autophagy by targeting Atg18 [77]; the DExD/H-box RNA helicase Dhh1 promotes autophagy by facilitating the translation of the essential factors Atg1 and Atg13 [78]; and Rift Valley fever virus (RVFV) nucleoprotein triggers autophagy to regulate innate immune responses [79]. Previous studies have shown that FMDV infection can induce autophagy through an AKT-MTOR-dependent pathway, regulating the expression of multiple proteins [25,39]. Here, we found that OTUD4 can also be regulated via FMDV-induced AKT-MTOR-dependent autophagy, enhancing glycolysis. In addition to AKT-MTOR-dependent autophagy, we found that FMDV infection can also trigger mitophagy through PINK1-PRKN-dependent pathway, leading to the enhancement of HIF1A and HK2, thereby decreasing the TCA cycle and promoting glycolysis. These results are the first to establish a direct link between autophagy and cellular metabolism during FMDV infection, highlighting the importance of autophagy-glycolysis axis in FMDV infection. Previously, we demonstrated that FMDV induces mitochondrial damage and dysfunction through multiple mechanisms [47]. Here, we further showed that FMDV also perturbs mitochondrial function by activating mitophagy, revealing a novel pathway through which FMDV regulates mitochondrial homeostasis.

On the basis of these findings, we propose a working model for how autophagy and glycolysis contribute to FMDV infection (Figure 9). FMDV promotes glucose uptake and glycolysis to facilitate viral replication. On the one hand, FMDV activates the AKT-MTOR-dependent autophagy pathway to suppress the expression of OTUD4, upregulating HK2 to facilitate glycolysis. On the other hand, FMDV decreases the level of SIRT3 through PINK1-PRKN-dependent mitophagy, leading to the increase of HIF1A, HK2, PFK1, and PKM, promoting glycolysis. In conclusion, our study elucidates how FMDV modulates glycolysis through two different autophagic pathways, which would contribute to our understanding of how the autophagy-glycolysis axis regulates viral replication. Materials and methods

Sample collection

PK-15 cells (1 × 107 cells per group) were mock-infected or infected with FMDV upon the same incubation time in the same culture medium. After incubation, cells were harvested with cell scrapers and centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min at 4°C. The precipitated cell pellets were resuspended in ice-cold PBS (Thermo Scientific, 10,010,023). Samples were then quenched with five volumes of ice-cold quenching solution (60% aqueous methanol, 0.85% (w:v) ammonium bicarbonate, pH 7.4), centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min to remove supernatant, and the pellets were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for metabolomics analysis. Cell culture supernatants were collected for viral titration to confirm FMDV infection. In parallel, serum and oropharyngeal tonsil samples collected from FMDV-infected pigs were subjected to metabolomic analysis using an equivalent sample size.

Metabolite extraction and data acquisition through LC-MS analysis

The procedures for metabolite extraction and subsequent LC-MS data acquisition were conducted as described in our prior publication [9,12]. Briefly, sample extraction was initiated by the addition of 50 μL of a pre-cooled methanol/acetonitrile/water solution (2:2:1, v:v), followed by vortex mixing for 20 s. The mixtures were subsequently ultrasonicated and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 20 min. The supernatant was isolated and dried under vacuum. For mass spectrometric analysis, the dried metabolite pellets were reconstituted in 100 μL of 1:1 (v:v) acetonitrile-water solution and centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC ultra-high-pressure liquid chromatography (UHPLC) under the following conditions: column temperature, 25°C; flow rate, 0.5 mL/min; sample volume, 2 μL. The autosampler was maintained at 4°C, and samples were injected in a randomized order. Mass spectrometer (MS) detection was carried out on an AB Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX, Danaher, Washington, DC, USA) employing electrospray ionization (ESI) in both positive and negative ion modes.

Metabolites analysis using kits

The levels of glucose, pyruvate, and lactate in PK-15 cells were detected using appropriate kits (Solarbio, BC2505, BC2205, and BC2235), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Detection of 2-NBDG in cells

PK-15 cells incubated with 2-NBDG (Solarbio, IN3630) were mock-infected or infected with FMDV. At the indicated time points, the fluorescence whithin cells was visualized using a Nikon Eclipse 80i fluorescence microscope.

Cells and viruses

PK-15 and BHK-21 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (Gibco, 11,965,092) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Gibco, A5256701) at 37°C under 5% CO2. hif1a-knockout RAW 264.7 cells were acquired from Ubigene Biosciences Co., Ltd., located in Guangzhou, China. For viral challenge, FMDV type O strain O/BY/CHA/2010 was used in this study. Viral infection experiments were carried out as described previously [80].

Preparation of glucose medium

Glucose (G8760) was purchased from Solarbio, Beijing, China and was dissolved into DMEM medium without glucose (Thermo Scientific, 11,966,025) to final concentration of 0, 2, and 10 mM, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To assess the impact of glucose on FMDV replication, the cells were pre-treated with different doses of glucose for 2 h prior to viral infection.

Plasmids and antibodies

The cDNA encoding porcine OTUD4 were amplified from PK-15 cells and cloned into p3xFlag-CMV-7.1 vector to generate an N-terminal Flag-tagged expression construct (Flag-OTUD4). The cDNA encoding human OTUD4 were cloned into pCMV-HA vector, resulting in an N-terminal HA-tagged expression construct (HA-OTUD4). DNA sequencing was performed to confirm the constructed plasmids. Transfection was performed using jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus, 101,000,046) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

The commercial antibodies used in this study included an anti-ACTB/actin beta monoclonal antibody (Thermo Scientific, MA5-15739), anti-MTOR monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 2983), anti-p-MTOR polyclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 5536), anti-CASP3 polyclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 14,220), anti-cleaved-CASP3 polyclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 9664), anti-HIF1A-OH polyclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 3434), anti-COX4I1 monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 4850), anti-AKT polyclonal antibody (ABclonal, A18675), anti-p-AKT polyclonal antibody (ABclonal, AP0637), anti-ATG5 polyclonal antibody (ABclonal, A19677), anti-OTUD4 polyclonal antibody (ABclonal, A15229), anti-HK2 polyclonal antibody (ABclonal, A0994SP), anti-PFK1 polyclonal antibody (ABclonal, A5477), anti-PKM polyclonal antibody (ABclonal, A20991), anti-HIF1A polyclonal antibody (ABclonal, A11945SP), anti-SIRT3 monoclonal antibody (ABclonal, A7307SP), anti-PINK1 monoclonal antibody (ABclonal, A7131), anti-HA monoclonal antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, H3663), anti-Flag monoclonal antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804), anti-FMDV 3D polyclonal antibody was prepared in our laboratory.

Analysis of apoptosis by TUNEL assay

Cellular apoptosis were tested using TUNEL assay, as described previously [9,81]. Briefly, the cells transfected with Flag-OTUD4 expression plasmid were washed once with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. They were then permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 5 min at room temperature. Apoptosis was assessed using the TUNEL detection liquid (Beyotime, C1086) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Finally, the fluorescence images were captured with a Nikon Eclipse 80i fluorescence microscope.

Knockdown of protein using siRNA

In this study, the siRNA was designed and synthesized by Tsingke Biological Technology. To knockdown endogenous proteins in PK-15 cells, transfection of the siRNA was achieved using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Scientific, 11,668,500), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. A non-targeting control (NC) siRNA was included as a negative control. The porcine OTUD4 siRNA sequences are F: GGAUACUGAAGAACGGAAA, R: UUUCCGUUCUUCAGUAUCC. The porcine TXNRD2 siRNA sequences are F: CGUCCCAGUGUUACAUAAA, R: UUUAUGUAACACUGGGACG. The porcine ZC3H12A siRNA sequences are F: GGAACUUGAAGACCACAAA, R: UUUGUGGUCUUCAAGUUCC. The porcine FLAD1 siRNA sequences are F: CAUCAAGAGGUACAAUCUA, R: UAGAUUGUACCUCUUGAUG. The porcine HIF1A siRNA sequences are F: GGAUGAUGACUUCCAGUUA, R: UAACUGGAAGUCAUCAUCC.

Western blotting analysis

PK-15 cells and tonsil samples were lysed using 1×protein loading buffer (Solarbio, P1040) and subsequently subjected to high-temperature inactivation. The target proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Sigma-Aldrich, HATF00010). Subsequently, the membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST and incubated overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies at a 1:1000 dilution. Following washes, the membranes were treated with secondary antibodies (1:10000 dilution) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, antibody-antigen complexes were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence detection reagents (Share-bio Biotechnology, SB-WB011).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Intracellular autophagy was examined by transmission electron microscopy [25]. In brief, cells were collected and centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min, and the resulting pellets were processed for TEM. Fixed sequentially with 3% glutaraldehyde (48 h) and 1% osmium tetroxide (1 h), the samples were then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, cleared with propylene oxide, and embedded in araldite resin (polymerized at 60°C for 48 h). Ultrathin sections were cut using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and then imaged on a JEM 1400 plus TEM at 80 kV using a Gatan SC 1000B camera.

mCherry-GFP-FIS1[mt101-152] (mito-QC) assay

Mito-QC serves as a validated reporter system for monitoring terminal mitophagy in cells [82]. This assay employs a tandem mCherry-GFP tag fused to a 51-amino acid mitochondrial targeting sequence derived from the outer mitochondrial membrane protein FIS1[101–152]. The resulting construct, mCherry-GFP-FIS1[101–152], exhibits both red and green fluorescence under normal conditions, producing a yellow signal when merged. Upon mitophagy induction, mitochondria are encapsulated by phagophores and delivered to lysosomes via mitophagosome-lysosome fusion. The acidic environment quenches GFP fluorescence, leading to the formation of mCherry-only puncta that indicate mitolysosomes [51]. In this experiment, cells transfected with mito-QC were washed with PBS at 6 hpt and further incubated for 36 h prior to viral infection. Imaging was performed using an Olympus confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Establishment of knockout cell lines using CRISPR-Cas9 system

The OTUD4, ATG5, SIRT3, and PINK1 knockout (KO) cell lines were established, as described previously [83]. To generate stable KO cell lines, the small guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed using the online CRISPR design tool (http://crispr.mit.edu/) and subsequently cloned into a pLentiCRISPR-puromycin vector. The constructs were transfected into cells by Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Scientific, L3000150). Following transfection, stable KO cells were selected with 3 μg/mL puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich, P9620) and verified by Western blotting, with empty vector-transfected cells serving as controls. Porcine OTUD4 sgRNA sequences are: GTCGTGCCTGTTCCGGGCCGTGG and TTCCGAAGATAGGCGTCCATGGG, porcine ATG5 sgRNA sequences are: AAGATGTGCTTCGAGATGTGTGG, porcine PINK1 sgRNA sequences are: GGCCCAGAGATCATTCCCCAGGG and ATGAGGCCACCATGCCTGTGTGG, and porcine SIRT3 sgRNA sequences are: CTACGACCTCCCCTACCCCGAGG and TCCTTGGCGAAAGTGAAAAACGG.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous data were compared using the Student’s t-test, while multiple group comparisons were conducted by analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*) and a p-value < 0.01 was considered statistically highly significant (**).
