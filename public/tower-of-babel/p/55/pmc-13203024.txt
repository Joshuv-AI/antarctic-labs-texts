Tackling obesity’s impact: the need to address metabolic syndrome in osteoarthritis management
Authors: Samuel Joshua Pragasam Sampath, Vijayalakshmi Venkatesan
Published: 2026

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common chronic degenerative joint disorder affecting millions worldwide. OA is characterized by progressive articular cartilage degradation, subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, bone marrow lesions, synovial inflammation, periarticular muscle weakening, alterations to the joint capsule, ligaments and menisci, affecting the entire joint resulting in chronic pain [1]. In 2020, OA affected ∼595 million people (7.6% of the global population) with a 132.2% increase in incidence since 1990. By 2050, it is projected that the number of OA cases will have increased by 74.9% for knee OA, 78.6% for hip OA, 48.6% for hand OA, and 95.1% for other kinds compared to 2020 [2]. Traditionally regarded as an age-related “wear-and-tear” disorder, emerging epidemiological evidence suggests a significant link between metabolic dysfunction and OA [3].

The intersection of obesity and osteoarthritis

Obesity significantly contributes to OA, influencing disease incidence, progression, and symptom severity through biomechanical and systemic pathways. Excess weight increases joint loading, misalignment, and muscle weakness, exacerbating joint instability and cartilage degradation. Mechanical overloading fuels cellular senescence in chondrocytes, and elicits pro-inflammatory signaling in chondrocytes and osteoblasts, promoting cartilage deterioration and subchondral bone thickening, respectively. Although excessive loading of weight-bearing joints such as the knee and hip elevates OA risk in obesity, the presence of OA in non-weight-bearing joints like the hands and wrists underscores systemic factors beyond localized mechanical stress. Excessive adiposity triggers systemic inflammation via adipose-derived cytokines (TNF- α, IL-1β, IL-6) and adipokines (leptin, adiponectin, visfatin, resistin, chemerin). This drives OA progression by amplifying cartilage matrix degradation, impairing chondrocyte and osteoblast function, and disrupting mesenchymal stem cell-mediated tissue repair [4].

Metabolic syndrome: a stealthy contributor to OA

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a constellation of risk factors including central obesity, insulin resistance (IR)/hyperglycemia, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [5]. A recent meta analysis involving 28 million participants estimated global MetS prevalence at 12.5–31.4% across regions [6]. These abnormalities are increasingly recognized in OA pathogenesis, with nearly two thirds of OA patients affected [7]. This overlap underscores MetS-associated OA as a distinct phenotype characterized by rapid joint degeneration and significant pain [8]. Rising rates driven by obesity, lifestyle changes, and aging populations are expected to impose a substantial global healthcare burden.

Emerging evidence suggests an association between OA and diabetes (hyperglycemia), with metabolic derangements proposed as contributing factors. Chronic hyperglycemia could expedite advanced glycation end products (AGEs) formation, enhancing cartilage stiffness, weakening mechanical integrity, and disrupting chondrocyte and synoviocyte function through elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF- α, IL-1β, IL-6) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) production. Elevated glucose levels are also associated with increased oxidative stress, compromising chondrocyte health. IR has been implicated in disrupting synovial and chondrocyte metabolism, thereby amplifying inflammation [9] and impairing autophagy. Diabetes has been associated with changes in subchondral bone structure and function, which may contribute to MetS‑associated OA phenotype progression [10].

Dyslipidemia, characterized by elevated levels of triglycerides, oxidized low-density lipoproteins (ox-LDL), altered fatty acid profiles, and decreased high-density lipoproteins (HDL) has been associated with MetS‑associated OA phenotype pathophysiology. Hypercholesterolemia is linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammation and apoptosis, that may contribute to cartilage degradation, osteophyte formation and subchondral bone alterations. Elevated cholesterol could activate signaling pathways that up-regulate matrix metalloproteinases and down-regulate collagen synthesis. Higher levels of ox-LDL are associated with inflammation and matrix degradation via receptor-mediated pathways [11]. Dysfunctional HDL-c may further influence MetS‑associated OA phenotype by promoting cartilage fibrillation and reducing proteoglycan content. Increased levels of saturated fatty acids have been implicated in triggering inflammation and chondrocyte apoptosis thereby disrupting joint tissue homeostasis [12]. Hypertension has been associated with altered joint blood flow, which may contribute to MetS‑associated OA phenotype progression through increased tissue degradation.

Addressing metabolic syndrome in OA management

Evidence suggests a bidirectional link between obesity and MetS‑associated OA phenotype, which may contribute to the intensification of disease progression. MetS‑associated OA phenotype can lead to decreased physical activity due to pain and functional limitations, which further contributes to weight gain, creating a vicious cycle. The strong association between obesity, MetS and OA makes it essential to incorporate a more comprehensive management strategy that addresses not only the mechanical stress on the joints but also the systemic metabolic perturbations contributing to MetS‑associated OA phenotype.

Weight loss

Weight reduction could serve as a key strategy in mitigating MetS‑associated OA phenotype progression, given its links to metabolic syndrome (MetS) and systemic inflammation. Clinical studies show that sustained weight loss improves pain, function, and cartilage biomarkers, with greater benefits observed when reductions reach 10–19.9% of baseline body weight [13]. Weight loss has also been associated with decreases in pro‑inflammatory mediators (leptin, IL‑6, CRP) and increases in anti‑inflammatory markers (IL‑10, adiponectin), contributing to symptomatic relief and improved joint function [14].

Metabolic bariatric surgery

Metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) is the most effective intervention for severe obesity, with updated guidelines recommending its use in patients with BMI ≥35 kg/m2 regardless of comorbidities, or ≥30 kg/m2 in the presence of metabolic disease [15]. Beyond weight loss, MBS promotes rapid metabolic and anti-inflammatory changes via gastrointestinal hormone and signaling pathway alterations, often improving type 2 diabetes before substantial weight reduction. Evidence suggests improvements in OA-related pain, stiffness, and physical function, and a reduced likelihood of clinical OA diagnosis, though these outcomes are largely symptomatic given the observational and heterogeneous nature of current studies. Long-term weight regain remains a challenge [16], yet emerging data also indicate benefits for inflammatory comorbidities such as psoriasis [17]. Collectively, MBS represents a valuable therapeutic option within holistic obesity management, offering meaningful metabolic and musculoskeletal benefits while warranting ongoing monitoring and further prospective evaluation of its role in structural disease modification.

Physical exercise

Physical activity is key to OA management, increasing energy expenditure and improving musculoskeletal health. Exercise - particularly aerobic exercise - could alleviate pain, improve joint function, and protect cartilage in animal models of OA. Exercise could improve synovial fluid IL-10 levels, increase cartilage glycosaminoglycan content, and improve overall physical function by increasing balance, muscle strength, and endurance, all of which can favor MetS‑associated OA phenotype management [18]. Moreover, fitness has been linked to improved survival in musculoskeletal diseases [19], while running has been associated with reduced risk of obesity-related hip and knee arthritis [20,21]. Together, these findings emphasis the central role of physical activity in mitigating both musculoskeletal and metabolic disease burden.

Nutritional intervention

Dietary modifications are important in managing both obesity and MetS. A diet rich in anti-inflammatory foods, such as fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and omega-3 fatty acids, may help reduce systemic inflammation and alleviate MetS‑associated OA phenotype symptoms. The Mediterranean diet, in particular, has been associated with improved OA outcomes [22]. Limiting refined sugars and processed foods could benefit both MetS and OA by improving insulin resistance and reducing fat accumulation. Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid supplementation could reduce pain, improve joint function, slow MetS‑associated OA phenotype progression and reduce the risk of all cause mortality in patients with OA, largely through anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, gut microbiota regulatory, and immune-modulating mechanisms [23]. However, while some observational and small interventional studies have reported benefits, the evidence remains inconsistent. Larger, well‑designed randomized trials are needed to confirm these effects and establish their clinical relevance. Despite established links between inflammation, adipokines, and lifestyle factors in MetS‑associated OA phenotype, key gaps remain. Deciphering the combined and sex‑specific effects of diet and physical activity on metabolic health and OA outcomes is a priority. Prospective, multidisciplinary studies are needed to evaluate whether tailored lifestyle strategies can differentially affect disease progression and be sustained long‑term, thereby facilitating evidence to practice.

Pharmacological therapy

Oral and topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), including COX-2 inhibitors, are first-line therapies for reducing pain and improving function in OA. However, their use requires caution in patients with comorbidities, such as increased cardiovascular risk. Intra-articular corticosteroid injections are administered directly in the joints to offer short-term pain relief in hip and knee OA [24]. Metformin has shown promise in preclinical and observational studies for potential disease-modifying effects in OA, but randomized controlled trial evidence remains limited [25]. The effect of statins and anti-hypertensive drugs on OA is unclear and more human studies are needed to validate their benefits in addressing MetS‑associated OA phenotype. The gut microbiome may represent a key link between MetS and MetS‑associated OA phenotype-related pain. Modulation with prebiotics (dietary fiber) and probiotics has shown potential to improve glucose sensitivity and metabolic parameters. Given the association between MetS and OA, targeting metabolic dysfunction may help alleviate MetS‑associated OA phenotype symptoms, though further research is needed.

Conclusion

A holistic approach encompassing dietary changes, physical activity and stress management could yield significant relief from pain and stiffness, improve physical function, improve body composition, inflammatory markers and metabolic profile. Real‑world implementation requires a practical, stepwise, and multidisciplinary strategy, beginning with structured lifestyle interventions such as tailored weight loss, low‑impact exercise, and culturally appropriate dietary guidance. Pharmacotherapy should be used adjunctively based on patient risk and response. Embedding these strategies into primary care, supported by patient education, regular follow‑up, and simple behavior‑change tools - including digital platforms - can enhance adherence and sustain long‑term outcomes.
