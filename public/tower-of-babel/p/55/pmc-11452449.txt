Comment on “Daytime versus nighttime appendectomy in term of complications and clinical outcomes: a meta-analysis”
Authors: Amit Pandita, Muhammed Shabil, Sanjit Sah
Published: 2024

We read with great interest the study by Shah et al. [1], titled “Daytime versus nighttime appendectomy in terms of complications and clinical outcomes: a meta-analysis,” which provides valuable insights into the safety of nighttime appendectomies. The authors’ finding that there is no significant increase in postoperative mortality or complication rates for nighttime versus daytime surgeries is noteworthy, and we commend them for undertaking this comprehensive analysis. However, we have some methodological concerns and recommendations that we believe could enhance the robustness of the conclusions.

Firstly, the authors combined randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-randomized studies in their meta-analysis. While pooling data can increase sample size and power, it is not methodologically appropriate to combine RCTs and observational studies, especially in intervention-based research. RCTs are generally considered to provide a higher level of evidence compared to observational studies due to their ability to minimize bias and confounding. Although the authors conducted a Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) assessment to evaluate the certainty of the evidence, combining RCTs and non-randomized studies risks lowering the overall quality of the evidence, as non-randomized studies are inherently considered to provide lower levels of evidence [2]. A separate analysis or stratification of the two study types might have provided a clearer interpretation of the results.

Secondly, while the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2) tool and the ROBINS-I tool were appropriately used to assess bias in RCTs and observational studies, respectively, a sensitivity analysis based on the risk of bias was notably absent. Incorporating a sensitivity analysis that excludes studies at high risk of bias would offer a more nuanced understanding of the impact of study quality on the outcomes. This would also help strengthen the validity of the meta-analysis findings.

Furthermore, although publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test and a funnel plot, we recommend that the authors consider employing additional, more modern methods for detecting publication bias, such as the DOI plot and the Luis Furuya-Kanamori (LFK) index [3]. These tools can provide a more detailed understanding of asymmetry in the data and can complement traditional methods, offering greater confidence in the assessment of publication bias.

This study contributes important findings to the literature on appendectomy timing and outcomes. Addressing these methodological concerns would further enhance the reliability and applicability of the results. We thank the authors for their significant efforts and encourage further refinement in future meta-analyses on this topic.
