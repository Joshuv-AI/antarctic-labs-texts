Temporal change in skeletal muscle index as a predictor of recurrence for patients with locally advanced colorectal malignancy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Noah B. Manz, Jordan Reed, Christopher D. Kanner, Jeremy A. Dressler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality with stage III disease carrying a substantial risk of recurrence despite curative resection. Accurate risk stratification is essential to optimize surveillance and guide adjuvant therapy. Traditional risk models rely heavily on pathologic features, but recent studies suggest that body composition metrics, particularly imaging-based assessments of skeletal muscle mass, may offer additional prognostic value. The skeletal muscle index (SMI), derived from routine CT imaging, has emerged as a promising surrogate marker of frailty. However, the relationship between temporal changes in SMI and cancer recurrence remains poorly understood.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed using single-institution data from over 500 patients who underwent resection for stage III colorectal malignancy. SMI at diagnosis (SMIdx) was measured using staging CT imaging and follow-up measurements (SMIfu) were obtained at the most recent scan prior to recurrence to capture changes preceding radiologic evidence of disease. Patients were paired using propensity score matching and temporal changes in SMI were evaluated using conditional logistic regressions. Receiver operating characteristics and decision curve analysis were performed to determine whether change in SMI can stratify patients into meaningful risk groups to guide surveillance.ResultsA decrease in SMI was independently associated with increased risk of disease recurrence with an odds ratio of 2.23 per 10% decline (95% CI: 1.15–4.35, p = 0.018). Baseline muscle status was not associated with recurrence (OR 1.01 per cm2/m2, 95% CI: 0.97–1.05, p = 0.585). The optimal risk stratification threshold was θ = 2.11%, (θ = SMIfu / SMIdx − 1) with a sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 60%. Decision curve analysis showed net clinical benefit over a wide range of thresholds, θ = ±11%. These results were reproduced in an internally validated cohort.ConclusionsPostoperative decline in skeletal muscle index is a significant, independent predictor of colorectal cancer recurrence. Clinically relevant risk stratification thresholds have been proposed and support the conclusion that longitudinal monitoring of SMI may have value to escalate surveillance intensity rather than being a passive prognostic marker. Future studies should focus on validating these thresholds in large, multi-center cohorts.

Background

Colorectal Cancer (CRC) remains one of the most common malignancies worldwide with a substantial risk of recurrence after oncologic resection. Current 5-year recurrence rates for stage III disease are ~33% with a median recurrence time of 15.9 months [1, 2]. Detection of recurrence has significant prognostic implications, as patients with isolated or oligometastatic disease may be eligible for curative interventions associated with improved long-term survival [3]. As such, identifying which patients are at high risk of recurrence is critical for optimizing postoperative surveillance strategies. Traditional risk stratification models focus on pathologic features including tumor stage, margin status and lymph node involvement [4–6], but previous studies have demonstrated a correlation between frailty and postoperative outcomes for patients who require oncologic resection [7–15]. In these studies, researchers employ various methods to assess patient frailty, however, no single clinical frailty score or surrogate marker has yet demonstrated clear superiority- an important limitation highlighted by recent meta-analysis [16]. Existing tools such as the Fried Frailty Phenotype and the Rockwood Clinical Frailty Scale rely on subjective evaluations which are often highly variable and susceptible to individual bias and error [17, 18]. Therefore, imaging-based myopenia assessment is increasingly seen as a viable solution to integrate frailty measurement into oncologic workflows while avoiding some of the limitations of clinical assessments. In this approach, a measurement of skeletal muscle area (SMA) can be taken at the mid-level of the third lumbar vertebral body on axial computed tomography (CT) scans due to its strong correlation with whole-body muscle mass [19]. The skeletal muscle index (SMI) heuristic can then be generated by dividing the SMA by the square of the patient’s height, and the progression of these SMI values over time can be tracked for postoperative surveillance. While sex-specific thresholds for SMI have been proposed in literature to define myopenia, optimal cut-off values may vary due to cancer type, patient population and other factors [20]. Furthermore, the body of longitudinal studies comparing temporal changes in SMI to long-term oncologic outcomes remains quite limited compared to the large number of cross sectional papers correlating outcomes to a single SMI datum [16–22]. As such, additional research is needed to validate a longitudinal approach to SMI surveillance and establish clinically meaningful cutoffs to guide the intensity of postoperative monitoring and adjuvant treatment. It is hypothesized that long-term decreases in SMI will be positively correlated with disease recurrence. Therefore, our study sought to better understand the time-dependent relationship between changes in SMI and postoperative recurrence risk in patients with locally advanced colorectal malignancy.

Methods

We performed a single-institution (University of Vermont Medical Center (UVMMC)) retrospective analysis that included patients aged 18 years or older who underwent oncologic resection for locally advanced stage III colorectal malignancy between years 2000 and 2020. This study was approved by the University of Vermont Institutional Review Board (IRB #00002150) and was conducted in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. Researchers utilized the institution’s digital imaging PACS software (Visage 7.1.19a) to measure psoas, paraspinal and abdominal muscle cross sectional area for patients at the time of their initial staging. Muscle regions were isolated by applying and manually refining a [−29, 150] Hounsfield Unit filter at the mid-vertebral body of the third lumbar vertebrae on axial CT scan (reference Fig. 1) [23–26]. Skeletal muscle indices were subsequently calculated as the quotient of this area and the square of the patient’s height [20]. A comprehensive electronic medical record search was then performed to obtain demographic information, types of chemotherapy and radiation treatment obtained, post-operative clinical outcomes, comorbid conditions and potential standard confounding variables. The cohort was then split according to cancer recurrence status between 6 months and 5 years following the initial diagnosis. This interval was selected to distinguish true disease relapse from early treatment failure and to align with the standard five-year clinical benchmark for long-term survival [2]. For patients who developed recurrence, a follow-up SMI measurement was obtained at the most recent follow-up scan prior to recurrence to capture changes preceding radiologic evidence of disease. If no intermediate scan was available, the scan at the time of recurrence was used. For patients who did not develop recurrence, a comprehensive list of their CT study dates was collected, and the Python Propensity Score Matching (PSM) library (version 0.3.13) was used to pair patients with and without recurrence [27]. The list of available CT study dates for patients in the non-recurrence category allowed for the duration between the initial and follow-up SMI measurements to be matched as one of the covariates. Patients were also matched based on treatment sequence including neoadjuvant treatment, adjuvant treatment and steroid exposure.1 With both initial and follow-up SMI measurements for patients in the recurrence and non-recurrence categories, a temporal change in SMI was calculated. The primary outcome measure of the study was these differences in SMI which were evaluated between paired patients in a conditional logistic regression (CLR) using the Python Statsmodels library (version 0.14.4). Differences were analyzed in two different ways: log-transformed change and linear change. Log transformation was used to account for proportional differences in muscle loss relative to baseline body composition, whereas linear change quantified raw differences in SMI but placed more weight on baseline muscle quantity. Results

A total of 519 patients were identified in the UVMMC database as undergoing surgical resection for colorectal malignancy between 2000 and 2020. Demographics from this source population are presented in Table 1. Chart review identified 292 patients with AJCC stage III colorectal cancer. For each patient, the highest recorded stage (from either clinical or pathologic assessment) was used. This approach captures both patients who underwent upfront surgery and those initially staged clinically, including cases where the stage may have been downgraded following neoadjuvant therapy. To control for factors that could independently influence recurrence or imaging frequency, we a priori excluded patients with concurrent malignancies, emergent presentation, extended hospital stays and significant comorbidity burden defined as having three or more concurrent conditions each with a Charlson Comorbidity Index weight of 1 (e.g., CHF, PAD, COPD, prior MI). Patients who did not undergo surgical resection were also excluded as surgery is central to curative treatment. After exclusions, 281 patients remained: 57 experienced recurrence between 6 months and 5 years following resection (median = 1.5 years, IQR 0.9–2.5 years) and 207 did not. An inclusion/exclusion criteria flowchart is shown in Fig. 2. For patients who did not experience recurrence, all archived CT study dates were obtained with a median of 6 plausible scan dates per patient (IQR 4–9 scans). The median duration between consecutive scans was 12 months (IQR 9–14 months). This distribution mirrors standard surveillance recommendations for colorectal cancer follow-up and suggests that most scans were performed routinely rather than prompted by clinical suspicion. Propensity score matching yielded 40 discordant pairs of patients using the standard caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations. Demographics from the matched cohort population are presented in Table 1. Patients were matched on the following covariates: duration between initial and follow-up CT scans, steroid exposure, chemotherapy type and radiation type. Steroid exposure was classified as intermittent, chronic (≥3 weeks continuous exposure) or withheld. Chemotherapy and radiation were each classified as neoadjuvant, adjuvant, both or withheld. Rather than matching on pathologic features of the disease (e.g. histologic subtype), we prioritized matching by treatment exposure which differs substantially between colon and rectal cancer and represents a more potent potential confounder for myopenia than the anatomic location and histology of the tumor. Scan durations were matched to the nearest month within a 6 month to 4 year window while recurrence status remained between 6 months and 5 years. The slightly narrower window for scan durations was utilized to ensure that the timing of imaging remained consistent across matched pairs. This adjustment allowed for more precise temporal comparisons of SMI without shortening the overall 5 year period used to define cancer recurrence. Standardized mean differences (SMD) <0.15 were observed for all covariates which is consistent with commonly accepted thresholds for satisfactory balance [29]. A sensitivity analysis evaluated the impact of varying caliper widths on the matching process with minimal observed variation in match rates. At a caliper width of 0.5, only 44 patients were matched, and a caliper of 0.2 was determined to be the narrowest possible width while maintaining adequate sample size. Formal power analysis was not conducted as the use of propensity score matching, by design, determines the final sample size based on covariate overlap and match quality. The reductions in the SMD of the covariates pre and post matching are outlined in Table 2. In addition to the covariates directly controlled for in Table 2, classifying patients as malnourished or non-malnourished at baseline could clarify whether recurrence occurs predominantly in already malnourished individuals, or whether even patients with normal baseline muscle mass who later experience a decline are at increased risk. Clinical malnutrition status, defined according to the GLIM framework, was calculated for each patient after matching [28]. The post-match standardized mean difference was 0.099 indicating acceptable balance between groups. The CT imaging for matched recurrence and non-recurrence patients was then evaluated and SMI data was collected and made publicly available in a Mendeley Data repository [30]. To assess measurement reliability, 20 SMI measurements were repeated and precision was found to be within 3%. The change in SMI for each patient was calculated as both a log-transformed difference, logΔ = log(SMIfu/SMIdx), and linear difference, Δ = SMIfu - SMIdx where SMIfu is the measurement at follow-up and SMIdx is the baseline measurement at the time of staging. The individual distributions of linear difference in SMI as a function of recurrence status are plotted in Fig. 3b with lines connecting the paired observations. Four regression analyses were performed on the matched pairs using different combinations of logΔ, Δ and baseline SMIdx as a sensitivity analysis. The results of these regressions are shown in Table 5. Regression #1 served as the primary analysis to test the null hypothesis and considered only the log-transformed difference showing an OR of 2.23 per 10% loss in SMI (CI: 1.15–4.35, p = 0.018) which is consistent with the findings from established literature [7–15]. To determine whether recurrence status is associated with baseline SMIdx in addition to logΔ, regression #2 was calculated which includes SMIdx as a predictor. The association between logΔ remained similar to regression #1 with an OR of 2.20 per 10% loss in SMI (CI: 1.13–4.30, p = 0.021). The association between SMIdx was insignificant with an OR of 1.01 per 1 cm2/m2 loss and equivocal confidence interval (CI: 0.97–1.05, p = 0.585). A similar pattern was observed in regression #3 which analyzed the influence of baseline SMIdx in the context of the linear difference in SMI (Δ) showing an OR of 1.16 per 1 cm2/m2 loss (CI: 1.02–1.31, p = 0.024). While the conditional logistic regressions quantified the association between SMI change and recurrence within matched pairs, clinical utility requires the estimation of absolute risk at the population level. Therefore, a marginal logistic regression (MLR) was calculated to estimate absolute recurrence probabilities. The MLR (Table 5 regression #4) demonstrated an OR of 2.05 per 10% loss in SMI (CI: 1.16–3.62, p = 0.013), consistent with the findings from regressions #1 and #2. Predicted probabilities from the marginal model were used to generate a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) with an area under the curve of 0.689 (CI: 0.585–0.791) as shown in Fig. 4a. Youden’s J statistic was 0.433 corresponding to a sensitivity of 85% and specificity of 53% at an SMI decline threshold of θ = 0.03%, (θ = SMIfu/SMIdx − 1). Decision curve analysis (DCA) was then performed as shown in Fig. 4b and revealed superior net clinical benefit of SMI surveillance between a wide range of threshold probabilities from 0.34–0.65, corresponding to SMI decline thresholds of θ = ±11%. To estimate confidence intervals for the ROC and DCA, the model was internally validated using Harrell’s heuristic optimism approach combining the matched data with the unmatched data from the propensity score analysis. Discussion

The results of the conditional logistic models show that temporal declines in SMI are associated with increased risk of colorectal cancer recurrence beyond static baseline assessments. However, to have utility as a screening tool to guide surveillance, thresholds must be established to stratify patients into clinically meaningful risk groups. Receiver operating characteristics derived from the marginal logistic model show discrimination greater than chance with an AUC = 0.689. Youden’s J statistic yielded a sensitivity of 85% and specificity of 53% at a threshold θ = 0.03% suggesting that even very small declines in SMI result in a true positive rate near 90%, consistent with typical oncologic screening benchmarks [32, 33]. An exact true sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 60% was obtained at θ = 2.11%. While the ROC curve provides a global measure of discriminative ability, it fails to account for the relative clinical consequences of false positives versus false negatives. Thus, the decision curve analysis was employed to weigh these trade-offs and quantify the net clinical benefit across a range of classification thresholds. Importantly, this analysis supports a range of thresholds θ = ±11% where SMI surveillance offers a clear net benefit as a clinical screening tool. Within this range the model reduces the number of false positives without a prohibitive loss in true positives where intervention with additional screening may lead to improved patient outcomes. However, outside these thresholds, the results suggest that SMI surveillance may either over-treat or under-treat patients. Given the consequences of a false negative in cancer screenings, a threshold of θ = 2.11% with a sensitivity of 90% is suggested as a cutoff for classifying patients as high or low risk. The clinical utility of the model is best illustrated by its ability to significantly alter post-test probability with a positive likelihood ratio (LR+) of 2.25 and negative likelihood ratio (LR-) of 0.17. At a threshold θ = 2.11%, the negative predictive value is 92% and positive predictive value is 53% using a pre-test probability of 33% for recurrence of stage III disease [1]. The primary clinical implications of the proposed thresholds as a screening tool are to guide optimization of surveillance intensity, particularly through shorter imaging and endoscopic intervals where definitive evidence of recurrence is most reliably established. SMI may also be valuable in situations where routine surveillance imaging yields indeterminate findings to justify repeat imaging or percutaneous biopsy. High-specificity biomarkers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) remain important adjuncts, however, prior meta-analysis has demonstrated that CEA has insufficient sensitivity for detecting recurrence in isolation (sensitivity = 84%, specificity = 84% at the optimal proposed threshold of 2.2 ng/mL) [34]. The data from this work support that SMI change may have comparable sensitivity to CEA and precede radiologic evidence of disease recurrence. Therefore, concordant positive risk classification by CEA and SMI would substantially strengthen suspicion for recurrence and justify shortening imaging and endoscopic intervals.3 Even in the absence of abnormal CEA or ctDNA, isolated declines in SMI may warrant intensifying routine surveillance as net clinical benefit was observed across a broad range of thresholds (θ = ±11%). As AI-based image segmentation becomes increasingly integrated into radiology workflows, these measurements of SMI may soon be performed automatically at the time of routine imaging interpretation. Thus, SMI-guided risk stratification may primarily serve as resource-neutral way to personalize imaging intervals and intensify surveillance when recurrence risk rises and potentially maintain standard intervals when risk remains low.

These results show that baseline SMI alone did not meaningfully influence recurrence odds, however the temporal loss of muscle mass also serves as a critical biomarker of declining physiologic reserve. Extrapolating from established prehabilitation literature, patients with greater physiologic reserve can better tolerate disease burden and treatment, so those identified as high-risk by SMI surveillance should be prioritized for intensive physical therapy and nutritional optimization as physiologic fortification [35–37].

While every effort was taken to ensure methodological rigor and minimize bias, certain limitations of the current work are present. First, the cohort from the propensity score matching was relatively small (n = 40 pairs) limiting the overall study power. This was a natural consequence of the strict inclusion/exclusion criteria and threshold of standardized mean difference that covariates were controlled to. Additionally, many reasonable and potentially important covariates could not be controlled for due to the limited initial dataset from this single-institution study. Increasing the number of covariates can reduce the quality of the propensity score match across all variables, whereas limiting the analysis to a few well-chosen covariates is often necessary for high-quality matching (SMD < 0.15). This limitation is also exemplified by the scan duration. While recurrence status was initially examined between 6 months and 5 years (reference Fig. 2), results were only reported for the 6 month to 4 year interval where the propensity score match provided sufficient data to support reliable conclusions.

Secondly, only SMI was evaluated given its established utility and ubiquity in prior studies [7–15]. However, the lack of assessment of visceral and subcutaneous fat limits the completeness of the anthropometric evaluation. Additionally, muscle density (which reflects muscle quality and fatty infiltration), has been shown to be an independent prognostic factor in colorectal cancer [38]. Extending the longitudinal methodology of this study to skeletal muscle density in addition to SMI would greatly expand the understanding of the influence of myopenia in disease recurrence.

Finally, as data was collected from a single institution, a potential selection bias was introduced. Scanner models and acquisition protocols could not be completely controlled for, and while the distribution of scan dates closely mirrors standard surveillance recommendations, it is not clear which scans were performed routinely rather than prompted by clinical suspicion. Future studies should focus on validating these SMI thresholds using true external validation in large, multi-center cohorts.

Conclusions

Assessing frailty is a crucial component to providing optimum multidisciplinary care for patients with colorectal cancer who require surgical resection. Imaging-based myopenia assessment is increasingly seen as a viable solution to integrate frailty measurement into oncologic workflows, though standardized surveillance protocols have not been established. While previous literature has demonstrated a correlation between frailty and recurrence status [7–15], the body of longitudinal studies comparing temporal changes in SMI to long-term oncologic outcomes remains quite limited [21, 22]. Our results compile data from a single-institution study of over 500 patients with colorectal malignancy over a 20 year period to estimate SMI decline thresholds for surveillance risk stratification. They support an increased risk of recurrence with decreasing SMI (OR of 2.23 per 10% loss in SMI (CI: 1.15–4.35, p = 0.018)) which is consistent with the findings from established literature [7–15]. Based on the results from ROC and decision analysis curves, the ideal SMI cutoff value is θ = 2.11%, (θ = SMIfu/SMIdx − 1) with a range of net clinical benefit of θ = ±11%. At this threshold the model supports sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 60% which is applicable to stage III patients 6 months to 4 years following surgical resection. These findings support the conclusion that longitudinal monitoring of SMI may have value to directly escalate surveillance intensity rather than serving solely as a passive prognostic marker.
