Free testosterone independently predicts metabolic syndrome severity in U.S. adolescent girls aged 12–19 years
Authors: Christopher R McCartney, Aaron F Pannone, Su Hee Kim, Matthew J Gurka, Mark D DeBoer, Christine M Burt Solorzano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextHyperandrogenemia is associated with increased risks for metabolic syndrome in asymptomatic women and women with polycystic ovary syndrome. The relationship between hyperandrogenemia and metabolic syndrome in adolescent girls remains poorly defined.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to assess whether free testosterone concentrations predict metabolic syndrome severity (MSS) in adolescent girls.Design/settingUsing data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2013-2016), we performed weighted regression analysis to evaluate whether free testosterone predicts MSS z-score after adjusting for body fat percentage, chronological age, and gynecological age. Similar analyses were performed using either total testosterone or sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) as the primary predictor variable.Participants287 girls aged 12–19 years.Main outcome measureMSS z-score.ResultsIn simple regression analyses, free testosterone and percent body fat predicted MSS z-score (R2 = 0.11 and 0.35, respectively; P < .0001 for both), but chronological age and gynecological age did not. In the multivariable regression model (R2 = 0.41), higher free testosterone and percent body fat were independently associated with higher MSS z-score (P = .0081 and < .0001, respectively), with no significant independent associations with chronological age or gynecological age. While SHBG was an independent predictor of MSS z-score (P = .0104) in a multivariable regression model, total testosterone was not (P = .77).ConclusionHigher free testosterone predicts greater MSS in a nationally representative sample of adolescent girls aged 12–19 years. This relationship at least partly reflects the relationship between MSS z-score and SHBG.

The metabolic syndrome represents a clustering of metabolism-related findings that predicts the development of diabetes and cardiovascular disease [1-3]. Metabolic syndrome affects 2% to 8% of U.S. adolescent girls [4] and is associated with adolescent obesity. Obesity has also been associated with increased androgen concentrations in adolescent girls [5-8]. However, the degree to which hyperandrogenemia may play a causal role in adolescent metabolic syndrome remains poorly defined.

Hyperandrogenemia has been linked with an increased likelihood of metabolic syndrome in adult women [9-16]. For example, in a recent study of eumenorrheic women without clinical hyperandrogenism, biochemical hyperandrogenemia was associated with a 2.9-fold (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.2-6.9) increased odds of metabolic syndrome, independent of body mass index (BMI) [14]. Similarly, androgen excess has been associated with metabolic syndrome among adolescent girls, especially those with obesity and/or polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) [17-23]. However, to our knowledge, no published studies have assessed the potential relationship between free testosterone and metabolic syndrome in a population-based sample of U.S. adolescent girls. We therefore analyzed data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to test the hypothesis that free testosterone is an independent predictor of metabolic syndrome severity (MSS) in adolescent girls aged 12–19 years.

Subjects and methods

The current cross-sectional analysis included female children and adolescents who participated in NHANES. NHANES is a national cross-sectional health survey in the US, directed by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), which is the health statistics unit of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). NHANES surveys—which involve standardized questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory assessments—are initiated via randomly allocated survey invitations guided by US Census-determined demographics [24]. This sampling permits investigators to use statistical survey weighting and stratification procedures to derive unbiased and nationally representative sample estimates. The NHANES study was approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of NCHS, and all participants provided informed consent and/or assent.

The primary predictor variable of interest was free testosterone, the calculation of which requires total testosterone and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) data [25]. This requirement limited data availability to the 2013 to 2016 NHANES databases. In addition, the primary outcome of interest was MSS z-score, the calculation of which requires BMI z-score, systolic blood pressure (SBP), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), fasting triglycerides, and fasting glucose data. Since triglycerides and glucose were not measured in NHANES participants younger than age 12 years, our analysis was restricted to girls aged 12–19 years inclusive.

We excluded from analysis any girl who reported ever having used oral contraceptive pills; those with a history of diabetes; and those who were taking medications that could plausibly impact components of metabolic syndrome (eg, metformin, insulin, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, glucocorticoids, antipsychotics) or antiandrogens (eg, spironolactone). We also excluded girls with a BMI-for-age percentile < 5% (ie, underweight).

Of note, NHANES questionnaires do not ask about pregnancy status for girls younger than 20 years old; and while a urine pregnancy test is obtained in menstruating girls aged 8–11 years, and in all girls aged 12 and older, pregnancy data from girls younger than 20 years are not disclosed in the publicly-available NHANES database. In addition, pubertal (Tanner) stage and bone age data were not available in the 2013-2016 NHANES datasets.

Hormone and metabolic measurements

Relevant laboratory methods used for NHANES 2013-2016 are reported on CDC webpages [26, 27]. Total testosterone was assessed via isotope dilution high performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (ID-LC-MS/MS) (limit of detection [LOD] 0.36-0.75 ng/dL; within-day coefficient of variation [CV] 2.3-2.8%; among-day CV 2.8-3.5%) [28, 29], and SHBG was assessed via chemiluminescence immunoassay (LOD 0.8 nmol/L; within-day CV ≤ 2.5%; among-day CV ≤ 4.3%) (Roche Cat# 03052001160, RRID:AB_2891165) [30, 31]. Free testosterone was calculated from total testosterone and SHBG, assuming a uniform albumin concentration of 4.3 g/dL [25]. Estradiol was measured using ID-LC-MS/MS (LOD 3.0 pg/mL; within-day CV 3.1-4.2%; among-day CV 3.2-4.6%) [28, 29]. Insulin was assessed using an immunoenzymometric assay (LOD 1.0 µU/mL; within-day CV 2.8-6.1%) (Tosoh Bioscience Cat# ST AIA-PACK IRI, RRID:AB_2915954) [32, 33]. Fasting triglycerides, fasting glucose, and HDL-C were assessed using standard methods as reported by the CDC [26, 27].

Data analysis

Metabolic syndrome severity z-score [34] was determined for each subject using the following parameters available in the 2013 to 2016 NHANES databases: age (years), sex (female), BMI (kg/m2), systolic blood pressure (mmHg), HDL-C (mg/dL), fasting triglycerides (mg/dL), and fasting glucose (mg/dL). The research team made an a priori decision to calculate MSS z-scores in a race/ethnicity-agnostic way, using the equation provided in Supplemental Materials [35]. This MSS z-score equation—a modification of the formula our group previously validated in children and adolescents (see below)—produces MSS z-scores that are sex-specific but not race/ethnicity-specific. Briefly, we used confirmatory factor analysis that evaluates for the latent metabolic syndrome factor driving correlations between the five components of metabolic syndrome listed above. This approach then assigns differential loading factors to each of these metabolic syndrome components separately for males and females. The loading factors are then used to produce equations that provide standardized z-scores (population mean 0, standard deviation 1) of metabolic syndrome severity. Metabolic syndrome severity z-scores were previously validated in children by demonstrating that (1) they were highly correlated with insulin, adiponectin, and surrogate risk factors such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and uric acid [2, 36], and (2) they were associated with long-term risk of both type 2 diabetes [3] and CVD [37]. Moreover, among adults, MSS z-scores tracked response to lifestyle treatment of prediabetes in a meaningful way [38].

To support sensitivity analyses described below, the race- and ethnicity-specific MSS z-scores [36] were also calculated (see Supplemental Materials [35]). Possible race/ethnicity inputs include Hispanic, Non-Hispanic Black, and Non-Hispanic White. For those self-identifying as Mexican American and Other Hispanic, MSS z-score was calculated using Hispanic for the race/ethnicity input. For those self-identifying as Non-Hispanic Asian and Other Race Including Multi-Racial, MSS z-score was calculated using Non-Hispanic White given that underlying data robustness is highest for the Non-Hispanic White group [38].

Gynecological age was calculated as chronological age minus age at menarche. For the 20 premenarcheal subjects, gynecological age was assigned a value of −1 to distinguish these subjects from those who were postmenarcheal by less than 1 year (gynecological age = 0).

Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as fasting insulin (µU/mL)×fasting glucose (mg/dL)/405.

Statistical analyses

In this cross-sectional study, we analyzed data from a nationally representative sample of 287 girls aged 12-19 years using NHANES data (2013-2016). Analysis was restricted to NHANES participants having all data required for our analyses, so data imputation was unnecessary. The statistical package SAS (version 9.4, Cary, NC) was used for statistical analyses, and NHANES estimation and weighting procedures were followed [39]. A P < .05 decision rule was applied for statistical significance. All statistical reports are included in Supplemental Materials [35].

Simple survey-weighted regression was performed to explore a number of relationships (eg, between free testosterone and MSS z-score). However, as the primary analysis, we used survey-weighted multiple regression to evaluate whether free testosterone predicted MSS z-score after adjusting for total percent body fat, chronological age, and gynecological age. A measure of adiposity (percent body fat) was included as an adjustment factor because adiposity is compellingly related to metabolic syndrome and its individual components; we did not use BMI z-score for this purpose because it is a component of the MSS z-score calculation. Since pubertal (Tanner) stage and bone age data were not available, we used chronological age and gynecological age as surrogates for maturational stage.

We performed two sensitivity analyses. First, we repeated the primary multiple regression analysis using race/ethnicity-specific MSS z-scores instead of race/ethnicity-agnostic MSS z-scores. Second, we repeated the primary multiple regression analysis using serum estradiol concentration, instead of gynecological age, as a marker of pubertal maturation.

We performed a number of secondary analyses. To explore the relative predictive strengths of testosterone production vs testosterone bioavailability, we repeated the primary multiple regression analysis using either total testosterone or SHBG as the primary predictor variable instead of free testosterone. To assess the potential explanatory role of family history of diabetes, we repeated the primary multiple regression analysis while also including reported family history of diabetes as an adjustment variable. In particular, participants were assumed to have a family history of diabetes if they both (a) answered “yes” to the question “[Do you/Does SP] feel [you/he/she] could be at risk for diabetes or prediabetes,” and (b) subsequently responded “Family history” to the follow-up question “Why [Do you/Does SP] think [you are/he is/she is] at risk for diabetes or prediabetes?” To assess the potential impact of free testosterone on individual components of the MSS z-score, we repeated the primary multiple regression analysis four times using different non-BMI components of the MSS z-score—systolic blood pressure, HDL-C, fasting triglycerides, and fasting glucose—as the dependent variable. Finally, to explore the relationship between androgens and insulin resistance, we repeated the primary multiple regression analysis using HOMA-IR as the dependent variable.

Sensitivity analyses

Mean (95% confidence interval [CI]) MSS z-score was −0.25 (−0.37 to −0.12) when using our primary MSS z-score calculation (ie, not race/ethnicity-specific) and −0.21 (−0.33 to −0.10) when using race/ethnicity-specific MSS z-scores. MSS z-scores derived from these two approaches were highly correlated. Simple regression analysis of MSS z-score with the alternative MSS z-score provided a weighted regression coefficient of 0.947 (SE 0.035) and an R2 of 0.8622 (P < .001). By simple regression analysis, higher free testosterone was associated with higher race-/ethnicity-specific MSS z-score (R2 0.1631, P < .0001). Free testosterone remained an independent predictor of race-/ethnicity-specific MSS z-score (P = .0002) in a multiple regression model that included total body fat, chronological age, and gynecological age as adjustment factors (model R2 0.5381, P < .0001).

When using serum estradiol concentration as a marker of pubertal maturation (instead of gynecological age), free testosterone remained an independent predictor of MSS z-score (P = .0094) in a multiple regression model that also included total body fat and chronological age (model R2 0.3993, model P < .0001). In this analysis, estradiol was not an independent predictor of MSS z-score, but body fat percentage and chronological age were (P < .0001 and .0045, respectively).

Discussion

This analysis of 2013 to 2016 NHANES databases supports the hypothesis that hyperandrogenemia independently predicts metabolic syndrome severity in adolescent girls aged 12–19 years. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first study to address the relationship between free testosterone and metabolic syndrome in a population-based sample of adolescent girls in the U.S.

Current results indicate that higher calculated free testosterone predicts greater metabolic syndrome severity in adolescent girls, and this relationship appears to be independent of percent body fat, chronological age, and gynecological age, with the latter two variables serving as surrogates for pubertal maturation. (We did not use BMI or BMI z-score as an adjustment factor given that BMI z-score was part of the MSS severity calculation.) We note that results were similar with the use of race-/ethnicity-specific (instead of race-/ethnicity-agnostic) MSS severity z-scores, and results were similar with the use of estradiol (instead of gynecological age) as a marker of pubertal maturation. Moreover, the addition of reported family history of diabetes as an adjustment factor did not materially change results. While simple regression models suggested that free testosterone is positively associated with SBP, fasting triglycerides, and HOMA-IR, and negatively associated with HDL-C, only SBP demonstrated a statistically significant relationship to free testosterone after simultaneously adjusting for percent body fat, chronological age, and gynecological age.

Of interest, although SHBG was a significant predictor of metabolic syndrome severity in both simple and multiple regression models, total testosterone was not. These findings are consistent with some prior adolescent studies [17, 19, 20, 23] and suggest that metabolic syndrome severity may relate to increased androgen bioavailability in particular.

A number of prior studies in adolescent girls have assessed the relationship between hyperandrogenemia and metabolic syndrome, especially in adolescent girls with obesity and/or PCOS [17-22]. For example, in a 2006 study by Coviello and colleagues [17], 49 adolescent girls with PCOS (ages 14-19 years; all self-identified as Non-Hispanic White; 18% with overweight and 55% with obesity) were compared to controls participating in NHANES III. In this study, the odds of having metabolic syndrome was higher among girls with PCOS compared to age-matched controls after adjusting for BMI (odds ratio 4.5 [95% CI, 1.1-17.7]) [17]. In addition, among girls with PCOS, those with metabolic syndrome had 74% higher bioavailable testosterone and 57% lower SHBG, but no significant difference in total testosterone, compared to those without metabolic syndrome; and each incremental quartile of bioavailable testosterone was associated with 3.8-fold higher odds of metabolic syndrome (OR 3.8 [95% CI, 1.4-10.2]) after adjusting for both BMI and HOMA-IR [17]. As another example, a study by de Sousa and colleagues [21], performed in a group of 160 adolescent girls with obesity presenting to a pediatric obesity clinic, suggested that total and calculated free testosterone were 20% to 23% higher, and SHBG was 50% lower, among those with metabolic syndrome compared to those without, despite no significant differences in BMI, BMI z-score, or age [21]. In addition, higher total and calculated free testosterone were associated with higher systolic BP, diastolic BP, triglycerides, and 2-hour glucose levels (oral glucose tolerance testing), independent of age, BMI, and insulin resistance as assessed by homeostasis model assessment [21]. Importantly, most of these studies in adolescent girls used a testosterone immunoassay (limited accuracy) [17-21, 23], employed clinic-based recruitment strategies (potential for recruitment/selection bias) [17, 18, 20-22], and/or focused on girls with obesity and/or PCOS [17, 18, 20-23].

Metabolic syndrome status was originally determined using consensus criteria, with an individual being classified as having metabolic syndrome if she has abnormalities in at least 3 of the 5 components (obesity, high blood pressure, high triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol, and elevated fasting glucose). However, this approach does not account for the spectrum of severity of metabolic syndrome and that higher degrees of cardiometabolic disarray could contribute more strongly to long-term consequences. We previously used confirmatory factor analysis to develop a metabolic syndrome severity score that is weighted by the degree to which the individual components inter-correlate. This score is a z-score, providing a continuous measure of the severity of metabolic syndrome that is standardized to the US adult population. The MSS z-score takes into account how the individual components of metabolic syndrome contribute to the latent metabolic syndrome factor. This acknowledges the interaction of these components in a way that may reflect the underlying processes that drive these abnormalities. For example, among adults, the MSS score is correlated with CVD and diabetes outcomes, even when included in models that contain the individual components [40, 41].

In addition to the use of MSS z-scores, strengths of our study include the use of a mass spectrometry-based testosterone assay, the relatively large population-based study sample, and the use of survey weighting procedures to provide valid population-level estimates. Our study also has important limitations. Firstly, data from our observational study cannot confirm causal relationships, and it seems possible that, for example, insulin resistance leads to both hyperandrogenemia and the metabolic syndrome. We note, however, that other evidence suggests that androgens and/or SHBG can directly impact risk for metabolic syndrome [42]. For example, experimental studies suggest that androgens can directly and negatively impact skeletal and adipose tissue insulin sensitivity [43], in addition to having indirect deleterious impacts on adipose tissue function and distribution [44]. Also of interest in this regard, Mendelian randomization analyses in (mostly menopausal) women suggest that higher genetically determined bioavailable testosterone concentrations, in addition to lower genetically determined SHBG concentrations, increase the odds of having type 2 diabetes mellitus and fasting hyperinsulinemia [45]. As a second study limitation, it is possible that the presence vs absence of adolescent PCOS at least partly accounts for the apparent relationship between free testosterone concentrations and metabolic syndrome severity. We were unable to interrogate this possibility given that the data provided in 2013 to 2016 NHANES databases did not permit us to reliably designate adolescent PCOS diagnoses. However, we note that similar relationships have been observed among women without PCOS (eg [14]), suggesting that PCOS does not fully account for the relationships observed in our study. Another important limitation is that pubertal stages were not available in 2013 to 2016 NHANES databases, so our statistical adjustments for maturational stage primarily relied on chronological and gynecological ages. Lastly, we recognize that the accuracy of family-history-of-diabetes designations was likely poor, but this reflects limitations of the NHANES datasets.

Conclusion

In a nationally representative sample of U.S. girls, higher free testosterone is associated with higher MSS z-score. This relationship at least partly reflects the relationship between MSS z-score and SHBG.
